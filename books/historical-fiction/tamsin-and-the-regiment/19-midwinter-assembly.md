## 19. Midwinter Assembly

The council hall had never held so many people.

Tamsin knew that the moment she stepped through its doors, because she had grown up measuring rooms by their emptiness — by where her mother stood at a bench, by how far sound carried across sawdust floors. The hall was built for two hundred souls on a generous day, and tonight it held nearer four hundred, packed onto benches dragged in from the meetinghouse and the schoolroom and somebody's kitchen, standing shoulder to shoulder along the walls, children perched on rafters-beams where the older boys usually smoked stolen pipefuls. The air was thick with wet wool and tallow smoke and the particular animal warmth of a crowd in winter, and beneath it all ran the smell of pitch — someone had been sealing window frames against the cold that afternoon, and the resinous sweetness of it clung to everything like an omen.

Tamsin found a place at the end of the gallery bench, wedged between old Marta Ellery and her granddaughter, and looked down at the floor below. There, in the space normally reserved for the master joiners' worktables, the nine members of the workshop council sat in a shallow crescent behind a long trestle board. Her mother sat at the center of the crescent, as she always did — not at its head, because the Regiment's council had no head, but at the point from which the others could most easily be seen. Odile Vant wore her best coat, which meant the one without glue stains, and her hands lay flat and still upon the trestle wood in a way Tamsin recognized from thirty years of watching: her mother was frightened, and was going to be very careful about letting anyone see it.

And there, three places down, seated among the councilors as if he belonged there, sat Merrick Hale.

That was the thing that made Tamsin's stomach tighten every time she looked at him. Not his fine cloak or his capital accent or the easy way he smiled at the wives of councilors — those were merely the trappings of a man who had traveled far to be disagreeable. It was the seat itself. He had arrived over the ice eleven days ago with Brann's lantern swinging ahead of him, and within three days he had contrived to be given a place at the trestle for this assembly, on the argument — delivered smoothly, reasonably, in the tone of a man offering a gift rather than taking one — that the republic's envoy ought to be present when the town deliberated on the republic's offer. The council, bound by its own law that no assembly might exclude any resident of the Regiment from its gallery, had found no clean way to refuse him the floor. So now he sat among them like a splinter driven deep into good oak: small, dark, and working inward.

Tamsin scanned the gallery until she found the third person who mattered.

Coral was near the back, half-hidden behind a pillar, wearing the brown wool dress she'd been mending all week by lamplight. She had come early — Tamsin had watched her slip into the hall before the crowd, choose that pillar, and stand there as though she'd simply drifted into the shadow of it. But Tamsin had spent enough evenings beside her at the bench now, sanding and planing and failing at dovetails, to know what she was looking at: not the pillar, but the sightlines. The back door. The gallery rail. The distance from the pillar to each. Coral did it the way other people breathed, without deciding to, and Tamsin had stopped being afraid of it somewhere around midwinter's first week. Now it just made her sad, in the way you feel sad watching a bird pace the limits of a cage whose door has quietly been left open.

*She could go,* Tamsin thought, watching her friend's still face catch the lamplight. *The river holds. She could walk out tonight and be gone by dawn, and nobody here would ever think about her again.*

The thought arrived with its usual companion, unwelcome and honest: *and I would think about her every day for the rest of my life.*

Marta Ellery's granddaughter leaned against Tamsin's arm and whispered, "Is it true they'll make us fight?"

"It's true they'll ask us," Tamsin whispered back. "Asking isn't making. That's rather the whole point."

"Hush, both of you," said Marta, though gently. "It's starting."

Odile Vant rose. She did not raise her voice — she never needed to; the hall quieted for her the way water settles after a stone — and when she spoke, her words carried to the rafters with the flat clarity of a chisel finding grain.

"You all know why we're gathered. For those who've been on the mountain and haven't heard: Master Hale, envoy of the Republic of Vessany, has laid before this council an offer. In brief: the Regiment signs over the rolls of its militia — the charter our founders wrote two hundred years ago, the paper that says who may bear arms in this valley and under whose word — and in exchange, the republic pledges us protection through the war, grain through the spring, and recognition of our workshops' rights of trade." She paused. "I will say plainly what some have been too polite to say. The war is coming closer. We have heard the guns across the pass twice since solstice. The republic is losing. Everyone in this room knows it, including, I suspect, the gentleman who is offering to protect us."

A ripple went through the crowd — not laughter exactly, but something adjacent to it, the nervous cousin of laughter. At the trestle, Hale smiled tolerantly, as though Odile had said something charmingly provincial.

"The question before us tonight," Odile continued, "is not whether we fear the war. Of course we fear it. The question is whether we sell the one thing that makes this town ours — the right to decide such questions ourselves — to buy a promise from a government that may not exist by thaw." She spread her hands. "I won't tell you how to vote. I'll only remind you what the charter says, because I helped recopy it last summer and the words are still in me: *The Regiment keeps its own books.* That's the whole of it. Two hundred years, five wars, three famines, and one flood, and that sentence has kept us free when kingdoms fell around us. Tonight we decide whether it keeps us free through a fourth."

She sat. And then, because the Regiment's law required it, the floor opened, and the town began to argue with itself.

---

They argued for two hours.

Tamsin listened the way her mother had taught her to listen — not to the words alone but to the grain beneath them, the way a joiner reads a board before deciding where to cut. Old Fenwick the cooper stood first, red-faced, and said the offer was a lifeline and only a fool refused a rope while drowning. A widow named Sorrel answered him in a voice like a blade drawn slowly: that ropes thrown from sinking ships drowned more swimmers than they saved, and she'd buried a husband who learned it at the battle of Corren Ford. The schoolmaster made a long, careful speech about obligations and history that lost the room by its third paragraph. Brann stood and spoke about opportunity — new markets, republican contracts, rank and standing for men of ambition — and Tamsin watched the way certain faces brightened at his words and certain others hardened, and understood that whatever happened tonight, something in the town had already cracked along that line.

And through all of it, Hale said almost nothing. He listened. He nodded. Twice he asked a clarifying question so reasonable, so modest, that Tamsin wanted to throw something at him. He was very good. He was, she thought, the best liar she had ever seen, because he barely lied at all — he just arranged the truth the way a dishonest merchant arranges scales.

Then, during a lull while the cooper fetched water, Tamsin glanced back toward the pillar, and found Coral looking at her.

Not at the council. Not at Hale. At her. And there was something in Coral's face that Tamsin had never seen there before — something stripped bare, urgent, almost pleading, as if Coral were trying to tell her something across forty feet of crowded air without moving her lips. Their eyes held for three seconds. Four. Then Coral looked away, down at the floor, and her hand rose briefly to touch her own wrist — the left wrist, where for Tamsin's entire acquaintance she had worn that battered leather cuff she never removed even to wash up at the bench.

*The case,* Tamsin thought. *Whatever's in that dispatch case she carries chained under her sleeve — it's about this. It's been about this the whole time.*

The realization didn't shock her so much as settle into place, heavy and inevitable, like a tenon sliding home into a mortise cut for it weeks ago. She had known, in the way you know a board is warped before you measure it. The stranger who counted exits. The woman who decoded nothing and memorized everything, whose silences were too practiced to be shyness. Whatever papers Coral Okonkwo had carried across the pass in autumn, they concerned the charter, the militia rolls, the future of everyone in this hall — and she had been sitting on that knowledge through frost and storm and the slow tightening of winter, saying nothing.

Tamsin should have felt betrayed. She examined herself carefully, honestly, the way she'd been taught to examine a joint for hidden flaws — and found that what she felt instead was fear. Not of Coral. *For* her. Because she had seen Coral's face just now, and it was the face of someone standing at the edge of a decision that would cost her everything either way.

---

The vote, when it finally came near, nearly destroyed itself over procedure.

It was Marta Ellery — of all people, eighty-one years old and sharp as a frosted nail — who rose in the gallery and demanded, in a voice that cut through the hubbub, to know why the council proposed a simple show of hands. "Half this hall can't see the count from where they're standing," she said, "and the other half will lie about what they saw. If we're deciding two hundred years of law, we do it by ballot. Written. Counted twice. Entered in the ledger." A murmur of assent ran through the room, and Tamsin saw her mother incline her head — *fair, and fair-seen* — and begin organizing the distribution of slips and pencils, and the whole great machine of the town's democracy creaked into motion, slow and deliberate as a tide mill.

Which was when Merrick Hale finally stood up.

He did it quietly, waiting for a pause, and when he spoke his voice was mild, almost regretful. "Master Vant. Councilors. Friends." He used the word *friends* the way a fisherman uses bait. "I've sat among you for eleven days now, and I want to say before you vote: whatever you decide, I will respect it. That is my office and my word." He let that land — let the room feel the generosity of it — and then, softly, added the knife. "I would only note, for the record of this assembly, that the republic's offer expires at the thaw. After that date, I am instructed to file a different report. One in which the Regiment of Tamsin's Ford declined lawful alliance with a government it claims to trade under, and instead armed itself independently in wartime territory." He looked around the hall, gentle as a physician delivering news. "I don't know precisely what becomes of a town under that report. Neither, I think, do you. Vote freely. I ask only that you vote knowing the calendar."

The silence afterward was the worst sound Tamsin had ever heard in that hall — worse than storms, worse than the crack of splitting timber. Because everyone understood what he had done. He hadn't threatened anyone. He had simply placed a date next to the word *freely*, the way you'd place a weight on paper to keep it from blowing away, and now every ballot in the hall would carry his fingerprints whether it bore his name or not.

Her mother's hands had gone white on the edge of the trestle.

Then old Sorrel was on her feet again, shouting that this was coercion, that no ballot cast under a deadline was free, that the Regiment had never once in its history voted with a knife at its throat and would not begin tonight — and half the hall was roaring agreement and the other half was roaring back that pride wouldn't stop cannonballs, and for a moment the assembly came apart entirely, four hundred voices in a hall built for two hundred, and Tamsin pressed back against the wall with her heart slamming and thought, with terrible clarity: *this is what he came for. Not our yes. Our noise. Give him a town that tears itself apart and he doesn't need to burn anything.*

It was Odile who ended it. She stood, waited — she was very good at waiting; she could out-wait weather — until the noise broke apart into fragments, then fragments into murmurs, then murmurs into the ringing quiet of four hundred people remembering themselves.

"We defer," she said.

The words dropped into the hall like a stone into a well.

"Not forever. Not out of fear — I'll not pretend his deadline means nothing, because it does. But no town votes well with a boot on its neck, and no ledger balances in a storm. We take seven days. We talk to our neighbors, in our kitchens and our shops, honestly, neighbor to neighbor. And in seven days we return to this hall, and we vote by secret ballot, calm, and whatever we choose, we choose *together* — or we do not deserve the charter we're arguing over." She turned to Hale, and though her voice stayed level, Tamsin saw something in her mother's face that made the hairs rise on her arms: the look of a master examining a joint she already knew she would have to cut out. "Master Hale, the Regiment thanks you for your candor regarding the calendar. Seven days. You'll find our hospitality unchanged."

Hale bowed, gracious, unhurried, and Tamsin understood suddenly, watching the smooth pleasure he failed quite to hide, that the deferral suited him perfectly. Seven days was time. Time for Brann to work. Time for doubt to grow in the dark like mold.

---

The hall emptied slowly, in knots of twos and threes, arguments continuing on the steps and out into the blue snow-light of the square. Tamsin stayed behind to help stack benches, as she always did, and so it was that she was still there, wrestling a bench onto the pile with the cooper's son, when the last stragglers filtered out and she saw Coral approach the trestle where Odile stood alone, rolling up the evening's tally slips that would be entered tomorrow.

She shouldn't have lingered. She told herself she was just slow with the bench. She lingered.

She couldn't hear the words from the gallery — the hall was too big, their voices too low — but she could see everything, and seeing, in her family, had always been nearly the same as hearing. She watched her mother look up. Watched Coral stand before the trestle the way a debtor stands before a ledger. Watched her say something short — three sentences, maybe four — and watched her mother go absolutely still, in the particular way she went still when a piece of information reorganized an entire project. Then Odile glanced toward the doors, checked the hall was empty, and said something back, low and long, one hand rising to rest flat on the closed ledger book as if to steady it — or herself.

And Coral reached to the cuff on her left wrist, and began, deliberately, to unclasp it.

Tamsin turned away then. Some joints you weren't meant to watch being cut.

But as she carried the last bench out into the cold, past the windows glowing amber against the dark, she passed close by the corner of the building — and stopped.

Two figures stood in the shadow of the woodshed across the lane, out of the lamplight. Brann, and beside him Merrick Hale, heads together, breath pluming. She couldn't hear them. But she could see Hale's gloved hand rise and tap Brann once, twice, on the breastbone — a gesture patient and proprietary, a man tapping a barrel to hear how full it was — and she could see Brann nod, eagerly, like an apprentice promised his own bench at last.

Seven days, her mother had said. Tamsin stood in the snow with her heart going hard and quiet, and understood that Hale intended to make sure the second vote never needed counting at all.
