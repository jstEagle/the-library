## 20. What Hale Knows

The summons came the way Coral had always feared such things would come: quietly, without witnesses, in a voice pitched for courtesy.

She was at the workshop, sanding a length of ash for a bedframe, when Tamsin appeared at her elbow with the particular set of her jaw that meant she was carrying someone else's message and disliked it. "Councilor Brann's asking after you," she said. "Says there's a gentleman down at the ferry house who wants a word. A republican gentleman." Her eyes were sharp on Coral's face, watching for what it would do.

"And he asked for me by name?"

"By your name here. Coral. The seamstress's cousin." Tamsin paused. "That bother you?"

"Should it?"

"It should if you're who I think you might be." She said it lightly, but not carelessly — nothing about Tamsin was ever careless — and then she shrugged and went back to her bench, leaving Coral alone with the shavings and the cold weight settling behind her ribs.

*He knows.* The thought arrived whole, the way ice arrives on still water: not gradually but all at once, everywhere, the surface gone from liquid to solid between one breath and the next. She set down the sanding block. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were steady. Fifteen years of practice did that much for you, even when everything beneath the practice had turned to water.

She finished the board first. That mattered to her more than she could have said — finishing the board, brushing off the dust, setting her tools in their places as Odile had taught her, each one home in its slot. If this was the day the mask came off, she would leave the bench clean. Some habits outlasted their reasons.

Then she put on her coat and walked down through the town toward the river.

* * *

The ferry house sat at the foot of the frozen landing, its timbers silvered with frost, the great wheel-hoist silent now until spring. Inside it smelled of tar and rope and old smoke. Merrick Hale stood by the iron stove with his hands clasped behind his back, studying the ferryman's tally-board as though the crossing counts of a mountain town were a document worth memorizing. He had removed his coat, which was a courtesy, and he had dismissed the boy who tended the stove, which was not.

"Cousin Coral," he said, turning. He smiled the way a man smiles when he has already won something and is enjoying the last moments before the loser understands it. "Thank you for coming. I know how busy the workshop keeps you."

"You have the advantage of me, sir."

"I don't think so." He drew a stool near the stove and sat, unhurried, arranging himself like a man settling in for a long negotiation over cards. "Sit, please. There's no one within earshot. The ferryman is drinking at the Hearthstone and won't be back for an hour; I paid him to make sure of it."

Coral did not sit. "If you have business with the council—"

"I have no business with the council. I have business with you, Courier Okonkwo."

There it was. Her name, spoken plainly, in that flat downriver accent, hanging in the smoky air of a ferry house at the edge of the world. Fifteen years since anyone had spoken it aloud. She felt it go through her like a blade finding the gap in armor she'd stopped maintaining years ago because she'd stopped believing anyone could reach it.

She made herself frown, faintly puzzled, a woman trying on an unfamiliar word. "I'm afraid you've mistaken me for—"

"Don't." Hale's voice didn't rise. It dropped instead, losing its polish, and that was worse. "Please. I've watched you for two months. You walk like a courier — weight forward, always noting the doors. You eat like a courier — fast, seated facing the room. When Master Vant showed you the archive shelves, you read the spines left to right, top shelf down, the cataloguing system used by exactly three institutions in the Republic, and I'd wager my pension you've worked in two of them." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight catching the gray at his temples. "You looked at the bridge collapse the morning after it happened and you measured it. Not worriedly. *Professionally*. And when the ferryman refused the crossing, everyone in that town saw a traveler disappointed at being stranded." His smile came again, thinner. "I saw a woman calculating how long it would take to walk out over the ice."

Coral said nothing. There was nothing useful to say, and silence was the oldest tool she owned. She let it stretch, let him fill it, watched his hands and the door and the window behind him where the light was failing blue over the river ice.

"You're wondering when I knew," Hale said. "Since the ferry. Since the last ferry, the one I came up on. You were standing at the rail of the landing with a basket you'd borrowed from the seamstress, buying dried apples, and you never once looked at the boat. Do you know why that gave you away? Because everyone else in this town looks at the boat. The boat is the only thing that comes. A woman who has been trapped behind a collapsed bridge all season should hunger for the sight of it." He spread his hands. "You looked at *me*. Once. Half a second. The way a professional looks at a stranger — counting my weapons, my boots, my letters of introduction. Then you went back to your apples and never looked again, because you'd already decided I wasn't dangerous." He laughed softly, without much humor. "You were wrong about that. But you weren't wrong about the method. I recognized it because it's mine too."

"The Republic sends many men," Coral said carefully. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears — level, mild, the voice of the seamstress's cousin. But the fiction was dead now and they both knew it, and continuing to speak inside its corpse felt like praying to a god you'd seen die.

"The Republic sends many men," Hale agreed. "And the Republic, I regret to inform you, is dying. You know it. I know it. The armies are forty days south and moving apart, not together. The treasury hasn't paid field salaries since the autumn equinox — I know because I haven't been paid either, and neither, I suspect, have you. We are both servants of a state that has stopped existing in everything but paperwork, and the paperwork—" he gestured at her wrist, at the place where the dispatch case's chain had left its faint pale scar through the autumn, a mark she'd thought no one had seen, "—the paperwork is the last part that still works. Isn't that so? The messages still move. The orders still arrive. Men like us still carry them. The corpse still twitches because we keep pumping it."

"You talk a great deal for a man who wanted privacy."

"I want you to understand the situation before you decide anything, because I think you're a reasonable woman, and reasonable women make reasonable choices." He reached into his breast pocket, slowly, showing her his empty palm first, and drew out a folded paper. He did not offer it. Just held it. "This is my commission. What remains of it. My instructions authorize me to secure the Regiment's militia charter for the war effort by any means the Council of Public Safety judges necessary. Do you know what *necessary* means, out here, this winter?" 

"I can guess."

"Guess better." He set the paper on the stove's iron top, deliberately, where she could see it but not take it. "If the charter comes willingly, the town keeps its workshops, its laws, its little ledger-book government. The rolls go south, the charter goes south, and Tamsin's Ford becomes a line in a supply column. If the charter does not come willingly, then the Republic declares this town in rebellion — a formality, a stamp, ink already mixed — and declares that any force hostile to the Republic may treat it accordingly. Which means the enemy army, when it comes up this valley in the spring, comes with permission. And this town burns with the Republic's blessing, and no one downriver will ever ask why."

Coral's mouth was dry. Not because she hadn't known — she had decoded enough of the second dispatch by now to know the shape of it — but because hearing it said aloud, in plain words, by the man whose job it was, stripped away the last comfortable fog of abstraction. *The town burns.* She thought of Tamsin at her bench, tongue between her teeth, fitting a joint. Of Odile's ledger. Of the children running through the sawdust.

"And your instructions to me," she said quietly, "are what, exactly?"

"To come home." Hale rose and crossed to the narrow window, looking out at the ice. "Deliver your dispatches. Report to me. Confirm what I report. In exchange—" he turned back, and for just a moment something that was almost honesty moved across his face, some tiredness older than this assignment, "—you survive. Both of us survive. Two professionals at the end of a dying state, doing the last small job well, going home when the roads open. There will be pensions, or whatever pensions become when there's a new government. There will be quiet postings. There will be *life*, Okonkwo. Or there is the other road, which is that you keep playing the seamstress's cousin until spring, and when my report goes downriver saying the courier went native, went silent, went *lost* — you know what happens to lost couriers. Their names go on a list. And lists outlive governments. I've seen it. The Republic falls, and some clerk in the new ministry finds the old lists, and finds them *useful*."

"You're threatening me."

"I'm describing the weather." He picked the paper up again and held it out to her this time, properly, an official document passing between officials. "Take it. Read it. You'll find my instructions match yours closely enough. Buy the charter. However it must be bought. The difference between us, Okonkwo, is that I intend to follow them."

She took it. Not because she needed to read it — she could recite the template from memory, had carried its cousins a dozen times into a dozen towns whose names she'd made herself forget — but because refusing to take it would be a move, and moves were for people with plans, and she needed three more minutes of not being a person with a plan while the rest of her caught up.

The paper said what he'd said it would say. Necessary measures. Rebellion designation. The dry language that had always been the Republic's true voice, kinder things being only its handwriting.

"They seem like good people here," Hale said, watching her read. His tone shifted, softened into something almost gentle, and somehow that was the cruelest stroke yet. "The joiners. The girl. I'll grant you, it's a better town than most I've been sent to unmake. And that's precisely why you should think hard. Because people like this — self-governing, stubborn, fond of their own rules — they always think their decency will protect them. It never has yet. What protects them is paperwork, and the paperwork is in our cases, yours and mine. We are the weather, Okonkwo. They're the crops. Don't stand in the field arguing with the sky."

"Is that what you tell yourself?"

"What I tell myself is that I was given orders, and that the alternative to following them is a list, and that I have a daughter in Corren's Landing who eats because I follow orders." Something flickered under the words — not guilt, Coral thought, or not only guilt. Weariness, worn smooth as river stone, the weariness of a man who had done this arithmetic so many times the numbers had stopped meaning anything. "You'll understand that eventually, if you don't already. Everyone who carries the case learns it. The case teaches you what you are."

"And what am I?"

"Mine," he said simply. "As of the ferry. As of the moment you looked at me and decided I wasn't dangerous. You've been mine for two months, Courier. All this time, every supper, every lesson in joinery, every laugh in the workshop — I've known. And you've known that the charter means soldiers and the refusal means fire, and you've said *nothing* to these good people, because that's your training. So don't stand there judging my arithmetic." He buttoned his coat, unhurried, a man concluding a pleasant call. "You have until the thaw. Deliver the charter, or deliver yourself, and either way you come to me before the ice breaks. Those are the terms. There aren't other ones."

He walked to the door, then paused, hand on the latch, and looked back at her with something that in another life, another country, might have been respect.

"For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much — I hope you take the sensible road. You're the best I've seen at this work. It would be a waste." A pause. "They'll never let you stay, you know. People like this. Sooner or later they ask questions you can't answer, and then the town that sheltered you becomes the town that handed you over, and you'll wish you'd chosen the list."

Then he was gone, boots crunching on the frozen mud, and Coral stood alone in the ferry house with the stove ticking as it cooled and the commission paper trembling very slightly in her hand.

* * *

She did not go straight back to the town. She walked out onto the landing instead, past the mooring rings furred with ice, and stood where the bridge timbers had been, looking at the black gap in the world where the crossing used to be, and beyond it the river running flat and white to the bend.

The wind came down the valley and cut through her coat, and she let it, because the cold was simpler than the thing happening inside her.

*He's known since the ferry.* Every meal. Every evening by the Hearthstone fire. Every moment she had spent measuring the town's exits, he had spent measuring her, patient as a spider in a corner of a room she'd believed she controlled. And the worst of it — the part that made her press her mittened hand against her mouth — was that he was right about her too. Two months in Tamsin's Ford. Two months of warm bread and lamplight and Tamsin's laughter over her crooked dovetails, and she had said *nothing*. Had watched the council debate rations while holding the knowledge that the debate was about whether they'd burn. Had told herself it wasn't time, wasn't safe, wasn't certain — the same words Sef had taught her, the same liturgy of delay, and underneath it the same old truth: that she had spent fifteen years becoming a woman who could watch a village from inside it and keep her face smooth, and the skill had worked so well that now it was working on her.

*Don't stand in the field arguing with the sky.*

But Sef had told her something else once, in the last lesson she'd gotten from him, the one that hadn't been in the curriculum. *A courier who only knows how to disappear,* he'd said, lighting his pipe with hands that shook from the cheap wine, *ends up serving whoever notices her least kindly. Learn to be seen, girl. Someday it'll be the only weapon you've got.*

She looked down at the ice. Solid. Walkable. Forty miles to the downriver road and a life of lists and survival, or a winter more of this warm, doomed, stubborn town.

Hale had given her until the thaw. He thought he had named the terms.

Coral turned back toward the town, where lamplight was coming up window by window against the early dark, and found that her fear had burned itself down to something harder and quieter underneath. He knew her. Fine. Let him know her. Knowing what a woman was had never yet been the same as knowing what she would do — and he had made one error tonight, one small professional error, in all that flawless surveillance.

He had shown her his deadline.

And deadlines, Coral thought, walking up from the river with the ice groaning somewhere beneath her feet like something turning over in its sleep, were the one thing a courier could always use.

Behind her the river lay locked and silent. Ahead, above the workshop, she could see the kiln's chimney breathing its thin smoke into the dark — and beside it, small against the snow, a figure with a lantern, waiting up, who had watched her walk down to the ferry house and had counted, Coral was suddenly certain, every minute she'd been gone.

Tamsin. Who deserved the truth, or as much of it as wouldn't get them both killed tonight.

Coral climbed the lane toward the light, and began, in the old silent language of her trade, to compose the first honest sentence she had spoken in fifteen years.
