## 22. Brann's Error

Tamsin had always known which floorboards in the council house creaked, which doors needed a shoulder, which lamps burned the oil fastest. She had been running errands for the council since she was nine years old, carrying wax tablets and tally-sticks between the workshop benches, and she knew the building the way Odile knew the grain of walnut — by feel, by habit, by the thousand small lessons a body learns without noticing it is learning them.

Which was why, on the grey afternoon in late midwinter when the snow came down like sifted flour and the whole town had drawn inward around its fires, she noticed at once that something was wrong with the council house's evening.

The building should have been dark. Council had met at noon and adjourned; the archive-keeper, old Mistress Pell, had gone home to her supper an hour past; the clerk's desk was supposed to be locked, the shutters closed, the iron box with the charter rolls under triple lock in the back room. But a thin line of lamplight showed under the shutters of the clerk's window, and smoke curled from the chimney in a thread too small for a fire, too steady for a stove that had been banked and left.

Someone was in the council house who was not supposed to be there.

Tamsin stood in the falling snow with her basket of mended hinges on her arm and considered this. She was seventeen, and she had grown up in a town whose whole constitution could be written on the back of a shutter: the Regiment kept its own books, chose its own council, answered to no lord and no republic, and the law of the town was simple enough for a child to recite. *What is owed is written down. What is written down is true. What is true belongs to everyone.* Her mother had helped write half of it.

So the correct thing — the lawful thing — was to go to the door, knock, and announce herself.

She did not do the correct thing.

Later she would tell herself it was because she was curious, and that would be true, but it would not be the whole truth. The whole truth was that she had been watching Councilor Brann for three weeks, ever since the night of the deferred vote, and there was a thing about him she could not put down, the way you cannot put down a splinter working toward the bone. Brann had been *happy* lately. Brann, who had spent the whole autumn arguing that the republic's charter offer was the town's only shield against the enemy army downriver, had taken to smiling at nothing, to humming while he sharpened his pen-knives, to a lightness in his step that had no honest source she could find. The vote had been deferred, which should have made him anxious. Instead he moved through the town like a man who already knew how the story ended.

And there was the other thing, the smaller thing, the thing she had told only Coral, sitting by the kiln with their feet in the warm ash: that Brann had asked her, twice now, casual as asking after her mother's health, what the workshop council discussed when the full assembly was not present. What Odile said about the republic's agent. What the ledger-keepers argued about. What Coral — the quiet seamstress's cousin, the woman who had come before the freeze and stayed — had been seen doing at the archive.

*Why do you ask about her so often?* Tamsin had said the second time, and Brann had smiled his new smile and said, *Because a town that takes in strangers should know what it's taken in,* and the sentence had been reasonable and had still felt, in Tamsin's chest, like a hand closing around something soft.

She set her basket down behind the woodpile, where the snow would not find it, and went around to the back of the council house the way she had gone around it a hundred times as a child, when sneaking in to hear the grown arguments had seemed like the most important work in the world.

The back door was not locked.

That was the third wrong thing. The clerk's door was always locked; Mistress Pell kept the key on a cord around her neck and slept with it against her sternum like a saint's relic. Tamsin pressed the door open with two fingers and slipped inside, and the warmth of the banked stove closed around her, and she stood in the dark of the passage with the snow melting off her shoulders and listened.

From the clerk's room came the small, wet sounds of a man working quickly: the scratch of a pen, the turn of a page, the soft *thock* of the copying-press, and under it all the breathing of someone trying to be quiet and not quite managing it.

Tamsin moved along the passage on the boards that did not creak. She had a plan, of a kind: look through the gap in the shutters, see who it was, leave the way she came, and then decide — with her mother, with Odile, with someone — what the seeing meant. She was not a spy. She was a joiner's daughter with ink on her cuffs and her mother's stubbornness in her jaw, and she told herself firmly that she was only gathering information, the way the town gathered everything, openly, to be weighed in common.

Then she looked through the gap in the shutters, and the plan died quietly in her throat.

It was Brann. Of course it was Brann. He sat at the clerk's desk with his coat hung on the chair back and his sleeves rolled, and before him lay the charter rolls — the militia rolls, the town's own book of names and arms and obligations, the document Hale had come to buy and the assembly had refused, for now, to sell — and he was copying them. Not reading them. *Copying* them, page by page, in a fair quick hand, the names of every man and woman in the Regiment who had ever stood a watch or owned a fowling piece or signed the muster, going down onto loose sheets that did not belong to the town's books and never would.

Tamsin's first feeling was not anger. It was a strange, cold clarity, like the moment on the ice when you hear the crack run out ahead of your boot. She watched Brann's pen move. She watched him reach the page headed *Standing Watch, Winter Rotation* — a page in her mother's hand — and copy that too, the watch-names, the watch-hours, the places where the sentries stood and the hours when the posts changed. She watched him fold the finished sheets into a packet no bigger than a prayer book and tuck it into the inner pocket of his coat, where it sat against his heart like the treacherous little organ it was replacing.

He was going to give it to Hale. There was no other explanation in the world. The republic's agent had arrived on the last ferry with soft words and a charter offer, and when the town would not sell, he had found the man in the council who would steal.

And Tamsin stood in the dark with her hand on the cold shutter and understood, with that same terrible clarity, that she was now holding something heavier than information. She was holding Brann. Everything he was, everything he had built in this town over thirty years — his bench on the council, his vote, his voice at the assembly, the respect of neighbors who had lent him tools and trusted him with their sons' apprenticeships — all of it was in her hand now, folded up as small as the packet in his coat.

The lawful thing was simple. The town's law said *what is true belongs to everyone.* She should walk into that room right now, or she should walk straight to her mother, and Brann should be brought before the council tomorrow, and the town would weigh him in the open the way it weighed everything. That was the whole machinery of the Regiment: no secrets, no private justice, everything written down.

But her feet did not move, because under the law sat another thing, older and less tidy, and it whispered to her in her mother's voice and in Coral's voice and sometimes, at night, in her own: *What will it cost, and who will pay it?*

If she named Brann tomorrow, the town would tear itself. Not with knives — the Regiment did not do its bleeding with knives — but with the worse wound of *knowing.* Every family that had ever broken bread with the Branns would spend the spring choosing sides. The vote on the charter, already deferred once, would collapse into fury, and fury was exactly the weather in which Hale's arguments would flourish: *you see how they turn on each other, you see how thin your order is, sell the rolls and sleep soundly.* She could hand the town the truth and watch the truth do the republic's work for it.

And there was Coral to think of. Coral, who had told her by the kiln — not everything, never everything, but enough — about what the dispatches said, about what the republic would do to a town that refused. Coral, whose name Brann had been asking after in exactly the tone of a man building a case. If Brann fell, Hale would not fall with him; Hale would simply find out who had felled his informant, and Tamsin did not think Hale's attention was a thing anyone she loved should stand inside.

So she did neither the lawful thing nor nothing. She did the thing that would trouble her for weeks after, the thing that was neither law nor justice but something in between, a joiner's daughter's fix, a splice in bad timber meant to hold until proper work could be done.

She waited in the dark until Brann finished. She watched him trim the lamp, gather his coat, lock the clerk's door with a key she now realized he must have copied or stolen from Mistress Pell's cord while the old woman dozed at the midwinter assembly — another theft, stacked on the first — and let himself out the back door not six feet from where Tamsin stood pressed against the passage wall, close enough that she could have touched the wool of his shoulder, close enough to smell the tallow and the ink on him. He did not see her. Nobody ever saw her; she had her mother's trick of standing in a room like a piece of well-made furniture, present and unremarked, and for the first time in her life she understood that this trick of hers and Coral's whole trained invisibility were not so far apart, and the understanding frightened her more than the theft had.

She followed him.

Not far, and not well — Brann was not a courier and did not check his back-trail, but the snow was falling thick and the streets were white silence, and she kept to the opposite side and let the flakes blur her. He went not to his own house but to the guesthouse by the ferry landing where Merrick Hale lodged at the town's expense, because the Regiment was too proud to let even a man they distrusted sleep in the snow. He went in the side door. The light in his window burned for a quarter of an hour. When it went out, Tamsin stood in the lee of the boat-shed with the snow gathering on her hood and felt the last of her childhood close behind her like a door.

* * *

She did not go home. She went to the workshop.

It was near midnight, and the great room was dark except for the banked forge breathing its dull orange under the hood, and for one lamp still burning at Odile's bench in the corner where her mother worked too late on nights when a joint would not sit right in her head. Odile Vant looked up when the door opened, saw her daughter's face, and set down her chisel without a word.

Tamsin told her all of it, standing, because sitting felt like permission to stay and she did not want permission; she wanted judgment. The unlocked door. The copying. The watch-rotation in her mother's hand. The packet against Brann's heart. The light in Hale's window. She told it in order, plainly, the way the town taught its children to give evidence at the assembly, and when she finished the forge ticked and settled and the snow ticked against the windows, and her mother was silent for a long time.

"You should have knocked on the door," Odile said at last. "That was the law. You know it was the law."

"I know it was the law."

"And you didn't, because you'd already decided the law's answer would cost more than the town could pay." Odile said it without heat, the way she read a board for the flaw hidden in its grain, and Tamsin felt the truth of it go into her like a splinter. "That's the first step on a road, Tamsin. Every tyrant downriver started as a person who decided the truth was too dangerous to tell. I want you to hear me say that before we go one word further."

Tamsin's jaw set. "Then tell me the other thing too. Tell me what happens tomorrow if I stand up in council and say it. Half the town will call Brann a traitor and half will call me a liar and Hale will sit in the middle selling both sides rope. You *saw* the midwinter assembly. We couldn't even agree on the charter when we liked each other."

Her mother was quiet again. The lamp threw their two shadows huge on the whitewashed wall, and Tamsin thought: *she's not angry, she's frightened,* and had never seen her mother frightened before, and found it was the worst sight of her life.

"Get Coral," Odile said finally.

"Coral's not on the council. Coral's not even —"

"No," Odile said. "But she's the only person in this town who knows what Hale does with stolen muster-rolls, and she's the only person Brann's been asking about, and I'll tell you something else I haven't said out loud." She looked at her daughter across the lamp. "The night of the storm, when the roof went, Coral was the first one up the ladder and the last one down, and she didn't calculate it. I've watched her for a season. She came here a courier and she's becoming something else, and if there's a way through this that doesn't bleed us, she'll see it, because she's spent fifteen years learning exactly how people like Hale break towns." She stood, and took her coat off its peg, and the lamplight caught the grey coming into her hair. "And because you told me first, which was right, and now I'm telling her, which is also right, and that's the whole difference between us and the republic. We pass the truth hand to hand. We don't hoard it and we don't spend it all at once."

* * *

Coral came quickly, wrapped in her shawl with the snow still in her dark hair, and she listened to Tamsin's account a second time with an stillness that Tamsin had learned to read as attention rather than coldness. When it was done, Coral asked only three questions. *Did he take the key from Pell's cord, or was the box already open?* — *Open,* Tamsin said; Pell would have felt a hand at her neck even asleep. *Was the packet sealed?* — *No.* And then, after a pause in which the fire ticked and Odile's face was stone: *Did he see you? Are you certain?* — and Tamsin said, *certain,* and watched something move behind Coral's eyes that might have been relief and might have been its opposite.

"Then here is what we know," Coral said, in the low even voice she used for facts. "Hale now has, or will have by morning, the full militia rolls and the winter watch rotation. That means he knows every armed soul in this town and every hour your defenses change. If the republic decides to declare Tamsin's Ford in rebellion — and we both know that dispatch exists, I decoded it — those pages are the difference between a raid and a massacre." She looked at Odile. "You can't take the copies back without showing him we know. You can't accuse Brann without splitting the town at the exact moment you need it whole. And you can't do nothing, because every day the pages sit in his coat, your law means less and his does."

"Then what?" Tamsin said. It came out harder than she meant. "You're the one who knows his tricks. Tell us the trick that beats him."

Coral was quiet for a moment, and Tamsin saw her do the thing she did — the counting, the weighing, the courier's arithmetic of exits — and then, deliberately, set it aside, the way you set down a heavy tool.

"The trick," Coral said slowly, "is that Brann made an error, and it's bigger than theft. He copied the rolls *before* the assembly has voted. As of tonight, the charter isn't refused — it's deferred. Which means the rolls he stole aren't the records of a town that said no. They're the records of a town still deciding. And a man who steals a town's decision out from under it, before the town has made it—" she glanced at Odile, and something passed between the two women that Tamsin could not follow and did not like, "—hasn't just broken your law. He's taken the vote out of every hand in this town but his own. That's not a crime against a person. That's a crime against the Regiment itself. And your law has a name for that, doesn't it? I've heard the children recite it."

Tamsin's mouth had gone dry. "*What is owed is written down,*" she said. "What's written down is true. What's true belongs to everyone."

"Then the answer isn't to hide what Brann did," Coral said. "And it isn't to shout it in the square. It's to make it *written down* — witnessed, dated, entered — before Hale's copies can be the only version of the truth in the world. Let Mistress Pell find her box disturbed tomorrow and record it. Let the council convene and note the disturbance in the book, no names, no accusations — just the fact, in the town's own hand, in the town's own book. Then, when the moment comes to name him, the ledger is already standing behind you. Hale's papers will say one thing. Your books will say another. And everyone downriver who ever looks at this will know which town kept its accounts honestly."

Odile let out a long breath. "You'd use the law as a shield instead of a sword."

"I'd use it as *timber*," Coral said. "You don't swing it. You build with it, fast, before the storm."

For a moment nobody spoke, and Tamsin looked from her mother to the quiet woman by the forge and felt the strange vertigo of watching two kinds of strength agree. Then Odile nodded once, the short nod she gave when a joint had seated true.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Pell at first light. Council at noon. And Tamsin—" she turned, and her eyes were tired and proud and afraid all at once, "—you say nothing to anyone. Not to your friends, not to the apprentices, not even to the wall. A secret held by three people is timber. Held by four, it's kindling."

They put out the lamp and let the forge's last orange hold the room, and Tamsin walked home through the falling snow with her mother's hand heavy on her shoulder, and she thought she understood, finally, what Coral had tried to tell her by the kiln: that the truth was not a thing you simply told. It was a thing you *placed* — like a peg, like a wedge — and the whole craft of a life was in the placing.

She was halfway home, boots squeaking in the fresh white silence, when she saw the figure standing under the eave of the guesthouse porch, half a street behind her, where the lamplight just reached.

It was Brann. And he was looking not at her, but at the workshop — at the window where, minutes ago, three shadows had stood close together around a single lamp.

He had not seen her in the council house. But he had seen the lamp burn late, and he knew whose window it was, and as Tamsin stood frozen in the snow he turned his head, slowly, and looked directly at her across the empty street.

"Tamsin Vant," he said, and his voice was pleasant, and his new smile was back. "Late night for a young woman. I wonder what your mother's been working on."
