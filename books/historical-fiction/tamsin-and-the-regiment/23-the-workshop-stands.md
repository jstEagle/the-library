## 23. The Workshop Stands

The storm came down off the pass two days after the midwinter assembly, and it came like something with a grudge.

Coral heard it first as a change in the sound of the town — a low, grinding pressure in the dark that made the windowpanes hum in their leads. She had been awake anyway, lying in the narrow bed above the seamstress's house where she lodged under her assumed name, running through the night's calculations the way she always did: the frozen river holding below, the road out closed, Hale's rooms at the inn dark or lit, Brann's shuttered windows, the three ways off the mountain and which of them would still be open by thaw.

She had done this every night for fifteen years. It was not a choice anymore; it was breathing. *If they come for me, I go out the back, over the tanning sheds, down to the ice. If the ice won't hold, I take the goat track west. If I have four minutes, I can be gone. If I have two—*

Then the wind hit the workshop, and all the arithmetic went out of her head at once.

The sound was enormous — a tearing, a groan of timber, a crash that she felt through the floorboards before she properly heard it. She was up and dressed before she knew she'd decided anything, boots half-laced, dispatch case — what remained of it, the leather shell she still carried out of habit more than duty — shoved under the mattress out of reflex. In the street, snow came sideways, thick as wool, and the lamps were already being lit against it, little trembling islands of orange in a world gone white and roaring.

People were running toward the crash. Not away from it. Toward.

She stood in the doorway a moment, snow stinging her face, and watched them come — joiners and potters and the ferryman's sons, old Odile Vant herself with a lantern held high and her grey braid whipping loose from its pins, Tamsin a step behind her mother with a coil of rope over one shoulder like a soldier's bandolier. They were shouting to each other over the wind, but there was no panic in it. It sounded almost like a roll call.

"The ridge beam's gone," someone yelled. "North slope's opened like a box!"

"Then we close the box! Tarps first, timber after — move!"

Coral moved.

* * *

The workshop was the heart of the Regiment, and everyone in Tamsin's Ford knew it, though no one would have phrased it so grandly. It was a long building of stone and heavy oak at the bend of the river street, raised by the founders three generations back and added to ever since, its interior a cathedral of benches and lathes and hanging planes, its air permanently sweet with sawdust and hide glue and linseed. Half the town's charters, ledgers, and records lived in the archive room at its eastern end. The other half of the town's life happened under its roof every day.

And now a third of that roof was open to the sky.

Coral reached it with the second wave of townsfolk and stopped dead in the lee of the doorway, snow crusting her lashes, and stared up through the wound. The storm had found the north slope's weakest span — an old repair, someone said, done hastily the summer before last — and peeled it back like a lid. The great ridge beam had cracked along a hidden flaw and dropped one end through the rafters, and the wind was pouring through the gap with a howl that seemed personal, driving snow down onto the workbenches in long white banners. Water was already finding the archive room's door.

"It'll take the whole south slope if we don't brace it," Odile shouted, and her voice, though pitched to carry, was calm in a way that Coral had learned to recognize as the town's native weather. Not the absence of fear. Fear, mastered and put to work. "Brann — you know the truss work better than anyone. Take six hands and shore the south wall. Merrick—" and here Odile turned, without a flicker, to the man standing at the edge of the lantern light, snow melting on the shoulders of his fine city coat, "—you've strong arms, I expect. Make yourself useful or make yourself scarce."

Hale's face did something very small and very controlled. Coral, watching from across the chaos, caught all of it: the flicker of contempt, the recalculation, the decision that refusing would cost him more than labor would. He set down his lantern, took off his coat, folded it with maddening precision over a dry bench, and rolled his sleeves.

"Tell me where," he said.

Coral looked away before he could look at her. But not before she noticed that he had chosen, deliberately, to work within sight of her.

* * *

What followed was the longest night of her life, and she had once spent three days in a root cellar outside Vessany City while soldiers searched the farmstead overhead.

They worked in chains of hands. Men on the remaining sound rafters passed tarps up into the dark and lashed them over the breach while the wind tried to strip them away; women below hauled the great oiled canvas sheets from the storage loft, heavy as ship sails, stiff with cold. The smith's boys ran a relay of lanterns and hot bricks. Someone brought out the timber stock — seasoned oak beams meant for spring contracts — and there was a moment when Coral saw old Ferren the cooper hesitate, his hand flat on a beam worth more than his house, before he said, "Well. It's only wood. We can grow more wood," and sent it up the ladder.

Nobody asked whether the town could afford it. Nobody consulted anybody. There was no vote, no council, no debate. The storm had called the assembly, and the assembly was everyone who could stand in the snow.

Coral found herself on a chain passing shingles and sacking, shoulder to shoulder with the ferryman's daughter, with the schoolmistress, with a boy of eleven whose mittens were soaked through and whose grin never wavered. Her hands, trained to pick locks and forge seals and do nothing that could be remembered, learned in an hour how to pass a load without looking at it, how to read the rhythm of a line of people, how to give and receive weight without words. Once she stumbled on the icy threshold and a woman she didn't know caught her arm, steadied her, and said, "There you are, love," as if Coral had lived here all her life, and moved on.

*THERE YOU ARE.* As if she had simply been expected. As if belonging were a thing you could fall into by accident, the way you fell into a river.

She worked until her shoulders burned and her fingers went wooden inside her gloves, and all the while some cold clerk in the back of her mind kept trying to resume its calculations — *exits, timings, if-thens* — and kept failing, because there was no version of flight that included this. No route off the mountain that contained the smell of hot glue and wet wool, the sound of forty people singing something tuneless and profane to keep the tarps from tearing, the sight of Tamsin Vant thirty feet up on a rafter with snow in her hair, hammering a temporary batten home by lantern light and yelling down, "Mother! Your beam is rotten as a pear! Who cut this?"

"I did!" Odile roared back, hauling on a rope below. "In a wet summer with a bad apprentice! Mind your own!"

"You *are* my own!"

The laughter that went down the line of hands was ragged and breathless and real, and Coral felt something crack in her chest that had nothing to do with the weather.

* * *

Near midnight the wind shifted, screaming around from the west, and the tarps began to go. One tore loose entirely and cartwheeled off into the dark over the roofs, gone like a gull. A second began to peel, its lashings chewing through the frozen rope, and Tamsin — who should by every law of sense have come down — went *up*, out along the slick ridge with the rope around her waist and three people below paying it out, and got her arm through the failing eyelet just as the gust took it.

For one heartbeat the whole line froze. Coral saw it all with terrible clarity: the small figure silhouetted against the racing clouds, the canvas bellied out like a sail trying to become a wing, the rope humming.

"Hold the line!" Odile's voice cut through everything, iron under the fear. "Nobody drops it. Tammy — feet wide, knees bent, let it pull you, don't fight it — *now lean!*"

Tamsin leaned. The line took the strain. Forty people hauled in silence except for the wind, and the tarp came down over the breach like a curtain falling on the last act, and the workshop exhaled beneath them, its broken timbers settling into their new bracing with a groan that sounded almost grateful.

When Tamsin came down the ladder her hands were shaking too hard to undo her own rope, and Coral was there before she had decided to be, working the frozen knot with her courier's fingers, quick and sure.

"You're mad," Coral said. The words came out rougher than she intended. "That rope would have taken you over the ridge."

"Probably." Tamsin's teeth were chattering; she was grinning anyway, snow-melt running off her chin. "But it wouldn't have taken the archive. Mother says a building is just a promise the town makes to itself. You keep the promise or you don't."

"A building is timber and nails," Coral said, and heard, even as she said it, how much she sounded like Sef — like the man who had taught her that nothing was worth dying for, that the only sacred object was the message and the only virtue was getting away.

Tamsin looked at her, and in the lantern light her eyes were sharp and warm at once, the way they always were, and Coral understood that the girl had heard exactly what she'd said and filed it away, the way she filed everything, to be brought out later at the worst possible moment.

"That's what a republic thinks a building is," Tamsin said. "Come on. There's glue to heat and a floor to dry. You're on the archive room — you've the steadiest hands in this town and don't think nobody's noticed."

* * *

They saved the archive by inches. Water had gotten under the door and stood in black pools across the flagstones, and the lowest shelves had begun to drink it. For two hours Coral knelt in the cold with a sponge and a lamp, passing books and scroll-cases and ledger-boxes hand over hand up to dry arms above, and it was there, in the smallest and most desperate theater of the night, that she finally understood what she had been looking at since autumn.

Because these were not just any books. She recognized the format of state archives; she had been trained in them. And mixed among the town's ordinary records — births, contracts, timber tallies — were documents that no self-governing workshop community should possess: copies of republican requisition orders, correspondence bearing ministry seals, debt instruments signed by officials whose names Coral knew from the coded briefings she had spent years carrying. The Regiment had been collecting the republic's paper against itself for decades. Patiently. Quietly. The way a joiner saves offcuts, never knowing what small thing they might someday build.

Odile appeared beside her, kneeling, sleeves rolled, lifting sodden scroll-cases two at a time. She didn't look at Coral. She spoke as if continuing a conversation they had started years ago.

"You see it now," Odile said. It wasn't a question.

"I see paper," Coral said carefully.

"Mm." Odile stacked the cases, rose, and gave Coral her hand up. Her grip was dry and immensely strong. "You see paper the way a spy sees paper. That's all right. Tonight you saw it the other way too — the way we see it. Paper's just timber, girl. What matters is who holds the roof up." She glanced up at the tarped breach, at the forty exhausted people still moving in the lamplight, and something crossed her face that was older than anger. "Somebody cut that beam knowing it was flawed. Or hoping. We'll find out who. But whoever it was —" her eyes came around, briefly, and rested on Coral with a weight that was not accusation and not quite absolution either, "— they reckoned without the roof-raising. Everyone does, the first time."

Then she was gone, bellowing for hot pitch and more hands, and Coral knelt alone a moment among the rescued books, her heart going hard and strange.

*Somebody cut that beam.*

Or hoped. Or paid. Brann's shutters had been dark all evening — no, not dark. Lit late, she remembered now, lit past midnight two nights running. And Hale had worked all night in plain sight, cheerfully, usefully, a man establishing an alibi with his own strong arms.

The cold clerk in the back of her mind woke up all at once and began, for the first time in weeks, to calculate toward a different destination. Not exits. Accusations. Evidence. A case to be built, quietly, the way the Regiment built everything — joint by joint, peg by peg, out of sight, until the day it bore weight.

* * *

Dawn came grey and still, the storm spent, the world rebuilt overnight into a sculpture of white. The workshop stood. Scarred, tarped, propped on fresh-cut shores, smelling of pitch and disaster — but standing, its archive dry, its fires relit, its benches already crowded by seven o'clock with people drinking barley coffee and arguing cheerfully about the proper angle of the new ridge beam as if the whole thing had been a festival rather than a siege.

Coral stood in the doorway with a mug she wasn't drinking, watching the light come up over the mountains, and realized that she had not thought about the frozen river once all night. Had not measured the ice, or counted the hours of darkness, or priced her own survival against anything at all. The habit wasn't gone — habits like hers didn't die, they only starved — but for one whole night it had gone unfed, and she had not noticed, because her hands had been full.

"There you are," Tamsin said, arriving at her elbow with sawdust in her hair and two mugs instead of one. She pressed the second into Coral's free hand. "You look terrible. Beautiful morning for having nearly died, isn't it?"

"Tamsin." Coral kept her voice low, her eyes on the crowd. "Last night. Before the storm. Did anyone go up to the workshop? Anyone at all?"

Tamsin's grin faded by degrees, the way frost fades off a south-facing pane. "Ferren checked his timber stacks at dusk. Why?"

"And Brann?"

A pause. Snow dripped from the eaves into the silence between them.

"Brann," Tamsin said slowly, "was seen coming down from the workshop yard an hour before the wind changed. By the miller's boy. Who mentioned it this morning, and then went very quiet when certain people looked at him." She turned to face Coral fully, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "You knew. Standing there all night with your sponge and your steady hands — you knew what you were looking at, same as Mother did. So tell me what I'm not supposed to ask you, Coral. Because I'm about to ask it anyway."

Down the street, unhurried, immaculate despite everything, Merrick Hale emerged from the inn and paused in the fresh snow to admire the damaged workshop — admiring it, Coral thought, the way a man admires a job half finished.
