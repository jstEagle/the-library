## 25. Tamsin's Choice

The summons came at dawn, folded under the workshop door in Odile's blunt hand: *Council chamber, midmorning. Come as you are.*

Tamsin read it twice, then a third time by the grey light of the window, and understood that the town had decided something without her — or rather, that it was about to decide, and that her mother wanted her there not as an apprentice but as a daughter of the Regiment, which was a different weight entirely. She dressed quickly, pulled her hair back with the leather cord Coral had given her at midwinter, and went out into streets still crusted with old snow.

She found Coral on the bench before anyone else arrived, sanding the same drawer front she'd been sanding for two days. Her hands moved steadily, but her face was pale as birchwood.

"You know," Tamsin said. It wasn't a question.

"I've known since last night." Coral set down the sanding block and looked at her, and Tamsin saw something she had never seen in those careful grey eyes: fear, yes, but underneath it a kind of terrible readiness, like a woman who has been waiting years for a door to open and is afraid of what stands behind it. "Brann went to your mother after assembly. He told her what he'd found in my room — or what he claims he found."

"And did he?"

Coral almost smiled. "He found exactly what I let him find. The question is whether he also found what I hid better." She turned the drawer front over, ran her thumb along the dovetails. "Tamsin. Whatever happens in that room — whatever they decide about me — I need you to know that none of it changes what this winter was."

"Don't," Tamsin said sharply. "Don't say goodbye before anything's even happened. My mother taught me joinery, not surrender."

"Your mother taught me more than joinery," Coral said quietly. "That's what frightens me."

* * *

The council chamber above the archive smelled of lamp oil and cedar shavings, as it always did, but today it held every member of the Regiment's inner circle: Odile Vant at the head of the long trestle table, the archive-keeper Maren Petch with her ledgers stacked like fortifications, the ferryman Doss with his arms crossed, old Harrow from the smithy, young Pell who kept the granary accounts, and half a dozen others whose faces Tamsin knew as well as her own hands. And at the far end, apart from them all, sat Brann Osker in his good coat, looking like a man who had rehearsed his grief in a mirror.

And Coral stood in the center of the floor, because they would not offer her a chair.

Tamsin took the gallery seat beside the window where apprentices were permitted to sit and be silent, though her mother caught her eye with a look that said *you are here to witness, and witnessing is work.* Below, the lamps threw long shadows across the floorboards, and Coral stood in the middle of them very straight, very still, the courier's stillness that Tamsin had learned to read as anything but calm.

Odile spoke first. "This council has heard Councillor Osker's accusations. Before we weigh them, the accused will speak. That is our way. We hear all sides before we cut any wood."

"Then hear me plainly." Brann rose, spreading his hands in the gesture of a reasonable man burdened with terrible knowledge. "I take no pleasure in this. The woman among us calling herself Coral — seamstress's cousin, widow's niece, whatever name she's worn this winter — is a courier of the Republic of Vessany. An agent. I have seen her cipher materials. I have seen dispatches in code hidden in the lining of her case. She came to this town on business, and her business is not ours, and while we debated our charter like honest folk she sat among us taking notes."

Murmurs ran along the table. Doss the ferryman spat into the spittoon. Old Harrow said nothing, but his knuckles whitened on the table edge.

"She has lied to every one of you," Brann went on, warming now. "To Master Vant, who gave her a bench. To the children, whom she's been teaching — teaching what? Cipher-work? Memory tricks? Do you want spies' craft in our schoolroom? I say she goes downriver when the ice breaks, or not at all, and the republic's agents can fish her out themselves. And worse—" he paused, savoring it, and Tamsin felt her stomach turn, "—worse, I believe she is not merely passing through. I believe she was sent to secure our militia charter by whatever means necessary. I believe some of you have been marked for recruitment. And I believe she has been working one family in particular to gain access to this council."

His eyes came up to the gallery. To Tamsin.

The room turned. Tamsin felt the heat crawl up her neck, felt twenty pairs of eyes settle on her like snowfall, and understood in one cold flash what Brann was doing: he could not prove his case against Coral alone, so he would make it against the both of them, and let the town's love for its own daughters do the cutting.

"Tamsin." Odile's voice was quiet. "You have something to say. I can see it in your jaw."

Her mother knew. Of course her mother knew — had known since the night by the kiln, since the confession Tamsin had carried home like a live coal and set down on the kitchen table between two cups of cider. Odile had listened without interrupting, asked three questions, and then said only, *Then tomorrow you'll tell them yourself. A daughter of mine doesn't hide behind my coat.* But that had been days ago, before Brann moved, before the accusation hardened into an agenda item.

Tamsin stood. Her legs felt strange, too light, as if the floor had gone soft. Fifteen winters of watching Coral perform harmlessness had taught her how invisibility worked; standing here now, she understood it from the other side, the way timber understands the saw.

"Coral Okonkwo is a courier of Vessany," she said. "That part is true."

The murmur that went around the table was ugly, and she talked through it.

"But before you vote, you should hear all of what's true, because Brann Osker has given you half a truth and dressed it up as courage. Yes, she came here carrying coded dispatches. Yes, she lied about who she was — because that is what the Republic trained her to do from the time she was younger than me. Because the man who raised her taught her that the only safe courier is a forgotten one. She has spent fifteen years being nobody so that people like him—" she pointed at Brann, and her hand shook, and she let it, "—people like the ones who sent her, could use her up and never have to see her face."

"Sentiment," Brann said. "We're discussing the safety of the Regiment."

"We're discussing the truth of it," Tamsin shot back. "You want the rest? Here's the rest. The dispatches she carries don't just buy our charter. If we refuse, the second message orders the Republic to declare us in rebellion and leave us to the enemy's army. And the third—" her voice caught, and she steadied it, "—the third orders the workshop burned, and the blame laid on the enemy, so that no record of our independence survives the war. She decoded all of it. Alone, at night, terrified. And instead of delivering it, instead of doing her duty and saving her own skin, she brought it to my mother. She chose us over them before any of you knew she had a choice to make."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the ears. Somewhere below, the workshop's great waterwheel turned in its race with a sound like slow breathing.

"That's a grave charge against the Republic," said Maren Petch slowly, "and a graver one against this woman's honesty. How do we verify any of it?"

"You don't take my word," Tamsin said. "Or hers. You take the evidence. Ask her to show you the dispatches. Ask her to decode them in front of you. Ask her why Merrick Hale has spent all winter circling this town like a hawk over a henhouse — ask him, if he'll answer honestly, who sent him and what his instructions were. Brann wants you to believe Coral is the danger. I want you to ask who benefits from you believing it. Because I've watched Brann all winter too. I've seen who he dines with. I've seen whose promises make him smile."

Brann came up out of his chair. "You impertinent—"

"Sit down, Brann." Odile hadn't raised her voice. She almost never did. "My daughter has the floor. In this hall, in this town, everyone has the floor. That is the whole point of us." She looked up at the gallery. "Is there more, Tamsin?"

There was. There was the whole of it — the kiln, the confessions traded back and forth like currency, the nights Coral had sat shaking after decoding the third dispatch, the morning after the storm when Coral had worked until her hands bled rebuilding the roof and had stopped, somewhere in that long night, calculating exits. Tamsin could have said all of it. Instead she said the truest thing, the one that mattered:

"Fifteen years ago, somebody taught Coral that the way to survive was to be forgettable. To pass through towns like weather. And it worked — she survived things none of us can imagine. But surviving isn't living, and I think she'd forgotten the difference. Then she got trapped here by a broken bridge and a stubborn ferryman, and we put a plane in her hand and teased her about her terrible dovetails, and something happened that nobody planned." Tamsin swallowed. "She became somebody to us. And she became somebody to herself. And if you send her away — if you hand her to Hale or turn her out onto the ice road — then Brann wins, and the Republic wins, and everything this town says it believes is just words we carve on the meetinghouse door and never mean."

She stopped, breath ragged, and realized her fists were clenched and her eyes stinging and that she had, without meaning to, stepped halfway down the gallery stairs.

Old Harrow broke the silence first. He was the oldest man in the room, a smith with forearms like rootstock, and he spoke the way he forged: slow, and once. "Master Vant. You've had this woman at your bench since the first snows. Your judgment carries weight here. Say it."

Every head turned to Odile.

Tamsin watched her mother — really watched, the way Coral had taught her to watch, reading the small tells. Odile's hands lay flat on the table, scarred from forty years of chisels. She looked at Coral for a long moment, and something passed between the two women that Tamsin couldn't parse, some accounting older than this morning.

"The Regiment keeps books," Odile said at last. "Debts and credits, labor and grain, what we owe and what we're owed. That's how we know what we are. So let me enter this into the record." She drew a breath. "Coral Okonkwo came to us a stranger and a liar, and I will not pretend otherwise, because pretending otherwise would insult this council. She also mended the roof trusses over the finishing room in a gale that would have killed a careless woman. She has fed the kiln, split the stock, swept the floors, and taught my daughter to read a grain line properly — which I confess I'd failed to do. When the Republic ordered this town destroyed, she burned nothing and warned everyone. That is the ledger as I know it. Debits entered honestly, credits entered honestly." She looked around the table. "In this Regiment, that ledger is the only citizenship paper there has ever been. I move we judge her by it."

"Seconded," said Doss the ferryman, to Tamsin's astonishment — Doss, who trusted the river and nothing else. "Woman paid her crossing debt twice over helping me patch the ferry hull. Good enough for me."

"Thirded," said Maren Petch, already reaching for a pen.

Brann stood again, white-faced. "You'd take the word of a spy over a sworn councillor—"

"I'd take the word of a woman's deeds over a man's ambition," said Harrow, "any day the sun rises. Sit down, Brann. Or stand outside. Your choice."

It didn't end cleanly — Tamsin hadn't expected it to. There were questions, hard ones, put to Coral directly, and Coral answered them standing, in a low level voice, laying out the dispatches and their decodings with the precision of someone presenting a bill of materials. She named Hale. She named the purge in the capital. She named, when Maren pressed her, the towns she had passed through and left uninformed, and her voice did not waver, but Tamsin saw her hands, hanging at her sides, close slowly into fists and stay there. The vote, when Odile finally called it, was not unanimous. It didn't need to be. Nine for, four against, one abstention. Coral Okonkwo would remain in Tamsin's Ford under the Regiment's protection and its law, subject to its books like anyone else.

Brann left first, coat swinging, door slamming hard enough to shake dust from the rafters. Nobody hurried after him.

* * *

Afterward the chamber emptied slowly, members gathering their papers, murmuring in knots. Tamsin stayed on the stairs, suddenly unsure whether she was wanted, whether she had overstepped, whether the fire in her chest was triumph or shame. Her mother squeezed her shoulder in passing — *well done* compressed into five pounds of pressure — and went to argue ration arithmetic with Pell.

Which left Coral, standing by the window where the light came through, looking out at the frozen river as if she'd forgotten the room had emptied.

Tamsin came down the last steps and stood beside her. For a while neither of them spoke. Outside, two boys were sliding on the millpond, their shrieks arriving thin through the glass.

"You didn't have to do that," Coral said finally. "Stand up in front of everyone. Spend yourself like that."

"Yes, I did." Tamsin studied the frost ferns on the pane. "You'd have done it for me. You did do it for me, all winter, every time you swallowed what you knew to keep us out of it. Besides—" she nudged Coral's elbow, "—someone in this town has to be able to give a speech. Mother saves hers for the important stuff."

A sound escaped Coral then — half laugh, half something else — and she pressed the heel of her hand briefly against her mouth, and Tamsin saw it happening and did not look away, because she understood that looking away was the old habit, the Republic's habit, the forgetting-her habit, and that the whole point of today was that this town did not do that.

Coral cried the way she must have learned to cry as a child: silently, economically, tears running straight off her jaw as though her body had never been allowed the luxury of sobbing. Fifteen years, Sef's letter had said. Fifteen years of practiced silence. Tamsin reached over and took her hand — the right one, the one with the fresh glue stains and the burn scar from the kiln and the faded chain-mark still faint on the wrist — and held it, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the hand-hold didn't say better.

"I thought," Coral managed at last, "for so long — that if anyone ever truly saw me, it would be the end of me. That being known was the same as being caught."

"And now?"

Coral looked around the emptying chamber — at the trestle table scarred by a century of debates, at Maren's ledgers, at the meetinghouse rules carved into the beam overhead: *ALL HAVE THE FLOOR. THE BOOKS ARE OPEN. NO ONE STANDS ALONE.* She looked a long time.

"Now I think being caught might be the best thing that ever happened to me."

They walked down together into the cold, and the town received them as though nothing extraordinary had happened, which was its own kind of grace — Doss nodding, Harrow's wife pressing a heel of bread into Coral's hands, children swarming Tamsin with demands about the cipher lessons. Ordinary life closing over the day like water over a thrown stone.

It was nearly dusk when Maren Petch found them at the workshop, breathless from the climb, a paper in her fist and a strange expression on her face.

"Coral," the archive-keeper said. "There's a rider at the south watch — came down off the pass before dark, half-frozen, refusing to warm himself until he delivered this. It's addressed to the courier of Vessany. By name." She held it out. "Sealed with the war office stamp. And Coral — the wax was broken and resealed before it ever reached us. Someone read this on the road."

Coral took the letter. In the lamplight her face went carefully, professionally blank — but Tamsin had spent a winter learning to read past that blankness, and what she saw beneath it frightened her more than Brann ever had.
