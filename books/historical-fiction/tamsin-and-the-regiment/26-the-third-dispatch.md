## 26. The Third Dispatch

The snow came down like a verdict all that day, thick and silent, erasing the road, the fences, the frozen river, until Tamsin's Ford seemed less a town than an idea the mountains were considering. Coral watched it from the window of the seamstress's back room — her room now, in the way of borrowed things that had quietly become one's own — and felt the peculiar calm that always preceded her worst work.

She had decoded a hundred messages in her life. She knew the ritual the way a joiner knew his chisels: the candle trimmed low, the door barred, the case opened with the little brass key that lived sewn into her cuff, the cipher sheets laid out in their careful order. She knew the ritual, and she knew, too, the strange suspension that came with it, that hour when she was no one at all — not the seamstress's cousin, not the apprentice who planed boards badly, not the woman Tamsin teased about her glue-sticky fingers. Only hands and letters. Only the work.

But tonight her hands were not steady.

She told herself it was the cold. The room's little stove had burned down to embers, and the window glass wore a skin of frost that turned the lamplight into a halo. She fed the stove a split of birch and watched the flame catch, and told herself again: *the cold, only the cold.*

The third dispatch had been in the case since the pass. It was the one she had saved for last, not out of any discipline — discipline would have meant reading them in order, understanding the whole design at once — but out of a fear she had not permitted herself to name. The first dispatch had been bad enough: the purchase of the militia charter, the town's men and its muskets folded into the republic's dying war like coins into a beggar's cup. The second had been worse: the threat dressed as a contingency, the town declared in rebellion if it refused, left open to the enemy's mercy like a door left open to the winter. She had carried those two messages through the pass and across the ferry and through all the long weeks of Hale's hunting, and she had told herself that she was only a courier, that she carried what others wrote, that the writing was not hers.

But the third dispatch she had not opened, and a courier who does not open a message is no longer a courier. She was a woman hiding a letter under her floorboards and calling the hiding a duty.

Tonight the hiding was over. She could feel it the way Odile could feel, so it was said, a flaw in a board through three thicknesses of oak — not seeing it, knowing it. The vote at Midwinter Assembly had been deferred. Hale had gone quiet in a way that was louder than noise. Brann's face had acquired the shine of a man who believes his fortune is approaching from downriver. And the republic, if Sef's letter was to be believed — and Sef had never been wrong about the capital, only about his own safety — was a corpse that did not yet know it, its agents still moving on old instructions like clockwork in a dead man's house.

Old instructions. That was the phrase that had woken her before dawn, that had brought her here with the candle and the cipher sheets. Clockwork in a dead man's house. What, she had asked the dark ceiling, was the third instruction?

She unrolled the dispatch on the table and weighted its corners with the flatiron, the spool of thread, the little carved horse Tamsin had made her — badly, lovingly — in her first month at the bench. The paper was good rag stock, watermarked with the seal of the Chancellery of War, and it had traveled folded eight ways inside a lead sleeve so that no fire or flood or curious eye could reach it before she did. She had carried it against her ribs for four months. She had slept with it. It was the closest thing to a child she had ever borne, and she had begun, without noticing, to love it the way one loves a burden — not despite the weight but because the weight had become familiar.

The cipher was the old field cipher, the one Sef had taught her in the attic room above the chandler's shop fifteen years ago, the one with the rotating keyword and the null-letters scattered like gravel through sense. *Never be remembered,* Sef had said, his hands over hers on the cipher sheet, smelling of tallow and tobacco. *The message matters. The messenger is a key, and a key is thrown away after the lock turns.* She had been thirteen, and she had thought it was wisdom.

She set the keyword — it changed monthly, and December's word had come to her in the second dispatch, buried in a null-string where only a reader who knew to look would find it: *VANTHOLM*, a place she had never heard of, chosen, no doubt, by some clerk who liked the sound of mountains — and began.

The first lines came slowly, the way they always did, nonsense resolving by degrees into sense, gravel into road.

*TO THE BEARER. THIRD AND FINAL INSTRUCTION. TO BE EXECUTED UPON FAILURE OF FIRST AND SECOND.*

Her breath fogged the paper. She waited for it to clear.

*FAILURE OF FIRST AND SECOND IS NOW ASSUMED. THE CHARTER WILL NOT BE SOLD. THE TOWN WILL NOT BE TURNED. THEREFORE THE FOLLOWING.*

She wrote the decoded words in her own hand on a sheet of the seamstress's plain wrapping paper, because the ritual required it, because the ritual was the only shape her life had had for fifteen years, and because her hands needed something to do that was not shaking.

*THE REGIMENT'S CLAIM UPON THE FOUNDRY LEASES RESTS UPON THE WORKSHOP RECORDS AND THE COUNCIL LEDGER. DESTROY BOTH. FIRE IS THE MEANS. THE FIRE IS TO BE LAID IN THE WINTER MONTHS WHEN THE TOWN IS CONFINED AND CANNOT REBUILD. ATTRIBUTION IS TO BE GIVEN TO ENEMY AGENTS. TWO BODIES WILL SUFFICE. THE COURIER'S OWN IS ACCEPTABLE.*

The candle guttered. Outside, the snow went on falling with its terrible patience.

Coral read it again. Then again, a fourth time, because the mind does this, the mind refuses, the mind goes back to the door it has locked and tries the handle and tries the handle and tries the handle.

*Two bodies will suffice. The courier's own is acceptable.*

She laughed. It came out of her like something breaking, one short bark of sound in the frozen room, and she clapped her hand over her mouth as though the town might hear, as though the town were listening for her, as though she had not spent fifteen years making herself the one person no one listened for.

*The courier's own is acceptable.*

She had been the contingency. She had always been the contingency. All the way across the pass, all the way down the ferry, all the weeks of planing boards and mending seams and learning the names of the joiners' children — she had been a fuse, walking, waiting to be lit. And the men who had written this, the clerks with their watermarked rag stock and their rotating keywords, had never once thought of her as a person. She had known that. She had *built* her life on knowing that. Never be remembered. The messenger is thrown away after the lock turns.

She had simply never understood, until this moment, in this cold room, with this paper, that the throwing away could be so literal, so administrative, so *brief*. Two bodies will suffice. Not *the courier will be regretted*. Not even her name. The dispatch did not contain her name. It said *the bearer*. She had carried it four thousand miles and it had never once said her name.

* * *

She did not remember lighting the second candle, but she must have, because there were two now, and the decoded sheet lay complete before her, and the frost on the window had thickened until the room might have been underwater, or inside a jewel.

She thought of Sef.

She thought of the last line of his letter, the one that had taken half a year to reach her because the capital's post had died with everything else: *Trust no handler, little wren. The work eats the workers first and the masters never.* She had read it in this same room, in November, and had wept — no, she had not wept; she had sat very still until the feeling passed, the way she had been taught, the way she had taught herself, and the feeling had not passed, it had only gone deeper down where she could not reach it, and it was down there now, rising.

Sef had died in the purge. Shot, the letter's writer said, or perhaps not shot — the writer was not certain, the writer had seen only the empty room and the confiscated papers. And the papers Sef had kept, the letter's writer said, included a file marked with her own designation, her courier's number, the string of digits that had been her name in the Chancellery's books for fifteen years. What was in the file, the writer could not say.

She could say now. She could say what was in the file. It was this dispatch, or one like it. It was the standing order. It was the clause that made her expendable, written down in some clerk's careful hand while she was still a girl learning ciphers in a chandler's attic, before she had ever carried anything anywhere. They had written her death before they had written her life. The work eats the workers first.

And the town.

She made herself think of the town, because the horror of her own expendability was, she understood with a cold clarity that felt almost like relief, the smaller horror. The workshop. She could see it from this window if the frost were gone — the long roof with its green shingles, the smoke rising from the glue-house, the sound that came out of it all day, the sound she had not known she loved until she feared for it: the plane's long shhh, the saw's patient teeth, the low talk of people working together at a thing too large for any one of them. Odile at the big bench, straight-backed at sixty, her chisels sharper than any argument. Tamsin with sawdust in her dark hair, laughing at Coral's crooked dovetails with a cruelty that was entirely kindness.

Two hundred and forty souls, the census said. The council ledger said more than that. The ledger said what the census could not: the debts, the obligations, the *claims* — the republic's own signature on leases and loans and promises going back ninety years, the paper by which the Regiment held its foundry rights against every duke and syndicate downriver. That was what the third dispatch was for. Not the people. The paper. Burn the workshop, burn the records, and the town's claim burned with it, and some syndicate in the capital — some clerk's cousin, some minister's brother-in-law — would take the foundry leases for a song from the ashes, and no one downriver would ever know the fire had been set from within.

*Attribution is to be given to enemy agents.*

She had done this before. That was the thing she had to make herself look at, the thing the room's cold would not let her turn from. Not arson — she had never set a fire in her life. But abandonment. The town she had left, in her ninth year of service — she had decoded a message there too, a message that named names, and she had delivered it as instructed because delivering was all she was, and the arrests had come in the night, and she had been on the morning coach before the doors were broken, and she had told herself for eleven years that she had had no choice. She had told it so well that she had almost believed it. The message had named names. She had carried it. She had not known what the names would cost.

But she had known what *this* would cost. She had known since the second dispatch, since the night she read *declared in rebellion* and understood that the town's refusal would be its death warrant, and she had gone on hiding the papers under her floorboards and planing her crooked boards and saying nothing. Every day of silence had been a choice. Every day she had chosen Tamsin's laughter over the town's safety, or told herself she was choosing caution, or told herself she was waiting for a better moment, and the better moment had never come, because there was no better moment, there was only the moment, and it was this one, and it had been this one for weeks.

The snow ticked against the window. The stove ticked as it cooled. Somewhere below, the seamstress's cat turned over in its sleep.

Coral Okonkwo sat in the cold room with her two candles and her dead republic's handwriting, and she understood, finally, completely, the shape of the thing she had carried across the mountains. She had thought she was carrying an alliance. Then she had thought she was carrying a threat. She had told herself in her darkest hours that she was carrying a bargain she might soften, a sentence she might commute. She had not let herself know that she was carrying a torch. The mission had been arson all along — not the charter, not the threat, not even the abandonment. Fire. Fire in the winter months when the town is confined and cannot rebuild. Fire laid by the bearer, who is then to be one of the bodies that proves the enemy's guilt.

They had not sent her to buy the town. They had sent her to burn it and be burned with it, and the second dispatch, the charter offer, had been nothing but the courtesy of an alternative — the lock offered before the key was broken off, so that the town's refusal would be its own doing, so that the republic's hands, in the histories that would never be written, would be clean.

* * *

What she did then was not the ritual.

The ritual would have been to memorize the decoded text, burn the paper and the dispatch both in the stove, scatter the ash, sleep, and report to her handler by the January dead-drop. The ritual would have been to begin, tonight, planning the fire — the glue-house first, where the spirits were stored; the records room second, where Odile's ledger lived behind its green door; the timing, the attribution, the two bodies. The ritual was fifteen years deep in her, deeper than language, deeper than grief. She could feel it pulling at her like the current under the river ice, smooth on top, and she understood that she was standing on it, and she understood that the ice was thin.

She picked up the flatiron instead.

Not to destroy the dispatch. She set the flatiron down again at once, her hand trembling — not with cold now, with the other thing, the thing rising from where she had sunk it. She looked at the decoded sheet, and at the dispatch beneath it, and she thought: *these papers are the last true thing the republic ever said. Everything else is dead men's clockwork. This is the living order. This is the hand still on the town's throat.*

And a hand on a throat can be turned.

She thought of Odile's ledger, the secret ledger, the one the archive-keeper had shown her by lamplight with the door barred: ninety years of the republic's own signatures, its debts, its leases, its broken promises — the town's leverage, and hers. She thought of the cipher sheets in the case, and of the Chancellery's formats, and of the dead-drop schedule still current in her memory, and of the phrase every courier learned in her first year and was made to repeat: *a message is believed by its form before its content.* She thought of all of it, and the thought assembled itself in her not as a plan, not yet, but as a direction — the way a board shows the joiner its grain, and the joiner knows, before any cut is made, what the board will become.

It could be forged. The third dispatch could be answered. A counter-order, in the republic's own forms, under the republic's own seals — *the town is to be preserved, the workshop is to be requisitioned as a supply depot, the bearer is to be protected* — and Hale, who was a bureaucrat of the republic down to his polished boots, who had come all this way on the strength of paper and would be undone by paper, might be made to obey the very machine he served.

But not tonight. Tonight she was too cold, too shaken, and above all too alone, and a forgery built by one frightened woman in a frozen room would be a forgery built on sand. It would need Odile's seals and the archive-keeper's hand and the town's own knowledge of what was being done to it. It would need, God help her, confession. It would need her to walk into the council room with the whole rotten truth in her arms — the case, the codes, the third dispatch, the clause about the two bodies, the eleven years of the town she had abandoned — and set it down on Odile's bench and say: *this is what I carried. This is what I am. Judge me.*

Never be remembered. She had lived by it for fifteen years, and it had kept her alive, and it had made her the perfect instrument for this, and Sef had died for it, and the town she had abandoned had died for it, and now Tamsin's Ford was to die for it, and the rule had never once, in all its long service, kept anyone safe but the men who wrote the orders.

She was done with it.

Coral folded the decoded sheet small and hid it in the false seam of her sewing basket, where the seamstress's cousin kept her thread. The dispatch itself she returned to its lead sleeve — not burned, not yet; it was evidence, it was leverage, it was the republic's own signature on the crime — and the case went back under the floorboard, and the cipher sheets went back into their order, and she trimmed the candles and sat for a long moment in the small twin flames with her hands flat on the table as if steadying something.

Then she heard, through the frost-thick window, through the snow-silenced street, a sound she knew: footsteps, two sets, unhurried, coming up the lane from the direction of the workshop. And under the footsteps, faint, a voice — Tamsin's voice, low, arguing about something, and a second voice answering, older, dry, unhurried: Odile.

They were coming here. At this hour, through this snow.

Coral stood up so quickly her chair went over behind her, and in the doubled candlelight she saw her own face reflected faint and ghostly in the black glass, and behind the ghost of her face, coming up the lane through the falling snow, two lanterns, and her name — her real name, the one she had not spoken aloud in fifteen years — rising in her throat like a confession already half out of her mouth.
