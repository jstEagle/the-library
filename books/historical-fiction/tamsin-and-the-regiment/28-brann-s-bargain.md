## 28. Brann's Bargain

Tamsin knew something was wrong before anyone told her. That was the way of small towns: the air itself changed when a man broke faith with his neighbors, and the change arrived ahead of the news like cold ahead of a storm front.

She was at her bench when it happened — or rather, she noticed the absence of what should have been happening. The workshop had its rhythms, and one of them was Brann's morning passage through the yard, his boots on the gravel path from his house to the council hall, his greeting called over the fence to whoever happened to be working outside. For twenty years that greeting had come without fail, a little too loud, a little too hearty, the greeting of a man who wanted very much to be liked and never quite trusted that he was.

This morning the path stayed empty.

"He'll not come," Odile said behind her. Tamsin hadn't heard her mother approach; she rarely did. Odile moved through the workshop the way weather moves through a valley, present everywhere at once, announcing herself only when she chose to. "I sent Rennick past his house an hour ago. Shutters drawn. His wife is packing."

"Packing for where? The river's frozen solid. There's no road out."

"Downriver, then. On the ice." Odile set a plane down on the bench with a soft click, precise as punctuation. "People do desperate arithmetic in winter. A frozen river starts to look like a road if you hate your neighbors enough."

Tamsin wiped her hands on her apron. Sawdust clung to the wool in pale streaks, and she found herself staring at it instead of at her mother, because looking at Odile directly just now felt dangerous — as if her mother's face might show her something Tamsin wasn't ready to know. "It's because of last night. Because of Coral."

"It's because of Brann." Odile said it flatly, without heat, which was worse somehow. Her mother reserved her anger the way other joiners reserved their best stock — carefully, for pieces worth the spending. "What he did with his own hands, he did. Coral only showed us the shape of it. Don't you go confusing those things, Tamsin. I've watched people do that their whole lives, and it's how towns rot."

"I'm not confused." But her voice came out smaller than she meant it to, and she hated that. Because here was the thing she couldn't say, the thing sitting in her chest like a splinter driven deep: *I brought him down.* She had caught him copying the charter rolls by candlelight, had held the evidence in her hands, had chosen — after two sleepless nights of turning it over — to tell the council everything, Coral's secrets along with Brann's. And now a neighbor's house stood shuttered, a neighbor's wife wept into packing crates, and a neighbor's son would grow up in a town where men pointed at his father's window and lowered their voices. Tamsin had pulled a thread, and the whole coat was coming apart, and she could not yet see whether anything worth wearing would be left when it finished unraveling.

"You are, though." Odile picked up the plane again and began smoothing a board, long even strokes that filled the workshop with a sound like breathing. "You're confused about whether you did right. You did right. Whether it feels like it is a different question entirely, and no one in this town will promise you it ever will."

* * *

Brann went to the ferry landing at dusk.

Tamsin learned this from Doss, the ferryman's boy, who came pelting up through the snow to the workshop with his hat half off and his breath smoking, bursting in the door without knocking, which he'd have been thrashed for in any season but this one. "He's there," he gasped. "Master Brann. He's got a lantern and a sledge, and he's talking to the river-man, and my da says—" He stopped, gulped air. "My da says there's another man come up onto the ice from the south side. Walking careful. And Master Brann went out to meet him."

The workshop went quiet around them. Not silent — the Regiment was never silent; somewhere a lathe was always turning, someone always planing, the town breathed wood-dust and industry the way other towns breathed smoke — but the quality of the noise changed, drew tight, the way a drumhead draws tight before it splits.

Coral was at the far bench. Tamsin saw her go still, utterly still, the stillness of an animal that has heard a sound humans can't hear yet. Then she set down her work, untied her apron, folded it over the bench rail with a neatness that looked almost ceremonial, and said, quietly, to no one and everyone: "Hale."

"You're sure?" Odile asked.

"He walked out onto a frozen river in winter to meet a man who fled his own town in disgrace." Coral's voice was level, but Tamsin, who had spent months learning to read her — learning the tiny tells, the flicker of the eyelid, the pause before certain words — heard what the town couldn't. Fear. Old fear, worn smooth as a river stone with handling. "That's not a meeting. That's a rendezvous planned before the council ever saw my dispatches. Before last night. Maybe before I ever decoded them." She turned to Odile. "He's known for days. Weeks. Everything I've done since the confession at the kiln, he's known, and he's been waiting to see how much of it reached the council."

"And now he knows all of it," Odile said.

"Now he knows all of it."

Tamsin felt the floor tilt under her, or imagined she did. "Then we stop them. Now. Tonight. We take lanterns, we go out on the ice—"

"With what?" Coral asked, and there was no cruelty in it, only the terrible patience of someone who had spent fifteen years watching plans die. "Half the town on river ice after dark? Hale would hear us coming from fifty yards. And if we take him — what then, Tamsin? Tie him in the root cellar until spring? He has handlers of his own. When he stops reporting, they send more than one man next time. We don't win by catching him. We win by making sure that when he reports, what he reports costs the republic more than the town is worth."

"Then what *do* we do?"

"We let him think he's won tonight." Coral was already moving, pulling her coat from its peg, and there was something in her face Tamsin had never seen there before — not the courier's blankness, not the apprentice's careful newness, but a third thing, hard and bright, like frost forming on a window. "And tomorrow we start building the lie that will keep everyone in this room alive."

* * *

They gathered in the council hall after dark, lamplight and woodsmoke and the low murmur of frightened people trying not to sound frightened. The archive-keeper came, old Maren with her ring of keys and her ink-stained fingers. The millwright came, and the smith, and the women who kept the granary books, and half a dozen others whose names Tamsin had grown up hearing spoken with the easy familiarity of a town that governed itself and had forgotten, mostly, that this was remarkable.

Odile laid it out plainly, because plainness was the town's armor and always had been. "The republic means to burn the workshop and blame the enemy army. That's what Coral's dispatches say. Brann has carried our charter rolls and our council minutes to Merrick Hale, and Hale now knows we know. So they'll move early. The question before this room is what 'early' buys them and what it costs us."

"They can't burn the workshop," the smith said. "Stone walls. Slate roof since the rebuild."

"The roof's timber under the slate," the millwright said grimly. "And the kilns, and the drying sheds, and the varnish stores. One fire in the wrong place and the whole yard goes. They wouldn't need to burn the building. They'd need to burn one barrel."

Silence. Tamsin watched the faces around the table and understood, with a lurch, what Coral had tried to teach her without teaching her: that power didn't live in crowns or armies. It lived in rooms like this one, in the space between a question and an answer, in who spoke first and who was believed. And right now the room was afraid, and fear made people stupid, and stupid people burned the wrong things.

Then Maren, the archive-keeper, spoke. "How long do we have?"

Coral answered. "If they move up the timetable — and they will, because Brann's flight told them the town is compromised — then days. Perhaps a week. Perhaps less. They'll want it done before the thaw brings traffic on the river, while every death and every fire can be blamed on winter and war."

"A week." Maren's keys chimed softly as she folded her hands. "Then we'd best get to work."

* * *

The work began that same night, and it was the strangest week of Tamsin's life.

By day, the town performed normalcy the way Coral once performed harmlessness. The lathes turned. The children went to their letters. Brann's shuttered house was left alone — that had been Odile's order, firm as iron: *no one touches his property, no one speaks against his family, and his wife and boy eat from the common stores same as anyone, because the sin belongs to the sinner and no one else.* Tamsin had seen Brann's wife at the granary, hollow-eyed, carrying her ration sack like something that might break, and she had watched the other women of the town nod to her, speak to her, ask after the boy — ordinary kindness, practiced deliberately, a wall built brick by brick against the poison of suspicion. It was the most political thing Tamsin had ever witnessed, and none of it was said aloud.

By night, the real work. Maren opened the archives and spread the republic's own documents across the great table, and Coral bent over them with the lamp turned low, and Tamsin sat watch at the window and marveled at what her friend became in that light. The hesitancy fell away. The careful, half-apologetic apprentice vanished. What remained was someone quick and ruthless and exact — a woman who read a ciphered dispatch the way Odile read grain in a board, seeing the structure underneath, knowing where to cut.

The forgery was Coral's idea and Coral's craft, but it needed the town's hands, and this was the part that astonished Tamsin most: how *good* the Regiment was at this. Maren had forty years of genuine republican correspondence filed and cross-referenced — requisition orders, depot designations, supply schedules. The paper was right. The seals were right, pressed from actual documents softened and re-struck. The ink was ground to match the fading of a letter months old. Even the phrasing — bureaucratic, faintly impatient, the voice of a ministry that regarded human beings as line items — Coral reproduced so faithfully that Tamsin felt a chill reading it. *Designate Tamsin's Ford a protected provisioning station. Militia rolls to be preserved intact. No action to be taken against works or personnel.* A lie built out of true parts, load-bearing in every joint.

"It's good joinery," Odile said the third night, studying the forged dispatch by lamplight, and there was something complicated in her voice — admiration and distaste sharing a bench. "You could have been one of us years ago, Coral Okonkwo."

"That's what frightens me," Coral said, and went back to her work.

* * *

On the fifth night, Tamsin took the watch alone, and the river spoke to her.

It was a sound she'd known all her life without listening to it — the frozen Vessany groaning in the cold, a long low complaint that traveled through the ice and up through the ground and into the bones of the houses nearest the bank. Her mother said the old-timers claimed you could read the river in winter like a ledger: which stretches were thick, which were thin, where the current ran restless under the white. Standing at the dark window with her breath fogging the glass, Tamsin thought about Brann somewhere downriver or down-road, warm in some safehouse paid for with copies of her neighbors' secrets, and about Hale, wherever Hale slept, and about how spring — which had always been simply the thing that came after winter, reliable as morning — had become a fortress wall they had to hold until it arrived.

Spring felt impossibly far away. Eleven weeks, maybe twelve, if the thaw came late, and the thaw always came late in the high country. Twelve weeks of pretending. Twelve weeks of a town holding one breath.

Footsteps on the stair. She didn't reach for the poker by the door anymore; she'd stopped doing that sometime around the fourth night, and only realized afterward what it meant — that she had stopped expecting the danger to be strangers. Coral came into the room carrying two mugs of the barley tea the town drank when there was nothing better, and handed one over, and stood beside her at the window without a word for a long time.

"He's out there," Tamsin said finally. "Hale. Somewhere past the tree line, in some cabin, waiting. And we're in here drinking tea."

"We're in here winning," Coral said. "It looks like tea."

Tamsin laughed — a short, startled bark of a laugh, surprised out of her — and Coral almost smiled, which from Coral was practically a festival. Then the smile faded and her eyes went back to the window, to the black line of the river, and Tamsin watched her face do the thing it did when she was calculating and couldn't help it, the courier surfacing through the joiner's apprentice like a fish rising under ice.

"What is it?"

"The timetable." Coral's voice dropped. "Everything I've forged, everything we've staged — it all assumes Hale sends word downriver and waits for confirmation. Waits for the bureaucracy. That was always his weakness; Sef used to say the republic could lose any battle but never an audit. But Brann changes that. Brann is local. Brann knows the drying sheds, knows where the varnish stores sit, knows which night the watch is thinnest." She turned from the window, and in the dark her eyes were very steady. "If Brann convinces him they can do it themselves — pitch, torch, a wind from the south — they won't wait for permission. And they won't wait for spring."

"How long?"

"If it were me?" Coral said. "Tomorrow."

* * *

It came the night after next.

Tamsin would remember afterward the ordinary shape of the evening that preceded it — supper of barley and salt pork, her mother checking the joints on a commissioned cradle with a candle held close to the wood, the small domestic sounds of a house settling into sleep. She was upstairs, half asleep, when the bell began.

Not the workshop bell. The *alarm* — the old bell in the council tower that had rung for floods and fires and the one bad winter thirty years ago when the pass collapsed and the town nearly starved. It rang in a broken, urgent pattern that every child in Tamsin's Ford learned before they learned their letters, and it went through the town like a blade through green wood.

She was down the stairs and out the door before she'd fully woken, coat over her nightdress, boots half-laced, and the street was already filling with people — lanterns blooming door by door like flowers opening in fast-forward, the smith with a hammer already in his fist, the millwright's daughters dragging buckets toward the cisterns. And above the rooftops, above the black teeth of the ridge to the south, the sky had begun to glow.

No. Not the sky. The tree line. Someone had fired the southern woods — dry brush, deadfall, a winter's worth of tinder stacked by nature and touched off by hand — and the wind, the south wind, was pushing it toward the town.

"There!" someone shouted from near the granary, and Tamsin turned, and saw them: three figures moving fast along the shadow of the drying sheds, low and quick, and one of them carried something that caught the lantern light with a wet, glistening shine.

Pitch. A whole keg of it.

And cutting across the yard from the workshop, apron flying, moving with a speed Tamsin had never once seen her use — Coral, with Odile and half the night watch fanning out behind her, herding the figures away from the varnish stores, away from the kilns, away from everything that mattered, toward the one corner of the yard where the town had been ready for five days and where, Tamsin knew, twenty of her neighbors stood waiting in the dark with ropes and staves and not one weapon among them meant to kill.

The bell kept ringing. The woods kept burning. And Tamsin ran into the yard after them, her heart hammering a single thought over and over like a hammer on a nail:

*Hold the line. Hold the town. Spring is coming — spring is coming — spring—*

The first torch hit the ground ten yards from the drying shed, and the night lit orange.
