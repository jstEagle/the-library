## 29. The Forgery

The archive room sat beneath the council hall like a held breath, a low stone chamber where the town kept everything it had ever decided to remember. Coral had been in it twice before — once to deliver a message she'd never delivered, once to steal an hour with the older ledgers — but never at night, never with the lamps turned up and three people bent over a table as if over a dying man.

The archive-keeper was called Maren Ostrow, and she was older than the bridge, older perhaps than the millrace, a small woman with ink-stained knuckles and eyes that had read so many documents they no longer bothered to see anything else. She had come down from her rooms above the hall without being asked why. She had listened to Coral's confession the night before with the same expression she gave a misdated receipt: patient, faintly disappointed in whoever had made the error, and entirely prepared to correct it.

"You'll want the good paper," Maren said now, unlocking a cabinet that smelled of tallow and cold iron. "Not the republic's stock. We don't have any, and if we did, I wouldn't give it to you."

"The paper matters," Coral said. "More than the ink. Hale will have handled a hundred dispatches. His hands will know the weight before his eyes know the words."

"Then his hands are the problem." Maren laid out sheets on the table one by one, turning each to catch the lamplight. "This is Vessany chancery stock — seized from a tax collector's strongbox eleven years ago, when he tried to buy the ferry charter with bribes instead of coin. We kept everything. We keep everything." She tapped a heavy cream-colored sheet. "Watermark's a running stag. That was the old directorate seal, before the war office took over procurement. If your dispatches came through a field channel, this is what they'd be written on."

Coral picked up the sheet and felt her stomach tighten with something between fear and recognition. It was right. The weight, the tooth of it under her thumb, the faint ribbing where the mold had pressed — it was right the way a voice is right when you hear it across a crowded room and know it instantly.

"Where did you learn all this?" Odile asked from the doorway. The master joiner had brought a lamp of her own and stood holding it like a verdict, her broad face unreadable.

"From keeping books," Maren said. "A forgery is just a ledger entry someone wishes they hadn't made. You find it by knowing what every honest page looks like." She looked at Coral. "You'll be the one who knows what a dishonest one looks like. Show me your dispatches."

Coral unrolled the true orders for the last time. *In the event of refusal of charter terms, the settlement designated Tamsin's Ford is to be denied protection. Assets of value to be removed or destroyed prior to enemy occupation. No survivors' testimony to be permitted regarding republican withdrawal.* The third dispatch, the worst one, lay beneath: the burning, the blame, the erasure of the town's claim to its own militia rolls. She had read them so many times in the past two days that the cipher had stopped feeling like cipher and started feeling like plain speech, which was its own kind of horror.

"We match these," she said. "Format, salutation, cipher key, courier marks. But we change the substance. Instead of abandonment — designation. The town becomes a supply depot for the southern line. Protected, provisioned, exempt from requisition. The republic doesn't burn its own granaries."

"The republic has already burned half its own country," Odile said flatly.

"Which is exactly why this will be believed," Coral said. "Hale isn't waiting for the truth. He's waiting for orders that let him stop doing the hard thing. Every agent past the point of no return wants paperwork that tells him he can go home. We're not forging a lie, Master Vant. We're forging a mercy, and mercies travel faster than cruelties because everyone wants to carry them."

Odile set her lamp on the table. For a long moment she said nothing, and Coral understood that the silence was not doubt but weighing — the same silence Odile gave a joint before committing it, testing whether the thing would hold.

"And after?" Odile said. "When spring comes and someone downriver compares files?"

"By then there won't be files," Coral said. "There may not even be a downriver. Sef warned me the capital was eating itself. Brann confirmed the republic's already fallen — Hale just hasn't admitted it to himself. This dispatch doesn't have to survive forever. It only has to survive until the ice breaks and Hale leaves on it."

"Paper that only has to live a season," Maren murmured. "That's the easiest kind. Seasons are forgiving." She pulled a stool to the table and sat with the decisive motion of a woman who had corrected worse things than empires. "Sit down, courier. Show me the hand."

* * *

The hand was the hardest part, and Coral had known it would be. Dispatches from the field were written by clerks, and clerks wrote in a particular way — fast, slanted, abbreviating where the cipher allowed, pressing harder on the downstrokes because they wrote standing up against a saddle-flap or a knee. Her own hand was a courier's hand, trained to invisibility: small, upright, forgettable. To forge the order she had to become someone else at the level of muscle.

She practiced for two hours before she touched the good paper. Maren supplied her with copies of genuine republican documents from the seized strongbox — requisitions, a writ, two letters from a dead tax collector who had died owing the Regiment more than money — and Coral copied them until her wrist ached, learning the clerk's habits. He crossed his sevens. He made his capitals taller than his lowercase by a full stroke. He grew lazier toward the bottom of a page, letters widening like a path worn by feet.

"He was tired," she murmured at one point, studying the last lines of a requisition.

"They're always tired," Maren said. "Tiredness is the truth in a document. Everything else can lie; the fatigue can't. When you write yours, get tired first."

So Coral got tired first. She worked the bench upstairs earlier that day until her arms shook — Odile had assigned her to hewing tenons without explanation, and Coral now suspected the assignment had been preparation — and then she came down and wrote while her hand trembled slightly, and the trembling went into the letters, and the letters looked like a man at the end of a long war writing orders he hoped someone else would have to execute.

"That's it," Maren said quietly, after the fourth full draft. "That's a man who wants to go home."

Odile leaned over the draft. She could not read the cipher, but she could read structure the way she read grain — where the load-bearing strokes fell, where something had been forced. "It holds together," she said at last. "Now tell me again what it says, in plain speech, word for word."

Coral recited it. *By authority of the Office of Procurement and Lines of Supply, the settlement known as Tamsin's Ford is hereby designated Depot Nine of the Southern Line. The garrison agent is instructed to secure the town's provisions, workshops, and militia rolls against all requisition, hostile or otherwise. Destruction of depot assets is forbidden. Protection of depot assets is the agent's first charge. Acknowledge by return courier.*

"'Hostile or otherwise,'" Odile repeated. A ghost of something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, but its foundation timbers. "You've made the town into a warehouse."

"I've made the town into something the republic cannot afford to break," Coral said. "A rebel village is fuel. A supply depot is infrastructure. Men like Hale don't defend principles. They defend inventories."

"And the acknowledgment by return courier?"

"Gives him a reason to leave. He sends his confirmation downriver and waits for countersignature that will never come, because there's no one left to sign it. By the time he understands that, the ice will be mud and the road will be open, and he'll have to decide whether to report his own gullibility or take credit for securing a valuable depot. Men like Hale always take credit."

Maren had been quiet through all of this, grinding ink — the right ink, iron-gall mixed to the gray-brown the chancery used, aged slightly with a pinch of rust scraped from an old hinge. Now she spoke without looking up. "The seals. That's where forgeries die. Everyone can copy a hand; almost no one can copy a seal honestly, because a real seal is pressed by a machine and a fake is pressed by a person."

"I know," Coral said. "I've seen a woman hang for a seal."

The room went still. Neither of the other women asked, and Coral found she didn't want to tell it — not tonight, not with the good paper lying on the table like a wound. She had watched a forger in Kellering executed over a wax impression that fooled every eye and failed one magnifying lens. She had been nineteen. She had carried the forger's last real message out of the city in her shoe and had never told anyone, because telling would have meant being remembered.

"Then we don't press a seal," Maren said. "We use one."

She rose, slow-backed, and went to the deepest cabinet, the one bolted to the stone wall, and worked a key that hung around her neck inside her shirt. From within she brought out a flat wooden box, joined so finely the seams were invisible until she thumbed the hidden catches, and opened it on the table.

Inside, nested in wool gone gray with age, lay a brass matrix — a seal stamp — bearing the running stag of the old directorate, and beside it a folded certificate of commission, the wax on it dark and intact, pressed by this very stamp some fifteen years ago by a republican official whose name had been struck from the town's memory of grievances only because the grievance itself had been paid off.

"The tax collector's," Odile said. "You kept his seal too."

"We keep everything," Maren said again. "It's not a virtue. It's a discipline." She lifted the matrix and turned it in the light, and the stag seemed to run along the walls of the archive room. "It's fifteen years old. Seals wear. If your dispatches came from a field office, their seal will be worn too — unevenly, in the places a stamp presses hardest. A perfect seal would be the suspicious thing. This one has spent fifteen years learning how to look real."

Coral stared at the brass and felt something turn over in her chest — not hope exactly, but its raw timber, rough-cut and unsanded. All her life her skills had moved outward, away from herself: messages in, messages out, towns saved or abandoned at the end of a chain she wasn't permitted to pull. Now the same craft — the cipher, the hand, the patience, the practiced forgetting — was being pointed like a tool at a single small place, and the place was home.

* * *

They worked until the third hour past midnight.

Maren heated the sealing wax over a spirit lamp, mixing two sticks to match the dull crimson of the original commission, and Coral learned that wax, like wood, had grain — that it wanted to flow certain ways, that a seal poured too hot would feather at the edges and too cool would sit proud like a scar. Odile, who had refused to leave and refused to touch anything, ended up holding the lamp steady with a joiner's immovable patience, and it was her steady hand that let Coral lower the matrix true, without the wobble that betrays a human wrist.

"Press straight down," Odile said. "Don't push. Let the weight do it. Same as driving a peg — the mallet's job is to arrive, not to argue."

Coral breathed out, and pressed, and counted, and lifted.

The stag ran across the cooling wax, worn in exactly the right places, imperfect in exactly the way fifteen years of honest use would make a thing honest-looking. Maren examined it against the old commission with a lens she produced from somewhere inside her shawl, and the archive room was silent for the length of ten heartbeats.

"It would pass me," Maren said. "And I have never yet been wrong about a document, which is a terrible thing to say aloud, so no one repeat it."

Coral sat back and looked at what lay on the table: four pages in a tired clerk's hand, ciphered in the republic's own key, sealed in the republic's own wax, ordering the republic's own agent to protect the town instead of feeding it to the war. A forgery. The finest work she had ever done, and the first piece of it that was not a betrayal of someone.

Her hands were shaking now, properly, the delayed shaking, and she let them. Odile saw and said nothing, but she pushed the spirit lamp out of reach and put the tea — someone had made tea, hours ago, and it had gone cold, and it was the best thing Coral had ever tasted — into her hands instead.

"There's one flaw," Maren said at last, and Coral's blood went cold until the old woman continued: "The flaw is you. Hale has hunted you since the ferry. If he sees your face anywhere near this document's arrival, the best forgery in the world dies of it. The dispatch must appear the way real dispatches appear — found, not presented. It comes down the river with the ice, or it comes off a dead courier, or it comes out of a satchel lost in the snow. It must have a history before it has a reader."

"A history," Coral repeated, and then, slowly, the shape of it came to her, and she almost laughed at the elegance. "Then we give it one. There's a stretch below the falls where the riverbank floods every thaw — the ferryman complained about it my first week. Paper dries out of river mud looking exactly like paper that drowned. We age it. We wet it, dry it, crease it, let the ink bloom a little at the folds. And then, the night before the assembly votes, a rider's horse is found grazing by the ford road with a torn satchel — and no rider. Snow covers the tracks. The town finds the dispatch, Hale authenticates it, and the republic spares us by its own order, in its own hand, witnessed by its own seal."

"And the rider?" Odile asked quietly.

"Was never there," Coral said. "I'm very good at never having been there. It's the one thing I was trained for that turns out to be worth something."

Maren began, deliberately, to clean the pen. Odile banked the lamp. And Coral looked down at the finished forgery — at the tired clerk's hand, the blooming ink, the running stag caught mid-flight in wax — and understood that she had spent fifteen years learning to destroy things with paper, and had needed a town full of joiners to teach her that the same craft could build.

Outside, the wind shifted, and beneath it, faint and rhythmic, came the sound of hammers: the night watch reinforcing the workshop doors against whatever Brann and Hale might try before the vote. Three days, perhaps four. The town was boarding its windows, and she was sealing its salvation in wax, and somewhere downriver the ice groaned and thickened, carrying toward them the last ferry of a dead republic.

Coral wrapped the dispatch in oilcloth, and in oilcloth again, and hid it where only she and Maren knew — and then went up into the cold to find Tamsin, because there was one more thing a forged letter needed, and that was someone willing to swear to a lie in front of the whole town, and she had no right to ask it, and she was going to ask anyway.
