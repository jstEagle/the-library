## 30. Fire Watch

The snow began an hour after dark, fine and dry as flour, the kind that squeaked underfoot and carried sound a long way across the frozen river. Tamsin stood in the shadow of the workshop's south wall with her hood up and her hands buried in her armpits, watching the road down from the ridge, and thought about her mother's rule for glue: *if it sets too fast, it breaks too fast.* Brann was setting fast. Everyone could see it. The question of the night — the question she'd argued about all afternoon in the council room until Odile banged the flat of her hand on the table like a mallet driving home a joint — was whether they would let him break himself, or break him first.

Odile had won the argument, which surprised no one, least of all Brann, who wasn't there to hear it.

"We take him breathing," Odile had said. "Whatever he's done, whatever he meant to do — we take him breathing, or we're not the Regiment anymore, we're just another mob with better joinery."

"And if he burns the workshop down around us?" That was Doss Halloway, whose family had held the ferry rights for six generations and who took every threat to the town personally, as though the river itself might be insulted.

"Then we rebuild," Odile said. "We've done it before. We haven't yet done it with blood on our hands, and I mean to keep the record clean."

Tamsin had said nothing at the table, because she had nothing to add that Coral hadn't already said in the lamplight two nights past, when she'd laid out the whole rotten architecture of her mission — the dispatches, the codes, the third message ordering the workshop burned and the blame laid on the enemy army so that no one would ever look for the town's ledger again. Coral had said it all in a voice like someone reading their own autopsy, and then sat down and put her hands flat on the table, palms up, the way an apprentice shows a master they're holding nothing back.

*He'll come at night,* Coral had said. *With pitch. He won't trust fire to anyone else, not for this. And he'll come from the kiln path, because it's the only approach without sightlines from the bridge.*

So now Tamsin was on the kiln path.

Not alone. Never alone, tonight. Maren Okonkwo — no, Maren was the archive-keeper, Coral was the courier, Tamsin still tripped over the names sometimes — Maren crouched by the cold kiln with a lantern shuttered to a coin of light, checking the wick for the fourth time. Doss's son Petey, fourteen and vibrating with the importance of being trusted with something, waited at the bend where the path met the millrace, with a signal horn he had been explicitly ordered never, ever to blow unless the workshop was already burning. Old Sela Vant, Odile's aunt and the fastest knitter in three counties, sat wrapped in blankets on the workshop porch doing absolutely nothing suspicious whatsoever, except that her knitting needles were steel and she knew exactly where everyone's knees were.

And above them all, on the roof that Brann had come to burn, half a dozen men and women lay along the ridge beam in the falling snow, quiet as shingles, holding ropes that ended in loops and nets and one very heavy coil of chain borrowed from the ferry dock.

The plan was Odile's, refined by Coral, and its genius — if it worked — was its smallness. No barricades. No weapons drawn. No shouting until it was over. A town that caught a man the way a joiner catches a slipping chisel: not by fighting the motion but by giving it somewhere safe to go.

"He'll bring help," Tamsin whispered to Maren, for the tenth time. "Hale promised him something. Hale wouldn't send him alone."

"Hale doesn't waste assets," Maren murmured back. Her breath fogged and vanished. "But Hale also knows what happens if the republic's hand is seen in this. If Brann is taken alive and talks — and he will talk, he's been talking his whole life — Hale wants to be able to say he never touched it. Alone is exactly how Brann will be sent."

"You sound like you admire him."

"I spent fifteen years admiring people like him," Maren said. "It's called knowing your enemy, and it kept me alive long enough to get here." She paused. "Mostly."

Tamsin looked at her — at the woman who had arrived as a nobody, a widowed seamstress's cousin, and turned out to be a courier of a dying republic carrying orders that would have erased everything Tamsin loved. She should have been afraid of Coral Okonkwo. She had been, for a while, in the way you're afraid of weather. But weather didn't sit beside you at the bench and hold the wood steady while you learned to cut a dovetail badly. Weather didn't laugh at its own mistakes. Weather didn't stand up in front of the council and hand them every secret it owned like a person emptying their pockets after being robbed.

"What happens to him?" Tamsin asked. "After. To Brann."

Maren was quiet long enough that the snow filled in her footprints.

"In Vessany, a traitor to the state was shot at dawn, and the dawn was scheduled by committee, and sometimes the committee argued about it for weeks while the man rotted waiting. Here—" She shrugged inside her coat. "Here I honestly don't know. That's why I'm standing in the snow instead of running for the ice."

Tamsin almost smiled. Almost. Her jaw was too cold.

* * *

They came at the hour when even the dogs give up, which Coral had predicted to within a quarter bell, and Tamsin would think about that later, about what kind of life taught a person the exact weight of the darkest hour.

She heard him before she saw him: not footsteps — he was careful, wrapping his boots in sacking the way poachers did — but the soft irregular *clink* of a crockery jar of pitch knocking against a tin of tinder in a sack that wasn't packed by a careful man. Brann had always been a man who planned speeches and improvised logistics. He'd served on the council nine years. He'd chaired the rationing committee through two hard winters. Tamsin had sat across from him at assembly since she was old enough to sit in the gallery, and she had watched him count votes with the patience of a man counting money that was already his.

Now she watched him come up the kiln path bent double under a sack of fire, moving through the town he'd helped govern like a thief through a house he used to own.

The pitch jar clinked again. Closer now. Ten yards. Snow settled on Tamsin's shoulders and she did not brush it off, because brushing off snow was movement and movement was light against snowfall and light was death to the plan.

Brann stopped at the corner of the drying shed, set his sack down, and straightened up to look at the workshop roof.

Tamsin understood, in that moment, exactly what he was seeing. He was seeing the building where half the town's wealth slept — the lathes, the saws, forty years of patterns and jigs, the great pine benches worn into maps of ten thousand hands. He was seeing what burning it would buy him: a seat under whatever government crawled out of the war's wreckage, rank, distance, escape from a town full of neighbors who would know him forever as the man who tried. He was doing arithmetic with fire. It was the same arithmetic Hale had done on the ferry, the same arithmetic the republic had done with every town it couldn't hold: *what can we take, what can we burn, what can we make the ashes say.*

And above him, along the roof ridge, the answer was already coiled and waiting.

Petey Halloway's signal was not the horn. It was a pebble, tossed from the millrace bend against the drying shed's north wall — one knock, two, three, the rhythm of a hammer finishing a joint, the oldest signal in the Regiment, the one children used to call each other to supper. Brann flinched at the sound. Looked the wrong way.

That was when the ropes came down.

They came down soft and simultaneous, four weighted lines dropping from the roof edge into the snow with barely a whisper, and the shapes on the roof came over the eaves like the building itself leaning — Aldous Crane and Tam Halloway and big Rutha Ives and Odile herself, gray-haired and immovable, descending on Brann Corver in the falling snow the way winter descends, which is to say completely and without anger.

To his credit, Brann fought. Tamsin watched him tear free of the first net, swing the pitch jar like a weapon — heard it shatter against the drying shed wall, pitch splashing black across the boards, close enough that the smell of it reached her by the kiln, tar-sweet and terrible — and then Rutha Ives simply walked into him. Not punched him. Walked into him, the way you'd correct a piece that wanted to twist, absorbing all that panic into her chest and arms and the sheer mass of a woman who lifted roof beams for a living, and took him down into the snow with a gracelessness that was somehow gentle. The tinder tin skittered away under the porch. Sela Vant, without rising from her chair, pinned it under one steel knitting needle and a boot.

"No blood," Rutha said, kneeling on him, conversational. "You hear me, Brann? Nobody's bleeding tonight. Don't make me the exception."

"They'll come for all of you," Brann gasped, face in the snow, arms pinioned by four sets of hands and one coil of chain that Tam was wrapping with the absent expertise of a man who moored boats. "The charter's dead, the republic's agents are everywhere, you're — you're nothing, you're a ledger and some sheds—"

"Yes," Odile said.

She crouched down in front of him. Snow gathered in her hair and she didn't seem to notice it. Tamsin, pressed against the cold kiln with Maren's hand suddenly tight around her wrist, watched her mother look at the man who had sat beside her at council for nine years, and the look was worse than fury. It was grief done properly. It was a joiner examining a joint that had failed, finding the flaw, and knowing it had always been there.

"The republic fell," Odile said quietly. "You may not have heard. It seems to have fallen on you first."

Brann made a sound that wasn't a word.

"Bring him to the hall," Odile said, standing. "Warm him up. Feed him if he'll eat. And somebody scrub that pitch off the shed before it sets — heat and lye, and mind the grain." She turned, and found Tamsin by the kiln in the dark, because mothers found daughters the way compasses found north, and something in her face eased by one degree. "Tamsin. Fetch the good lamp oil out of the shed before the pitch ruins it. And then come to the hall."

"Aren't you going to ask him why?"

Her mother considered this, snow melting on her shoulders.

"He'll tell us why at trial, or he'll tell us why in twenty years, or he'll die not telling us and we'll have to live with it. That's what keeping your own books means, love. You write down what happened, not what you wish had." She glanced back once at Brann, being hauled upright between Halloways, sack of failed fire abandoned in the snow. "What I wish had happened is none of his business."

* * *

They put Brann in the council hall, in the good chair by the stove, with a blanket over his knees, which was either mercy or the most devastating insult the town could devise, and Tamsin genuinely believed it was both.

By midnight nearly the whole Regiment had crowded into the gallery and along the walls, wet with snow, loud with the particular hush of people trying not to talk. Someone had brought mulled cider. Someone had brought the pitch-soaked door plank from the drying shed and propped it by the stove as evidence, black streaks steaming gently. Petey Halloway stood by the door wearing the expression of a soldier home from a war that had lasted four hours.

Coral stood at the back. Tamsin went to her.

"Well?" Tamsin said.

"It worked," Coral said. Her voice had a strange, thin quality, like a board that hadn't dried yet. "Nobody bled. You caught him like — like he was yours. Like he belonged to you even while betraying you."

"He does belong to us. That's the whole problem." Tamsin studied her. In the stove-light Coral looked older than her years and younger than her fears, both at once. "You've seen this before. The other way. Towns that caught traitors."

"Towns that caught traitors," Coral said, "and towns that were caught by them. There isn't a difference you can see from the outside. Only from here." She nodded at the hall, at the blanket, at the cider going around, at Sela Vant loudly telling anyone who'd listen that she'd disarmed a man with a knitting needle. "I spent fifteen years learning how power works when it's afraid. This is the first time I've watched it work when it isn't."

Down on the floor, Odile called the session to order with the flat of her hand on the table — the same gesture, Tamsin realized, that she used to seat a stubborn tenon, and probably the same force.

"Brann Corver." Her voice filled the hall without effort. "You are accused, by evidence and by your own sack, of conspiring to burn the workshop of the Regiment and lay the fire upon others. The charter agent Merrick Hale stands implicated in your correspondence, which the archive-keeper has read to this council and will read again if anyone doubts it. Under the laws of the Regiment, you are entitled to speak, to counsel, and to a trial before the full assembly at midwinter week."

Brann lifted his head. His face was wrecked — not beaten, just wrecked, the way a man looks when the future he sold everything for turns out to be counterfeit. His eyes went around the hall, cataloguing faces, finding no allies, and stopped — Tamsin saw it happen — on Coral.

"You," Brann said hoarsely. "The seamstress's cousin. I knew there was something—"

"There usually is," Coral said, from the back, in a clear dry voice that carried, "but not what you think. Ask me at trial, Councilor. I'll be there. I'm in the book now."

Something passed over Brann's face then that Tamsin couldn't name — not fear, not quite defeat. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man realizing that the town had absorbed one more stranger, secrets and all, and closed over her like water, and that there had never been any crack in it for him to widen, because cracks weren't how the Regiment failed. It failed, if it failed, the way timber failed: slowly, along the grain, from things done years ago in the dark.

Which meant, Tamsin thought, watching her mother's face, that they'd want to check the grain.

"Before trial," Odile said, "there is the matter of the other one. Hale came upriver on my ferry, ate at my table, and negotiated with this council under a flag of office belonging to a state that no longer exists. He is, as of tonight, a man with no commission, no army, and no exit except the ice or the spring thaw." She let that sit. Around the hall, the hush sharpened into something almost eager. "Doss says the river won't bear a loaded sled for another month. But a man on foot, traveling light, with papers..."

"A man on foot could cross by the narrows," Doss Halloway admitted reluctantly. "If he didn't mind the risk. Ice is thick enough there for a man. Barely."

"He'll run," Tamsin breathed, mostly to Coral. "The moment he hears Brann's taken — he'll run for the narrows tonight."

"He'll run," Coral agreed softly. And then she smiled, and it was not entirely a nice smile, and Tamsin thought: *there she is. The courier. Ours now.* "Which is why, before anyone goes to bed, I need the archive-keeper, the forgery kit, and about an hour. Let him run. Let him carry something."

Odile raised an eyebrow from the floor. "Carry what?"

"A dispatch," Coral said. "Ordering him home. From a government that can't stop him, signed by an authority that doesn't exist — and written, every word of it, in the republic's own hand."

She was already rolling up her sleeves.
