## 31. Hale's Last Ferry

The ice went out of the river on a Tuesday, with a sound like a giant clearing its throat.

Coral heard it from the workshop loft where she'd been sleeping since midwinter — a long grinding groan upstream, then a series of cracks that rolled down the valley like applause from an enormous, indifferent audience. She was at the window before she was fully awake, breath fogging the cold glass, watching the dark water shrug off its white skin and begin to move again. Chunks of ice the size of cart wheels rode the current past the town, tilting, spinning, dissolving into the brown rush of spring.

The road out was open. The ferry would run by week's end. And in three days, if Odile Vant's plan held, Merrick Hale would be standing on that ferry with nothing to show for a winter's work but chilblains and a folder of papers that said exactly what Coral Okonkwo had written them to say.

She dressed in the dark and went downstairs, because there was no sense pretending she'd sleep more.

* * *

The forgery had taken eleven nights.

That was the part no one who hadn't done courier work would ever understand — that the actual writing was the smallest piece of it. Anyone with steady hands could copy a hand. What took time was everything around the ink: the age of the paper, the wear of the fold, the particular way a document traveled. Real dispatches were folded twice along lines that softened as they passed from saddlebag to glove to inner pocket. They accumulated small damages honestly. A forgery that arrived pristine might as well have arrived wearing a sign.

So Coral had aged the paper with weak tea and a warm stone, folded and refolded it until the creases looked like they'd been made by a hundred anxious hands, and then — the hardest discipline of all — put it away and touched nothing for two days so her own eagerness wouldn't leak into the thing.

Odile had watched the whole process from the archive room doorway, arms crossed, saying almost nothing. On the fourth night she'd brought tea without being asked. On the seventh she'd sat down. By the ninth she was asking questions that showed she understood the answers: *Why not seal it fresh? Why does the wax matter? Who countersigns a regional order?*

"The undersecretary for logistics," Coral said, pressing the signet — borrowed, via the ledger's records, from a dead man whose seal impression lived faithfully in the town's own books of republican debts — into the cooling wax. "A man named Corvess. I met him once. He had wet hands and apologized constantly."

"And you're certain his seal is right?"

"I'm certain the impression in your ledger is right, because your clerk pressed the original order flat between pages to keep it. That's how I learned what his seal looks like at all." She blew gently across the wax. "The republic taught me to copy things. Your town kept better records than the republic did. Between us, we can make anything true."

Master Ellery Vosk, the archive-keeper, had contributed the final and most important element: the routing marks. Every dispatch moving through the republic's logistics chain acquired stamps and docket numbers like rings on a tree. Vosk, who had spent thirty years cataloguing the paperwork of every creditor who'd ever done business with the Regiment, knew the format of the eastern circuit's dockets better, he admitted with some embarrassment, than he knew most psalms. He wrote out the docket sequence in his precise archival hand, checked it against three separate references, and then sat back and looked at what they'd made.

"It's beautiful," he said, and sounded genuinely troubled by that.

"It's meant to be," Coral said. "Bureaucracy runs on beauty. Nobody questions a clean form."

Now, on this first morning of open water, the forgery sat sealed in its oilcloth wrapper on the council table, waiting to be presented to Merrick Hale as though it had come upriver with the thaw — the latest word from a capital that no longer existed, ordering him to stand down.

Coral stood at the window of the council hall with a cup of tea going cold in her hands and watched the river run, and tried to feel like a victor. What she felt instead was something quieter and stranger: the sensation of holding a tool she'd spent fifteen years sharpening for other people's purposes, and using it, finally, for her own.

* * *

Hale came to the council hall at the second bell, as punctual as he had been all winter.

He had changed over the months, in ways that were easy to miss unless you'd been studying him the way Coral had studied him — the way one studies weather, or a wound that hasn't decided whether to close. His coat was the same good wool. His boots were the same. But the man inside them had gone slightly soft at the edges, the way garrison soldiers did after too long among civilians: he'd gained weight from Tamsin's Ford's cooking, lost the hollows under his eyes, picked up the local habit of nodding to people in the street. He'd been seen, more than once, playing draughts with the ferryman's boy. Coral suspected he hated himself for every hour of it.

He stopped just inside the door and read the room the way she would have — the full council at the table, Odile at the head, the archive-keeper with a folder squared precisely in front of him, and herself, Coral, seated to one side with the unremarkable air of a woman who took minutes. He looked at her a moment too long. He always did. It was their private language now, the only honest conversation they had left.

"Councilor Vant," he said. "You sent for me."

"We received dispatches," Odile said, "with the first boat up from Marrow Landing. From your ministry." She let that sit. "Concerning this town."

Something moved behind Hale's eyes — not fear, Coral thought, but the specific alertness of a man hearing terrain shift underfoot. "I wasn't informed any post had come through."

"No," Odile agreed. "You weren't."

Vosk opened the folder and slid the document across the table. Hale crossed the room and picked it up, and Coral watched him read with the detachment of someone watching a play she'd helped write.

She had composed those sentences herself, in the flattest ministerial register she could produce — that bloodless dialect in which human beings became *assets*, towns became *installations*, and atrocity became *reallocation*. *By order of the Undersecretary for Logistics, Circuit East: the installation known as Tamsin's Ford is hereby designated a supply depot of the third class. All prior directives concerning the installation are superseded. No action affecting the installation's infrastructure or personnel is authorized. Acknowledge receipt by return courier.*

*No action affecting the installation's infrastructure.* Eleven words that quietly buried the third dispatch — the real one, still chained to nothing now but Coral's conscience, the order to burn the workshop and blame the enemy. Eleven words that turned Merrick Hale from an instrument of arson into a custodian of warehouse shelving.

Hale read it twice. Then a third time, slower, and Coral saw the exact moment the arithmetic finished in his head — saw his gaze flick up, briefly, involuntarily, to her face, and saw her give him nothing back. Not a smile. Not a blink. Just the mild, patient blankness of a woman waiting for the men to finish with paperwork.

"This is—" He stopped. Started again. "This routing mark is from the eastern circuit. My instructions came through the central directorate. There may be some—"

"Conflict?" Odile said. "Yes. We wondered about that ourselves. Master Vosk, what does the Regiment do when two authorities disagree?"

"Lower authority yields to higher," Vosk said comfortably, "and the undersecretary's office outranks a field directorate in all matters of supply and infrastructure. It's in the fourth article of the administrative code. I looked it up." He said it with the serene satisfaction of a man who had indeed looked it up, in triplicate, and enjoyed every minute.

Hale set the paper down very carefully, as though it might go off. "Councilor Vant. May I speak plainly?"

"You may always speak plainly here. Whether anyone believes you is another matter."

"Whoever produced this document," Hale said, "is not acting in the republic's interest."

"No?" Odile raised her eyebrows. "It orders you to leave us intact. It protects our workshop, our mills, our people. From where I sit, it's the kindest thing the republic has ever done for this town."

"It's a *forgery*."

"Is it?" Odile leaned back. "Prove it."

The silence that followed was, Coral thought, the most beautiful sound of her entire adult life.

Because that was the trap, and Hale could see every wire of it. To prove the document false, he would have to produce his own credentials, his own orders — including the third dispatch, the burning order, which existed nowhere except in his possession and hers. Producing it would mean confessing to the council that he had come to their town carrying instructions to destroy it. And even if he did confess, even if he laid the true dispatch on the table beside the false one and demanded comparison — well. Two documents, both bearing seals, both claiming supreme authority, arriving in a town that had spent a winter learning exactly what the republic intended for it. Which one would the Regiment believe?

The answer was written in every face at that table. Hale read it too.

"Councilor," he said at last, and his voice had dropped its official warmth entirely; what was underneath was tired, and oddly young. "If I send this downriver for verification, the verification will take until summer. If I act without verification and this document is genuine, I'll be answering for insubordination in wartime. If I act and it's false, I'll be answering for the destruction of a loyal installation on forged authority." He laughed once, short and without humor. "You've built me a box where every wall is a court-martial."

"Yes," Odile said. "We're joiners. Boxes are rather our specialty."

Brann, at the far end of the table, had not said a word all morning. Coral noted it, and noted also the sheen of sweat at his hairline, and filed both away.

* * *

They gave Hale until the evening ferry to decide, which was a courtesy, Odile explained afterward, and a message: the Regiment did not deal in ultimatums, only in schedules.

Coral found him at the landing an hour before the boat came, because she had known he would be there. There was nowhere else for a man in his position to stand — at the edge of the thing that had defeated him, looking at the way out.

The ferry was already loading downstream cargo: casks of the winter's joinery, bolts of woven wool, the town's goods going out to a market that no longer quite knew what money was worth. The ferryman's boy waved at Hale, who did not wave back.

"You knew since the ferry," Coral said, coming to stand beside him at the rail. "Since the day you arrived. So say it properly, at least once, to my face."

"Merrick Hale, directorate field agent," he said, to the river. "And you're Coral Okonkwo. Best courier the eastern circuit ever lost. They told stories about you at the training house. The woman who was never remembered." He shook his head slowly. "Do you know what I thought, when I worked out it was you? I thought: finally. Someone competent on the other side of this. Someone I don't have to carry."

"I was never on your side."

"No," he agreed. "You were never on anyone's side. That was the whole legend." He turned to look at her, and up close his eyes were bloodshot and ordinary, and she felt the strange grief of seeing the monster resolved, at last, into merely a man. "But you're on theirs now. I watched it happen all winter. You sit in their meetings. You eat at their tables. When the roof came down you were on it with the rest of them at four in the morning." He smiled faintly. "You know what the difference is between us? I never forgot what I was. You finally did."

The words landed harder than she expected. She let them sit a moment before answering.

"The republic fell in the autumn," she said. "Maybe before that. Maybe it fell years ago and we were just couriers for a ghost. Sef died in the purge. Half the people who signed my original orders are dead or fled, and the other half are fighting each other over the wreckage. You're not serving a state, Hale. You're serving a habit."

"So are you." He nodded at the council hall up the hill. "Every one of them, every day. Habit called law. Habit called the Regiment." But he said it without force, and they both heard the hollowness in it. Some habits built bridges. Others burned workshops. The word covered too much ground to argue over.

Downstream, the ferry sounded its horn — one low note off a tin trumpet, the same note that had announced every arrival and departure all winter.

"I'll file my acknowledgment," Hale said. "Supply depot, third class. No action authorized." He straightened his coat, and for a moment he was entirely the ministry man again, crisp and gray. "Your paperwork is flawless, by the way. Better than the real thing. Corvess would be flattered, if he weren't presumably dead."

"He had wet hands."

"He had terrible hands." And then, stepping back from the rail, quiet enough that only she could hear it: "There's a list, you know. Of names the new powers want found. Yours is on it — both of yours, the real one and the invented ones. Stay north of the ford line and no one will bother collecting. Come south, and the paper you wrote won't protect you, because paper doesn't. Nothing does. It's people or nothing."

He walked down to the boat. The boy took his fare without meeting his eyes — the town's small, complete verdict, delivered in copper coin. Hale stood at the stern as the ferry swung out into the current, and he did not look back at the town, but he did look, once, at the workshop roof — patched now, the new timber pale against the old — and Coral understood that look completely. It was the look of a man taking inventory of the one asset he'd failed to liquidate, and finding, against every instruction, that it had appreciated.

Then the bend of the river took him, and the water ran brown and busy in his wake, and that was all. No arrest. No confrontation. No last duel on the deck. Just a man leaving on a boat, undone by a routing mark and a town that kept better books than his ministry did.

Coral stood at the rail a long time after the ferry was gone.

* * *

The celebration, when the town got around to having it, was entirely disproportionate to the drama: someone dragged a barrel of the winter cider into the square, and the fiddlers appeared as fiddlers always did, and by full dark half the Regiment was dancing on planks laid over the mud of the thawing square.

Coral lasted twenty minutes among them before the noise of so many unguarded happy people drove her out to the edges, where Tamsin found her leaning against the kiln shed with a cup of cider she wasn't drinking.

"He's gone," Tamsin said. "You should be dancing. Odile danced. I saw it. There are witnesses."

"I'm metabolizing."

"That's a terrible use of a victory night." Tamsin leaned beside her, shoulder warm against her shoulder. After a moment: "What did he say to you? At the landing. You've had that face since."

Coral considered lying, and found she didn't want to anymore, which was perhaps the whole story of her winter told in one small refusal. "He said there's a list of names. People the new powers want found. Mine's on it."

Tamsin absorbed this with a frown, not fear. "And?"

"And nothing. He said stay north of the ford line and no one comes collecting."

"Then we stay north of the ford line." Tamsin said it the way she'd say *then we fix the roof* — a logistical observation, a job for Thursday. She bumped Coral's shoulder with her own. "Also, you're on the list under four different names, so really they'd have to catch you four times. Terrible odds for them."

Despite everything — the ache behind her ribs, the cold coming off the river, the memory of Hale's face at the stern — Coral laughed. It came out rusty, underused, real.

Inside the shed, the kiln still held its heat from the day's firing, and through the gap under the door Coral could see the coals banked low and red, patient, waiting. Tomorrow she would burn the true dispatches on that fire — tomorrow, when her hands were steady, when Odile and Vosk could witness it, when the last chain to the dead republic could go into the flames with proper ceremony instead of drunkenness. Tonight the papers still sat locked in the archive, one thin folder containing the entire rotten architecture of her old life.

Tonight she stood in the cold with cider she wasn't drinking and a girl who talked about lists of hunted names like ration schedules, and somewhere downriver Merrick Hale was writing his acknowledgment of receipt in a careful ministry hand.

Tamsin drained her cup and pushed off the wall. "Come on. One dance. Odile's watching, and she counts your dances the way she counts everything else — in the ledger."

"One."

"One," Tamsin agreed, and grinned, and hauled her toward the music.

Behind them, in the banked heart of the kiln, the fire waited for what tomorrow would bring it — and Coral walked toward the light with the list of her own names folded small in her mind, and for the first time in fifteen years, was not afraid of who might be reading.
