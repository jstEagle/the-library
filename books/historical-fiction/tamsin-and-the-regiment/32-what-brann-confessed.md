## 32. What Brann Confessed

They kept Brann in the old drying shed behind the cooper's yard, the one with the low door and the iron hasps that had once held green timber through the damp of autumn. It was not a cell. The Regiment did not have cells. It had a stove, a cot, a bucket, and a lock on the outside that Odile Vant herself had fitted with a new key, and it had Coral Okonkwo sitting across from him in the lamplight because no one else could hear what he had to say and know what it meant.

Coral had argued against being the one to sit with him. She had argued it quietly, in the way she had learned to argue — sideways, with reasons that looked like someone else's reasons. Let Tamsin go in; Tamsin had caught him at the copying. Let Odile go in; Odile had the weight of the council behind her voice. But the council had voted, all of them looking at her over the tallow lamps of the workshop hall, and Master Joiner Vant had said only, "You speak his language. We don't."

She had wanted to say: I stopped speaking it. She had wanted to say: that language is why there are chains on your bridge and pitch on your doorstep and an agent from downriver sleeping in the guest room of the inn like any traveler. But the vote had been taken, and votes were the whole point of this town, so she had put on her shawl and walked through the cold with Tamsin at her shoulder as far as the shed door, and then alone into the yellow light.

Brann sat on the edge of the cot with his hands between his knees. He was a big man, a sawyer by trade before the council had raised him up, and he had always filled rooms — not with noise, exactly, but with the sense that here was a person who took up more space than others were using. Now he seemed folded, like timber left too long in the rain. His beard had gone three days untrimmed. He did not look up when she came in.

"You're the courier," he said.

"Yes."

"I knew." He said it flatly, without pride. "From the first week. The way you counted doors. Nobody counts doors unless they mean to leave by them or keep people out of them." He laughed, a short dry sound with nothing in it. "Hale knew too, of course. Knew before me. He told me you'd been sent to do what we were doing, only better paid."

"And you believed him."

"I believed what made my life easier." He finally raised his head. In the lamplight his eyes were red-rimmed and very tired. "That's the whole confession, mistress. You can write it down. I believed what made my life easier. A rank under whatever comes after. My name kept clean while other men got dirty. I told myself the republic would win, because if the republic won then everything Hale asked for was just... prudence. Foresight." His hands turned over, palms up, empty. "I didn't ask myself what I'd be if they lost."

Coral sat down on the stool by the stove and let the silence stretch. This, too, was craft. Sef had taught her that a silence held long enough becomes a room the other person walks into on their own.

"They haven't lost," she said at last. "The war goes on. That's why Hale came. That's why the dispatches—"

"Read the rest of them," Brann said. "The ones you didn't burn yet. The third one, the one about the fire — read past the orders. Read the routing marks."

Something cold moved down Coral's spine, slow as meltwater. She had decoded the third dispatch in the dark hours before dawn, alone, her fingers shaking so badly she had misread two cipher groups and had to start over. Orders to burn the workshop. Blame the enemy. Erase the town's claim on its own charter and its own ledger. She had read it four times and each time the words had been the same, and she had carried them to the council in the dark like a live coal in both hands.

But she had not read the routing marks. Routing marks were the plumbing of the trade — dates, stations, relays. You learned to skip them the way you learned to skip the sound of your own heartbeat.

"Why?" she said.

"Because I asked Hale the same question," Brann said. "Three nights ago, before your people took me. I asked him how the republic could spare an agent and a burning detail for one small town when the front's been collapsing since autumn. And he laughed at me. Not cruelly. That's the worst part — he laughed like I was a child asking where the sun went at night. And he said—" Brann's voice caught, and he steadied it the way a man steadies a plank he knows is going to split anyway. "He said, *the republic fell in the summer, man. Where have you been?*"

The stove ticked. Outside, wind worried the eaves of the cooper's yard. Coral sat absolutely still and felt the sentence go through her like a peg driven home.

"Explain," she said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, calm and professional, Sef's voice, the courier's voice, the voice of a woman who had spent fifteen years learning to sound like nothing at all.

"He wouldn't say more at first. Then he drank half a bottle of the ferryman's plum brandy and he said it all. Said the capital went in the summer. Said the Assembly was dissolved in June, or July — he wasn't sure which month, he'd been on the road, everyone was on the road. Said the army split in three and two parts of it went over to the coastal lords and the third just... stopped existing. Deserted into the hills. Said there hasn't been a republic anywhere south of the mountains since the harvest." Brann looked at his hands again. "And then he said the part that matters to us. He said the orders never changed. Because the orders weren't really orders anymore. They were *habits*. Men like him, men like you, still carrying out missions for a government that doesn't exist, because carrying them out is the only thing they know how to be."

*Habits.* Coral thought of the dispatch case chained to her wrist for all those weeks, the weight of it so constant she had stopped feeling it, the way you stop feeling your own name. She thought of the cipher keys she still kept memorized, the dead-drop protocols, the contingency phrases, all of it maintained with perfect discipline for masters who had ceased to exist before the first snow ever touched the pass above Tamsin's Ford.

She thought of Sef, dead in the purge — *the capital's purge*, the letter had said, dated months ago, delayed on roads that had stopped delivering anything but rumor — and understood suddenly that the purge itself had been one of the last things the dying republic ever managed to accomplish. It had killed its own servants almost as its final act. It had burned its own house down around the people keeping the books.

"The enemy army," she said. Her mouth was dry. "The one the second dispatch said would be let loose on the town if the charter was refused."

"Hale says it isn't an army anymore either. Says it's whatever the coastal lords couldn't absorb — starving companies under captains who've declared themselves something between generals and bandits. He says if word goes downriver that Tamsin's Ford refused terms, some of those captains would come up here for the winter stores whether anyone ordered it or not. No declaration needed. No rebellion to justify." He swallowed. "He said the burning order was mercy, compared to that. He actually used the word *mercy*. Like he was doing you a kindness."

Coral rose and walked to the little barred window, high in the wall, where the blue of evening was coming down over the yards. Below, she could see lamplight moving in the workshop — the night shift, the joiners finishing a cradle order, unhurried, certain that tomorrow would come and require planks. She watched the light and tried to feel what she was supposed to feel.

Grief, she thought. I should be grieving. My country is dead.

But what rose in her instead was stranger and colder: the vertigo of a woman who has been faithfully carrying water to a house that burned down while she walked. Fifteen years. Fifteen years of silence, of aliases, of counting doors, of never being remembered, of giving her life — her actual life, her name, her face, her right to be loved by anyone in any place — and the thing she had given it to had died last summer, in June or July, while she was walking some mountain road with a case chained to her wrist, decoding instructions from ghosts.

*Trust no handler,* Sef had written. He had known. Of course he had known; he had been in the capital when it happened, or near enough to see the smoke. His last letter had reached her months late, carried by roads collapsing into rumor, and every word of warning in it had been true, and she had read it and grieved him and kept walking anyway, because the mission was real, the mission had to be real, because if the mission was real then the fifteen years had meant something.

The mission had been a habit. The mission had been a ghost giving orders to the living.

"Do you understand what you're telling me?" she said, turning back to him. "Do you understand what this means for the town?"

"It means there's nobody coming," Brann said simply. "No relief, no reinforcement, no spring campaign to hold the line below the ford. It also means—" and here he did look at her directly, and she saw that underneath the brokenness there was still the ambitious man, the clever man, the man who had copied documents in the dark because he believed the future belonged to whoever planned for it. "It means everything is negotiable now. There's no republic to owe the ledger debts to. There's no charter to sell except to whoever's strongest in the spring. Hale wants to hand the town to the coastal lords as his entry fee. I was going to help him." He paused. "Was. Past tense. I'm telling you this instead, which should tell you what I think my prospects are."

"Your prospects," Coral said, "are whatever the council decides they are. Not me."

"The courier decides. Everyone knows it. They took me alive because you told them to take me alive — I heard that from the ferryman's boy, who hears everything." He gave the dry laugh again. "You could have let them break my arms. Some of them wanted to. Instead you sat up all night with their archive-keeper making paper. So I'll tell you the truth, courier, because you're the only person in this valley who'll know what to do with it, and maybe because a man who's been a traitor twice would like, just once, to be something else."

* * *

She stayed another hour. She made him say it all again, slowly, in order, the way you make a witness walk a road twice to see if the footprints match. Much of it was haze — Hale had been drunk, Brann had been frightened, and frightened men remember in shards. But the shape of it held.

The republic had fallen in midsummer. The Assembly dissolved, the ministries scattered, the archives burned or looted — and among the looted archives, Brann repeated, haltingly, were the courier rosters. Names. Routes. Cover identities. Which meant that somewhere in the hands of the coastal lords' new clerks, or in a ditch, or in the memory of whichever clerk had grabbed the box and run, was a list on which a widowed seamstress's cousin named Coral might appear beside a string of other names, most of them already dead.

The war was over. No one had won. The lords had the coast and the ports; the hill captains had the passes and the granaries they stripped; and in between, everywhere in between, were towns like Tamsin's Ford, holding their own books, keeping their own laws, waiting for a spring that would bring not armies now but hunger, refugees, and men with titles freshly painted on their coats, all of them looking for stores, for shelter, for charter rolls to sign and taxes to levy and young backs to fill out companies that no longer served anything but themselves.

"No one won," Brann said again, at the end, and this time it was not information. It was mourning. For all his scheming, for all his soft corruption, he had loved the republic in the way men love the ladder they climbed. He had wanted it to survive so that his climb would have meant something. "I sat in that inn for a week telling myself Hale was lying, that it was a test, a loyalty probe. That's what we used to do — plant false despair and see who repeated it. I kept waiting to be arrested for believing him." He looked up. "Nobody's coming to arrest anybody, mistress. That's the whole of it. The world where arrests mattered is over."

When Coral came out, the cold hit her face like water and she stood a moment in the yard, breathing, letting the dark settle over her. Tamsin was waiting by the gate, hooded against the wind, having disobeyed the instruction to go home with a directness that Coral had long ago stopped finding surprising.

"Well?" Tamsin said.

"He talked."

"What did he say?"

Coral opened her mouth to give the report, the summary, the clean professional digest — and found she could not. What came out instead, in the freezing dark, to a nineteen-year-old apprentice with snow melting on her hood, was the true thing, the naked thing:

"Tamsin. The country I worked for died last summer. Before I ever crossed the pass. Everything I carried, everything I hid from you, everything that nearly got your mother's workshop burned — it was orders from a corpse. I gave fifteen years to a ghost." Her voice cracked on the last word and she was glad of the dark, glad of the wind, glad of anything that covered the sound. "I didn't even get to lose it properly. It was already lost. Someone else was wearing its face the whole time, signing its name, and I marched for it like a good soldier."

Tamsin was quiet for a moment. Then she did the thing Coral was beginning to understand was the whole character of the town: she did not console, and she did not recoil. She considered.

"So the dispatches," she said slowly, "the forged one we sent downriver with Hale. The supply-depot order. It doesn't matter anymore? There's no ministry to honor it?"

"It matters more," Coral said. "Because it's the only paper of its kind left in the world. Whoever reads it downriver won't be able to check it against anything. A forgery addressed to a dead state can't be contradicted by the state. It's..." She stopped, hearing her own words arrive somewhere new. "It's the strongest document I've ever written."

"Then we did it," Tamsin said. "We beat them. The whole rotten machine, and it was already dead, and we beat it anyway."

"We survived a war that ended without us," Coral corrected, but she heard the wonder in her own voice despite herself. "There's a difference."

"There isn't, out here." Tamsin bumped her shoulder, rough and warm. "Come on. Mam will want to know everything, and she'll want it standing up, and she'll pretend she isn't pleased, and she'll be pleased."

They walked toward the workshop lights together. But at the door Coral paused, one hand on the cold iron latch, and looked back once at the dark bulk of the drying shed, and beyond it at the black ribbon of the frozen river running south toward a capital that no longer existed, toward roads full of the disowned servants of a fallen thing, clerks and couriers and soldiers all walking with cases chained to their wrists, all decoding orders from ashes.

Fifteen years of silence, and the silence had been for nothing — no. Not nothing. She turned the thought over like a board she was checking for warp. The silence had been for sale, and she had sold it cheap, to buyers who were already dead. But the silence itself, the craft of it, the cipher and the memory-work and the reading of rooms — those were hers. Those had survived the republic, the way tools survive the death of the workshop that bought them.

Tomorrow, she thought, the council would need to hear all of it — not just the fall of the republic, but the roster, the names in the looted archives, the possibility that other couriers might come up the pass in the spring wearing borrowed faces, carrying habits dressed as orders. Tomorrow there would be ledgers and debates and votes.

But tonight, standing on the threshold of the warm lit workshop with Tamsin's breath steaming beside her, Coral understood that the next message she decoded would be the first one in fifteen years addressed to the living — and that she would be the one writing it.

Inside, Odile looked up from the great bench, saw both their faces, and set down her plane.

"Sit down," she said. "Both of you. Start at the beginning."
