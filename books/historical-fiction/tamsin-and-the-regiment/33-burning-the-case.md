## 33. Burning the Case

The kiln had been cooling for two days when Coral came to it in the blue hour before dawn, and even now the air around its brick flanks shimmered faintly, like a mirage of summer standing stubborn in the middle of winter. She carried the dispatch case against her chest with both arms, the way she had carried it across the pass a lifetime ago — though the chain was gone from her wrist now. Odile's cold chisel had taken care of that three nights past, four precise taps on the rivet, and Coral had watched the links fall away into the joiner's broad palm as if watching someone else's arm being freed.

She had not slept. That was not unusual; she had gone whole campaigns on less. But this was a different kind of wakefulness than she knew — not the flat gray vigilance of the courier counting hours until dawn let her move again, but something closer to the night before a festival she'd been invited to rather than hired to work.

The snow was knee-deep in the workshop yard, unmarked except by the fox that had learned to hunt the grain stores' edges. The sky was the color of slate washed too many times, and the mountains to the east held the last of the dark along their spines. Somewhere behind her, in the sleeping town, a dog barked twice at nothing and thought better of it.

Coral stopped before the kiln and set the case down in the snow.

It was an ugly thing, when she looked at it plainly. Black oilcloth over iron ribs, scarred at the corners from a decade of baggage trains and river landings and one memorable fall down a scree slope in the Karst. The lock was republican standard, third pattern — she could have opened it blindfolded, with her teeth, in a moving cart, which she had once actually done. It weighed almost nothing. That had always been the joke of it, the private joke she and Sef had shared: the most dangerous object in any room, and light as a loaf of bread. Kingdoms had risen on heavier paperweights.

*Kingdoms,* she thought. *Republics.* The word felt strange in her head now, the way a word repeated too many times goes strange. *Republic of Vessany.* Brann's confession had been delivered in the flat voice of a man reporting weather: the capital fallen since midwinter, the Assembly scattered or dead, the armies dissolving into brigands and refugees, the war ended because there was no longer anyone left to fight it. A country was a story people agreed to tell together, and Vessany's storytellers were all dead or fled, and the story had simply stopped being told somewhere between one dispatch and the next.

Which meant, Coral understood, standing in the cold with her breath hanging silver before her, that the orders in this case had been issued by a ghost. And ghosts, Sef had taught her, were the most obedient masters of all — they never countermanded, never forgave, never released you. You served them until you decided otherwise.

*Trust no handler.* His last line. He had written it knowing what happened to couriers when the state they served collapsed: they became loose ends, and loose ends got tidied. Merrick Hale had known who she was since the ferry, had offered her survival priced in the town's blood, and had left downriver defeated by paperwork — but Hale would be replaced. There was always another Hale. Unless the papers themselves died.

She crouched and opened the case.

Inside, wrapped in waxed linen against damp, lay everything she had been for fifteen years. The first dispatch, long decoded, its cipher key still penciled faintly in the margin in her own hand: instructions to secure the militia charter. The second: the threat dressed as contingency, the town declared rebellious if it refused, handed to the enemy army like a plate of meat. And the third, the one she had read only days ago by lamplight while the council waited below in the dark — the order to burn the workshop, to salt the accusation onto the enemy's name, to erase Tamsin's Ford from the ledger of living places so that no claim of theirs could ever be honored. Arroyo's cipher, third variant, the one with the transposed vowels that had cost her a headache and half a night.

There were other things too. A letter of introduction under a name she hadn't used since she was twenty-two. Three blank warrants, signed in advance by an undersecretary whose signature she had practiced until she could forge it better than he wrote it himself. A thin folio of names — safehouses, contacts, other couriers, some of whom might still be alive, might still be running, carrying cases just like this one through a country that no longer existed to receive them.

That folio gave her the longest pause. She knelt in the snow with it open on her knees for a long time, reading names she knew, faces rising behind each one. Marek, who taught her the dead-drop under the bridge at Ilvane. The twins from the coast road whose real names she had never learned. Sef, at the top of the first page, in his own handwriting, the list begun decades before her, amended and crossed out and amended again like a ledger of the living kept by a man who expected most entries to end badly.

*They're free now too,* she told herself. *Whether they know it or not.*

But that wasn't quite true, and she had promised herself, kneeling in the dark of the council chamber with all of them watching her, that she was done lying — to the town, and worse, to herself. Some of those names belonged to people who would keep serving the ghost. Who would burn towns on the ghost's orders, if the orders came. The kindest thing she could do for them was destroy the network's spine, here, now, in the only place she had ever chosen to stand.

Still, her hands hesitated. Fifteen years of discipline does not dissolve in a week, however warm the walls around it. Sef's voice lived in her fingers: *A courier protects the case before her skin. The case is the mission. The mission is the state. The state is—*

*The state is ash,* she finished, aloud, and the words made little clouds in the cold. *I've seen it. I'm looking at it.*

She fed the folio into the kiln first.

The fire inside had settled to a deep orange bed, banked two days ago after the last firing of the season's wares, hot enough still to catch paper with a sound like a startled bird. The pages blackened at the edges, curled, bloomed briefly into flame, and were gone. Names she had risked her life to memorize, gone in the time it took to draw one breath. She felt the loss and the relief arrive together, twinned, indistinguishable.

Then the warrants. Then the letter of introduction. Then, one at a time, holding each a moment as if honoring a fallen soldier — no. Not that. Not honor. Acknowledgment, maybe. The way you nod to an opponent across a table. — then, one at a time, the dispatches themselves.

The first went easily. The charter order. She had already read it aloud to the council; its secrets were spent; it burned like any other old letter.

The second resisted her more, though the paper did not know that. The second was the one that had named this town expendable, and her hand shook slightly as she fed it in — not from fear but from the effort of letting go of the evidence. Part of her wanted to keep it, wanted proof, wanted the council to be able to hold the actual page that had condemned them and understand viscerally what had stood over them all winter. But the archive-keeper, Marta, had copied it faithfully into the town's own records two nights ago, in the council's presence, and had said something afterward that Coral kept turning over: *We don't need their paper to know what they meant to do. We need our own, to remember we survived it.* The town kept its own books. It always had. That was the whole point of the Regiment.

So the second dispatch burned, and the words declaring Tamsin's Ford a permissible loss curled up orange and vanished, and the town remained, snow-covered and breathing, its chimneys beginning to send up their morning smoke behind her.

The third dispatch she saved for last, and she sat back on her heels in the snow and held it and did not read it again. She knew every word. She would know them for years; some knowledge doesn't burn. *Ensure the workshop is rendered unusable by hostile action. Casualties among the artisan population will strengthen the narrative.* She had read that line to Odile by lamplight and watched the old woman's face do something very quiet and very terrible, the face of a master craftsman examining a joint put together by someone who didn't care whether it held.

Casualties among the artisan population. Tamsin's laugh. Odile's hands. Old Ferren at the ferry, and his terrible tea. The children she had started teaching cipher just that week, little Wren with her quick fingers and her ferocious concentration. All of it reducible, on this page, to a strengthening narrative.

"Here's your narrative," Coral said to the paper, and dropped it in the fire.

It caught. The waxed linen wrapper followed. Then she took up the empty case itself — the iron-ribbed, oilcloth-skinned, decade-faithful case — and considered it.

Logically, it should be kept. Marta had suggested as much: an artifact, a record, something for the archive. And part of Coral agreed; she believed in records now, believed in ledgers and minutes and names written down where empires couldn't rub them out. But the case was different from its contents. The case was the shape of her service. Every morning for fifteen years she had strapped it on the way a soldier straps on a weapon, and the body remembers. If it hung in the archive, she would feel it there, feel its weight at her wrist forever, a prosthesis of the old life.

Besides — and this was the thought that finally decided her, arriving with the simple clarity of a well-cut joint sliding home — a courier's case, found intact, invites questions. Invites hunters. Let it be understood, wherever Hale's replacements eventually looked, that the courier Okonkwo had destroyed everything and gone to ground. Let the file close itself.

She worked the hinges open wide and pressed the whole black shell down into the coals.

Oilcloth takes flame reluctantly and then completely. The iron ribs would stay, she knew — a skeleton in the ashes, which seemed right somehow. She watched the fabric char and split and glow, watched ten years of Vessany become heat and smoke, and she stayed crouched there until the last of the cloth crumbled, because it felt dishonest to walk away early. You stayed for the whole of a burning. That, too, was craft.

The sun cleared the eastern ridge while she worked, striking the snowfields above the town so that the whole bowl of the valley lit up at once, white going gold. Smoke from forty chimneys rose straight in the windless cold. Down the lane, someone was splitting wood — she could hear the rhythmic crack of it, unhurried, the sound of a person doing a chore in a town that had voted to defer its own peril rather than surrender it, and had won, and was now simply splitting wood on an ordinary morning.

Coral realized she was crying.

It crept up on her without ceremony — not the racking grief of the purge-letter night, when she had learned Sef was dead, but something quieter and stranger, tears that arrived the way warmth arrives into a cold house, gradually and then all at once. She let them come. Her face was numb with cold anyway; no one would see; and she had learned in this town that feelings survived being witnessed. That was practically the Regiment's founding principle.

She grieved, she understood, for the girl who had learned courier craft in a rented room above a dye shop, who had been taught that invisibility was safety and silence was strength, who had believed it because believing it hurt less than the alternative. Sef had loved her — she knew that now, knew it the way you know things about the dead, too late to matter and just in time to matter enormously — and love had not stopped him from teaching her to disappear, because he genuinely believed disappearance was mercy. Maybe it had been, once. But mercy practiced too long becomes habit, and habit becomes a cage with very comfortable bars, and she had lived in that cage so long she'd called it freedom.

The fire popped. An iron rib sagged and settled. Coral wiped her face with a mittened hand, rough wool against cold skin, and felt — searched for the word, tested several, discarded *lightness* as too easy and *relief* as too small — felt *unbound*. Unstrung, like a bow unbraced after a long campaign. Not empty. Resting. A tool put down at the end of its work, not thrown away, not lost, just — finished.

She thought of what she would say to Odile, because Odile would ask, in her blunt way, whether she regretted it, and Coral wanted to have an honest answer ready. Here it was: no. And yes. She regretted nothing in the fire, and she mourned everything that had made the fire necessary, and both of those things were true, and the town — this was the miracle of the town — the town had room for both truths in its books. Debts and credits, losses and gains, entered in the same steady hand.

Footsteps crunched in the snow behind her.

She didn't startle. That was new, too — or not new, she corrected herself, but changed. A month ago she would have registered the footsteps, catalogued the gait, mapped two exits, and arranged her face into harmlessness, all within the space of a heartbeat, without any conscious thought at all. Now she simply turned, slow and unhurried, and saw Tamsin coming up the lane with a basket on her hip and her breath steaming and her sharp eyes finding the burning case in the kiln's mouth immediately, because Tamsin missed nothing, had never once missed anything about Coral, from the very first day.

"You started without me." No reproach in it — or rather, reproach worn lightly, the way this family wore everything, like a coat you could take off.

"I wanted to do the names alone," Coral said. "The ones in Sef's book. It seemed right."

Tamsin came and stood beside her, shoulder almost touching hers, and looked into the coals. The basket smelled of fresh bread and woodsmoke; Odile's household ran on bread the way other households ran on water. For a while neither spoke. The iron ribs glowed down in the ash like the ribs of a small burnt ship.

"It's ugly," Tamsin said at last. "The burning. Everyone thinks it must feel wonderful and it mostly feels ugly."

"Yes." Coral studied her. "You've burned something."

"Our old sign, when Father redid it. I was twelve and I'd painted the original and I cried over the fire like an idiot." She shrugged. "Odile said a thing I've never forgotten. She said, *the wood isn't the work. The work is in your hands now.*"

Coral turned that over, and found it fit her hand exactly, the way Odile's tools always did. *The wood isn't the work.* The dispatches weren't her. Fifteen years, and they had never actually been her — only the material she'd been given, badly milled, full of hidden knots. What she had made of herself in spite of them — the memory-craft, the patience, the precision, the capacity to hold secrets without being hollowed by them — none of that was in the fire. All of that was standing in the snow beside an apprentice with a basket of bread.

"The work is in my hands now," she said, and heard how it sounded, and Tamsin heard it too, and grinned at her sideways, that quick sharp grin that had undone more of Coral's caution than any interrogation ever managed.

"Come inside before you freeze," Tamsin said. "Marta wants to show you how she'll enter today in the ledger — she says there's a proper formula for the destruction of instruments of foreign states, can you believe there's a *formula*, she found it in a book from before the Founding — and Odile wants you at the bench by midmorning because the refugee bunks won't build themselves, and—" she paused, and something moved under her briskness, something careful, "—and there's a rider on the downriver road. Ferren spotted him at first light. Alone, no colors, riding easy. Could be a trader. Could be nothing."

Could be nothing. Coral felt the old machinery begin to turn — assess, catalogue, map the exits — and let it run for exactly one breath, and then set it down, deliberately, like a tool returned to its rack. Whatever came down that road, it would find no courier at Tamsin's Ford. It would find a member of the Regiment, entered in the ledger, debts and all, with sawdust in her hair and cipher-lessons to teach and a bunk to build.

But it might be someone looking for the woman who had burned the republic's last orders. And the town deserved to know what was coming while it was still far off.

"Ahead of him?" she asked.

Tamsin's grin sharpened. "Ferren's boy is already saddled, riding the ridge path. We keep our own books." She shifted the basket to her other hip and nodded at the kiln, at the ash, at the small black ship's-ribs cooling in the fire. "Is it done?"

Coral looked a long moment. The flames had gone back to their deep orange bed. The snow around the kiln was printed with her knees and her boots and nothing else — no chain-links, no lock, no case. In a year the ash would be raked out and spread on somebody's garden, and the iron would rust quietly in the scrap pile, and that would be the whole of the Republic of Vessany's claim on her: garden soil and rust.

"It's done," she said.

And she picked up the other end of the basket, because that was how things were carried here, together, and walked with Tamsin toward the smell of bread and the sound of splitting wood, while behind them the kiln breathed its slow heat into the morning, keeping its fire for whatever the town would ask of it next.
