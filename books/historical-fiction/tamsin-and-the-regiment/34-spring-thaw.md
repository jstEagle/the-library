## 34. Spring Thaw

The ice went out on the Vessel on the ninth morning of Thawmonth, and Tamsin heard it before she understood it.

She was at the bench early — earlier than Odile liked anyone to be, because the first hour belonged to sharpening and to silence — when a sound came up through the floorboards of the workshop. Not a crack exactly. A groan. Long and low and patient, like the river had been holding its breath all winter and had finally decided to let it go.

She put down her plane. The shaving curled on the bench in front of her sat perfectly still, and she watched it for a moment as though it might tell her something, and then she ran. Out the door, across the yard where the frost had gone soft and grey overnight, past the tannery sheds with their smell of bark and salt, down the path that switchbacked toward the water. Her boots slipped twice on mud that hadn't been mud since Harvestmonth. She didn't care. She could hear other people running too — doors banging along Mill Row, someone shouting, children's voices high and bright as gulls.

At the bend where the town's path met the old ferry landing, Tamsin stopped and looked at the river, and the river looked back at her like something newly born.

All winter it had lain under its lid of white, solid enough to walk, solid enough to cart timber across if you were fool enough or desperate enough. Now it was breaking itself into pieces. Great plates of ice ground against one another and tipped and sank; slabs the size of workshop tables rode the current downstream, spinning slow, catching the early sun so that the whole river seemed scattered with broken mirrors. The sound was enormous and intimate at once — a grinding, a booming, a hiss of water finding every gap — and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, came the creak of the old ferry rope going taut as the current leaned on it.

"Spring," said Ferro beside her, who must have run from his house half-dressed, because he was wearing one boot and a blanket like a mantle. He said the word the way other men swore. "Well. There it is."

Tamsin found she was grinning. She couldn't help it. The whole town seemed to be arriving at the landing by ones and twos, breath steaming, faces lit with the particular joy of people who have survived a thing together and are permitted, now, to be glad about it. Old Mistress Halloway stood at the water's edge and threw a handful of last year's dried lavender onto an ice floe as it passed, which was either a blessing or a funeral and was probably both.

And then Tamsin thought of Coral, and the grin faltered.

Because spring meant the road. Spring meant the pass over the shoulder of Mount Cerrin drying out, meant the bridge timbers repaired or the ferry running again, meant the world beyond the Ford opening its arms to anyone who wished to leave it. And Coral had come here in autumn with a case chained to her wrist and a face like a locked drawer, and everyone in town knew — knew now, after Midwinter Assembly, after the lamplit confession, after Brann and Hale and the fire watch — what that case had held and what Coral had chosen to do about it.

But knowing a thing in winter was different from watching the door open in spring.

* * *

Coral did not come down to the landing that morning.

Tamsin noticed. Of course she noticed; she'd been noticing Coral Okonkwo for the better part of a year now, in the way you notice weather, in the way you notice a splinter working its way toward the surface of your skin. She scanned the crowd twice without letting herself look like she was scanning, and Coral wasn't there, and Tamsin told herself it meant nothing, and then spent the rest of the day failing to believe it.

"She's at the bench," Odile said that evening, not looking up from the joint she was fitting. They were in the workshop, mother and daughter, doing the quiet end-of-day accounting that Odile performed with shavings and glue-pot instead of ink. "Been there since dawn. Cut three tenons and ruined two of them."

"That's not like her. She's gotten good."

"She's gotten *careful*, which is better." Odile tapped the joint home with the flat of her chisel handle, a soft wooden knock. "Careful is what you want in a joiner. In a person, I'm less sure." She straightened and looked at Tamsin over the bench, and there was something in her face that Tamsin had learned to read the way she read grain — the long lines of it visible only if you looked along them, never across. "You're going to go talk to her."

"I hadn't decided."

"You'd decided before you finished the sentence in your head." Odile wiped her hands on her apron. "Go on, then. And Tamsin—" Tamsin paused at the door. "Whatever she chooses, let it be hers. That's the hardest joinery there is. Two pieces that stay together because they *choose* to hold."

Tamsin walked back across the yard in the blue dusk with her hands jammed in her pockets and her heart doing something inconvenient against her ribs.

The workshop was lit. One lamp, low, the way Coral always kept it — enough light to work by, never enough to be seen by. Tamsin stood outside the door a moment and listened to the small sounds within: the whisper of a plane, the pause, the whisper again. A rhythm she knew. A rhythm she had helped teach, sitting beside this woman through a winter of badly-cut dovetails and laughter that Coral produced so rarely it always startled them both, like a bird landing on a windowsill.

She knocked, though the door was open, though knocking was absurd between two people who had confessed crimes to each other by kiln-light. Habit, maybe. Or ceremony. Some things deserved marking.

"Come in, Tamsin." Coral's voice, dry and even. "You knock louder when you're worried."

"I don't knock loud ever."

"You knock loud when you're worried about me specifically. It's a distinct knock. I've catalogued it." Coral set down her work and turned on her stool, and the lamp caught the side of her face, and Tamsin saw that she had been crying. Not recently. The kind of crying that happened earlier, alone, and had been carefully packed away — the eyes a little swollen, the voice a little too level. Fifteen years of courier craft lived in how well Coral could hide a thing. Eleven months of friendship lived in how Tamsin could see it anyway.

"The river broke," Tamsin said.

"I heard it from here. Sounded like the world coming apart." Coral smiled, thin. "Or being made. Hard to tell the difference sometimes."

"Coral—"

"The pass will be open inside the month. Ferro says the first ferry runs when the current drops below the second mark on his post, and the melt's fast this year, so—" She stopped herself, the way she always stopped herself, mid-calculation, as if she could catch the arithmetic of escape before it finished and pretend it hadn't started. Her hand had drifted, without her seeming to notice, to her left wrist. The wrist where the chain used to sit. It had been off for weeks now — burned dispatches, burned chain, the whole apparatus of her service surrendered to the kiln fire — and still, when she was frightened, her fingers went there and found bare skin and seemed surprised by it.

"The road out reopens," Tamsin said quietly. "That's what you're telling me."

"That's what the river's telling me. I'm just the messenger." Coral laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "I've always hated being the messenger."

Tamsin crossed the room and sat down on the stool beside her, the apprentice stool, the one with Coral's initials burned discreetly into the underside where Odile pretended not to know they were. For a while neither of them spoke. The lamp hissed. Outside, far off, the river kept up its grinding conversation with itself.

"I keep waiting," Coral said at last, "to feel relieved."

"Relieved?"

"That it's over. The war, the republic, Hale, the codes, all of it. The country I served doesn't exist anymore — Brann confirmed it, and the refugees will confirm it again when they arrive, and every week some new piece of news comes downriver to say the same thing: it fell, it fell, it fell, and nobody won." She turned her hands over, palms up, studying them as though they belonged to a stranger. Tamsin had watched those hands all winter — learning saw and chisel, learning patience, learning the particular humility of sanding something smooth for an hour only to have Odile find the flaw in four seconds. They were steadier hands than they'd been in autumn. "I should feel free. Instead I wake up every morning and count the exits."

"And how many are there?"

"Three. The road, the river, and the lie." Coral's mouth twisted. "Sef taught me that. Three ways out of any room, and you should know all of them before you finish entering. Fifteen years, Tamsin. Do you understand? Fifteen years I entered every room in the republic as someone leaving it. Every friendship was cover. Every kindness was tradecraft. I was *good* at it." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious. "I was so good at it that when your mother offered me a bench last autumn, my first thought — my honest first thought — was *what does she want*. When you brought me bread on my third night, I checked it for—"

She stopped.

"For poison?" Tamsin said gently.

"For notes. Written between the slices." Coral pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "You gave me bread, and I took it apart looking for orders. That's what they made me. That's what fifteen years of *never be remembered* makes of a person. And now the road is open, and I can feel myself already composing the departure — the harmless traveler moves on, no one remembers her face — and the worst part, the part I can't say aloud even now, is that some cold little clerk in the back of my skull has already drafted it. Has the route planned. Has the story ready for the first inn."

Tamsin reached over and took Coral's hands away from her eyes, and held them, and felt them tremble like something small trapped under ice.

"So don't go," she said.

"It isn't that simple."

"It's exactly that simple. It's the simplest joinery there is. You cut the piece, you fit the piece, you decide it belongs, and then you *keep* it." Tamsin heard her own voice getting fierce and didn't soften it. "You want to know what I learned this year, watching you and watching Brann and watching the whole rotten parade? Leaving is easy. Brann left. Hale left. My father left before I could walk, and the republic left us the moment we stopped being useful. Everybody leaves. Staying—" she squeezed Coral's hands, "staying is the hard cut. Staying is where the grain fights you and the wood checks and the glue fails and you have to start the joint over, and over, and you do it anyway, because the piece matters more than your comfort. Anyone can walk out a door, Coral. Walking *in* one and bolting it behind you — that takes nerve. That's the courage nobody writes songs about."

Coral stared at her. The lamp flame wavered in a draft from under the door, and shadows moved across her face like water.

"You make it sound like craftsmanship."

"Everything worth doing is craftsmanship. Mother taught me that. You're just the first person who taught me it applies to people."

For a long moment Coral said nothing. Then she laughed — really laughed, wet and helpless, the rare bird landing — and pulled one hand free to scrub at her face.

"A year ago," she said, "a handler told me I'd die the day I stopped moving. That stillness was death for people like me. And here I am, still, in a town full of witnesses, with my name in your ledger and sawdust in places sawdust should never reach, and I have never in my life felt more—" she hunted for the word, rejected three, settled on none, and simply shook her head. "He was wrong. Sef was wrong about that too, in the end. All my teachers, wrong about the same thing."

"What thing?"

"That invisibility was safety." Coral looked around the workshop — at the benches, at the lumber racked against the wall, at the half-finished cradle for the Aldous baby that she'd been secretly sanding smooth every evening, thinking no one knew. "Being seen and staying anyway. That's the only shelter that holds. You taught me that. Your mother taught me that. This whole stubborn, bookkeeping, peg-driving town taught me that."

Outside, the river boomed. Somewhere upstream, another great plate of ice gave way, and the grinding rose and fell like breathing.

* * *

They walked down to the landing the next morning, together, deliberately, the way you walk toward a thing instead of away from it.

The whole town had the same idea. Thaw was a festival in the Ford whether anyone decreed it or not — the day the river opened was the day the year truly began, and everyone turned out to watch the last of the ice ride seaward. Ferro had his boat ready, tarred over winter, and was taking wagers on which floe would clear the bend first. Children hurled pebbles at the ice to hear them ring. Mistress Halloway threw more lavender. Somebody had brought a fiddle, because somebody always did.

Coral stood at the rail of the old ferry dock with the current pulling past below her feet, and Tamsin stood beside her, and Odile stood on Coral's other side, and the three of them watched the river carry winter away.

"There it goes," Odile said. "Road's open. Pass'll be dry in three weeks. Ferry runs when Ferro grows a spine." She said it plainly, without weight, the way she said everything — and that plainness was itself a gift, Tamsin knew. No pressure. No plea. Just the facts of the world, laid out like stock on a bench, for Coral to take up or leave.

Coral was quiet a long time. Tamsin watched her watch the water — watched her do the old thing, the counting thing, her eyes tracking the current the way they once tracked exits — and then watched the counting stop. Watched her shoulders come down half an inch. Watched her turn from the river as if closing a ledger.

"You know what I keep thinking about?" Coral said. "The bunks."

"Bunks?" Tamsin blinked.

"For the refugees. Downriver word says two hundred souls out of the capital, maybe more, walking north with whatever they carried. They'll follow the river. The river leads here." Coral's voice had changed — steadied, purposeful, the voice Tamsin had heard the night she laid her codes out before the council by lamplight. "Two hundred people need beds by summer, and the Regiment keeps its own books, and I've read enough of those books now to know what this town does when the world breaks open at its door." She looked at Odile. "I've been sketching bunk frames. Stackable, so we can fit them in the meeting hall. Mortise-and-tenon throughout, no nails, so they can be struck and stored after. I'd need help cutting them."

Odile's face did the thing it almost never did in public — softened, entirely, like ice going out all at once.

"Fourteen frames," she said. "I had the same sketches in my head since Harvestmonth. We'll need to order pine downriver before the prices rise." She paused, and the corner of her mouth moved. "Unless you were planning to be elsewhere when the delivery came."

Coral looked at the river one last time — at the last floes riding out toward the low country, spinning in the sun, carrying the winter and everything frozen in it away — and then she turned her back on the water, which was, Tamsin understood, the bravest thing she had ever seen anyone do.

"No," Coral said. "I know exactly where I'll be. I'll be at the bench."

Behind them, the fiddle struck up. Ahead of them, up the hill, the workshop waited with its lamps and its lumber and its long lists of work. And somewhere downriver, invisible but certain as the current, two hundred strangers were walking toward a town that would take them in — walking toward beds that didn't exist yet, frames uncut, joints unfitted, a whole season's labor not yet begun.

Tamsin caught Coral's eye and grinned.

"Well," she said. "We'd better get started."
