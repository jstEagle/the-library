## 35. The Ledger Settled

The summons came on a morning so clear that the mountains seemed close enough to touch. Tamsin brought it up the stairs to the loft two days before the assembly was to convene, and she did not bring it gently; she brought it the way she brought everything, at speed and slightly out of breath.

"Council sits Thursday," she said, dropping onto Coral's bed as though it were her own, which by now it very nearly was. "Full council. You're summoned."

"Summoned." Coral set down the chisel she had been honing. Her hands, these days, bore the marks of honest work—a healing nick along the thumb, calluses hardening at the base of each finger—and they were not quite steady. "People aren't summoned to councils here. People are *asked*. I've watched you do it for months."

"You're being asked," Tamsin amended, grinning. "Formally. Odile says you'll want to dress like it matters. I told her you'd want to dress like it doesn't. She said we were both wrong and you'd wear whatever was cleanest."

Odile, as usual, was right. Coral stood before the small cracked mirror in the loft that evening and understood, for the first time in fifteen years, that she did not know how to dress for a thing that mattered. Every garment she owned had been chosen for erasure—the greys and browns and faded blues of a woman who could walk through any market in Vessany without one eye turning toward her. She had built herself out of nothing-on-purpose, and now the nothing was being asked to stand up in front of a room and have a name.

She wore the blue. The good blue, the one Tamsin had dyed with woad from the eastern plots two seasons back, deep as dusk over the river. It mattered, and she let it matter, and her hands shook a little while she buttoned the collar, and no one saw, which was its own kind of miracle.

* * *

The council chamber above the workshop had been built, like everything in Tamsin's Ford, by people who expected their work to be argued with. The benches were plain oak but beautifully joined—Coral could read the maker's hand in every dovetail now, could feel Odile's own early work in the long table where the seven councilors sat. Lamplight pooled yellow on the wood. Beyond the windows the last light drained off the western peaks, and somewhere below, the town went about its evening: hammer-taps from Harrow's smithy, the smell of bread from the communal ovens, children's voices rising from the lane like birds startled out of hedgerows.

The gallery was full. That was the first thing that undid her—not the formality, not the gravity, but the fact that half the town had simply come. There was nothing to watch, really. No spectacle. A woman being entered into a book. And still they came, filling the benches, because in the Regiment a thing done formally was a thing done together, and being together was not an occasion to be missed.

She found Tamsin's mother's old seat near the rail—no, not Tamsin's mother, she corrected herself, Odile's sister Maret, dead nine years, whose bench Tamsin had claimed by right of grief—and sat, and folded her hands, and waited to find out what she had agreed to.

Master Odile Vant presided. She did not stand for it; she simply let the room settle around her the way water settles around a stone. Beside her sat the archive-keeper, old Wenna Ilse, who had spent thirty years maintaining the town's records through flood, fire-scare, and the slow rot of the republic downriver, and who looked tonight as though she had dressed for a funeral and decided at the last moment it might be a wedding.

"Recorded business," Odile said. Her voice carried without effort; Coral knew now that this was joinery too, that the acoustics of the room had been shaped by Odile's grandfather with wedges and patience. "Item one. The petition of Coral Okonkwo, lately of the Republic of Vessany, for entry into the Regiment as a member in good standing, with all rights, obligations, and debts appertaining."

*Debts.* The word landed in Coral's chest like a dropped chisel. She had known it would be there—Tamsin had warned her, Odile had been blunt about it over supper—but knowing and hearing were different countries.

"Before the record is made," Odile continued, "the petitioner will speak. Briefly. We are joiners, not orators."

A ripple of laughter ran through the room, warm and unkind to no one. Coral rose. Her knees felt borrowed. She walked to the open floor before the long table and turned to face not the council but the gallery, because that was where the town actually lived, and because fifteen years of reading rooms told her that power sat at tables but truth sat in benches.

"I don't know most of the proper words," she began, and her voice held, mostly. "I've spent my adult life learning words that leave no mark. Ciphers that burn. Names that belong to no one. I came to your bridge in autumn carrying papers that would have cost you your charter, your militia rolls, and by midwinter—" she paused, because the next part still cost her something to say aloud, even now, even here, "—by midwinter, possibly more than that. I was the instrument of that. Not the author. But instruments don't get to claim innocence either. An instrument that knows what it does is just a hand with extra steps."

Silence, but the listening kind. In the Regiment she had learned there were many kinds of silence, and only one of them was empty.

"You took me in anyway. Not because you knew—I hid well, hiding is the one craft I'm genuinely excellent at—but because a traveler caught by the snow is a traveler owed shelter. That law saved my life before anyone in this room decided I deserved saving. I have thought about that all winter. That your kindness preceded your knowledge of me, and survived it." Her throat tightened. She let it. "I burned the orders that would have destroyed you. I forged others in their place. Those acts are in your record already, entered by this council's own vote, and I won't relitigate them. I only want the record to hold all of me. Not the cleaned version. The whole ledger."

"That," said Wenna Ilse dryly, dipping her pen, "is the only kind of entry we accept."

More laughter, gentler now. Coral looked up at the gallery and found Tamsin's eyes, bright and steady, and found next to her the seamstress Marta whose cousin Coral had pretended to be, wearing an expression of vast theatrical satisfaction, as though the entire performance had been Marta's idea and she intended to take credit for it at market for the rest of her natural life.

"The petition includes declaration of assets," Odile said, when the room had settled. "State them."

"I have one skill worth naming." Coral said it plainly. "Cipher-work, memory-craft, the reading of rooms and documents. Everything I know about disappearing, and some things about appearing. I've begun teaching the children, and I mean to continue. I have wages saved from winter bench-work—eleven crowns, four bits. My tools are the workshop's already; Odile holds them against my apprenticeship fees. And I have—" she hesitated, then finished it, "—I have knowledge of roads south and east, and of people along them, some of whom owe me favors I intend to spend carefully, if ever this town needs spending done."

"Noted," said Wenna, writing.

"And liabilities." Odile's voice flattened into the register Coral privately called the bench-voice, the one she used when a joint wouldn't sit true and flattery was wasted on the wood. "Read them."

Wenna read. Coral listened to her own account of herself rendered in ink:

"*Owed to the Regiment: one season's lodging and board at the Vant household, valued at eighteen crowns, tendered against apprenticeship. One share of roof timber expended in the storm repair, valued at six crowns, tendered against wages. One debt of conscience, unvalued, arising from services rendered to the Republic of Vessany prior to arrival, the nature of which the council has heard in closed session and judges discharged by acts since performed within these walls.*"

The last item made the gallery stir. Coral kept her eyes on the table's grain. They had judged it. Months ago, in the lamplit dark after her confession, they had weighed what she had been and what she had done, and they had ruled the scales balanced—not absolved, *balanced*, which was a different thing and a harder one, the way a well-made joint is not invisible but true.

"Against these liabilities," Wenna continued, "*credited: service rendered in the exposure of the agent Merrick Hale and the councilor Brann Ostrow; service rendered in the prevention of arson against the workshop; service rendered in the procurement of documents securing the town's charter against republican seizure; service rendered in the instruction of the town's children, ongoing. Net standing upon entry: debtor in the amount of twenty-four crowns, eleven bits, terms of repayment to be set by the master to whom the debtor is apprenticed.*"

"Which is me," said Odile, "and the terms are: work."

Laughter again, bigger this time. Someone in the back—Harrow, by the sound—called out, "Twenty-four crowns! At bench rates that's what, five years?"

"At her current rate," Odile said gravely, "seven. She sands like a scholar."

"I sand like someone who was never taught," Coral said, "which is the same thing, and you know it, and you charge me double out of spite."

"I charge everyone double out of spite. Ask anyone." Odile let the laughter run its course, then raised one hand and the room came quiet as falling snow. "Council votes on the petition. All in favor of entering Coral Okonkwo into the Regiment as a member in good standing, with debts and credits as read."

Seven hands rose. Coral had expected it—had been told, in the blunt way of this town, that it would pass—but expectation and witness were different countries too, and she stood very still while it happened, the way you stand still when a wound that has hurt for years finally stops.

"All opposed?"

No hands.

"Abstentions? None. So entered." Odile nodded to Wenna, and the old archive-keeper rose, and the room rose with her, and Coral understood that what came next was the actual ceremony, and that everything before had merely been the fitting of the joints.

* * *

They brought the book forward.

It was not large. That surprised her, though it should not have; she had learned by now that the Regiment distrusted anything that existed mainly to be impressive. It was a ledger bound in oiled leather gone dark with handling, its corners worn round, its spine repaired at least twice with strips of newer leather stitched over old. It fit in Wenna's two hands like a loaf of bread. But when Wenna set it on the reading stand and opened it, the pages breathed out the smell of a century—iron-gall ink and beeswax and the faint sweet ghost of the tannin in the paper itself—and Coral felt, absurdly, the way she had felt at eight years old opening her grandmother's chest of linens: that she was touching time laid flat and pressed.

"This volume begins in the year the ford was cut," Wenna said, turning leaves with fingers that handled each one like a bird's wing. "The first page names Tamsin Okoro, for whom the crossing is named, and the eleven families who signed the first covenant. Since then, every member of the Regiment has been entered here. Births. Deaths. Debts forgiven and debts honored. The republic has fallen"—she said it steadily, without bitterness, the way you report weather—"and the kingdoms east of the mountains change their names every generation, and none of it appears in this book except as it touched us. Empires don't get pages here. People do."

She wet the pen. She looked at Coral.

"Your name as you wish it written."

And there it was again, that question, the one that had followed her down every road of her life in one disguise or another. *Who are you.* She had answered it falsely to border guards and innkeepers and lovers; she had answered it not at all to Sef, who had taught her that the true answer was a liability no courier could afford; she had answered it finally, terribly, to this council in the dark with the lamps turned low. Every answer had cost her something. This one, she suspected, was the only one that would give something back.

"Coral Okonkwo," she said. "Spelled as it sounds. Nothing added. Nothing hidden."

Wenna wrote, and the nib whispered, and Coral watched her own name go down onto the line beneath a hundred years of neighbors—beneath Maret Vant, beneath old Tam Brightwater the ferryman who had refused her crossing in the first week of her trap and thereby, though he never learned it, saved the town; beneath children born in spring and elders buried in fall; beneath the whole slow patient population of people who had decided, over and over, across a century of floods and wars and collapsing bridges, to remain accountable to one another.

Below the name, Wenna wrote the date. Below the date: *Entered by unanimous vote of council. Debts as stated.*

Then she sanded the ink, blotted it, and closed the book, and the room exhaled.

What happened afterward was not in any protocol. It never is. The gallery simply came down off the benches and surrounded her—Marta gripping her arms, Harrow clapping her shoulder hard enough to stagger, the miller's twins hanging off her sleeves demanding to know if she was rich now that she was official (she was not; she was poorer; she owed twenty-four crowns), and Tamsin arriving last through the crush with the expression of someone who has won a bet with herself and will never admit it.

"Well," Tamsin said. "Member."

"Debtor," Coral corrected.

"Member-debtor. Very prestigious." But Tamsin's voice caught slightly on the middle of it, and her eyes were shining in the lamp-light, and she hugged Coral quickly and fiercely and said, into her ear, where no one else could hear: "I told them in closed session I'd vouch for you with my place in the book if they hesitated. They didn't hesitate. But I'd have done it."

"I know," Coral said. And she did know, and the knowing was the strangest wealth she had ever carried.

Later—much later, after the chamber had emptied and the lamps had been trimmed and Odile had gone downstairs muttering about tomorrow's glue-pot—Coral climbed back up to the gallery alone, where Wenna was locking the ledger in its cedar chest. She watched the old woman turn the key.

"How long will that book survive?" Coral asked. It was not a small question. She had spent her life around documents built to die—ciphers meant to be burned, orders meant to be swallowed, whole archives of a republic now ash in the capital. She wanted, suddenly and completely, to know what permanence looked like when it was done honestly.

Wenna considered. "The paper's rag, sized well. The chest is cedar; keeps out the damp and the bugs. When the binding fails, we resew it. When the ink fades, we recopy—there's a copy of the first hundred years in the cellar, and one buried in lead under the kiln yard besides, because Odile's grandmother didn't trust the cellar in a flood year." She patted the lid once, affectionately, the way you'd pat a horse. "Books last, girl, if somebody decides they're worth the trouble of lasting. That's the whole secret. Same as bridges."

Downstairs, the workshop was dark, but through its windows Coral could see the river below the town, black and moving, carrying ice-floes down from the high country toward the thawing lowlands. The road out was open now. Had been for weeks. Wagons would come with it, eventually, as they always did in spring—traders, pilgrims, the displaced, the curious, the desperate.

And with the wagons, she thought, standing alone in the lamplight with her name newly inked into a book that would outlast every empire she had ever served—news. News of what remained downriver. News, perhaps, of particular people, particular faces she had left behind in other towns in other years, people she had passed silently through on the way to missions she no longer believed in.

The thought arrived quietly, the way important thoughts do, and settled into her chest like a peg driven home.

Somewhere down that river, the world was knocking. And for the first time in her life, Coral Okonkwo—member, debtor, apprentice, neighbor—would be here when it knocked, with her name in the book and her debts on the page and nowhere else she needed to be.

She put out the lamp and went down to bed.
