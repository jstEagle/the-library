## 36. Apprentice

The morning Odile Vant asked her the question, Coral was sanding a drawer front she had ruined twice already.

The workshop smelled of pine resin and hide glue warming on the stove, and the light came slantwise through the east windows in long dusty blades that turned the sawdust hanging in the air into something like weather — a private snowfall, indoors, gentle. Coral had learned to love that light over the winter without ever quite admitting it to herself. It was the kind of light that made even mistakes look almost forgivable.

Almost. She ran her thumb along the edge of the drawer front and felt the hollow where she had sanded through the veneer for the second time. Her first instinct, old and deep as a scar, was to set the piece aside and say nothing. Let it disappear. Let no one remember she had touched it. Fifteen years of craft had gone into making herself unmemorable, and it turned out those same instincts were fatal at a joiner's bench, where every error lived in the wood like a name carved in bark.

"You've done it again," Odile said, from behind her.

Coral didn't startle. She had stopped being able to startle around this woman months ago; Odile moved through rooms with a weight that announced itself on the floorboards, deliberately, the way a good teacher enters a sleeping classroom. "Yes," Coral said. "I have."

"And your plan?"

"Plan?" Coral turned the board over, considering its grain, its flaws, its history. "There's a knot near the bottom corner. I could inlay something over the thin spot. A small flower. Or a star."

Odile came around the bench and looked, not at the mistake but at Coral's hands — the way she held the board, the way her thumb kept moving across the damaged place like a tongue returning to a broken tooth. "A patch to hide it," Odile said. "Or you could take it apart, re-veneer the face, and do it properly. That would cost you two days."

"Two days," Coral said.

"Two days," Odile agreed. "You'd miss the assembly on Thursday, or come late and dusty. Tamsin will tease you. Brann's empty chair won't be filled yet, and people will talk about it while you're not there to hear."

It was a test. Coral understood that now; nearly everything Odile said was a test, delivered flat as a level laid against a wall. The old Coral — the courier, the ghost, the woman who had crossed borders inside other people's names — would have smiled, apologized charmingly, and quietly hidden the drawer front under the bench until it could be slipped into someone else's pile of work. A week ago she might still have done it.

Instead she said, "I'll take it apart."

Something shifted in Odile's face. Not a smile exactly — Odile's smiles were rare enough to be events, recorded in the town ledger of small wonders — but a loosening around the eyes, the look of a woman setting down a weight she had carried longer than she'd meant to.

"I was going to ask you something this morning," Odile said. "I wanted to see what you'd answer first."

* * *

She asked it an hour later, when the glue pot had been scraped and the drawer front lay in pieces on Coral's bench like a disassembled argument.

"The Regiment keeps apprentices the old way," Odile said. She was sharpening a chisel as she talked, drawing it across the stone in slow strokes that matched her breathing. "Seven years, if you're young. Fewer if you come to us with hands that already know some things. You'd start where Tamsin started. Sweeping. Sorting offcuts. Grinding pigment. The work nobody wants and everybody needs." She tested the edge against her thumbnail. "You're twenty-nine years old and you can pick a lock with a hairpin and read four languages, and I'm going to hand you a broom. I want to know how that sits with you before I say more."

Coral looked down at her hands. They had changed over the winter. The ink stains were fading from her right middle finger, slowly, the way any stain fades that has been renewed daily for half a life. In their place were blisters that had hardened into calluses, a splinter-scar along the heel of her palm, a small burn from the glue iron shaped like nothing at all. Hands that had spent fifteen years learning to leave no trace were learning, instead, to make one.

"It sits," she said, "like the first honest thing anyone's offered me in years."

"Broom's by the door, then," Odile said, and went back to her chisel, and that was the whole ceremony. No contract. No oath. In Tamsin's Ford they wrote things down afterward, not before — the ledger recorded what had been done, never what was merely promised. Coral found she loved that about them more than she could say.

But that evening, Odile took down the apprentice book.

It was a slim volume bound in gray calf, older than Coral, its corners worn round by decades of thumbs. Odile entered the new names each spring, she explained, when the winter intake was settled — but she opened it now, unprompted, and turned to a fresh page, and dipped her pen, and paused.

"Not signing tonight," she said. "Signing's for when you've earned the line. But I want you to see what goes above it." And she wrote, in her square, unhurried hand: *Coral Okonkwo, taken on the twelfth of the thaw month by Odile Vant, master joiner, to learn the trade from shaving to structure.* Then she blotted it and closed the book, and Coral understood that somewhere in the town's memory, in a form that would outlast both of them, she had already begun to exist.

That night she lay awake in the loft above the workshop listening to the building settle, and thought about names. For fifteen years hers had been a tool, interchangeable — *Maren, Sister Anselm, Widow Kess, the cousin from upcountry.* She had worn them the way a spy wears gloves, and discarded them as easily, and somewhere in all that discarding she had misplaced the one underneath. Now a book in a mountain town had written her down with her own name, attached to another person's name, joined like two boards in a joint that was meant to hold for a hundred years. She fell asleep with the strange sensation of being glued — clamped fast between past and future, wet and malleable, waiting to set.

* * *

Apprenticeship, it turned out, was humiliation with a curriculum.

Her first week she swept. She swept shavings and dust and the fine pale curls of pine that gathered in the corners like shed skins, and she learned that sweeping a workshop properly was not the mindless labor she'd assumed but a discipline of attention: you learned the floor, and from the floor you learned everything that happened above it. Which benches shed more than others. Where Tamsin worked fast and sloppy and where she slowed into genius. Which tools had been left out overnight, which joints had been cut dry, whose mood had soured by the pattern of waste beneath their station. Sef, she thought, would have approved. He taught her to read a room by its leavings; he just never told her the skill could be used for something kinder than survival.

The second week Odile gave her the glue pot, and Coral discovered that hide glue had opinions. Too hot and it weakened; too cool and it seized; neglected, it soured and stank like a drowned thing. It demanded tending the way a fire demands tending, patient and undramatic, and Coral tended it with the same vigilance she had once spent watching roads for riders, and found the vigilance itself transformed — no longer fear wearing a working face, but simply care.

The third week she ground pigment for Tamsin's finishing work, and burned her first proper batch of linseed oil, and stood in the yard waving smoke out of her hair while Tamsin laughed so hard she had to sit down on the chopping block.

"You know," Tamsin said, wiping her eyes, "most people take years to fail that comprehensively."

"I've always been a quick study."

"Oh, you are. That's the frightening part. You're terrible at everything and getting better at everything at the same rate, like watching a race between two horses that hate each other." She hopped off the block and took Coral's blistered hand, turning it over with the proprietary frankness the town extended to everyone's body — scars examined, injuries diagnosed, comfort administered whether sought or not. "Mother says your joinery's coming faster than mine did. Don't let it go to your head. Mine was bad enough to frighten livestock."

Coral looked at their hands together, Tamsin's broad-knuckled and sure, her own narrow ones mapped with new marks, and felt something in her chest that had no name in any of her four languages.

* * *

The work itself came slower than the sweeping, but it came.

Odile taught the way water teaches a channel: persistently, without hurry, finding every low place. She did not praise. She corrected by doing — taking Coral's chisel, making three perfect cuts beside Coral's ragged ones, and handing it back without comment, so that the difference spoke for itself in a language Coral was learning to be ashamed in and, gradually, less ashamed. When Coral's dovetails gaped at the corners, Odile showed her how to see the line before cutting it: *the eye leads the blade, always; wood forgives planning and punishes hope.* When Coral rushed a joint because the light was failing, Odile made her undo it by lamplight anyway, because *tired work undone tonight is one evening lost; tired work kept is a lie you live inside forever.*

That last one landed harder than Odile could have known. Coral sat with it long after the workshop emptied, turning it over the way she'd once turned dispatches, decoding the part that was meant for her alone. How much of her life had been tired work kept? How many lies was she living inside — smaller now than they'd been at midwinter, when she'd laid her whole double life before the council by lamplight, but not yet gone? The forged dispatch had gone downriver with Hale in the spring's first ferry traffic; the true ones were ash on the kiln wind. The republic was a corpse, Brann was gone south in disgrace, the war was over and no one had won. And still, some mornings, she woke reaching for a case that wasn't chained to her wrist anymore, and had to lie in the gray light remembering who she was now, which was someone whose worst secret was a ruined drawer front.

Humility, she discovered, was not the abasement she'd always assumed it to be. It was simpler and stranger than that: it was standing in a room full of people better than you at something, and staying. Letting yourself be the worst joiner in the Regiment, openly, on purpose, day after day, while the town watched you improve and thought nothing of it — because in Tamsin's Ford, being a beginner was not a disgrace. It was a season, like mud season or thaw, and it passed.

In the capital, competence had been currency and ignorance was death. Here, ignorance was just the beginning of a very long conversation with a piece of walnut.

* * *

By the time the high meadows had gone fully green, Coral could cut a respectable mortise, flatten a board true to winding sticks, and keep six glue pots alive simultaneously on a deadline. Her dovetails still gaped if she hurried, but she had stopped hiding the gaps and started showing them to Odile first thing, holding the work out like a confession, and Odile would squint and grunt and say *better* — one word, worth more to Coral now than every coded commendation the republic had ever sent her.

It was on one such afternoon — late spring, doors open to the yard, bees loud in the lilac by the fence — that the rider came down from the pass.

Coral heard the horse before she saw it; some skills don't fade, only change employment. Hooves on the switchback road, unhurried, single mount. She set down her chisel and stepped to the doorway, and beside her, uncannily, Tamsin appeared too, flour-dusted from the noon bread run, both of them wearing the same expression of the town's whole quiet alertness: not fear, exactly. Attention.

The rider wore travel grime and no colors, and led a second horse loaded with bundled figures — children's bundles, blankets, a birdcage wrapped in sacking. Refugees, then, or the herald of them. Word had been drifting upriver for weeks, fragments passed ferryman to ferryman: the capital's fall had shaken loose thousands, and the roads south and west were filling with people looking for towns that still had roofs and rules and room.

The rider drew rein at the workshop gate and called out the question Coral had been dreading and expecting in equal measure:

"Is this Tamsin's Ford? We were told — we were told this town takes people in."

From the house, Odile emerged drying her hands on her apron, and behind her came others, drawn by the same current of news — the ferryman, the archive-keeper, a knot of neighbors already moving toward the green with the unconscious coordination of a town that governed itself. Odile looked at the bundles on the lead horse, and at the road beyond the rider where, faintly, more figures were visible on the far switchback, walking.

"We take people in," Odile said. "How many are coming?"

"As many as heard the same story we did," the rider said. "Forty on the road behind me. More behind them, they said. Maybe a hundred by week's end."

A murmur went through the little crowd — not dismay, Coral noticed, but arithmetic. Rations counted, bunks imagined, timber estimated. She caught Tamsin's eye and read there the same thing she felt in her own chest: the town was about to become something larger than itself, overnight, and there would be work — weeks of it, hammer-and-nail work, bunk-building work, work for every pair of hands in the Regiment.

Including, Odile was already saying, turning back toward the workshop with her chin lifted in the direction of the lumber store, the hands of every apprentice she had.

Coral picked up her chisel. Then she set it down again, and followed her teacher toward the timber, into whatever was coming down the road.
