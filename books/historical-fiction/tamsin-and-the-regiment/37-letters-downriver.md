## 37. Letters Downriver

The letter came up from the ferry landing in the hands of a boy with frozen ears, and by the time it reached the council hall it had been read aloud at three doorways and argued over twice, which was how news traveled in Tamsin's Ford — the way water travels through cloth, wicking into every seam whether you wanted it there or not.

Tamsin heard about it before she saw the paper itself. She was at her mother's bench, cutting dovetails for a chest of drawers that Odile had pronounced "adequate," which in Odile's vocabulary was nearly a parade. Her mother came in without knocking — she never knocked in her own workshop — and stood in the doorway holding a sheet of paper like it might bite.

"From downriver," Odile said.

"Bad news?"

"Depends who's asking." Odile crossed to the stove and held her hands over it though they weren't cold; it was a thinking gesture, not a warming one. "The republic's gone. Properly gone this time, not the rumors we've had every month since midwinter. The capital fell in the autumn — Brann told us that much truth, anyway — but now the whole river valley's emptied out. The garrisons dissolved, the tax farms burned, and everyone who ever signed their name to a republican ledger is walking north with what they can carry."

Tamsin set down her chisel. The wood shavings curled on the bench between them, small pale spirals, and she found herself looking at them instead of her mother because it was easier than looking at her mother's face when her mother talked about the war. "How many people?"

"The letter says four hundred. Maybe more by the time they reach us." Odile smoothed the paper against her apron. "They're following the old drovers' road. Some of them have coin. Most of them have nothing but the clothes they're standing in and whatever skills they carried out of the city under their coats."

"And they're coming *here*."

"They're coming everywhere. But some of them remember us. We've sold joinery downriver for thirty years; we've taken apprentices whose families live in the capital; we've lent money to half the merchants on the middle river." Odile folded the letter once, precisely, and put it inside her apron pocket where Tamsin knew it would stay until council met that evening. "The Regiment has debts all along that road, girl. And some of those people are coming to collect."

* * *

Council convened at dusk, and the town convened with it. That was the thing about Tamsin's Ford that strangers never understood until they'd lived through it: there was no separating the governing from the governed, no gallery of spectators and floor of deciders. When the council met in the long hall above the workshop, anyone could come, and nearly everyone did, filling the benches along the walls with wool-capped heads and steaming mugs, children asleep against their mothers' shoulders, the oldest Vant cousins occupying the same corner seat they'd occupied since before Tamsin was born.

She sat with Coral near the back, close enough to the stove to be warm and close enough to the door to leave if the meeting turned ugly. Coral had taught her that positioning without ever saying it aloud — just arrived early one evening, looked around the room, and settled where she settled, and Tamsin had understood it as instruction the way she understood everything Coral did now: as craft, passed hand to hand.

Coral had changed since midwinter. Not in any way a stranger would notice. She still sat quietly; she still spoke only when speaking was worth the cost of words. But the watchfulness had softened at the edges. She no longer counted exits when she entered a room — Tamsin had tested this, casually, half a dozen times, watching her teacher's eyes, and Coral's gaze went to faces now, not doors. To people, not ways out.

Tonight, though, as Odile read the letter aloud from the front of the hall, Tamsin watched something old flicker back across Coral's face. A tightening around the mouth. The look of someone hearing numbers and doing arithmetic nobody else wanted to do.

"—four hundred souls, traveling in family groups mostly, led by a woman named Sereth Iln, who signs herself late under-secretary of the river customs office," Odile read. "They ask shelter through the thaw and labor in trade. They bring carpenters, a physician, two millwrights, a schoolteacher, dyers, a bookbinder, and—" Odile paused, and Tamsin saw her mother's jaw set in the particular way it set when the news required her to be careful, "—and an unknown number of persons who served the republic's ministries in capacities they do not name."

A murmur went through the benches. Old Marta Hessel, who had buried two sons in the border wars, said loudly enough for everyone, "Then let them keep their capacities unnamed somewhere else."

"Sit down, Marta," said her own sister, "you took in a smuggler every winter of the nineties and fed him off your best plates."

"That was different. He was *useful*."

Laughter broke some of the tension, the way it always did, and Odile waited it out with the patience of a woman who had spent forty years waiting for glue to cure. When the room quieted, she laid the letter flat on the table.

"Here is the arithmetic," she said. "We have grain to last ninety souls past spring if the fishing goes well. We have roof space for perhaps sixty more if we build bunks in the workshop loft and open the meetinghouse. Four hundred people cannot winter here. They don't expect to — Sereth Iln asks only for a week's rest, provisions to continue, and work for wages enough to buy passage or seed. But a week of four hundred is a hundred weeks of twenty-five, and we should know what we're agreeing to before they're standing in our square."

"We're agreeing to be human beings," said Uncle Ferren from his corner. "That's the whole of the argument. Everything after that is carpentry, and carpentry we can do."

"It's never been the whole of the argument," said Councilor Dree, carefully. "There will be agents among them. There were agents among *us*, this winter, and one of them came with pitch and torches." Eyes moved, briefly, toward Brann's empty seat — empty since the fire watch, since the trial, since he'd been bound out to the quarry camps for seven years rather than hanged, because the Regiment did not hang its own even when its own deserved it. "I say we count the risk honestly before we vote it warm."

"And how do you propose to sort honest refugees from agents?" Marta demanded. "Make them recite the Articles? Check their hands for ink?"

It was then that Coral spoke, and the whole room turned, because Coral almost never spoke first and never spoke loudly, but when she did the room had learned to listen.

"You don't sort them at the gate," she said. "You sort them at the table. Feed everyone. House everyone. Watch nothing, announce nothing — suspicion is the one thing an agent can use against you, because it makes neighbors inform on neighbors, and that's how towns die. What you do instead is give them work. An agent needs privacy to write reports and idle hours to recruit. A tired person with sawdust in their hair and blisters on their hands has neither." She glanced at Odile. "Build the bunks in the workshop loft, not the meetinghouse. Make them sleep where the work is. Anyone who avoids the bench will tell you more in a week than a dozen interrogations ever could."

Silence followed, the good kind, the kind where a thought settles over a room like snowfall. Then Uncle Ferren chuckled deep in his chest.

"There's the courier's daughter talking," he said. "Fifteen years running messages through a dying republic, and what did it teach you? How to spot a spy by his blisters."

"How to *be* one," Coral said evenly, "and therefore how to starve one."

Odile struck the table once with the flat of her palm. "Motion to prepare for four hundred. Bunks in the workshop loft, stores opened under the quartermaster's seal, wages drawn from the common purse for refugee labor. All in favor."

Every hand in the hall went up except Marta Hessel's, and Marta kept hers down with such theatrical reluctance that her sister pulled it up for her, and the hall laughed, and it was done.

* * *

They had eleven days.

Tamsin learned in those eleven days what her town was made of, the way you learn what a beam is made of only when you load it. The sawmill ran double shifts. The women who kept the drying sheds gave up their racks to cure lumber faster. Old men who hadn't held a plane in a decade appeared at the workshop door each morning asking to be pointed at something. Children collected shavings in baskets for kindling and bedding. Nobody had ordered any of it. The Regiment simply bent, all together, like grass deciding which way the wind needed to go.

Coral worked harder than anyone, and Tamsin watched her do it, and understood something she'd been circling for months.

Her mother had taken Coral on as an apprentice in the spring — properly, formally, chalk marks on the doorframe and everything — but Tamsin had privately wondered how long it would hold, whether Coral wouldn't grow restless when the danger passed, whether a woman built for roads and codes could bear the slow patience of wood. Now she watched Coral lay out bunk frames with a chalk line and a speed that would have embarrassed journeymen, and she understood: Coral wasn't restless. Coral was building. This was the first thing in fifteen years that she had built *toward* instead of merely through, and she attacked it the way she must once have attacked a difficult cipher — totally, gratefully, with both hands.

"Your mortises are drifting left," Tamsin told her, crouched beside her at the second frame, mostly to tease. "Again."

"My mortises are *honest*. They go where they please."

"They go where your elbow tells them, and your elbow is lazy on the draw side." Tamsin knocked her shoulder against Coral's. "Mother says if the third bunk rack sags, she's sending you back to sweeping."

"Your mother says that about everyone. She said it about *you*."

"She said it about me and I *did* sag. The Halliday bedstead. Ask anyone."

"I heard about the Halliday bedstead," Coral said gravely. "It collapsed beneath the Hallidays."

"Under the Hallidays, thank you, there's a difference, and they were large people, and the joint design was flawed before I ever touched it—"

Coral laughed. It still caught Tamsin off guard every time — the sound of it, low and unpracticed, like an instrument played after years in a case. A year ago Coral Okonkwo had been a ghost in a borrowed coat, a widowed seamstress's cousin whom nobody remembered five minutes after she left a room. Now she sat in sawdust arguing about bedsteads, flour on her sleeves from the morning's bread detail, her dark hair tied back with a strip of the same red cloth Tamsin used for hers. Tamsin had cut both strips from the same bolt and had never explained why and Coral had never asked, and that, too, was a kind of language between them now.

They worked. The loft filled with the smell of fresh pine and hot glue and the ring of mallets. By the fourth day the first tier of bunks stood finished along the north wall — thirty beds, straw ticks already stuffed, blankets from the communal press folded at the foot of each one in gray and undyed white, because the Regiment did not decorate for guests it hadn't yet met.

On the fifth day, Tamsin found Coral sitting alone on the finished bunks after supper, in the lamplight, holding one of the blankets.

Not folding it. Holding it. Both hands in the wool, absolutely still, looking at nothing.

Tamsin climbed up beside her and didn't say anything. That was another thing she'd learned — when to leave silence open like a door.

"I slept in places like this," Coral said finally. "For fifteen years. Way stations. Barracks. Once a hay barn in the Kessrin pass with snow coming through the roof, and I lay awake all night doing sums in my head because sums were safer than sleeping. Every bunk I ever occupied, I occupied alone, and I always woke before dawn and was gone before anyone could learn my face." She rubbed the blanket between her fingers. "Four hundred people are going to sleep in this room together, and snore, and argue, and steal each other's blankets, and in the morning nobody will vanish."

"No," Tamsin agreed. "That's rather the point of them."

Coral looked at her sideways. "Was that a joke?"

"A poor one. Mother says my humor sags too."

That earned the laugh again, quieter this time, and Coral leaned her shoulder against Tamsin's shoulder, and they sat like that while the lamp burned down, two women on a bunk built for a stranger, keeping it company until its owner came.

* * *

The refugees came on a gray afternoon nine days later, and Tamsin watched them come from the new bridge, because of course she did — the whole town did, lining the rail from end to end, silent in the way crowds are silent when they're trying to decide how to feel.

They came up the drovers' road in a strung-out line half a mile long. Families first, the letter had promised, and so it was: mothers carrying children too big to be carried, old men leaning on younger men, a girl of maybe six leading a goat on a rope and patting its neck at intervals as though reassuring it. Their carts were overloaded and breaking. Their clothes were city clothes gone thin — good wool, good boots, the remains of respectability worn down to the thread of survival. As they drew closer Tamsin could see the particular look in their eyes, the one she'd seen on Coral's face the day she'd first arrived at the Ford: the arithmetic of exhaustion, counting the distance left against the strength remaining and finding the sum uncertain.

Sereth Iln walked at the head of them. She was not what Tamsin expected — small, gray-haired, plain as a fence post, with the posture of someone who had spent decades standing behind more important people. She stopped at the bridge's foot and looked up at the crowd of strangers lining the rails, and Tamsin saw her gather herself, preparing a speech, reaching for whatever official dignity remained in her ruined office.

Odile saved her from it. She came down the bridge steps, crossed the road, and stuck out one saw-scarred hand.

"Odile Vant, master joiner, of the Regiment of Tamsin's Ford. You'll be wanting soup before speeches. We've both."

Something in the gray-haired woman's face came apart, very slightly, like ice going out from a shore. "We have nothing to pay—"

"You'll pay in labor or you won't pay at all, and either way you'll eat first. That's the law here." Odile turned to the crowd on the bridge and raised her voice. "Well? Don't stand there admiring the view. The kettle's on."

And the town poured down onto the road like water finding its level — bowls appearing from nowhere, blankets, hands taking bundles off shoulders, children being lifted onto fences to see better, the Hessel sisters already arguing over which family would lodge where. Within an hour the square smelled of barley soup and wet wool, and the line of refugees had become a crowd of guests, and the guests were becoming, without anyone decreeing it, neighbors.

Tamsin worked the soup kettles until her arms ached, ladling, refilling, learning names in batches — the millwrights were brothers named Corvane; the schoolteacher was a stooped man called Pell who wept openly into his first bowl and apologized for it and was told by three separate grandmothers to stop apologizing; the physician was a tall, quiet woman who, within an hour of arriving, was already setting the arm of a local boy who'd fallen off the woodpile, as though she'd lived there years.

Coral worked beside her through all of it, and Tamsin noticed — filed away, the way Coral had taught her to notice — that Coral watched the refugees the way a shepherd watches a flock in weather. Not with fear. With responsibility. Twice she drifted to the edge of the crowd and stood a while near certain newcomers, saying little, listening much: a lean man who answered questions a beat too slowly, a pair of sisters who asked unusually precise questions about the council's schedule. By evening she sought out Odile and murmured something, and Tamsin, passing with an armload of bowls, caught only the tail of it:

"—the one in the green coat, watch him at the bench tomorrow. If he avoids work again, put him on the night kiln shift where I can see him."

Her mother grunted assent without breaking stride, as though being handed intelligence assessments by her apprentice were the most natural thing in the world, which Tamsin supposed it now was.

* * *

Night came down on the fullest town Tamsin had ever seen. The workshop loft glowed yellow through its windows, four hundred bodies' worth of warmth pressing against the glass. From outside, crossing the yard with the last of the washing water, Tamsin paused and looked up and listened.

The loft was loud. Not with distress — with life. Snoring already, yes, and a baby crying somewhere, and low talk, and one corner singing something soft and mournful in a river dialect, and underneath it all the great settling creak of a building full of breathing people, a sound the workshop had never made before, a sound like a ship.

Coral came out to the yard a few minutes later, wiping her hands on her apron, and stood next to Tamsin in the cold, looking up at the lit windows.

"Well?" Tamsin said.

"They'll do," Coral said. "Most of them. The green coat worries me. And the schoolteacher — Pell — he's genuine, but he was in the archives ministry, and he knows names. If he talks freely about who served where, half the loft will panic by Thursday." She exhaled, breath silvering. "I'll sit with him tomorrow. Archivists trust other archivists. It's a disease."

"You were a courier, not an archivist."

"I was *adjacent*. It counts." Coral was quiet a moment. Then, in a different tone, lighter but not entirely light, she said: "When I decoded that final dispatch — the burn order — there was a line at the bottom I never told the council about. Standard boilerplate. *All records pertaining to the operation to be destroyed upon completion.* Names, ledgers, correspondence. Everyone who'd touched the mission, erased on paper." She nodded up at the glowing loft. "Those people down there? Half of them are erasures. The republic fell, and its paperwork burned or scattered, and now there are hundreds of people walking north with no record anywhere that they exist. No ministry, no registry, no ledger. Nothing to prove who they were."

"That sounds—" Tamsin hesitated. "Freeing?"

"Terrifying," Coral said. "To them. I know exactly how it feels, because I spent fifteen years manufacturing that condition on purpose, and I can tell you it's not freedom. A person no ledger holds is a person nothing protects." She turned to Tamsin, and the lamplight from the loft caught the side of her face. "Your town writes everyone down. Debts, births, quarrels, apprenticeships — everything, in books that outlast empires. Do you know what that's worth to people who've just been unwritten? Tomorrow, when they've slept and eaten, someone should take the ledger to the loft. Write them in. Name by name. *So-and-so, arrived such a date, owes such a debt, owns such a skill.* Give them pages again."

Tamsin looked at her for a long moment. "You've thought about this."

"I've been on the wrong side of it," Coral said simply. "Once, a long time ago, I was the reason a whole town's book burned. I'd like the next entry I influence to go the other direction."

Below the yard, downriver, the dark rolled on toward the drowned capital and everything that had ended there. Above them the loft glowed and groaned and sang. And Tamsin — who had grown up believing the Regiment's books were merely bookkeeping, mere housekeeping, mere habit — felt the ground shift slightly under everything she knew, the way it shifts when you discover a tool you've owned all your life has a second edge.

"Come on," she said, taking Coral's sleeve. "Before Mother assigns the night watch without us. And Coral — tomorrow, when you write them in? Start with the green coat. If we're giving people pages again, I want to see what he does with his."

Coral smiled — that rare, unguarded smile — and let herself be tugged toward the light. But at the workshop door she paused, glancing back down the dark road they'd all come by, and Tamsin saw her expression sharpen into something older and colder, the courier surfacing one last time in the joiner's wife-to-be, the apprentice, the neighbor.

Because far down the drovers' road, past the last lamp, a lantern was moving. Small. Alone. Following the refugees' path north.

And it had stopped — just for a heartbeat, just at the edge of sight — as if whoever carried it had noticed the two figures in the doorway, and was noting them down in some book of its own.
