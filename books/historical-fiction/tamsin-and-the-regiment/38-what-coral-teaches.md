## 38. What Coral Teaches

The first lesson was a lie, and Coral told it on purpose.

*Nothing in this room is important,* she said to the six children gathered on the workshop's low benches, their feet not yet reaching the floor, their faces lit by the grey morning that came through the south windows. *Nothing you learn here will ever matter. If anyone asks, we are practicing sums.*

They believed her, which was the point. Believing easily was how children stayed children, and Coral had spent fifteen years learning what happened to those who stopped.

She had proposed the school to the council in her careful, halting way — the way of someone still unaccustomed to being listened to. The republic had fallen; refugees were coming upriver with spring; the town would need its own people able to read what arrived, and to keep from being read themselves. Brann's betrayal had proven that even Tamsin's Ford grew informants like any other soil. The council had nodded, Odile had said *about time*, and that had been the whole of the debate — no crowns, no committees, just the town deciding, as it always did, that knowledge kept in one pair of hands was knowledge at risk.

So now there were benches, and chalk, and six children aged seven to twelve, and Coral Okonkwo standing before them feeling like a woman who had opened a door onto a room in herself she had boarded shut so long ago she'd forgotten the room existed.

"Say nothing," she began, "is the first rule."

A small boy named Perrin, the ferryman's grandson, raised his hand. "But we're talking right now."

Coral almost smiled. Fifteen years ago she had been the child asking that question, sitting across from Sef in a rented room above a chandler's shop, and he had laughed his dry laugh and said *good, you'll do.* She could still see him: the grey stubble, the ink-stained cuff he never cleaned because he said clean cuffs were for men with nothing to hide. Dead two years now in the capital's purge, buried somewhere unmarked or not buried at all, and everything he'd taught her arriving here anyway, across the distance, like seeds on water.

"We're talking," she agreed, "because this is practice. Practice is where you're allowed to be clumsy." She held up a strip of paper. On it, in a fair round hand, she had written: THE RIVER FREEZES IN WINTER. "By the end of the month, each of you will write me a message that looks like nothing at all. A shopping list. A verse of a song. And I will read it, and know exactly what it says."

Perrin squinted at the strip. "Magic?"

"Worse," said a girl at the back — Marette Kilnwright, ten years old, sharp-eyed as her mother. "Trickery."

"The best kind," Coral said. "The kind that keeps people alive."

* * *

Sef had taught her cipher first and silence second, but he'd told her once, near the end of her apprenticeship, that he'd gotten the order wrong.

"Memory," he said, tapping his own temple with a pipe that wasn't lit, because Sef never actually smoked, only carried the pipe like a prop in a play about himself. "Codes can be taken from you. Paper burns. But what's in here—" tap, tap— "they have to take the whole head, girl, and heads that remember well are heads that are hard to catch."

He taught her the palace method then: the great house built in the mind, room by room, each fact set on a shelf, behind a door, under a specific loose flagstone in a specific imagined kitchen. He made her build hers over weeks — a house with forty rooms, though she had never seen a house with more than four — and she had carried that house through every border crossing, every checkpoint, every year of the war, her dispatches living nowhere but in the architecture of her own skull.

Now she stood in the workshop's warm sawdust light and taught the same craft to children who had never needed a hiding place in their lives, and found — this was the strange part, the part that caught in her throat when she wasn't guarding against it — that teaching it did not wound her. She had expected it to hurt. She had braced for it, the way you brace before touching a burn scar. Instead each lesson was like taking an old injury and setting it to work: the silences, the hiding, the years of practiced blankness, all of it turning in her hands into something useful, something given rather than endured.

"Close your eyes," she told them. "Build a room. Any room. Fill it with five things you'll never forget. Now put our message in the room — one word on each thing."

Marette kept her eyes open. "Why not just remember it?"

"Because memory without a place is weather," Coral said. "It blows away. Memory with a place is furniture. It stays until you move it." She paused. "My teacher taught me that. His name was Sef. He's dead now. So everything I'm giving you came from him, and if you use it well, some part of him keeps working in the world. That's what inheritance is. Not money. Working parts."

The children were quiet, which among children meant she had said something real.

* * *

Odile watched from the doorway the second week, arms crossed, ostensibly inspecting a delivery of birch stock that didn't need inspecting.

"You're good at it," she said afterward, in the yard, while Coral stacked offcuts. It was not quite a question and not quite a compliment; Odile Vant dealt mostly in observations, delivered like verdicts.

"I'm good at secrets. This is nearly the same trade."

"It's not," Odile said. "Secrets are hoarding. What you're doing is spending." She picked up an offcut, turned it, set it down again. "When I took my apprenticeship oath, my master told me the difference between a craftsman and a merchant of craft. The merchant sells what he makes. The craftsman makes what outlives him." She looked at Coral with those level grey eyes that missed nothing and forgave selectively. "You were a merchant of survival a long time. You're crafting now."

Coral bent back to her stacking, because her eyes had gone hot and she refused to weep over kindling twice in one lifetime. "Tamsin talks too much," she said. "You've been comparing notes."

"My daughter reports facts. It's not talking, it's inventory." A pause, and then, gruffly, almost gently: "Your inventory's improving."

* * *

The third week, she taught them the hardest lesson, and she made herself teach it honestly.

"Sometimes," she said, "someone will ask you a question, and answering truthfully will hurt someone you love. And lying will make you feel like you're coming apart inside." She had their full attention now; even Perrin had stopped fidgeting. "There's a third thing. You don't lie. You don't tell. You give an answer that's true, but not the true answer. My teacher called it the honest door beside the locked one."

She gave them examples. *Where are you going? — To the mill, eventually. Who sent this letter? — Someone who trusts me. Did you see anything? — I saw the river, and it was very high.*

Then she made them practice, pairing off, asking and deflecting, and the workshop filled with the absurd music of children learning to be slippery — *did you eat my apple? I ate an apple, yes* — laughing, falling into it, getting better.

But afterward, alone, sweeping the shavings, Coral sat down hard on a bench and let the weight of it arrive, because honesty demanded it even here, even with no one watching. She was teaching children to survive interrogation. In a town that had voted, in a town that kept its own books, in a town whose worst corruption had been one ambitious man copying papers — she was teaching seven-year-olds the tools of her fifteen darkest years, and calling it inheritance.

Was it a gift or a contagion? She turned it over the way Odile turned timber, looking for grain, looking for flaws.

And then she thought of the bunks going up in the long hall — forty of them now, sixty, Coral planing the rails herself — for the refugees coming with the thaw, people who had lost a capital, a country, possibly children. She thought of what those children would need to know when they arrived, hollow-eyed, carrying whatever they'd carried out of the burning. Not cipher, maybe. Not yet. But memory-craft, yes. The honest door. The knowledge that a person could be emptied of everything and still hold a house inside their head that no one could confiscate.

The tools weren't wounds. Tools were tools. It depended entirely on the hands.

Hers, she decided, were steadier than they'd ever been.

* * *

On the last morning of the month, the children presented their messages.

Perrin brought a shopping list that decoded, letter by letter along the item prices, to FERRY ICE THIN STAY OFF. Coral read it twice and felt cold walk up her spine — because it was true, the ice was thinning at the landings, and the boy had noticed, and hidden the warning where anyone searching him would find only eggs and lamp oil.

Marette brought a verse of a work song, four lines, innocent as bread, and folded inside it the entire council seating order and who had spoken longest at the last assembly. "For practice," she said, with a shrug far too old for her. "In case someone needs to know who runs things, and can't ask."

Coral looked at her a long moment. "Who taught you to think like that?"

"Nobody." Marette shrugged again. "I just watch. Like you do. You think I haven't seen you counting exits since autumn? There's only three doors in this building and you look at all of them every time you come in. I started doing it too. Makes me feel safe."

Somewhere in Coral's chest, something both ached and settled, like a joint going back into place. The cage and the gift, the wound and the tool — the girl had already made the choice Coral had spent a winter making, and made it lightly, without the scar tissue.

"Good," Coral said, and her voice was steady. "Keep watching. And someday — not yet — teach it to someone smaller than you. That's the whole law. Everything passes down or it dies."

* * *

That evening she climbed to the kiln terrace, where Tamsin sat mending nets with the quick fingers she applied to everything, and Coral sat beside her and showed her the children's work, word by word, proud in a way she didn't bother disguising anymore — disguise had become a coat she hung by the door.

"They're better than I was at their age," Coral said. "Faster. Braver. They have something I never had."

"What's that?"

"Nowhere to hide from each other. It makes them strong in ways spies can't be." She watched the river below, running dark and heavy with the thaw, groaning in places, shifting. "Sef gave me his whole craft to keep me alive in a country that was dying. I get to give it to children in a country being born. Same tools. Different hands."

Tamsin tied off a knot and leaned her shoulder against Coral's, an easy weight she no longer flinched from. "And what do you call yourself now? Still courier?"

Coral considered it. Below them the river pushed its ice toward the sea, and upstream the road out stood open for the first time in half a year, empty, patient, irrelevant.

"No," she said. "Teacher sounds too grand. Keeper of the boring sums, maybe." She smiled. "But there's one thing I haven't taught them yet, and I'm not sure I should."

Tamsin looked at her, waiting, in the way of someone who knew that waiting got more answers than asking.

"How to forget," Coral said quietly. "How to put something down and leave it down. Everything I learned was about holding on. Nobody ever taught me how to set it free." She looked at the water. "And tomorrow the first boats come upriver with the refugees, and some of those children are going to be carrying things that need forgetting far more than keeping."

The river boomed against the landing pilings, loud as a door.

Tamsin took her hand. "Then we'll figure it out together," she said. "First boat's sighted. Papa's ringing the bell."
