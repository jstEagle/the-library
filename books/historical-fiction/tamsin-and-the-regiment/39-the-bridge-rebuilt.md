## 39. The Bridge Rebuilt

The ice went out of the river in the third week of thaw, and it went out loudly.

Tamsin had gone down to the bank at dawn to see it, because her mother said you should always witness the things that frighten a place, and the breaking of the river had frightened the Ford for as long as anyone could remember. All winter the water had lain beneath its lid of grey glass, patient, muffled, a river only by rumor. Then one night the temperature turned and the whole channel began to talk — groans like a felled tree, cracks that ran from bank to bank with a sound like a whip made of lightning — and by morning the ice was in pieces the size of workshop doors, riding the swollen current downriver, grinding against the broken stumps of the old crossing, shoving each other aside with a low continuous thunder.

Tamsin stood on the muddy bank with her breath smoking and watched the wreckage of winter go past. Behind her, up the slope, the town was already stirring. She could smell woodsmoke from forty chimneys, could hear the first hammering from the drying sheds where the new timbers had been curing since midwinter — oak and larch cut in the autumn before anyone knew they would need them, stacked under canvas, waiting the way good timber waits: without complaint.

The old bridge had died in the first snows, its rotted center beam giving way under nothing more than wind and bad luck, and for five months Tamsin's Ford had been a town with no road out. Five months of ferryless silence on the far side, of news arriving late or not at all, of Coral Okonkwo standing in their kitchen with a courier's case chained to her wrist and nowhere left to carry it.

Tamsin thought about that sometimes — how the bridge's collapse had been the luckiest accident in the town's history, though it hadn't felt like luck at the time, watching her mother's face when she understood what the broken span meant. A trap springing shut. And then, slowly, over the long dark months, becoming something else. Not a trap. A hearth.

She turned and walked back up through the mud toward the square, where the day's work would begin, and where — she allowed herself the thought without embarrassment now, mostly — Coral would be waiting.

* * *

They raised the bridge over nine days.

It took the whole Regiment, which was the point of it, Odile said. Anyone could buy a bridge. Only a town could raise one. The council had voted the timber and the labor at the first assembly after thaw, and Master Ferrow of the smithy had re-sharpened every auger and froe in the valley, and on the first morning the entire population of Tamsin's Ford came down to the banks like a tide going the wrong direction.

Tamsin worked the near shore the first days, hewing the new abutment stones into their seats while the men waded the shallows in wool greased with tallow, setting the great caissons. The water was still knife-cold with melt; when it broke over a man's chest he'd come up gasping and swearing and be laughed at and handed a mug of something hot until his color came back. On the third day the first bent rose from the riverbed — two trunks leaned together and lashed, tall as the meetinghouse roof — and the whole bank stopped working for a quarter hour just to look at it, this first bone of the thing standing upright out of the water, dripping, impossible.

"It looks like a wish," said little Anselm Petch, who was seven, and nobody corrected him, because he was right.

Coral worked the far shore.

Tamsin watched her across the water more than she meant to, the way you watch anything that has changed shape recently. The woman who had come off the pass in autumn had been all edges held very still — a traveler made of careful hands and counted exits, courteous the way a locked door is courteous. The woman driving stakes on the opposite bank now had sawdust in her hair and mud to the knee and shouted instructions to the Vant crew in a voice that carried, and when someone teased her about her joinery — which everyone did, constantly, because her joinery remained a scandal and probably always would — she gave back as good as she got, dry and quick, and Tamsin had learned to read the small tells underneath: the flush she couldn't hide, the way her eyes went soft before her mouth caught up.

Fifteen years of practiced silence, Coral had told them, lamplight and confession, the night everything came out. Fifteen years of being no one, so that no one could be blamed for knowing her.

Now half the town knew her handwriting. Now children ran to her in the lane. Now she had opinions about glue.

On the fifth day the bents were all standing, eight pairs of black oak legs marching across the channel, and the crews began laying the stringers — the long beams that would bear the road itself. These were the heart of the job, thirty-foot lengths of seasoned larch, and moving them required every rope in the valley and a system of levers Odile had drawn on slate and argued about for two evenings with the ferryman's widow, who knew the river's temper better than any living soul. They walked the beams out hand over hand, sixty people leaning together, and when the first one dropped into its notches with a boom that shook the bank, the cheer started on the far shore and crossed the water faster than the sound did, and Tamsin found herself shouting with the rest of them, hoarse and filthy and happier than she could account for.

That evening, around the cook fires on the bank, Councilor Brann's empty chair came up in conversation only once, briefly, the way a scar comes up in conversation — touched and moved past. He was gone downriver in the Hale party, taken alive at the fire watch and released to the republic's remnant in exchange for papers, and the town did not speak his name so much as handle it, carefully, like a tool with a cracked haft that might yet be useful again. The republic itself was a ghost; Brann's confession had settled that. But ghosts kept ledgers too, Odile said, and the Ford intended to keep better ones.

Tamsin ate her stew beside Coral and said none of this aloud, because some accounting belonged to daylight and some to lamplight and some, she was learning, to silence shared between two people who had finally run out of secrets and discovered what was left when the secrets were gone: just the two of them, and the fire, and the sound of the river running free below.

* * *

On the eighth day they laid the decking, and on the ninth, there was one peg left.

Odile announced it at the morning muster with the gravity of a woman reading a sentence of exile. One peg. The final trunnel that would lock the last plank of the last course into the frame, and tradition — invented, Tamsin suspected, roughly four minutes prior, though she'd never have said so — held that the final peg must be driven by the person whose need of the road was greatest.

There was a pause after Odile said it. The sort of pause a town can produce when it has decided something unanimously without saying so.

Then old Marta Hollis, who had pulled Coral's name out of the river mud herself the night of the storm, said, "Well, we all know whose that is, don't we," and there was laughter, warm and broad, and Coral — standing at the edge of the crowd with a maul in her hands and an expression like a woman ambushed by kindness — looked around for escape routes the way she used to, professionally, and found, for perhaps the first time in her life, that there weren't any.

"You'll want to strike it true," Odile said, coming to stand beside her, voice pitched low enough that only Tamsin, crowding close on the other side, could hear it over the wind off the water. "One blow. Not three timid ones. The peg doesn't care about your feelings, girl. It cares about your commitment."

"I've noticed," Coral said, "that everything in this town works that way."

"Good. You're learning." Her mother's mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling — a compression at one corner, a brightness in the eye. "Maul's too heavy for you. Use mine."

She held out her own maul, the one with the worn hornbeam handle, the one she'd driven ten thousand pegs with, the one Tamsin had never once been allowed to touch as a child. Coral took it the way she'd once handled dispatch cases — carefully, like something with weight beyond its weight.

They went out onto the bridge together, the three of them, out along the fresh-laid deck between the crowds lining both rails, to the far end where the last course met the far abutment. Below them the river ran loud and green-brown with snowmelt, carrying the last ragged rafts of ice down toward the low country, toward the drowned capital, toward whatever was being built or mourned down there now that the war was over and no one had won. The wind came up the valley smelling of mud and cut oak and the first green things pushing through the bank grass.

Tamsin stood at her mother's shoulder and looked at the two of them — Odile Vant and the woman who had been a spy and a ghost and was now, officially, pending one signature in a ledger, almost family — and felt something settle in her chest that she didn't have a word for and didn't need one. The town was watching from both shores. Nobody spoke. Even the children had gone quiet, sensing ceremony, or maybe just sensing their mothers' elbows.

"The road out," Odile said quietly. "You understand what you're driving shut?"

"No." Coral looked down at the peg — a foot of white oak, its head flush against the last plank, waiting. "I've been trying to work that out all week. It isn't shut. That's the thing. It's open. It's the most open thing here. Any day I want I can walk across it and keep going south and be nobody again by the next ford." She set the maul's face against the peghead. "I'm driving it open. I just won't be using it."

Her mother regarded her for a moment. "That," Odile said, "is the correct answer. Strike."

Coral struck.

One blow, true, full-shouldered, the sound of it cracking up the valley like the ice going out, and the peg sank home with a shudder that traveled through the whole span — Tamsin felt it under her boots, the entire bridge taking the blow and answering it, every joint and stringer and bent passing the force along and settling deeper into itself, a structure accepting its last piece and becoming, in the same instant, finished. The crowd roared. Someone on the near bank let off the smithy's signal gun, which was against several regulations and nobody cared. Anselm Petch was lifted onto the railing by his father and waved both arms at the far horizon as if greeting the entire world personally.

And Coral stood there with the maul still raised, breathing hard, staring at the driven peg as if she didn't quite believe her own arm, and then Tamsin hugged her — sudden, public, in front of the entire Regiment — and felt her go stiff with old reflex and then, deliberately, muscle by muscle, choose to soften.

"That was terrible form," Tamsin said into her shoulder.

"I learned from your mother."

"So did I. You should have seen me at fourteen."

"I've seen your dovetails at nineteen. Fourteen must have been a war crime."

Tamsin laughed and didn't let go, and around them the town surged onto the bridge — Marta Hollis and the ferryman's widow arm in arm, the smith's apprentices daring each other to hang off the rails, Odile walking the span behind them with her hand trailing along the new rail like a woman checking a horse's neck — and the first feet crossed the river on the new road out, back and forth, testing, claiming, the boards already learning the particular creak of this town's particular weight.

* * *

The celebration ran until well past dark, out on the bank and up in the square, trestle tables and the last of the winter pork and the cider pressed from the orchard's survivors, fiddles that nobody remembered fetching but that appeared anyway, the way instruments do in towns like this. Tamsin danced twice, badly, with the ferryman's widow and then with Anselm Petch standing on her boots. Coral danced zero times and watched everything, and Tamsin saw her doing it — the old census-taking glance, counting faces, cataloguing exits — and saw, tonight, something different underneath it. Not exits anymore. Faces. Names. People she'd sat with through the winter, people whose debts she'd helped settle, people who had chosen her back in the gallery of the meetinghouse when choosing her had cost something.

Near midnight the fires burned down to orange cores and the crowd thinned to stragglers and stubborn dancers, and Tamsin found Coral sitting apart on the abutment stone, the cold stone warming slowly under her, looking out at the bridge.

It stood black against the stars, the new oak pale along its top rail, solid, lithe, real. Downriver the current ran steady and unhurried, done with its violence, going about its ordinary business. Somewhere down that river was the capital, and the ruins, and the refugees the scouts had begun reporting — carts on the south roads, whole families walking north with what they could carry, following word of a town in the mountains that had stood through the fall of a republic and come out with its books balanced and its people fed. Word traveled strangely these days. But it traveled. And the Ford had always known, in its bones, what a road was for.

"They'll start arriving within the month," Coral said, without turning. "Maybe sooner, if the passes clear early. The letters said as much."

"Mm. Mother's already drawing bunk frames. Forty beds to start. She wants them built like the bridge — to outlast whoever builds them."

"She would." A pause. Firelight moved on Coral's face. "I've been thinking about the ledger."

Tamsin waited. She was getting good at waiting; it turned out to be a family trait, acquired rather than inherited.

"There's a page for new members," Coral said. "I've seen the book — you showed me, after the council, that night. Debts entered and discharged, names and dates. It goes back a hundred and sixty years." She turned then, and the firelight caught the thing in her expression that Tamsin had learned meant she was about to say something true. "My name isn't in any book anywhere. Not the republic's rolls — those are ash, or worse than ash. Not Sef's records — he burned them himself, protecting me to the end. Fifteen years, and I don't exist on paper. I used to think that was freedom." Her hand found Tamsin's on the cold stone between them. "Tomorrow, when the council sits — will you be there? When they write it down?"

Tamsin looked at her — at the courier's stillness finally gone out of her shoulders, at the sawdust that even a festival night hadn't fully washed from her knuckles, at the woman the whole town had watched drive one perfect peg into a bridge today, opening the road and staying on this side of it — and understood that the question was bigger than tomorrow's council, that it had been building all winter the way pressure builds under ice, and that the answer, whatever shape it took, would go into a book meant to outlast empires.

"Yes," Tamsin said. "I'll be there. First row. Taking notes — someone has to make sure they spell you properly."

Coral laughed — the real laugh, the rare one, the one that had cost her fifteen years to get back — and above them the new bridge stood open against the stars, and downriver, unseen in the dark, the first slow carts of the northbound refugee road were already rolling toward the light.
