## 40. A Name in the Book

The ledger lay open on the long table of the council hall, and the ink had been mixed that morning from oak galls and rainwater, the way Odile insisted on mixing everything — by hand, slowly, with attention.

Coral stood over it for a long moment before she touched the pen.

One year. She had counted it the way she once counted days left in a season: carefully, secretly, with an ear always turned toward the road. But somewhere in the counting the arithmetic had changed. The days were no longer what remained to her. They were simply hers.

* * *

It had begun, as most things in Tamsin's Ford began, with work.

Spring thaw had come late and loud that first year, the river shrugging off its ice with a sound like artillery, and Coral had stood on the bank beside half the town and felt something loosen in her chest when the last floe went grinding past the ferry landing. The road out was open. She was free to go. She had known this the way she knew the weight of her own hands, and she had gone back to the bench that afternoon and planed a board badly, and no one said anything about the road at all, because everyone had understood before she did.

Now the second spring was settling in, green coming up the mountainsides like a tide, and the council hall smelled of beeswax and woodsmoke and the particular dry sweetness of old paper. The archive-keeper, Master Ellery, had laid out the ledger himself, turning pages with cotton gloves until he found the new one. Around the table sat Odile Vant, arms crossed, watching with the expression she wore when she was trying not to look pleased. Tamsin sat beside her mother, seventeen now by the town's reckoning, her hair tied back with a leather cord, a smear of hide glue still on her knuckle that she hadn't noticed or hadn't cared to wash away. Brann's empty chair had been taken out months ago; no one spoke of him except in the flat factual voice reserved for weather damage and dead stock.

"You don't have to take the name," Odile said. It was the fourth time she'd said it. "The book takes you either way. That's how it works."

"I know how it works," Coral said. "I've read every page."

That was true. Through two winters she had read them all — the debts and credits, the births entered in a joiner's hand, the deaths entered in another's, the year the flood took the lower mill, the year the Regiment voted to feed downriver refugees through a famine rather than fill their own stores. She had read the page where Odile's husband appeared, and the page where he stopped appearing. She had learned that a ledger was not an account of money. It was an account of memory, kept by people who believed memory could be built to last, like a roof.

"The name," Tamsin said, grinning, "was my idea, so if you're going to blame anyone—"

"Tamsin."

"—blame me, is all I'm saying."

Coral looked at the pen. It was goose quill, cut by Ellery, nothing special. She had written with pens worth more than this whole valley — pens carried in hollow boot heels, pens disguised as sewing needles, pens that had signed away towns she would never see again. This pen had been sitting in a jar on the archive shelf since before she came. It had never been used for anything but true entries.

Her hand, she noticed, was steady. Fifteen years of courier craft had taught her hands to be steady under any circumstances — under interrogation, under pursuit, under the particular terror of being almost remembered. But this steadiness was different. This was not the steadiness of concealment. It was the steadiness of a person standing on ground that would hold.

* * *

They had given her a year, formally, though no one had called it a trial. In the weeks after Hale's last ferry slid downriver with its defeated cargo of paperwork, the town had done what the town always did: it had watched her work.

She had expected suspicion. What she got instead was tasks. Odile handed her the fiddliest glue-ups in the shop, the ones requiring patience beyond apprenticeship. The miller asked her opinion on grain prices. When the refugee letters started arriving — families walking out of the fallen capital in ones and twos and then in dozens, the war over and nobody having won it — it was Coral who organized the bunks, who calculated rations, who taught the children of strangers where the privies were and which floorboards creaked. She did it with the same competence that had once moved coded paper across three borders, and the town absorbed her the way good joinery absorbs a peg: without comment, permanently.

There had been one hard evening. A woman among the refugees, a former clerk from the Ministry of Roads, had recognized something in Coral's manner — the way she scanned a room, the way her eyes found exits. She'd cornered Coral by the wash line and asked, quietly, whether they'd served together. Coral had stood there with wet sheets in her arms and felt the old machinery engage, the smooth denial rising like water finding its level.

And then she had set down the sheets and told the truth. Not everything. But enough. *I was a courier,* she said. *I carried things I should have burned.* The woman had looked at her for a long moment, and then she had said, "Half this valley is running from something the Republic made them do. You'll do." And she had taken the other end of the sheet, and they had hung the washing together, and that was that.

That was the thing about the Regiment, Coral had come to understand. It didn't forgive. Forgiveness implied a debt, and the Regiment didn't deal in debts between members — only in the ledger, only in work owed and work rendered. What it did instead was simpler and harder: it assumed you would keep showing up. Everything else followed from that.

* * *

"Whenever you're ready," Ellery said gently. "Ink dries at its own pace, but there's no rush in this building. There's never been a rush in this building."

Coral dipped the pen.

The entry itself had been drafted and redrafted in the workshop over the previous month, argued over at supper, defended by Tamsin with a ferocity that had made Coral laugh for the first time in years — really laugh, the undignified kind, the kind Sef would have teased her mercilessly for. Sef. She thought of him often now, and it hurt less each time, the way a well-set bone hurts less each season. His last letter still lived in the false bottom of her old dispatch case, the case itself empty now, its chain removed and hanging in the workshop as a reminder of nothing in particular. *Trust no handler,* he'd written. He'd been wrong in the way dying men are wrong — too absolute. The right rule, she'd decided, was: *choose whose hands you put yourself in, and then let them hold you.*

She wrote:

*Coral Okonkwo Vant, admitted to the Regiment this day by vote of the council and consent of the whole, apprentice to Odile Vant, master joiner. Debts: none outstanding. Skills: cipher, memory-craft, the reading of strangers, the keeping of quiet. Owed to her: wages in arrears, one winter's boots, and the gratitude of thirty-one souls sheltered through the hard months. Entered by her own hand.*

She signed beneath it. Her signature was small and clear, no flourish. For fifteen years she had practiced signatures that weren't hers — a dozen identities, each with its own handwriting drilled into muscle memory. Writing her own name felt strange in the hand, like speaking a language after years of silence. The letters came out slightly stiff. She didn't mind. Stiff things, Odile liked to say, are just things that haven't been worked yet.

Odile leaned over and inspected the entry the way she inspected a mortise. "Skills," she read aloud. "'The keeping of quiet.' Is that a skill we want in the book?"

"It's the skill that saved your workshop," Tamsin said.

"It's the skill that nearly burned it," Odile said. Then she looked at Coral, and her face did the thing it rarely did in public, the thing Coral had spent a year learning to read: the softening around the eyes, the almost-smile that lived there like a pilot light. "Which is why it stays in the book. We don't record only our virtues. We record our whole accounts. That's the difference between a ledger and a legend." She straightened up. "Well. It's done. Nobody's ever gotten out of it yet."

"Do people try?"

"One man, forty years back. Walked out in autumn, came back in spring, asked to be struck from the roll. Council said the book doesn't unwrite. He stayed anyway. Became our best cooper." Odile shrugged. "People think the bridge is the way in and out of here. It isn't. The bridge is just timber. The book is the way in, and there's no way out of it, because you carry it with you wherever you go."

* * *

Afterward they walked down to the river, all four of them — Ellery staying behind to press the page flat, the way he pressed every page, with stones and patience.

The new bridge had been up since midsummer, and it was beautiful in the way the Regiment made things beautiful: without ornament, without apology, every joint doing honest work. Coral had driven the final peg beside Tamsin and Odile, three mallets falling in rhythm while the whole town watched from both banks, and she had understood then — not decided, *understood*, the way you understand a joint is seated by the feel of it rather than the sight — that she was home. Not sheltering. Not hiding. Home.

Tamsin ran ahead onto the span and stood at the middle, over the deepest water, arms on the rail. "You know what I keep thinking?" she called back. "A year ago today you were probably someplace terrible, carrying somebody else's secrets."

"A year ago today I was in a barn loft outside Marrow Ford, eating raw turnips and memorizing a supply schedule," Coral said. "So. Yes."

"And now you eat cooked turnips like a civilized person, and the only secret you keep is where Odile hides the good sandpaper."

"That's a state secret," Coral said gravely. "I'll die before I tell."

Odile snorted. They crossed to stand on either side of Tamsin at the rail. Below, the river ran high and bright with snowmelt, carrying the last of the ice toward whatever low country waited past the gorge. Somewhere downriver was the capital, and the ruins of the Republic, and the mass graves, and the new flags already flying over the ministries — she'd heard the names, in the refugee letters, of the men who'd won. None of them would ever find this valley. And even if they did, they would find a town with its books in order and its charter buried in three generations of legal precedent and its militia rolls kept, as the forged dispatch had promised, as a supply depot of the Provisional Authority — a fiction that had hardened, over a year, into something functionally true. Paperwork, Hale had once told her, was how power talked to itself. She had turned his own weapon around and used it to build a wall.

"You're doing it again," Tamsin said.

"Doing what?"

"Counting exits. Your eyes go all — sideways." Tamsin tapped her own temple. "I can always tell. You're checking whether the world is still going to ask you to run."

Coral considered denying it. Old habit. Instead she looked downstream, at the bright destructive water, and found — genuinely found — nothing in herself that wanted to follow it.

"No," she said. "I was thinking that the river's the same river. Same water that trapped me here. Same water that would have carried me away if I'd wanted." She put her hand flat on the rail, on the wood she had helped raise, smelling faintly of linseed and sun. "It's the banks that changed."

Tamsin bumped her shoulder against Coral's shoulder, the way she did, wordless, sufficient. Odile pretended to be very interested in the joinery of the railing.

Downstream, past the bend, the road climbed away over the pass — the last road out, the first road in, depending entirely on which direction a person was facing. Coral had walked it twice in her life. She would walk it again someday, perhaps, carrying nothing but her own name, visiting the refugee settlements the town sponsored, teaching cipher in other valleys that wanted to learn to keep their own books. But that was travel. Travel was different. Travel was what you did when you had somewhere to come back to.

Behind them, up the hill, the council hall bell rang the hour, and Ellery would be closing the ledger now, pressing it shut under its stone, adding one more volume to the shelf of volumes that would outlast every empire she had ever served. Somewhere in that quiet stack, on a fresh page in fading gall-ink, her name sat drying — Coral Okonkwo Vant, courier no more — written by her own hand, witnessed by her own people, belonging nowhere else and needing to.

She stayed at the rail until the light went gold, and then she went up the hill to supper, because supper was at seven, and in the Regiment, you kept your accounts current.
