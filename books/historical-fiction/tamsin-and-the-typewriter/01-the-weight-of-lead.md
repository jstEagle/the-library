## 1. The Weight of Lead

There is a particular sound a printing shop makes at dawn, before the city has properly woken and decided to be difficult: a settling of metal, a creak of the old oak beams, the faint tick of the type cases as they warm with the stove. My father used to say the shop talked to him. He would stand in the middle of it with his arms folded like a man listening to an orchestra tune itself, and he'd announce, "The ten-pica case is sulking again, Bea."

I never heard the shop talk. What I hear, most mornings, is arithmetic.

Four hundred pounds of lead type in the racks, give or take what Wren's been pilfering for practice. Eleven reams of paper left from the autumn order, which means I have perhaps three weeks before I must either buy more — at prices that have doubled since the governor's new stamp tax, because of course they have — or begin printing on the backs of things people have already given up reading. The press itself, a Stanhope my father bought secondhand from a bankrupted bookseller in Marlowe's Reach, needs its platen packing renewed before spring, or every impression will come out grey as dishwater.

This is what I do instead of sleeping properly. I lie in the room above the shop and count the weight of my inheritance until the bell of St. Anselm's strikes six, and then I go down and lift it anyway.

* * *

The morning's work was sorting. There is no task in this trade more despised by apprentices and more beloved by masters, and I am apparently both. Distributing type — returning each letter to its compartment after a job — is the closest thing printing has to penance. You hold each piece upside down and backward, because that is how type lives, mirror-wise, waiting to be made legible only through pressure and paper and someone willing to stand over it while the ink dries. A lowercase e goes home to its box; a question mark goes home; a piece of someone's argument, set yesterday and struck today, goes home to sit in the dark with all the other unused words.

I find this comforting in a way I'd be embarrassed to explain to anyone who asked. Nothing in the case complains about being put away. Nothing in the case notices that I am a woman doing a dead man's work, alone, in a city that has lately begun to smell like a struck match.

Outside, the ice on the Vell was talking. It does that in late winter — a long groaning complaint, like a giant shifting in bed — and the bargemen stand on the frozen towpath stamping their boots and arguing about whether it will break clean or ugly this year. A clean break and the barges run in a fortnight. An ugly one, and the floes take out the piers, and half the fishwives starve until the channel clears. Everyone in Vellham reads the river the way priests read entrails. I read it too, though mostly I just want it to stop making noise while I'm trying to think.

By seven I had sorted two cases and mended the shop's third disaster of the season: the tympan had split along its old crease, and I'd glued a patch of good calfskin over it, weighting it with the flatiron while I worked. This is the other thing I do instead of grieving. My father died eleven months ago — his heart simply quit mid-sentence, between setting a wedding announcement and a burial notice, which even I admit was almost funny — and since then I have kept myself so thoroughly occupied with the machinery of the trade that I have not once had to sit still long enough for the grief to catch me. Work is a very fast horse. As long as you keep whipping it, it doesn't look back at you.

* * *

At half past eight, Mrs. Pell from the chandlery came in under pretense of needing five hundred billheads printed, and stayed forty minutes under pretense of watching me do it.

"They broke the windows at Grubb's warehouse last night," she said, in the tone of a woman reporting weather. "And there's a notice nailed to the Corn Exchange saying the militia will patrol from tonight. You'll want your shutters painted, Beatrix. They're marking doors, they say. Loyal houses get a white cross."

"Who's 'they'?"

"That's the trouble, isn't it." She picked up one of the billheads, examined my father's ornament border — acanthus leaves, cut years ago, worn soft now — and put it down carefully, as if it might be evidence. "Nobody knows who's writing those pamphlets. But everybody knows somebody who's read one."

I said nothing. In my experience, silence is the cheapest material in the shop, and I keep plenty of it in stock.

Mrs. Pell paid her deposit and left, and I stood a moment at the door watching the street. It looked like any street in Vellham: boys sweeping snow-slush into gutters, a milk cart, two militiamen walking their beat with the particular swagger of men who've been told they matter. But there were more knots of people than usual, standing close together, talking low. And on the corner post of the cooper's yard, someone had pasted a broadside — cheap paper, hasty ink, already curling at the edges.

I crossed the lane and read it. It was the usual fare lately: grain prices beside wage prices, set side by side so the arithmetic did the shouting. *A day's labor buys four loaves. A day's labor bought nine, two winters past.* Below it, in letters sized for anger rather than beauty: *WHO COUNTS THE COUNTING?*

Good line, whoever wrote it. Better than good — it had the economy you can't fake. Someone with a compositor's discipline had set that, someone who knew you don't waste ems on adjectives when a noun will bleed.

I tore it down, folded it into my apron pocket, and went back inside, telling myself I'd taken it down because loose bills make the neighborhood look disorderly, and disorderly neighborhoods get searched.

I am very good at telling myself things. It may be the finest craft I possess.

* * *

Midday, the light through the front windows goes amber and lands on the press like something holy, and if you stand in exactly the right place you can see the whole history of Eastwick's in one glance: my grandfather's specimen posters framed on the wall, yellowed; my father's master woodcuts above them; and beneath, the long workbench where I now ate bread and cheese standing up, because sitting down felt too much like admitting the day had a shape.

My father — Aldous Eastwick, printer to the parish, printer to the Custom House, printer to anyone with coin and clean copy — believed that a print shop should be open to any argument, provided the argument paid. He set sermons and seditious ballads within the same week and saw no contradiction, because he said the press was a river and not a church. Words come downstream, you set what floats to you, and God sorts the current.

He also believed, less philosophically and more practically, that his daughter should learn every part of the trade and then never be listed anywhere as having done so.

It wasn't cruelty. I want to be fair to him, because fairness is another thing I hoard. He taught me everything — casting, composing, locking formes, the black art of getting ink off your hands and the deeper art of getting it to stay on where you want it. He let me set pages when he had fever and couldn't see straight. But when the Guild came round, when the aldermen visited, when customers called, I was "the girl who keeps the books." Not the compositor. Never the printer. Vellham's printing trade is a guild of loud men with ink to their elbows and opinions to their knuckles, and my father, whatever else he was, was a survivor before he was anything else, and survival meant keeping me invisible. A woman who sets type openly gets the shop burned around her, he said. Or worse: she gets noticed, and noticed women in this city are like lit windows in a storm — everyone wants something from them, and nobody brings an umbrella.

So I learned to hide. In the back rooms, in the ledger columns, in the routine. I became very small and very competent, which is a combination that makes you nearly unseeable, and I told myself this was safety, and some days I even believed it.

Eleven months of lifting four hundred pounds of lead alone, and the Guild still sends its quarterly letter addressed to *the Estate of A. Eastwick*. The estate is me. The estate has hands. But paper is patient with lies, and so am I.

* * *

Afternoon brought the one job on the books: two hundred playbills for the Corinthian Rooms, their winter concert series, which meant setting the same elegant lines over and over until the music seemed to leak out of the type itself. I worked the composing stick with the automatic grace of long habit — and I will confess, since we are being honest in here, that I love it. I love the stick's cold weight in the left hand, the thumb's small dance, the way a line of type clicks together like a sentence deciding to exist. My father said I had "a quick eye for the white," meaning the space between letters, the silence that makes speech readable. Of all the things he gave me, I think that eye is the one I'd least survive losing.

Halfway through the fourth forme, the shop door opened without knocking.

Cold air, snow-smell, and a boy — no older than fourteen, cap in hands, cheeks raw. He looked around the shop with the wide-eyed caution of a sparrow in a barn, and I recognized him vaguely: one of the river-children who run errands along the quays for pennies.

"You're Eastwick's?" he said.

"The estate of," I said, which is my standard joke and got, as always, no laugh.

"I'm to ask if you do private work. Small work. Quick."

"We do all kinds. Who sent you?"

"A man." He shifted. "He said you'd know the price without asking twice, and he said—" the boy screwed up his face with the effort of exact recall, which told me the message mattered to whoever composed it— "he said, 'Tell her the white is where the truth hides.'"

I set down the composing stick. Slowly, the way you set down anything when your hands have gone strange.

That was my father's phrase. His own, offhand, tossed to me over a case of type when I was twelve and struggling with a crowded title page: *Don't cram it, Bea. The white is where the truth hides.* No one else in Vellham had reason to know it. I'd certainly never said it aloud to anyone since the funeral.

"Who is he?" I asked.

"Dunno. Grey coat, gloves, keeps his face turned. Paid me a crown not to look hard." The boy shrugged with magnificent indifference. "He said wait for a knock after dark, and don't answer it unless it's three slow and two quick."

"And if I don't answer?"

"He said you would." The boy glanced at the press, at the type walls, at me, with a flicker of something almost like pity. "Everybody answers, miss. That's what the man said. Everybody answers eventually."

Then he was gone, the door swinging, the cold folding shut behind him, and I stood in the amber light among my orderly, silent, obedient letters, feeling the floor tilt a degree beneath everything.

* * *

I finished the playbills. Of course I finished the playbills; there were two hundred due Thursday and panic is a luxury item I stopped stocking last year. I pulled the proofs, hung them, cleaned the roller, swept the floor, banked the stove. All the while my eyes kept going to the door, and my ears kept going to the stairs, and the afternoon crawled the way time does when you're pretending not to wait for it.

Three slow and two quick. I turned the rhythm over all evening like a coin I suspected of being clipped.

Here is the thing about hiding: it works beautifully right up until the moment someone knows exactly where you are. For eleven months I had been invisible, unlisted, the estate without a name, and I had begun to believe invisibility was armor. Now some stranger with grey gloves had walked past all of it — past the ledger name, past the shutters, past the careful nothing I presented to Vellham every day — and knocked directly on the one door I kept closed. He knew my father's words. Which meant either my father had said them to many people and I never knew the width of his life —

— or someone out there had known him. Known him well enough to quote him. Well enough to want something from what he'd left behind.

I ate supper standing up again. I did not taste it.

* * *

Nine o'clock. The shop dark except for the banked stove's orange seam and one candle I told myself I needed for mending the press's frayed cords, which could absolutely wait until morning. The river groaned outside, longer and lower than this morning, a sound like a held note finally breaking. Down the lane, the militia patrol passed, boots crunching, lantern-light sliding across my shuttered window and moving on.

Then: a knock. Three, slow. A pause you could park a cart in. Two, quick.

I stood in the middle of my shop, in the smell of ink and oil and my whole inherited life, and I thought about Mrs. Pell's white crosses, and the torn broadside still folded in my apron, and my father's voice saying *the press is a river,* and how I had spent my entire adulthood standing safely on the bank.

Everybody answers eventually. The boy was right about that much.

I blew out the candle — habit, or instinct, or cowardice wearing instinct's coat — and lifted the bar.

The door opened on darkness and snow-smell, and a figure stepped into the doorway, and the first thing I saw clearly, in the spill of the streetlamp behind them, was a pair of cuffs stained black to the wrist with ink.
