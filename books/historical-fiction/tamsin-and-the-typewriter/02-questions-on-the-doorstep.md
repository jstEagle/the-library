## 2. Questions on the Doorstep

The knock came at half past nine on a Tuesday, which is the hour and day reserved by God and the Ministry for bad news.

I knew this because my father taught it to me, in his way — not with lectures but with a lifetime of flinching at the door. A knock before ten meant a creditor. A knock after supper meant a customer who'd had ideas over ale. And a knock in the middle of ordinary working hours, firm and official, three raps and a pause like a man clearing his throat on wood — that meant the government had remembered we existed, which was never a thing to celebrate.

I put down my composing stick. I looked at the forme I'd been setting — a funeral notice for old Mrs. Halloway, forty-two points of dignity — and I considered, briefly, pretending the shop was closed. I had gotten quite good at not being home over the years. It's a skill, invisibility. You practice it the way a fiddler practices scales. My father used to say the Eastwicks survived Vellham by being too dull to tax and too useful to burn. He said it laughing. He meant it seriously.

But the door of Eastwick & Son Printers has one small pane of glass beside it, frosted with our name in gold leaf that has gone the color of weak tea, and through that glass I could see a figure standing very still. Not shifting weight. Not checking a pocket watch. Just standing, the way a heron stands over water, waiting for the river to make its mistake.

I opened the door.

"Good morning," the figure said. "I'm looking for the head of household."

"Then you've had a wasted walk," I said. "He's dead eleven months."

I want it noted that I did not plan to say that so plainly. It simply came out the way type comes off a badly inked roller — unevenly, with more pressure than intended. The stranger took it without blinking, which was my first clue that this was not an ordinary census man. Ordinary men blink when you hand them grief across a doorstep. They look down at their boots, they mumble about condolences, they perform sympathy the way a choirboy performs piety — correctly and without conviction. This one just looked at me, filed something away behind those eyes, and produced a ledger from under one arm with the smooth economy of a card sharp producing the ace they'd been sitting on all night.

"Then I'll count you," they said. "One household, one occupant. Name?"

"Beatrix Eastwick."

They wrote it. I watched them write it, because I watch everyone write things — occupational hazard, and also the closest thing to a religion I possess. The handwriting was small and upright and unnervingly neat, each letter seated in its row like a well-behaved congregation. The cuff of the right sleeve was stained black to the second buttonhole. Ink stains don't come from filling out census forms. Census forms take a page an hour, if that. Ink stains to the second buttonhole come from someone who writes the way other people breathe.

"Tamsin Quill," they said, though I hadn't asked. "Ministry of Records. Household census, revised schedule." A pause, precisely measured. "You're the printer's daughter."

"I'm the printer."

That got the first flicker of genuine expression — a quick up-and-down look, taking in my smocked apron, the lead dust ground into the creases of my knuckles, the bruise on my forearm where the press lever had caught me yesterday. Reassessing. I've spent my life being reassessed. Shopkeepers do it, priests do it, the men who come to buy type do it, and every single one of them arrives at the same surprised conclusion, as though a woman running a press were a dog playing the fiddle. This one arrived at the conclusion faster than most and hid it better than any.

"The late Mr. Eastwick's work is known to the Ministry," Tamsin said. "May I come in? There are questions beyond the count itself."

"There usually are."

"That wasn't a question."

"No," I said. "It was a warning."

And then — and here is where I will confess to the reader, since apparently I must confess everything eventually — I let them in. Not out of politeness. Politeness would have been giving them tea and asking after their mother. I let them in because I wanted to see what they'd look at. When you spend your days arranging other people's words into rows, you develop a taste for watching what people reach for first. It tells you everything. A man who goes straight to your specimen book is shopping. A man who goes straight to your waste bin is searching. A man who stands in the middle of your shop and looks at nothing in particular is memorizing it for someone else.

Tamsin Quill stood in the middle of my shop and looked at nothing in particular.

* * *

The shop smells the way it has always smelled, which is to say like oil, lampblack, and cold metal — the smell my father carried home in his clothes until I stopped being able to tell where he ended and the trade began. Two presses. The great one, Father's Albion, squatting in the back like a sleeping ox; the little hand-press by the window where I do jobbing work. Cases of type along the wall, four hundred drawers of small wooden coffins, each holding its little population of letters. The stone floor worn into shallow dips where three generations of Eastwick feet have stood at the same spots doing the same work.

Tamsin walked the room the way a customs officer walks a hold — slowly, hands clasped behind the back, touching nothing, missing nothing. I set Mrs. Halloway's notice aside and pretended to tidy a case of long primer, which gave me an excuse to watch sideways, which is the only direction I trust.

"You've kept everything," Tamsin observed. It wasn't quite a question either. This person had a gift for statements shaped like questions and questions shaped like accusations.

"Type holds its value."

"Mm. And the ledgers?"

"In the drawer where ledgers go."

"And your father's correspondence?"

"Correspondence burns. Paper's dear; ash is free." I said it lightly. I'd rehearsed it lightly, though I hadn't known I was rehearsing anything. That's the thing about hiding — done long enough, it stops being a performance and becomes a personality. I had been the invisible Eastwick for so many years that even my lies came out pre-worn, comfortable, broken in like good shoes.

Tamsin made a note. I saw only the shape of it — a short line, unhurried — and hated how much I wanted to read it.

"Occupation?" they asked, pen poised.

"Printer."

"Proprietor?"

"Both."

"Apprentices?"

"None."

"Employees?"

"I just told you I'm both."

"A form is a form," Tamsin said, with the mild patience of a person who has heard ten thousand objections to forms and buried them all in neat little graves of ink. "Employees?"

"One part-time. A boy who sweeps and runs deliveries. He's not here mornings."

Name, age, address of the boy. I gave them Wren's brother's name — Tam Ossel, fourteen, honest as bread — and felt the small hot coal of guilt settle in my chest, because Tam has never swept anything in his life and doesn't exist at the address I gave, which is the Ossels' old stable, which burned. But a census is a net, and a net is only dangerous if it catches something. Better it catch a boy who isn't there than a girl who is.

Because that was the arithmetic underneath my morning, the sum I was carrying while I answered questions with the flat calm of a woman discussing the weather: there were pamphlets in this city with my father's fingerprints all over them, and my father had been dead eleven months, and I could explain neither fact to my own satisfaction, and here stood the Ministry at my counter with a pen already wet.

* * *

Here is what I mean. Three weeks past, a pamphlet had come around the district the way pamphlets do — passed hand to hand, left on benches, tucked into market baskets like contraband plums. *The Grievances of Vellham*, it called itself. Six pages, cheap paper, no printer's name. And it listed things the city preferred not listed: the grain levy that doubled while wages halved, the men pressed onto the river barges without pay, the prisoner who died in the Marshgate cells and was recorded as having "departed of natural inclination." It named names. It cited dates. It quoted, word for word, a speech the governor had given to the wool factors' guild — a speech that had never been printed anywhere, that existed only in the memory of the forty men in that room.

And it was set in my father's typeface.

Not similar. Not a cousin of it. His. The deep-bowled 'a' he cut himself because he thought commercial foundries made them too tight. The 'g' with the flat ear he'd carved from a spare piece of brass one winter because he was bored and brilliant and couldn't leave well enough alone. Any compositor in Vellham would know it the way a woman knows her mother's handwriting on an envelope. Half the city's printers had learned their trade on cases cast from Eastwick matrices.

So either someone had stolen or bought a font of my father's type — possible; he sold casts freely, generosity being another of the traits that kept us poor — or someone was printing with the actual case that sat thirty feet from where I slept, in a shop I locked every night with a key that lived on a string around my neck.

I had turned the shop inside out twice looking for evidence. I found none. No fresh impressions, no spilled type, no disturbed dust — and I know my dust the way a shepherd knows his sheep. Whatever was happening was happening cleanly, or elsewhere, or in some manner I lacked the imagination to conceive. So I had done what Eastwicks do. I had said nothing, seen nothing, and waited for the river to decide.

And now the river had sent me a heron.

* * *

"May I see the presses?" Tamsin asked. "For the inventory column. Equipment valuation."

"Valuation is on page four of the form. You haven't reached page four."

"I like to be thorough."

"Thoroughness," I said, stepping aside anyway, "is how ministries turn into inquisitions. One clerk wants to be thorough, then his superior wants to be thorougher, and within a decade they're counting teeth."

Something moved at the corner of Tamsin's mouth. Not quite a smile. The place where a smile would live if the building hadn't been condemned. "Noted," they said, and wrote something down, and I had the distinct uncomfortable feeling they had written down *counting teeth*.

They went first to the little jobbing press, ran one finger along the platen, checked the dust — I saw them check the dust, saw them note its evenness, saw them note that I saw — and then to the big Albion in the back. They stood before it longer than a valuation requires. Their hand hovered near the press bed without landing, the way people hover near relics.

"My father worked a press like this," they said suddenly. "In Corvess, before the mills closed it down. He'd let me stand on a crate and pull the bar. I was six. I pulled it with my whole body and the impression came out crooked and he kept it pinned above his bench for a year. Called it my first edition."

The words came out level, but underneath them was something else, the way you can hear water moving under ice. I hadn't asked. I hadn't encouraged. Tamsin Quill had simply decided, for reasons of their own, to put a piece of themselves on my counter like a coin, to see what I'd give in change.

I gave nothing. Old habit. But I noticed — and noticing, in my experience, is the first step of every trouble.

"Your equipment is sound," they said, recovering their official voice like a man retrieving a dropped glove. "The Albion especially. That's a Ruthven casting, isn't it? Pre-war?"

"It is."

"They don't make them anymore."

"No," I said. "They don't make much anymore. They mostly unmake."

Another note. Longer, this time. I began, quietly, to catalogue what this stranger knew about printing — which was real, not decorative, the knowledge of someone who'd grown up around it — and to wonder why a census taker needed to know anything at all about presses. The census counts heads. Heads have no platens.

* * *

It happened at the type wall. I should have expected it; I should have steered them to the window, offered them a view of the frozen gutter, discussed the weather with the desperation of a hostess whose husband is drunk in the pantry. But Tamsin drifted along the cases with their hands clasped, reading the labels — Brevier, Minion, Long Primer, Great Canon — and stopped at the third case from the end.

Father's case. The one with the brass-cut face. I keep it locked now. I keep it locked because of the pamphlets, because of the suspicion I can't prove, because locking it was the one thing I could think of to do, and a lock is a prayer addressed to locks.

Tamsin touched the padlock. Lightly. Two fingers.

"This one's different from the others."

"It's temperamental."

"A lock is temperamental?"

"The case. The type sticks. I keep it shut so it stays clean."

"You print with it, though." Not a question. A trap laid gently, baited with logic, and I stepped in it with both feet because I was tired and off balance and because some traitor part of me wanted to talk about my father's typeface to the first person in eleven months who'd looked at it like treasure instead of scrap metal.

"On occasion. For fine work."

"Hm." Tamsin crouched, brought their eyes level with the case's lower drawers, and I watched them do what I had done three weeks ago with the pamphlet held to the lamp — compare. Compare the label lettering. Compare the wear patterns. File. Then they straightened, and from the inner pocket of their coat they drew a folded paper, and they unfolded it, and they held it out to me with the neutral courtesy of a man offering directions.

"Does this come from your shop?"

I took it. Of course I took it; refusing would have been worse. *The Grievances of Vellham*. Page two. The governor's speech, quoted whole. Set in brass-cut brevier with the deep-bowled 'a' and the flat-eared 'g', and there — I felt my stomach drop through the floorboards and keep falling — there, in the last line, a kerned ligature of 'ct' that exists in exactly one font in the city of Vellham, because my father cut that ligature himself as a joke, as a signature, as a private wink to anyone who knew how to look.

"It comes from a printer," I said. "Vellham has eleven."

"Ten, since Marlowe's closed." Tamsin's voice stayed pleasant, almost apologetic. "This face is unusual. Distinctive. Someone in the Ministry thinks it may be distinctive enough to trace."

"Someone in the Ministry," I said, handing the paper back, "should learn to count heads and leave letters alone."

"Perhaps. But the Ministry has developed a recent interest in letters. In who sets them, and where, and why they keep appearing in places that inconvenience important men." They refolded the pamphlet with two crisp movements and put it away. "You understand. It's not personal. I'm required to ask every printer in the ward the same questions."

"And do you believe the answers?"

The question escaped me before I could weigh it, and I regretted it instantly, because it was too sharp, too invested — the answer of a woman with something under her floorboards rather than a woman with nothing to hide. Tamsin looked at me for a long moment. Herons look like that before they strike. Or before they decide not to strike. From the outside, the two looks are identical.

"I believe," they said finally, "that answers are like type. What matters isn't what's cast, it's how it's distributed." They clicked their pen shut. "Thank you for your time, Mistress Eastwick. One occupant, one proprietor, no apprentices present, equipment valued per attached schedule. I'll file it today."

"At the Ministry."

"At the Ministry."

"And it will sit in a drawer."

"All documents sit in drawers," Tamsin said. "Until someone takes them out."

They saw themselves to the door. At the threshold they paused — one hand on the frame, the frosted glass throwing weak gold light across their shoulder — and looked back at the type wall, at the locked case, for exactly as long as it takes to commit a room to memory.

"Mind the damp," they said. "It's hard on type. And on paper."

Then they were gone, footsteps fading down Coppersmith Lane, unhurried as a tide going out.

* * *

I stood in the middle of my shop for a long while afterward, doing sums.

A census taker with a printer's childhood. Ink-stained cuffs and a memory like a compositor's stick. Questions arranged in careful rows, and one of them baited. And beneath it all, that odd moment at the press — the story about the father and the crooked first edition — offered like a key, or like a test of whether I'd pick it up.

I went to the locked case. I unlocked it. I lifted out a handful of my father's type and let it run through my fingers, cool and heavy, each letter a small dense piece of him, and I thought: whoever printed that pamphlet used these letters, or their twins. And whoever asked me about it today wrote everything down, and everything written down in this city lately seems to end up in the same drawer, and the drawer opens for someone.

Outside, the wind had shifted; it came off the river smelling of thaw and coal smoke, and somewhere down toward the docks the ice groaned — a long, low complaint, like a giant turning over in a bed too small for it. Spring was coming. Things under surfaces were going to start moving.

I put the type away. I locked the case. And I sat down at my bench and pulled the ministry form out of my mind and examined it, the way I'd examine a suspect proof, and found the error in it — the one detail Tamsin Quill had gotten wrong, or pretended to get wrong.

They had never once asked my father's name.
