## 3. What the Census Wants

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in government offices, and I have spent six years learning to breathe it. It is not peace. It is the silence of a room full of people pretending their work matters more than it does, which is a different thing entirely — a silence with teeth in it.

The Ministry of Enumeration occupied the third floor of the Customs House, above the salt stores and below whatever department had the misfortune of being blamed for everything else. My desk sat by the window where I could watch the river when the clerks weren't watching me. The river was honest, at least. It went where it went and froze when it froze and nobody asked it to account for itself on a quarterly basis.

"Quillby."

I did not look up immediately. One learns that in the Ministry: never look up too fast. Eagerness is noticed, and noticed things get used.

"Quillby." Sharper now. Mr. Harrow, senior clerk, keeper of the ledgers, a man built like a filing cabinet and about as warm. He stood at my desk holding a folder the color of weak tea, which meant it came from upstairs, which meant it was trouble wearing an official seal.

"Sir." I set down my pen with the deliberate slowness of a person who has nothing to hide and would like everyone to know it.

He dropped the folder on my blotter. "New directive. From the Undersecretary's office." He said *Undersecretary* the way other men said *plague*. "You'll want to read it before tomorrow's rounds."

I read it. That was my first mistake, though I didn't know it yet. A good Ministry man would have skimmed it, nodded, and filed it somewhere in his conscience between *not my department* and *someone else's problem*.

The directive was three pages long. The first page was about household counts — normal business, ink and tedium. The second page introduced a supplementary schedule, Form 11-C, to be appended to each district census. The third page explained what Form 11-C was for.

*Enumerators are further instructed,* it said, in the Ministry's favorite dialect — passive voice, no actor, no accountability — *to record all premises within their assigned districts where printing apparatus is maintained, together with the names of persons operating same, the nature of materials produced, and any indication of production of unlicensed or seditious matter.*

Seditious. There was the word, sitting there on official paper like a spider someone had pressed into the page while it was still wet.

"Printing apparatus," I said.

"You can read. Good. Saves time."

"And what happens to these lists, sir? Once compiled?"

Harrow shrugged, which from Harrow was a whole speech. "They go upstairs. Upstairs sends them wherever they go." He adjusted his cuffs. "Not our concern how they're used. Our concern is accuracy. You've got the Millwright Ward, haven't you?"

"I have Millwright Ward."

"Good density of trades there. Shops, workshops, that print place on Cordwainer Lane." He said *that print place* the way you'd say *that dog that bit people*. "Eastwick's, it's called. Old man Eastwick ran it for thirty years. Died in autumn, I hear. There's a daughter, apparently."

"Apparently," I said, and heard something in my own voice I didn't care for. Curiosity, maybe. Or its disreputable cousin, interest.

"The daughter kept the shop open, they say. Unusual." He let the word sit there doing its work. "Women don't usually keep shops open, Quillby. They usually keep houses."

"They usually aren't asked to," I said, and then regretted it, because Harrow wrote things down. Not formally — he wasn't taking notes — but in that ledger behind his eyes where he kept accounts of who was loyal and who was merely employed.

"Hm," he said, which in Harrow's currency was roughly equivalent to *I've marked your card*, and walked away.

*

That evening I took the directive home with me, which was against regulations, and read it again by lamplight, which was against sense.

My rooms were above a chandler's off Tanner's Row — one room, a stove that smoked if the wind came from the east, and a window that looked out on nothing but another wall. I had lived there four years and knew the wallpaper better than most of my neighbors. This is not a complaint. Walls are reliable. Walls do not ask you what you believe.

I laid Form 11-C on the table and looked at it for a long time.

Here is what you should understand about me, since we're going to be spending some time together: I am very good at my job. Not the counting — anyone can count. I mean the other part, the part that isn't written into any manual. When I come to a door, I see the house. I see which shutter hangs crooked, which means money trouble or a bad winter or a husband who drinks. I see whether the children's shoes match the mother's story about wages. I see the books stacked under the bed that aren't on any inventory because inventories don't count books unless you make them.

A census taker sees everything and writes down almost none of it. The gap between those two things — the seen and the recorded — is where I have made my home for six years. It is a narrow home but a sturdy one.

And now the Ministry wanted to move the wall.

Because here is the thing about Form 11-C that Harrow either didn't know or didn't say: lists like that don't sit in drawers. Lists become actions. I'd been in Vellham long enough to remember the weavers' register, eight years back — names of men who'd struck over piece rates, collected by well-meaning enumerators doing their duty, filed upstairs, and followed, within the season, by arrests, evictions, two drownings in the canal that the coroner called accidents because the coroner also ate from the Ministry's hand. Nobody ever connected the list to the drownings officially. Everybody connected them privately. That's the difference between a record and a truth.

I held the form over the stove. Just to feel it. To know what it would be like.

The paper warmed. The ink didn't even smudge. Official ink doesn't smudge; that's practically its defining virtue. It stays exactly where it's put, legible forever, ready to be acted upon by whoever comes asking later.

I put it back on the table.

This is the part I'm least proud of, so I'll tell it plainly: I didn't refuse. Refusal has consequences, and I had seen them — Quist the enumerator, dismissed without pension for questioning a directive; the woman in Salt Ward whose shop license developed problems after her husband signed a petition. I told myself I was being practical. I told myself I could fill the form thinly, vaguely, that a name on a list wasn't a sentence, that the list might go nowhere, that upstairs lost papers all the time.

All true. All cowardice dressed in true clothes, which is the most comfortable kind.

But something had cracked, and cracks don't close. Somewhere between *printing apparatus* and *seditious matter*, the Ministry had stopped being a building I worked in and become a thing I was inside of, and I had looked up from my desk — really looked up, for the first time in years — and found I couldn't see the edges of it.

I slept badly. The river ice groaned all night, the way it does in deep winter, that long complaining sound like a giant shifting in its sleep. In the morning it was still frozen. Everything was still frozen. But I lay there listening to it and thought: it always sounds like breaking before it breaks.

Then I got up, and dressed, and picked up my satchel, and went out to count the city.

*

Millwright Ward in January is a study in gray endurance. Smoke stands over the rooftops in columns so straight they look drawn on. The cobbles wear a skin of refrozen slush that takes ankles as tribute. People move fast with their chins tucked and pretend not to see the census man coming, because the census man means questions, and questions in Vellham that winter were starting to feel less like paperwork and more like exposure.

I worked my way down Cordwainer Lane door by door. Widow Marsh, three lodgers, one loom, no complaints she'd admit to. The Ferro family, seven souls, a locksmithy that smelled wonderfully of oil and brass filings. A boarding house where the landlady counted her lodgers twice, once for me and once, I suspected, for whoever else might come asking, and gave me different numbers both times. I wrote down neither. Some arithmetic is mercy.

By late morning I stood across the lane from Eastwick's.

From the outside it looked like what it was: an old shop, older sign, windows fogged with the breath of type metal and ink and the small stubborn economy of a place that refuses to die. The sign said EASTWICK & SON, which interested me, because Harrow had said daughter, and because signs are promises buildings make and don't always keep.

I knocked.

She opened the door herself — the daughter. Small woman, ink to the elbows, hair escaping its pins in a way that suggested pins were a formality she tolerated rather than honored. She had the look of someone interrupted mid-thought, and the deeper look, underneath, of someone who had been interrupted mid-thought so many times that she'd begun building walls around the thought itself.

"Mistress Eastwick? Beatrix Eastwick?" I consulted my book, mostly for theater. "Tamsin Quillby, Ministry of Enumeration. Household census. It won't take long."

"It never does," she said, but she stepped aside and let me in, and I noted that — the reluctance, and the courtesy overcoming it. Both were data. Everything is data; that's the occupational hazard.

Inside, the shop smelled the way I imagine old churches smell to people who believe in them. Ink and paper and oil and cold iron and, beneath it, something faintly sweet — the paste they use for binding, maybe, or just age settling into the wood. The press dominated the middle of the room like a sleeping animal, all black iron and wooden beams, and along the walls ran cases of type, hundreds of little wooden drawers, each one labeled in a hand that had gone elegant through use.

I have been in perhaps forty print shops in my working life. I want to be clear that this one was different, and I want to be equally clear that I cannot fully explain why. It was tidier than most. It was quieter than most. And it was quiet in the specific way of a room where someone has been listening hard for footsteps.

"So." I opened my book. "Household. Who resides here?"

"Just myself."

"Just yourself." I wrote it. Then I let the pause stretch, because pauses are free and answers are not. "A large establishment for one person. Two presses, I'd guess — there's a second frame under that canvas. Who operates them?"

"I do."

"All aspects? Composition, presswork, distribution?"

Her chin came up a fraction. "Is composition a census question, Master Quillby?"

"Tamsin," I said, before I could stop myself, and watched surprise flicker through her like light through water. "Quillby. Yes. Well. The Ministry likes thoroughness." Which was true, and was becoming, page by page of Form 11-C in my satchel, a thoroughly ugly thing.

She gave me the numbers then — one resident, one trade, no apprentices, no journeymen. All of it probably true. What interested me was what circled around the edges of the true. Her eyes went, twice, not to the press but to a type case near the far window — an old case, darker than the others, sitting slightly apart like a guest who'd overstayed. And when a cart rattled past outside, loud on the frozen cobbles, she went still in a way that had nothing to do with carts.

I closed my book. "That's the household done. Thank you for your patience."

"That's it?"

"For today." I smiled what I hoped was an apologetic smile. "The Ministry does love its return visits."

At the door I paused, because I am weak, and because the question had been sitting in my chest since Harrow's office like a stone swallowed wrong. "Your father," I said. "Elias Eastwick. The Ministry records show him as proprietor until his death. He was... quite known in the trade, I understand."

Something happened in her face. Grief, yes, but grief of a strange shape — grief layered over alertness, mourning worn like armor. "He was known," she agreed.

"He printed pamphlets once, didn't he? Years ago. Sermons, almanacs, that sort of thing."

"Sermons and almanacs," she said evenly, "and bills of sale and funeral invitations and every scrap of honest work this ward brought to his door. If the Ministry remembers anything else about him, Master Quillby, the Ministry remembers more than I do."

It was beautifully done. Every word defensible, every word a locked door painted to look open. I nodded, thanked her, and stepped out into the cold with my heart going harder than a doorway conversation warranted.

*

I didn't fill in Form 11-C that night. I want that on the record, such as it is.

I sat in my room above the chandler's with the form before me and the directive beside it and my pen uncapped, and I wrote *Eastwick, B., Cordwainer Lane — printing apparatus, one press (two?), sole operator, widow's daughter, deceased father formerly proprietor* — and then I stopped, because I saw it, suddenly and completely, the way you see a fall only after it's happened: I saw the list finished. I saw it going upstairs. I saw it crossing whatever desk decided such things, and I saw it coming back down converted into boots. *Premises where printing apparatus is maintained.* Boots on Cordwainer Lane. Boots on every lane with a press behind its doors. And at the center of it, a small woman with ink on her arms standing in a shop full of her dead father's letters, holding a door she couldn't hold forever.

The ice groaned under the window. That long, patient, terrible sound.

Here is what I decided, and I want to be precise about it, because precision is the only integrity I have left: I did not decide to betray the Ministry. I decided to write the truth slowly. I would log the shops — refusal would only bring another enumerator, a hungrier one, a Harrow-trained one. But I would log them thin. No conjecture. No *indications of unlicensed matter*, which is the phrase that kills, because *indication* means whatever the reader wants it to mean. I would give the Ministry facts and nothing else, and facts, I told myself, hurt no one.

I knew even then that this was a half-measure. Half-measures are what cowards call principles. But a crack, I reminded myself, runs both ways. A crack lets the cold in, yes — but it also lets the light out. I was choosing, in my small clerkly way, which direction mine would run.

I filled in three entries that night, each one drier than communion wafer. Eastwick's went last. I sat over it a long while.

In the end I wrote: *Eastwick premises, Cordwainer Lane. Apparatus present, aged, partially disused following proprietor's death. Current operations appear limited to jobbing work.* 

*Appears limited.* Two words doing enormous labor. Every fact in the entry true, and the entry itself a fortress with the gates left quietly unbolted. Let Crane or whoever came after squint at *appears*. Let them wonder whether the enumerator was dull or careful. Dullness, I have found, is the best camouflage in the world, because no one suspects it of ambition.

I blotted the page. I felt — I want to report this honestly — I felt sick, and I felt free, and I felt both about the same small act of adverbial hedging, which tells you something about how low the bar for heroism sits in a census man's soul.

Outside, the river spoke again. Groan, settle, groan. Weeks yet before the break, the bargemen said. Weeks of everything holding, everything straining, everything pretending to be solid.

I capped my ink, and blew out the lamp, and lay in the dark thinking of a dark type case by a frosted window, and of the way Beatrix Eastwick had gone still at the sound of a cart — still the way a person goes still when they are used to listening for boots.

Somebody was afraid in that shop. Somebody was hiding something in it, or from it, or both. And I had just written down the address.

The next morning, Form 11-C went upstairs in the morning post, and I went back out into the gray with my satchel and my book and my brand-new, carefully rationed conscience, telling myself the worst was filed and done.

I believed that for almost exactly four days.
