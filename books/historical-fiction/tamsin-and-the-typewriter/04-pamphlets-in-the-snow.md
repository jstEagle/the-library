## 4. Pamphlets in the Snow

Snow has opinions about Vellham. It falls on the rich quarters first, softening the slate roofs of the Governor's Hill like a kind word before an insult, and by the time it reaches Tanner's Row it has collected soot from a hundred chimneys and comes down grey, mean, and determined to be someone else's problem. My father used to say the city's weather was as honest as its ledgers — everything clean at the top and filthy by the time it trickled down. He said it while setting type, which was his way of saying dangerous things without saying them.

He'd been dead eleven months when I found the pamphlets.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is the only inheritance he left me that doesn't weigh anything. Eleven months, three weeks, and four days. Not that I was counting. Counting is simply what happens to a mind that sorts small metal letters for a living; it counts everything, whether you ask it to or not. Days since the funeral: three hundred forty-nine. Customers since Tuesday: one, and he only wanted a bill of sale for a mule. Pamphlets in my father's type case: I didn't know yet. That was the trouble.

* * *

The morning it happened, I was doing what I did most mornings, which was losing an argument with the shop.

Eastwick & Son, said the sign over the door, though there had never been a son, and now there wasn't even an Eastwick in any sense the neighborhood recognized. There was only me, Beatrix, who had spent twenty-six years being the daughter who swept the floor and fetched the coffee and knew where every ligature in the shop lived, and who had somehow failed to become a printer in anyone's estimation but her own. When Father died, the guild sent a delegation of four men to express condolences and, in the same breath, to inform me that the shop's license would need "regularizing." Regularizing turned out to mean: we will let you keep sweeping if you don't make noise about it.

So I kept the shop open because closing it would have meant admitting something had ended, and I set type for the tradesmen who still came, and I mended the press, and I did not make noise. It's remarkable how quiet a person can become when she decides invisibility is safer than ambition. I had gotten very good at it. Good enough that some mornings I startled myself coming around the corner of the composing room, as if I were a stranger trespassing.

That morning the argument was with the job case — the old one, the big walnut cabinet with thirty-two drawers that Father had bought secondhand from a bankrupt almanac printer in the year I was born. It sat in the back corner like a dowager aunt, creaking, opinionated, full of things nobody needed. I was reorganizing it because reorganizing is grief's favorite chore. You can't argue with the dead, but you can alphabetize their drawers.

Drawer fourteen was the long primer, and it jammed. It always jammed; the runner had swelled with damp years ago and Father had never fixed it because, he claimed, a case with no resistance made a lazy compositor. I pulled. The drawer stuck, groaned, gave way all at once — and came out too far, past its stop, tilting into my hands with a weight that was wrong. Too heavy for type alone.

I set it on the workbench and looked inside.

Under the trays of long primer, where the bottom of the drawer should have been flat and empty, there was a false floor. A panel of thin pine, cut to fit, sitting maybe two inches proud of where it ought to sit. And poking up at one corner, white against the dark wood, the edge of paper.

I stood there a long moment with my hands in the drawer like a woman reading entrails. The stove ticked. Snow tapped the window with the patience of a creditor. Somewhere out on the Row, a cart horse slipped and swore in the universal language of cart horses.

Here is what you should know about me: my first instinct in every crisis is tidiness. Fear, in my experience, looks exactly like the urge to put things back where they were. I nearly did it. I nearly slid the drawer home and went back to arguing with the em quads and let whatever was under that board stay buried another year, another decade, until the case rotted or I did.

But my father had built that false bottom. I knew his joinery the way you know a handwriting — the dovetails cut slightly shallow on the left side because he'd broken that wrist falling off a barge at nineteen and never told anyone why. Nobody else could have made that drawer lie so convincingly. Which meant that whatever was hidden in it, he had hidden it himself, and he had hidden it from me, and he had died without telling me it existed.

That decided me faster than courage would have. Curiosity is a much better lever than bravery; it doesn't require you to be brave first.

I lifted the panel out.

* * *

There were perhaps forty pamphlets, folded once, bound in bundles of ten with plain twine. Cheap paper, the kind sold by the ream to chandlers and fishmongers for wrapping. On top lay a loose printed sheet, unfolded, as if whoever had stowed the rest had been reading it last.

The title read: *A True Account of the Seizures at Coalport Wharf, Set Down by Those Who Saw.*

No author. No printer's imprint. Nothing but the title, the date — the eleventh of October, this past autumn, seven months after my father's coffin went into the ground at Saint Ferrel's — and beneath the text, nothing at all, which is itself a kind of signature, if you know how to read it.

I know how to read it. God help me, I know how to read it the way a mother knows a child's cry through three closed doors.

The body text was set in Caslon, old style, the face Father favored for anything meant to be believed. But look closer — and I looked, because looking closely at type is not a skill I can switch off any more than breathing — look closer and there it was. In the word *seizures*, the *z*, that particular slightly-narrowed *z* he cut down himself with a graver because he thought the foundry's version stood too wide among lowercase. A modified sort, unique to our shop, unique to his hand. We owned perhaps six of them. They lived in the case by the window.

And the spacing. Whoever set this page had wordspaced wide after commas and tight after periods — backwards from every rule in every manual — because that was how Aldous Eastwick composed, a habit so ingrained it survived him the way a limp survives its injury. I had teased him about it for twenty years. *Papa, you set copy like a man fleeing a fire.* He'd say a man fleeing a fire gets the news out first.

The pamphlet in my hands was set like a man fleeing a fire.

It was also set seven months after the man was dead.

I sat down on the stool. Not gracefully. My knees made their own decisions.

*The seizures at Coalport Wharf.* Everyone in Vellham had heard whispers — grain barges requisitioned without payment, a warehouse keeper named Pell drowned under circumstances the coroner declined to examine too closely, three dockworkers taken by soldiers in the night and returned, weeks later, as a list of names read out at a funeral no family was permitted to organize properly. The whispers grew louder all winter. They were part of why the census men had come sniffing down the Row, counting households and, rumor said, counting other things besides. But whispers are weather. Paper is evidence. Paper has weight and date and a typeface, and a typeface has a home.

And this typeface's home was my shop.

Which left exactly two possibilities, and I turned them over like coins to check for clipping. Either someone had come into my shop — my locked, shuttered, snowed-upon shop — and used my father's altered sorts to print sedition in his style, after his death, and then hidden the results in a compartment they could not have known about unless they knew my father's habits as well as I did. Or —

Or the third possibility, the one I didn't want, the one that sat in my chest like swallowed ice. That the pamphlets were older than their date. That they'd been printed before he died, backdated, prepared — and that my father, quiet, careful, head-down Aldous Eastwick, had been part of something I had never seen, in a shop I had swept every day of my life, and had trusted the hiding of it to a false-bottomed drawer rather than to his own daughter.

I didn't like any of the coins. All of them came up wrong.

* * *

I did what any sensible tradeswoman does with an impossible discovery. I made coffee, and I read everything.

Forty pamphlets, five distinct titles. *A True Account of the Seizures at Coalport Wharf.* *On the Unlawful Quartering of Soldiers in Private Houses.* *The Case of Anselm Pell, Warehouse-Keeper.* *Names of the Taken, with Their Trades and Parishes.* And one more, shorter than the rest, cruder, printed on even cheaper stock: *What the Census Counts, and What It Doesn't.*

That last one made my neck prickle, and not only because of the subject. The composition was worse — looser spacing, two typos I could see, a *w* inverted in *wharf* that had been corrected in later impressions. Someone learning. Someone new at the case, working fast, working angry.

The others were different. The others were *good*. Measured, damning, beautifully argued — each one ending with the same line, set small and centered:

*Set down so that it cannot be unsaid.*

Father's phrasing. I heard it in his voice. He used to say it about obituaries, of all things — he took the newspaper's death notices as a solemn trust, insisting that a life reduced to type deserved the best kerning in the shop. *Set it true, Trixie,* he'd tell me, *because print is the only promise that outlives the promiser. Set it down so it cannot be unsaid.*

Trixie. Nobody had called me that in eleven months, three weeks, and four days. My eyes were doing something inconvenient, so I blew on my coffee and blamed the steam.

The arithmetic refused to behave. If the pamphlets predated his death, why bear October dates? Unless they'd been printed recently — within the season — on his types, in his manner, by someone with access. The lock on the street door was old but sound. The cellar hatch, though. The cellar hatch had a latch that hadn't latched properly since the flood of two winters ago, and I'd been meaning to fix it the way I'd been meaning to do half the repairs in this building, which is to say with the particular diligence of a woman who has quietly stopped believing in tomorrow.

Someone had been coming in through my cellar. Setting type by night in my shop. Printing accusations against the government on my father's presses — no, on the *small* proofing press, I realized, checking; the great iron hand press stood untouched, its tympan folded neatly, but the little Washington press in the corner bore fresh ink ghosts on the platen, and the ink slab smelled of last week, not last decade.

And they had known about the drawer.

I looked at the empty compartment for a long time. Then, because I am my father's daughter down to the marrow and the marrow apparently runs toward concealment, I did not report the find to anyone. I counted the pamphlets again (forty-one; the loose sheet made forty-two), wrapped them in oilcloth, and considered where a sensible woman would hide them.

Then I laughed at myself, alone, in an empty shop, which is the saddest sound there is — because the sensible hiding place, the place that had already proven itself across months and possibly years, was the drawer. Whoever used this shop hid things in drawer fourteen. Fine. I returned the bundle to its compartment, replaced the false bottom, slid the tray of long primer over it, and left the drawer sticking exactly as badly as ever. Let the jam be a lock. My father would have appreciated the economy.

What I did change was the cellar latch. That I fixed that same afternoon, properly, with a new hasp and a padlock whose key went on a cord around my neck, under my shirt, where it lay against my sternum like a small cold fact.

Then I waited for dark, because I intended to meet whoever was walking through my father's ghost.

* * *

Waiting, I'll admit, is not my strongest discipline. Patience at the case, yes — I could space a line of twelve-point for an hour without fidgeting. But waiting to be afraid, on purpose, in the cold, that's a different trade entirely. By nine o'clock I had rearranged the distribution bins twice, sharpened three knives that didn't need it, and drunk coffee enough to vibrate at a frequency visible to dogs.

I banked the stove so the shop cooled, doused the lamp in the front room, and settled into the chair behind the paper store with a blanket and the patience of a spider. Through the gap between the cases I could see the cellar door and, beyond the frosted window, the lane, where snow came down in that thick, silent, conspiratorial way it has, laying a fresh page over the whole city and inviting everyone to sign it.

Whoever walked to my door tonight would walk in print.

I thought about Father while I waited. That was the trouble with waiting — it left room. I thought about the nights he'd worked late, which I'd always taken for devotion to craft, and which might have been something else entirely. I thought about the men from the guild, and how their eyes had gone over the shop during their condolence call, quick and inventorying, like appraisers at a bankruptcy. I thought about how he'd taught me to set type — patiently, seriously, never treating it as a curiosity for a girl but as a birthright — and how he'd never once let me print anything under my own name. *Shops have names, Trixie. Names on type get read by everyone, including the wrong everyone.* I'd assumed it was protectiveness. Sitting in the dark with a blanket around my shoulders, I began to suspect it had been training. All those years of invisible competence — maybe he wasn't keeping me out of the work. Maybe he was keeping me safe in it, the way you keep a lantern lit behind shuttered glass.

Or maybe I was romanticizing a dead man, which is the national pastime of grieving daughters everywhere. The dead are easy to make brave. They can't contradict you.

Somewhere past midnight — I had stopped pretending I wouldn't stay the whole night somewhere around the second knife-sharpening — I heard the hatch.

Not the street door. The cellar. The old latch, before my repair, had a particular complaint, a wooden knock and scrape, and my body remembered it before my mind did, going still in a way that felt ancestral. Footsteps on the stairs. Soft ones. Careful ones. Then silence, the listening kind, and I held my breath and listened back, and for a moment the shop and I were perfectly aligned in our attention, every drawer and forme and hanging sheet of drying paper straining toward the dark like fur toward lightning.

A match scraped. Candlelight bloomed low in the front room, behind the cases, throwing moving shadows up the wall — the shadow of a head, smallish, slight, crowned by the shadow of what might have been a cap or might have been untidy hair.

Then the sound I knew better than my own heartbeat: the slide of a type tray. Drawer by drawer. The visitor knew the geography of my shop. Knew where the good Caslon lived. Was composing, or checking a compose — pulling sorts, testing lines, with the quick sure rhythm of someone who'd done it many times before, in the dark, by feel.

I should have been terrified. Some part of me registered that this was the appropriate response and filed it away for later. What I actually felt, crouched behind the paper store with my heart going like a devil's tattoo, was something hotter and less useful: offense. This was *my* case. Those were *my* father's sorts, worn smooth by *my* family's thumbs, and some stranger was handling them in the dark like an heirloom they'd inherited.

I stood up.

I don't fully recommend this method. Later I would learn better ways — quieter ways, cleverer ways. But that night I simply rose from behind the paper store, crossed the floor in six strides with the blanket still around my shoulders like a batallion cloak, and turned the corner into the candlelight.

"Put down the stick," I said, "or explain yourself."

The figure spun, candle guttering, one arm raised — and I saw it wasn't a stick in that hand at all. It was a composing stick. Loaded. Held like a club, which tells you something about a person, that their nearest weapon is typography.

It was a girl. Younger than me — sixteen, seventeen. Thin as a rule, all elbows and shadow, hair escaping a knitted cap, eyes enormous in the candlelight and absolutely unapologetic. She looked at me. I looked at her. Between us, on the composing stone, lay a half-set galley, and even upside down I could read the first words of the line, and my stomach dropped through the floorboards into the cellar.

*They took the blind compositor's tools today,*

"You're the apprentice," I said, stupidly, because my mind had gone blank and that was the only fact left in it.

"And you're the daughter," she said. No fear in it. Or fear, but riding underneath something harder, the way grit rides under ink. She lowered the composing stick by degrees but didn't put it down. Smart girl. "Beatrix Eastwick."

"That's my name on your lips, not mine. Who gave it to you?"

She glanced at the galley. At the drawer fourteen, which she must have opened, must have *always* opened. At me, standing there in a blanket at two in the morning, and whatever she saw in my face made her decide something, the way I had decided something over a jammed drawer that morning. People like us, we decide fast or not at all.

"He did," she said. "Before he died. He said if anything happened to him, the shop would know. He said—" Her voice caught, and she steadied it the way you steady a forme, pressing down, taking the shake out. "He said the daughter keeps the type. The daughter knows the case better than anyone living. He said when I ran out of pages, I should stop running and ask you."

Outside, the snow kept falling, patient as print, covering the lane in fresh white sheets, and somewhere across the sleeping city a church bell counted the hour — one, two, three — each stroke landing on my chest like a platen coming down.

"Ask me what?" I said.

The girl set the candle on the stone. In its light her face was young and scared and utterly resolved, and she said, "How to finish the book."
