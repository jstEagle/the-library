## 5. The Night Visitor

There is a particular kind of fool who sits up all night in a cold shop waiting to catch a ghost, and I had become her.

I want it noted that I resisted for three full days first. Three days of telling myself that the pamphlets in my father's type case were a trick of memory — that he must have printed them before he died, and grief had simply misfiled the dates, the way grief misfiles everything. Three days of sorting type I'd already sorted, oiling hinges that didn't need oiling, performing diligence like an actress who has forgotten why anyone would applaud.

But on the fourth morning I took out one of the pamphlets and held it to the window light, and there it was again: the *th* ligature, kerned so tight the crossbar of the *t* nearly kissed the shoulder of the *h*. My father's habit. His signature written not in ink but in metal. And beneath it, faint as a watermark, the ghost of a correction — someone had pulled a letter from the forme and reset it, and the paper remembered the pressure differently there. A second pair of hands. A living one.

Dead men do not set type. Dead men's presses do not smell of fresh candle wax and lampblack when their daughters open the door at seven in the morning and find the stone floor swept clean of footprints that weren't there at closing.

So: the fool's vigil.

I told Mrs. Halloway next door that my father's press required a night of settling — that old iron shifts with the cold, which is even true — and she looked at me with the particular pity Vellham reserves for the Eastwick girl and said she'd leave her kitchen lamp burning late, just in case. Kindness is its own surveillance, in this city. I thanked her and resolved to avoid her window.

* * *

The shop after dark is a different country. By day it is mine by inheritance and habit; by night it reverts to something older, a place where the lead itself seems to remember every word it ever held. I banked the stove low, moved my father's old chair into the shadow behind the paper racks — the one spot from which both the street door and the back stair are visible — and settled in with a blanket, a stub of candle I did not intend to light unless absolutely necessary, and a cold meat pie I had promised myself I would save for midnight and ate by nine.

Vellham in winter keeps its own hours. The river ice groaned out in the dark, long complaintive sounds like a giant turning over in bed, and between those groans the city was so quiet I could hear the frost forming on the inside of the windows — a fine crackling, like someone whispering through gritted teeth. Twice a patrol went past on Quay Lane, boots ringing, lanterns swinging their little yellow opinions across the ceiling. Both times I held my breath like a child playing at hiding, and both times felt foolish about it afterward, and both times did exactly the same thing on the third pass anyway.

Somewhere past two o'clock I began composing arguments for whoever might appear. If it was a thief, I would say, take the copper quoins, they're worth more than you'd think. If it was a ministry man, I would say I couldn't sleep, my father died, have some pity. If it was my father's ghost — well. I had a shorter speech ready for that one, mostly consisting of *where were you*, which I did not intend to deliver, because ghosts answer questions the living can't survive hearing.

I must have drifted. The cold does that — pulls you under like the river pulls a dropped glove. What woke me was not a sound but an absence: the ice had stopped talking, and into that silence came something smaller. A click. The particular, unmistakable click of a type case drawer sliding on its runners.

I know that sound the way other people know their mother's voice on a staircase.

* * *

He — or she; the shape in the dimness gave me nothing yet — had lit no lamp at the door, but a candle burned somewhere beyond the composing stone, and its light moved in small pulses as hands worked. I rose from the chair with the exaggerated care of the very cold, joints announcing me one by one, and crossed the floor along the wall where the boards don't speak. My father taught me that too. *A compositor knows her floor,* he used to say. *Every board has an opinion. Learn which ones keep quiet.*

The candle sat on the lower shelf of the case cabinet, stuck to the wood with its own wax, and bent over it, sleeves rolled, fingers black to the second knuckle, was a person who could not have been more than sixteen, setting type with a speed that made my chest ache with recognition. Not my speed — nobody has my speed but me and the dead man whose chair I'd been sitting in — but the same grammar of movement. Compositor's hands. Hands that think in letters.

I should have spoken immediately. A sensible woman would have. Instead I stood in the dark and watched, because watching is what I do instead of living, and what I saw unsettled me more than any intruder could have: the intruder was correcting. Pulling a line, resetting it, checking it against a paper scrap by candlelight — and the corrections were being made in a hand I knew. Not handwriting. *Setting*-hand. The instinct to tighten a *th* until it sang. Whoever this was had learned at the same knee I had, or at a knee that had learned from it.

"You're distributing into the wrong cases," I said.

The effect was gratifying. The apprentice came up off the stool as though the bench had bitten them, knocked the candle sideways, caught it before it fell — good reflexes, wasted on crime — and stood pressed against the cabinet with one arm flung protectively across the open drawers as if I might steal them.

"I'm sorry — I thought — Mr. Quill said—" A boy's voice cracking in the middle, then abandoning the pretense halfway through, which was how I learned my intruder was a girl doing a poor impression of one. She had a smudge of ink across her chin like a bruise and eyes that went everywhere in the room except to me, cataloguing exits. There were only two, and I was standing nearer to both.

"Mr. Quill," I said.

"The pawnbroker." She swallowed. "On Tanner Street."

"Mr. Quill told you that you could set type in a dead printer's shop at three in the morning."

"He said the shop stood empty." Her chin came up, and there was the first flash of something that wasn't fear. "It doesn't look empty. It looks *kept*. Someone oils the press. Someone sorts the cases. Empty shops don't smell like work." She stopped, hearing herself, and her face did something complicated — the arithmetic of a person realizing she has just complimented her way deeper into trouble.

"It isn't empty," I said. "It's mine."

Something moved behind her eyes — recalculation, rapid and total. I watched her arrive at the sum: the daughter. The Eastwick girl. The one the neighborhood talked about in the past tense, as though she'd been buried with her father.

"You're Beatrix," she said.

"And you're a burglar."

"I'm *not*—" and then, because she seemed constitutionally incapable of lying badly without correcting it, quieter: "I'm Wren. My name's Wren. And I'm sorry about the cases. It's been a hard week for remembering which is which."

* * *

We negotiated by candlelight, the way all important things in Vellham seem to be done lately. She would not sit down. I put the kettle on the banked stove because my hands wanted something to do besides shake, and because tea is how my father ended every argument short of brawls, and I found I wanted the ritual of him around me just then.

She was sixteen, or near enough. An apprentice, genuinely — bound papers and everything — to a printing house on Cordwainer Lane that had closed its doors in the autumn when the owner decided that his health and his politics could no longer share a roof. Since then she'd been odd-work: carrying type, running errands, and, apparently, breaking into shops.

"Why here?" I asked. "Of every shop in Vellham. You could have picked a hundred presses."

"Because this is the only one with the right face." She said it as though it were obvious, and to her it plainly was. "Your father's type. The *th*. Everyone in the trade knows it. When the pamphlets started coming round — the ones about the grain seizures, the ones the soldiers tear down faster than they go up — I knew that face anywhere. I've handled his sorts since I was twelve. He let me once, at the fair, when our master wasn't looking. Let me hold a line of his type and feel how tight it sat." Her voice did something young and unguarded. "He said, *metal remembers what hands teach it.*"

The kettle began its low complaint. I stared at it rather than at her.

"The pamphlets aren't mine," I said. Which was true, and insufficient, and we both knew it.

"No," she agreed. "They're printed on your press, though. Or one exactly like it, which amounts to the same thing in a city this size." She hesitated, and I saw her weigh something — saw the scales move behind her face — and then she reached into her coat and laid a pamphlet on the table between us. Not one of the ones from my father's type case. A different one. Older, softer at the folds, read a dozen times. "Do you know what these say? Have you actually *read* them?"

"I've read them."

"Then you know they're not sedition. They're *minutes.*" She smoothed the creased page flat with both hands, the gesture tender as smoothing a child's hair. "Meetings. Dates. Names of men taken off the grain barges last spring and never charged with anything. Letters people wrote from the Tolsey jail and never got answered. Your father's circle — the printers, they called themselves the circle, though it was never just printers — they kept a record. Everything the governor's people did and denied doing, written down while it happened, because everyone knew the day would come when someone official would write it down differently."

The stove ticked. Out on the river the ice groaned again, long and low, and Wren glanced toward the sound the way sailors do.

"My father was one of them," she said. "Josiah Wren. He kept the ledger. He was taken in the autumn raid on Cordwainer Lane — not arrested, nothing so clean. Just gone. My mother went to ask after him and was told there was no record of any such person." She smiled, and it was the worst smile I have ever seen, bright and cracked straight down the middle. "No record. In a city where your family built half the records. That's the joke, isn't it? They've learned it from us. Erasure is just typesetting in reverse."

I poured the tea because my hands needed occupation and my voice needed postponement. When I finally spoke, it came out more level than I had any right to expect.

"The pamphlets in my father's type case. The new ones. Who prints them?"

"I do." No flinch. "And Tam Broadhurst does the ink, and the ferryman's boy carries them, and none of us is half the printer your father was, so the impressions come out grey at the edges and we burn a lot of spoiled sheets. But the type was already set, most nights. Stored. Hidden in the shop where the circle always kept it, in a place only members knew." She looked up at me, and I braced myself, and still the blow landed harder than expected. "In your father's old case. The bottom tray. We thought the shop stood empty. We thought—" She stopped. Started again, gentler, the way you speak to someone at a funeral. "After he died, nobody came. The circle didn't dare ask after the shop, with the raids on. We assumed the family had sold it, or fled, or worse. We assumed there was no one."

"There was no one," I said. "You weren't wrong."

"You were here."

"I was *here*," I said, and heard how thin it sounded, and hated it. "Here is not the same thing as present. I sorted type and mended the press and kept my head down for a year and a half, and I never once opened the bottom tray of that case, because opening it would have meant admitting the trade was mine instead of his, and I wasn't—"

Ready. The word arrived a beat too late to be spoken with dignity, so I drank my tea instead.

Wren watched me over her cup with the frank, unnerving directness of the very young, who have not yet learned that most truths should be approached sideways. "My da said you'd have been the finest compositor in Vellham if you'd been born loud," she said.

"He talked about me?"

"He talked about the shop like it had a daughter in it the way a bell has a sound inside it. Waiting." She set the cup down. "Look. I'll give the shop back. All of it. I'll tell the others we can't use it anymore. But I'm asking — I'm asking properly, on my knees if you want, though I warn you I've been on cobbles all day and I'd be slow about it — let us keep using it. Until the ice breaks, until whatever's coming comes. Because the ledger exists, or part of it does, and the rest is scattered among people who are old and scared and dying, and every week another piece burns or rots or gets confiscated, and when it's gone it's *gone*, and then the governor's historians get to write that Vellham loved him."

The candle flame leaned in a draft I hadn't noticed. Somewhere upstairs in the loft, mice went about their own quiet sedition among the stored rag paper.

"I'm not brave," I told her. "I want that understood before anything else. I hide from Mrs. Halloway. I've spent eighteen months being furniture. If soldiers come through that door, I will be the most convincingly innocent woman you have ever seen, because I have rehearsed innocence my whole life."

"Good," said Wren, sixteen years old, entirely serious. "That's exactly what we need. Everyone else in the circle is either loud or blind or already watched. What we need is someone who's invisible and owns a press." She gathered her coat, her cap, the crumpled boyish posture she wore like borrowed clothes, and paused at the door with her hand on the latch. "There's a ledger. Da's ledger. Half of it was seized in the raid, but the other half is safe, and I can show it to you. Tomorrow. Tonight. Whenever you're ready."

She left the sentence hanging there like type awaiting imposition, went out into the blue-black cold, and her footsteps faded up Quay Lane toward the river.

I stood a long while in the empty shop, in the smell of candle smoke and lampblack and my whole inherited life, listening to the ice grind out its slow countdown under the city.

Then I went to the type case, knelt, and drew out the bottom tray.
