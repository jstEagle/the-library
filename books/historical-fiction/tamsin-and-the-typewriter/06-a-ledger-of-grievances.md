## 6. A Ledger of Grievances

Wren did not produce the ledger so much as confess to it.

She came back the following evening — I had half expected her to vanish forever, the way guilty things do, and I had spent the intervening hours constructing elaborate arguments for why I should report her to the constable and equally elaborate arguments for why reporting her would be the single stupidest act of my life. Neither argument won. They sat in my chest like two compositors squabbling over the same case, and I got no useful work done at all.

She knocked this time. Three soft raps, spaced like someone who had learned that knocking too confidently invites the wrong sort of answer.

When I opened the door she was holding a oilcloth bundle against her chest the way a child holds a loaf of bread stolen from a market stall — close, protective, and with the air of someone prepared to run.

"You'll want to sit," she said.

"I've been sitting for two days," I said. "Sitting is all I've done since you crawled out of my father's type case like something he forgot to bury."

"That's more or less what it is," Wren said, and stepped past me into the shop without waiting to be invited, which told me something about how frightened she was. Frightened people either grovel or barge. She barged.

* * *

She set the bundle on the stone slab where we mixed ink, and unwrapped it with the care of a surgeon and the speed of a thief, both at once, which is a difficult thing to watch. Inside was a book. Not a fine book — the boards were plain calf gone grey at the corners, the spine re-sewn at least twice with cord of two different colors, as if it had been mended in haste by different hands at different times. But I knew the stitching on the second repair. Small, even, slightly too tight. My father sewed the way he set type: beautifully, and with a tension that suggested he expected the world to pull against him.

"He kept it under the floor of his sleeping room," Wren said. "Then under a loose brick at Halvard's tavern. Then it moved about. It's been moved eleven times that I know of."

"Eleven," I said.

"Twelve if you count last night." She looked at me with those unblinking apprentice eyes. "It's been in your type case for six weeks. The false bottom. He showed me once, years ago, when I was small enough to be shown things without being made responsible for them."

Six weeks. Six weeks of that book lying beneath the case where I sorted my lowercase e's every morning, beneath my own hands, while I dusted and mended and kept my head down and told myself the quietest grief was the most dignified one. I felt something turn over in me — not anger exactly. Anger would have been simpler. It was nearer to the feeling of finding a letter you wrote years ago and never sent, addressed to yourself, from yourself, saying everything you'd refused to say.

"Open it," I said.

"I think you should open it," said Wren. "You're his daughter."

"I'm the woman who sweeps this floor," I said. "He never told me anything."

"No," Wren agreed, with the brutal economy of the young. "But he built the compartment where you were standing when I found you, and he taught you every mark of his hand, and he left the press to you instead of selling it when there were nine men in this city who'd have bought it off him at double value just to stop its mouth. He told you everything, Beatrix Eastwick. He just did it in lead."

I have been struck before by sentences — by a line of scripture read aloud at a funeral, by a drunk singing in the lane — but I had never been struck by one and felt it *set*, the way type sets, with a small definitive click. *He told you everything. He just did it in lead.*

I opened the ledger.

* * *

The first pages were in my father's hand, and seeing it was like hearing a voice in an empty house. He wrote the way he composed — upright, economical, no flourishes he couldn't defend. A heading in careful capitals: **A RECORD OF GRIEVANCES OF THE PEOPLE OF VELLHAM, KEPT THAT IT MAY NOT BE DENIED.**

Beneath it, dated entries. Names. That was the first thing, and the thing I was least prepared for: names, columns of them, in his tidy hand. Not the names of the famous — not Governor Marest's cronies, not the customs men whose faces were on the broadsheets. Ordinary names. *Oskar Fen, dockhand, taken 14 October for speaking at the grain wharf. Held six days. Released owing four months' wages.* *Marta Ilves, widow, fined for sheltering her nephew after curfew; loom confiscated as payment.*

"They're testimony," Wren said quietly, watching me read. "People came to him. Word got round that if something happened to you — if the soldiers took your goods, your wages, your son — and you wanted it written down somewhere that wasn't the ministry's version, you came to Eastwick's after dark. He'd take it down himself if you couldn't write. He'd ask the questions. When did it happen. Who gave the order. Did anyone see. Then he'd read it back to you and make you say it was true."

"How many?"

"In that book? Four hundred and some entries before the raid. Some of them only a line. One of them is eleven pages — a cooper who was pressed onto a grain barge and kept three years." She turned pages for me, gently, the way you turn the pages of a book that has already survived one fire. "There are letters too. Folded in. Confiscated letters people smuggled out of the debtors' ward, out of the garrison jail. A whole packet from the Hessian weavers."

I touched one — a folded sheet gone soft at the creases, written in a hand that started strong and deteriorated down the page until the last lines were barely legible, as if the writer had been writing against time or pain or both. I did not read it. I could not, yet. Some documents you must approach the way you approach a cold river.

"And these—" I stopped. Further in, the handwriting changed. Several hands, in fact. A round schoolroom script. A spiky, slanting fist. One entry in enormous letters, each word a centimeter tall, written by someone who clearly could barely write at all and knew it and was damned if that would stop them.

"The circle," Wren said. "Father wasn't alone. There were seven who kept it regular — him, Halvard the tavern-keeper, old Semyon who used to composite for the *Courier* before they dismissed him for his eyes—"

"Semyon Brandt," I said. The name arrived from somewhere deep, a name I'd heard through a floorboard twenty years ago, men laughing low over pipes. "He used to call my father 'the abbot.' Because of all the rules."

Wren blinked. "He still does."

The page turned. And then the book changed.

* * *

The change was physical before it was anything else. You learn to feel paper — any compositor does, the way a baker feels dough. The early leaves of the ledger were good stock, rag paper, my father's preferred weight. Somewhere past the midpoint the leaves thinned. Cheaper. Greyer. And then, abruptly, the numbering broke. Entry two hundred and ninety-one ended mid-sentence — a widow named Corrag, describing soldiers at her door — and the next leaf jumped to entry three hundred and forty, in a different hand entirely, the round schoolroom script, beginning with the words *resumed as follows from memory.*

Half the book was gone. Not torn out in rage — cut, carefully, at the gutter, with a blade. Someone had removed forty-nine leaves with a knife and a steady hand rather than let them fall into the wrong pockets.

"The raid," Wren said. "In the autumn. Soldiers came to Halvard's — not a search, a *raid*, they knew what they were looking for. Semyon had the book that week. He heard the knock coming up the stairs and he had maybe a minute. He couldn't get the whole book out — the window's barred — so he took the knife to it."

"Took half of it," I said. My voice sounded far away to me. "And the other half?"

"Taken. Forty-nine leaves, in the ministry's hands since October." Wren's jaw was tight. "Every name in those leaves. Every testimony. Corrag the widow. The Hessian letters. Eleven pages of the cooper. All of it, wherever Aldous Crane keeps his papers."

I had heard the name Crane before — everyone in Vellham had, lately, though few could have said precisely what his office was. *Ministry Historian,* the gazettes called him. A man appointed to write the account of the unrest, now that the unrest had been put down hard enough to be called something else. I had thought of him, insofar as I thought of him at all, as a man with a pen, which in Vellham is a harmless-sounding profession.

Looking at that cut gutter, I understood for the first time that a pen can be a weapon the way a knife can, and is harder to disarm.

"What happens," I asked slowly, "to the people in the missing pages? If Crane has their testimonies?"

Wren didn't answer immediately. She picked up the oilcloth and began folding it, refolding it, a nervous loop of hands. "Corrag was arrested in November," she said finally. "Charged with sedition on evidence nobody's seen. The Hessians were deported in a batch — the gazette said 'administrative relocation.' The cooper drowned in January. Officially drunk."

"Officially."

"There isn't another kind of record anymore, is there?" Wren said. "That's the point. That's the whole point of Crane. The ministry doesn't need to burn the truth. They only need to be the ones writing it down. Everything in those forty-nine leaves — every person who trusted us — their words exist now only in the government's copy. Where they can be filed, or altered, or lost." She looked up at me, and for the first time the girl who'd broken into my shop looked genuinely young, and genuinely terrified. "Your father used to say: whoever holds the ledger holds the people in it. That's why he kept it moving. That's why it's twelve hiding places in ten years. Because it isn't a book, Beatrix. It's four hundred hostages."

* * *

We sat with that a long while. The lamp burned down. Outside, the river ice groaned — it had begun doing that nightly now, a long complaining sound like a giant turning over in bed, and I had taken to hating it, because it meant winter was loosening and spring was coming and spring meant barges and barges meant movement and movement, in this city, on this subject, meant danger for somebody.

I turned back to the first page and read the heading again. *Kept that it may not be denied.* My father had written that, I guessed, five or six years ago, when this was still a private stubbornness and not a conspiracy. He had believed a book could hold. He had believed paper was stronger than governors, if the paper was hidden well enough and carried by enough hands.

He had been right about the carrying, anyway. Here it was. In my shop. Under my e's.

"Why bring it here?" I asked. "Truly. Not because of the compartment — you could keep it there forever and tell no one. Why show me?"

"Because I'm the only one left who moves it," Wren said, "and I'm seventeen, and they caught Halvard's boy asking questions at the wharf last week, and I don't know if it's random or if someone's following the book. And because—" she stopped, and started again, angrier at herself for stopping. "Because you're his daughter and you didn't know, and that seemed like the worst injustice in the whole city. Worse than the raids. He built all this around you. Around this *shop*. And you swept the floor."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere across the lane a dog started up and thought better of it.

"It's not safe with me," I said.

"Nowhere is safe," Wren said. "That's the lesson of the year. There's only safer, and only for a while. Semyon says a record isn't kept safe, it's kept *moving*, like water — you let it freeze solid in one place, that's when they crack it open." She pushed the ledger an inch toward me across the stone slab. An inch. It might as well have been a mile. "Take your shift."

"My shift."

"Everyone in the circle carries it in turn. Father's rule. No one holds it longer than a fortnight, no one knows all the hiding places, no one carries it twice running." She almost smiled — a thin, exhausted, fifteen-year-old-apprentice smile that had seen more than my whole quiet decade. "You're overdue by six years. He'd have said you were shirking."

I looked at the book. At the heading in my father's capitals. At the cut gutter where Corrag's sentence ended mid-word, *and then the captain said—* and nothing after, forty-nine leaves of her and the others folded away into a ministry drawer, hostage to whatever Crane decided they meant.

Here is what I knew about myself, and had known for ten years: I was a woman who kept her head down, who hid in work, who had made an art of being overlooked. I had thought that art was survival. Sitting there with the ledger's warmth — it was warm; books carry body heat, I hadn't known that until that moment, some previous carrier's warmth still in the paper — sitting there, I understood that my art of hiding had a cost I had simply never been sent the bill for. While I swept, Corrag went to prison. While I mended the press, the cooper drowned. Not because of me. But in the same city, in the same season, under the same sky, and someone had to write it down or it hadn't happened.

My father had chosen his daughter to inherit the press. Perhaps he had also, in his silent way, chosen her to inherit this.

"I'll take it," I said. "On conditions. Nobody else touches my type case. If it goes out of this shop, I go with it. And when the missing pages come back — *if* they come back — I'm the one who sets them in type. Properly. With his face marks. So they can't ever be cut out again."

Wren stared at me. "Set them in *type*? Printed? Do you know what printing it would mean? A pamphlet they can burn. A hundred pamphlets they can burn—"

"A plate they can't," I said, and heard my father's click again, the sound of a line setting true. "Lead remembers, Wren. Paper burns. Lead melts only at temperature, and even then it wants recasting first. We'll speak of plates another night. Tonight the book sleeps under the e's, and tomorrow you'll tell me every name you know from the missing leaves, everything you memorized, everything Semyon and Halvard remember, and we'll start rebuilding what Crane stole. From memory. From proofs. From anywhere it survives."

"That could take years."

"Good," I said. "I've been idle a decade. I have the stamina."

She laughed then — a startled, rusty sound, as if the mechanism hadn't been oiled — and for a moment the shop felt less like a tomb and more like what it had been in my childhood: a place where words mattered, where men argued happily about kerning and tyranny in the same breath, where my father stood at the press with ink to his elbows looking like a man doing exactly what he was made for.

Wren wrapped herself in her coat, took up the candle stub I allowed her, and paused at the door.

"One more thing," she said. "The census man. Tamsin. They came back yesterday — asking after you at the chandler's, at the pie stand. Whether you live alone. Whether you receive visitors at night." Her eyes met mine, level and grim. "Census takers don't usually do follow-up visits, Beatrix. Somebody upstairs wants a second look at this house."

She slipped out into the dark, and I stood listening to the river groan, with four hundred hostages warm under my hands, and understood that the counting had already begun — and that I was on the list.
