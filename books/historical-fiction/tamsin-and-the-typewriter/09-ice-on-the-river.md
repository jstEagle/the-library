## 9. Ice on the River

Winter in Vellham arrives the way debt does — gradually, then all at once. One morning in late December I woke to a sound like a giant cracking its knuckles beneath the floorboards, and lay there in the dark of my room above the shop, listening to the river groan.

The Vell had frozen solid. From my window I could see it: a plain of gray-green ice stretching bank to bank, scarred with the tracks of sledges and the dark stitched lines of fishermen's holes. Barges sat locked where they'd moored in November, their hulls cased in ice up to the gunwales, looking less like ships than like the bones of ships, something the river had swallowed and decided to keep.

For three hundred years that river had been the city's whole argument for existing. Grain came down from the high valleys, coal came up from the low country, paper came in bales, and everything — everything printed in this city, every broadside and ballad sheet and bill of lading — went out on the water. The Vell was Vellham's bloodstream. Now it was shut.

You'd think a frozen river would mean quiet. It didn't. It meant the city was holding its breath, and a held breath is louder than shouting if you listen long enough.

* * *

I spent that first hard morning doing what I always did when the world grew too large: I sorted type.

There is a particular peace in a case of letters that has been properly put right. Every character in its compartment, every em quad flush with its neighbors, the whole alphabet lying in wait like an obedient army. My father taught me to sort before he taught me to read, more or less — he'd set me on a stool at the stone with a case of broken English and say, *Find the e's, Trixie. There are always more e's than you think.* He was right about that. There are always more e's than you think, in language and in most other things.

Wren arrived mid-morning, stamping snow off her boots on the mat she never otherwise used, cheeks red as sealing wax. She carried a sack of coal in each arm because she refused to let me carry anything heavier than a composing stick, which was either gallantry or an insult to my arms, and I had decided not to determine which.

"River's froze," she announced, dumping the sacks by the stove. "Old Cobb says earliest he's seen it in twenty years."

"I heard it crack at dawn. Sounded like the whole city clearing its throat."

"That's what I told him." She pulled off her mittens finger by finger, a ritual she performed as though the mittens were reluctant to release her. "He said no, girl, that's the ice settling. Says it'll be solid till Candlemas at least, maybe later. Says we could walk across to Millbrook and back carrying a piano and never wet our boots."

"An odd thing to want to do with a piano."

"You'd be surprised what people carry across frozen rivers." She said it lightly, but her eyes didn't stay light. They flicked toward the type cabinet — the one with the false bottom, the one with the ledger sleeping inside it under forty pounds of lead sort — and away again, quick as a tongue checking a chipped tooth.

We had gotten good at that, Wren and I. Good at the glance-and-look-away. The ledger had been living in the cabinet for six weeks now, and in six weeks I had developed the habit of touching the cabinet's edge whenever I passed it, the way you touch a pocket to confirm your purse is still there. I hated the habit and couldn't break it. A secret you have to keep checking isn't a secret; it's a wound you keep prodding to see if it still hurts.

It still hurt.

"Tea," I said, because there are only so many sentences a person can hold unspoken before one of them escapes, and tea prevents escape.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about hiding a dangerous thing in a small building: the building shrinks.

The Eastwick print shop occupied a narrow lot on Cordwainer Lane, squeezed between a chandler's and a house that had been empty so long the ivy had taken the windows. Shop below, rooms above, yard behind barely wide enough for the privy and the drying lines. In summer it had been snug. In winter, with the ledgers of my father's circle sealed under the type cabinet and soldiers' boots audible on the lane outside, it had become a box. I caught myself measuring things by it. The press stood four feet from the wall. The stair was eleven steps. The distance from the front door to the back was thirty-two, and I knew because I had walked it, one night unable to sleep, counting, like a prisoner pacing a cell and telling herself it was exercise.

Claustrophobia, my father used to say, is just imagination with nowhere to go. He said it of compositors who froze at the case, terrified of the thousand small decisions in a page of type. *Your mind wants to run, Trixie. Give it somewhere to run.* So I gave mine the work. I re-mended the frisket leather though it didn't need mending. I recut a battered quoins. I inked the press bed with the last of the good varnish and ran clean sheets through until they came out blank and soft, just to hear the machine breathe.

And underneath all of it, ticking like a clock inside the clock, the thought I couldn't set down:

*When the ice breaks, the barges leave.*

That was the arithmetic of it, and I did the sums nightly. The archive — the true account of what Governor Malbrec and his captains had done, the testimony of prisoners, the names of the disappeared, half of it seized and half of it scattered among the surviving members of my father's circle — could not stay in Vellham. Crane's ministry historians were already writing their version, the version where the unrest was criminal and the suppression was order and nobody had been disappeared at all, merely *processed*. When spring came, that version would go out on the barges, bound and official, into every bookshop in the low country. If our account left with it — hidden in a bale of rags, sewn into a cargo of tallow, whatever means we could find — then the record might survive. If it stayed past the breaking of the ice, it would die here, in a cellar or a fire or a ministry incinerator, and the only history anyone would ever read would be the one written by the men with the seals and the soldiers.

So the ice was both prison and hourglass. It kept us penned in this shrinking box of a shop, and it kept Crane's men penned too — nothing moved easily on a frozen river, no barges to search, no traffic to watch. But the moment it broke, everyone's plans would float free at once, and ours were the plans least able to afford daylight.

Patience, I told myself. Patience is a craft like any other. You can learn it the way you learn kerning: badly at first, then adequately, then — if you live long enough — without thinking.

I was still at the badly stage.

* * *

On the third day of the freeze, Wren came back from the market with bread, a twist of salt, and news.

"They've posted notices on the Custom House," she said, setting her basket down with exaggerated care, which is what she did instead of slamming it. "New ordinance. All river traffic suspended pending — what was the word —" She screwed up her face. "*Historical review.*"

"Historical review of barges?"

"Of cargoes. Everything going out gets inspected and logged. Paper especially." She looked at me directly then, and I saw she'd been carrying the sentence the whole way from the Custom House, saving it. "They know paper matters, Beatrix. They're not stupid. Cruel, yes. Stupid, no."

"No," I agreed. "Crane isn't stupid."

Aldous Crane. I had met him exactly once, in this very shop, with his soft voice and his offer wrapped in velvet — protection, a commission, my father's typefaces surrendered as *evidence*, meaning surrendered as trophies, melted or archived or displayed behind glass in some ministry hall labeled *INSTRUMENTS OF SEDITION*. I had declined. My heart had pounded so hard during the conversation that I'd been certain he could see the pulse in my throat, a little traitor beating out *afraid, afraid, afraid*. But I had declined, and he had smiled the way a chess player smiles when you make a move he expected, and he had gone out into the snow, and I had locked the door behind him and stood with my forehead against the wood for a full minute.

Since then: silence. Which was worse. A man like Crane doesn't forget a refusal; he files it. Somewhere in the ministry there was a drawer with my name in it, and the drawer was getting fuller.

"Paper's inspected," Wren was saying, "but here's the thing — Cobb says the bargemen are furious. Sealed port, no pay, sitting on their hands since November. There's talk of trouble at the coal wharf. Talk of—" she dropped her voice even though the nearest person was forty feet of frozen air away, "—talk of the guilds petitioning. Even the loyal ones. You don't starve a river town's bargemen and expect them to sing the governor's praises."

"Malbrec thinks freezing the port makes the city safer," I said. "He hasn't noticed he's freezing his own friends out with everyone else."

"People notice cold before they notice politics."

"People notice cold *instead of* politics, usually. But sometimes the cold teaches them." I turned a sort in my fingers without looking at it — an 'h', I discovered later, which I then had to find the proper home for, which served me right. "Keep your ears open at the market. And Wren—"

"I know. Careless printing cost us already." Her jaw set. She didn't like being reminded of the broadside, the one she'd run too soon, the one that had brought soldiers' boots up our stairs while I knelt fitting the ledger into the false bottom with my heart going like a treadle. She hadn't forgiven herself and I hadn't asked her to. Forgiveness that comes too easy doesn't hold weight, same as a forme that isn't locked up tight. "No new work till you say. Nothing leaves this shop. I remember."

"I'm not scolding."

"You're always slightly scolding. It's how you show affection." She grinned, and for a moment the shop felt almost warm in the ordinary way, two people and a stove and a joke, and then her gaze went again to the cabinet, and the warmth thinned like ink washed in too much water.

* * *

That night I dreamed of the river open.

In the dream it was April, and the Vell ran brown and muscular between its banks, hauling floes downstream like a crowd shoving through a gate, and I stood on the wharf watching a barge — our barge, somehow, though barges don't belong to people who dream about them — slide past loaded with our papers, our plates, our dead men's testimonies, and everyone on the wharf cheered, and I woke with my fist clenched around the blanket and the real sound in my ears: not running water but ice, groaning, settling, patient. The sound of a thing that could wait longer than I could.

I got up and lit the lamp and went down the eleven stairs in my nightdress because sleep was clearly finished with me, and I stood in the cold shop with my hand flat against the type cabinet, feeling the wood like a pulse.

"All right," I whispered, to the ledger, to my father, to whoever keeps books on such conversations. "All right. We'll be ready when you are."

Which was a lie, of course. We weren't ready. We didn't have the plates yet — those were still buried wherever my father had buried them, following whatever logic he'd followed, and I had found nothing in six weeks of searching his habits. We didn't have a barge, or money enough to bribe one, or a route, or anything except a hidden ledger, a frightened apprentice, a blind compositor across town, a tavern-keeper with a grudge, and a census taker whose motives I trusted perhaps sixty percent, which in Vellham that winter counted as devotion.

But saying *we'll be ready* aloud is a kind of typesetting too. You set the words, and then you spend your days making the page match them.

* * *

The deep cold settled in for a fortnight after that. The kind of cold where the nails in the walls ache and the ink goes syrupy and you have to warm the composing stick over the stove between pages. The kind of cold where the whole city walks bent forward, hurrying, eyes down, breath hanging in flags behind them.

I learned the rhythms of the frozen river whether I wanted to or not. The dawn groan, when the expanding ice flexed against the pilings. The midday tick and ping as the sun worked the surface. And at dusk, my favorite and my dread: a long, rising note from the channel near the middle span, like a bowed string, like a held vowel, that would climb and climb until I thought it must surely break — and then stop. Never break. Just stop, and hold, and wait.

The bargemen said the river was singing because it was thinking about breaking. They said it the way sailors say anything, as fact and omen at once. Old Cobb told Wren the ice sang loudest the year of the great break, forty years back, when it shattered all at once on a Tuesday afternoon and took the lower wharf with it and drowned nine men and every barrel of wine in the customs warehouse, and for a week the whole city drank off the floodwaters and called it providence.

"When it breaks," Wren reported, relaying this, "it breaks fast. No warning worth having. Cobb says the smart ones move *before* the song stops."

"The smart ones," I said, "move before the song starts."

And then, because I am who I am, I got out a sheet of scrap paper and began, in pencil, in my smallest hand, a list. Not of grievances — the circle had plenty of those, a ledger's worth — but of needs. Plates: unknown location, search ongoing. Barge or boat: none. Money: eleven crowns, seven shillings, and a coin of doubtful nationality. Routes: one, involving a coffin, which I will explain when there is time and trust enough. Allies: fewer than needed, more than I deserve. Time: —

I sat looking at that last line for a long while. The lamp hissed. Upstairs a beam popped in the frost. Underfoot, faint through the floor, the river sang its long unwavering note, climbing, climbing, refusing to finish.

Time, I wrote finally, *measured in ice.*

And that was when the knock came — three slow raps on the shop door, unhurried, deliberate, at an hour when honest visitors were home and dishonest ones were asleep — and I blew out the lamp before I remembered that whoever knocks like that already knows you're awake.

Through the frosted glass of the door, a shape. Small. Still. Waiting.

I knew the shape of that coat. I knew the way that silhouette stood, weight on one hip, head tilted like a bird considering whether the worm was worth the risk of the robin.

Census takers, I thought, do not call after dark. Census takers file their numbers and go home to their suppers. Whatever was standing on my step in the blue-black cold had stopped being a census taker somewhere between the doorstep and tonight, and had come — off duty, off the record, off something — to my door.

I crossed the thirty-two feet of floor and lifted the latch.
