## 10. Tamsin Returns Off Duty

There are two kinds of courage in Vellham. The loud kind gets you hanged by spring. The quiet kind — walking back to a print shop you have no official reason to visit, wearing your civilian coat over your clerk's collar so the neighbors won't mark you — that kind just keeps you up at night, which is arguably worse.

I had told myself I wasn't going. I had told myself it very firmly, twice a day, for six days, in the tone one uses with a dog that keeps getting at the larder. And then on the seventh day I found myself standing outside Eastwick's with snow in my boots and my heart behaving like a trapped sparrow, and I thought: well. So much for self-discipline.

The shop was shuttered but not dark. A line of lamplight leaked through the gap beneath the door like something spilled and not yet mopped up. I knocked — three soft raps, then, because I am constitutionally incapable of leaving things simple, four more.

The light went out.

"Miss Eastwick," I said to the door, quietly. "It's the census man."

Nothing.

"I've come without my ledger."

A pause long enough for me to reconsider my entire life, and then the bolt drew back and there she was: Beatrix Eastwick, ink to the second knuckle, holding a candle in one hand and — I noted with professional interest — a compositor's bodkin in the other, angled the way you'd angle a knife if you'd never used a knife but had thought about it.

"You said you wouldn't come back," she said.

"I said the ministry wouldn't send me back. Both statements can be true." I lifted my hands, palms out, the universal gesture of the unarmed and slightly ridiculous. "May I come in before I freeze into a curiosity someone else has to catalog?"

She looked at me the way she'd looked at the pamphlet I'd shown her last time — as if deciding whether I was evidence or merely nuisance. Then she stepped aside, and I stepped through, and somewhere behind us the bolt slid home with a sound like a small verdict.

* * *

Inside, the shop was warmer than I expected. A stove ticked in the corner, and the smell was the same as before — lead and oil and old paper — but underneath it now, faintly, woodsmoke and something that might have been coffee kept warm too long. She'd been living here, or near enough to it. Sleeping badly, judging by her face. Working constantly, judging by everything else.

The forme she'd been mending sat open on the stone bench, half-locked. She moved to stand between me and it, casually, the way a person stands between a stranger and a drawer. I pretended not to notice. Pretending not to notice things is most of my trade.

"You said flagged," she said. No greeting, no tea. Good. Tea would have meant trust, and neither of us could afford trust yet. We could afford information, though. Information was the only currency in Vellham that hadn't been devalued.

I took off my gloves finger by finger, buying time. Here was the difficulty: everything I knew came from my employment, and telling her meant betraying that employment, and betraying that employment meant becoming, in the eyes of the ministry, precisely the sort of person I had spent three years counting instead of being.

"Your name appeared on a list," I said. "Not mine. I don't make the lists. I feed them." I heard how that sounded and didn't flinch from it, because flinching would have been dishonest in a different direction. "Eastwick's, cross-referenced with the pamphlet seizures from the autumn. Someone upstairs noticed that the typeface on the seditious material matches the typeface registered to this shop. Your father's typeface."

"My father is dead," she said. "He died in October. You counted him yourself."

"I did," I said. "That's rather the point. Dead men don't set type. But somebody did, in this shop, after his death, using his matrices — or ones cut from them. Crane's people believe either the dead man left an accomplice, or the dead man isn't dead, and they find both possibilities equally interesting."

"Aldous Crane." She said the name carefully, testing its weight.

"The ministry historian. You've met?"

"He came. He made an offer. I declined it." Her chin lifted a fraction. "He wanted Father's typefaces. As 'evidence.'"

"And he'll get them," I said, "unless something intervenes. That's why I'm here. Miss Eastwick — Beatrix, if I may — your name isn't just on a list. It's been *flagged*. There's a difference. A name on a list is a fact. A flag is an intention. It means someone has decided what happens next and is waiting for the paperwork to catch up. In my experience the paperwork always catches up."

The stove ticked. Somewhere out in the lane, ice fell off an eave and shattered, and we both flinched — a small, synchronized cowardice that, absurdly, made me feel less alone.

"What kind of intention?" she asked.

"The kind I'm not authorized to know, which means it's serious. Clerks are told everything trivial and nothing true. But I file the household returns, and I saw the annotation in the margin of yours, and the annotation had Crane's seal on it." I hesitated. This was the edge of the plank. "And then I went home and couldn't sleep, which is also not trivial, because I have slept soundly through famines, floods, and one minor insurrection."

"Why?" she said. Not *what do you want* — why. She wanted to understand me before she trusted me even an inch, and I respected that, because I was trying to do exactly the same thing with her and getting nowhere.

Why indeed. Because of a marginal note in a clerk's hand, six years ago, that had turned a family of four into a number and then into ash. Because Aldous Crane had explained to me, with the patience of a man describing weather to a child, which version of the uprising would be permitted to have happened. Because I had watched him take a city's memory and trim it like a hedge, and I had gone back to my desk and sharpened my pencil.

"Because I count things," I said finally, "and lately everything I count adds up wrong. That's not an answer you can file. But it's the honest one."

* * *

She didn't trust me. Of course she didn't. Trust, between two people who survive by observing others, is a currency that takes weeks to mint and seconds to spend. What she offered instead was a test.

"There's a book," she said, when I'd been silent long enough that the silence itself became a question. "A ledger. It came to me through — channels. It contains testimony. Names. Accounts of what was done to prisoners in the governor's custody, letters that were confiscated, a record of the disappearances. Half of it is missing — seized in a raid on the printer's circle before my father died."

I felt the cold leave my feet and settle somewhere behind my sternum. "You're telling me this why?"

"Because you offered a bargain, implicitly, by standing in my shop uninvited and risking your neck. So here is mine, explicitly." She folded her arms, ink-stained fingers tucked away, a woman literally keeping her hands hidden. "You misfile my census entry. Make the household read as empty, or dormant, or transferred — whatever clerical fiction keeps the flag from attaching. In exchange, I'll show you the ledger, and you'll tell me what the ministry does with records like it. Where seized pages go. How Crane reconstructs what he confiscates. You're trained in documents, aren't you? Then help me understand what this document means — to them, and to anyone who might still be alive whose names are in it."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you walk out that door, and I bolt it, and we both go back to pretending the other is nobody."

It was, I reflected, a remarkably fair offer from a woman whose life I was endangering merely by association. That fairness frightened me more than threats would have. Threats I could report. Fairness I would have to carry.

"If I agree," I said slowly, "and it goes wrong — if the misfiling is discovered — they won't fine me, Beatrix. They'll ask me questions under lamps. And I don't know yet whether I'm built to withstand lamps."

"No," she agreed. "Neither am I. That's not a reason to avoid finding out. It's a reason to make sure the thing we're lamp-resistant about is worth it." She said it flatly, without drama, the way she seemed to say everything — as though courage were simply housework, another chore on the list between sorting type and banking the stove.

I looked at the forme on the bench, the little metal letters locked in their frame like a secret mid-sentence. I looked at the candle, burning down toward nothing. I thought about the ledger I carried in my head — every household, every headcount, every neat column of citizens reduced to figures — and about the other ledger she was describing, in which the figures had fought their way back into names.

"All right," I said. "Misfiled by Friday. I'll enter the premises as 'workshop, inactive; proprietor deceased; no resident household.' It's even mostly true, which is the best kind of lie — it survives scrutiny because it wants to be believed." I paused. "In exchange, show me the ledger. Tonight."

Something shifted in her face — not warmth, nothing so generous, but a recalculation. Two accountants arriving independently at the same figure.

"It's not here," she said.

My stomach dropped through several floors. "Then where—"

"Somewhere Crane hasn't thought to look. Which is the only place anything is safe in this city anymore." She relit the candle from the stove and the light steadied between us. "But I can show you the part that matters most. One page. Wren copied it before the raid — the apprentice. There's a girl involved, Tamsin. Nineteen years old. Her father was in the circle. If your ministry comes for this ledger, they come for her too. Keep that in your columns."

* * *

The page was hidden inside a case of twelve-point bodoni — pulled up from beneath the type like a floorboard. Wren's copy hand was young and urgent, letters leaning forward as if running. Beatrix held it flat under the candlelight and I read, and the cold behind my sternum spread until I think it reached my eyes.

Testimony. Dated, signed, witnessed. A prisoner named Halloran describing three days without water. A list of confiscated correspondence with senders' names — names I recognized from my own census rolls, households I had personally enumerated, reduced now to entries in a record of the disappeared. And at the bottom, in a different hand — older, smaller, controlled — a correction to the text. A single word struck through and replaced, with a note in the margin: *accuracy matters more than eloquence.*

"That's my father's writing," Beatrix said quietly. "Wren confirmed it. He corrected their testimony. He didn't soften it — he made it *precise*. He wanted it to survive scrutiny." Her thumb hovered over the marginal note without touching it. "He knew it would have to survive scrutiny."

And there it was — the thing I had walked seven days' worth of resolve to say, suddenly unnecessary, because she already understood. She understood better than I did. She had inherited not just a typeface but a discipline: the belief that truth, properly set, is load-bearing.

"Crane will have the missing pages," I said. "When his office seizes material, it goes to the Ministry Archive first, then to his study, then nowhere — officially, nowhere. Unofficially, he works from originals. He compares, collates, reconciles. He treats a city's crimes like a proofreading exercise." I met her eyes. "But here's what he doesn't know, and what you should: he can't reconcile what he can't complete. Seized pages tell him what the circle recorded. They don't tell him what the circle *remembered*. If the surviving members can reconstruct the missing testimony from memory — dates, names, order of events — then whatever Crane holds becomes incomplete, and incompleteness, to a historian, is worse than absence. Absence he can footnote. Incompleteness he has to fear."

Beatrix stared at me for a long moment. Outside, the river groaned — a deep, structural complaint, the frozen miles of it shifting in its bed, and we both listened the way you listen to a floor at night.

"You've thought about this," she said.

"I've thought about almost nothing else for a week. It's interfered with my filing." I put my gloves back on, finger by finger, reversing the ritual. "Friday, your entry reads 'inactive.' After that, I'll come again — off duty, same knock. If you mean to rebuild those pages, you'll need someone who knows how the ministry reads documents. What it believes, what it checks, what it ignores." I hesitated at the door, hand on the bolt, and gave her the last piece honestly, because she'd earned honesty and I was tired of paying in anything cheaper. "Understand what I'm giving you, Beatrix. Not friendship. Not yet. Friendship requires a future, and I haven't agreed to one. I'm giving you an alliance of the temporarily useful. If it starts costing more than I can carry, I'll tell you, and you'll let me walk away, and you'll never use my name."

"And if it costs *me* more than I can carry?" she asked.

"Then you'll tell me," I said, "and I suspect you won't let either of us walk away, because you strike me as a woman who finishes what she sets in type."

Almost — almost — she smiled. It transformed her face the way a struck match transforms a room, briefly and dangerously.

"Bolt it behind me," I said, and stepped out into the cold.

* * *

I walked home the long way, along the river road, because the short way passed the garrison and I was done, for one night, with walking past power and keeping my eyes forward. The ice stretched black and pale to the far bank, groaning at intervals like some enormous animal dreaming uneasily. Underneath it, the current ran on regardless — it always does; that's the whole trick of rivers.

I thought about the entry I would file Friday. *Workshop, inactive. Proprietor deceased. No resident household.* Three lines of fiction that would keep a flagged woman unfindable, and unmake, in the smallest possible way, the machinery I had served. It would cost me nothing, probably. Almost certainly nothing.

But I had seen the marginalia in Crane's files. I knew how much weight this ministry could hang on three lines.

At the bend by the coal yards I stopped and looked back at the city — the smoke, the steeples, the dark bulk of the Ministry Archive where the missing pages slept in their drawers, patient as seeds. Somewhere in that mass of roofs, a nineteen-year-old apprentice was sleeping badly, and a blind compositor was remembering, and a woman with ink to her knuckles was bolting a door against a world that had already decided what she was.

Friday, I would lie in triplicate. And then, God help me, I would come back, and sit at that stone bench, and help rebuild a record the state had forbidden — one page, one name, one date at a time.

The river groaned again, longer this time, and I stood listening until it stopped, and what I felt was not fear.

It was relief.
