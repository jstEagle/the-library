## 11. Reading the Missing Pages

Tamsin arrived at the shop on the third morning of our bargain carrying a leather folio, two sticks of sealing wax, and an expression I can only describe as a clerk's version of guilt — which is to say, no visible expression whatsoever, but with the eyes doing all the apologizing the mouth had been trained out of.

"You're late," I said, because someone had to say something and it was clearly going to be me.

"I'm not late. I'm punctual for a person who does not officially exist this morning." Tamsin set the folio on the composing stone with the reverence other people reserve for relics. "I told the ministry office I was conducting follow-up interviews in the Tanner's Row district. Which is true. I simply neglected to specify that the interviewee is you."

"And if they check?"

"They won't. Nobody checks the census. That's rather the tragedy of my profession, Beatrix — we are trusted precisely because we are boring." A pause. "It has its uses."

I had spent two days preparing for this, though I'd have denied it under oath. I had cleared the long table by the window, laid out the ledger, and dug from beneath the press a box of proofs I hadn't opened since before Father died — pull-sheets he'd kept for correction, some marked up in his cramped left-leaning hand. I had also, I will admit, baked nothing, because grief and domesticity have never shared a roof in my house, but I had set out two cups, which in my personal economy amounts to a feast.

Wren had been sent to the tavern-keeper's with a message and instructions to stay gone until dark. She'd gone reluctantly, looking back twice, the way a dog does when it suspects you're about to do something interesting without it.

"So," Tamsin said, unbuckling the folio. "Show me what we've lost."

* * *

The ledger lay open between us like a patient on a table. Forty-one pages survived of what Wren said had been ninety or more. The circle's record: testimony taken down from prisoners in the Saltgate cells, copies of confiscated letters, names of the disappeared with dates and last known addresses, the governor's requisition orders transcribed verbatim because — as my father had apparently insisted — *the crime is in the exact wording, never the summary.*

The raid had taken half. The question was whether the missing half existed anywhere else.

"Memory," Tamsin said, running a finger down the surviving index — yes, there was an index; my father had indexed his sedition, God rest him — "is a terrible archive. It loses dates, invents dialogue, flatters everyone involved. But it remembers *structure*. If we know what sections are missing, we know what kinds of things filled them."

"You sound like a man describing how to rebuild a burned house from the shape of the ashes."

"I am a census taker. Rebuilding households from their shadows is the whole trade." They pulled a blank sheet close and began, in a small immaculate hand, to list the gaps. "Here — entries eleven through nineteen in the testimony section. The index says these concern the Saltgate prisoners taken in the autumn arrests. And here, the letters section jumps from a widow named Corvane to a merchant named Pell. Whatever sat between them was seized."

"The autumn arrests." I remembered them without wanting to. The whole street had pretended not to hear the wagons. My father had gone out that night and come back at dawn smelling of river mud and ink, and when I'd asked where he'd been he'd said, *auditing*, and I had believed him, because believing him was easier than the alternative.

"Your father took the testimony of four of those prisoners," Tamsin said quietly. "The index says so. 'E.W., dictation, Saltgate, 3rd–7th.' Those pages are gone."

"Then we get them back."

"From where?"

"From everywhere they might be." I went to the proof box and set it on the table. "He kept everything. Pull-sheets, spoiled impressions, make-ready sheets with his corrections. Printers are magpies. We hoard our own mistakes." I fanned the proofs across the stone. "If any part of those testimonies passed through this press — even as practice, even as a false start — it's here somewhere."

Tamsin looked at the sprawl of paper, then at me, and something shifted in their face. "You've already been through these."

"Some."

"How many times?"

"That's none of the ministry's business."

"It isn't. That's rather the point of today." And they rolled up their cuffs — ink-stained, always ink-stained, I'd noticed it the first day on my doorstep and noticed it now with a different feeling entirely — and began to sort.

* * *

We worked until the light went thin and I lit the lamps, and here is what I learned about Tamsin Voss over the course of one long afternoon: they read the way my father set type.

Father worked in passes. He never read a page straight through. First pass, structure — did the argument hold, were the sections in order. Second pass, facts — every name, every date, checked against source. Third pass, the language itself, word by word, because he maintained that tyranny entered through vague phrasing before it ever arrived with soldiers. *"A man who writes 'excesses' instead of 'murders' has already surrendered,"* he used to say, jabbing his bodkin at some official report. *"Precision is resistance."*

Tamsin did exactly this. I watched them take a single surviving fragment — the tail end of testimony, a prisoner's account of a cellmate who'd died of fever after three days in an unheated cell — and interrogate it in three separate passes. Structure: where would this have sat in the sequence? Facts: the cellmate's name, partially legible, cross-checked against the index and against a name Tamsin remembered from their own rounds, a family on Cordwainer Lane that stopped answering the census questions in a particular month. Language: the phrase *died of fever* underlined twice, with a note in the margin in Father's hand — *fever is what the garrison surgeon writes. Ask what the prisoner saw.*

"Ask," Tamsin repeated softly. "As if the dead could answer."

"He meant ask whoever came next. Testimony is a chain. Each witness holds a link of the same story."

"Then we're missing most of a chain." But Tamsin wrote the note into the reconstruction anyway, preserving even my father's marginal demand, and I found I had to look out the window for a moment at the gray slush of the lane, at nothing, at the ordinary world going about its ordinary business while I sat ten feet from my father's handwriting telling me to keep asking questions he could no longer answer himself.

Grief, I have decided, is mostly this: discovering that the dead are still issuing instructions, and that you intend to follow them.

* * *

The breakthrough came near dusk, and it came, fittingly, from a mistake.

One of the pull-sheets was a botched impression — the press had been unevenly packed, and half the page had printed ghostly and pale, barely legible. I'd nearly tossed it aside as worthless. Tamsin held it up to the lamp instead, tilting it so the light raked across the indentations.

"There's text under the text," they said. "Look — the sheet was used twice. Something else was printed on the back first, and it's bleeding through. Or—" they turned it, squinting. "No. This is makeready. Someone printed a draft, didn't like it, and used the sheet to pack the forme for a second attempt. The draft is crushed into the paper."

I took it from them. My heart had begun doing something inconvenient.

Under the lamp, tilted just so, the ghost-text rose off the page like a body surfacing in a river. Dictation format. Numbered answers. And there — the third question down, in Father's hand, written directly on the proof: *Do not summarize. He wants to tell it in order. Let him.*

"It's one of the Saltgate sessions," I said. "A draft of one of the missing pages. He printed it once, corrected it, reprinted it clean, and the clean copy is what got seized." I laughed, and it came out wrong, half sob. "He threw away nothing. He *never* threw away anything. I used to fight him over the clutter."

"Beatrix." Tamsin's voice had gone careful. "How many sheets in that box?"

I counted. We both counted. Two hundred and six.

"Well," Tamsin said, and for the first time since I'd known them they smiled with their whole face, quick and crooked and entirely un-clerical. "Then we'd better work fast."

* * *

We reconstructed three full pages that night, and pieces of two more.

The method, once we found it, was almost musical. Tamsin would hold a ghost-sheet to the lamp and transcribe what surfaced; I would match the handwriting, identify which session it belonged to by the prisoner's numbered answers; and then — this was the part that undid me — we would compare the draft against Father's surviving corrections to reconstruct what the final seized version must have said. Because his edits followed patterns. He struck flourishes. He cut anything that sounded like rhetoric and kept anything that sounded like a human being talking. Where a draft read *we were treated with unspeakable cruelty*, the correction read *they beat Tomas Oduya with a stave until his arm broke, and the sergeant laughed*. Where a draft apologized for a witness's confusion, the correction crossed out the apology and wrote: *confusion is evidence. Record it as such.*

"He made it plainer," Tamsin said, copying the reconstructed line. "Every time. Plainer and more specific. Most writers go the other direction as they revise — they polish, they elevate—"

"He wasn't writing literature." I turned another sheet to the lamp. "He was building a case. You don't ornament evidence."

"No," Tamsin said. "You don't." And they wrote for a while in silence.

Somewhere past midnight I caught myself watching them instead of working — watching the way Tamsin wet the tip of one finger to turn fragile pages, the way they mouthed difficult phrases silently before committing them to the reconstruction, the way their clerical training showed in the ruled margins and the tiny, disciplined hand that never varied no matter how tired they got. There is a particular intimacy in watching someone handle your father's ghosts gently. I hadn't expected it. I hadn't prepared any defenses against it, mostly because I hadn't known it was a thing that required defending against.

"You're staring," Tamsin said, without looking up.

"You're writing my father's testimony in your own hand. I'm deciding how I feel about it."

"And?"

"It hasn't killed me yet. Give it till morning."

They huffed — almost a laugh — and kept writing.

* * *

Near two in the morning we hit the wall. Page seventeen of the missing testimony existed only as a fragment: a ghost-sheet bearing the opening lines of a prisoner's account and nothing more. No corrections. No second draft. The rest of that night's session was simply gone, unless—

"Unless," Tamsin said slowly, "someone else kept a copy."

"The circle. The foreman, the compositor—"

"The blind compositor couldn't read print. He'd have memorized it." Tamsin said it matter-of-factly, but I saw their pen stop moving. "That's how they'd work, wouldn't it? A man who can't be caught holding papers, because his archive is in his head. Your father dictated to him, he set it in memory, and if the pages were ever seized, the type still existed somewhere safer than paper."

"Old Hesper." I'd met him once, years ago — a dry, precise voice in the dark of the shop, complimenting my spacing before I knew he'd never seen my work at all. "He'll be seventy if he's a day. Can he still recite it?"

"Can your father's training survive in you?" Tamsin shrugged. "Training like that doesn't leave. It waits."

I looked down at the table — at the wreckage of proofs and the growing sheaf of reconstruction, at the lamp burning low, at the two cups gone cold hours ago. Three pages raised from the dead in one night. Eighty-odd to go, scattered between this room and the memories of old men and the cellars of people I hadn't yet dared to visit.

It should have felt hopeless. Instead — and this surprised me — it felt like the opposite of hopeless. It felt like my father's hand on mine at the composing stick all those years ago, guiding, correcting, saying *no, closer, again* — except now the instruction had passed to me, and I was the one saying *again*, and there were other hands learning alongside mine.

"We'll need to see Hesper," I said. "And the tavern-keeper. And the foreman, if he'll speak to us — Wren says he's exiled to the mill district and bitter as vinegar."

"One thing at a time." Tamsin began stacking the reconstructions, squaring the edges with a clerk's compulsive neatness. "Tomorrow I file my false report on Tanner's Row. After that, the compositor."

At the door they paused, folio under their arm, lamplight catching the ink on their cuffs.

"Beatrix. The pages we recovered tonight — the ones about the boy who died in the cold cell. His family's on Cordwainer Lane. I counted that household myself, two winters ago. I logged them as four persons." A breath. "When I go back tomorrow, there will be three."

Neither of us said anything for a moment. The lamp guttered.

"Then write him down properly this time," I said. "All of it. What was done, and who did it, and what he was called."

Tamsin nodded once and went out into the dark, and I stood a while in the doorway listening to the river ice groan in the distance — a long, complaining sound, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Inside on the table, my father's ghosts waited for morning. So, I realized, did a question I hadn't let myself ask yet: if Old Hesper had memorized the missing testimony — what else, in that blind old head, had my father entrusted to him?

And why had he never entrusted it to me?

I banked the fire, locked the type cabinets, and stood a moment with my palm flat against the press bed, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal.

"All right," I told it. "Tell me tomorrow."
