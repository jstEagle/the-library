## 12. Wren's Mistake

There is a particular sound a hand-press makes when it is being worked by someone who does not yet respect it. My father called it "the amateur's cough" — that dry, arrhythmic thump of a platen meeting paper without conviction, the ink laid on too thick or too thin, the tympan clacked shut with more temper than care. I had grown up falling asleep to the good version of that sound, the steady heartbeat of Eastwick's, and so when I heard its sick cousin drifting up through the floorboards at half past six in the morning, I knew two things before my feet touched the cold boards: Wren was in the shop, and Wren was printing something.

I should have known a third thing as well, but I will be honest with you, since no one else is likely to be: I did not think of soldiers until the soldiers were already thinking of us.

I came down the stairs with my hair unbound and my shawl over my nightgown like some mad widow out of a morality play, and there she was — sleeves rolled to the elbow, forearms black to the wrist, feeding sheets into my father's press with the fierce concentration of a girl defusing a bomb. A stack of finished broadsides sat on the composing stone, still warm-smelling, and I crossed the room and picked one up before she could stop me.

*THE GOVERNOR'S PRISONS ARE FULL OF MEN WHOSE ONLY CRIME WAS ASKING WHAT THE CENSUS IS FOR.*

Oh, Wren.

The type was mine — well, my father's — the condensed gothic he'd cut for funeral announcements, which lent any sentence set in it an air of doom even when the sentence was about fish prices. The ink was laid on unevenly. The margins wandered. And at the bottom, where a prudent printer would have left blank space, someone had set a single line in italic:

*Printed by no one. Distributed everywhere.*

"You've been busy," I said.

Wren's chin came up in that way she had, all stubbornness and no strategy. "It needed printing."

"It needed *planning*. There's a difference, and the difference is whether we're still here next week." I held up the sheet. "Where are these going?"

"Taverns. The fish market. Under doors." She wiped her hands on her apron, which only redistributed the ink. "Tamsin said the city's ready. Tamsin said people are asking why the census takers keep coming back to the same houses."

"Tamsin said," I repeated, "that the city is *restless*. Restless and ready are not neighbors, Wren. Restless is what you get when you feed people broadsides they can't act on. Ready is what you get after months of work, and we are not months into anything. We're barely weeks into *surviving*."

"You hid my ledger in a cabinet bottom," she said. "You decoded your dead father's secret compartment. You're not timid, Beatrix Eastwick. You just dress like it."

That stung precisely because it was half true, and I opened my mouth to explain — patiently, condescendingly, the way one explains things to apprentices and younger sisters — and that was when the bell above the shop door gave its little tin cough and three men in garrison grey came in out of the snow.

* * *

Later — much later, when I could think about it without my heart trying to exit through my ribs — I would reconstruct those ninety seconds the way Tamsin reconstructs a seized page: from fragments, from inference, from the terrible clarity of hindsight. The first soldier rang the bell because the door was unlocked, because *I* had come downstairs in my nightgown without stopping to lock anything behind me, because terror apparently begins at home with ordinary stupidity. The second soldier went straight to the stack on the stone, because stacks of seditious broadsides have a way of announcing themselves. The third stayed by the door, hand resting on his belt in a manner meant to look casual, and looked at me looking at him, and I understood that the whole scene had the composition of a painting titled *Caught*.

The tall one — a sergeant by his braid, with the patient face of a man who has searched a hundred shops and found nothing in most of them — picked up a broadside and read it twice. He read slowly. I remember being grateful for that; slow reading bought seconds, and seconds were suddenly the only currency I dealt in.

"Lovely morning for literature," he said.

"The morning is not up to me," I said, "and neither, apparently, is the literature."

He looked at me then, properly. "You're the printer?"

"I'm the printer's daughter."

"And who printed this?" He turned the sheet toward me, the italic line facing out. *Printed by no one.*

"Then I couldn't say," I said. "Whoever it was has been very thorough about telling us."

Something flickered across his face — not amusement exactly, but the recognition of a familiar animal in unfamiliar clothing. He had expected a blusterer or a weeper, and I was neither, and that confused him for the length of a breath. In that breath, my eyes did the thing I hate that they do, the thing that made me useful to my father and invisible to everyone else: they counted. Three soldiers. One at the press now, running his thumb along the platen. One holding perhaps thirty broadsides. One at the door. The stairwell behind me. The type cabinet against the north wall, with its false bottom, with its ledger, with its four hundred pages of names that would hang every person in them if they were found.

And Wren — Wren standing in the open with ink to her elbows and guilt written on her in the only language she spoke fluently.

"Sergeant," I said, before he could ask the obvious question, "if you'll give me a moment to put on clothes that belong to daylight, I'll answer whatever questions you're authorized to ask."

"I haven't asked any questions yet."

"No," I agreed. "You've been very thorough about that too."

The flicker again. Behind me I heard Wren shift her weight, heard the small betraying scrape of her boot, and I raised one hand behind my back — a gesture I had last used on customers, *one moment, sir, the forme isn't locked* — and prayed she could read it.

"Corporal Voss found these nailed to the fish market posts an hour ago," the sergeant said. "Nailed through the middle of the governor's own proclamation, if you can believe it. Somebody with a hammer and a sense of humor. We traced the paper." He let that sit. "Tracing paper is dull work. You'd be surprised how few mills make this stock. Two in the city. One of them sells mostly to trade printers."

"Then your corporal has narrowed the field to every shop on Printer's Row," I said. "Congratulations to him. Shall I fetch you a list of my competitors? I keep one for billing purposes, and I'd love to see their presses searched instead of mine."

"There it is," the sergeant said softly. "The tradeswoman."

I don't know what he saw when he looked at me. I know what I felt: a strange, cold steadiness, as though fear had frozen solid and become something I could stand on. My father used to say that a compositor's job was to impose order on chaos one character at a time, and I discovered, standing barefoot on my own floorboards while a sergeant of the garrison decided what to do with me, that terror works the same way. Character by character. Breath by breath.

"Search the shop," he said at last, to the one by the door. "Thorough. The girl waits outside."

"She's my apprentice," I said. "Whatever you find, she answers for it beside me. That's how apprenticeship works."

"That's not how arrest works," he said, and gestured, and the soldier took Wren's arm — gently enough, I noted, gently enough that orders had been given about gentleness, which meant someone above the sergeant wanted this quiet — and walked her out onto the step, where she stood glaring at the snow like it had personally betrayed her.

Which left me alone in my father's shop with one searching soldier and one patient one, and a false-bottomed cabinet full of condemned names, and perhaps four minutes of borrowed time.

* * *

You want to know what I did. Everyone always wants to know what I did, as though heroism were a trick you can learn. Here is what I did: I made tea.

Not as theater — though it was that too — but because my hands demanded employment, and because a woman making tea is furniture, and furniture is not watched. I filled the kettle from the rain barrel with a steady arm while Corporal Whoever-He-Was pulled open drawers. I set the kettle on the stove. I took down three cups, because offering tea to men ransacking your home is either madness or genius, and I have never learned to tell the difference in myself.

The cabinet stood against the north wall. The false bottom was in the third drawer from the top, beneath a layer of fourteen-point bodoni that no living soul had ordered since the funeral trade dried up. To open it properly required pressing the hidden catch inside the drawer's left wall — a brass pin, flush, invisible unless you knew — and lifting with the fingertips at a specific angle. It could not be done quickly. It could not be done at all while anyone watched.

The corporal started with the press. Of course he did. He ran his hands along the frame, peered under it, tapped the bed the way a man taps a wall listening for hollow places, and my heart performed a slow, cold roll, because the plates were in the bed and the ledger was in the cabinet and I could not save both and I had perhaps chosen wrong — no. I hadn't chosen. That was the lesson arriving early, ahead of schedule, wearing its boots. *Carelessness chooses for you.* Wren's broadside had chosen this. My unlocked door had chosen this. Every shortcut taken by everyone who ever said *it needed printing* had conspired to put me here, choosing between my father's craft and other people's lives, and hating that the choice was even offered.

The kettle began to sing.

"Tea?" I asked the room, brightly, insanely.

Neither soldier answered. The sergeant was reading spines off the type case labels — *Gothic Condensed, Baskerville No. 3, Long Primer, Pica* — with the frown of a man who suspects the alphabet itself of sedition. The corporal had moved to the stone sink and was lifting the ink slab, and I understood with a lurch that within a minute he would work his way down the row of cabinets, left to right, and the third drawer from the top was eleven steps away and might as well have been across the river.

So I dropped the kettle lid.

It hit the stones with a clang that made both men flinch — soldiers flinch, whatever their patience — and hot water slopped over my wrist, scalding, and I cried out, genuinely, generously, and grabbed a cloth, and in the four seconds of commotion that followed I was at the cabinet, my fingers finding the pin, pressing, lifting, and the false bottom came free and the ledger came up into my arms and —

and there was nowhere to put it.

This is the detail I leave out of the retelling when Tamsin asks, and include here because a record worth keeping is worth keeping honestly: I stood in my father's shop holding the true history of the Vellham uprising in my bare hands and I had no plan for it beyond *not there*, and the corporal was turning around, and the sergeant was watching me over the type case, and my mind — my clever, cowardly, cataloguing mind — went white as fresh paper.

Then I sat down on the floor.

I sat down hard, the way a scalded woman sits, ledger crushed between my body and the cabinet base, skirts puddled around it, and I gasped and cradled my wrist and swore — actually swore, words my father would have washed my mouth with soap for — and the sergeant came around the type case fast, and there I was, a hurt woman on a floor with her burned hand and her scattered dignity, and the ledger underneath me doing what ledgers do best: bearing weight quietly.

"Let me see that," the sergeant said, crouching.

"It's nothing," I said, which was the first fully formed lie of my life, and it came out smooth as a freshly cast slug. "Boiling water and wool. I'll live."

"Get her the lanolin," he told the corporal, without taking his eyes off me, "in the blue jar, second shelf," and while the corporal turned his back — while he *turned his back*, gentlemen, may your mothers forgive me — I slid the ledger flat along the floor behind me, under the shadow of the cabinet skirt, and pinned it there with my heel.

The sergeant took my wrist. His grip was careful. That was the worst part, somehow — the gentleness of it, the professional courtesy extended to a woman whose shop he was stripping. He turned my hand over, examined the welt rising pink along the skin, and said, "You should see the surgeon at the garrison. They're good with burns."

"How kind," I said. "Do they also treat suspicion? Because I suspect you'll be needing a lot of it."

"Why do you print pamphlets, Mistress Eastwick?"

"I mend presses," I said. "I cut typefaces. I print invoices, playbills, and the occasional memorial card for people whose relatives can afford sentiment. If someone in this city is printing treason, they're doing it better than I ever did, because treason pays worse than funerals and I have rent."

He held my gaze for a long moment. Snow ticked against the window. From the step came the muffled sound of Wren arguing with the soldier guarding her — she had a carrying voice, our Wren, a voice built for street corners, and I sent a silent, furious prayer upward that she would run out of adjectives before she ran out of rope.

"We'll take the broadsides," the sergeant said finally, standing. "All of them. And you'll come to the garrison tomorrow morning to make a statement. Nine o'clock. Captain Halloran's office." He paused at the door. "You strike me as a woman who keeps records, Mistress Eastwick. So keep this one: the captain asks his questions once. After that, answers start costing."

The door shut behind them. The bell coughed. The snow swallowed their footsteps.

I lay back on the cold floorboards, my heel still pinning four hundred pages of testimony to the ground, and laughed — one short, cracked syllable of a laugh with no humor anywhere in it — and then I got up, opened the door, and pulled Wren inside by the collar before she could finish whatever sentence she was aiming at the street.

* * *

We did not speak for a while. She stood dripping in the middle of the room, ink-stained and stricken, watching me retrieve the ledger, dust it off, and slide it home into the false bottom with hands that were not quite steady. Then I relocked the pin, closed the drawer, stacked fourteen points of bodoni on top of it, and turned around.

"Sit down," I said.

"Beatrix—"

"*Sit down.*"

She sat. I poured the tea I'd made for the enemy, and we drank it, the two of us, out of the cups meant for soldiers, and the silence between us had weight and temperature, like the ice on the river.

"They'll come back," Wren said at last.

"Tomorrow. Nine o'clock. Captain Halloran." I wrapped my burned wrist in the cloth, more for something to do than any comfort it gave. "They took the broadsides, which means they have the typeface, which means they have the paper stock, which means every printer in Vellham is now a suspect, which means the circle's tavern contacts are a suspect list waiting to be read aloud. Do you understand what one morning of yours has cost?"

"I understand." Her voice was small. Small voices from Wren were rarer than honest officials, and I found I didn't have the stomach for them.

"Do you? Because I nearly lost the ledger today. Not to cleverness. Not to a search that beat us. To an unlocked door and a kettle and the fact that neither of us thought past the moment the ink dried." I set down the cup harder than I meant to. "Your father died for that book, Wren. Half the circle is dead, fled, or blinded by men who wanted names out of it. And you printed thirty copies of its existence and nailed them to a fish market because it *needed printing*."

Her eyes filled, and she blinked it away the way young ones do, furiously, as if tears were another mistake to be corrected. "Someone has to fight," she whispered.

"Yes," I said. "And fighting and printing are the same trade, which is why we do it *carefully*, character by character, the way you set a page — because one reversed letter doesn't ruin the letter, it ruins the sentence, and one ruined sentence ruins the argument, and one ruined argument gets people killed. Carelessness isn't innocence, Wren. It's a weapon pointed at whoever's standing nearest you. Today that was me. Tomorrow it's the blind compositor, or the tavern-keeper, or Tamsin, whose name is sitting in a ministry file right next to ours."

She looked at her ink-blackened hands for a long time. When she spoke again, the stubbornness was still there — it always would be, God help us — but it had acquired a foundation under it.

"What happens at nine o'clock?"

"I lie," I said. "Fluently, I hope, for the first time in my life. And you—" I pointed at her with my bandaged hand, "you will be nowhere in this building. You'll go to Tanner's Row, to the cellar, and you'll stay there until I send word, and if word doesn't come by noon, you'll take the south route and not look back. Swear it."

She swore. She was young enough to mean it completely, which frightened me more than the sergeant had.

After she slipped out the back into the falling snow, I stood alone in the quiet shop among the disturbed drawers and the cooling kettle, and I flexed my scalded hand and thought about the sergeant's parting words. *The captain asks his questions once.*

Through the frosted window, across the lane, a figure in garrison grey took up position by the cooper's shutter — not hiding, not even pretending to. Watching.

Nine o'clock was sixteen hours away, and I had never told a lie that mattered before.

I intended to practice.
