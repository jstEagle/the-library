## 13. Interrogation by Lamplight

They came at half past nine, which told me something before they'd said a word. Soldiers on routine patrol knock at a decent hour and move along. Soldiers who come at half past nine in the evening, when the shutters are closed and the lamps are lit and every honest soul in Vellham is at supper — those soldiers have been sent.

I heard the boots first. That's the thing about soldiers; they never learn to walk like neighbors. Neighbors shuffle, drag their heels, announce themselves by the creak of familiar weight on worn stairs. These boots struck the cobbles in unison, six of them at least, and stopped outside the shop with the particular silence of men who have rehearsed their arrival.

The ledger was in the false bottom of the type cabinet four feet from where I sat. I had put it there two nights ago, after Wren's broadside went up on the tannery wall like a flare, after I'd spent an hour with my heart hammering while she wept and swore and I did not swear because swearing would have meant admitting how afraid I was. The cabinet stood against the north wall. Between me and it: the composing stone, three galleys of waiting type, and a lifetime of practice at being nobody worth noticing.

I blew out the second lamp so there would be only one pool of light, and I picked up the forme I'd been mending — the lockup for a funeral notice, as it happened, which felt like an omen with a sense of humor — and I began to work the quoins.

Then came the knocking. Not knocking, really. Pounding, delivered with the flat of a gauntlet, hard enough to rattle the glass in the transom.

"Eastwick Print Shop. Open in the name of the Governor's Commission."

*In the name of.* Everything official came wrapped in that phrase, like bad fish in good paper.

"One moment," I called, and made my voice small and startled and slightly hoarse, the voice of a woman interrupted at her chores. "One moment, please, if you'd — the bolt sticks."

It didn't stick. I gave it thirty seconds anyway, because a timid woman fumbles. A timid woman apologizes for the delay twice. I counted to thirty, listening to the boots shift on the step, and then I opened the door with the lamp held up and my face arranged into the expression my father used to call *the shopkeeper's apology* — brows lifted, mouth turned down at the corners, everything about me suggesting I was sorry for existing and would stop as soon as it was convenient.

Six soldiers. A captain in front, young for the rank but not young enough to be stupid, with a face like a ledger page: ruled, ruled again, nothing written on it yet that couldn't be erased.

"Mistress Eastwick." He didn't ask. He knew.

"Yes, sir. Is — is something wrong?" I let the lamp tremble just slightly. Not much. Trembling too well is its own kind of performance, and I'd learned even in these last weeks that the trick to lying is to do it badly enough to be believed. Perfect composure is suspicious. Perfect fear is suspicious. What people believe is imperfection, honestly rendered.

"There's been seditious printing in this district." He stepped past me without invitation, and his men flowed in behind him like water finding the low places. "You'll have seen the material. A broadside. Posted Tuesday."

"The one about the granaries?" I shook my head, wringing my hands on my apron. "I heard talk of it at market. Dreadful stuff. My father — when he was living — he always said the press is a trust, Captain. We print notices, bills of sale, funeral cards. Honest work." I gestured at the forme on my stone as though presenting credentials. "I'm sorry to have ink on my hands at such an hour. Mending a lockup. It won't hold square."

He glanced at the forme. I watched him glance at it and I understood, in that instant, something that would keep me alive for the rest of the night: he knew nothing about type. To him it was a tray of metal junk. He could no more read a galley than a cat could read a psalter. All the danger in this room lived in objects that looked, to a soldier's eye, identical — and the only person here who could tell him which object mattered was me.

That realization should have terrified me more than it did. Instead it settled over me like a coat fitted to my shoulders. I had spent twenty-nine years invisible in this shop, the daughter who swept the floor while the loud men talked. Now invisibility was armor, and I found I knew how to wear it.

"Search the premises," the captain said. Two men went toward the back room, where the press stood under its dust sheet. One lingered by the type cabinets, lifting lids without interest. None of them went near the false bottom, because none of them knew to look, and Wren's cabinet — the one with the loose back panel where she kept her own things — was upstairs in the living quarters, and I had moved her papers into the flour bin yesterday, because flour gets searched and flour also gets spilled, and spilled flour gets everything dirty and slows everyone down.

Small economies of cowardice. I was becoming fluent.

* * *

The captain introduced himself — Captain Harrow, of the Third Garrison — and then proceeded not to interrogate me but to *chat*, which was worse. He picked up a printed funeral card from the shelf, read it with genuine or performed interest, and set it down.

"You've run this shop alone since your father died?"

"A year now, sir. Nearly." True. Let the true things pile up like ballast; they steady the boat you're lying in.

"And business is good? In these times?"

"These times?" I laughed, a small dry sound, the laugh of a woman who finds the question absurd rather than ominous. "Captain, nobody commissions a wedding bill during a bread riot. I survive. The church keeps me busy — the dead are reliable customers."

One of the soldiers snorted. Harrow didn't smile. He drifted along the shelves, trailing one gloved finger across the cases, and I tracked that finger the way one tracks a wasp near a sleeping child. Case by case. Upper case, lower case, the jobbing faces. His finger paused at the case nearest the window — my father's text face, the one cut special for him in Amsterdam forty years ago, with its reversed e that old Josiah Quill had teased him about until the day he died. The e that appeared, kerned and backwards, in every pamphlet of the circle's that had ever been set.

Harrow looked at it the way a man looks at furniture.

"Your father taught you the trade?"

"He tried." I dropped my eyes to my hands, ink-blackened, and worked a quoin key that didn't need working. "I can set a fair line and mend what breaks. He had apprentices for the fine work. Men." The word *men* carried all the bitterness of a woman who had heard *it's heavy work, love* her whole life, and I let it carry that bitterness deliberately, because a resentful woman is a legible woman, and legible means predictable, and predictable means harmless.

"Apprentices. Names?"

"Gone, sir. After father died, they scattered. There wasn't wage enough to keep one, let alone two." This was true as far as it went, and it went far enough. Wren had never been on any book. Apprentices were registered; girls who swept floors were not.

Harrow turned from the shelves at last and faced me fully, and I saw the change come into him, the chat draining out and leaving the other thing underneath. "Mistress Eastwick. The broadside posted Tuesday names the officers responsible for the granary order. It quotes correspondence that has never been published. Someone in this city has access to government paper, and someone with a press set it, and the two are likely the same someone or close kin to them. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"No, sir." And I widened my eyes, and I let my breath go shallow, and inside myself I observed, with a strange detached horror, that none of it was effort anymore. The fear was real — God knows the fear was real, real as the cold — but the *shape* I gave it was chosen. I was lying fluently, the way one becomes fluent in a language by drowning in it.

"I'm telling you that harboring sedition is a hanging matter. Aiding it is transportation at best. And that shops like this one—" he touched the frame of the door, gently, almost fondly, "—have a way of catching fire in troubled times. Accidents happen to presses, Mistress Eastwick. I would hate for you to be standing next to one."

Behind him, in the back room, I heard the dust sheet come off the press. Heard a soldier say, "Just a machine, sir. Big wheel thing." Heard another laugh.

"It belonged to my father," I said, and here I did not lie at all, and the truth cracked something open in my chest that I had to close fast, before it showed. "It's all I have of him."

Something moved in Harrow's face — not softening exactly, but recognition. He had a father once. Everyone had a father once. Even captains.

"Then you'll want to keep it safe," he said quietly. "Which means keeping it clean. If anything comes through that door that shouldn't — if anyone leaves anything, if you find anything, if a stranger asks to rent your basement — you send for me. Not for the magistrate. For me." He drew a card from his breast pocket and laid it on the composing stone, next to the funeral forme, and I thought: there it is, the omen paying off. "Can you do that?"

"If I find anything, sir, you'd be the first to know. I want no trouble. I'm a widow's—" I stopped. Corrected. "I'm alone. Trouble finds alone women easily enough without my inviting it."

He held my gaze a moment longer. I let him see timidity, fatigue, the faint resentment of a tired tradeswoman whose evening had been ruined. I did not let him see the ledger eight feet away, or the girl asleep — God willing still asleep — in the loft above us, or the fifty proofs hidden in the flour bin, or the woman I was becoming underneath the costume, who was taking notes on *him*: the scar on his knuckle, the way his men obeyed him without affection, the fact that he'd said *send for me* rather than *report to your magistrate*, which meant either ambition or a private doubt, and both were useful, both were levers, someday.

"Lock up well," he said. "There are thieves about."

And then they were gone, boots receding in unison down the lane, and the door closed, and the bolt — which stuck not at all — slid home under my hand.

* * *

I stood in the middle of my shop in the lamplight and discovered that my legs had opinions about the night's events, namely that they wished to conclude operations immediately. I sat down on the composing stool and put my head between my knees and breathed, and above me, after a long minute, the loft ladder creaked.

Wren came down in her stocking feet, gray-faced. She'd heard everything. Of course she had; the loft floorboards broadcast like town criers.

"He offered to protect you," she whispered. "Did you hear that? He wants you to *inform*."

"I heard." I straightened up. My hands, I noticed, had gone to the forme without my instructing them — the funeral notice, the quoins, the little honest geometry of locked-up type. Work as anchor. My father's habit, inherited without my consent.

"What do we do? Bea, what do we—"

"We do what I did tonight. We keep our heads down and our hands busy." But even as I said it, I knew it wasn't true anymore, and the knowing frightened me more than the captain had. Because here was the cost of deception, arriving on schedule, itemized: I had lied well. Better than well. I had enjoyed it — not the fear, but the *craft* of it, the fluency, the discovery that beneath twenty-nine years of timidity there had been all along a performer with perfect pitch, waiting for a stage. The woman who kept her head down had done so partly because looking up meant seeing what needed doing, and doing it meant risk.

Tonight I had looked up. Tonight I had lied to an officer of the garrison with my hands steady and my heart singing some terrible brave hymn, and I could not unknow it. There was no going back down. The timid tradeswoman was a coat I could still put on, but it fit differently now. It was a costume. Costumes come off.

"Wren," I said, "go wake the flour bin. We're moving everything to Tanner's Row tomorrow, and after that—" I looked at the card on the stone, at the name written there in a clerk's careful hand, at the man who thought he'd recruited a spy and had instead shown a printer exactly which pressure points the enemy guarded most poorly.

"After that, we print."

Above the river, far off, the ice groaned — a long low sound like a giant shifting in sleep, like a deadline being read aloud in a language older than any of ours. Spring was coming whether Crane liked it or not.

And so was I.
