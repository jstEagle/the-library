## 14. The Printer's Circle, Reassembled

My father had a saying about type cases that he applied to everything else in life, which was that you could tell what a man valued by what he kept nearest the composing stick. The letters he reached for most — the e's and t's and s's of ordinary speech — lived closest to hand. The rare ones, the k's and z's and q's, sat at the edges where a man might go years without touching them.

I had spent twenty-nine years being one of the edge letters. Quietly sorted, rarely reached for, useful only when something unusual needed saying. It is a strange thing to discover, in your thirtieth year, that there were other letters all along — scattered across the city, tucked into taverns and tenements and one very surprising upstairs room — and that together we spelled out something my father had been composing his whole life.

It began, as most dangerous things do, with a list.

Wren gave it to me on a Tuesday, written on the back of a spoiled galley proof in her looping, impatient hand. Three names. Three addresses. She'd pressed it into my palm the way you press a hot coal into someone else's hand — quickly, apologetically, and with every expectation that it would burn.

"The circle wasn't just Father," she said. "You knew that, didn't you?"

I hadn't known it. Or rather, I had known it the way you know the river has a bottom — abstractly, without ever expecting to stand on it.

* * *

The blind compositor lived above a chandler's shop on Rope Walk, in a room that smelled of tallow and old paper, and he answered my knock before I'd finished knocking, which I suppose is what decades of listening does for a man.

"Beatrix Eastwick." Old Hollis turned his face toward me — toward the sound of me, rather — and smiled with half his mouth. "Your father's daughter. I'd know that tread anywhere. He walked the same way. Like the floor owed him money."

"Mr. Hollis. I don't know if you remember—"

"I set your first forme." He said it flatly, a fact laid down like a line of type. "You were nine. You couldn't reach the case without standing on a crate, and you put an f where an s belonged, and your father laughed so hard he had to sit down on the stone step. I remember everything, girl. That's the trouble with going blind at fifty-three. Everything stays."

I had not known this man existed. That is the sentence I kept returning to, over the following weeks, like a tongue finding a missing tooth. My father had worked beside this man — learned from him, apparently, been laughed at by him — and had never once spoken his name in our kitchen. The circle had been a secret even from its own families. Perhaps especially from them. Secrecy, I was beginning to learn, is not a wall you build around a thing. It is a wall you build around yourself, brick by brick, until you can't remember which side of it you live on.

Hollis poured tea without spilling a drop and asked me questions no census taker would have dared: whether I still bit my lower lip when I locked a chase, whether the old Albion press still pulled to the left the way a mule pulls to the left, whether I had found anything odd about the way Father spaced his em-quads.

The last question stopped my cup halfway to my mouth.

"Odd how?"

"Odd in the way that Aldous Manx spaced *his* em-quads," said Hollis, "which is to say, wrong. Deliberately, beautifully wrong. Your father didn't invent that habit, Beatrix. He borrowed it from the rest of us. We all space our quads a little strangely, those of us who were ever in the circle. A fingerprint. So that any page we touched could be recognized by anyone who knew how to look." His blind eyes fixed on some middle distance only he could see. "We called it our signature. Because a signature, properly made, cannot be forged by a man who doesn't know it exists."

I thought of the pamphlets in the snow. Of the reversed e. Of Wren setting type by candlelight in my father's shop, inheriting habits she didn't understand from a man she'd never met.

"What happened to the circle?" I asked.

Hollis was quiet long enough that the chandler's boy could be heard below us, wrapping candles, whistling something tuneless and cheerful.

"Sixteen of us," he said finally. "Compositors, pressmen, a bookbinder, two paper merchants, a woman who ran a stationer's stall by the Corn Exchange. We documented things. What the governor's men did at Marsh Lane. What they did at the granary. Who was taken and who came back and who didn't. We printed it small and hid it smaller. And then the raids started, and we learned that memory has weight, and weight can be dropped on you from a great height." He turned his teacup in his fingers. "Four of us stayed in Vellham that I know of. Two are dead. One went upriver and doesn't answer letters. And one—" he smiled again, that same half smile, "—one pours bad beer at the Sign of the Broken Quoin and pretends he was never anything but a publican."

* * *

The Sign of the Broken Quoin stood at the crooked end of Tanner Street, where the street gave up on geometry entirely, and it was the sort of tavern that survives by being too shabby to be worth taxing. The keeper was a barrel of a man with forearms like a press bed and a beard that appeared to have been set in metal type and left there.

He took one look at me across the smoke and gestured me into the back room with a jerk of his head, before I had said a word.

"You're Eastwick's girl," he said, once the door was shut. "Don't look surprised. You've got his hands. Ink in the knuckle creases that never comes out. Only printers get that, and only Eastwicks get it that deep."

"Mr.—"

"Ferris. Josiah Ferris." He said the name as if daring me to have heard of it. I hadn't. That seemed to please and wound him in equal measure. "And before you ask — yes, I was in the circle. No, I won't tell you what I kept. Yes, I still have it. No, I haven't shown it to anyone in eleven years, and I'm not starting tonight, and you can drink your cider and go home."

"That's a great many answers to questions I haven't asked."

"Save us both the time." But there was something under the gruffness — a carefulness, the wariness of a man who has practiced being unremarkable for so long that the practice has grown over him like bark over a fence post. He looked at my hands again. "Eleven years," he said, quieter. "You want to know why I stopped? Because they took Denny Marshman. Denny, who wouldn't hurt a fly and couldn't keep a secret from his own shadow. They took him and asked him questions for six days, and on the seventh day they let him go because there was nothing left of him worth keeping. And I sat in this room and I thought: everything I've hidden, everything I've remembered, it isn't worth one more Denny. So I buried my piece of it under the cellar floor and I pulled beer for eleven years and I told myself the record didn't need me."

"And now?"

"Now you come walking in with your father's hands and your father's jaw, and there are pamphlets turning up in the snow with his typeface on them, which I'll thank you not to pretend you know nothing about, because everyone on Tanner Street knows about the pamphlets and half of them think Eastwick's ghost is doing the printing." He leaned across the table. The lamplight caught something fierce in him, banked but not dead, the way coals stay alive overnight under ash. "And now there's a historian from the Ministry buying up private papers by the sackful and burning what he doesn't like, and I find myself thinking that maybe Denny died so that something would survive, and here I am, having survived, having kept nothing surviving at all."

He straightened up, embarrassed by his own eloquence, and busied himself with a cloth and the tabletop.

"There were sixteen of us," he said. "Everyone held a fragment. That was the design — your father's design, though he'd never admit to designing anything; he'd say the circle designed itself, which was nonsense, it was him through and through. Sixteen fragments. No one person held enough to be dangerous. No one person's arrest could end it. The ledger pages, the testimonies, the confiscated letters — split up like a case of type, each letter in its own compartment." He snorted. "He used to say a secret should be distributed the way lead is distributed. Evenly, and in pieces nobody would steal."

"Where are the others?"

"Gone or dead or silent. There was Ambrose Pell — our foreman, best pressman on the river — but he went upriver after the raids and good riddance, if you ask me, though nobody did." Something flickered across Ferris's face when he said the name, quick as a mislocked forme. "There was Marta Quill at the stationer's stall; dead three winters. The bookbinder, Oskar, drowned in the break-up flood, years back. And there was—" he hesitated, and I watched a man decide how much truth a stranger could carry, "—there was one more who stayed close. Closest of all. But she's not someone you can visit, girl. Not anymore."

"Who?"

Ferris shook his head slowly. "Ask me again when you've earned it. Or better, don't ask at all. Some fragments are safer broken."

* * *

Ambrose Pell was the hard one, which surprised me, because on paper he was the easy one — alive, reachable, a day's journey upriver to the quarry town of Harrow Bend, where he had supposedly settled into honest work dressing stone. Wren's list ended with him and a single annotation: *won't come back, won't write, don't trust him.* No reason given. Wren herself had never met him; the note was her father's, copied out faithfully, and dead men's suspicions weigh more than living ones.

I went anyway. I told myself it was thoroughness. In truth it was the pull every compositor feels toward the empty compartment in the case — the place where a letter ought to be, where the whole word stumbles without it.

Harrow Bend in February is a gray idea of a town arranged around a hole in the ground. I found Pell at the quarry office, a lean man gone silver at the temples, with hands that had clearly once done finer work than they did now. When I gave my name he went very still, the way water goes still before it freezes.

"Eastwick," he repeated. "You're his?"

"I'm his."

He looked past me, out the frosted window, at nothing. Then he said, "Come back at dark. Not before. People talk here worse than they print in Vellham."

What he showed me, in a rented room above the quarry office, was a fragment indeed: a bundle of papers wrapped in oilcloth and hidden inside a hollowed paving stone, which he lifted from beneath his bed with the ease of long practice. Prisoners' testimony, copied fair. Lists of names. Dates. And — my breath caught — proofs. Galley proofs in a familiar hand, corrected in the margins in another familiar hand entirely. My father's corrections. His little hooked question marks, his habit of striking through a word twice rather than once, as if once weren't emphatic enough.

"You kept these eleven years," I said.

"I kept them twelve." Pell's voice was dry as stone dust. "And I'll tell you what Ferris won't, since he's too busy being noble behind his bar. I didn't leave Vellham out of principle. I left because they came for me. I was warned — by her, by the one Ferris won't name — with an hour's notice, and I ran, and I have hated myself efficiently and continuously ever since." He rewrapped the oilcloth with precise, bitter care. "A coward keeps a good archive, Miss Eastwick. Guilt is excellent for preservation. Nothing rots faster than a clear conscience."

"Why won't you come back? If Crane is gathering everything, burning everything—"

"Because the last time I trusted the circle's security, men died. And because—" he paused, and for a moment the bitterness cracked and something older showed through, something that looked almost like grief, "—because I broke, in the end. Before I ran. They questioned me for two days before the warning came, and I talked. Small things. Names of the dead, mostly, which they already had. But I talked, and I've never known how much of what followed traced back to what I gave them. So no, I won't come back to Vellham. But take the papers. Take them all. Better they travel than that I keep practicing cowardice on them a thirteenth year."

* * *

I came home on the evening barge with a satchel against my chest and the river ice groaning underneath us like an old man turning over in bed. The city rose out of the dusk — the mill chimneys, the Ministry dome, the crooked spire of St. Verity's — and it looked exactly as it always had, which suddenly struck me as the most dishonest thing I had ever seen. All those years I had thought my father worked alone. All those years I had thought the shop's silence was grief. It had been neither. It had been a compartment in a case, holding its letter, waiting for the rest of the word to come home.

Wren was waiting for me in the doorway, trying to look as though she hadn't been waiting in the doorway, which is a skill that takes years to fail at properly.

"Well?" she said.

"Hollis remembers everything. Ferris kept his piece under the cellar floor and hates himself for it. Pell kept his under a paving stone and hates himself louder." I set the satchel on the composing stone and unwrapped the oilcloth, and watched Wren's face change as she saw the proofs — my father's hooked question marks marching down the margins like a flock of small birds. "Three fragments. Maybe four, if Ferris ever digs up his cellar floor. Out of sixteen."

"Then it's a start." She touched the proofs lightly, reverently, the way you touch a forme you're afraid of disturbing. "It's more than a start. It's—"

She didn't finish, because there came a knock at the shop door. Three knocks, unhurried, official. Through the fogged glass I could see the shape of a lantern, and behind the lantern, the shape of a man.

Not Tamsin. Tamsin knocked like an apology. This knock had paperwork behind it.

Wren looked at me. I looked at the satchel, open on the stone, my father's handwriting naked under the lamp — and then at the false bottom of the type cabinet, eleven feet away, and the longest eleven feet I have ever measured in my life.

"Through the back," I whispered, and went to answer the door.
