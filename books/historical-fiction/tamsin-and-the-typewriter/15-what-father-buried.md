## 15. What Father Buried

I did not sleep. This will surprise no one who has spent a night in a print shop, which is a place where sleep comes reluctantly even on ordinary nights — the smell of ink keeps vigil, the press stands in the dark like a patient ox that expects to be worked at dawn, and the type cases tick faintly as the wood contracts in the cold, as though hundreds of small metal letters were whispering to one another about you.

But this was not an ordinary night. Wren had gone home to Tanner's Row with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth under her coat, walking quickly and not looking back, because I told her not to look back, because people who look back look guilty, and because I wanted to be alone with what she'd shown me. Half the pages seized in the raid. Half remaining. Names of prisoners. Confiscated letters. Testimony taken down in my father's shorthand of dashes and crossed letters, the hand I had known since before I could read.

I sat up with a candle and the last proof Wren had pulled before the soldiers came, and I looked — properly looked, for the first time in two years — at my father's work.

Here is what I mean by properly. When he was alive, I read his printing the way you read any printed page: as a window. You look *through* it at the words. But a printer's daughter ought to know better than anyone that a page is also a wall, and walls can have things hidden inside them. My father used to say that type was honest twice over — once when it was set and once when it was read — but that between those two honesties there was room for a great deal of cunning.

So I looked at the wall instead of through the window. And I found what I should have found the night the pamphlets appeared in his old type case: his habits, repeated like a signature he couldn't stop signing.

Every compositor has them. Small departures from perfect practice that no master ever trains out of them because no master notices. My father kerned certain ligatures too tight — the *ff* in particular, drawn so close the letters nearly kissed, which old Mr. Halloran at the guild school would have called sloppy and which I now understood was deliberate, because tight-kerned *ff* appears exactly nowhere else in Vellham printing. And there was the other thing. The thing I had noticed on the pamphlet and dismissed as a damaged sort.

A reversed *e*.

One 'e' on each page, cast backwards, so that its counter faced the wrong way and the letter sat in the line like a man standing in a crowd facing the opposite direction. A flaw, I had thought. A worn piece of type that needed replacing. My father kept his cases immaculate; he would sooner have set a page with his teeth than let a battered sort into a forme. It was never a flaw. It was a flag. A mark planted in every page he touched, saying *this is mine, and I am telling you something.*

He had been telling me something for years, and I had been reading through the window.

* * *

The candle had burned down to a stub by the time I stopped shaking. I want to be honest about that: there was weeping, briefly and without dignity, the kind that happens when grief is interrupted by a discovery it doesn't know how to hold. Because the reversed 'e' meant he had known. Standing at the case, setting line after line, he had known the account might be seized, might be burned, might survive only in fragments — and he had built into the very body of the text a thread leading somewhere, and he had trusted that someone would follow it. Trusted me, or trusted whoever came after me, which amounts to the same thing when you are the someone who came after.

Then I blew my nose on a rag that had ink on it, because sentiment is no excuse for a dirty face, and I went to work.

Where does a reversed 'e' point? I asked myself, and then felt foolish, because it doesn't point anywhere; it's a letter. But it sits in a line, and lines sit in pages, and pages come off a press, and the press is the one thing in the shop that cannot be hidden, broken up, or carried away. If you were a man who loved your trade and distrusted the ministry in equal measure, where would you bury your treasure? Not in the floor — floors get pried up by looters. Not in the walls — walls get searched. In the machine itself. In the great iron heart that every searcher's eye slides past because it is furniture, it is background, it is the reason the building exists and therefore invisible as the sky.

The Albion-style common press my father bought secondhand from a dissolving house in Marlow stood against the north wall, and I had oiled it, mended it, tightened its staples and replaced its frisket cords for two years without once asking it a question. It weighed as much as a loaded cart. Underneath, the bed — the flat marble-and-iron table where the forme lies — was bolted to the frame, and beneath the bed was the coffin, and beneath the coffin, legs, and dust, and darkness.

But my father had taught me to strip and true that press when I was fourteen. He had put my hands on every part of it. *Know your machine like your own mouth,* he said, *because when something goes wrong at two in the morning with an order due, you'll be talking to yourself.* And there was one thing about the bed I had always accepted without thinking, the way you accept a scar on a parent's face: a slight raised lip along its southern edge, a doubling of metal that he called "the reinforcement," added, he said, by some previous owner to stiffen the plate.

Reinforcements do not usually have corners that meet too neatly. Reinforcements do not usually sound — when you rap them with a knuckle, as I did at four in the morning, alone, heart going like a drum-boy — different from the metal around them.

Hollow.

* * *

I will spare you the hour of prying, except to say that a woman with a candle, a compositor's bodkin, a mallet, and her father's stubbornness can defeat a lot of clever joinery, though not gracefully. The false plate was held by two countersunk pins dressed to look like rivet heads, and once I understood they were pins and not rivets, the whole thing came away with a squeal that I was certain would wake the whole of Cooper's Lane, and probably woke nobody, and left me crouched on the cold flags with my heart slamming and a shallow iron tray exposed in the bed of the press.

Inside the tray: oilcloth. Inside the oilcloth: wax-sealed packets, forty-one of them, laid in rows like loaves. Each packet bore a number in my father's hand. Inside the packets —

Plates. Master plates, cast lead-alloy, page after page after page of the true account. Not proofs, not copies, not the fragile paper testimony that burns — the matrices themselves. The source. Enough to print the whole history again and again until the ministry ran out of soldiers. He hadn't just written the record. He had armored it, and sunk it into the one place in Vellham no one would ever search, because no one searches the thing they intend to smash.

And tucked at the top of the tray, folded into a square no bigger than a prayer card, a letter. My name on the outside. His hand.

I sat down on the floor — the cold came up through my skirt and I didn't care — and I read it by candle stub, and I will copy it here entire, because it belongs in this account, and because if I don't write it out I will convince myself by spring that I dreamed it.

---

*Beatrix —*

*If you are reading this, then either the shop is yours and the times have caught up with us both, or you have sold the press to some fool who has stripped it for scrap and found more than scrap. I pray it is the first.*

*You will have found the plates. Do not reproach me for not telling you. Telling you would have been a cruelty — it would have made you a keeper of dangerous things while you were still a girl, and I wanted you to inherit my trade, not my enemies. Instead I have left you my habits, which are a plainer inheritance than money and harder to steal. You were always the one who watched. Your mother said you counted everything before you could walk. Counting is how you'll find your way back to me, I think.*

*What is here is the account the circle kept: what the governor did, whom he disappeared, what was sworn by those who saw it and did not live to swear it twice. Half our number believed it should be printed at once, consequences be damned. I believed it should survive first and speak later. A truth that dies shouting saves fewer people than a truth that waits and lives. I may be wrong. The dead are famously poor judges of timing.*

*If the circle still exists, give the plates to it. If it does not, find hands you trust — printers, ideally; printers are unreliable in every other respect but they do not destroy their own. And Beatrix: when it is finally printed, put my name on it if you judge that safe, and leave it off if you judge otherwise. The words matter more than the man. But if you ever set your own name beneath mine — well. Nothing would make me prouder, and nothing ever has.*

*Oil the press. She sticks in damp weather.*

*Your father*

* * *

Dawn came grey through the south windows while I sat among the scattered oilcloth and read the letter a fourth time. Somewhere outside a cart went by, and the river ice groaned its long complaint, and Cooper's Lane began its ordinary noises — shutters, sweeps, the pie-woman's bell — all of it proceeding as though the world had not quietly changed shape overnight.

Here is the thing I could not get past, and I have decided not to try. *Nothing would make me prouder.* He wrote that knowing I might never read it. He buried pride in iron alongside atrocity and names of the dead, and he trusted the iron to keep it until I came. Two years I had lived in this shop feeling like a caretaker of another person's life — keeping his trade warm, keeping his customers polite, keeping my head down below the level of a loud world — and all the while I had not been keeping his shop at all. I had been keeping his trust, unopened, the way you keep a letter you're afraid to read.

He trusted me all along. He simply hadn't told me, because telling me would have made the danger real, and love, apparently, in my father's grammar, meant carrying the weight silently and leaving instructions in the margins.

I could be angry about that. I expect I will be, some days. That morning I was not. That morning I repacked the packets, resealed the tray, reset the false plate, drove the pins home, and swept every shaving into the stove, and when I rapped the bed of the press it sounded like solid metal all the way across, and I put my palm flat against the cold iron and left it there for a moment, which is not something I do, and which I did anyway.

Then I heard the knock at the door.

Not Wren's knock — Wren knocks like she's apologizing to the door. Not the pie-woman. Three slow, official raps, spaced like a clerk counting. Through the frosted glass: a shape in a good coat, hat brim level, waiting with the patience of a man whose patience costs him nothing.

I stood up in my dead father's shop, with his letter folded against my ribs and his plates sealed under my palms, and I thought: *the times have caught up with us both, Father, rather faster than either of us planned.*

I opened the door, and Aldous Crane, historian to the victorious, stepped in out of the snow.
