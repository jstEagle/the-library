## 16. Crane Closes In

There is a particular quality to being summoned by a man who does not summon you — a summons that arrives instead as an errand, a casual note slipped into the day's work basket, unsigned, unsealed. *Clerk Tamsin will attend the Ministry at half past nine. Bring the Vellham district files.* That was all. No reason given. No reason is the reason.

I read it three times by the grey light of my lodgings and then folded it into quarters, the way I fold everything, because folding things is what I am for. A clerk folds. A clerk files. A clerk makes the world small enough to fit in drawers. And somewhere in the last month, without noticing the exact moment of it, I had begun making the world small enough to fit in drawers *wrong* — misfiling one household here, blurring one entry there, letting Eastwick's census record dissolve into the general sludge of the river district like a spoonful of salt in the current. Small sins. Deniable sins. The kind of sins that feel, from inside, like virtues.

But Crane did not rise to his position by failing to notice salt in water.

I dressed carefully. Not too well — suspicion attaches itself to a clerk who suddenly owns a new coat — and not too shabbily either, because Crane's eye catalogued neglect as readily as vanity. Ink on the cuffs. There is always ink on my cuffs; it has become less a stain than a signature. I looked at myself in the cracked mirror over the washstand and saw what Beatrix Eastwick had seen on my first visit to her shop: a person built entirely of observation, watching everyone while remaining essentially unwatchable. It had been useful once. Now it felt like wearing someone else's face, and the seams were showing.

The Ministry building had been a merchant's palace before the uprising, all marble staircases and pilasters, seized and repurposed the way the victors repurpose everything — with fresh paint over old blood, or rather old paint over fresher blood, depending which side of the ledger you kept. Guards at the door nodded me through. My name was expected. Of course it was.

Aldous Crane received me in an office that contained, notably, no papers. Every other office in the Ministry drowned in paper — petitions, requisitions, the endless administrative silt of a city trying to govern itself after nearly drowning itself. But Crane's desk held a single lamp, a single blotter, and nothing else. He kept everything in his head, which meant he could burn the whole Ministry tomorrow and lose nothing but the furniture. I have thought about that since, about what kind of man arranges his life so that he is always one match away from clean.

"Clerk Tamsin." He gestured to the chair across from him. He did not offer tea. He never offered tea; tea, I suspect, struck him as a form of bribery conducted at close range. "You've been with the census bureau how long now?"

"Six years, sir."

"Six years." He turned the phrase over like a coin he suspected of being clipped. "And in those six years, your error rate has been — I checked this morning, so forgive me if I recite your own history back to you — negligible. Point-zero-four percent. Remarkable. Clerks twice your tenure make three times your mistakes." He leaned back. "And then, beginning in the autumn, your error rate climbs to two-point-one percent."

I kept my hands still in my lap. Hands tell truths when mouths are lying; I'd learned that watching depositions. "I'm sorry, sir. The winter quarter is difficult. Illness in the districts, households refusing entry—"

"Oh, absolutely. Refusals, illness, the ice blocking the upper streets for three weeks." He waved a hand, gracious, magnanimous, terrible. "All perfectly ordinary explanations, and I have accepted them. Accepted every one." He smiled, and the smile was the worst thing in the room, worse than anger would have been, because anger would have told me where I stood. "Do you know why I've accepted them, Clerk Tamsin?"

"No, sir."

"Because errors are information too. A clerk who makes no mistakes is a machine, and machines don't think. A clerk who suddenly begins making *specific* mistakes—" he tapped the blotter with one finger, once, softly, like a man knocking at a door he already knew would be opened, "—is telling me something. Every misfiled household in the river district sits within four streets of Eastwick's print shop. Every blurred entry belongs to someone who bought paper wholesale in the last year. You haven't become careless, Tamsin. You've become *curious*. And curiosity in a census clerk is either a liability or an instrument, depending entirely on whose hand holds it."

The room was very warm. Or I was. I thought of the ledger hidden beneath the false bottom of Beatrix's type cabinet, of Wren's burned fingertips from the candle stubs she hoarded, of fifty names in a dead printer's cramped hand, and I made my face do what clerks' faces do best, which is nothing at all.

"I don't understand what you're asking of me, sir."

"What I'm asking." He rose and went to the window, hands clasped behind him, looking out at the frozen river as if it were a document he was proofreading. "What I'm asking is simpler than that. I'm not asking anything. I'm *assigning*. Effective today, you're transferred out of field enumeration. You'll be cataloguing instead. The garrison has seized eleven print shops since the uprising — presses, type cases, stock, correspondence. All of it sitting in warehouses gathering damp while officers argue about jurisdiction. Nobody has made an inventory. An inventory requires someone meticulous, incorruptible, with an eye for detail." He turned back to me. "Someone with a point-zero-four percent error rate."

My stomach performed a slow, cold revolution. Eleven shops. And among them — I knew it before he said it, knew it the way you know thunder before the sound arrives —

"One of them is Eastwick's," I said. Flatly. A fact. Facts were safe. Facts were what clerks dealt in.

"Eastwick's," he agreed, pleasantly. "The late Josiah Eastwick's establishment, currently under seal pending review. You'll begin there. Full catalogue: every tray of type identified by foundry and size, every forme examined, every press measured and its condition noted. And Tamsin—" he paused, and the pause was a knife laid flat on the table between us, not yet picked up, "—pay particular attention to anomalies. Type that doesn't match the shop's known output. Furniture that doesn't fit the joinery. Drawers with false bottoms. Printers are sentimental men, and sentimental men hide things in their craft the way priests hide relics in their altars. If there is anything in that shop that shouldn't be there, you will find it. Because if you do not find it, I will be forced to conclude that either the shop contains nothing—" another smile, "—or that the clerk cataloguing it has opinions."

He let that sit in the air between us, cooling like a brand taken from the fire.

"You're dismissed. Reports weekly. In writing. I dislike surprises, Clerk Tamsin, and I especially dislike them in triplicate."

---

I walked out through the marble corridors with my mind moving faster than my feet, and my feet, mercifully, knew their own business well enough not to stumble. Catalogue the seized shops. Including hers. He had handed me a lantern and pointed me at the powder store, and whichever way I moved — thorough and true, or thorough and false — I would be lighting something.

Because here was the arithmetic of it, the ugly sum I turned over all the way down the stairs: if I catalogued honestly, I might find what her father hid — if anything remained to find — and then my own meticulous hand would deliver it to the fire. And if I catalogued dishonestly, Crane would smell the falseness the way he smelled salt in water, and my two-point-one percent would climb to something terminal. There was no clean line through this. There was only the question of which lie could survive inspection.

I spent the afternoon in the records annex, pulling the seizure manifests, and that was where the dread truly took root, because the manifest for Eastwick's listed, in a garrison clerk's indifferent scrawl: *one double-crown press, iron, condition fair; four type cabinets; assorted furniture; quantity of lead type, unmixed; one composing stone; miscellaneous.* Miscellaneous. The most dangerous word in any language, because it means *we didn't look.*

They hadn't looked. The soldiers who sealed the shop had been soldiers, not printers — they'd seen a press and some boxes and written *miscellaneous* the way tired men write anything, wanting only to go somewhere warm. Whatever Josiah Eastwick had buried in his craft was still buried, provided no one with a compositor's eye went looking.

Provided no one like me.

* * *

I went to the shop at dusk, walking the long way, doubling back twice out of habit even though no one was following me — or rather, everyone was following me now, potentially, permanently, and the habit of checking had curdled from professional caution into something closer to prayer. The seal on the door was intact. The garrison's wax, stamped with the portcullis and crossed quills, unbroken across the latch. Through the grimy window the interior sat in blue evening shadow: the press a dark humped shape, the type cabinets ranked against the wall like coffins awaiting occupants.

Beatrix let me in through the cellar hatch, because we were past the age of using doors. She'd been sorting type by candlelight, I could see, the little brass sort-case open on the trestle, her fingers black to the second knuckle.

"They've reassigned me," I said, without preamble. There was no gentle way to build up to it and I have never believed in cushioning bad news anyway; cushioning just gives the blow further to fall. "Crane himself. I'm to inventory the seized print shops. Starting with this one. Weekly reports, in writing, to him personally."

Her hands went still over the case. Not dramatically — Beatrix does not do dramatic; grief and fear in her arrive quietly, like weather changes. Just stillness, the stillness of someone listening to ice shift underfoot.

"When?"

"I report Monday."

"And what will you report?"

"That," I said, "is rather the question, isn't it."

She set down the type she was holding, one piece of lead, precisely back in its compartment, because even at the edge of ruin Beatrix returns things to their proper places. Then she looked at me, and what was in her face was not fear. That was the thing that undid me slightly, standing there in the candle-smell and the cold. What was in her face was calculation — the same flat, patient attention she gave a locked forme or a stubborn press screw.

"He wants you to find something," she said. "Or he wants to see whether you'll pretend not to find it. Either way, the report isn't really about the shop. It's about you."

"Yes."

"Then we don't give him either answer. We give him a third one." She was already moving, candle in hand, toward the type cabinets, running her fingers along their edges the way a blind woman reads. "You can't catalogue this shop falsely, Tamsin. You'd be caught inside a week — you said yourself he checks everything. So we catalogue it *truly*. Every tray, every font, every drawer, exactly as a meticulous clerk would. And everything in it is exactly what it appears to be. Because it already is."

"The ledger—"

"Is not here anymore." Something flickered across her mouth, not quite a smile. "You helped move it yourself. This shop is empty of secrets, Tamsin. It's empty of almost everything else, but it is genuinely, verifiably empty of secrets. The plates are gone, the ledger is gone, Wren's night work is six months cold. What's left is lead and iron and a dead man's dust in the corners." She set the candle down on the composing stone. "So you write the most honest inventory of your career. And when Crane reads it and finds nothing, he'll have learned exactly one thing: that Clerk Tamsin did flawless work and the shop held nothing worth having."

"Unless he doesn't believe it. Unless 'nothing' is precisely what a guilty clerk would report."

"Then he comes himself, or sends someone who knows presses." She shrugged, a small tight motion. "We can't control that. We can only make sure that whoever opens these cabinets finds nothing but honest type. Which is why—" and now she did smile, thin and sharp, "—you're going to learn enough about type to pass inspection. You're going to spend the next three days learning what a font looks like, what a mixed case looks like, what a repaired forme looks like. Because if your report says *assorted lead type, unmixed*, Crane will know you're a fraud. And if it identifies fourteen fonts by size and body with the foundry marks noted, he'll believe you, because only a real catalogue looks like that."

I stared at her. "You want me to become competent at the thing I'm betraying so that I can betray it better."

"I want you to become competent at the thing you love," she said, "so you can protect it properly. The betrayal part you were already good at."

It should have stung. It landed instead like absolution — wry, unsentimental, the exact register in which Beatrix Eastwick forgives people, which is to say she forgives them by declining to treat them as fragile.

* * *

So we worked. Three days, evenings and stolen mornings, and I learned more about the anatomy of a print shop than six years of confiscating them had taught me. She taught me to distinguish foundry faces by their serifs and their stress — this one cast in Vellham before the war, this one imported upriver, its moulds worn soft so every *e* came out slightly closed, like an eye narrowing. She taught me why a compositor spaces by eye and not by rule, and showed me her father's spacing, unmistakable to her, generous around the capitals the way he was generous with strangers and stingy with himself. She taught me to read a forme the way I read a deposition: what it says on the surface, and what it says underneath by accident.

"You're cataloguing a corpse," she said on the second evening, watching me measure the press bed with her father's brass rule. "Be respectful, but don't flinch. Flinching is what gets people caught. Guilty people flinch away from details; honest people walk straight through them."

"And which am I?"

"Both," she said, without cruelty. "That's why you have to practice."

On the third evening she stopped me at the type cabinet — the fourth one, the one against the north wall, the one with the false bottom that had held the ledger until fear moved it elsewhere. She knelt, ran her thumb along the cabinet's lower rail, and showed me the seam. Then she showed me how the bottom lifted, and what lay beneath it now: nothing. Clean wood, swept, wiped, smelling faintly of linseed where she'd refreshed the grain.

"It stays," she said. "The false bottom stays. A perfect cabinet with no false bottom in a printer's shop is its own kind of anomaly — Crane's men won't notice it, but a printer would. Any compositor who ever worked this bench built false bottoms the way other men build cellars. It's furniture. It's tradition. Let it be found empty and described honestly, and it becomes the most convincing thing in the whole report."

"An honest lie supported by a genuine secret compartment containing nothing."

"Now you understand the trade," she said. "Welcome to the family business."

I wrote the report over two nights at my lodgings, by my own lamp, on ministry stock with ministry ink, in my own hand, which is to say the hand that had logged ten thousand households and betrayed perhaps forty of them. I described fourteen fonts. I noted the press's wear pattern and the replacement of the frisket cords with newer hemp, dated by rot to approximately eight months prior. I recorded the false-bottomed cabinet as *type cabinet, pine, four drawers, lower drawer with sliding panel (common printer's modification), empty*. I walked straight through every detail and flinched at none of them, because she had trained me not to, and because every word was true, and the truth, arranged correctly, is the deepest lockpick there is.

At the bottom, where the form asked for remarks, I wrote: *No materials present inconsistent with lawful trade stock. Recommend release of seal; premises hold no evidentiary value.*

Recommend release of the seal. Read that again, as Crane would read it, and then as I intended it: an honest finding, or an invitation — come and look yourself, Aldous, since you trust nothing, least of all me. Both readings available. Both readings true. I sealed it, and my hand was steady, and I despised how steady it was, and I was grateful for it too.

* * *

I delivered the report on Monday morning. Crane took it without comment, weighed it in his hand as though he could assess truth by mass, and set it on the blotter — the blotter that had held nothing, and now held my future in four pages.

"You recommend releasing the seal."

"The shop holds nothing of value to the inquiry, sir."

"Mm." He did not open the pages. He looked at me instead, that flat archival gaze, filing me the way I filed others. "You visited the premises how many times before drafting this?"

"Three. Plus the initial survey."

"Three visits, four pages, fourteen fonts." He nodded slowly, as though confirming an invoice. "Thorough work, Clerk Tamsin. Almost — thorough." He let the word hang. "I'll tell you what troubles me, and then you may go. What troubles me is that the Eastwick woman's household record was among your autumn irregularities. The same shop. The same street. Errors cluster, Tamsin, like witnesses. Coincidence has a geography, and yours is very small." He slid the report into a drawer — I never learned which; his drawers were like his silences. "Your recommendation is noted. The seal stays. I may send an assessor — a printer, someone who speaks the trade's dialect. Sentimental men hide things in their craft, and it takes a craftsman to search them properly. Nothing personal, you understand. You've done exactly what I asked." The smile arrived, brief and bloodless. "Which is, of course, what concerns me."

I walked out through the marble corridors, past the guards, down the steps into the white cold of the street, and stood for a moment breathing air that tasted of coal smoke and the river's mineral patience. Somewhere under the ice, the water was still moving. It always is; that's the secret the frozen river keeps. It looks like a dead thing, sealed, catalogued, *miscellaneous* — and underneath, the whole time, it's going somewhere.

I went to find Beatrix. An assessor was coming. A craftsman, speaking the trade's dialect, would open those cabinets and run knowing fingers along every rail — and one rail, one sliding panel, one empty-but-not-supposed-to-be-empty drawer, would tell him everything about what this shop had been and who had kept it.

We had days, perhaps. And I had one more skill Beatrix hadn't yet thought to ask for, the one skill six years of the census had drilled into me deeper than any craft: I knew how the Ministry wrote its paperwork, and therefore I knew exactly what a forged inventory needed to look like — and exactly which clerk's signature, forged convincingly enough, could make a warehouse full of honest scrap out of a printer's whole inheritance.

It was time to teach her the other family business.
