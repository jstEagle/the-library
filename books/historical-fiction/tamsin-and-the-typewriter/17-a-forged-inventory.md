## 17. A Forged Inventory

There is a particular smell to official paper. I had never noticed it until I needed to make it myself.

It is not the ink, though the ink matters — a government bureau uses a cheap iron-gall mix that browns at the edges of every letter like old blood. It is not the paper, though the paper matters too: rag stock, unbleached, cut one finger wider than any commercial stationer would dare, because bureaucracy has always loved wasting width. No. The smell is older than all of that. It is dust from the ministry clerks' room, lamp oil, cold tea, the sweat of ten thousand anxious petitions. It is the smell of patience wearing a uniform. And on a grey morning in the Eastwick print shop, standing over a sheet of blank paper with my hands trembling in a way they never trembled over genuine filings, I realized I did not know how to counterfeit patience.

"You're holding it wrong," Beatrix said.

"I'm holding it exactly as I've held four hundred of them."

"You're holding it as someone who wants it to look right." She took the sheet from me, turned it ninety degrees, and set it down again under the type case's edge. "Hold it as someone who doesn't care. That's the trick of every honest document ever written. Nobody who filed a true inventory ever worried about whether it looked true."

I wanted to argue. I am, by training and temperament, an arguer — it is what the ministry pays me for, or rather what the ministry fails to notice me doing. But she was correct, and we both knew it, and there is a particular humiliation in being out-crafted at your own trade by a printer who has never set foot inside a records office. So I said nothing, which Beatrix correctly interpreted as surrender, and handed me back the pen.

* * *

We had been at it since before dawn.

The problem was this: Aldous Crane had assigned me — his diligent little census clerk, his quiet pair of eyes — to catalog the seized and seizable printing concerns of Vellham, Eastwick's among them. A full inventory. Presses, type cases, furniture, stock, and — the item that mattered more than all the others combined — "any plates, dies, or matrices of historical significance," which was Crane's way of saying *the truth, if you find it, bring it to me so I can kill it properly.*

If my inventory reported no such plates, Crane would wonder why. He had already begun wondering about my filings; I could feel his suspicion the way you feel weather change behind a closed window. If my inventory reported the plates present, he would come for them, and everything — the ledger, the reconstructed pages, the master account her father had hidden in the press bed like a heart inside a chest — would go into the ministry historian's fire within the week.

But there was a third door, and it had been Beatrix's idea, because most good terrible ideas are.

"They melted them," she'd said, two nights past, turning a lead slug over in her fingers by lamplight. "That's what shops do when type wears out. You melt it down and recast. It's the most ordinary sentence in my trade. *Melted for recasting.* Write that, Tamsin. Write it so boringly that he falls asleep reading it."

So that was the forgery: not a lie dressed as truth, but a truth dressed as tedium. An inventory of Eastwick's shop in which the plates appeared once, briefly, and were disposed of. Worn matrices. Cracked. Melted down for scrap lead in the autumn, before the frost made the workshop too cold for casting. Dated. Witnessed. Stamped.

The trouble with lies is that they want to be interesting. Every instinct in a liar's body pushes toward detail — the extra flourish, the convincing specificity, the little flourish of authenticity. And every one of those flourishes is a hook for a suspicious man to hang his doubts on. Crane was the most suspicious man in Vellham. We could not afford a single hook.

Which is why I spent that grey morning learning to write boredom.

* * *

The paper came first. Ministry stock was not something one could buy, but it could be acquired. I will not record here precisely how; let it suffice that a junior clerk in the Office of Seizures owed a debt to a certain tavern-keeper, and that the tavern-keeper was a member of a certain circle, and that debts among conspirators are paid in goods rather than gratitude. Three dozen sheets arrived wrapped in oilcloth inside a delivery of tallow candles, and Beatrix held one up to the light and pronounced it "lovely — you can see the rags fighting each other."

"That's a flaw."

"It's character. Your ministry buys cheap and proud." She was right, of course. She was irritatingly, consistently right about materials. Her father had trained her eye on type and paper the way other fathers train sons on horses and ledgers, and she could tell the provenance of a sheet by its tooth the way I could tell a forged signature by its hesitation strokes.

The seals were harder. A genuine ministry inventory carried two marks: the stamped seal of the Office of Seizures, pressed in wafer rather than wax because wax was considered extravagant, and the initials of the filing clerk in iron-gall ink at the foot, small and slanted and utterly without personality. My own initials, as it happened. I had initialed hundreds of filings. My hand at the bottom of a document was itself a kind of official mark now, and using it felt like signing away a version of myself I wasn't sure I'd miss.

The stamp was the real problem. We could not steal it — its absence would be noticed within a day, and noticed by Crane personally, because Crane noticed absences the way other men noticed faces. So we made one.

Beatrix cut it. Of course she cut it. She sat up two nights with a piece of boxwood and a graver finer than a needle, working from an impression I had taken — legally, in the course of my duties, from a filing I had every right to handle — pressed into soft putty and brought to the shop under my coat like a stolen kiss. She enlarged nothing, simplified nothing. She reproduced even the crack in the die's border, the hairline fault running through the left side of the civic wreath that some careless engraver had introduced years ago and no one had bothered to repair.

"Why keep the crack?" I asked her, watching her work by candlelight, her breath held, her whole body curved around the little block of wood like a mother around a sleeping child. "A perfect seal would be safer. A cracked one invites scrutiny."

"A perfect seal invites scrutiny," she corrected. "Nobody looks at a cracked seal. They've seen it ten thousand times. The crack is the proof it's real." Then she laughed, short and sharp. "God help me, my father taught me that. He used to say the flaws are where the truth lives in a typeface. I spent three years resenting him for the lesson. Now I'm grateful." She blew a curl of boxwood shaving off the block. "Don't tell him I said so. Wherever he is. Whatever he is now."

* * *

By mid-morning we had a stack of false papers that would have fooled me, and I say that as a compliment to no one.

The inventory ran eleven pages. Eleven pages of presses described by maker and year, of type cases counted by fathom and font, of ink casks gauged and furniture listed by the pound. Beatrix supplied the technical language — I knew the words but not their weight, and a document's weight lives in its details — while I supplied the grammar of bureaucracy: the passive constructions, the careful hedges, the phrases that commit to nothing. *"Condition assessed as serviceable."* *"Quantity approximate owing to disarray of premises."* *"No anomalies noted."*

"No anomalies noted," Beatrix read aloud, and snorted. "Four sentences. Do you know how many men have died for want of that sentence, Tamsin? How many shops burned, how many names struck? *No anomalies noted.* It might be the most murderous phrase in the language."

"Then let it murder our enemies for once."

She looked at me then — a long look, the kind she rarely gave, because looking directly at things was against both our natures. "You've changed your allegiance very quietly," she said. "I keep waiting to catch you grieving for it."

I considered lying to her, which would have been ironic given our occupation that morning. "I grieve for it at odd hours," I said finally. "Usually around three in the morning. Then I remember Aldous Crane explaining to me, with perfect courtesy, which version of the uprising would be permitted to exist, and the grief goes away and something colder takes its place. I don't think it's allegiance. I think it's just arithmetic. There were names on my census lists, Beatrix. Names I wrote down in good faith. I know what he does with names."

She nodded slowly and went back to the type case. "Then let's finish the arithmetic."

* * *

The plates entry itself took us the better part of two hours, and we argued over nearly every word of it.

Too much detail and it sang; too little and it vanished entirely, and a vanished entry is its own kind of flag — Crane read inventories the way priests read scripture, alert to every omission. The entry had to exist, be findable, be mundane, and be closed. Finished. A question already answered before he thought to ask it.

In the end it read:

*Item 47: Matrix plates, copper-faced, approx. fourteen (14) pieces, various sizes, condition deteriorated (cracking, worm). Assessed as unusable. Disposed: melted for scrap lead, 9 October last, in ordinary course of workshop economy. Proceeds credited to stock account. Witness: J. Harrow, compositor.*

J. Harrow. Josiah Harrow, the blind compositor, whose name on the line gave the entry flesh — a witness who existed, who could, if it ever came to it, swear with a straight face and sightless eyes that he had stood in the yard and watched lead run into pigs. Whether Josiah would thank us for the honor remained to be seen. Beatrix swore he'd laugh. I hoped she was right. Conspiracies live and die by whether their jokes land.

The date mattered more than anything. Ninth of October — before the frost, plausible for casting work, and crucially, before the raid on the circle that had seized half the ledger. If the plates had been melted in October, then whatever Crane believed still existed had been destroyed before anyone knew it needed protecting. Innocence requires a timeline, and timelines require cruelty: we had to make it appear that Beatrix had destroyed her father's work casually, seasonally, without knowing its worth. That was the part that cost her. I watched her write the date, and I watched her hand pause above the numerals, and I understood that for a moment she was not forging a document but testifying against herself — swearing, in official ink, that she had been the kind of daughter who melts her father's legacy for scrap.

"You could date it differently," I offered. "Later. After the raid. Say you panicked."

"No." She finished the numeral firmly. "Panic leaves traces. Ordinary doesn't. Besides—" and here her voice did something complicated, "—if I'm going to sign a lie saying I melted his plates, I'd rather it be a calm lie. He'd have respected the craftsmanship."

* * *

We aged the documents that afternoon, and aging, I learned, is a science of patience and abuse.

Fresh ink shines. Fresh paper is stiff. Fresh folds are crisp. Any clerk who handles documents daily reads age the way farmers read sky, and Crane had handled more documents than God. So Beatrix took the finished sheets and folded them wrong — folded along lines a filing clerk's hands would naturally make, quartered for a folder, rolled once for transport, refolded slightly off the original crease so the paper showed the memory of two different foldings. Then she pressed them under the type case's iron frame overnight, weighted, so the fibers relaxed into acceptance.

The ink she dulled with a breath of fine ash, worked in with a rabbit's foot — an actual rabbit's foot, which Wren produced from somewhere with the shameless pride of a street child contributing to the cause. The ash took the shine off the iron-gall and left the letters looking dried-out, tired, official.

And the smell. This was the part I confess I resisted, right up until I understood it. Beatrix kept a drawer of things in the shop — I had noticed it before, a clutter of rags and oddments — and from it she took a scrap of blotting paper that had lain for months beneath a pile of her father's business correspondence. Old, faintly sour, smelling of lamp oil and cold tea and dust. She laid our finished pages on it overnight, weighted again, and in the morning they smelled — faintly, unmistakably, only to a nose that knew — like the interior of a ministry file room.

"There," she said, handing them to me. "Smell."

I smelled. My eyes stung unexpectedly. "It smells like the worst room in Vellham."

"It smells like the truth, as your masters define it." She was smiling, but not widely. "That's the joke of it, isn't it? We're not forging a lie. We're forging the exact thing they'd have made if they'd done the work themselves. All we're doing is saving Crane the labor. We're being helpful."

"The most dangerous word in the language."

"*Helpful,*" she agreed. "Worse than treason. Treason gets you hanged. Helpful gets you promoted."

* * *

I filed the inventory three days later, in person, through the front counter of the Records Office, because a document delivered secretly is a document worth investigating, and a document delivered tediously is a document worth shelving.

I wore my uniform. I waited in the queue behind a widow contesting a seizure and a brick merchant disputing a tax assessment, and I listened to their grievances with the patient blankness of seven years' practice, and when my turn came I slid the inventory across the counter with the receipt book open beside it and said, "Eastwick concern, complete, no anomalies noted," and the counter clerk — a boy named Pellish with a cold and a career ahead of him consisting entirely of this counter — stamped it received, initialled my initials, and dropped it in the basket.

The basket. That was the moment. I watched that cheap wicker basket swallow eleven pages of the most careful lying in the history of Vellham, and I felt something rise in my chest that took me a moment to name, because I had never felt it before in that building.

It was pleasure. Pure, professional, exhilarating pleasure. For seven years I had filed truths for people who bent them, and it had tasted like chalk. This lie — this beautiful, seamless, perfectly bored lie — tasted like craft. Like the thing I imagine Beatrix feels when a forme locks up tight and the first proof comes off clean. Competence, finally pointed in the correct direction. I walked out of the Records Office into the grey afternoon and I am not ashamed to say I bought a candied plum from the corner seller and ate it walking, like a child, like a criminal, like a free man.

* * *

Beatrix met me that evening at the shop, having entered separately, having not spoken to me on the street, having observed all the small disciplines that keep the living alive. She was sorting type when I came in — she sorted type the way other people pray, constantly and without needing a reason — and she didn't look up.

"Well?"

"Filed. Received. Stamped. Shelved by Thursday."

"And Crane?"

"Nothing yet. Which is either very good or very bad."

"Both," she said. "With him it's always both." She set a line of type and justified it with a tap of her thumb, then looked over at me at last. "Do you want to know the strange part? Part of me hopes he reads it and believes it instantly. And another part of me — a vain part, a craftsman's part — hopes he studies it. Turns it over. Checks the watermark against three other files. Because if he does all that and still believes it—" she smiled, and it was not a gentle smile, "—then for one shining moment Aldous Crane will hold absolute certainty, and the certainty will be ours. We'll have written something inside his head. That's printing, Tamsin. That's all printing ever was."

I thought about telling her then. About the small thing, the wrongness I had noticed that afternoon — the way Pellish the counter clerk had looked at me half a heartbeat too long, or perhaps hadn't; the way the queue had felt observed; the fact that when I left the office I was near-certain no one followed me, and near-certain was a word I had stopped trusting weeks ago.

I didn't tell her. Not because I doubted her, but because she had earned one evening of triumph, and because I told myself it was probably nothing.

It was not nothing. Two days later, a summons came to my desk in the Records Office, on Crane's personal stationery, requesting my presence at his convenience regarding "a discrepancy noted in recent filings."

I read it twice. Somewhere across the city, Beatrix was teaching Wren to cast lead, and the river ice groaned in its sleep, and I sat holding a single sheet of paper that smelled of nothing at all — no ash, no lamp oil, no forgery, just the plain cold scent of genuine ministerial attention.

He had found something. Or he wished me to believe he had. With Crane, I was learning, the two were often the same document.
