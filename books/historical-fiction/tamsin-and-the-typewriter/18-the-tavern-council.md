## 18. The Tavern Council

The Drowned Bell smelled of tallow smoke, spilled porter, and forty years of arguments that had outlived every man who started them. It was the tavern-keeper's pride and, I suspected, the reason he kept the place — not for the coin, which was thin, but because a room where people shout at each other honestly is worth more than a church where they whisper.

His name was Oskar Brandt. He had been my father's friend, though I had only ever known him as the large man who once bought me a honey cake and told me printers were the only honest drunks in Vellham. Now he stood behind his bar with his arms folded like a man guarding a fortress gate, and around his tables sat the remains of my father's secret circle: Wren, vibrating with the importance of being included; Marta Ilves, the blind compositor, whose fingers could read a galley of type faster than most men read print; and Ferenc Kovacs, the exiled foreman, who had arrived last, late, and loudly, and who had spent the walk from the door glaring at everyone as if they owed him money.

And in the corner, hood up, nursing a cup of small beer she had no intention of drinking, sat Tamsin — off duty, off the census rolls, and visibly uncomfortable at being the only person in the room who still technically worked for the ministry.

"Before we begin," Oskar said, "I want it understood that whatever is said here stays here. The walls of the Drowned Bell have heard worse and said nothing."

"The walls of this tavern," Ferenc said, "are the reason half our people are in the governor's cells."

The silence that followed had edges.

"That," Oskar said carefully, "is a matter we agreed to set aside."

"We agreed *you* set it aside," Ferenc said. "I was in Marlow's cellar when the soldiers came through a door only four people knew about. So forgive me if my memory is less agreeable than yours."

Marta's hand found the table edge and rested there, steady as a composing stick. "Ferenc. Sit down."

"I am sitting down."

"Then sit down more quietly."

I had met these people exactly twice before, and both times I had come away with the impression that my father had not so much founded a circle as adopted a family — one that argued at funerals and loved each other anyway, in the way that people love whom they cannot leave. Tonight the affection was buried deeper than usual. Tonight there were plates to move and an ice sheet groaning under its own weight, and everyone knew the deadline better than they wanted to say it aloud.

* * *

We began, as all doomed meetings begin, with maps. Oskar cleared the bar top and spread out a river chart so old the ink had gone the brown of dried blood. Vellham sat at the bend; below us, the river ran sixty miles through the gorges to Harrow Ford, and past Harrow Ford into the low country, where the presses printed whatever they liked and the governors pretended not to notice because the paper trade paid their taxes.

"The ice breaks within the fortnight," Oskar said. "The bargemen say sooner. When it goes, the first barges down are coal and grain, and after them everything else. We put the plates on a boat in that crowd, and we have a chance."

"And until then?" Wren asked. "They sit in the press bed?"

"They sit where they sit," Ferenc said flatly. "Which is why I say we don't wait. We print now. Tonight. A thousand copies. Five hundred. Whatever the press can hold. You scatter them through the city before Crane can burn a single stack, and then let him explain to the city why its own history is contraband."

"You'd be dead by morning," Tamsin said quietly, from the corner.

Everyone turned. It was, I think, the first time Tamsin had spoken in that company without being introduced, and the audacity of it hung in the smoke.

"Probably," Ferenc said, unbothered. "But dead men make poor targets for revision. Martyrs stay printed. That's the whole craft, isn't it, Eastwick?" He jabbed a finger toward me. "Your father understood it. Ink outlives flesh. Print the truth while you're alive to defend it, and no historian can unpick it from your cold hands."

"My father also spent fifteen years hiding those plates instead of printing them," I said. "Fifteen years, Ferenc. He wasn't a coward. He was patient. There's a difference, and the difference is that he intended the account to arrive complete, with names and dates and testimony, not as a broadside someone scrapes off the wall before breakfast."

"A broadside in every doorway—"

"—is a bonfire by noon," Marta finished. Her voice was dry as old paper. "You've forgotten what Vellham looked like two winters ago, Ferenc. Or perhaps you weren't here for the aftermath." She turned her face toward the rest of us — toward me, precisely, which is a strange thing to receive from a blind woman. "He means well. He always meant well. But meaning well has already cost us Marlow, and Sef, and the Hollis brothers, and I will not stand in this room and vote to spend anyone else."

"No one's asking you to stand," Ferenc said. "That's rather the point. Some of us are willing to lie down for it."

"Some of you," Marta said, "have been lying down for so long you've mistaken it for principle."

Oskar slammed a mug down — not thrown, just placed, hard, the way he presumably ended arguments for a living. "Enough. Beatrix. You found the plates. You're the keeper now. Say your piece."

Which was, I realized, the trap and the honor of the evening both. Every eye came around to me, and I felt the weight of lead in my chest, the way I feel it when I'm holding a full case of twelve-point and know one dropped tray undoes a day's work.

* * *

Here is what I said, or near enough, though I confess my hands were not steady and my voice cracked somewhere in the middle, which I will blame on the tavern's dust:

My father built the archive to survive, not to die spectacularly. He hid it in the press itself because a press is the one object in Vellham no one burns lightly — even Crane wants working presses confiscated, not destroyed; paper is power and he intends to keep the machinery of it. If we print now, we print fifty copies against a garrison of four hundred and a historian whose entire profession is making inconvenient paper disappear. We lose the plates the moment the first soldier kicks down the door, and we lose them forever, because there is exactly one set.

If we move them downriver — quietly, inside something no one wants to inspect — they reach the low country presses, where they can be set and re-set and printed a thousand times over, and no raid, no fire, no clever man with a ministry seal can ever gather them back up. My father didn't hide the truth to bury it. He hid it so it would live long enough to matter.

"But," I added, because fairness demanded it, "Ferenc isn't wrong either. If we wait too long, the choice makes itself. The ice breaks, the port fills with soldiers doing spring inventory, and the plates die in a cabinet. So we need both: a way to move them, and insurance in case moving fails. Which means—" I took a breath, because this part had been forming in me since the night I pulled the press bed apart and found my father waiting inside it like a letter postmarked fifteen years late, "—which means someone should print a partial edition. Not the plates themselves. From memory. From the ledger pages we've reconstructed. Names, dates, the worst of it, set quickly and printed fast, and scattered wide enough that even if the barge never leaves, the seed is already in the ground."

Silence. The good kind, for once.

Wren spoke first, and her voice shook with something between awe and grievance. "You'd let me set it. You said you'd train me on the press. Let me do this. Please. After the broadside—" She stopped. We all remembered the broadside, and the boots on the stairs, and how close the ledger had come to a soldier's lantern.

"After the broadside," I said gently, "you've earned the chance to do it properly. Yes."

"It's a compromise," Ferenc grumbled. "Compromises are how revolutions drown."

"Compromises," Marta said, "are how revolutions learn to swim. Vote."

* * *

The vote went as such things go: hands raised in a smoky room, grudges swallowed like bad beer. Five for the plan — move the plates, print the insurance copy in parallel. Ferenc abstained, which from Ferenc was practically applause. Then came the part nobody wanted, which was the assigning.

Someone had to carry word to the bargemen on the coal wharf and negotiate passage without knowing which bargeman might sell them to Crane for a purse. Someone had to scout the route through the tannery district. Someone had to keep watch on the shop, which was watched already. And someone — this fell to me, by acclamation, before I could arrange my face into refusal — had to compose the partial edition, which meant choosing what went into it.

That was the sentence that stayed with me after the meeting broke. *Choosing.* Fifty names in the ledger. Testimony from prisoners, some of whom had died since. Atrocities documented in my father's tight, furious hand. And room, on a practical press schedule, for perhaps a quarter of it. Memory owes the living everything, but it can only pay in installments, and someone must decide the order of payment. My father had made choices like that for fifteen years, alone, in the dark, and I had spent my whole life in the shop beneath him without once wondering what it cost him.

I wondered now. Standing among the emptying mugs, I wondered so hard I missed the first part of Oskar's farewell toast.

* * *

The others filtered out — Ferenc first, slamming the door behind him with a theatricality that fooled no one, least of all himself; Marta on Oskar's arm, murmuring something that made the big man laugh low in his chest; Wren last, walking backward toward the door so she could keep looking at me, mouthing *the partial edition* like a prayer.

Tamsin remained, hood back now, watching me across the ruined map with the expression of someone who has been counting all evening — not heads, but odds.

"You did well in there," Tamsin said.

"I shouted a little."

"You mediated. There's a difference. Ferenc came in ready to burn the city down and left agreeing to a plan. In the census office we call that a successful interview."

"What do you call it when the interviewer starts forging documents for the interviewees?"

Tamsin smiled, briefly, tiredly. "Career advancement."

Outside, the wind had swung round from the south, warm and wrong for February, carrying the smell of mud and rotting ice. Through the tavern's fogged window I could see the streetlamps shivering, and beyond them, invisible but audible to anyone who had learned to listen, the river — creaking, shifting, testing its seams like a prisoner testing bars.

"One more thing," Tamsin said, and the tiredness in their face sharpened into something else. "When I walked here tonight, I passed a clerk from the historian's office coming out of the tannery district. Making notes. Asking questions about cellar space and wharf access." They hesitated. "Beatrix — Crane isn't waiting for spring. He's mapping the river district *now*. Whatever we do, we've got days. Maybe fewer than the ice gives us."

The Drowned Bell's sign creaked on its bracket outside, and beneath the floorboards, faint as a heartbeat, I could almost hear the river counting down.
