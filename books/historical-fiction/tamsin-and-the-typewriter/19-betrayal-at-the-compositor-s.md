## 19. Betrayal at the Compositor's

The knock came at half past four in the morning, which is a hour when no honest news travels.

I knew it was bad before I opened the door, because Wren was on the other side of it, and Wren never knocks. She comes through doorways the way water comes through a cracked wall — quietly, and only where there's already damage. But this morning she was standing in the lane with her fists jammed in her armpits and her chin doing that thing chins do when their owners have decided crying is a luxury they cannot currently afford.

"They took Pell," she said.

I stood aside to let her in, because you don't discuss arrests in the street, even a street as asleep as Quill Lane was. The lamps hadn't been snuffed yet. Somewhere a dog was making its opinions known about something. Otherwise the city had the particular silence of a place holding its breath.

"Tell me from the beginning," I said, once the bolt was drawn. I put the kettle on, partly for her, mostly because my hands wanted an occupation that wasn't shaking.

"There isn't a beginning. There's just — soldiers. At the Tanner's Row end of Cooper's Stair, around three o'clock. Six of them, maybe eight. They went up quiet, no drums, no shouting. That's what frightens me, Beatrix. Soldiers who are quiet already know who they've come for."

Ambrose Pell. The blind compositor. He'd sat in our shop not nine days ago with his fingers walking across a galley of type like a man reading scripture by touch, telling us about the raid on his lodging house in '39, laughing about how they'd confiscated his spectacles along with everything else, which had been a joke, since he hadn't needed them in twenty years. He'd held the circle's oldest fragment of testimony in a hand-stitched wallet under his coat and recited it to us from memory because the paper copy was too dangerous to keep anywhere at all.

And now the soldiers had come for him quietly, in the dark, by name.

"Did they say a charge?" I asked.

"Does it matter?"

It didn't. Charges were decoration. But I asked anyway, because asking questions is a way of postponing the answers you're afraid of.

* * *

By seven we had gathered in the back of the Gilded Anchor, in Doss's private room above the taproom, which smelled of tallow and old beer and, these days, of fear trying very hard to smell like nothing. Doss brought up bread and small beer nobody touched. Corvin Hale sat by the window with his chair angled so he could watch the street below — a habit he claimed came from his exile years in Marrowford, though I suspected it predated the exile and had caused it. Tamsin arrived last, in plain clothes, having taken a route that would embarrass a smuggler, and stood dripping melted snow on the floorboards looking like a clerk who had wandered into the wrong conspiracy.

Which, historically speaking, they were.

"Six men," Doss said. He was counting on his fingers, which was unnecessary, because we had all been counting all morning. "Six soldiers, no warrant read aloud, no neighbor summoned to witness. And they knew the stair entrance. Not the tavern entrance on Fetter's Lane — the stair."

"The stair isn't secret," said Hale.

"No," Doss agreed. "But it isn't obvious either. You'd have to know Pell's habits. You'd have to know he comes down the stair at dawn to buy his bread warm, because warm bread tells him which baker's window faces east." Doss's voice did something complicated on the word *east*. "Somebody told them that."

Nobody said the next sentence. We all thought it, in six different directions, and the room got very small.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is meant to be the whole point of our enterprise: my first thought was not *poor Ambrose*. My first thought was *who*, and my second was a quick, shameful inventory of everyone in that room. Hale, whose loyalty had been purchased back from Crane's clutches with forgiveness none of us were sure he'd earned. Doss, whose tavern profited handsomely from garrison custom. Even Wren, young enough to be flattered, frightened enough to be turned. And Tamsin — Tamsin most of all, if I'm laying out the full ledger of my shame — Tamsin, who until recently had worn the ministry's collar and still moved through its corridors, who could have traded us all for a pardon with a signature and a shrug.

That's what betrayal does. It doesn't break trust like a plate breaks — cleanly, all at once, into pieces you can count. It gets into the mortar. Every brick becomes a question.

"We should assume they'll come for the rest of us," Hale said. "In order of usefulness. Pell first because he knows the most names."

"He knows *my* name," Wren said suddenly. "He's known my father's whole circle since before I could read. If they—" She stopped. Chins are one thing; voices are another.

"If they question him," Tamsin said carefully, "he'll hold. Blind men hold better than sighted ones, in my experience. Nothing to write down, nothing to identify. But holding isn't the same as being safe, and Crane doesn't need him to talk. An arrest is itself a message."

"To whom?" Doss asked.

Tamsin looked at me. It was a small look, quickly done, the kind of look two people learn to exchange when they can no longer afford long conversations in company. *To us,* the look said. *To you.*

* * *

I walked home the long way, past the frozen river, because I needed to think and the river thinks better alongside you than any empty room does. The ice had gone grey-green and sullen, swollen up in ridges along the banks like a brow furrowed in sleep. Underneath it the current went on regardless, patient, indifferent to the affairs of printers and ministries. I envied it.

Here is what I kept circling back to: the raid pattern.

I laid it out in my head the way I'd lay out type, piece by piece, checking each character against the case. Two months of raids across Vellham, and I could now recite them like a litany. The bookbinder on Candlemas Wynd, seized with a crate of unbound pamphlets. The stationer's cellar off Rope Walk. The widow Quennell, whose late husband had run a jobbing press and whose son had been taken for questioning and released after four days, white as whey and unable to speak of it. Each raid small, precise, quiet. Never a sweep of a district, never the broad stupid net the garrison throws when it wants to be seen throwing it. These raids were surgical. And surgery requires anatomy.

Crane had the seized shops catalogued — Tamsin had told me that much, grimly, over our reconstruction work. But cataloguing explains the raids on *shops*. It does not explain the stair at Cooper's Stair. It does not explain knowing which fragment Pell carried or why he mattered. Someone had drawn the garrison a map of the circle itself — not its premises but its people. Its habits. Its warm bread.

And there was another thing, one I hadn't said aloud to anyone, because saying it made it more real than I could bear. The ledger pages we'd reconstructed — the missing pages, the ones taken in the original raid on my father's circle eleven years ago — Crane had those. Which meant Crane knew what the circle's testimony contained. And in recent weeks, certain details had begun appearing in the official histories he was drafting. Sanitized, inverted, repurposed — the prisoners' words put in the governor's mouth. He wasn't just erasing the record. He was *mining* it.

A man who mines a record knows exactly what remains unmined. He knows what fragments are still loose. He knows, in short, precisely whom to arrest.

Unless someone was helping him keep his list current.

* * *

Wren was waiting in the shop when I got back, sitting on the composing stone with her boots swinging, which she knew I hated, which is presumably why she did it. Grief makes teenagers ornery. I let the boots go.

"You think it's Hale," she said. Not a question.

I hung up my coat with great care, giving myself time. "I think we don't know anything."

"I saw your face at Doss's. You did your thinking face. You get a line right here"—she drew a furrow between her own brows— "and you were doing it every time Hale spoke."

"Wren."

"He confessed, didn't he? He broke once already. Everyone says so. He broke under the threat of torture before anyone even touched him, and now Crane has him by the same handle, and you'd pull that handle again, wouldn't you, if you were Crane? Why waste a new man when the old one already knows how to open?"

She said it fast and bitter, the way people say things they want to be wrong about. And the worst part — the part I've chewed on many nights since — is that her logic was sound. That's the corrosive genius of suspicion: it always sounds like reasonableness. Every step follows. It's only the destination that's unbearable.

"Hale fed Crane half-truths to protect us," I said. "He chose the harder road and it cost him."

"Or that's the story he told us, and we believed it because wanting to believe it felt nicer than the alternative." Wren's voice cracked on *nicer*. She was fourteen and carrying a dead father's cause in a bag over her shoulder, and I forgot sometimes, watching her set type with a journeyman's competence, how thin the ice was under all that competence. "My father trusted the circle. He died trusting them. If one of them sold him out—"

"Your father," I said, "trusted the circle because a circle is the only shape that holds. A line can be broken at any point. A circle has no ends." I heard my own words and recognized my father in them, and hated how comfort arrives wearing dead men's voices. "We test. Quietly, carefully, the way we discussed. We don't convict anybody on a furrow in somebody's forehead."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she took her boots off the composing stone, which was, from Wren, an apology of unusual eloquence.

* * *

Tamsin came after dark, hooded, letting themselves in through the yard with the latch-code we'd agreed on, and found me at the press pretending to mend a forme I had no intention of mending. I'd been standing at it for an hour. The forme remained unmended. Mendlessness is a kind of prayer.

"Tamsin." I kept my voice level. Practice. "Tell me about the arrest order."

They unwound their scarf slowly. I watched their face in the lamplight, and I watched myself watching, and I noted with clinical disgust that I was doing it — running the inventory again, even here, even on them. *Who.* The word had gotten into my mortar and I couldn't dig it out.

"It came through the provost's office," Tamsin said, "not through census channels. Which is itself information — it means Crane bypassed the usual requisition process, which means he didn't want the order visible to clerks. To people like me."

"Or he no longer trusts people like you."

The pause was half a heartbeat too long. "He has reason not to trust anybody," Tamsin said. "That's the nature of his work. Erasure is lonely employment." They crossed to the window, checked the lane out of habit. "Beatrix. There's something else. I pulled the duty rosters — I still have friends in the registry who owe me small favors, and I called one in. The detail that took Pell was drawn from the garrison, yes. But the intelligence summary they acted on — the one naming him, describing the stair, the dawn bread — it originated somewhere else entirely. It came in as a citizen deposition. Unsigned."

"Unsigned depositions get ignored."

"These didn't. This one was marked *verified* — by the ministry historian's office. Crane verified it personally." Tamsin turned from the window. "Which means either Crane manufactured it himself, from his files, and dressed it up as a tip — or someone gave it to him and he laundered it to protect his source."

"And if the source is inside the circle, laundering protects them from *us* finding out."

"Yes."

I sat down on the composing stone myself then, boots and all, because my knees had developed opinions. The lamp hissed. Somewhere out in the lane the snow was coming down again, soft as erasure.

"So we can't know," I said. "If Crane forged it, chasing the source tears the circle apart for nothing. If the source is real, chasing nothing leaves him free to feed Crane the rest of us, one dawn at a time." I laughed, and it came out wrong, tinny. "Do you know what the truly obscene thing is? He's built it so that suspicion itself does his work. He doesn't need to catch us. He only needs us to start looking at each other. Trust is load-bearing, Tamsin. Take out one brick and the whole arch comes down on our own heads."

Tamsin crouched in front of me, which they almost never did, and took both my hands — ink-stained, trembling, guilty of thoughts I hadn't confessed. Their cuffs were stained, as ever. Some things stay constant while the world burns.

"Then we don't take out bricks," they said. "We test the mortar instead. Quietly. One person at a time. Starting with Hale, because he's the likeliest and because if it *is* him, better we find out gently than the garrison finds out loudly. And Beatrix — listen to me. Whoever it is, whatever it turns out to be — it won't be what your fear is drawing right now. Fear drafts badly. It inks everything the same weight. We'll read this page properly. Together. Like we did the ledger."

I wanted to believe them. I noticed, with the same clinical disgust as before, that I also inventoried that wanting. Was my relief evidence of trust, or evidence of a fool's excellent instincts for self-deception?

"First thing tomorrow," I said. "Before the city wakes. And Tamsin—" I gripped their hands harder. "Pell is in the Stonecells by now. Whatever Crane wants from him, he'll begin extracting it soon. We may not have weeks. We may not have days."

Outside, far off toward the river, the ice groaned — a long, deep complaint, like some enormous animal turning in its sleep. We both went still and listened. It was only February being February. It was only winter shifting its weight.

Probably.

But I thought of Ambrose Pell reading type by touch in our lamplight, and of the unsigned deposition sleeping in Crane's files, and of the six bricks of our circle, and I understood that the sound I'd heard wasn't only the river. Something else was cracking too. The only question — the question that would keep me awake until dawn and well past it — was which of us had handed Crane the wedge.
