## 20. Suspicions

There is a particular arithmetic to betrayal, and I learned it the week after Josiah Quill was taken: one arrest, one confession overheard or coerced, and every friendship in Vellham becomes a sum that won't balance.

I had always thought of suspicion as a coarse thing — the province of magistrates and jealous husbands. I was wrong. It is a fine instrument. It slips into the seams of everything. It sits with you at breakfast and weighs your bread. It reads over your shoulder while you set type, and it whispers, *that man held your father's forme in his hands; how well did you ever really know him?*

Tamsin arrived at the shop on a grey Tuesday with snow in their collar and a look I had come to dread — the look of a clerk who has found an error in a column and cannot unsee it.

"We need to talk about Garrick Voss," they said.

* * *

Let me name the circle, because naming them was, in those days, an act of faith, and faith needs its litany.

There was Mother Anselm — not a mother at all, but a tavern-keeper on Cooper's Lane with forearms like dock rope and a memory that held forty years of grievances the way her cellar held casks. There was Elias Fenwick, the blind compositor, who could read type by touch better than most men read it by eye, and who had set half the pamphlets of '09 without ever seeing them. There was Wren, seventeen, all elbows and urgency, currently under my reluctant tutelage and chafing at it. And there was Garrick Voss, the exiled foreman, who had run the pressroom at Harrow & Sons before the governor's men closed it, and who had been banished from the print trade entirely — forbidden, on pain of imprisonment, to so much as *touch* a composing stick.

Voss had been my father's foreman for eleven years. He had wept at the funeral. He had also, Tamsin now told me, been seen three times in the fortnight since Quill's arrest walking past the Ministry annex on Candler Street, and once coming out of it.

"Coming out of it," I repeated.

"Coming out of it," Tamsin said. "At dusk. Alone."

"Perhaps he was lost."

"Beatrix." They took off their coat and sat down across from me at the stone sink where I washed the ink balls, which meant this was going to be long. "Nobody is lost on Candler Street. It's four buildings long and shaped like a coffin."

I kept washing. The water had gone grey. "You're a census clerk," I said. "Since when do you surveil people?"

"Since I started misfiling entries for you, I suppose. It seemed only fair to keep my own records current." There it was — that dry, self-deprecating flicker Tamsin used like a screen door: you could see through it, but it still kept the flies out. Then the flicker went away. "Quill didn't talk his way into a cell. Someone gave Crane the pattern. Three safe houses, two couriers, and the compositor's lodging house, all inside nine days. That's not luck. That's a map drawn by someone who knew where everything was."

"And you think Voss drew it."

"I think someone did. And Voss is the one with the grudge, the debts, and the evening walks."

I dried my hands. My heart was doing something unhelpful and loud. "He carried my father's coffin," I said. "One of six."

"People carry coffins for all sorts of reasons. Guilt is load-bearing."

* * *

We agreed — and I use the word *agreed* loosely, because what actually happened was that Tamsin argued for caution and I argued for certainty and we ended up somewhere between, which is where all their bargains seem to land — that we would test each member of the circle quietly, without accusation, without telling Wren, and above all without telling anyone we were testing them.

"It's like proofing a page," Tamsin said, and I hated how well the metaphor fit. "You don't accuse the type of being wrong. You pull a proof and see what shows."

So we pulled our proofs.

Mother Anselm first. I went to the tavern on a Thursday night, when the room was loud enough to swallow conversation, and I let slip — carefully, artfully, feeling like a forger of my own friendships — that the plates would move on Saturday. Not true. The plates were in the tannery cellar and would stay there until the ice spoke. If Anselm was the leak, Saturday would bring soldiers to Tanner's Row.

Saturday brought nothing but sleet and a drunk fiddler. Anselm passed.

Elias next. This was harder, because testing a blind man means lying to his hands, and his hands know more than most faces. I brought him a forme to check — a harmless broadside, nothing seditious — and while his fingers walked the type I asked him, casually, whether he'd heard anything from Voss lately. His fingers stopped.

"Garrick came to me last week," he said. "Asked if I remembered the old signals from the Harrow days. The chalk marks. Which windows meant safe, which meant watched."

My mouth went dry. "And did you tell him?"

"I told him the signals died with your father. Which is true." He turned his blind face toward me, and I had the peculiar sensation of being *read*. "Why are you asking, Bea?"

"No reason. Curiosity."

"Hm." He resumed his walking fingers. "Curiosity hanged more men in this city than treason did."

That night I lay awake doing the corrosive arithmetic. Voss asking after signals. Voss outside the Ministry annex. Voss, Voss, Voss — the name fitting the shape of the hole in our security the way a piece of type fits its quoin, snugly, inevitably.

But there was another voice in me, quieter and more stubborn, and it sounded disconcertingly like my father. *Evidence,* he used to say, tapping a galley, *is what the reader brings to the page. Meaning is what the writer buried in it.* He meant it about typography. He might as well have meant it about Aldous Crane.

Because here was the thing that nagged at me like a reversed letter in an otherwise clean line: Crane was not a stupid man. A stupid man burns pamphlets. Crane curated them. He understood that a conspiracy dies fastest not when you arrest it but when you make it arrest itself — when you feed it one suspicious fact, well-placed, and let suspicion do the soldier's work. Every hour we spent watching Voss was an hour we didn't spend moving the archive. Every bond of trust we snapped was a hand withdrawn from the work.

Or perhaps that was exactly what a guilty man's defender would think. You see the problem. Suspicion is a press that prints both sides of the sheet.

* * *

Tamsin, meanwhile, worked the paper trail, because Tamsin could find a story in a ledger the way other people found faces in firelight.

The Ministry kept visitor books. This was not secret; it was vanity. Crane liked a record of who came to consult the historical record, because it flattered the record. Tamsin, whose misfiling skills were by now frankly professional-grade, arranged to reshelve a decade of correspondence in the annex and spent an afternoon with the visitor books for the months since the uprising began.

They came to the shop that evening smelling of cold candlewax and government dust, and they laid it out on my worktable with clerical precision.

"Fourteen visits to Crane's office by persons connected to the print trade," they said. "Nine were routine — licensed printers summoned about their registrations. Two were Harrow & Sons' lawyer. One was a woman I couldn't identify. And two—" they tapped the page "—were signed G. Voss."

"When?"

"The day after Quill was arrested. And again four days later."

I stared at the entry. The ink was official blue, the hand confident. *G. Voss, foreman (ret.), re: testimony.*

"There," I said. "It's there in ink."

"Yes," Tamsin said slowly. "In ink. In a book kept by the man we believe is manufacturing history." They looked up. "Ask yourself what a forger does best, Beatrix."

I didn't want to ask myself. Asking myself was the whole disease. But the question sat there anyway, patient as lead.

"He writes," I said finally. "In a convincing hand."

"Mm. And who taught half this city's clerks their copperplate? Who ran the apprenticeship program at Harrow & Sons that fed the Ministry its copyists?" Tamsin's voice was very quiet. "Garrick Voss taught penmanship to a generation of government clerks. Including, incidentally, the clerk whose register book this is. Crane wouldn't even need to forge it himself. He'd just need to suggest to a frightened junior man that a certain visit ought to be recorded, for completeness."

"So either Voss betrayed us twice," I said, "or Crane forged evidence that Voss betrayed us once, knowing we'd find it, knowing we'd tear ourselves apart looking at it." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Do you know what the worst part is? Both stories are perfectly typeset. They both justify. I can't break the line."

"No," Tamsin said. "You can't. Not from in here." They folded the notes away. "Which is why there's only one test left that means anything."

"Which is?"

"You talk to him."

* * *

I want to record, for whoever inherits these pages, that I did not want to do it. I record it anyway, because the record is the point, and a record that omits cowardice is just another kind of forgery.

Voss lived in a rented room above a chandlery on Rope Walk, in the kind of exile the Ministry specializes in: not prison, merely irrelevance. He answered the door in shirtsleeves, older than I remembered, his forearms still printer's forearms — scarred where hot metal had kissed them over thirty years — and when he saw my face he went pale in a way that no innocent man should be able to manage and no guilty man could fake. I have thought about that pallor for many nights since. I still don't know which it was.

"Beatrix Eastwick," he said. "God. Come in before the street sees you."

The room was poor and clean. On the table, a single candle, a loaf, a knife. And beside them — my stomach dropped — a sheet of paper covered in practice strokes of copperplate, the loops and flourishes of a man keeping his finest skill alive in exile, the way a dancer keeps her ankles.

He saw me looking. "Habit," he said. "You spend your life teaching boys to write their names beautifully and then they take the skill to the Ministry and you're left writing yours in the dark." He almost smiled. "What do you want, Bea? Nobody visits me. Visiting me is expensive."

"Is it?" I said. "How much does the Ministry charge?"

The silence that followed had weight, like a forme locked tight. Voss set down the knife he had been holding — I hadn't registered that he was holding it until he wasn't — and sat down heavily on the bed.

"So it's come to that," he said. "Quill's arrested, and now the daughter comes with questions instead of the soldiers. That's efficient of them. Or of me, depending on which of us you've decided I am."

"I haven't decided anything."

"Liar." He said it without heat. "You've decided something or you wouldn't be here alone, without your census friend, without the girl. You came to weigh me." He looked at his hands. "All right. Weigh me. Ask."

I asked. Where were you on the night of the twelfth. Why were you outside the annex. Why did you ask Elias about the signals. Why does the visitor book carry your name twice.

And he answered — every question, plainly, wearily, in the flat tone of a man reciting a colophon he's said a thousand times. He had gone to the annex, he said, to offer himself. To tell Crane he knew nothing and would swear to it publicly, hoping to buy himself out of suspicion and, less nobly, out of terror. Crane had received him for eleven minutes, offered him money for the location of the plates, and shown him the door. He had asked Elias about the signals because he wanted, if the circle ever needed a bolt-hole, to know whether the old routes still lived — and because he was frightened, and frightened men check the locks twice. The visitor book entries were real. The rest, he could not prove, because exiles have no witnesses; nobody watches a man who no longer matters.

"Why should I believe you?" I said, when he finished.

"You shouldn't," he said simply. "Belief isn't mine to give you. I can only tell you what I know." He leaned forward, and his eyes — my father's foreman's eyes, eyes that had watched over every forme my father ever locked — were wet and level. "But I'll tell you one thing more, and you can weigh it however you like. When Crane offered me money, he named a figure. Forty crowns. And he said, *for the location of the master plates.*"

I kept my face still. Inside, something turned over.

Nobody outside the circle knew the word *plates*. We said *the ledger*, we said *the archive*, we said *the account*. The word *plates* existed in exactly two places: in the hidden compartment of my father's press bed, and in the mouths of the five of us who knew it was there.

Crane had used the right word. Which meant either Voss had already sold him the truth and this entire performance was theater —

— or Crane wanted me to think so, and had slipped the word deliberately, knowing Voss would repeat it, knowing I would hear it and feel the floor tilt, knowing that a poisoned gift arrives wrapped in exactly this much plausibility.

"That's a strange detail," I managed.

"It's the detail that's been keeping me up," Voss said. "Because I've spent two weeks wondering the same thing you're wondering. Whether he said it because he knows, or said it so that you'd think he knows." He laughed, short and bitter. "Your father would have known how to read it. He used to say a forged page always tells you it's forged, somewhere, if you know the compositor's habits. But I never learned to read *people* that way. Only type."

I stood to go. At the door I paused, and I did the thing that the quiet stubborn voice in me insisted on, against all the fine sharp arithmetic of suspicion.

"Garrick," I said. "When the plates move — and they will move — I'm going to need a decoy. Someone the Ministry already believes is theirs. Someone they'll follow instead of the barge."

He looked at me for a long moment. Whatever moved behind his face, he let me see all of it, which is more than any innocent man can safely do and more than any guilty man would dare.

"I'm too old to run," he said. "But I'm not too old to be followed."

* * *

Walking home through the snow, I tried to explain to Tamsin — who met me at the corner of Rope Walk with their collar up and their breath smoking — why I had done what I'd done, and I found I couldn't, not fully, not in the language of evidence.

"You're gambling," Tamsin said.

"I'm choosing," I said. "There's a difference. Evidence ran out three streets back. Everything past that point is a decision about what kind of person I'm going to be. Crane wants a circle that devours itself. He's built the machinery for it — the visitor book, the word *plates*, the timing, all of it machined to fit. And the only way to beat a machine like that isn't to find the flaw in it. It's to refuse to operate it."

"That's very noble and possibly fatal."

"Most true things are both."

Tamsin was quiet for half a block. Snow ticked against shutters. Somewhere down toward the river, the ice groaned — a long, deep note, like a bell struck underwater, the sound that had become the city's heartbeat, counting down.

"For what it's worth," Tamsin said at last, "I checked one more thing today. The junior clerk who keeps the visitor book. I bought him a drink. He's terrified of Crane, he drinks too much, and after two glasses he told me that Crane personally instructed him on certain entries to be made 'for completeness.'" They glanced at me sideways. "He wouldn't say which entries. But he cried into his beer, and guilty conspirators generally cry *after* the money, not before it."

"So Voss is clean."

"So Voss is probably clean. Probably." Tamsin smiled thinly. "Welcome to my profession. Everything is probably."

We reached the shop. And that was when we saw it — the thing that ended the argument about probabilities forever.

The door of Eastwick's stood ajar. Not broken, not forced. Opened with a key.

Inside, the lamp I had left trimmed and low was burning high. And sitting at my worktable, calm as a creditor, turning one of my father's type cases slowly in his white hands, was Aldous Crane.

"Miss Eastwick," he said pleasantly. "Come in. I believe we have a discrepancy in our inventories."
