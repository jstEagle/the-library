## 21. The Mole Confesses

I want it recorded — somewhere, in some honest ledger if one exists — that when Aldous Crane sat in my shop turning my father's type cases like a man appraising silverware, I did not faint, weep, or confess. I made tea.

It was Tamsin who taught me that, though they'd deny it. When you cannot fight and cannot flee, make tea. It fills your hands, slows the room down, and makes the other man wait on *your* schedule. Crane waited. He was good at waiting. That was the whole horror of him.

"There is," he said, when the cup was in front of him and untouched, "a discrepancy. The inventory of this establishment lists four hundredweight of foundry type. My men weighed what they could see through the window last night. There appears to be rather less lead in this room than paper."

"Paper's heavier than people think," I said.

He smiled. He had a smile like a drawer closing. "Miss Eastwick, I am not a cruel man. I am a thorough one, which cruel men always mistake for cruelty because it produces the same paperwork. I have no interest in you. I have interest in a set of plates that were removed from a press bed in this shop — or so a source of mine believes — and I will find them whether you help me or watch me do it."

"And if I knew nothing about any plates?"

"Then you would be an innocent woman sitting across from a tedious official, drinking bad tea, and we would both be wasting a morning." He set down the cup, rose, straightened his coat with two precise tugs, and left the type case exactly where he had found it — but rotated, I noticed afterward. Rotated so that the compartments faced the door. So he could look at it again from the street.

Tamsin came out of the back room where they'd stood with a hand on Wren's shoulder to keep the girl still. We looked at each other for a long moment over the cooling tea.

"He knows," Tamsin said quietly.

"He suspects. There's a difference."

"Not anymore there isn't. Not with him." They pulled their coat on. "Beatrix. Someone told him about the press bed."

* * *

We spent two days proving what we already knew. Tamsin went back through the seized-shop inventories, cross-checking dates against the raids, and I did what I do best, which is sit very still and think about metal.

The pattern was ugly in its neatness. Every location the circle had touched — Pell's lodging, the Anchor & Line, the old tannery storeroom we'd considered and rejected — had been visited within days of being discussed at a meeting. Not searched. *Visited*. Crane's men came, looked, asked polite questions, and left. As if following a list written by someone who had been in the room.

Everyone at those meetings had been vetted by grief. Everyone had lost something to the governor's regime. But grief, I learned, does not make a man loyal. Sometimes it just gives him something worse to lose a second time.

"It's Fenn," Wren said flatly. She was sweeping type dust into the pan with more force than the task required. "Ambrose Fenn. The foreman. I've thought so since the Anchor & Line meeting — he kept asking where things would be stored. Nobody else asked. Everybody else was careful not to ask."

"Fenn carried your father's coffin," I said. "Fenn taught half the compositors in this city their craft. Fenn was broken out of Marsh Gate prison with a lung full of stone dust and a confession beaten out of—"

"You don't know that," she said. "You're doing what everyone does with him. You're weighing what he was against what he might be now, and the scales are rigged."

She was right, and I hated her a little for it, the way you hate anyone who hands you a truth before you've finished dressing it up.

* * *

We didn't go to Fenn with accusations. We went to him with work.

His rooms above the cooperage smelled of camphor and boiled linen, and he received us propped in a chair by the window with a blanket over his ruined chest, hands folded like a man at his own funeral who had decided to enjoy the flowers. His eyes had gone milky at the edges — Marsh Gate's gift, along with the cough — but he turned his face toward the door when we entered as if the light itself had announced us.

"Eastwick's girl," he said. "And the census clerk. And footsteps I don't know — small, quick, angry. Wren."

"You can't possibly know that from footsteps," Wren said.

"I know it from the way the floorboards flinch." He gestured us to stools. "Well? You've come all this way to ask me something, and nobody offers an old man brandy first, so it must be ugly. Ask."

I had rehearsed a dozen openings on the walk over. In the end I used none of them.

"The Anchor and Line," I said. "The twelfth. You asked where the second ledger would be kept."

Silence. The cooperage clattered below us — hoops being driven, a sound like distant artillery, and I thought absurdly of the river ice.

"Yes," Fenn said.

Not *what do you mean*. Not *how dare you*. Just *yes*, quiet as a closing book, and I felt the floor tilt under me the way it tilts when a press slips its quoins.

"They took me in October," he went on, to the window, since he could not look at us and we could not bear to be looked at anyway. "Two men and Captain Ressler's clerk. They didn't beat me. I want that understood — they never laid a finger on me. They put me in a cell next to a boy of nineteen, a typesetter's devil from Corben Street, and every day they came and took him out, and every day he came back worse, and every night through the wall he cried for his mother in a voice I could not stop hearing. On the ninth day they told me the boy would be hanged for sedition unless someone gave them the circle. Not names — they had names. Locations. Where we met. Where papers moved. And they gave me until morning."

"And you gave them locations," Tamsin said. Not accusing. Recording. That was their gift, I was beginning to understand — they could say the worst thing in the world in the tone of a man entering figures in a column.

"I gave them three," Fenn said. "Three places, and every one of them empty by the time they knocked, because I chose ones I believed already burned or abandoned. The loft on Chandler's Row — cleared out weeks before. The dead drop behind the chandler's — retired since summer. And—" his hands tightened on the blanket "—and I told them the plates were hidden in a press bed. I said it because it was true enough to survive checking and vague enough to be useless. There are forty press beds in Vellham. But then your father died, and the plates stayed where they were, and everything I said became true in the worst possible way."

The hoop-driver below fell silent. In the quiet I heard Wren breathing fast through her nose, and Tamsin not breathing at all.

"Why come to us now?" I asked. "Why confess?"

"Because Crane sat in your shop two days ago." My face must have done something. He smiled without humor. "Word travels faster than shame, girl. A neighbor saw his coach. And I know what it means when Aldous Crane visits a print shop and leaves without arresting anyone. It means he's certain, and he's savoring it. He doesn't pounce until the trap is dressed." He turned his clouded eyes toward me with terrible precision. "So either you move the plates tonight, or you use me. Those are the only two mercies left in this room."

* * *

We argued on the stairs going down, in whispers, while the cooperage hammered away below like a conscience.

"We use nothing of his," Wren said. "He sold locations once. What stops him selling the barge plan, the Tanner's Row cellar, all of it?"

"What stops him is that Crane already owns whatever Fenn knows," Tamsin said. "That's the arithmetic. If Fenn were feeding Crane fresh information, we'd have been taken apart weeks ago. Everything Crane has acted on, Fenn gave him months ago, and it was stale on delivery. A spy who's stopped spying isn't an asset. He's a liability Crane will eventually tidy up." They paused on the landing. "Which means Fenn has one thing left to sell, and it isn't to Crane. It's his own usefulness. To us."

"That's not forgiveness," Wren said. "That's accounting."

"Everything worth having is accounting," Tamsin said, "with the heart added at the bottom of the column."

I thought about that sentence for a long time after. About my father, who had trusted Fenn with the circle, with the plates' existence, with everything except the hiding place — because even in trust, my father kerned his confidences, left gaps, made every secret readable only by the person meant to read it. Had my father suspected Fenn would break? No. Had he planned for the possibility? I was beginning to believe he planned for everything.

And I thought about what forgiveness actually was, stripped of the sermon. Not a feeling. Feelings were unreliable as cheap ink. Forgiveness, the kind that held weight, was a decision about what to do with a broken tool: throw it out, or grind a new edge on it and hold it carefully by the unbroken part. Fenn was broken. But broken things still bearing weight — that was most of the world. That was me, some mornings. That was certainly Tamsin, carrying a ministry badge and a burned conscience in the same coat pocket.

We went back up.

* * *

Fenn had poured himself brandy while we were gone, or perhaps he'd had it all along, waiting for the verdict the way men wait at trials. He lifted the glass toward the sound of our return.

"Well?" he said. "Do I hang in your ledger too?"

"No," I said. "But you're going to earn your way out of it, and it won't be pleasant."

I laid it out, and as I spoke I watched him assemble the shape of it from my voice alone — the way blind men do, reading the architecture of a plan off the air. Crane was watching the shop. Crane expected the plates to run before the ice broke. Crane had, somewhere in his files, an informant inside Eastwick's circle whose reports had gone quiet, and a quiet informant is a thread he'll pull eventually — or better, a thread he'll leave hanging, hoping we'll tug it ourselves.

"So you tug it," Fenn said slowly. "You let him see me moving."

"You're going to be seen," I said. "Badly. Clumsily. An old man asking questions at the wharves about coal barges leaving before the seal lifts. Asking about the river road south. You'll visit the Anchor and Line twice, and the second time you'll be followed, and you'll lose your tail in the fish market because you'll genuinely get lost — your eyes aren't what they were, and we'll make sure the market's crowded."

"And Crane concludes?"

"That his old informant is back in play, and panicking, and trying to buy passage off the sinking ship. Which means every resource he spends watching you is a resource not watching us. And anything you tell him—" I took a breath, because this was the hinge, the whole terrible beautiful hinge "—you tell him the truth, Fenn. All of it. That the circle plans to move the archive downriver before break-up. That there's a safe house on Tanner's Row. That a barge has been arranged. Let him verify every word."

Wren made a sound of pure protest. Even Tamsin shifted.

"Because it will all be true," I finished, "and all of it will be wrong by the time he acts on it. The Tanner's Row cellar empties tomorrow — we move the archive to the third place, the one only four of us know. The barge arrangement dissolves; we take a different boat, different watermen, different night. He'll have a complete picture of a plan that existed for exactly as long as it took you to describe it. You won't be lying to him. You couldn't lie to that man and live — he'd smell it, and he'd know you'd turned, and then your value dies. You'll be his informant, faithfully reporting a conspiracy that keeps politely dissolving ahead of him. Half-truths saved you in Marsh Gate. Now we make them a weapon."

The silence stretched. Below, the coopers resumed hammering, hoop after hoop, iron singing around wood.

"It's clever," Fenn said at last, and there was something awful in how much he admired it. "Your father would have hated it and built it anyway. He hated using people. He'd have done it, mind — he'd have hated it neatly, the way he set a page, margins exact." He set down the glass. "There's a condition."

"Name it."

"When this is done — if it's done — you put me in the record. The true account. Whatever it costs me. Don't soften it. Write that Ambrose Fenn broke, and write what broke him, and write that the boy from Corben Street was released unharmed because they got what they wanted from me — because he *was*, I paid for that knowledge ever since, and no one in this city knows it. Write the whole weight of it." His voice cracked on the last words, an old beam giving. "A man should be remembered at his full measure. Not the heroic inch of him. All of it."

I looked at him — at the shaking hands, the milky eyes, the blanket over the chest that Marsh Gate had filled with stone dust — and I understood that I was not looking at a traitor. I was looking at what the regime made of loyalty when it got hold of it: not absence of courage, but courage with a boy crying through the wall. The victors' histories would record Fenn as an informer, one line, no context, ink dried. My father's account would record him whole or not at all.

"All of it," I promised. "In type."

"Then I'm yours." He reached out and found my wrist with startling accuracy, his grip dry and strong as a clamp. "One more thing, Eastwick's girl. Crane will test me early — some small errand, some checkable fact. Feed me one true detail you can afford to burn. Something recent. He'll verify it, it will check, and after that he'll believe the channel is open. Give me that coin to spend."

I glanced at Tamsin. Tamsin, after a moment, nodded slowly — and then went pale in a way that had nothing to do with Fenn at all.

"What?" I said.

"The warehouse," Tamsin said. "For the fire diversion. The one on Coal Quay that Wren is meant to light." They swallowed. "If Fenn is going to be seen asking about barges and wharf traffic — if Crane starts connecting an informant's reports to actual events — then the fire can't happen anywhere near anything Fenn touches. We need to rework it. Tonight. Because Beatrix—" and here Tamsin smiled the thinnest smile I had ever seen on them, "—there's one more thing your new decoy doesn't know. When I was summoned before Crane, he showed me a map of the garrison's surveillance posts. And the cooperage below this room is marked. Fenn has been watched since October. Which means everything we just agreed to in this room, we may have just performed for an audience."

Below us, the hammering stopped.
