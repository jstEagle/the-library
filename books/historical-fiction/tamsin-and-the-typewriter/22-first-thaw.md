## 22. First Thaw

The wind changed on a Tuesday. I know this because Tuesdays were the day I cleaned the ink stones, and I had my hands in the trough when it came through the broken pane above the door — warm, smelling of mud and something almost green — and I stopped what I was doing and stood there like a fool with black fingers raised, letting it move through the shop.

Warm wind in Vellham in late winter is not a blessing. It is an alarm.

My father used to say the river kept better time than any clockmaker: you could argue about politics, about prices, about whose God was angrier, but when the ice began to groan, everyone in the city understood that the argument was ending. The barges would come. The port would open. Whatever you meant to do before the world started moving again, you had perhaps a week to do it.

I went to the window. Down at the quay, the ice still held, white and grey and pocked like old bread, but out toward the middle there was a seam of dark water opening along the channel where the current ran strongest, a black thread being pulled through white cloth. And beneath everything, faint but unmistakable, came a sound I hadn't heard since childhood — a long, deep creak, like a house settling, except the house was a mile wide.

"Beatrix." Wren's voice from the back room, sharp with the particular panic of an apprentice who has just done something irreversible. "The forme. I think I've — come look at this before I make it worse."

I made it worse first, of course. That is tradition. But we'll get to that.

* * *

We had been meeting in the shop after closing for six days now — the whole strange family of us: Wren with her ink-burned knuckles; Tammas Vole from the Drowned Rat, who smelled permanently of pipe smoke and grievance; old Sera Quill, the blind compositor, who could read a galley by touch faster than I could read it by eye and who never let anyone forget it; Marten Hale, the exiled foreman, who sat apart from the others and said little and was watched by all of them, though after his confession no one had yet found the courage to say *forgiven* aloud; and Tamsin, who arrived last each evening, in plain clothes now rather than census grey, and who had begun — I noticed it without wanting to notice it — leaving their satchel under the counter as if the shop were a place one kept things.

They stood around the press while I told them what the river had told me.

"A week," Tammas said. "Maybe less if the wind holds warm."

"Then we stop arguing about whether and start arguing about how," I said. "Barges don't leave Vellham empty. Someone on the coal run owes you, Tammas?"

"Owes me and hates me," Tammas said, which in my experience was the more reliable debt. "Brannock of the *Kestrel*. He'll want payment."

"We have the rest of the circle's fund," Wren said. "Forty-one crowns and some loose silver."

"That's not enough for silence plus cargo."

"It's enough for silence plus *coal*," Sera said mildly, her hands moving over the type case she was reorganizing by feel, "if the coal happens to have very heavy paper wrapped around some of it."

There was a pause while everyone pretended not to be impressed. Sera lived for those pauses.

"The greater problem isn't the river," Marten said, and even now, even having earned back a sliver of trust, his voice had the flatness of a man reading his own indictment. "It's the port. Crane won't let the season open clean. A thaw means movement, movement means inspection. He'll seal the quay the moment the first floe goes downstream — mark me. We need to be aboard before the ice breaks, not after."

"Sooner, then," I said. "Days, not a week."

"And between now and then?" Tammas asked, looking around the lamplit shop at the ledger hidden in its false bottom, the plates buried in the press bed upstairs in the loft where I slept, the fifty copies' worth of type not yet set. "Between now and then, someone has to actually *make* the thing we're risking all this to move."

Everyone looked at me.

I had been dreading this moment for a week, and here it was arriving the way dread always arrives — punctually, wearing work clothes.

"Wren sets," I said. "Wren prints. I'll compose the master pages myself, but the pull, the make-ready, the presswork — that's hers now. She has to be able to do it alone."

Wren went very still, the way young creatures do when they sense either a trap or a gift and can't tell which. "Alone?"

"If something happens to me — and given the current state of our fortunes, 'something' is doing optimistic work — the press has to keep speaking. That's the whole point of everything your father's circle built and everything mine built before him. It was never meant to live in one pair of hands."

"In two pairs," Wren said quietly, looking at her own.

"In yours now. Come here. Show me your forme and we'll see how much unmaking you've done."

* * *

What she had done was classic, and I confess it warmed me even as I winced: she had tightened the quoins unevenly, left side hard, right side barely finger-tight, because she'd worked clockwise around the chase and grown bolder — or more tired — as she went. Any apprentice makes this mistake. My father had made it. I had made it, and he'd told me the story of his making it, which is how traditions survive in a trade: as an inheritance of errors.

"You've built a forme that will print beautifully for forty impressions and then walk off its feet," I said. "Tighten in opposition. Left, then right. Hard, then matching-hard. The type doesn't want to be squeezed, it wants to be *held*. There's a difference, and your hands need to learn it before your head does."

She redid it. Better. Not right.

"Again."

"Again? It's—"

"Again. You're printing the true history of a city's suffering. If the forme loosens on impression thirty, page thirty is a smudge, and somewhere in thirty years a scholar squints at your smudge and writes that the account is unreliable. Every impression has to be as true as the first. That's the discipline. Anyone can be accurate once."

Something moved across her face — not resentment. Recognition. She'd heard this speech before, I realized, or its ghost. From her father, maybe, or from mine through him. Her father had apprenticed in this shop, after all, back when the circle was young and my father still laughed easily. Somewhere in Wren's muscle memory was a version of my father's voice.

"Again," she said herself, this time, and did it right.

I showed her make-ready next — the patient crime-scene work of pulling a proof, studying where the ink sat too heavy or too thin, pasting tiny slips of paper onto the low letters until the whole surface of the forme agreed to meet the paper as one voice instead of twenty arguments. This was the part of the trade I loved most, and I hadn't known how much until I watched her do it badly and felt my chest ache with the effort of not simply taking over.

"Your father," she said, on the fourth attempt, her eyes down, her paste brush trembling slightly, "did he teach you like this? Making you do everything four times?"

"He taught me like this. Then he died before I was half finished learning, and I spent three years teaching myself the rest, badly, alone, talking to a dead man in an empty shop." I heard my own tone and was surprised by it — not bitter, exactly. Factual, the way frost is factual. "So yes. Four times. Because the alternative to my patience is your solitude, and I wouldn't wish my solitude on the governor himself."

Wren looked up at that. For a moment neither of us printed anything.

"Show me the packing," she said finally, gruffly, which I have since learned is how she says *thank you*.

* * *

By the time we'd pulled a clean proof — a test page, nothing seditious, just a passage of type specimen my father had kept for training hands — the light through the windows had gone the colour of weak tea, and the others had drifted back in from their errands. Tammas brought news; Tammas always brought news, delivered in the tone of a person reading a menu they disliked.

"Brannock will take us aboard the night the ice goes, not before. Says a loaded barge sitting at the quay during the break is a confession. But—" they held up a hand before anyone cheered, "—he says the harbourmaster has posted new regulations. All outbound cargo manifests to be reviewed by the Office of Historical Record before departure. Reviewed *personally*, they say. Guess whose office."

"Crane," Wren spat.

"Crane," Tammas confirmed. "He's turning the thaw itself into a checkpoint. Every barrel, every coffin, every bale of rags gets a clerk's eye before it touches water. Which means our coal with its unusually literary wrapping needs paperwork so dull that no historian alive would read past the second line."

"I'm good at dull paperwork," Sera said serenely, sorting type she could not see into an order only she could perceive.

"You are terrifyingly good at dull paperwork."

"Then it's settled," Marten said, rising. "Manifests by Sera. Barge by Tammas. Plates packed and ready within four days. And the printing—"

"The printing finishes tonight, tomorrow, whenever the formes are ready," I said. "Fifty copies. No more. Fifty tells the story; five hundred tells the garrison where we sleep."

Marten nodded and paused at the door, and I saw him wrestle with something. "Eastwick," he said — not Beatrix, Eastwick, and I understood he was giving my father's name back to me like a borrowed coat, "your father once told me the press was the most honest witness he knew. It can't lie about what's set in it. It just says what's there, over and over, until somebody listens." He cleared his throat, embarrassed by his own eloquence. "Make sure what's set in it is worth saying fifty times."

He left. One by one they all left, until it was Wren and me and the creaking river and the lamp.

* * *

We worked. God, how we worked. I taught her the lockup, the tympan, the frisket — the vocabulary of the machine, because my father believed a printer who couldn't name a part would someday fear it, and fear made sloppy hands. I taught her to listen to the press the way Sera listened to type: the healthy knock of a well-oiled carriage, the wrong note of a bearing going dry, the whisper of paper seating true against the points.

And she taught me things too, though I didn't realize it until later. She asked questions I'd stopped asking. Why the platen was iron and not brass. Who decided which words got italicized. Whether I was afraid. She asked it plainly, the way children and apprentices ask terrible questions — *are you afraid* — and I gave her the honest answer, because lying to her at the press would have felt like a kind of blasphemy.

"Yes. Every day since the ledger came out of hiding. Fear is fine. Fear is the trade's oldest apprentice. You just don't let it set the type."

She thought about that while she inked the rollers, and the sound the brayers made — that soft rhythmic tack-tack against the stone — filled the shop like breathing.

"My father used to say the opposite," she offered. "He said courage was just fear with good posture."

"Your father," I said, "was a better phrasemaker than mine. Mine said things like *mind your kerns* and *don't lean on the press, it's not a table*."

"But you loved him."

It wasn't really a question, but I answered it anyway, because the wind was warm and the river was groaning and life felt briefly too short for evasion. "I loved him the way you love weather. Constantly and without thinking about it, until one day it changes and you understand what you had."

She nodded slowly and pulled her first complete impression of the test page. It was good. Not perfect — the *h* in *history* kissed the paper a hair too hard, and I said so, and she fixed it — but good. Clean, square, confident. A stranger reading it would believe it had been printed by someone who knew exactly what they were doing, which is, I suppose, the definition of competence: the successful impersonation of certainty.

"There," I said, peeling it free and holding it up to the lamp. "That's your first presswork. When this is all over and nobody's hunting anybody, you'll frame it."

"When this is over," Wren said, taking it from me with both hands, careful as a communicant, "will you teach me the rest? The parts you learned alone? So I don't have to talk to a dead man?"

The question landed somewhere beneath my ribs and stayed there.

"I'll teach you all of it," I said. "Every part. Even the parts I got wrong — especially those. Errors are the cheapest teachers and the most expensive lessons, and I intend for you to have mine secondhand."

Outside, the river creaked again, longer this time, a groan that rose and fell and seemed to pass beneath the floor of the shop and up through our boots. We both stopped and listened. Somewhere out in the dark, a floe the size of a chapel shifted, and ten thousand tons of winter began, very slowly, to reconsider its position.

"It's really going," Wren whispered. "The ice."

"It's really going. And when it does, everything moves — the barges, the soldiers, Crane's clerks, the whole frozen machinery of this city. Nothing stays put after the break. Not the river. Not the story. Not us." I took down the lamp to trim the wick, and my hand was steady, which surprised me. "Which is why tonight we set the opening lines of the true account, and tomorrow we print, and the day after we pack, and none of us sleeps properly until the plates are on the water. Understood?"

"Understood." She was already pulling the case for the master text, her fingers finding the compartments without looking — my father's layout, her father's training, and now, if the gods of printers were paying attention, my teaching layered over both.

We had been composing perhaps an hour — the shop warm now with lamplight and industry, the type clicking into the stick in that rhythm I love best in all the world, speech made solid one letter at a time — when footsteps stopped outside the door. Not passing footsteps. Stopping ones.

Then three slow knocks, spaced like a man who wanted to be identified by his patience.

Wren froze. I blew out the lamp without deciding to — the body learns hiding faster than the mind learns courage — and through the gap in the shutter I saw the figure on the step, tall, still, hatless despite the cold, and beside him on the lane, waiting, a ministry carriage with its lanterns lit.

Nobody knocks like Aldous Crane except Aldous Crane. And he was not, I noted with the cold clarity that arrives precisely when it's least welcome, alone.

"Beatrix," Wren breathed in the dark.

"Back of the shop," I murmured. "Now. Quietly."

I put my hand on the bolt and listened to the river groan, and opened the door.
