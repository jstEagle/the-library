## 23. Crane's Summons

The summons came on a Tuesday, which I have since decided is the ministry's favorite day for ruining people. Mondays are for paperwork and Tuesdays for consequences — that is the natural order of bureaucracy, and Aldous Crane respects the natural order of bureaucracy the way a cat respects the movements of birds.

The note was delivered by a boy who wouldn't meet my eye, which told me everything before I'd broken the seal. Ministry couriers who look at you are merely delivering paper. Ministry couriers who don't are delivering verdicts.

*Mr./Ms. Tamsin Vell will present themselves at the Office of Historical Record, Quay Street, at the ninth hour, to assist Historian Crane in matters pertaining to census verification.* Assist. In my experience, when a man of Crane's temperament uses the word *assist*, what he means is *confess*. The word has that particular ministry flavor — the same flavor as *relocate*, *reassign*, and *retire*, three verbs under which this government buries everything from inconvenient clerks to inconvenient corpses.

I read the note twice, then a third time standing in the doorway of my lodgings while Mrs. Halloway's kettle screamed behind me and I did nothing about it. There is a special quality to fear that arrives on official stationery. It doesn't make your heart race. It makes everything very slow and very clear, like the moment before ice takes a man's boot.

Ninth hour. That gave me four hours, which was either generous or deliberate. Crane was not a man who left things to chance; if he wanted me rattled, he would give me exactly enough time to become so.

I did not go to Beatrix. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only here, in the account I am writing for no one: I considered it. I stood with my coat half-buttoned and thought about the walk to Eastwick's, about her hands dusted with lead, about how she would look at me and know — she reads people the way I read ledgers, line by line, looking for the entry that doesn't balance. And I thought: if they are watching me, they may be watching her, and every step I take toward that shop is a step I take for both of us.

So instead I sat down at my little table, took out a fresh sheet of ministry paper, and spent two hours doing the most important work of my life: I reviewed my own files from memory.

This is a thing clerks learn early, though we rarely say it aloud. Your records are not what happened. Your records are what you wrote down, and the gap between those two things is where you live or die. I went through the spring filings in my head, entry by entry. The Eastwick household: one printer's daughter, occupation listed as *typefounder's assistant*, no mention of presses in operation, no mention of pamphlets, no mention of Wren, no mention of the ledger. All of it misfiled with great care — dates smudged, an ink blot placed precisely where a second occupant might have been noted, the whole entry made dull enough to sleep through.

And then there were the other filings. The ones I had altered more recently. The inventory corrections. The cross-references I had quietly rerouted away from Crane's desk. A clerk's sabotage is a quiet thing, but quiet things leave fingerprints too, if a man knows where to press his thumb.

Had I been careful enough? I had thought so. But then, every forger thinks so until the expert holds their work up to the light.

Mrs. Halloway knocked eventually to ask why the kettle had boiled dry, and I heard myself say, in a voice so steady it frightened me, "Long morning. Lost track of it." She looked at me with the mild suspicion of a landlady whose rent is paid on time, and let it go. There is a certain safety in being unremarkable. I have built a life inside that safety the way a snail builds a shell, and on Tuesday morning I understood, finally, how thin the shell was.

*

Quay Street at the ninth hour smelled of tar and old rope and the particular institutional staleness of buildings that have changed masters three times in a decade without changing their curtains. The Office of Historical Record occupied a former customs house, all granite and tall windows, and someone had hung a new plaque over the door in letters so freshly gilded they still glittered: *THE RECORD ENDURES.*

I stood under it for a moment and thought: yes. That is rather the problem.

A clerk — young, nervous, wearing the ministry gray that fits everyone badly — took my name and made me wait in an anteroom furnished with one bench, one potted fern in the last stages of surrender, and forty-one minutes of silence. I counted. Counting is what I do when I am afraid. Numbers are honest; they go up and they go down and they never pretend to be anything other than what they are. By minute thirty I had catalogued every scratch on the bench, every water stain on the ceiling, and every possible version of what waited behind the door at the end of the hall, ranked from survivable to catastrophic.

At minute forty-one the door opened and the nervous clerk said, "Historian Crane will see you now," in the tone of a man announcing the tide.

Crane's office was larger than I expected and colder. He kept the window open despite the season — March wind coming off the river, carrying the groan of ice that the whole city had begun to talk about the way sailors talk about weather. He sat behind a desk bare except for a lamp, a decanter he did not offer from, and three folders arranged in a neat row. Three. Not one. Not a dozen. Three, spaced with the precision of a man laying out surgical instruments.

"Tamsin Vell." He said my name the way he said everything, in a voice like a well-bred knife. "Sit."

I sat. I had prepared a face on the walk over — the face of a tired functionary, mildly curious, faintly resentful at being pulled from useful work. I put it on now, and it fit the way my coat fits: adequately, and only because no one looks closely.

"Historian." I let a beat pass. "I hope the census abstracts were satisfactory. District Nine required some—"

"District Nine." Crane opened the middle folder without hurry. "Let us begin there, since you raise it." He turned the folder toward me. On top lay a page I knew intimately, because I had written it: the Eastwick household entry, my careful, boring, deliberately bloodless record of a shop with one occupant and no secrets.

"Do you recall this filing?"

"Of course." Keep it simple. Clerks who elaborate are clerks with something to hide. "The Eastwick premises. Deceased proprietor, one surviving family member. Routine."

"Routine." He repeated the word, tasting it. Then he drew a second page from beneath the first and laid them side by side.

My stomach performed a slow, cold revolution.

The second page was also an Eastwick household entry. Also in my hand — or near enough. Near enough to be damning. Different date. Different details. A second name. A reference to printing equipment *in active use*. Someone had produced a forgery of my forgery, and laid the two together like a magistrate laying a dead man beside his portrait.

"This second document," Crane said, "was recovered during a routine audit of district files. Note the discrepancies. Note especially—" his finger touched the second page, "—the alteration of the occupancy count, the omission of equipment, and the curious ink blot here, which our examiners inform me was applied after the fact. Blotting ink over wet ink to obscure a figure. A technique, incidentally, taught in the second year of clerical training. You would know it."

I looked at the forged page for a long moment. Long enough to seem like study. Short enough to seem unafraid. Somewhere in that arithmetic was my survival, and I could feel myself sweating inside my collar while my face did its dull, dutiful work.

"It's a good imitation," I said.

That surprised him. I saw it flicker across his face — a man who expects denial or collapse, receiving instead professional appraisal. Good. Surprise is a door left open. Walk through it slowly.

"You'll note," I continued, tapping the false page with a steadiness I did not feel, "the descender on this 'g.' Too shallow. And whoever did the blotting used iron-gall with too much gum arabic — it sits proud of the paper. Real blots sink." I shrugged. "If you're asking whether I filed the original, yes. If you're asking whether I filed that, no. It's competent work, but it isn't mine."

Silence. The wind off the river filled it, and beneath the wind, faint but unmistakable, the long groan of the ice shifting. Even the river sounded interrogative.

"And yet," Crane said softly, "the alterations run in a consistent direction. Every discrepancy between these documents favors the Eastwick household. Fewer occupants. Less activity. Less interest." He closed the folder with two fingers, gently, the way one closes a trap. "Forgery tends toward self-interest, Vell. It is practically a law of nature. So either someone is forging documents to protect a print shop, or someone is forging documents to make it appear that a census clerk protected a print shop. Which do you find more likely?"

There it was. The razor, laid flat on the desk between us, edge up.

Because that was the genius of it — if it was genius, and not luck, and I genuinely could not tell which. Either Crane had found my real tampering and dressed it up as someone else's forgery to watch me squirm, or someone had manufactured evidence of tampering that I hadn't committed, to burn me for a crime that would lead him straight to the crimes I actually had committed. Confess to the false charge and I confirmed the pattern. Deny it and I denied it in exactly the voice a guilty clerk would deny it. He had built a room with no honest exits.

I thought about Beatrix's hands. I thought about the ledger in the false bottom of the type cabinet, fifty miles of river ice away from this office, and how many people's names slept inside it. I thought about the blind compositor, arrested three weeks ago, and what they might have already wrung out of him in a room worse than this one.

Then I did the thing I had spent four hours preparing, and confessed to something smaller than the truth.

"There is something," I said.

Crane went very still. Stillness, in men like him, is appetite.

"When the unrest began in the autumn," I continued, keeping my eyes on the folder, on the desk, anywhere but his face, "district supervisors issued guidance. Log everything, flag everything, err on the side of inclusion. Some of us..." I hesitated. Let the hesitation show, just a crack, just enough. "Some of us felt the guidance exceeded the statute. Flagging households on suspicion alone — those lists don't get corrected afterward, Historian. They get acted upon. I moderated some entries. Occupancy figures, trade descriptions. Nothing material. I told myself it was precision. It was—" I exhaled. "It was mercy, if I'm honest, and I knew it wasn't mine to give. I never forged anything. I just... rounded the truth downward."

Another silence. Longer. The wind moved the papers on his desk an inch, and he pinned them with one finger without looking.

"You understand," he said at last, "that what you've just described is still a flogging offense."

"I understand that it's the truth."

"The truth." He almost smiled. It was the closest thing to warmth the room had seen, and it was worse than the cold. "You clerks love that word. You wield it like a broom — sweep what you want into view, sweep what you don't under the carpet." He leaned back. "Here is what interests me, Vell. The audit that surfaced these documents was not random. It followed the arrest of a compositor named Ferro, who — before his memory became unreliable — mentioned that census records in this district had been *managed*. Managed. His word. So I asked myself: managed by whom? And I pulled every filing touching the Eastwick premises, and I found your signature on all of them, and I found this—" he tapped the forged page again, "—which is either your clumsiness or your frame. And I confess I cannot decide which, and I dislike not deciding things."

"Perhaps," I said carefully, "Ferro named me because I was thorough. Thorough clerks get remembered. It's rarely a compliment."

"Perhaps." He studied me for a long moment, and I gave him back the full weight of my blandest expression, the one I wear for supervisors, the one that says *I am a filing cabinet with legs*. "Or perhaps Ferro named you because you were useful, and whoever managed him wanted a clerk's neck in the noose instead of their own."

"Should I be flattered or frightened?"

"You should be precise." He opened the third folder — the one I had been watching since I sat down, the one I had privately assigned to the category *catastrophic*. Inside, I glimpsed a list. Names. My eye caught the Eastwick entry before I forced my gaze back to his face. "Effective tomorrow, you are reassigned to this office. Provisionally. You will assist in cataloguing the seized inventories of the closed print shops — including, as it happens, the Eastwick premises. Consider it a test of your precision. Consider it, if you prefer, a chance to demonstrate that your rounding errors were errors of conscience rather than conspiracy." He slid a transfer chit across the desk. "Sign."

My hand did not shake. I made it not shake, the way you hold a full cup across a crowded room. I signed. The pen scratched, and the sound seemed enormously loud, and underneath it the ice on the river groaned again, longer this time, a sound like the city clearing its throat.

"You'll report to the archive annex at the eighth hour," Crane said. "The Eastwick materials arrive Thursday. Press type, formes, cabinets, correspondence — everything the garrison carried out. You will produce a complete inventory for the historical record." He paused, and the pause had teeth. "A complete inventory, Vell. Whatever the record contains, the record contains. Omissions discovered later will be attributed to whoever signed the inventory. That would be you."

"I understand."

"I don't think you do, quite. But you will." He rose, and I rose, and for a moment we stood on opposite sides of the desk like two men on opposite banks of a freezing river, each measuring the ice. "One more thing. The forged document. Our examiners will keep working on it. If it turns out to be yours after all, the conversation we've just had becomes a different conversation. If it turns out someone made it to destroy you—" he gathered his folders, "—then you and I share an enemy, and I am, when circumstances permit, a generous ally. Think about which outcome you'd prefer to arrange."

"I'll think about it, Historian."

"See that you do. Dismissed."

*

I walked out past the gilded motto and down Quay Street with the transfer chit in my pocket and my heart going like a drum in a procession, and I did not let my pace change until I was three streets away and certain — mostly certain — that no one was behind me.

Then I stopped in the mouth of an alley and laughed, once, a short ugly sound with no humor in it, because the alternative was being sick against the wall.

He had given me exactly what I needed and exactly what would destroy me, wrapped in the same assignment. Access to the Eastwick materials — the plates' cover story lived or died on that inventory, and now I would be the one writing it, with Crane reading over my shoulder. A complete inventory. Whatever the record contains, the record contains. If the plates appeared as melted scrap, I would need paper, seals, provenance, weight tickets, a foundry's mark — a forgery perfect enough to survive the examiners who had already caught one of my forgeries, except the one they'd caught wasn't mine, and I couldn't even prove that, and maybe that was the point, maybe the forged page was his, planted to keep me honest, a leash disguised as evidence—

I pressed my back against the cold brick and breathed until the alley stopped tilting.

Real or staged. His question or mine. There was no way to know from inside that office, and there was only one place to take the question, one mind sharp enough to read a forged page the way I read a ledger, one pair of hands that knew what ministry ink should weigh.

They would be watching me now. Almost certainly. A reassigned clerk is a watched clerk; Crane would want to know who I ran to, what I carried, where I lingered. So I could not go directly. I would go home, eat a supper I would not taste, sleep — or lie awake performing sleep — and in the morning, on my way to the archive annex, I would take the long route. The route that passed, entirely by coincidence, entirely in the ordinary course of a clerk's day, along Cooper's Lane.

Where, if the gods of small mercies were paying any attention at all, a certain typefounder's assistant would happen to be sweeping her doorstep at the exact moment I happened to pass, close enough to speak, far enough to seem like strangers.

I pushed off the wall and started walking. Behind me, down at the quay, the ice groaned again — longer, louder, a deep structural complaint, the sound of a thing that had held as long as it could and was beginning, unmistakably, to let go.
