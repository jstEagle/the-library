## 24. The Shop Is Watched

There is a particular kind of silence a print shop makes when it knows it's being watched. I don't mean the press goes quiet — the press has no opinions. I mean the shop itself: the way the type cases seem to hold their breath, the way the ink smell thickens as though the room were trying to become less noticeable by becoming more itself. For three days now, two soldiers in garrison grey have loitered across the lane, ostensibly guarding the chandler's doorway, which even they must know contains nothing worth guarding but candles.

They are not good at loitering. One of them — young, freckled, with the posture of a man who'd rather be anywhere else — keeps drifting to look at our window display of wedding invitations. The other stands like a fence post and picks his teeth. Between them they have consumed an alarming quantity of pipe tobacco and, judging by the frequency of their trips around the corner, most of the chandler's privy's patience.

"They're not watching for us," Wren said on the first day, peering through the gap in the shutters with the enthusiasm of a girl who had never yet been shot at. "They're watching for *supplies*. Crane thinks we'll try to move something."

"Crane is right," I said.

"Well, yes. But he doesn't know *what*."

He didn't. That was our one mercy. Aldous Crane knew that the Eastwick shop was the heart of whatever stubborn organism the printer's circle had become, and he knew that somewhere in this city sat the master plates of the true account — the record of the governor's atrocities, the names of the disappeared, the testimony of prisoners whose voices the ministry historians had already declared fictional. He did not know that the plates currently resided four feet beneath my feet, in the false bottom of the type cabinet, wrapped in oilcloth, patient as seeds.

What he also did not know — what none of us had yet solved — was how to get them out.

* * *

The circle met that night in the back room of the Drowned Bell, which sounds dramatic and was in fact damp. Marta the tavern-keeper had cleared her cellar of barrels to make room for chairs, then thought better of it and left the barrels, so we conducted our conspiracy perched on oak staves that smelled of vinegar and better years.

Present: myself; Wren, vibrating; old Hollis the blind compositor, who heard more than any sighted man in Vellham; Tam Voss, the exiled foreman, whose confession weeks ago still hung between us like smoke that wouldn't clear; and Marta herself, arms crossed, radiating the particular authority of a woman who had thrown out drunkards twice the size of anyone present.

"The port is sealed," Tam said without preamble. He never used preamble; preamble, he claimed, was for men who liked the sound of their own arrangements. "Crane's order came down yesterday. 'Pending historical review.' No barge leaves without inspection. Every gate has a roster."

"So we wait," Marta said. "The ice breaks, the review ends, life resumes."

"The review doesn't end," Hollis said quietly. "Reviews like that don't end. They end when he finds what he wants or decides it isn't there. And either way, someone hangs." He turned his blind eyes toward me, which is a thing blind men do — aim their faces like instruments. "Beatrix. How long can you keep them under your floor?"

I did the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do. "If Crane escalates from loitering to searching? Days. A week if we're lucky and he's stupid, and he is not stupid."

"Then we move them before he searches."

"Through a sealed port," Marta said, "past gates with rosters, past soldiers who are already sitting across the lane counting our chimney smoke."

"Yes," said Tam, with the flat cheer of a man proposing to swim the river in February. "That's the difficulty, isn't it. Glad we've all located it."

It was Wren who broke the silence, and she broke it the way she broke everything — accidentally, at speed. "What about the monument yard?"

We all looked at her.

"Corben's yard. On Tanner's Row. He does gravestones — cutting, polishing, and the rubbings. You know, when families want a copy of an inscription, he lays paper on the stone and blacks it over, and the letters come up white." She shrugged, defensive under our staring. "My mother's stone. I went to order a rubbing last month. There were crates of them everywhere. Big sheets, rolled, packed in straw. Nobody looks inside those crates. Who wants to look at a crate full of dead people?"

The room went quiet in the specific way rooms go quiet when a child has said something both terrible and true.

"Nobody," I said slowly, "looks inside a crate full of mourning."

"Mourning is invisible," Hollis murmured. "Best camouflage in any city. Soldiers will stop a cart of grain, a cart of iron, a cart of pamphlets. But a cart of funeral goods?" His mouth did something that was almost a smile. "They'll step *around* it. Superstition's cheaper than duty."

I should say here what I felt, which was a cold sliding sensation, half revulsion and half recognition. My father taught me that every trade has its honest disguise — that a pamphlet smuggled as songbook pages survives where a pamphlet smuggled as itself dies. He would have loved this plan and hated himself for loving it. Grief, turned into freight. The dead, made to carry the living out of a burning city.

But the dead, I reminded myself, had been carrying things for us all along. Every name in that ledger belonged to someone who could no longer object to the use made of their memory. It seemed only fair to give them employment.

"I'll need to see Corben's operation," I said. "And I'll need Corben."

* * *

Ephraim Corben was seventy if he was a day, built like the monuments he cut — broad, weathered, lettered deep. He received me in his yard among the blank stones waiting for names, and when I explained what I wanted, he listened all the way through without moving his face, which is a skill I have spent my whole life failing to learn.

"You want to put your father's plates in with my rubbings," he said. "Ship them downriver in a coffin crate marked for a family in the low country."

"Not a coffin. A shipping crate. Coffins get opened — grief invites witnesses. Shipping crates get waved through."

"And if they don't get waved through? If some captain with a bayonet decides today he fears no ghosts?"

"Then we've chosen the wrong city to die in," I said, "but at least we'll have died trying to keep a promise."

Corben looked at me for a long moment. Around us the blank stones stood in rows like a congregation waiting for a sermon. "Your father set type for my inscriptions once," he said. "Forty years ago, when my eyes first went soft. Did the kerning by hand so 'THOMAS' wouldn't gape between the O and the M. Never charged me." He spat, ceremonially, into the sawdust. "All right. But hear the terms. Nothing goes in my crates that I haven't packed myself. And the family name on the manifest will be real — the Delvernes, out past Marrow Ford, ordered a double stone for parents gone these two years. Their grief is genuine and therefore unimpeachable. We do not manufacture mourning, Miss Eastwick. We only borrow its shadow."

"I wouldn't dream of manufacturing it," I said. "I have plenty of the genuine article."

Something moved behind his face — sympathy, maybe, or recognition. "So I've heard. Your father's daughter. The one who kept the shop." He said it without pity, which I appreciated. Pity, in my experience, is just curiosity wearing black.

* * *

The problem, once Corben agreed, became geometry. The plates were heavy — lead-faced steel, forty-one of them, each the size of a hymnal and dense as sin. The false bottom of the type cabinet had held them fine, but a shipping crate has different physics: it gets lifted, tilted, occasionally dropped by men who resent their wages. Straw padding would muffle sound but not weight. Weight was the tell. Any soldier who hefted that crate would feel a mass no collection of paper rubbings could explain.

"We distribute them," Tamsin said.

I looked up from the floor plan I'd been scratching on butcher's paper. Tamsin had come after dark, off duty, hood up, looking like exactly what they were — a census clerk committing treason by lamplight. "Distribute them how?"

"Corben ships more than one crate. Rubbings go out constantly — every family in the low country with money orders stones. So we don't put forty-one plates in one crate. We put six in this one, five in that one, spread across three shipments over four days. If one crate is stopped, the rest survive. If one courier talks—" Tamsin caught themselves. "When one courier talks. Someone always talks. Then he gives up one crate's worth, not the archive."

I stared at them. "You've been thinking about failure."

"It's my profession," Tamsin said. "Census work is mostly thinking about what's missing. You count a household and you ask yourself: who isn't home? Who's hiding? What number is the truth pretending to be?" They pulled off their gloves finger by finger, a habit of theirs I'd come to read like punctuation. "Crane thinks in totals. One seizure, total victory. So we deny him totals."

There was a time — I could date it precisely, the evening of the interrogation by lamplight, when I lied to a captain with my hands busy and my heart hammering — when deceiving men in uniform would have seemed impossible to me. Now I was planning deception the way my father planned a forme: piece by piece, checking every lockup, trusting nothing to hope.

"Three shipments means three departures," I said. "Three chances for the rosters at the gates."

"Two gates," Tamsin corrected. "Tanner's Row carts use the Foundry Gate; it's the wide one, for trade. I've seen the rotation schedule." They hesitated, and I watched them decide to say the next thing. "I'm on the census verification detail for the Foundry Gate next week. Tuesday and Thursday."

The room got very quiet, in the way of rooms where someone has just volunteered to stand between their friends and a bayonet while wearing the enemy's coat.

"That's not misfiling a ledger," I said. "That's waving through contraband in person. If Crane checks—"

"If Crane checks, I'm finished regardless. My filings have been lying for a month. He suspects; he simply hasn't proven." Tamsin's voice stayed level, but their hands had gone still on the table, which for Tamsin was the equivalent of shouting. "Beatrix. I burned my own census originals. Do you understand what that means? There is no version of me that goes back to being a clerk. The only question left is whether I'm useful on the way out."

I wanted to argue. I want to be honest about that — I wanted to argue, partly for their safety and partly because arguing is what I do instead of admitting fear. But I had learned something in the months since Tamsin first appeared on my doorstep with ink-stained cuffs and too many questions: that survival is a collaborative art, and that the people who offer to stand in the dangerous places are not offering lightly, and that refusing their offer is its own kind of theft.

"Tuesday," I said finally. "First shipment Tuesday. Six plates with the Delverne crate."

"Six plates," Tamsin agreed, and then, because they were Tamsin: "And I'll log the cart as carrying 'monumental rubbings, assorted, non-seditious.' I've always wanted to write 'non-seditious' on something."

* * *

Packing took two nights. Corben came to the shop after midnight — an old man moving through streets he had no business moving through, guided by Wren, who had appointed herself his escort over everyone's objections including her own — and together the three of us worked by a single shuttered candle.

He packed the way he cut stone: slowly, exactly, without a wasted motion. Each plate went into oilcloth, then into a bed of straw shaped to its dimensions, then a layer of rubbings over it — great grey sheets covered in white lettering, IN MEMORY OF, BELOVED WIFE, RESTING UNTIL THE MORNING. The plates lay beneath epitaphs. I found I couldn't look away from that.

"Do you believe any of it?" I asked him, nodding at the sheets. "The morning. The resting."

Corben smoothed a rubbing flat with two fingers, careful as a man touching a sleeping child. "I believe people need the words," he said. "Whether or not they're true. And I believe—" he pressed the sheet down over a plate, sealing it under RESTING UNTIL THE MORNING— "that sometimes the words underneath matter more than the ones on top. Your father understood that. Why do you think he buried his account under the floor of his own shop? Same trick. Truth sleeps until the morning. Somebody just has to be there to dig it up."

Wren, cross-legged on the floor sorting straw, said nothing, but I saw her mouth the words of the rubbing she held, and I thought: she is learning this trade from all of us now, the packing and the hiding and the grief, and God help her, she's learning it well.

By dawn on Monday the first crate stood in Corben's yard, innocent as anything, breathing straw and stone dust. Two more waited in his drying shed. The remaining thirty plates still slept beneath my floor, and above them, across the lane, the fence-post soldier picked his teeth and the freckled one admired my wedding invitations, and the river ice groaned at night like an old man turning over in bed, counting down days none of us could name.

* * *

Tuesday came grey and windless. I did not go to the Foundry Gate — my presence would have been a signature on the whole enterprise — but Marta sent her nephew's boy, a quick-eyed child of eleven named Pip whose entire criminal career consisted of delivering messages and stealing apples, to watch from the wall by the tannery and report back.

The cart left Tanner's Row at midmorning. Corben drove it himself, in his good coat, with the Delverne crate and two others stacked behind him and a face carved from professional sorrow. Pip, perched on the wall with the studied boredom of childhood, saw it all and reported it in the breathless grammar of eleven-year-olds:

"Cart goes to the gate. Queue's long, maybe eight carts. Soldier comes, asks papers, Corben gives papers. Different soldier comes — officer, red sash — looks at the crates, taps one, says something. Corben says something back. Officer opens the top crate — the middle one, not yours — pulls out a rubbing, looks at it, puts it back, wipes his hand on his coat like it's dirty. Then—" Pip paused for effect, having learned dramaturgy from Wren— "then the clerk at the gate table waves them through. Just waves. Like shooing a chicken."

The clerk at the gate table. I did not let my face do anything, because Wren was watching, and Hollis was listening, and there are moments when a circle survives by everyone agreeing not to say the obvious thing aloud: that Tamsin had sat at that table in a ministry uniform and waved a crate of sedition through a sealed port with a motion of one hand, and that the gesture had cost them something no one at this table would ever fully price.

"First shipment's through," I said instead. "Thirty-five plates remain. Second shipment Thursday."

Hollis raised his cup of tea — Marta's terrible tea, which he drank with the serenity of a man who had survived worse flavors — and said, "To the dead. Best couriers in Vellham."

We drank. Outside, the ice groaned again, longer this time, a sound like the city clearing its throat to say something.

And it was that same evening, with the second crate half-packed and the candles low, that Pip came pelting up the alley and hammered on the back door in the rhythm we'd agreed meant *trouble, open quickly, trouble.* He fell into the kitchen with snow on his shoulders and his eyes enormous.

"There's a man at Corben's yard," he gasped. "Been there since dark. Not a soldier — a gentleman. Grey coat, gloves, standing across the lane just *looking*. And Corben sent me running the moment he saw him, and Corben says—" Pip swallowed, delivered it like a line he'd rehearsed and hated— "Corben says the gentleman's face is familiar from the government offices, and Corben says to tell you: *he's moved up his timetable, and Thursday may be a funeral after all.*"

Wren looked at me. I looked at the crate, half-packed, thirty-five plates short of salvation.

Thursday, I thought. Two days. And Aldous Crane was already standing in the dark, counting our stones.
