## 25. Funeral Procession

There is an art to grief, and I had never learned it. My father died on a Tuesday in October, and by Wednesday I had swept the shop and begun sorting his type, because sorrow that cannot be put to work curdles into something useless. So when Corben's message arrived — *Thursday may be a funeral after all* — it was Wren who understood it first, and I who had to be taught.

"We dress you as a mourner," she said, "and the plates as your dead."

The coffin came from Hallow & Sons, who did not ask questions because coffins are one of the few commodities in Vellham that no one ever wants examined too closely. It was elm, plain, with brass handles gone dull from a decade of handling. We paid for it with the last of the tavern-keeper Marta's hidden coin, and we paid extra for the man to deliver it at dusk, wrapped in sacking, saying loudly to anyone who asked that it was bound for a family upriver who'd lost their mother.

Into it went the archive.

I will not pretend my hands were steady. Thirty-one years of my father's secret work — the master plates of the true account of Governor Vess's reign, every confiscated letter, every prisoner's testimony, every name of every disappeared soul in Vellham — went into that coffin wrapped in oilcloth, packed around with sawdust, weighted to sit low and level. Wren laid them in the way she'd learned to lay type: carefully, in order, each plate where it could be found again. At the last she tucked something small among them. When I looked, she wouldn't meet my eye.

"It's nothing," she said.

It was her apprentice's indenture papers, folded small. The only document in Vellham that proved who she was. She was sending herself downriver along with the truth, or the part of herself that had belonged to someone else's shop. I didn't say anything. There are mercies that can only be delivered by silence.

Above the plates, a false bottom of pine; above that, a shroud; above that, a body-shaped mound of sandbags and old sacking dressed in a dead woman's nightgown that Marta swore belonged to her sister, God rest her, though I privately doubted the sister had ever existed. The face was wax and flour. It looked, in the dim light of the tannery cellar, like every corpse I'd ever been afraid of seeing, which is to say it looked convincing precisely because it was terrible.

"You'll have to cry," Wren said.

"I don't cry."

"Then you'll have to look like you're trying not to." She studied me with those quick compositor's eyes, reading me the way she read a galley, looking for the errors. "People don't watch criers. They watch people who won't cry. That's what makes them certain there's grief under there."

She was nineteen and had already learned more about performance than I had managed in thirty-two years of keeping my head down. I hated her a little for it, which is to say I loved her completely.

* * *

We rehearsed Wednesday night in the cellar among the hides and the reek of lime, and it was the strangest evening of my life until every strange evening that followed it.

Marta played the priest, having once, she claimed, buried two husbands and could manage the tone. Old Fenwick, the blind compositor, would walk beside the coffin with his hand on it — he could not carry it, but a blind man touching the box of his dead friend read truer than any hired pallbearer. The exiled foreman, Doss, whose confession still hung between us like smoke that wouldn't clear, took the rear with Pip and three of the street children scrubbed into presentability, which is to say their hands were washed and their thieving fingers were warned.

And I was chief mourner. Widow, they decided — my father's widow, since a daughter might be asked questions a widow would not. Black dress, black veil, black gloves to hide the ink stains that never fully leave a printer's hands. Marta pinned the veil so it fell past my chin.

"The veil is your armor," she told me. "Behind it, nobody exists. You're not Beatrix Eastwick. You're a woman whose whole world was in that box, and now the whole world is empty. Can you find her?"

I stood in a tannery cellar smelling of dead animals, wearing a dead woman's weeds over a living woman's fear, and I reached for it — and to my astonishment it was not far. Because it was true. Something of my world *was* in that coffin. My father's work. His hand. His thirty-one years of stubborn, dangerous faith in paper. Whatever else the procession was, I was genuinely walking my father's legacy to its grave-or-glory, and grief needs no rehearsal when it has honest roots.

"Oh," Marta said softly. "There she is."

* * *

Thursday dawned grey and raw, the river fog lying in the streets like spilled milk. A good day for a funeral, the kind neighbors say, meaning it, because fog spares mourners the indignity of being watched in full light.

We assembled at the tannery door just past eight. The coffin rode on a simple bier cart borrowed from Hallow's, drawn by a horse so old it seemed to have outlived ambition itself. I walked first behind it, veil down, hands folded. Fenwick's white hand rested on the elm lid. Behind him Marta in her priest's stole — genuine, purchased decades ago from a defrocked clergyman for reasons she declined to specify — murmured psalms in a voice pitched exactly between piety and exhaustion.

Word had gone out overnight through the channels street children occupy and adults never see: a funeral on Tanner's Row, a woman who lost everything, come pay respects if you're decent. And Vellham, bless its rotten heart, turned out. Fishwives with their caps off. Two soldiers' widows. The lamplighter, standing bareheaded. Children who knew Pip solemnly holding his cap in both fists. Grief is the one procession this city joins without asking what it's for. Perhaps because everyone in Vellham had something to mourn by then, and nowhere official to mourn it.

For six streets, it was almost beautiful. I mean that. Fog, slow wheels, Fenwick's hand on the wood, the mumble of Marta's psalms, and forty ordinary people walking because a stranger was sad. If the archive had been only plates and oilcloth, I think I'd have wept honestly by Cornmarket. Instead I held my borrowed grief like a full cup, careful not to spill it before the checkpoint.

Because there was a checkpoint. Of course there was. Crane had sealed the port pending historical review, and the roads to the water funneled through two barriers, and ours stood at the bottom of Quay Lane, where the street gave onto the stone steps and the barges beyond.

Sergeant Brase commanded it — I knew him slightly, a heavy man who bought pamphlets he couldn't read and burned them unread, and I had always assumed this made him safe. But Crane had moved up his timetable, and men move timetables by tightening checkpoints, and when we rounded the corner into Quay Lane I saw that today Brase had four men with him instead of two, and a table set across the lane, and — my heart performed a slow cold somersault — a clerk's portable desk, and seated at it, cataloguing, a ministry clerk.

They stopped us twenty feet out. Brase raised a hand with the weary authority of a man who had waved through forty funerals already that winter and resented each one personally.

"Name of the deceased?"

"Mistress Aldery," Marta said, without a breath of hesitation, giving the name we'd seeded through the neighborhood for two days. "Died of the lung fever. I'm conducting the service. This is the widow."

Brase peered past her at me. I kept my head bowed, veil still, hands clasped. I thought of chapter thirteen of my life — the captain questioning me while I mended a forme and lied fluently for the first time — and I thought: I have done this before. I can do it again. But lying to a searching soldier alone in your own shop is one craft; lying in public, in mourning clothes, with a city watching, is another, and my palms were sweating inside black gloves.

"Fever," Brase said. "Half the city's coughing. Open it."

The world went very quiet and very loud at once. Somewhere behind me Pip's breathing changed. Fenwick's hand tightened almost imperceptibly on the coffin lid — he heard everything, our Fenwick, heard the clerk shift papers, heard Brase's boots, heard my heart probably, blind men hear what sighted people only look at.

"With respect, Sergeant," Marta said, mild as milk, "the widow has already fainted once this morning. The sight of her husband—" 

"*Wife*," I said, before I could stop myself, and immediately wanted to die, because correcting the gender meant speaking, and speaking meant the veil lifted slightly, and lifting the veil meant—

Brase was looking directly at me now. "Open the coffin," he said, not unkindly, which was worse than unkindness. "It's orders. Fever deaths get inspected. There's sickness going downriver on barges and the port's closed for a reason. Ten seconds and you'll be on your way."

Four soldiers. One clerk with a pen. One elm box containing thirty-one years of treason packed in sawdust, and beneath the false pine bottom, a wax-faced woman in a borrowed nightgown who would take perhaps four seconds to expose as sawdust and fraud.

Fenwick began to pray aloud. Not psalm-mumbling — actual words, an old compositor's prayer for the dead that I had never heard and understood instantly was invented on the spot, and around us the crowd, our crowd, the fishwives and the widows and the lamplighter, bowed their heads, and the soldiers hesitated, because interrupting a prayer for the dead in front of forty mourners takes a specific kind of officer and Brase was not that kind.

Ten seconds. The clerk licked his pen. Brase stepped toward the coffin.

"Let them pass."

The voice came from the side, from the shadow of the customs house, and it wore the flat boredom of authority. Tamsin stepped out in census uniform, ledger under one arm, and I nearly broke cover right there, because the last time I'd seen that face it had been across my own counter, ink-stained cuffs and dangerous curiosity, and now it was buttoned to the throat in ministry blue and looking at our coffin with the profound indifference of a person who has seen ten thousand coffins and cared about none of them.

"Ministry business," Tamsin said to Brase, producing papers with the crisp efficiency of long practice. "Historical review. The port closure includes documentation transfers — records moving to the review office. Coffin's flagged." A pause, delicately bored. "You did receive yesterday's circular?"

Brase had received no such circular. Brase knew he had received no such circular. But the circular was written on proper ministry paper with a proper ministry seal, and I recognized the paper, because I had helped make it — two nights earlier, at my own press, printing a forgery so perfect that even I believed it, and here was the moment every kerned ligature and aged edge earned its keep.

Brase squinted at the seal. The clerk craned to look. Somewhere in my chest a drum counted seconds that did not exist.

"Circular's annexed to the port order," Tamsin continued, folding the papers away with finality. "Funeral transports pre-cleared under schedule C. You want to hold a flagged transfer, Sergeant, that's a report to the review office, and the review office—" a glance at the clerk, one professional to another— "is behind on its filings. Your name would go in the margin."

No one wants to be in the margin. I have spent my whole life in margins and I can tell you it is no country anyone emigrates to willingly. Brase looked at the coffin, at me, at the bowed heads of forty mourners still frozen in prayer, and made the calculation that keeps sergeants sergeants.

"Move along," he said. "Keep it orderly."

Tamsin stepped aside, back into the role, back into uniform, and did not look at me even once, and that absence of a look was the most eloquent thing I have ever witnessed. We passed the barrier. The horse walked. The wheels creaked. Forty mourners breathed again as one body, and behind us the checkpoint swallowed the next cartload of ordinary lives.

* * *

At the water stairs, the coffin went onto the ferry barge under sacking and netting, consigned as "household goods," because the dead travel better disguised as furniture than furniture does as the dead. Fenwick gripped my arm hard enough to bruise.

"That voice," he said quietly. "The census one. They were at your shop in the autumn. Asking about pamphlets."

"Yes."

"And now they wear blue and wave coffins through barriers." He turned his blind eyes toward the river. "Beatrix. A man who lies well for you today will lie well against you tomorrow, unless—" he tapped my arm— "unless what they're lying for is bigger than what they're lying to. Which is it?"

I watched the barge take the current, watched it shrink between the ice floes grinding slowly downstream, carrying my father's life's work and a girl's indenture papers toward sympathetic presses in the low country.

"They burned their own census books," I said. "Tuesday night. I watched them do it."

Fenwick absorbed this the way he absorbed all information, silently, thoroughly, filing it somewhere behind the blindness. "Then they've got no country left," he said at last, "except the one they're building. Mind you build yours the same way — out of true type."

He went down the gangplank to catch the east road. Marta embraced me, smelling of incense and gin. Doss shook my hand and did not meet my eyes and I let him not, because forgiveness is a thing you extend in installments.

And then the quay was empty except for me and the fog and the distant grind of the ice, and I turned for home with my borrowed widow's weeds under my coat, and found Tamsin waiting in the mouth of Tanner's Row, out of uniform now, coat collar up, looking younger and considerably more frightened than the person who had stared down Sergeant Brase.

"You used my name in the margin threat," I said. "That circular will be traced."

"It was traced by noon." Tamsin's breath fogged. "Crane sent for me an hour ago. He has the circular on his desk, Beatrix. He has *my* filing signature under it." A pause, and then, in a voice stripped of all officialdom: "He's ordered me to attend him tonight. And I don't think it's about paperwork anymore."

The ice groaned somewhere out on the river, a long low sound like the city clearing its throat.

"I'm coming with you," I said.

"No," said Tamsin. "But I'll tell you tomorrow whether there's still a tomorrow."
