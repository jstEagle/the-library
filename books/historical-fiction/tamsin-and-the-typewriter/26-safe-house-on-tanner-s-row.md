## 26. Safe House on Tanner's Row

The tannery smelled the way grief smells — sharp, chemical, impossible to get out of your clothes. That was the argument for it, apparently. Nobody with a working nose wanted to linger in a cellar where forty years of hides had been scraped and salted and steeped in lime, and so nobody came looking. The dead trade of leather had become the living refuge of paper.

"It's perfect," Wren had said when she led us down, ducking under a beam that had clearly claimed scalps before ours.

"It's revolting," I said.

"That's what makes it perfect."

She wasn't wrong. The cellar of Harl & Sons — the sons long gone to sea or to graves, leaving only the name and the vats — was a low brick room with a dirt floor that sweated even in February, lit by a single lamp hung from a hook that might once have held a carcass. Crates of tallow along one wall. A drain in the corner that I tried very hard not to think about. And in the middle of it all, stacked like ordinary freight: the coffin.

We had carried my father's life through half of Vellham inside a coffin, behind a funeral procession I wept at with genuine feeling, past a checkpoint where Tamsin's uniform did more good than all our mourning combined. Now the coffin sat among the tallow crates, lid nailed shut, and nobody in the world knew it was there except five people and God, and I had begun to suspect God kept his own census.

Tamsin set the lamp down and looked at me across the box.

"Well," they said. "Aren't you going to open it?"

I had been avoiding this moment since Tanner's Row. In the shop, the plates had been work — something hidden, guarded, moved. A task is a wonderful thing. A task keeps your hands busy and your heart out of the way. But here, in the lime-and-rot dark, with nothing left between me and the archive but a few nails and my own nerve, there were no tasks left. There was only reading.

"I'm going to open it," I said. "I'm deciding how."

"You've been deciding for an hour."

"Deciding takes time."

Wren snorted from her crate by the wall. She had appointed herself sentry, though the only thing likely to attack us down here was the smell. "You're frightened," she said, with the blunt cruelty of the young.

"Of course I'm frightened." I took up the pry bar. "My father has been dead three years. Tonight he starts talking."

* * *

The nails came free easier than nails should have. He'd wanted them to yield — that was the first thing I understood, kneeling there in the lamplight as the lid lifted and the smell of oilcloth rose out of the dark. He had built this box knowing someone would have to open it in a hurry, in fear, by lamplight, with shaking hands. He had thought about the hands.

The plates lay wrapped in oilcloth, packed in wool waste, each one sleeved in its own folder of heavy brown paper. Forty-one folders. I counted them twice because counting is what I do when I cannot bear what I'm counting. Each folder bore a number in his hand — that upright, patient script I knew better than my own reflection — and a title of no more than four words. *The Grain Requisition. Winter of the Empty Shelves. Names Taken at Kessel Bridge.*

"He documented everything," Wren whispered, as if the plates could hear her. Maybe they could. Lead holds an impression; why shouldn't it hold attention too?

"Not everything," I said. "He couldn't. Nobody could. He documented what he could reach." Even as I said it I didn't know if I was defending him or indicting him, and I suppose that's what inheritance is — you take over the debts along with the shop.

I unwrapped the first folder with the care you'd give a sleeping animal. Inside, a plate the size of a hymnal page, and on it — set in type, locked into its forme with wax and wire — the opening lines of an account I had somehow always known existed and never once let myself imagine.

*Being a true relation of certain proceedings in the city of Vellham, gathered by those who witnessed them, set down by one who set them in type, and committed to lead against the day when ink may again speak freely.*

Against the day. He had printed truth into metal because paper burns and lead endures. My father, who mended the press with his own hands and complained about the price of rag stock, had spent his last years casting testimony into a material that would survive a fire. He had known there would be a fire. He had simply assumed it would come after him.

It did.

* * *

Tamsin read over my shoulder. They read fast — clerical training, eyes trained on columns of figures, trained to extract meaning from a page in the time it takes a soldier to grow bored — but tonight they read slowly, and I felt their breath change against the back of my neck.

"This is the grain requisition," they said quietly. "Winter before last. The ministry recorded it as 'provisioning delays.'"

"The ministry recorded starvation as weather," I said. "Read what he wrote."

Tamsin read aloud, because some things need a voice:

*"On the twelfth of Frostmonth, the granaries of Vellham stood at one-tenth capacity while barges loaded under military guard carried the harvest downriver. The requisition order bears the seal of the Governor's Office. I have set the seal itself in type — see plate fourteen — so that no clerk may later claim forgery. The names of those who petitioned for relief, and were arrested for sedition in consequence, follow. There are sixty-one names. Fourteen have since died in the Marsh Street cells. Their families were billed for their keep."*

Billed for their keep. Tamsin stopped reading. I heard them swallow.

"I filed those petitions," they said. It came out flat, the voice of someone reciting their own indictment. "That winter. Dozens of them. I logged every name, every address, every complaint. I thought — I told myself I was recording grievance, keeping a record, that someday the record would matter." They laughed, and it was the worst laugh I'd ever heard from them. "I was building the arrest list. I handed them the list myself, neatly ruled, in triplicate."

"You didn't know."

"I had everything I needed to know. I chose the version that let me sleep." They sat back on their heels. Down here in the dark their face was all shadow and lamplight, and I watched the two fight for possession of it. "Crane's historians will write 'provisioning delays.' And it will be true, in the way that a lie made of true words is still a lie. That's the craft of it, Beatrix. That's what I never understood until now. They don't burn the record. They file it."

* * *

We worked through the night, and I will not pretend to describe it well, because there is no well in it.

Folder after folder. The plates were dense — he had set small, tight, economical, cramming testimony onto every square inch of lead like a man packing for a voyage he might not return from. Prisoner testimonies transcribed verbatim, with the transcriber's note: *Set exactly as spoken. Grammar is theirs; the shame is ours.* Confiscated letters, copied whole. A list of the disappeared, maintained like a ledger, entry after entry: name, trade, street, date taken, date last seen, date reported dead where such report existed, blank where it did not. The blanks were the worst part. Sixty-one names in the first account; by the final plates, the list ran to two hundred and thirty-eight, and beside nearly forty of them, in his hand, in type, the word *unknown*.

Unknown. He hadn't given up on them. Unknown meant the record refused to close the question. Unknown meant: *we do not yet know, therefore we must survive to learn.*

There was a section on the Kessel Bridge shootings — nine dead, though the official count said three, and he had set both numbers side by side with the witnesses' names beneath, daring anyone to reconcile them. There was the account of the Marsh Street cells from a woman named Odile Ferrant, who had been released and re-arrested three times and whose testimony ended mid-sentence, with a compositor's note in the margin: *Her account continues; she was taken before she could finish. This is where the record breaks. Let the break be visible.*

Let the break be visible. I put the plate down and pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes and sat there in the tannery stink and wept, without grace or economy, for my father and for Odile Ferrant and for the forty unknowns and for the woman I had been three years ago, sorting type by candlelight and calling my silence prudence.

Wren cried too, but differently — furiously, wiping her face with her sleeve like the tears were a personal insult. She had known pieces. Everyone in the district knew pieces. But no one had ever assembled the pieces, and seeing the whole was like being shown the map of a country you'd been lost in all your life.

"He was one man," she said finally. "With one press. How did he do all this?"

"He wasn't one man." I turned to the colophon page, the last plate, which I had saved for last the way you save the last of anything. "Look."

*This account was gathered by many hands,* the plate read. *The circle of printers who made it: E. Eastwick, compositor; M. Quill, foreman; Hoss the Blind, reader of proofs; Sera Voss, keeper of the tavern door; and those who must go unnamed because they are not yet safe. If this record survives, it survives because each of us believed the others would carry it if we fell. We were right about most of us.*

"We were right about most of us." Tamsin repeated it softly. "He wrote that before the raid. Before Quill broke, before Hoss was taken. He already knew the arithmetic."

"And he set it anyway." I touched the letters, the little reversed *e* he hid in the word *account* — his signature, the one he thought no one but me would ever find. "Not hope. Arithmetic plus stubbornness. That's what I inherited."

* * *

Near dawn — or what passes for dawn in a windowless cellar, which is to say when the lamp burned low and our backs gave out — we laid the plates back in their wool and closed the coffin, and none of us spoke for a while.

Then Wren said, "Fifty copies. You're still printing fifty copies."

"If the barge goes."

"When the barge goes." She had learned stubbornness somewhere; I suspected my father's plates, read by lamplight, had taught her overnight.

"Fifty copies," Tamsin said slowly, "against Crane's presses. Against the ministry's historians, the schoolbooks they'll commission, the anniversary orations, the whole machinery of forgetting. Fifty copies is a drop in the river."

"Yes," I said.

"And you're smiling."

"I am." And I was, though my eyes were still raw. "Because you've got it backwards, Tamsin. Crane doesn't fear fifty copies. He fears one. One true copy, surviving, anywhere, is a splinter in the official story that works outward forever. He can't kill a fact by outnumbering it. He can only kill it by making sure it never existed — and we've just spent the night proving it exists, in lead, in his own seal, in sixty-one names. He's not fighting paper anymore." I rested my hand on the coffin lid. "He's fighting metal. And metal remembers."

Tamsin looked at me for a long moment, and then at the coffin, and something in their face settled — the same settling I'd seen the night they burned their census originals, a decision completing itself in silence.

"There's something else," they said. "I should have said it earlier, but you needed to read first. You needed to know what we're carrying before I told you the cost." They drew a folded paper from inside their coat. "The port notice went up this evening. Officially it's 'pending historical review of outbound cargo' — Crane's phrase, his fingerprints all over it. Every barge master on the river has been ordered to submit manifests. Every exit is watched."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere above us, the city woke to another gray morning, and beneath the floor, faint as a heartbeat, the river ice shifted in its sleep.

"So we haven't outrun him," Wren said. "We've just gotten ahead by the length of a coffin."

"For now," Tamsin said. "But Beatrix — if the plates are what I think they are, if Crane reads even one folder of what we read tonight—" they tapped the coffin lid, twice, gently, like knocking on a door "—then this isn't cargo anymore. It's a verdict. And men like Crane don't wait for verdicts to be delivered. They go looking for the courtroom."

I put my ear against the wood, the way I used to put my ear against my father's chest when I was small and couldn't sleep. Wool, oilcloth, lead, and under it all, faint and patient, the smell of ink.

"Then we'd best be quick," I said. "The ice won't hold its grudge forever."
