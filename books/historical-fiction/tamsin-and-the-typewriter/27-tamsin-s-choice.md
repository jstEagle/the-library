## 27. Tamsin's Choice

There is a particular kind of paper the Ministry uses for good news. It is heavier than the ordinary stock, cream-colored instead of gray, and it arrives in an envelope sealed with green wax rather than black, because apparently even bureaucracy understands that a person should be able to feel their ruin coming through the post.

I held the envelope for a long time on the walk back from the census office, turning it over, weighing it like a coin I suspected was clipped. The seal cracked with a small, satisfying sound — one of the last honest pleasures I would take from that institution — and inside, on that expensive cream stock, was my reward for eleven years of faithful counting.

*Promoted to Senior Registrar, Second Class.* Transfer effective at quarter's end. *To the provincial office at Marrowfield.*

Marrowfield. Three hundred miles upriver, a town whose chief exports were wool, piety, and a climate that made Vellham's winters look like a warm bath. A town where nobody had ever printed so much as a seditious hymnal, where the most dangerous document I would ever handle was a petition about drainage ditches. A town where Aldous Crane's long arm could not reach me, and where his suspicion — if suspicion it truly was — could not follow.

A generous salary. A house with a garden attached to the office. A pension that would keep me in tea and dry stockings until I died respectable.

And Beatrix Eastwick, three hundred miles behind me, waiting for the ice to break and the soldiers to come.

* * *

I did not go straight home. I walked, because walking is what I do when thinking fails me, and my feet took me the long way along the river road, past the coal yards and the ropewalks, down to where the ice stretched gray and white to the far bank like a road built by something that hated travelers.

The river was talking to itself. It had been doing that for three days now — low groans, sudden cracks that sounded like furniture breaking in an empty house, a deep shifting mutter under everything, as if the water beneath had grown impatient and was rehearsing its argument. The bargemen stood at the edges of the ice in little knots, spitting, reading the cracks the way clerks read ledgers. One of them told me — unprompted, the way bargemen tell you things — that when the river spoke in sentences rather than single words, you had four days. Maybe five.

Four days. Five. And then the barges would go, and whatever left Vellham on them would be gone for good, and whatever stayed would belong to the fire.

I stood there with the promotion letter in my pocket and understood, finally and completely, that I had already made my choice. That I had probably made it months ago, the night I first misfiled a household entry — *Eastwick, B., occupation: compositor (deceased concern), no presses of record* — and felt nothing but a strange lightness, like a man setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying. Every day since had just been the long ceremony of catching up to myself.

But knowing a thing and doing it are separated by exactly the distance a coward can stretch, and I have always been flexible.

* * *

Crane summoned me at the end of the week, which surprised me; I'd expected him to let the promotion do its work quietly, like poison in soup. Instead I was shown into his office above the Ministry archive, a room I had never entered despite eleven years in the building, because rooms like that are not entered by people who count. They are counted *in*, from a distance, on lists.

He was smaller than I expected. That is the thing they never tell you about powerful men: how much of their size is furniture. Seated behind a desk built for a man twice his consequence, Aldous Crane looked like a clerk who had stolen someone else's chair and was daring the room to mention it. He had ink on his fingers too. I noticed that before anything else, and hated myself for the kinship it implied.

"Registrar Quill," he said. My name, pronounced carefully, like a word in a language he found distasteful. "Sit."

I sat.

"Congratulations on your advancement." He slid a second sheet across the desk — the transfer order, already signed, already dated. Already done. Which meant this conversation was not a conversation; it was a formality performed over a decision made without me. "Marrowfield is a quiet posting. Quiet postings suit certain temperaments."

"I've heard the wool is excellent," I said.

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. An inventory of it, taken and shelved.

"You've been with the census eleven years," he said. "Your filings are... thorough. Meticulous, some have said." He let *some have said* hang in the air between us like smoke. "Thoroughness is a virtue, Registrar. But thoroughness has a cousin, and the cousin is called interference. Do you know the difference?"

"Roughly half a page of marginalia," I said.

This time the flicker became, briefly, an actual expression — something almost like respect, or perhaps just appetite. Crane leaned back. "The Eastwick household," he said. "Vellham parish, district nine. Tell me what your records show."

My heart did not pound. That was the strange part. Eleven years of interviews with grieving widows and lying merchants and frightened children had taught me that the face does its best work when the chest is in open revolt. I kept my voice at the pitch of mild bureaucratic boredom, the voice I used for asking men about their chickens.

"The household of the late Josiah Eastwick, printer. One surviving dependent, a daughter. No registered trade following the father's death. The press was listed as sold for scrap in—" I paused, precisely, "—the autumn inventory."

"Mm." He opened a drawer. I watched his hand move among the papers and thought, with the detached clarity of a drowning man observing the quality of the water, that every document in that drawer had passed through hands like mine. That the entire architecture of the state — every list, every ledger, every neat column of names — existed because people like me wrote it down, and could therefore be un-written by people like me. Crane believed he commanded an army of facts. He commanded an army of clerks. It is not the same thing, though he would spend the rest of his career failing to learn the difference.

"There are irregularities," he said. "Small ones. A misfiled inspection here. A delayed report there. Nothing provable. Nothing yet worth the trouble of proving." He closed the drawer and folded his hands, and looked at me with the frank, patient attention of a man examining a horse's teeth. "You are being given Marrowfield, Registrar, because Marrowfield is far away and unimportant, and because I prefer solutions to scenes. Take the posting. Keep your meticulousness for drainage petitions. And if, in your last days in this city, you should happen to recall any irregularity — any at all — my door is open to you."

"And if I don't recall any?"

"Then I expect you'll enjoy the wool."

He dismissed me with a nod, the way one dismisses weather. At the door I paused — I couldn't help it; the dramatist in me, starved these eleven years, seized its moment — and asked him why he wanted the plates so badly. Why not simply burn them and be done? Why hunt paper?

Crane considered the question with genuine scholarly interest, which was somehow worse than anger.

"Burning proves nothing," he said. "Burning only proves there was something worth burning. No — the account must be *replaced*, Registrar. Overwritten. A history with a hole invites questions. A history full to the brim with better stories invites none." He smiled then, fully, and I saw the whole of him at last: not a monster, merely an editor. "That is what your census is for, you understand. Not counting heads. Counting what may safely be forgotten."

I thanked him for his time. It is astonishing what the mouth will do on its own, once trained.

* * *

I went home by way of the print shop.

Beatrix was at the case when I came in, sorting type by candlelight — she sorted type the way other people pray, I think, hands moving through the little lead letters while her mind went somewhere the rest of us couldn't follow. She looked up when the bell over the door moved, and read my face in the space of a breath. She is very good at reading faces. We recognized each other early on as fellow professionals in that particular trade.

"Well?" she said.

"Marrowfield," I said. "Senior Registrar. A garden. A pension."

She set down the composing stick. "When?"

"Quarter's end. Which is to say—" I glanced out the dark window, toward the river I could not see but could hear, muttering under its ice, "—before the ice breaks."

We looked at each other across the shop, across the formes and the ink and the smell of lead and lamp oil, and I watched her understand what I was telling her. That they knew. Not everything — Crane had said *nothing provable*, and Crane does not bluff with nothing; if he could prove it, I'd have been arrested in the archive stairwell, not promoted. But he suspected, and suspicion was enough to move me along like a slow stain spreading toward the edge of the cloth.

"They're offering you a way out," she said.

"They're offering me a way *away*. It's not the same thing." I sat down on the stool by the stone sink, suddenly tired in a way that had nothing to do with my legs. "Do you know what I did today, before the summons? I catalogued a widow's household goods. Six chairs, one loom, a Bible. Her husband drowned in the mill race last spring and the state needed to know, officially, whether she owned six chairs. Eleven years of that. Eleven years of learning exactly how much a life weighs when you put it on a scale, and the answer is: less than the paper it's written on." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "And I was *good* at it, Beatrix. That's the shameful thing. I was very good."

She didn't comfort me. That's another thing about her — she knows comfort, offered at the wrong moment, is just another way of changing the subject. Instead she said, "What will you do?"

And there it was. The question, laid out plain on the counter between us like a forme waiting to be locked.

I had been carrying the answer around for days, wrapped in the promotion letter, and I found I could not set it down gently. There was no gentle way. So I said it the way you pull a tooth.

"I'm going to burn my files."

* * *

Not all of them. Let me be exact, because exactness was the only craft I ever had, and I mean to keep it even now, especially now. Burning *all* of them would be a gesture, and gestures get people hanged. What I burned was mine.

For eleven years I kept private copies. Every registrar does — not because we're traitors, but because the ministry loses things, and a registrar who cannot produce a duplicate becomes the explanation for the loss. Mine lived in two oilcloth packets under a floorboard in my rooms: household returns going back to the first year of my appointment. Names, ages, trades, possessions. The whole parish rendered in my own careful hand, in columns so straight you could have shot a pistol down them.

Eleven years of Vellham, written down by me. And I knew — I had known since the night Crane explained it to me with that editor's smile — exactly what my beautiful columns were for. Every name I had recorded was a name the Ministry could find again. Every trade I logged was a trade they could suppress. When the arrests came after the pamphlets, when they came for the tavern-keeper and the blind compositor and God knew who next, the men with the lists would be working off documents that began their lives as *my* handwriting, in *my* neat, straight, obedient columns.

I had thought of myself as a camera. All this time I had been a gun sight.

So on the last night of the quarter, after dark, I took both packets up to my room, and I fed them into the stove a handful at a time.

I want to tell you it was hard. Some of it was. There was the page from my second year — the Hollis family, canal-side, seven children, and I remembered the youngest girl showing me her collection of river stones with the solemn pride of a museum curator. There was old Marta Kessling's return, marked *deceased* in my hand three winters back, and beneath it the note I'd made because the official form had no box for it: *neighbors say she taught half the street to read.* There were hundreds of pages like that. Lives in my handwriting. And the fire ate them in seconds each, greedily, without discrimination, the stove making no distinction between a widow's six chairs and a child's stones, because fire, unlike the Ministry, is honest about what it does.

But here is the truth underneath the sentiment, and I set it down because whoever reads this account deserves the ugly parts too: most of it was easy. Most of it was relief. Page after page went into the flames and what I felt was not grief but *unbuttoning* — the sensation, coat after coat, of putting down weights I had mistaken for organs. For eleven years I had been Tamsin Quill, Registrar Second Class, and the name fit like a uniform fits: close, warm, and not mine. The fire took the uniform. What was left sitting on the floor of that cold room, watching the last of the oilcloth curl, was somebody with no title, no posting, no pension, no address that would survive the season.

It should have been terrifying. Mostly it was terrifying. But underneath the terror, quiet as the current under the ice, was something I had not felt since childhood, and it took me a while to name it, because I'd had no use for the word in my line of work.

It was possibility.

* * *

I went to the shop the next morning with ash still faintly under my nails, and Beatrix took one look at my hands and understood.

"It's done?" she said.

"It's done. I'm nobody official as of today. Which means I'm also nobody protected." I laid the transfer order on her counter, face up, the green wax broken, Crane's signature drying at the bottom like a fly in amber. "They'll notice within the week when I don't present myself at the coach office. Less, if Crane checks. He'll check."

She studied the order for a long moment. Then, in the driest voice I have ever heard from her — and Beatrix Eastwick has a very dry range — she picked up a composing stick and began, letter by letter, setting the words *SENIOR REGISTRAR, SECOND CLASS* in fourteen-point.

"What are you doing?"

"Galleys," she said. "For wrapping fish. Waste paper has to earn its keep." She broke the line down again immediately, but I caught it — the joke, the deflection, the thing she does instead of saying *I'm glad, I'm afraid, don't leave, stay.* I have spent eleven years reading forms. I can read a woman setting type.

"Wren's asleep in the back," she said. "She was up all night with the plates — we're packing them against the move. Oilcloth, tallow-paper, straw. The coffin arrangement is set for tomorrow night." She looked up. "Tamsin. Once you miss that coach, there's no filing your way back. You know that. Everything you were goes on the fire with the rest."

I thought about the stove. About the Hollis girl and her river stones. About eleven years of columns, and the difference — half a page of marginalia wide — between thoroughness and interference.

"Everything I was," I said, "was a very tidy list of everyone they intend to hurt. I'm not mourning it." I pulled the stool out from the type case and sat down, and reached for the spare composing stick that was hanging where it always hung, because some habits deserve to survive the conflagration. "Now show me how you lock a forme properly. If I'm to be a fugitive, I'd rather be a useful one."

She showed me. Her hands over mine on the quoins, correcting my grip the way her father must once have corrected hers, and outside the window the river spoke again — a long, cracking sentence this time, not a single word, and we both stopped and listened until it finished.

"That's sooner than they said," Beatrix murmured.

It was. Four days, the bargeman had told me. Maybe five. That had been yesterday.

And far off across the rooftops, thin but unmistakable in the morning cold, came the sound I had been listening for all week without admitting it: boots. Many boots. Moving in our direction, unhurried, the way men walk when they believe the doors will open for them.

Down the lane, a whistle — the garrison's two-note signal for *secure the block*.

Beatrix blew out the candle over the type case, though the morning was bright enough to work by, and said, very quietly:

"They're early. Get Wren."
