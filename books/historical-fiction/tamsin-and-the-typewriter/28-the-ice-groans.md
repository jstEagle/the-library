## 28. The Ice Groans

The river spoke first.

I was in the shop before dawn — a habit I'd inherited from my father along with his ink-stained fingernails and his talent for worrying in the dark — when the sound came up through the floorboards like a word spoken by something enormous and slow to anger. A groan, then a crack, then a long shuddering sigh that rattled the type cases and set the loose pane in the back window buzzing against its putty.

I stood with my hand flat on the press bed, listening. Anyone who has lived beside a frozen river all winter knows that sound. It is not the ice breaking. It is the ice *considering* it. It is the river turning over in its sleep and muttering that spring is coming whether the governor likes it or not.

By midmorning the bargemen on the quay were saying what they always said when the ice began to talk: three days, maybe four, maybe less if the warm wind held out of the south. Three days until the floes went downriver and the coal barges with them. Three days until our window closed — or opened, depending on how you liked your metaphors, and I had lately developed a taste for the kind where the window opens onto a river full of moving ice and men with rifles on the far bank.

Wren arrived at seven with bread and bad news, which had become her signature contribution to any gathering.

"Port's sealed," she said, dropping the loaf on the composing stone hard enough to make the furniture quads jump. "Crane's order. Posted at the customs house an hour ago. No vessel leaves Vellham pending — " she made her voice go smooth and official, a fair imitation of Crane's particular brand of velvet menace — " 'historical review of materials relevant to the civic record.' "

"Historical review," I repeated.

"That's the phrase."

"He reviews history the way a butcher reviews a pig."

Wren almost smiled. Almost. She'd been doing that less since the compositor's arrest — old Hollis, who had taught half the printers in this city their letters and now sat in some ministry cellar learning what the ministry did to people who knew too many of them. The guilt of it sat on her like wet wool. I knew the feeling. I owned several garments cut from the same cloth.

"The mole," Wren said quietly. "The foreman's decoy errands. They didn't work?"

"They worked exactly as designed." I tore the loaf and handed her half, because grief goes easier with bread. "They bought us time. Time is not the same as an exit."

* * *

Here is what we had, as of that morning: one complete set of master plates containing the true account of the uprising — the confiscated letters, the prisoners' testimony, the names of the disappeared, my father's careful corrections in the margins — hidden in a tannery cellar on Tanner's Row beneath forty hides and a smell that could strip paint. One bribed coal barge captain named Oskar Voss, whose price had consumed the last of the circle's money and whose loyalty was, by his own cheerful admission, "thick as river mud but about as deep." One press, one apprentice, one blind tavern-keeper who could still set type faster than most sighted men, one exiled foreman running errands for both sides, and one census clerk whose name appeared in no ledger I kept but whose absence from my mornings had begun to feel like a missing piece of punctuation.

And here is what we did not have: a way out. Because Aldous Crane, who understood better than any man in Vellham that history is a cargo like any other, had sealed the port.

I spent the day at the window pretending to sort type, which is my version of pacing. From the upstairs casement I could see the lane, and across the lane the doorway of the shuttered chandlery where two soldiers had taken up residence sometime after dawn. They were not good at loitering. They stood too straight. They took turns going for food, always one remaining, and they had established a brazier outside the door with the confident permanence of men who expected to be there through thaw, flood, and the second coming.

Every exit watched. That was the arithmetic of it. The quay had checkpoints at both ends. The bridge over the Millrace had been closed "for inspection" — a bridge that had never once been inspected in my lifetime. The south road out of the city passed the garrison, which rather defeated the purpose. And the river, our river, our great grey argument for hope, was scheduled to open in three days and was already ringed with customs men carrying new paper seals and old-fashioned patience.

"We could wait them out," Wren offered, without conviction. We were eating the rest of the bread over the type case like conspirators, which is to say hunched and quiet and dropping crumbs among the lowercase.

"For how long? Crane doesn't wait. Waiting is for people who believe time is neutral." I brushed crumbs from the case with the little camel-hair brush Father had used, and felt the usual small treason of touching his things while planning things he'd have hated. Or would he? I still wasn't sure. The man had buried master plates inside his own press. There was more conspirator in his grave than there had ever been in his obituary.

"Tamsin will come tonight," Wren said. "They sent word through the boy."

Through the boy — Pip, eleven years old, fleet as spilled mercury, who ran messages between Tanner's Row and the shop for the price of buns and the thrill of importance. I had come to rely on a network consisting largely of children and one nearsighted tanner, and I tried not to think about what that said regarding the professionalism of our enterprise.

* * *

Tamsin came at midnight, hood up, walking with the deliberate unhurried gait of someone who has learned that hurrying is what guilty people do. They let themselves in with the key I'd cut for them — a fact that still produced in me a small internal argument between prudence and something warmer that I declined to examine too closely, the way you decline to look directly at a candle flame.

"You heard," they said, pulling back the hood. Their face was grey with fatigue. Census work had always left marks on Tamsin — the ink stains, the squint — but this was different. This was the face of someone who had spent the day being looked *at*.

"I heard. Tell me the rest."

"The rest is worse." They sat on the type cabinet, which no one else was permitted to sit on, a privilege they had never been informed of and exercised anyway. "Crane's sealed the port under a writ of historical preservation. It sounds absurd and it is entirely legal — he drafted the statute himself last autumn, before the uprising, which tells you how much faith he had in the government's ability to win cleanly. Nothing leaves by water. Nothing leaves by road without a manifest countersigned by his office. And he's ordered a final inventory of every print shop in the city. Sealed shops, active shops, ruined shops. Everything gets catalogued into the official record."

"Catalogued or confiscated?"

"In his language those are the same word." Tamsin turned their hands over, studying the palms as though the answer might be written there. "Beatrix, Eastwick's is on the list. I saw it. Third from the top, right under the two shops already burned. Your inventory is scheduled for — " they hesitated, and I learned to hate that hesitation — "the day after tomorrow."

The fire crackled. Somewhere out on the river, the ice groaned again, long and low, a sound like a door being forced.

"So we move tomorrow night," I said.

"On what?" Tamsin's voice cracked upward, and I realized with a small cold shock that they were frightened — properly frightened, not the managed fear they wore like their uniform. "There are soldiers on the quay. There are soldiers across your lane. The barge can't take on cargo without a manifest, and a manifest requires Crane's seal, and the only way you get Crane's seal is by giving Crane the plates."

"Then we don't use the quay."

"Which leaves the bridge, the roads, or a miracle."

"I've never much cared for bridges." I pulled the candle closer and laid it out for them the way I'd been turning it over all day, the plan that had been assembling itself in the back of my skull with the slow inevitability of ice shifting on a pier. "Voss's barge isn't the only boat on this river. Down past the tannery, where the old slaughter channel meets the main current, there's a stretch of bank nobody watches because nothing official happens there. Before the port seal, Oskar mentioned a friend — a eel fisher, winter-lines under the ice, works the channel at night. If the ice breaks early, if the channel opens first the way it always does because the current runs warmer under the tannery outflow —"

"A rowboat," Tamsin said. "You're proposing to move forty pounds of lead plates down a freezing river in the dark, in flood conditions, in a rowboat."

"When you say it like that it sounds reckless."

"How else would you like me to say it?"

"With more admiration for the eel fisher."

Tamsin laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly smothered, the laugh of a person remembering where they were and what was coming. Then they stopped laughing altogether, and what replaced it was worse.

"There's something else," they said. "About the day after tomorrow. The inventory." They were looking at their hands again. "Crane assigned it to me."

The candle flame leaned in the draft. Neither of us moved to shield it.

"He suspects you," I said.

"He suspects everyone; it's his profession. But this is more than suspicion. He gave me the Eastwick inventory specifically. He watched my face when he said it. Beatrix, either he knows and he's setting a trap, or he doesn't know and he's handing me the keys to the shop's official record — which means whatever I write down becomes the truth that every future clerk inherits." Tamsin looked up. "If I list the plates as present, they're seized and melted and everything we've done dies in a ministry furnace. If I list them as absent, and he checks —"

"He'll check."

"He'll check."

Outside, the ice groaned. Closer now, or perhaps only louder — the whole river talking in its sleep, counting down. Three days, the bargemen said. Maybe less.

* * *

We argued until two in the morning, which is what people do when they are frightened and fond of each other and out of options in roughly equal measure. Tamsin wanted to forge the inventory outright — list the plates as scrap lead, sold for melting, a tidy bureaucratic fiction. I pointed out that Crane had personally inventoried my father's estate after his death; he would know to the pound what lead should be in this building. Tamsin proposed delaying the inventory through clerical maneuver — misfiled requests, a forged quarantine notice, anything to buy another week. I pointed out that Crane did not lose weeks; he collected them, like a man collecting debts.

What we finally agreed on, somewhere around the second candle, was that we needed the forgery to be perfect and we needed a distraction worthy of the risk, and that neither of us knew yet what shape either would take. It was not a plan. It was a place to stand while the plan arrived. But Tamsin took my hand across the type cabinet — briefly, firmly, the grip of someone passing weight rather than affection, though I suspected it was both — and said, "We've done impossible things before. With worse lighting."

After they left I stood at the window a long time, watching the soldiers' brazier spark and gutter across the lane, listening to the river.

It was louder now. Not the occasional mutter of a week ago but continuous — cracks and groans and sudden sharp reports like distant gunfire, the floes grinding against the pilings, the whole frozen surface of the city's great artery straining toward its own release. The bargemen said days. The ice said otherwise. Ice, I thought, keeps no census. It breaks when it breaks, and it does not file its intentions with anyone.

And that was when the thought arrived — the plan, the real one, walking in through the back door of my mind the way the best ones always do, dressed as a complaint.

The ice would break when it broke. Crane had built his wall of seals and manifests and soldiers on the assumption that the river would cooperate with his schedule. He had assumed the deadline. Everyone had. Three days, four, a neat bureaucratic interval in which to conduct inventories and set traps.

But the river had never once in the history of Vellham respected a schedule drawn up by the Ministry of Historical Accuracy.

If the thaw came early — if the channel opened tomorrow instead of Thursday — then every plan on both sides of this quiet war became suddenly, catastrophically obsolete. Crane's trap would be sprung on an empty stage. His inventory would catalog a shop whose secrets were already sliding downriver in the dark. All his walls, all his patient closing circles, and the one thing he couldn't seal was the weather.

We couldn't beat his deadline. So we would have to steal it.

I woke Wren, who slept in the loft above the press these days, wrapped in a printer's apron and claiming it was comfortable, which no one who had ever touched an apron could believe. She came down the ladder with her hair full of lint and her eyes already fully awake, because Wren had never in her life required more than half a waking.

"The ice," I said. "How fast can you find out what the eel fisher charges, and whether his boat holds forty pounds of lead and one nervous woman?"

She grinned at me in the candlelight, and for a moment she looked so much like her father must have looked at that age — the circle's youngest member, the inheritor of other people's dangerous truths — that I had to look away and busy myself with the banked fire.

"Give me until dawn," she said.

Out on the river, the ice groaned — long, cracking, impatient — and for the first time since Crane's seals went up, the sound sounded less like a warning and more like an answer.
