## 29. Fire as Diversion

Wren came back before dawn with frost in her eyelashes and a look on her face that I had learned to distrust — the look of a person who has found an answer and has not yet thought through what the answer will cost.

"The eel fisher's name is Hobb," she said, stamping snow off her boots in the doorway of the back room. "He wants payment in salt pork and silence, both of which we have. His boat is ugly enough that nobody will look at it twice, which is worth more than seaworthiness. And—" here she paused, and I knew, I *knew*, what was coming— "there's a warehouse on Coal Lane. Empty since autumn. The Marlow shipping concern went bankrupt and left it full of packing straw and tar barrels."

I kept my hands on the kettle. "And?"

"And it would burn beautifully."

The kettle sang. I took it off the fire and poured, because pouring water is a thing you can do while your stomach turns over, and a woman who hesitates over a teapot tells everyone in the room what she is thinking.

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard the rest."

"I don't need to. You want to set a fire in a city full of soldiers who are already jumpy about pamphlets and sedition, three streets from the port Crane has sealed. Wren, they'll come running to put it out, yes. They'll also come running to find out who lit it."

"They won't find anyone." She sat down across from me, and I saw that her hands were not steady, which was its own kind of honesty. "That's the whole design. Hobb explained it — he ran cargo during the flour riots, he knows how garrisons think. A fire draws every man with a bucket for half a mile. The checkpoint at Tanner's Stair gets abandoned, because the captain there will want his soldiers visible, saving things. The barge goes through at slack of the watch. And by the time anyone counts heads, we're all elsewhere."

"Elsewhere being?"

"Being nowhere in particular," she said, with the serene vagueness of the very young, and I thought: your father must have worn exactly that expression when he told Aldous Crane's predecessors that no, he had never seen a seditious word cross this shop, and God rest him, he was better at it than either of us.

I drank my tea. Outside, the ice groaned again, longer this time, a sound like a door being forced somewhere beneath the world.

"Who lights it?" I asked finally, which was a surrender dressed as a question.

She looked at me across the table, and her chin came up, and I understood that this had been decided before she ever walked through my door.

* * *

We argued about it for two days. That is not quite accurate. *I* argued about it for two days, and Wren received my arguments the way a wharf post receives the tide — standing firm, unmoved, occasionally submerged but never displaced.

"It should be someone quick," said Tamsin, who had come to the shop on the second evening wearing a borrowed coat and the expression of a person doing arithmetic they don't like. "Someone small, someone known in the district as harmless. Someone who could be seen stumbling home from a tavern if the worst happened."

"Then it should be me," I said. "I am famously harmless. Ask anyone."

"Ask anyone," Tamsin repeated gently, "and they'll tell them the Eastwick woman hasn't been drunk or careless in her whole life. Your reputation protects you, Beatrix, but only if you don't spend it. And you're needed ashore anyway — you're the one who knows the plates' wrapping order, you're the one Hobb trusts, you're—" they stopped, and their ears went pink, and they finished gruffly, "you're the one who can't be spared. That's all."

"That's all," I agreed, and pretended not to notice that we were both looking anywhere but at each other.

It was the tavern-keeper, old Sorrel, who named the real problem, at the council we held in his back room among the barrels, our breath fogging in the cold. "The girl means to do it herself," he said, not asking. "You can see it sitting on her like a hat."

"She made one mistake," I said. "One broadside printed too soon. She's paid for it in nightmares; I've heard her pay for it."

"Which is why she'll fight you on this," Sorrel said, "and why she'll win. A body needs to burn something down sometimes, Miss Eastwick. Better a warehouse than herself." He turned his glass slowly on the table. "I lit fires in the dock riots of my youth. I know what I'm talking about, and what I'm telling you is: don't stand between a young person and her redemption. Stand behind her instead. Where you can catch her."

So we did not forbid it. We did something harder, which was to help.

* * *

Coal Lane ran behind the tanneries, a mean street of shuttered warehouses and the sort of mud that remembers every foot that ever crossed it. The Marlow warehouse had a loading door facing the lane and a coal chute facing the river stair, and it was full, per Wren's reconnaissance, of straw, broken crates, and eleven tar barrels that the bankruptcy trustees had never bothered to sell.

Hobb walked us through it on the last night, moving with the unhurried certainty of a man who has been trespassing professionally for forty years. He was perhaps sixty, built like a mooring post, with a face so ordinary that I forgot it twice in the first hour.

"Fire wants three things," he said, crouching by the coal chute. "Feed, air, and a start. We've got feed enough to burn till Thursday. Air comes free through these chutes — see how they face the water? Wind off the ice funnels straight up into the building. All we need is a start, placed right, timed right." He tapped the floorboards. "Here. Against the tar barrels, upwind side. It'll take hold slow for a quarter hour, then all at once, and once it's all at once nobody puts it out until the walls come down. Which," he added, "is the point. A fire they can extinguish is a fire they ask questions after. A fire that eats a warehouse whole — that's just weather happening to a building."

"And the garrison?" Tamsin asked.

"Ah." Hobb smiled, showing teeth stained the brown of strong tea. "The garrison is the easiest part, and that's the truth of it. Soldiers are men, and men are frightened of two things: drowning and burning. Give them a fire and every man jack of them becomes a hero, or wants to be seen trying. Captain Voss at Tanner's Stair — I've watched him twenty years — he'll pull his pickets the moment the smoke shows, because he'd rather explain an open checkpoint than a burnt street. It's not even courage or cowardice. It's arithmetic."

"When?" I asked.

Hobb looked out the chute at the river. The ice had gone grey-green and rotten-looking at the edges, and the sound it made now was constant, a low muttering like a debtor outside a door.

"Tomorrow night," he said. "Wind's coming round northerly. And the night after, the ice goes — any bargeman can hear it coming — and after the ice goes, the port opens whether Crane likes it or not, and then we're smuggling instead of escaping, and smuggling is slower." He spat, expertly, into the dark. "Tomorrow night. Slack watch, moonless. The girl lights it at the second bell, and we're through the stair by the third."

* * *

I did not sleep that day. I sorted type I had already sorted. I oiled the press screw until it gleamed like a black bone. I wrapped and re-wrapped the master plates in oilcloth until Tamsin, arriving at dusk, took the bundle gently out of my hands and said, "They're fine. They've been fine for an hour."

"She shouldn't be the one to light it," I said.

"No," Tamsin agreed.

"Say it like you mean it."

"I mean it like this: she shouldn't, and she will, and the difference between those two facts is called being twenty years old, and neither of us survived our twenties by listening to older people." They set the plates down inside the false bottom of the type cabinet, carefully, the way one sets down anything breakable. "My mother wanted me apprenticed to a candlemaker. Safe trade, respectable. Instead I took a clerkship counting other people's houses, and I have spent six years telling myself it was prudence rather than cowardice." They looked up. "If Wren doesn't light that fire, she'll spend the rest of her life telling herself a story too. Hers will be worse than mine. Mine at least had a salary."

I laughed despite myself, a short ugly sound. "When did you become the philosopher of this operation?"

"About the time I started burning government documents," Tamsin said. "It loosens a person up."

* * *

The second bell came at half past eleven, and with it the wind, rolling off the river flat and cold and smelling of ice.

We were stationed where Hobb had put us: Sorrel at his tavern with a clear view of Coal Lane's mouth, playing the concerned publican with his apron on and his buckets staged; Hobb and two of the bargemen at the Tanner's Stair landing, minding the boat; Tamsin and I wedged in the doorway of a closed chandlery two streets off, close enough to see the corner of the warehouse, far enough to be furniture if anyone looked.

And Wren, somewhere on the roofline above Coal Lane, with a lantern, a tinderbox, a pot of pitch, and eleven barrels of her own private absolution.

I had kissed her forehead before she went, which embarrassed us both enormously, and told her the only useful thing I knew: "Your father set type for truths that got men hanged. He didn't do it because he was fearless. He did it and then shook afterward, Sorrel says. Shaking afterward is allowed. Shaking *before* is also allowed. Just don't stop moving."

"I won't," she'd said, and gone up the drainpipe like smoke going backward.

Now we waited. Waiting is the true trade of everyone in this story — printers wait for ink to dry, clerks wait for signatures, cities wait for spring — but waiting with a purpose is a different animal from waiting without one, and I found my heart behaving less like a heart and more like a fist knocking steadily on the inside of my ribs.

"Tell me something boring," Tamsin whispered. "It helps. That's a professional observation."

"The price of gallnuts has doubled since the blockade," I said. "A good lampblack costs more than a bad dinner. My father used to cut his own quoins out of pearwood because he said bought ones were soft as clergy." Tamsin's shoulder shook against mine. "There's a knot in the press frame that I've been meaning to plane for four years and now I never will, and I find I mind that more than the shop itself."

"That's not boring."

"It's the truest thing I own."

Across the roofs, a light moved. Once. Twice. Three times — the signal, Hobb's daughter's signal, the signal Wren had practiced in Sorrel's cellar until we all dreamed in flashes.

"Second bell," Tamsin breathed.

We waited. One minute. Two. Five. The wind came off the river and I counted my own breaths and thought absurdly of the smell of pearwood shavings, and then—

Smoke. Not much of it at first, a thin grey thread rising off the warehouse roof like something escaping, and then a second thread, and then the threads leaned together in the wind and thickened, and a dull orange bloom opened behind the high windows like a flower deciding quickly.

"There," Tamsin said softly. "Oh, she did it."

The city woke the way a body wakes to pain — in stages, then all at once. First a dog. Then a window. Then the watch bell on Corn Exchange began hammering, and boots hit cobbles in a dozen directions, and torchlight bloomed along Coal Lane like fever spots.

What happened next I can only describe as arithmetic becoming visible. The garrison had spent weeks being an occupying force, which is to say a machine for standing still. Now it became a bucket line. Soldiers came at a run — actual running, helmets crooked, carrying each other's coats — and behind them came half the neighborhood, because a fire is the one spectacle a curfew cannot suppress, and because Vellham has always loved a disaster it isn't personally having.

"Tanner's Stair," I said.

"Voss will pull them any moment—"

He pulled them sooner. We saw the pickets at the river checkpoint gather, hesitate, and then go pelting toward the glow, their officer waving them on with the frantic generosity of a man who has decided tonight's history will remember him kindly. The stair stood empty. The lanterns there swung unattended in the wind.

Down at the landing, almost invisible against the black water, a shape detached itself from the shadows and slid out onto the ice-strewn current: Hobb's ugly boat, riding low, forty pounds of lead wrapped in oilcloth and one eel fisher who asked for nothing but salt pork and silence, and got, I hoped, a great deal more than he bargained for.

But no Wren.

"Wren," I said, and was moving before Tamsin caught my sleeve.

"The plan—"

"The plan was the second bell. It's nearly the third." I pulled free. "She lit it from the roof, Tamsin. The roof of a building that's about to be a chimney."

* * *

I will say this for panic: it makes the legs honest. I ran streets I had walked cautiously for thirty-one years, vaulted a cart, took the corner onto Coal Lane at a speed that would have scandalized every version of myself I had ever presented to the world, and stopped dead in the crowd's edge with heat on my face like an open oven door.

The warehouse burned the way Hobb had promised — wholly, magnificently, tar barrels going up one after another with soft whumping reports that the crowd received with gasps and, I am sorry to report, applause. Firemen and soldiers formed their lines and threw their water, and the water rose off the hot walls as steam, and the whole street filled with a white choking fog shot through with orange, biblical, terrible, beautiful.

And on the roof of the chandlery next door — not the warehouse, the *chandlery*, because Wren had more sense than any of us gave her credit for — a small figure sat with her knees drawn up, soot-faced, watching her work.

I climbed the drainpipe badly. She didn't help me over the parapet, exactly; she mostly kept me from dying, which was generous under the circumstances.

"You went the wrong way," I shouted over the roar.

"I went the *safe* way!" she shouted back. "You think I'd light a fire and stand on it? Who taught you planning, the Ministry?"

Below us, the crowd roared as a wall section fell inward in a fountain of sparks. Somewhere in that chaos, a barge was slipping through an abandoned checkpoint, carrying everything my father had buried and I had dug up and all of us had nearly died for. I felt it go the way you feel a tooth leave — absence, sudden and clean.

"Redeemed?" I asked her.

She considered the flames with the serious attention she gave to type cases. "No," she said at last. "But I'm on the way. There's a difference. Redemption's a journey, Sorrel says."

"Sorrel says a lot of things. Some of them are even wise."

"He charged me for the pitch," she said, "and then gave it back after. That's how you know."

* * *

We came down separately, as planned, and met at Sorrel's among the bucket-brigade stragglers drinking his brandy at heroic prices, which he donated entirely to the cause of looking busy. Tamsin arrived twenty minutes later, coat scorched at one cuff, having spent the interval being loudly helpful in ways that established them, permanently, as a civic-minded census clerk who had heroically assisted at a fire.

"The boat's through," they murmured into their cup. "Hobb signaled from the bend — three lanterns. He says the floes are thinning near the middle channel. Two days, maybe three, and the main channel opens."

"Two days," I said.

"In which Crane learns his checkpoint was empty for an hour, counts his warehouses, and starts wondering what else in this city is missing." Tamsin turned the cup slowly. "Beatrix. When he wonders — and he will wonder fast, he's not a fool, whatever else he is — whose name sits at the center of every answer he finds?"

I looked at my hands, ink-stained, soot-streaked, steady for the first time all night.

"Mine," I said. "It always was. Now it's official."

Outside, past Sorrel's smoky windows, the warehouse collapsed in a long exhalation of sparks, and the crowd sighed, and the ice on the river groaned — closer now, louder, a sound like the whole city clearing its throat to speak.

Two days. And fifty copies yet to print.
