## 30. Loading the Barge

The smoke came first, as smoke always does — ahead of the fire itself, rolling down Tanner's Row like a rumor that had gotten out of hand. I stood in the doorway of the print shop and watched it climb the eastern sky, a great grey column leaning against the wind, and I thought: *Wren, you magnificent fool, you lit it.*

She had insisted. After the broadside, after the soldiers pounding up our stairs while I lay to a captain with my hands busy in a forme of type, Wren had carried her mistake like a stone in her chest for three weeks. Now she'd asked for the torch herself, and the circle — being the fractious, sentimental lot that it was — had let her.

"Controlled," she'd said at the tavern council, chin up, daring anyone to laugh. "An empty warehouse. No one hurt. The garrison will send every man they've got."

Old Hask, the tavern-keeper, had sucked his teeth. "And when it spreads?"

"It won't."

"And if it does?"

"Then Vellham burns," Wren said, "which it was going to do anyway, one way or another."

Nobody had laughed. That was the thing about our circle — we were far past laughing.

* * *

Now the hour was upon us, and I discovered that all my careful plans looked very different when viewed through actual smoke instead of the tidy smoke of imagination.

The plan, as devised: the garrison would pour toward the burning warehouse on Cooper's Lane. The checkpoint at Saltwharf would thin to a skeleton crew. And in that window — Tamsin estimated half an hour, maybe less — the plates would go from the tannery cellar to the river, into a rowboat, and out to the *Marguerite*, a coal barge whose captain had agreed to carry forty crates of "type metal scrap" downriver for the last of the circle's money.

The last of the money. I want that on the record too: we spent everything. Hask emptied the tavern strongbox. The blind compositor, Merrick, contributed savings he'd kept in a boot for eleven years, and when I tried to refuse he said, "Girl, I can't see what they do with this city. Let me at least buy its memory a boat ride." Captain Oduya of the *Marguerite* took the purse, weighed it in his hand, and said, "This buys silence, not heroism. If soldiers board me, I'll weep and cooperate beautifully."

"Good," Tamsin had said. "Weeping is convincing. Heroism gets people shot."

That was Tamsin all over now — the census clerk's caution repurposed, every skill once used for counting heads turned to keeping them attached.

* * *

The tannery cellar smelled the way it always did, of hides and lime and old salt, but tonight I barely noticed it. Lamplight. Forty crates, each lined with wool scavenged from three different houses so no single merchant could be traced by his cloth. Each plate wrapped in oilskin, packed face-to-face so the letters wouldn't bite each other. I had packed them myself over four sleepless nights, and my hands knew each one the way a mother knows sleeping children — the heavy opening signature with the governor's name spelled out beside the word *murders*, the pages of testimony from Stonecell Prison, the list of the disappeared that ran to two hundred and eleven names and stopped only because the paper stopped, not because the disappearing did.

My father had made these plates in secret, in the years I thought he was only mending the press late at night. He had hidden them where no one would look until I looked properly. And for three days they had existed in my hands as something more than lead — they were the whole argument of my life, the proof that the quiet man in the quiet shop had been loud all along, in the only way that mattered.

"Beatrix." Tamsin stood in the cellar door, still in the grey ministry coat, which they had stopped wearing on duty and now wore like armor anyway. "The fire's taken the warehouse roof. Garrison's moving. We have our window."

"How long?"

"However long Oduya's patience lasts. Which is to say: move."

There were six of us in the cellar — me, Tamsin, Wren (back from Cooper's Lane, smelling of pitch and triumph), Hask, Merrick guided on Wren's arm, and the exiled foreman, Corvin, who had betrayed us and been forgiven and now carried crates like a man doing penance in lead.

We went out the tannery's back gate, down the stinking alley to the water stairs, and there was the rowboat, and there was the river — black, moving sluggishly under its last skin of ice, floes grinding somewhere out in the dark like millstones.

I want to say I felt noble. What I actually felt was cold, and terrified, and absurdly aware that my boots were leaking.

* * *

Loading a rowboat with forty crates of lead plates is not a romantic operation. It is arithmetic. Weight distribution, gunwale height, the current's pull, the distance to the barge — perhaps two hundred yards of open water, black as ink, dotted with drifting ice.

"Two runs," Corvin said. "Twenty crates each. Any more and we ship water."

"One run," I said.

Everyone looked at me.

"The garrison won't stay chasing smoke forever. One run, low in the water, straight across. I've poached eels off these stairs since I was nine; I know how she sits."

"You're not coming?" Wren said.

"No."

It came out steadier than it had any right to. I had decided it an hour ago, alone, packing the last crate, and I hadn't told anyone because telling makes things negotiable, and some things mustn't be.

"The press," I said. "The press stays here, and so do I. There's time tonight — the smoke buys us hours, maybe till dawn — and I'm going to print. Fifty copies. On Father's press, with Father's type, before they burn it or take it. The plates go downriver to be safe. But safe isn't enough. A record nobody's read is just lead with opinions. There have to be copies in hands. In pockets. Sewn into coats, as we planned."

Tamsin watched me with that expression I had learned to read over the winter — the clerk's face, cataloguing, except what was being catalogued now was grief.

"Fifty copies," they said. "On a hand press. Alone."

"I'm faster than I look."

"You're fast because your father drilled you," Tamsin said. "You told me. Pull, wipe, set, pull — four hundred an hour when he was angry about it."

"He was often angry about it."

"So. Two hours of printing, minimum, if nothing goes wrong, plus composition, plus—" They stopped themselves. I watched them decide not to argue. It cost them visibly. "Then I'm staying too."

"No," I said. "You go with the plates. If the barge is boarded, they need someone who can produce documents, forge a manifest, lie in ministerial grammar. You're the best liar I know, and I mean that as the highest compliment available to either of us."

Something moved across Tamsin's face — pride and horror sharing a carriage. "You've changed," they said quietly. "When I met you, you couldn't look a stranger in the eye."

"I still can't. That's why the plates need you looking for me."

Merrick cleared his throat. He couldn't see the river, but he could hear it, the way blind men hear everything twice. "The ice is talking," he said. "Listen. She's going soon — a day, maybe two. Whatever you're doing ashore, girl, do it before the river does it first."

We loaded the boat. Twenty crates, then twenty more, wedged and roped, sitting so low that the first swirl of river licked over the gunwale and soaked the hem of Hask's coat. He swore with the fluency of a man who kept a tavern, and then blessed the boat with the same mouth, which seemed fair.

Corvin took the oars. Of course he did. Nobody asked him to; he simply took them, and I understood that this was the rest of his penance — to pull these crates across black water while the city he'd helped betray slept and smoked behind him.

"Go," I said.

They went. I stood on the water stairs and watched the boat dwindle — a dark shape among darker shapes, the oars dipping silent, the floes parting around it like bad manners around a bishop. Somewhere out on the water a piece of ice groaned, long and low, the sound I had been hearing in my dreams since December.

And then the *Marguerite*'s lantern swung twice — the signal — and men were hauling crates aboard, and Oduya's voice drifted across the water, mild and profane, and it was done. Or begun. I honestly didn't know which.

Wren put her hand on my shoulder. "Come on," she said. "You've got fifty truths to print and I've got ink to mix."

* * *

The shop at midnight, with the city burning politely two districts over, was the strangest room I have ever worked in.

Through the windows, the glow on the eastern sky turned everything amber — the type cases, the composing stick, my own hands. Outside, bells began to ring, the fire bells, and boots went past in the lane, running east, always east, away from us. Wren had bought us the whole night with one well-behaved warehouse fire.

I set the forme by feel and lamplight. The opening page — the one my father had composed, the one I knew letter by letter now better than any prayer:

*To the citizens of Vellham, and to whoever comes after: this is what happened.*

Then the governor's name. Then the first date. Then the first dead man's name, Tomas Reele, dyer, hanged without trial on the ninth of October, and the testimony of the woman who cut him down.

Pull. Wipe. Set. Pull.

The press spoke its old language under my hands — the groan of the screw, the kiss of platen on paper, the small wet sound of fresh ink. Four hundred an hour when Father was angry about it. Tonight I managed perhaps two hundred, because my eyes kept blurring, and I let them, because there was no captain to lie to here, no one watching but the ghost of a quiet man who had trusted me with everything and never said so out loud.

Halfway through the run, Wren touched my elbow. "Someone's in the lane."

We blew out the lamp and stood in the dark, listening. Boots — but slow ones, unhurried, and then a soft double knock, our knock, the circle's knock.

Tamsin.

I opened the door and they slipped in smelling of river and coal smoke, hair wild, ministry coat gone, replaced by a stevedore's jacket that fooled absolutely no one.

"They're aboard," Tamsin said. "Oduya cast off ten minutes ago. He'll hold at the moorings below the bridge until the ice breaks, then run with the floes." They were breathing hard, and grinning, actually grinning, which I had seen perhaps three times all winter. "Beatrix. They're *away*. Forty crates of the truth, floating downriver under a cargo manifest that says 'scrap lead, 640 lbs, for bell-founding.' I wrote 'bell-founding.' I want credit for that forever."

"You'll get it," I said. "Page forty-one, acknowledgments."

That was when I saw their face change.

"What?"

"The port," Tamsin said. "Crane's sealed it. Ordered this afternoon — 'pending historical review.' Every exit watched. The bridge, Saltwharf, the ferry landings. Soldiers on the ice road itself, checking barges." They paused. "I got past the checkpoint because I showed papers I forged last week and the sergeant was watching the fire like everyone else. But the *Marguerite* has to pass the bridge boom when the ice goes. And Crane will be waiting for anything that moves."

The press stood warm beside us. Fifty copies, thirty printed. My father's type gleamed faintly in the firelight from the east.

"Well," I said, and heard my own voice come out level, which surprised me, "then we'd better finish these before the river does."

Outside, far off under the grinding of the ice, the first floe cracked loose and went whispering past the water stairs, heading for the bridge, and whatever waited there.
