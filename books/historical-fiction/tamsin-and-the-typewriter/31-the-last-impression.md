## 31. The Last Impression

There is a particular hour in a print shop — I have known it my whole life and never had occasion to name it until now — when the oil in the lamp burns low enough that the type stops looking like metal and starts looking like language again. Not letters to be sorted, not lead to be weighed, but words. The hour when the forme stops being work and becomes a sentence.

It was that hour when I finally set the last line.

Fifty copies. That was the number Wren and I had agreed on, argued over, and settled on somewhere past midnight with the arithmetic of desperate people: enough copies to matter, few enough to finish before the garrison finished us. Fifty copies of the true account of the Vellham uprising, set from the master plates my father had hidden in the bed of his own press like a man burying his heart under the hearthstone and then living on top of it for twenty years.

I had been composing since dawn. My fingers knew the case better than they knew my own face; I could find an *f* blind, and frequently did, because grief makes you clumsy and I had been grieving all day in small installments.

"You're doing it again," Wren said from the corner, where she was stacking paper with the exaggerated care of someone who has been told twice not to hurry.

"Doing what?"

"Talking to the type."

"I was not talking to the type."

"You said 'hold still' to a *k*."

"It was crooked."

Wren looked at me over the paper stack with that expression she had perfected — fifteen years old, orphaned by the same city that had orphaned me in slower ways, and convinced she was the only sane person in any room. "Everything's crooked tonight," she said. "The *k* is fine. You just wanted something to fix."

She was right, which was irritating, which was usual. There is a comfort in straightening a crooked letter that no sermon and no solace can match. You see what's wrong, you correct it, and the world is briefly, measurably better. I had spent my whole life hiding inside that comfort. My father used to say I mended formes the way other people prayed. He meant it kindly. He also meant it as a diagnosis.

Tonight there would be no more fixing after this. Tonight the shop itself was the crooked thing, and no hand could true it up.

* * *

Let me tell you what a print shop sounds like when it knows it is dying. It doesn't. That's the thing. It sounds exactly as it always does — the tick of cooling metal, the sigh of the press rope, the wind finding the gap in the shutters it has always found. The shop has no more sense of its own ending than a man humming at his work has of the year of his death. All the doom in the room was mine, and I carried it around like a full ink ball, careful not to spill it on anything that mattered.

We had moved everything that mattered already. The plates were gone — rowed out through the smoke two nights ago to a coal barge whose captain took our money and our silence in equal measure. The ledger was gone, sewn into the coat lining of a tavern-keeper who had once watered my father's beer and never once been forgiven for it aloud. The circle had scattered along routes drawn in candle soot on the back of a funeral bill. What remained in Eastwick's was furniture, fuel, and one press that had outlived its maker and was about to outlive its home.

And me. And Wren. And fifty copies yet unprinted.

"The paper's damp," Wren said, bringing the first stack to the stone. "I wetted it an hour ago, like you showed me."

"You did it right?"

"I did everything right. You checked."

"I'm checking again."

"You always check again." She said it without rancor. She had learned my liturgies well enough to recite them back to me, which is either apprenticeship or mockery, and with Wren it was both.

Dampening the paper is the humblest part of the craft and the part most people skip, and their work shows it. Dry paper takes ink the way a proud man takes advice — superficially, and only where it's pushed hardest. Damp paper receives. My father taught me that when I was seven, standing me on a crate so I could reach the water trough. *The paper has to want the ink, Bee,* he'd said. *You can't force a page to hold something. It has to open to it.* At seven I thought he was talking about paper. I am still not entirely sure he wasn't talking about me.

I laid the first sheet against the tympan. My hands were steady. I note this because it surprised me. I had expected them to shake — the hands of a woman committing treason by lamplight — but they were as steady as they'd ever been pulling a proof of a wedding invitation or a bill of lading. Perhaps that is the whole secret of the trade, passed down through every printer who ever lived: the work steadies the worker. You cannot tremble and pull a clean impression at the same time. The press will not have it.

I pulled the bar.

The press spoke. It has one word and says it often — that deep wooden *thock*, half heartbeat, half door closing — and tonight it sounded less like machinery than like a voice answering. *Here. Still here. Say your piece.*

The sheet came off the platen. I held it to the lamp.

Black ink on white paper, crisp as frost. The opening line of the account, the one my father had composed in secret while the governor's men drank toasts three streets away: *In the winter of the ice, the people of Vellham learned that the river keeps every record the city tries to drown.*

"Oh," Wren said softly.

"Yes," I said.

"That's —"

"I know what it is."

Because here is what I had not told her, what I could hardly tell myself: I had read these pages a hundred times since we found the plates, and every reading had been preparation, study, reconnaissance. This was the first time they were becoming a book. Ink into paper. Secret into object. Something that could be handed from one living hand to another, and would go on being handed long after every hand in this room was cold. A pamphlet is a small thing. A pamphlet is also the most durable weapon ever devised, because you cannot arrest a piece of paper once ten thousand people have read it. You can burn the shop. You cannot burn the reading.

"Again," Wren said, and there was something new in her voice — not eagerness, exactly. Reverence with its sleeves rolled up.

"Again," I agreed.

* * *

We printed through the night, and I will not pretend it was solemn. Printing never is. It is repetitive, physical, faintly absurd work — lay, pull, lift, stack, lay, pull, lift — and by the twentieth sheet Wren was singing under her breath some dockside song about a bargeman's wife, and I was letting her, because the alternative was silence, and silence in a room with a working press feels like mourning, and I had decided I was done mourning in advance. Whatever tears were owed for this place, I would pay them after, at the fire, at the proper hour. While the press ran, the shop was alive, and I refuse to hold a wake for the living.

Somewhere around the thirtieth copy my arms found the old rhythm, the one my father's arms must have found, and his father's before him if the family stories are true — the rhythm where the body works and the mind goes walking. And my mind went walking to strange places. To the census taker, Tamsin, who had sat on that very stool by the window with ink-stained cuffs and too many questions, and who was at this hour, God willing, misfiling documents with the serene face of a person who has never once misfiled anything. To the blind compositor, Josiah, who had felt the face of every letter in this case with his fingertips and could probably set this entire account faster than I could, and who was at this moment somewhere south of the walls trusting his life to strangers because we had asked him to. To the foreman, Halloran, broken and half-mended, running his decoy errands through the snow with soldiers' eyes on his back. To my father, everywhere, in the grain of the wood and the wear on the bar and the specific way the friskin pins needed a quarter-turn to sit true, a quirk I had never seen in another press and understood now, finally, as a signature.

He had built his confession into the machine. He had trusted the daughter everyone called quiet to find it. And I had spent thirty years believing myself invisible, when in fact I had simply been unread — and there is a difference, and the difference is a press.

"Bee." Wren's voice, careful. "Sheet forty-eight came out light on the left margin. Do I redo it?"

I crossed the room and looked. She was right — the left margin pale, the ink starved, the impression uneven. A lesser flaw. A flaw nobody but a printer would ever notice.

"Redo it," I said.

"It'll cost us time."

"It'll cost us truth if we don't. If we let one sloppy page through, someone someday will use that page to call the whole account a forgery. They'll say, *look, the margins fade, the work is careless, how do we know the names are right?*" I heard my own voice harden. "Every page has to be beyond argument. That's the whole point of what we're doing. We're not making a plea. We're making a record. Records don't get to be tired."

Wren looked at me for a long moment, and then she nodded, and pulled the sheet again, and it was perfect.

That is the other thing I understood that night, standing in my father's shop with his press under my hands and his enemy's torches presumably being discussed in some warm office across town: inheritance isn't what you're given. It's what you insist on. He left me a shop and I am watching it burn tomorrow. He left me a name and I hid behind it for years. But he left me a standard — *every page beyond argument* — and that travels. That fits in a pocket. That survives fire.

* * *

The forty-ninth copy came off clean. The fiftieth.

And then the press was silent, and the silence was enormous, and Wren and I stood in it looking at the stack of fifty sheets like two people looking at a boat they've built without quite believing it will float.

"Now," Wren said. "Now the colophon. You promised."

"I promised nothing of the sort."

"You said, and I quote, *when the copies are done, I'll decide about the name.* The copies are done. Decide."

I had been deciding all night, actually, in the walking parts of my mind, and the decision had made itself somewhere around sheet twenty-five, quietly, the way all my real decisions make themselves — not with thunder but with a click, like a letter seating into its notch.

The account ended with a list. Of course it did. My father had ended it with the names of the disappeared — forty-one men and women taken by the governor's men in the autumn of the ice, their bodies unreturned, their files burned, their existence officially converted into rumor. The last line of the text read: *Set down in Vellham, in the shop on Cooper's Lane, that these names might outlast the men who erased them.*

Beneath that, on the master plate, in type my father had cut himself — I knew the hand now, the slightly narrow *E*, the ligature he kerned too tight out of habit rather than economy — was his name. **Edmund Eastwick, Printer.**

One name. One man credited with a document that had been written by a circle, guarded by a circle, smuggled by a circle, and would be carried forward by a circle. He had signed alone because he had died alone in a locked shop, and the dead sign alone whether they want to or not.

But I was not dead. Not yet. And standing there with ink to my elbows and fifty perfect copies breathing on the stone, I found I was done letting other people — the living and the dead alike — decide what my name was attached to.

"Wren. Bring me the jobbing case. The small caps."

Her eyes went wide. "You're going to —"

"I'm going to add a line. Hand-set. On each copy."

"Fifty times? By hand? Bee, that's hours, and Crane's men —"

"Crane's men can wait for history. History has been waiting for me for thirty years; it can stand the reverse for a few hours more."

So we did it. I set the line once, in twelve-point small caps, locked it in a little chase barely bigger than my palm, and then — because a locked line can't print on a bound book, and these were loose sheets — I printed it separately, fifty impressions, one for each copy, to be tipped in below my father's name. Lay, pull, lift. Lay, pull, lift. Each impression identical. Each one mine.

The line read:

**AND BEATRIX EASTWICK, HIS DAUGHTER, WHO FOUND IT.**

Not *who finished it*. Not *who preserved it*. Found. Because that was the truest word and I have never much trusted the flattering ones. He wrote it; I found it; between us and a hundred others, the thing got out alive. Authorship, I have come to believe, is mostly retrieval. Someone has to be the one who refuses to let the truth stay buried, and that someone deserves ink as much as the one who first set it down.

On the tenth impression my eyes blurred and I had to stop and wipe them with the back of my wrist, and Wren pretended very hard to be checking the dampness of paper that needed no checking, and neither of us said anything about it, because we were printers, and printers know which flaws matter and which don't.

On the fiftieth impression my hands were perfectly steady again.

* * *

Near dawn we collated. Fifty copies, folded, stitched with linen thread Wren had unpicked from her own spare shirt, which struck me as exactly the right kind of sacrifice — the kind nobody notices and everybody benefits from. We sewed nine of them into clothing: Wren's coat, my petticoat hem, the lining of a satchel bound for the tavern route. The rest went into the false-bottom cabinet, into flour sacks, into the one place no soldier searches thoroughly because no soldier has ever respected it — a widow's prayer book box, which held forty-one thin pamphlets snug as psalms.

Then there was one copy left over. Fifty-one, it turned out; Wren had pulled an extra without telling me, "for luck," she said, though I suspect for me.

I took it to the window as the sky went the color of dishwater and old silver. Down the lane, the river showed through the gaps between warehouses, high and black and groaning, the ice working itself apart the way a fist works itself open. Across the lane, in the doorway of the chandler's, a soldier stood with his collar up, watching our windows. He had been there since midnight. We had printed fifty-one copies of treason directly in front of him, and he had watched us do it, and he had seen nothing but a woman and a girl working late, because that is what he expected to see, and expectation is the best camouflage in the world.

Let him report lamplight and industry. Let it go in his log. *Eastwick's, Cooper's Lane: quiet. Women at work.*

I held the extra copy — the fifty-first, the luck one — and read the last page one final time. My father's name in his own type. Beneath it, in mine:

**AND BEATRIX EASTWICK, HIS DAUGHTER, WHO FOUND IT.**

For thirty years I had been the woman who kept her head down, who mended formes and avoided eyes and let the loud men have the world. For thirty years I had thought invisibility was safety. It was only ever erasure with better manners. Well. The erasers were coming for the shop — I could feel it the way you feel weather in an old scar — and when they came they would find ash and a cracked bell and nothing to rewrite, because the record was already moving, sewn into hems and hidden in psalters, traveling toward presses in cities whose names I might never learn.

They could burn the building. They could not burn the byline. I had checked the impression myself. Every page beyond argument.

Wren came and stood beside me at the window, and together we watched the soldier watch us, and the river break itself open a little further, and the light come up mean and gray over Vellham.

"They're crossing the lane," Wren said suddenly.

I looked. Two figures, not one — the soldier joined by a second man, tall, unhurried, wearing a good coat and carrying himself with the terrible calm of a person who has never once needed to run. Even at this distance, even in this light, I knew the walk. I had heard it on my stairs once before, offering me protection and a commission in the same breath.

"Aldous Crane," I said.

"At dawn?" Wren whispered. "Nobody comes at dawn."

"He would," I said. "Dawn is when historians arrive. It's when the record changes hands."

I put the fifty-first copy down on the stone, face up, my father's name and mine gleaming wet-black in the gray light — and went to answer the knock before it fell.
