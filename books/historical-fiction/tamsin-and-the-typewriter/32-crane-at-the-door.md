## 32. Crane at the Door

The press was still warm when I heard his boots on the cobbles.

I want to say I knew it was him by some instinct of the hunted — that my blood rose in warning, that the hair on my arms stood like type in a forme waiting to be locked. The truth is less romantic. The truth is that Aldous Crane walked the way he wrote: evenly, deliberately, with no wasted motion and no doubt that he had a right to be wherever he was standing. I had heard those boots once before, on the day he came to offer me protection and a commission, and I had spent enough nights since lying awake replaying them that I could have picked them out of a parade.

Fifty copies. That was what I had. Fifty copies of the true account of Vellham's uprising — her father's account, my father's account, the one the ministry would have us believe had never been written — stacked on the stone shelf beside the press with their edges squared as neatly as I could square them. My hands were black to the wrist. There was ink under my fingernails that I suspect will be there at my funeral, whenever and whatever that turns out to be. The last sheet had come off the press perhaps four minutes earlier. Four minutes. I have since come to believe that my whole life turned on four minutes — that if Crane had arrived five minutes sooner, or if I had worked five minutes slower, everything after would have gone differently.

But he didn't, and I hadn't, and so when the knock came — three measured raps, the knock of a man who has never once had to wait to be let in — I did not hide.

Old habit said hide. Old habit said douse the lamp, slip into the cellar, let the door stay shut and let him stand in the lane talking to nobody, because Beatrix Eastwick had spent eleven years being nobody and had gotten quite good at it. But old habit had died somewhere between the interrogation by lamplight and the coffin procession, and what was left in its place was a woman with ink on her hands and fifty books and nothing left to lose except the things worth losing.

I opened the door myself.

"Madam Eastwick." He removed his hat. He always removed his hat; courtesy was Crane's sharpest tool, honed finer than any threat he'd ever issued. "I hope I'm not intruding."

"You are," I said. "But you're coming in anyway, so we may as well skip the part where I pretend otherwise."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. Crane did not smile; he *permitted* expressions, the way a censor permits a word. He stepped past me into the shop, and I watched his eyes do what his eyes always did — move over the room the way a clerk's hand moves down a column of figures, taking inventory, finding discrepancies.

He found them immediately, of course. He was very good at his work. That was the thing about Aldous Crane that people got wrong, thinking him merely a functionary, a pen-pusher in an expensive coat. He was an artist. His medium was absence. Where other men built monuments, Crane built gaps — the missing page, the unrecorded name, the testimony that existed nowhere because he had arranged for it to exist nowhere. Standing in my father's shop, he read the room the way I read a galley proof, and I knew he saw the fresh ink, smelled it, saw the stack I had not had time to conceal and had chosen, in the end, not to bother concealing.

"Printing late," he observed.

"I find the night hours suit the work. Fewer interruptions." I gestured at the stool by the composing stone. He declined it, which suited me; I wanted him standing. Standing men negotiate from height. Sitting men negotiate from weakness, and Crane was never weak, but I confess I took a small satisfaction in keeping him vertical.

He drifted toward the press. His gloved fingers hovered above the frame without touching it — a connoisseur's restraint, or a man's reluctance to leave fingerprints. "An Albion pattern. Older model. Your father's?"

"My father's," I said. "Rebuilt twice. It will outlive both of us."

"One imagines it already has outlived several of its owners' intentions."

"There it is," I said. "I wondered how long you'd go on pretending this was a social call."

* * *

He turned then, and looked at me fully, and I understood that the pretense had never been for my benefit. It was for his own record. Somewhere in the ministry archives there would be a note, in his immaculate clerk's hand: *Visited Eastwick premises, 11th of the thaw month. Subject cooperative.* The fiction mattered to him. Fiction always matters to men who deal in official truth.

"The port is sealed," he said. "You know this."

"I know bargemen complain about it in taverns, which is how I know everything, eventually."

"The garrison has orders regarding all river traffic pending historical review." He said *historical review* the way a priest says a benediction — smoothly, meaningfully, with the weight of an institution behind it. "No barge leaves Vellham. No packet boat. No skiff. The ice is breaking within days, and when it does, the river belongs to the ministry."

"That must be very satisfying for the river," I said. "Being told who it belongs to."

"Madam Eastwick." He clasped his hands behind his back — the lecturer's posture, the historian addressing a seminar of one. "I am going to speak plainly, because I have a certain respect for you, and because plainness saves time, and time is the one commodity neither of us can print more of."

"Do go on. I'm enjoying the compliments. They're rare enough to be collectible."

"Fifty copies," he said quietly.

The shop went very still. Even the lamp flame seemed to hold itself.

"You printed fifty copies tonight," he continued, "of a document your father composed, documenting events this office has adjudicated as follows: exaggerated, seditious, and materially false. You printed them on a press that is, as of this morning, listed in the seizure schedule under my authority. And you intend — I don't need to guess, Beatrix, guessing is for census clerks — you intend to put them on the river."

I could have denied it. I want that noted, whoever reads this — if anyone reads this — that denial was available to me, and I considered it for approximately the length of time it takes ink to dry on cheap paper. Then I thought about Wren, who was at that moment two streets away watching the warehouse fire smoke rise into the dark sky and believing she was redeeming herself. I thought about Tamsin, in uniform, holding a checkpoint open through sheer nerve. I thought about the blind compositor, Marta, sitting in a cell because someone had talked, and about the foreman carrying decoy errands through the city like a man carrying broken glass in his pockets.

Denial was available. Denial was also useless, and worse than useless — it was small. I had spent my whole life being small.

"Yes," I said. "Fifty copies."

Crane exhaled through his nose. If I hadn't known better I'd have called it relief.

"Why?" he asked. And here is the strange thing, the thing I keep returning to: he asked it as though he genuinely wanted to know. Not the interrogator's *why*, designed to trap. A scholar's why. A man who had spent years deciding what history would say, suddenly confronted with a woman willing to burn everything she had for a version of it he had personally ruled out of existence, and wanting — needing — to understand the arithmetic.

"Because it happened," I said. "Because forty-one people died on the Quay Steps and the official record says nine. Because there are women in this city whose husbands were taken in the night and whose names appear in no ledger anywhere, and I have held the pages where their names *do* appear, in my father's hand, and I will not be the Eastwick who lets them vanish. Because your version requires everyone to forget, Mr. Crane, and forgetting is a skill, and I have discovered I'm bad at it."

"People forget," he said. "It's what they're for."

"No," I said. "People remember. It's governments that forget on their behalf."

For a long moment he said nothing. The lamp hissed. Outside, faintly, I could hear the city — the fire bells beginning, far off, the garrison drums answering, the whole apparatus of our diversion grinding into motion. Crane cocked his head slightly at the sound, and I watched him decide, visibly, whether to care. He decided not to. That frightened me more than anything else he did that night. A man who ignores a burning warehouse while standing in a printer's shop is a man who has already accounted for fires in his planning.

"Here is what I can offer," he said at last. "The same offer, extended once more, with amendments. Surrender the plates and the copies. Sign an attestation — I'll draft it myself, fair terms, no perjury required beyond what the age demands of everyone. In exchange: the charges against you are filed as *misprision*, not sedition. A fine. Possibly a short confinement, comfortable, brief. And afterward, a position. The ministry needs trained compositors for the new state printing works. Your father's typefaces preserved, catalogued, honored. Beatrix Eastwick, keeper of the national type collection." He paused. "It is not nothing, madam. It is a life."

"It is a cage with good lighting," I said.

"It is the only life on offer."

"And the forty-one dead? Is there a position for them in the national type collection?"

"They are," Crane said, "the cost of order. Every founding has them. Every founding buries them. The only question history ever answers is *who does the burying*. I am offering you a shovel, madam, instead of a place in the hole."

I have been afraid of many men in my life — the loud ones, the drunk ones, the soldiers with their boots and their boredom. I had never before stood across from a man who frightened me by being *reasonable*. That is Crane's genius, and I want it recorded accurately, because caricature is its own kind of lie: he was not a monster. He was something harder to fight. He was a man who believed, completely, that he was the responsible adult in the room, and that the room full of screaming children needed him.

So I did the only thing left. I picked up the top copy from the stack, crossed the three steps between us, and held it out to him.

"What is this," he said. Not a question.

"A copy," I said. "Of the true account. For you. Personally."

His eyes went to the book and stayed there. I watched him not take it — watched the war behind his face, duty against curiosity, discipline against the scholar underneath, the one who had gone into this trade, I'd have bet my remaining type cases, because he had once loved documents the way I loved them. Before the institution got him. Before the forgetting became his job instead of his enemy.

"Take it," I said. "Read it. Burn it after, if you must — burn it in your own hearth, privately, where no one files a report. But read it first. You've ruled on a book you've never read, Mr. Crane. Whatever else you are, you call yourself a historian. Historians read."

"That," he said softly, "is precisely what historians are for preventing. When the record threatens the state, the historian's duty is to the state."

"Then you're not a historian," I said. "You're a censor with a better title. And I'd rather be hanged as a printer than promoted as one of those."

He did not take the copy. He looked at it for another moment — and I swear to you, whoever you are, that his hand twitched at his side, that the fingers curled once toward the book and back — and then he straightened, and replaced his hat, and the window closed.

"Keep it, then," he said. "Frame it. It will make a fine exhibit at your trial."

"Trials require evidence."

"Evidence," Crane said, moving toward the door, "is a matter of production. I produce what the record requires. That is my entire art, madam Eastwick, and I have practiced it longer than you have practiced yours." He stopped at the threshold, half in the lamplight, half out, and turned back, and what he said next he said without heat, without malice, in the tone of a man stating the weather, and it has lived in my ears ever since:

"History will find you."

"Good," I said. "That's rather the point."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "You misunderstand. You think history is a friend coming to vindicate you. History is not a friend. History is a river, and rivers drown the people who stand in them claiming to know the current. I have watched better-armed convictions than yours go under. Yours will too. The only mercy I can promise is that I will file your name correctly." A pause. "Consider the offer open until dawn."

Then he was gone, boots even and deliberate on the cobbles, fading into the noise of the burning city.

* * *

I stood in the doorway until I couldn't hear him anymore. The glow of the warehouse fire lit the low clouds orange over the rooftops, and the drums kept up their idiot rhythm, and somewhere a bell was ringing the wrong number of strokes, and none of it — none of it — was for me. It was for the plates. It was for the barge. It was for Wren's redemption and the foreman's errands and the coal barge waiting at the coal stairs with a bribed master and a cargo hole big enough for a nation's memory.

I went back inside and touched the stack. Forty-nine now. Warm at the edges from the press, cool on top where the night air had found them.

Forty-nine copies, and one offered to the enemy and refused, and I found — this is the part I'd never admit aloud, not to Tamsin, not to anyone — that I was glad he'd refused it. Because now the fiftieth copy belonged to no one, and a book belonging to no one is a dangerous book. A free agent. I set it apart from the others, on the shelf where Father used to keep his quires of good rag paper, and I looked at it for a long moment.

Then the bells changed. The fire bells shifted their pattern — two long, one short, repeated — the signal we'd agreed on, passed mouth to mouth through the circle, meaning *it's working, they've taken the bait, move now.*

And beneath it, fainter, rising from the direction of the river, came a sound I felt in my teeth before I properly heard it. A groan. A long, deep, structural groan, like a giant shifting in sleep, like the world's largest forme being unlocked.

The ice.

Not breaking yet. But waking.

I snuffed the lamp, tucked the fiftieth copy inside my coat, against my heart where it beat like a second pulse, and went out into the smoking dark to see a book onto a boat.
