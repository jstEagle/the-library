## 33. The Burning of Eastwick's

The fire came at dawn, which was a courtesy. Most things in Vellham that meant to destroy you preferred to do it in the dark, when witnesses were scarce and deniability abundant. But Captain Voss's men arrived with the grey light and the smell of coal oil, and they did it in full view of the street, because this was not meant to be deniable. It was meant to be a lesson.

I was not there to learn it.

I was on Tanner's Row, watching the last crate go down the tannery stairs on Wren's shoulder and mine, my arms shaking, my lungs full of someone else's smoke from the warehouse fire across town, when a boy came running up the lane with his shirt untucked and his face lit like a festival.

"Eastwick's," he gasped. "They're burning Eastwick's."

* * *

You would think I would remember the run. Everyone says you remember these things — the cobblestones, the cold, the way your breath tears out of you. I remember almost none of it. What I remember is a gap: leaving the tannery cellar and then suddenly being in Cordwainer Lane with my hands burning from the cold and no memory of the streets between, as if the city had simply agreed not to make me cross it.

The lane was full. That was the obscenity of it — the lane was *full*, neighbors in their nightclothes and their working clothes, dockmen still black with the soot of the diversion fire they'd been fighting since midnight, all of them standing in a loose half-circle with their hands in their pockets or folded across their chests, watching my childhood burn.

Because that's what it was. Not a print shop. I want to be precise about this, because precision was my father's religion and I inherited it the way you inherit a limp.

It was the smell of lead shavings and lamp oil. It was the specific complaint the floorboard made outside the composing room — he used to step on it deliberately every morning, one firm press, like greeting a horse. It was the chalk marks on the doorframe where he'd measured me every year of my life and then kept measuring after I stopped growing, just to have something to mark. It was the type case where Wren had hidden pamphlets in the snow. It was the forme I'd been mending when the captain questioned me, lying fluently for the first time in my life, my hands busy and my heart learning its new trade. It was fifty years of other people's words, set and printed and sent out into the city, and underneath them, unprinted until three days ago, the true account, which was now safe in a coffin-shaped crate in a tannery cellar and on a coal barge, and which could not burn, because we had made sure of that.

So it was not a print shop. But God, it was everything else.

Two soldiers stood at the mouth of the lane with pikes, keeping the crowd back — not for our safety, I understood immediately, but for ours. So that no one would try to save anything. So that the lesson would be complete.

The front windows had already gone. Glass doesn't shatter dramatically in a slow fire; it softens and slumps and drops like sugar melting, and then the fire leans out through the empty frames with an appetite that seems almost personal. Inside, my father's posters — the ones he'd hung on the walls decades ago, specimens of faces and borders, *EASTWICK & SON, PRINTERS* — were curling off the walls. The *SON* was gone first. Of course it was.

And there was the press.

They'd left it. That was deliberate too — Crane's order, I learned later: let the machine burn visibly, let the trade see what happens. My father's press, the iron hand-press he'd bought secondhand from a bankrupt printer in Marlow and rebuilt bolt by bolt on the kitchen table while my mother complained about the oil, stood in the middle of the room with flames walking up its frame like ivy in fast motion. The iron wouldn't melt. Iron doesn't melt at the temper of a house fire. But iron screams in heat — creaks, groans, shifts in its bed like something trying to wake — and the bell.

My father had a bell mounted above the press. A ship's bell, salvaged, he claimed, from a wrecked collier, though I suspected he'd bought it from a tavern and improved the story. He rang it when a job was done. One clean strike, and the whole shop knew: another thing finished, another thing true, another set of words loose in the world. When I was small I thought the bell was magic — that it didn't announce completion but caused it, that the sound itself bound ink to paper.

Now the fire reached it, and the bronze began to sing.

Not ring. *Sing* — a cracked, wavering, rising note, the bell expanding past its own tolerance, sounding the way I imagine a voice sounds when it knows it won't be needed again. It held that note for a long time. Longer than seemed possible. The whole lane heard it. People put their hands over their hearts, some of them, the old ones who remembered my father, and I saw Mrs. Halloway from the chandlery mouth *Eastwick's bell,* and the man beside her take off his hat.

Then the note climbed, thinned, and broke — a clean crack through the bronze, audible even over the roar, the way a bone breaks in a quiet room — and the bell went silent forever, and I went forward.

I don't mean I decided to go forward. There was no decision anywhere in it. My legs simply informed me that the shop was burning and I was standing still, and these two facts could not both continue to exist. Later Tamsin told me I made a sound — a short wordless thing, nothing dramatic, more like the noise you make when you sit down wrong — and started walking toward the soldiers with the flat unhurried gait of someone who has forgotten that pikes are pointed at the business end.

Tamsin caught me at the third step.

* * *

They came out of nowhere, which was characteristic; Tamsin had a gift for materializing at the exact moment I least wanted witnesses. Their hand closed on my arm just above the elbow — census-taker's grip, firm, practiced, the grip of someone who has steered drunk householders away from their own front doors — and swung me around, and suddenly I was looking at a face smudged with ash and sleeplessness and something worse than fear.

"Don't," Tamsin said. Just that. Quietly, under the fire-noise, pitched for me alone. "Beatrix. Don't give them this."

"They're burning my—" And here my voice did something humiliating. It cracked. Like the bell. Like the whole city was cracking this morning and my voice wanted to join in. "That's my father's press."

"I know." Tamsin's eyes flicked to the soldiers, to the crowd, back. "I know exactly whose press it is. Which is why you're going to stand here and watch it like a neighbor, and grieve like a neighbor, and be nobody. Do you understand me? You spent thirty years being nobody in this lane. Be nobody for one hour more."

"I'm tired of being nobody."

"I know." And Tamsin's grip shifted — from restraint to something that was almost, briefly, a hold — thumb against the inside of my wrist where the pulse was going like a runaway galley. "One hour. Then you can be whoever you printed yourself to be. I read the last page, remember. I know your name now."

Which should not have undone me. It was only my name; I'd had it my whole life; I'd set it myself in twelve-point Caslon three days ago beneath my father's, the first time in thirty years the two names had shared a line. But hearing it in Tamsin's ash-smoke voice, in the middle of the ruin of everything I'd built my hiding place out of — that undid me thoroughly, and I let it, for about four seconds, and then I straightened up and turned back to the fire and became, with an effort that cost me something permanent, a neighbor.

A neighbor watches. A neighbor murmurs. A neighbor says *shameful, right in broad daylight, what did the poor woman ever do* to the person next to them, loudly enough for the soldiers to hear, and the soldiers look bored, because they've burned shops before and the murmurings always say the same thing. A neighbor does not run into a burning building after a press that no longer exists except as a very heavy ghost.

We watched. The roof went, folding inward with a sigh, releasing a column of sparks that rose straight up in the windless morning like the shop's soul making its exit, and I confess I thought it, even though I don't believe in souls making exits, and I think my father would have laughed at me and then quietly thought it too.

The chalk marks on the doorframe went early. I looked away for that part.

* * *

Crane came at mid-morning, when the fire had settled from a roaring animal into a sullen industrial process. He arrived in a hired carriage — not a ministry coach; he wasn't fool enough to put official wheels on this — and stepped out into the heat-shimmer with his coat buttoned and his face arranged into an expression I recognized from our last meeting: sympathy worn like borrowed gloves.

He found me easily. He'd have known my face from the census files, of course. From his own files. From the list.

"Mistress Eastwick." He stopped beside me, at a respectful distance from the heat, and regarded the ruin with his head slightly tilted, a man appraising a purchase. "I'm sorry it came to this."

"Were you." I kept my voice at neighbor-volume. Flat. Uninterested. A woman watching a fire she had no part in.

"The shop contained materials relevant to an ongoing historical review. The review requires that such materials not circulate." He said it smoothly, the way he said everything, each clause load-bearing and hollow at once. "You'll understand that history is a discipline of selection, Mistress Eastwick. What survives must be curated."

"Is that what they're calling arson now. Curation."

Something moved behind his eyes — not anger; disappointment, almost, the look of a teacher with a promising student who insists on failing. He produced, of all things, a card, and offered it between two fingers. "When the smoke clears, you will find yourself without premises, without trade, and without protection. The ministry compensates displaced tradespersons who cooperate with the historical record. Generously." He glanced at the burning shell of my inheritance. "Your father, I believe, understood the value of a well-chosen silence. His shop operated thirty years without incident."

"He chose his silences carefully," I agreed. "He also chose what to fill them with. You might study the distinction."

For a moment — just a moment — the gloves slipped, and I saw the actual Aldous Crane: not the historian, but the clerk beneath him, the man whose entire edifice depended on the belief that paper obeys whoever burns the rest. He looked at me the way a man looks at a ledger that won't balance.

"History will find you, Mistress Eastwick," he said softly.

"It already has," I said. "That's rather the point of it."

He held my gaze a moment longer, weighing me, cataloguing me, filing me somewhere — and then he tipped his hat with terrible courtesy, returned to his carriage, and was gone in a rattle of wheels, and I stood there with the card still in my hand because I hadn't taken it, and realized I'd been holding out my empty palm to the air like a beggar, and closed it, and laughed.

I laughed. Standing in the ash-drift with my eyebrows singed and my father's name burning behind me, I laughed until Tamsin took my arm again, differently this time, and steered me out of the lane, past the soldiers, past the murmuring neighbors, past Mrs. Halloway who pressed something into my pocket as I passed — a twist of paper; later I found boiled sweets inside, the kind sold to children — and out into the street where the wind had picked up at last and was carrying the smoke east, toward the river, toward the ice, toward the barge.

* * *

We walked. Nowhere in particular; the nowhere in particular where fugitives go, which is any direction that isn't watched. Tamsin had shed the uniform days ago — it hung in a tanner's loft now, along with the census rolls Tamsin had burned, page by page, with a steadiness I'd watched and hadn't interrupted, because some funerals you attend in silence. In plain coat and cap, Tamsin looked like what Tamsin had become: nobody, officially. A person with no entry in any register. We matched, suddenly. Two blanks in the great ledger of Vellham.

We ended up on the embankment steps above the river, because everything ends up there eventually; the river is the city's gravity. Below us the ice lay white-grey and vast, seamed with dark cracks that hadn't been there a week ago, and as we sat, it spoke. A long groan rolled upstream under our feet, deep enough to feel in the teeth, in the chest, in the oldest parts of the ears.

"There goes your deadline," Tamsin said.

"There goes everything," I said. "Barge leaves with the break, if the pilot's honest. Circle scatters on the planned routes. Wren goes downriver with the crates. And we—" I stopped. I hadn't actually finished that sentence yet. Neither had Tamsin. We had been two people moving from crisis to crisis with such momentum that neither of us had paused to ask what remained standing when the crises ran out.

"We," Tamsin said, testing the word.

"My shop is ash. My name is on a government list, and also on fifty copies of a seditious document, which is a strange pair of places for a name to be." I pulled the fiftieth copy from inside my coat — I'd carried it all morning, through the fire, against my heart like the sentimental fool I apparently was — and looked at it. The smoke had given the top edge a faint grey blush. Otherwise it was perfect. My father's words. My name beneath his. *Set, corrected, and printed by B. Eastwick.* Thirty years of hiding, concluded in one italic line.

"You know what I keep thinking," I said. "Everyone down there watched it burn, and half of them hated my father's politics and half of them loved them, and every single one of them knows my name now. Not from the census. Not from the guild rolls. From the *book*. I hid for thirty years and the fire introduced me to my own street."

Tamsin was quiet a moment. Then: "Mrs. Halloway gave you sweets."

"She gave me *sweets.*"

"That's a funeral custom, in the chandlery families. Sugar for the grieving. She buried a husband and two sons; she knows the rites." Tamsin looked out at the ice. "You got mourned properly today, Beatrix. In public. By name. That's more than most of us get."

Below us, the ice groaned again — longer this time, a groan with intent in it, and somewhere in its middle a sharp crack like a pike snapping, and a seam of open water appeared far out near the channel and sealed itself again, hesitating, like a door deciding.

The bargemen along the quay had come up from their cabins and were standing at the rails, looking at the ice the way farmers look at clouds. One of them spat thoughtfully and said something to his mate, and the mate said something back, and though the wind stole most of it, I caught the shape of it, the same sentence every bargeman on that river was saying to every other:

*Tomorrow. Maybe the day after.*

I put the fiftieth copy back inside my coat. I found Tamsin's hand in the cold without looking, the way you find a rail in the dark.

"One hour ago I had a home and a press and a hiding place," I said, "and now I have none of the three, and do you know, I believe I've never once in my life been this free. Or this frightened. They turn out to live in the same house."

"Then we'd better knock," Tamsin said, "before the ice beats us to it."

Out in the channel, the water showed itself again, wider this time, and stayed.
