## 34. The Ice Breaks

The river had been threatening us for a week.

That's the thing about rivers in late winter — they don't announce anything so much as grumble about it, the way my father used to grumble about taxes and the way I now grumble about everything, having inherited both his type cases and his complaints. All night the Vellham had groaned beneath its skin of ice, a long low complaint that came up through the ground and through the soles of our boots and settled somewhere behind the sternum, where it sat like a debt unpaid.

Tamsin and I stood in the ruins of what had been, until roughly nine hours ago, Eastwick's Print Shop. There wasn't much left to stand in. The walls were charcoal skeletons; the roof was a memory; the press bell — my father's bell, cast before either of us was born — had cracked in the heat with a sound I will hear until I die, which is, I suppose, the one form of immortality available to bells.

"You're staring at the ashes," Tamsin observed. They had a gift for observation, Tamsin. It was how we'd met, after all — them standing on my doorstep with ink-stained cuffs and a census ledger, asking questions no census taker should ask. "Is this grief, or are you checking for salvageable type?"

"Both," I admitted. "Though mostly the second. Grief doesn't melt down well."

It was meant as a joke. It landed somewhere near the truth instead, which is where most of my jokes land these days, whether I aim them there or not.

* * *

We had come back to the shop's corpse because there was nowhere else to be. Tanner's Row was crawling with Crane's men by dawn, sweeping up stragglers the way street children sweep up dropped coppers — quickly, thoroughly, without much interest in who the copper belonged to. The circle had scattered along the routes we'd planned in the tavern, each of them carrying copies sewn into coat linings and trouser seams and, in old Hobb the foreman's case, into the lining of a hat so threadbare that no soldier alive would confiscate it on principle.

Wren was on the barge. That was the arrangement, argued over three nights and two pots of terrible tea: someone young and quick had to ride with the archive, someone who knew the plates' packing order and could lie about it convincingly if the low country customs men asked questions. Wren had volunteered with the particular ferocity of a girl making amends. She'd made her mistake with that broadside printed too soon; she'd lit the diversion fire herself to redeem it; now she would carry the truth downriver through the floes like some small fierce saint of movable type.

I had kissed her forehead at the coal dock and told her not to look back, and then I had looked back for both of us, and she had looked back too, and neither of us pretended otherwise. Sentimentality is a luxury of people whose shops haven't burned down, but I find I have fewer principles than I once did.

Now, standing in the ashes with Tamsin, waiting for a river to make up its mind, I discovered that the waiting was worse than the fire had been. Fire, at least, is honest about what it intends.

* * *

"It'll go today," Tamsin said. They said it the way they filed reports — flatly, precisely, with an authority that came from years of writing down other people's fates. "The bargemen were right. Warm wind off the southern hills since yesterday, and the current underneath has been eating at the ice from below for a week. It never holds past the first warm wind. My grandmother used to say the river keeps its own calendar and lets the rest of us read it wrong."

"Your grandmother sounds like she'd have made a good compositor."

"She made a better smuggler, according to family legend." A pause. "I've been thinking I should write some of it down. What she did, I mean. What any of them did. The ones nobody counts."

I turned to look at them properly for the first time that morning. Tamsin stood amid the wreckage in their ministry coat, which they had not yet stopped wearing even though they'd burned their own census originals two nights ago and become, officially speaking, a fugitive in a government uniform. There was something almost funny about it — the last functionary of a dead administration, standing in the ashes of a dead print shop, watching a dead man's work float away on a living river.

"You're shivering," I said.

"I'm always shivering. It's February."

"It's nearly March."

"It's Vellham. There's no meaningful difference." But they moved closer anyway, shoulder against mine, and we stood together in the ruin like two figures on a monument nobody would ever commission.

* * *

The sound, when it finally came, came without warning.

There had been the groaning all week, the deep complaining grind of ice under strain — I want to be clear about that, because what happened next was nothing like it. The groaning was the river muttering. This was the river shouting.

It began somewhere upstream, near the bend below the granaries, as a single crack — a sharp white crack like a whip laid across the morning. Then another. Then a dozen, overlapping, chasing each other along the banks. And then the whole sheet of ice between the bridges simply *let go*, all at once, with a boom like cannon fire, like siege guns, like the sky itself splitting along a seam, and the frozen face of the Vellham broke into a thousand shifting plates and began to move.

I have heard artillery. We all have, this past year; it was the percussion section of our daily lives. This was louder. This was the earth clearing its throat after a long silence, and every soul within earshot of the water stopped what they were doing to listen.

Down at the docks, men shouted. Somewhere a church bell began ringing — not an alarm, I think, though who could tell the difference; perhaps the ringer simply felt the moment deserved a bell. Dogs barked themselves hoarse. And out on the channel, the floes began their slow grinding procession seaward, great slabs the size of dining tables heaving and tilting and colliding, shrieking against each other like doors that hadn't been oiled in a century.

"There," Tamsin said, and gripped my arm hard enough to bruise.

* * *

The barge was exactly where we'd bribed it to be, wedged into the lee of the coal wharf, and I watched it now with the peculiar double vision of a woman watching her entire life's meaning float away on somebody else's cargo boat. The plates were below, packed in the false coffins we hadn't needed after all — the funeral procession had gotten them past the first checkpoint before the port sealed, and after that the archive had slept in a tannery cellar while we arranged this final leg. Now the crates rode in the hold under three feet of coal, and Wren stood at the rail in a borrowed oilskin four sizes too big, looking smaller and more determined than anyone has a right to look at seventeen.

The bargeman — a broad, silent man named Kessel who had accepted our coin and asked no questions, which in Vellham is the highest form of patriotism — worked his poles against the wharf. The current took him almost immediately. That was the terror and the beauty of it: all our planning, all the forged inventories and staged funerals and burned warehouses, and in the end the river simply picked the barge up the way a hand picks up a leaf, and carried it.

Through the floes.

I held my breath. Everyone on the dock held their breath. A slab of ice the size of a hay cart swung toward the hull, and Kessel leaned on his pole with a grunt, and the barge slid sideways, scraping, groaning — and cleared it by what looked like inches and was probably more, but the eye is a dramatist. Another floe. Another. The barge threaded them like a needle working through scattered beads, and the low gray light caught the ice and made it blaze, and for a moment the whole channel looked less like a river than like a road paved with broken mirrors, every shard reflecting a different piece of the sky.

"They're through the narrows," Tamsin breathed.

"I can see."

"You're gripping my arm."

"You're gripping mine."

"We appear to be holding each other up," Tamsin concluded. "How fitting. Neither of us stands independently anymore."

The barge reached the open channel below the second bridge, where the floes ran thinner and the current ran stronger, and gathered speed. Wren turned at the rail — she'd promised not to look back and broke the promise instantly, and I loved her completely for it — and raised one arm, the oilskin sleeve flapping like a flag. Too far to see her face. I didn't need to. I know that face. It's the face of every apprentice the first time the forme locks tight and the first proof comes off clean: triumph wearing the mask of concentration.

Then the bend took her, and the coal smoke took the barge, and the river took everything else, and the archive of the true account of the Vellham uprising — the names of the disappeared, the confiscated letters, my father's careful corrections, the testimony of prisoners whose bodies we could not save but whose words we could — went south toward the low country at the speed of meltwater.

* * *

We stood on the dock for a long time after it vanished. Around us, the city performed the strange theater of a port reopening. Bargemen who'd been idle for weeks shouted orders. Crates appeared as if conjured. A boy ran the length of the wharf shouting that the ice was out, the ice was out, as though reporting a victory, which in a sense he was.

"The port seal," Tamsin said quietly. "Crane ordered it pending historical review. Every exit watched. And yet."

"And yet."

"Some corporal on the bridge will have logged a coal barge leaving with the break-up traffic, same as forty others. Nobody stops the first barges of the season. It's tradition." Their mouth did something complicated. "Tradition, it turns out, is on our side. I intend to note the irony in my memoirs."

"You're writing memoirs now?"

"I'm writing *something*. I told you. The story of the counters as well as the counted." They watched the water. "Someone has to record what the record-keepers did. Otherwise Crane wins by default, and I refuse — I *refuse*, Beatrix — to let that man win anything by default."

There it was again, that flint in them. When I'd first met Tamsin on my doorstep they'd been all soft questions and watchful eyes, a clerk wrapped around a conscience like paper around wire. The wire was showing now. I found I liked it better. Wire, at least, can be set into a forme and made to hold.

"He'll know," I said. "Eventually. When the account surfaces in the low country, he'll trace it back. The coffin. The checkpoint. You, waving us through in uniform."

"Yes."

"He'll come for you first. You're easier to find than a name sewn into a hat."

"Probably." Tamsin considered this with the equanimity of someone who had already done the arithmetic. "But here is what I keep arriving at: I spent years filing lists that became death warrants. I burned the originals, but burning a list doesn't unhang anyone. The only currency I have left is being *findable* when the reckoning comes. Let him come. I'll be the one holding the receipts."

* * *

We walked back up from the docks as the city shook itself awake around us, and the sound followed us — the endless grinding roar of the break-up, ice riding on ice, the whole river in motion at last. It struck me, climbing the lane past the tanneries, that I had spent an entire winter listening to that river groan and counting it a deadline. When it breaks, the barges leave, and so does any chance to move the archive unseen — I'd written that fear into my bones sometime around midwinter and carried it ever since, a stone behind the ribs.

And now the deadline had passed, and the stone had gone with it, and what remained was not relief exactly. Relief is too clean a word. It was closer to what I imagine a prisoner feels when the cell door opens and he realizes he has forgotten how to walk freely and must learn in front of everyone.

Nature itself had declared the deadline met. The river had voted, unanimously, and its vote was *go*.

At the top of the lane we paused. Below us lay the black scar of the shop, still smoking faintly, still steaming where the firefighters' water had frozen in gray sheets across the threshold. My father had kept that shop for thirty years. I had hidden inside it for most of mine — in the type cases, in the routine, in the small safe rituals of a trade full of loud men who never once thought to look at the quiet woman mending the formes. The fire had taken the hiding place. Standing there, I understood that it had also taken the need for one.

"Beatrix." Tamsin touched my elbow. "Look."

I looked. Not at the shop. At the lane beyond it, where a knot of figures had gathered at the corner — and my stomach dropped before my mind caught up, because knots of figures at corners have meant only one thing in this city for weeks.

But these weren't soldiers. It was Hobb the foreman, hatless despite the cold, the threadbare hat presumably carrying its contraband copy somewhere safer. Beside him the blind compositor's daughter — because old Maren was still in Crane's cells, God help him, and his daughter had inherited his fragment of the circle like a debt. Behind them, two more faces I knew from the tavern council, and a handful I didn't: printers, binders, a typesetter I recognized from the Gazette who must have walked out on his masters the moment the ice spoke.

They had come to the ruins because there was nowhere else to gather, and because gathering was the point. Word travels fast when a river breaks — faster than soldiers, faster than historians, faster, with any luck, than Crane.

Hobb raised a hand to us, not quite a wave. An acknowledgment. *You're alive. Come.*

Tamsin exhaled slowly. "Stragglers," they murmured. "Exactly the kind Crane's men are sweeping the district for."

"Mm."

"We should scatter. That was the plan. Planned routes, sewn copies, no reunions."

"Mm," I said again, and started walking toward them, because apparently the woman who spent thirty years keeping her head down has retired, and her successor makes poor decisions at a confident pace.

Tamsin sighed — the sigh of a former census taker watching data walk off the ledger — and fell into step beside me.

Behind us, the river roared on, breaking, moving, keeping faith with spring.

And ahead, at the corner of the ash-gray lane, Hobb lowered his hand and said words I will carry down my own river for the rest of my life:

"They've taken Maren to the garrison house. And someone just told me — Crane himself is asking for you. By name. Both your names."
