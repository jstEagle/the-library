## 35. Aftermath in Ash

The bell cracked at dawn.

I heard it from three streets away — or rather I felt it, the way you feel a tooth go, a wrongness in the jaw of the morning. By the time Tamsin and I reached the lane where Eastwick's had stood for sixty-one years, there was nothing left to feel it with. The shop was a black ribcage against the grey sky. Smoke still rose from it in a thin, obedient column, as if even the fire had been given instructions and was following them precisely.

My father's press had screamed when the heat took it. Wren told me later she'd heard it from Tanner's Row — a long metallic shriek, like a kettle giving up, and then that bell-note, high and clear and utterly wrong, ringing out over the rooftops. A dying bell. She said it made every hair on her arms stand up, and Wren's arms were usually too busy to bother with hair standing on them.

I stood in the ash-strewn lane holding a satchel that did not contain my life, because my life was currently sewn into the linings of forty coats scattered across the city, and felt remarkably little. That frightened me more than grief would have.

"You're doing the thing," Tamsin said beside me.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stand very still and count your own pulse instead of feeling anything. You did it when Crane offered you the commission. You did it when we loaded the barge. It's very useful, I imagine, for surviving. It's terrible for mourning."

"I'm not mourning," I said. "The press is downstream. The plates are downstream. The shop was just — wood and iron."

"Wood and iron you slept beside for nineteen years."

"Wood and iron," I repeated, because repetition is a kind of wall, and I have always been a good mason of walls.

Tamsin let it lie. That is one of the things I had learned about them by then: they knew exactly which silences to leave unopened, the way a good clerk knows which files not to pull. They stood with me in the cold and watched the smoke behave itself, and after a while their hand found mine, ink-stained cuff against ink-stained cuff, and neither of us remarked upon it, because remarking is also a kind of wall, sometimes, and we had built enough.

* * *

We regrouped at noon in the tannery cellar, because regrouping is what circles do, and ours had been scattered like type from a kicked case.

Wren arrived first, smelling of smoke and horse, having run half the city to tell us what we already knew: the garrison had taken eleven people from the warehouse district overnight. Stragglers. Curious folk who'd come to watch the fire and stayed to watch the arrests, and been swept up for the crime of being present at history without credentials.

"The cooper's boy," Wren said, pacing between the curing racks, her voice pitched low the way she'd learned to pitch everything that winter. "Old Marta who sells chestnuts by the bridge. Two dockhands I don't know. And—" she stopped, and her face did something complicated, "—Hollis."

Hollis. The tavern-keeper. Our fractious, brave, insufferable Hollis, who had argued at the council meeting that publishing immediately was worth any price and then, when outvoted, had shouldered his share of the sewing anyway, grumbling that if he had to be a smuggler he'd be the best-dressed smuggler in Vellham.

"He was at the fire," Wren said. "He couldn't stay away. He said—" her voice caught, "—he said someone ought to witness it properly. Someone who understood what was burning."

Nobody spoke for a moment. In the cellar above us, hides creaked on their frames, a sound like slow applause.

"Eleven," Tamsin said at last, and there was the clerk in them, the terrible arithmetic of the census, counting heads even now. "Of how many?"

"Forty coats," I said. "Thirty-six carriers. Plus those of us already gone." I thought of the coal barge, somewhere past the second bend by now, riding high and light through the floes with its improbable cargo. "Most of the circle scattered on schedule. These eleven were bystanders and one man who chose to stand still."

"They'll be questioned," Tamsin said. "Crane will want to know who organized the procession. But the procession was real — real mourners, a real coffin, a real dead man in it, God rest him. The rubbings shipment was real. Every layer of our lie had a true thing underneath it, which is why it held." They rubbed their eyes. "He'll interrogate them and find nothing but genuine grief, and genuine grief makes a poor confession."

"And if he finds something?" Wren asked.

"Then we're gone anyway." I heard my own voice come out flat and steady and wondered whose it was. "The routes were planned. Everyone knows their leg and no one else's. That was the whole point of splitting the archive across thirty-six bodies."

It was true, and it was insufficient, and I knew both things at once. There is a particular exhaustion that comes not from fear but from the aftermath of fear — the body's long complaint, filed after the emergency has passed, itemized like an invoice. My hands ached from rowing. My throat ached from playing chief griever at a funeral for a stranger while my actual inheritance sailed away inside the coffin lining. And underneath all of it, absurdly, stubbornly, something that could only be called gratitude was trying to get out.

* * *

Gratitude is a strange crop to harvest from ashes, but there it was.

By evening we had counted what remained. Not property — property was done, burned or seized or sold downriver under false inventories. What remained was people, and paper, and one another.

Mirele came at dusk, the blind compositor, tapping her white cane ahead of her like a dowser's rod, guided by her granddaughter through streets she knew better by ear than most men know by eye. She had sewn four copies into her own clothing — she'd insisted, despite everyone's protests, saying that nobody searches a blind woman thoroughly enough to find anything worth finding, and that anyone who tried would answer to her cane. She sat down on a curing rack, turned her face toward the sound of my breathing, and said:

"You're alive, then. Good. Your father owed me six months' wages when he died and I never collected. Now I never can. Sit here where I can hear you, girl, so I can pretend you're paying interest."

"Mirele—"

"Don't. If you thank me I'll cry, and if I cry my granddaughter will think I've gone soft, and if she thinks that she'll stop fearing me, and if she stops fearing me I'll have nothing left to manage her with." Her chin lifted. "Tell me instead whether the copies are legible. Fifty impressions on a failing press with a cracked forme — describe the impression to me."

So I described them. The last fifty sheets off my father's press, printed in the small hours while the city smoked around me: the true account, the whole ledger of grievances restored page by page, the names of the disappeared set in lead, and beneath the final line, for the first time in the history of Eastwick's print shop, two names instead of one.

*E. EASTWICK & DAUGHTER.*

She listened with her head tipped back, and when I finished she was quiet a long moment.

"He'd have hated it," she said finally. "Sharing the imprint. He was vain as a peacock about that line of type." Then her mouth twitched. "And he'd have cut out his own tongue before he admitted he was proud. Go on. Tell me about the kerning. Was the daughter line kerned proper?"

"Tight as a secret."

"Good," she said. "Good." And her hand found mine in the dark and squeezed once, hard, and let go, and that was the whole eulogy, and it was enough.

* * *

The arrests continued through the week. We kept the cellar, but we stopped sleeping there; instead we moved each night along a rotation Mirele's granddaughter had mapped, sleeping in lofts and coal holes and once, memorably, in the vestry of a church whose vicar owed my father a debt he settled without being asked, pressing a hot supper into our hands and refusing to meet our eyes, which in Vellham was the local dialect for *you're welcome*.

Crane's men worked methodically. We watched them from windows. They arrested the exiled foreman on Thursday — I saw it happen, actually saw it, and my stomach dropped through four floors before I remembered that this was part of it, that Perrin had volunteered for exactly this, walking openly through the checkpoint district carrying nothing incriminating, letting himself be taken, feeding Crane the same half-truths he'd fed him before, buying time with his own freedom the way he'd bought it once with broken confessions. Forgiveness, I had learned, was not a warm thing. It was strategic and moral in equal measure, like a well-made joint — strong because it bends.

They did not arrest me. This puzzled me until Tamsin explained it, in their dry clerical way, over a supper of bread and pickled herring in a hayloft.

"You're more useful to him loose," they said. "A known associate of a burned print shop, unemployed, grieving, easy to watch. He'll have someone assigned to follow you. Possibly several someones. He wants to see who you run to."

"So I'm bait."

"You're a thread he hasn't pulled yet because he's hoping you'll lead him to the spool." Tamsin tore their herring with great precision. "Which means, incidentally, that every step you take must be boring. Devastatingly boring. Grief-stricken widow-of-the-trade wanders the ruins, takes work mending type at Ferro's shop on Cordwainer Lane — yes, I arranged it, don't look at me like that, Ferro owes me from the census days and besides his compositor is hopeless — and goes home each night to a rented room and does nothing whatsoever interesting."

"Ferro's shop," I said slowly. "On Cordwainer Lane."

"Two streets from the river stairs. Yes. I did notice. I do notice things; it was in my original job description."

I looked at them — this former functionary of a ministry that would happily have erased us all, sitting in hay dust with fish grease on their fingers, having dismantled their entire official existence brick by brick to stand in a hayloft and arrange employment for a woman they'd met as a name on a list. Somewhere in the ministry's records there was presumably still a file with Tamsin's name in it, flagged, suspicious, awaiting review. Somewhere Crane was assembling his case. And here they sat, homeless by choice, eating herring, planning escape routes with the meticulousness of a man who had spent years filing other people's lives into categories and had decided, at last and irreversibly, which category they belonged in themselves.

"Thank you," I said.

"See, now you've done the thing I said not to do."

"That was Mirele's rule."

"It's a good rule. Universal application." But they were smiling, tiredly, and their hand crossed the hay to find mine again, and this time we did remark upon it, briefly, and then went back to the herring, because there is a limit to how much sentiment a person can absorb alongside pickled fish.

* * *

On the ninth day after the fire, Wren came to Cordwainer Lane with news, and her face told me before her mouth did.

"They got someone else," I said.

"No. That's the thing." She pulled up a stool among Ferro's cases, glanced at Ferro himself — bent over his stone, deaf as a post and conveniently so — and lowered her voice. "The eleven from the warehouse district. Crane released eight of them yesterday."

"Released?"

"Turned them loose. No charges. Marta the chestnut-seller says a clerk interviewed her for an hour and then gave her a receipt for her confiscated shawl and apologized for the inconvenience." Wren's eyes were bright with a fierce, disbelieving delight. "Apologized. To Marta. Who swore at him in three languages."

I felt it settle over me slowly, the meaning of it, the way snow settles — silently, and then all at once everywhere. Eight released. No charges. Because Crane had interrogated them and found nothing but genuine grief, and genuine grief makes a poor confession, just as Tamsin had said. Because every layer of our lies had had truth beneath it, and truth, interrogated honestly by men who didn't know what they were looking for, had come up clean.

"Hollis?" I asked.

Wren's brightness dimmed a degree. "Still held. Him and the two dockhands. Crane's keeping the ones who might plausibly have known something." She hesitated. "But Beatrix — eight people walked free. Out of eleven. When we started this winter, do you remember what we assumed? We assumed we'd all hang. We said it as a joke at the council meeting, and nobody laughed, because it wasn't a joke."

I did remember. I remembered the tavern cellar and the candle stubs and Hollis's voice saying *worth any price*, and the silence after, full of everyone's private arithmetic of rope.

"And instead," Wren went on, "the barge is through the ice fields by now, and thirty-odd copies of the account are walking around this city sewn into coat linings, and Crane burned the wrong building and arrested the wrong people, and eight of those people are home eating supper tonight." She laughed — a short, cracked, astonished laugh. "We survived. Actually survived."

"Not survived," I said. "Escaped. It's not the same thing. Surviving is what happens to you. Escaping is what you do." And then, because Wren was looking at me with that expression she had, the one that demanded the whole truth and settled for nothing less, I added the rest of it: "And we haven't escaped yet. We're still in the city. Crane's still pulling threads. Half the circle is scattered along roads I won't know are safe until letters arrive, if letters arrive. Hollis is in a cell. So yes — we survived the fire. Call it the first victory if you like. But a first victory is only a down payment, Wren. The war on memory doesn't end when the printing does. It ends when the reading does — years from now, in cities we'll never see, by people who'll never know our names unless the record holds."

Wren absorbed this the way she absorbed everything: completely, and then sideways. "You sound like your father's ledgers."

"I am my father's ledgers."

"No," she said, with the blunt certainty of the young. "You're the correction margin. He wrote the text. You're the hand in the margins, fixing his errors." She grinned. "I read the proofs. His spelling was atrocious."

Despite everything — despite the ash and the cell and the herring and the hay — I laughed. It came out of me like the ice cracking, sudden and enormous, and Ferro looked up from his stone and smiled vaguely at whatever deaf men smile at, and Wren laughed too, and for one whole minute in a print shop on Cordwainer Lane we were simply two women laughing over spelling errors, and it was the richest minute I had owned in months.

* * *

That night, in my rented room, I took out the single copy I had allowed myself to keep — not sewn into any coat, not bound for any distant press, but folded against my own ribs, next to the heart, which is where a printer keeps things that matter.

Fifty impressions had left the shop. One stayed with me. The mathematics of it pleased me in a grim way: fifty seeds scattered, one root retained. If Crane took every coat in Vellham tomorrow, the account would still exist, here, under my ribs, waiting.

I unfolded it by candlelight and read the final lines again. My father's account of the governor's abuses, the confiscated letters, the testimony from prisoners — all of it restored, page by page, from memory and proof fragments and marginalia, by four pairs of hands across one impossible winter. And below it, in type I had set myself, tight-kerned, the two names.

*I have signed my father's name,* the colophon ran, *because the record was his. I have signed my own, because the keeping of it is mine. Whoever reads this: the river carried it. The river carries everything, eventually.*

Below that, smaller:

*If you are reading this in another city, another year, another country entirely — then it worked.*

There was a knock at the door. Three knocks, then a pause, then two — the signal we'd agreed on, though we'd agreed on it only that morning and I hadn't expected it used so soon.

Tamsin slipped in, and I knew from the set of their shoulders, before they said a word, that the news was large.

"A letter," they said, holding it out. "Came by barge-hand to Ferro's, addressed in a hand you should see for yourself. Postmarked Lowmark — that's past the second bend, past the toll bridges, past everything."

I broke the seal with fingers that had gone suddenly clumsy. Inside, in a careful commercial hand I did not recognize, was a single paragraph:

*The cargo arrived intact and dry. All leaves present and accounted. The local brethren have begun setting the first pages and ask, respectfully, how many copies the originators wish struck in the first edition — and whether the undersigned may presume to add a preface explaining the provenance of the plates, and the names of those who carried them. Please advise. The river here runs open.*

I read it twice. Then I handed it to Tamsin, whose eyes moved down the page and then lifted to mine, and we sat in the candlelight of a cheap room in a occupied city, holding a piece of paper that proved the whole thing had worked.

"They're asking about a preface," Tamsin said at last. "With names."

"With names," I said.

"Including, one presumes, the names of whoever sent the plates downstream in a coffin."

"Including those."

Tamsin set the letter down carefully, the way one sets down something explosive, or holy, or both. "Beatrix. If you reply — if you put your name in writing, going downriver, into the hands of strangers, printers we've never met, in a country where Crane's writ technically runs —"

"I know."

"—then it's no longer a rumor he can dismiss or a smuggling case he can close. It's a claim. Public, permanent, attributable. He would hunt it. He would hunt *you*."

"He already hunts me. I'm a thread leading nowhere." I looked at the letter, at the words *the river here runs open*. "But a claim, Tamsin — a claim is a thread leading *somewhere*. It leads to me. And I find, sitting here in the ashes of everything I owned, that I am extremely tired of being nowhere."

Outside, far off toward the docks, the river boomed — the ice shifting in the night, breaking itself apart the way it always did, the way it always would. Spring was coming for Vellham whether Crane liked it or not.

Tamsin picked up the pen from the table and held it out to me, handle first, the way you hand someone a tool you trust them to use.

"Then tell them," they said, "how many copies."
