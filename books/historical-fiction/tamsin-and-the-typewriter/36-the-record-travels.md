## 36. The Record Travels

You would think that after everything — the coffin, the fire, the river cracking open like a dropped plate — I would have learned to stop being surprised. But I am a printer's daughter, and printers are professionally acquainted with the fact that you never truly know what a page will do once it leaves your hands. You set the type. You pull the lever. And then the words go out into the world and do whatever they like, the way children do.

So when word began to come back to us — fragments, rumors, secondhand and thirdhand accounts drifting upriver like spring debris — I confess I was surprised. Not that the copies survived. I had staked my life on that. But that they *lived*. That they behaved like living things.

What follows are the pieces as they reached us, over that long green summer, carried by bargemen and peddlers and one remarkably talkative pigeon-fancier from Harsmere. I have set them down in the order they arrived, because that is how memory works — not in tidy chapters, but in arrivals.

* * *

The first account came from a coal-heaver's cousin who had wintered in Marnsford, three days downriver, where the canals braid through the town like grey ribbons and half the population makes its living off the other half's cargo.

There was a tavern there — the Drowned Bell, which is either an excellent name for a canal-side pub or a terrible omen, depending on your constitution — where on cold nights the boatmen paid for their ale with songs, complaints, and news. Sometime in late April, according to this cousin, a barge captain named Oskar Vell stood up on a table, produced a folded pamphlet from inside his coat, and announced that he had something better than singing.

He read it aloud. All of it. The true account of Vellham — the governor's confiscations, the letters taken from prisoners, the names of the disappeared, the whole ledger of grievances that my father and his circle had spent years assembling in secret and that I had spent one terrible winter keeping out of Aldous Crane's hands.

The cousin said the room went quiet in stages. First the men nearest the table, then those playing cards, then even the barmaid stopped mid-pour. He said that when Oskar read the list of names — forty-one men and women taken from their homes between two autumns, most never charged, several never seen again — an old woman near the hearth stood up and said her brother's name was on it, and that she had been told for six years he had run off to the coast to avoid his debts.

"He didn't run," the old woman said. "Say it again."

And Oskar, who by all accounts was not a reading man and had calluses where most men keep fingerprints, read the name again. Then the next name. Then the next. Forty-one times, while the tavern held its breath.

I will tell you what I did when I heard this, and you may laugh if you like. I was sorting type at the time — old habit; my hands sort type the way other people pray — and I sat down on the floor of the shop with the galleys still in my arms and cried like a girl at her first funeral. Because here is the thing nobody tells you about grief: part of it is loneliness. Part of it is the fear that the dead go wherever the record doesn't follow. And somewhere in a canal-side tavern full of strangers, my father's careful, stubborn, kerned little letters were saying a brother's name out loud so that a sister could stop mourning a liar's version of him.

Wren found me on the floor. She didn't ask what was wrong. She set down her composing stick, sat beside me, and said, "Which one got through?"

"The Drowned Bell," I said. "In Marnsford."

She nodded slowly, the way you nod at a name you intend to remember forever. "Forty-one," she said. "That's more than we knew about."

It was. The ledger had listed thirty-three. The missing pages — the ones seized in the raid, the ones Tamsin helped us reconstruct from proofs and memory and marginalia — had accounted for five more. Three names existed nowhere except in the master plates my father hid in the press bed, trusting me to find them by the shape of his reversed *e*.

Somebody, somewhere, had known the last three. And had gotten them to us, or we to them, or the type itself had remembered what none of us could. That is the part I cannot explain and no longer try to. Lead has weight; everyone knows that. Nobody warns you it also has a kind of loyalty.

* * *

The second account came by letter, which surprised me more than the first, because letters can be intercepted and whoever wrote it plainly knew that and wrote anyway.

It was from a journeyman typesetter named Ilse Brandt, working in Corvenna — two hundred miles away, a city I had never seen, famous for its law courts and its lace and nothing else that anyone mentions. She had gotten a copy of the pamphlet from a linen merchant. She wrote to the return address on the colophon — *Eastwick's, Candle Lane, Vellham* — knowing, she said, that the address might be ash and the name might be a ghost, but writing regardless, because there was something she needed someone in Vellham to know.

"I have set type for nineteen years," she wrote. "Compositor for three houses, piecework for two more, and I could identify a dozen masters' faces blindfolded by their letterforms alone, the way you know a friend by their walk. When I opened the pamphlet I did not read it. I *looked* at it. And then I put my hand over my mouth, because I knew the face of that type."

She described it precisely, this stranger two hundred miles away: the slightly narrow bowl on the lowercase *b*, cut to save lead during the scarcity year; the serif on the capital *T* that hooks left instead of standing square; and above all the reversed *e*, that small deliberate flaw that my father cut into his matrices himself and kept in every fount he ever cast, "so that any man who ever worked under me could find his way home by it."

Ilse Brandt had worked under my father. For eleven months, twenty years ago, as a girl of seventeen, before she took the Corvenna post. She wrote that she had assumed him dead or retired, that she had thought of him perhaps twice a year across two decades, and that when she saw his reversed *e* marching across a seditious pamphlet in a city she'd never visited, she wept onto the linen merchant's counter and had to pretend it was dust in her eye.

"Tell his family," she wrote, "that his type is recognized. Tell them a compositor in Corvenna knows that face anywhere, and now knows what he died for, and has begun — quietly, in her own shop, after hours — setting the account in her own house style, so that it can travel further than any single printing can carry it."

His family. I sat with that phrase a while. There was no family. There was only me, invisible in a trade of loud men, and I had signed the last printing beneath his name not because I belonged there but because I refused to be left out of it any longer. And here was a woman I had never met addressing me — assuming me — into a family I hadn't known I had: the scattered, unnumbered congregation of everyone whose hands had ever touched Eastwick type.

I wrote back. Of course I wrote back. I told her who I was, and what had burned, and that the address on the colophon was indeed ash but the name was not a ghost, because ghosts don't answer letters. I asked how her after-hours edition was coming along.

Her reply came six weeks later with a proof sheet enclosed. On the back, in pencil, in a compositor's neat hand: *"Third impression. The guild master thinks I'm doing practice exercises. In a sense, I am."*

* * *

The third account was not written down at all. It arrived in person, attached to a peddler of tinware named Fenn who worked the river towns between Vellham and the low country and who had, in the manner of peddlers, heard everything.

Fenn sold Tamsin a dented kettle at our door — yes, our door; Tamsin had by then given up the census office entirely and had taken to sleeping in the ruined loft above the print shop, mending the roof by day and arguing with me about commas by night — and while Tamsin counted out coins, Fenn counted out gossip, because that was his real currency.

There was a village called Sedgeholt, he said, up one of the tributaries, where the mill workers had gotten hold of a copy — he didn't know how, these things move like mice — and the mill owner had forbidden anyone to read it aloud on pain of dismissal. So they had done the only sensible thing.

They had learned it by heart.

"It's quite a performance," Fenn said, warming to it. "They meet in the tithe barn on rest days, and one of them recites a section — the confiscations, the prisoner letters, whichever bit he's been assigned — and the others correct him if he gets a word wrong. They've made a game of it. The children too. There's a boy of nine who can do the governor's proclamation verbatim, sneer and all. His mother says it's the proudest she's ever been and the most trouble he's ever been."

Tamsin, who had spent years making lists of people for the ministry and had lately become very quiet about the whole profession of counting, set down the kettle and asked, carefully, "How many of them? Reciting?"

"Hundred and fifty souls in Sedgeholt, give or take," Fenn said. "Call it a hundred and twenty who've got pieces. The mill owner sacked four of them. It didn't help. Now they meet in the reed beds instead, and the reeds carry sound something wonderful, so really he's improved the acoustics for them."

Tamsin looked at me when Fenn had gone, and I looked back, and neither of us said anything for a long moment, because we were both thinking the same thought and it was too large to fit through either of our mouths.

Aldous Crane had built his career on the principle that history is a document, and documents can be seized, burned, catalogued, and misfiled — that if you control the paper, you control the past. He had burned my father's shop on exactly that theory. And here were a hundred and twenty millworkers storing the account in the one archive no soldier could raid: each other. Memory, portable after all. Memory, distributed. Memory, corrected nightly in a reed bed by a nine-year-old doing impressions.

"They'll get it wrong eventually," Tamsin said finally. "Versions will drift. Details will change."

"Yes," I said. "And then someone will find another printed copy, or Ilse Brandt's third impression, or one of the fifty I pulled off that press while it cooled, and they'll correct themselves, and start again." I picked up the dented kettle. "Type drifts too, Tamsin. Every impression is slightly different from the last. That's not corruption. That's how a text proves it's alive."

* * *

There were smaller accounts, too, and I want to set down at least two of them, because the large ones are easy to remember and the small ones are the ones I think about at night.

A schoolmistress in the low country wrote that she had copied the list of the disappeared onto a board and hung it in her classroom, and that parents had begun sending her the names of their own missing relatives to add, until the board ran out of room and she started a second one, and then a third. The authorities had ordered her to take them down. She complied — removed the boards, very publicly, with great ceremony — and repainted the entire list on the inside of the schoolroom shutters, which were closed every morning at prayer and opened every afternoon for ventilation. The children called it "letting the names breathe."

And a bargeman told Wren — Wren, who had gone to work the river routes as our courier, sixteen years old and fearless in the way that only recently frightened people are fearless — that in one lock-keeper's cottage upstream, a copy of the account had been framed and hung above the hearth next to the family portraits, and that when the lock-keeper's daughter married, the guests were invited to read a name aloud instead of giving a toast. "Forty-one names takes some time," the bargeman said, "but the ale holds out, and nobody's ever left early."

* * *

It was Wren, though, who brought me the account that undid me most completely, and she brought it in her usual fashion: bursting through the door without knocking, waving a scrap of paper, breathless, saying, "Beatrix. Beatrix, you have to hear this," and then reading it aloud before I'd agreed to anything, which is her way and always has been.

A printer in Ghent-markets-downriver — a jobbing printer, no name given, the kind of man who prints playbills and customs forms and would never touch politics for a wage — had taken in a copy of the account as waste paper, bundled with scrap for pulping. He had glanced at it, as such men do, checking the stock. And had stopped.

Because he recognized the paper.

It was stock from the old Vellham paper-mill, the Hollis mill, closed seven years before — the same stock my father had bought in bulk in his last year, cheap, because the mill was failing and he liked the mill-owner's daughter and wanted to keep her orders coming a little longer. A sentimental purchase. A kindness, dressed up as thrift.

"So this printer in Ghent-market," Wren read, "who has never heard of Eastwick's or Vellham or any of it, is holding a dead man's kindness in his hands — the paper bought to keep a dying mill alive — and on it, the truth. And he couldn't pulp it. He says—" Wren squinted at her notes. "He says, 'I have pulped ten thousand pages in my life and never hesitated, but this sheet felt like it had already been saved once, and I hadn't the heart to be the man who un-saved it.'"

She lowered the paper. "Do you suppose your father knew?" she said. "When he bought the stock. Do you suppose he knew what it would end up carrying?"

I thought about it honestly, which took a while. My father was a careful man and a secretive one, but he was not a prophet. He bought failing paper to help a failing friend. He cut a reversed *e* so his apprentices could find their way home. He hid plates in his press bed because he trusted — against all evidence — that someone would come looking, and that whoever came would be someone who knew his hands well enough to read them.

"No," I said. "He didn't know. He just did the decent thing in every direction at once, and the decent things turned out to point somewhere." I looked at the press — or rather, at the space where the press had been, because the shop was still a burnt shell then, and we worked among the char and the pigeons. "That's all inheritance is, Wren. Decent things pointing somewhere. You don't get to see where. You just have to keep pointing."

Wren absorbed this with the expression she reserved for ideas she intended to steal. Then she said, "The Ghent printer sent it on, by the way. Couldn't pulp it, wouldn't keep it — too hot. He gave it to a bookbinder he trusts, who's having it rebound properly, sewn signatures, boards covered in—"

She checked her notes again and grinned.

"—funeral black."

Of course it was. Everything of ours traveled in mourning clothes. We had sent the plates downriver in a coffin; why shouldn't the copies wear crepe? I laughed until I had to sit down, and Wren laughed with me, and outside the burnt window the river went on moving, high and brown and free, carrying whatever we entrusted to it toward whatever waited downstream.

* * *

That autumn, Tamsin began writing. I noticed it gradually — the loft lamp burning later, the scratch of a nib where there used to be only the scratch of a pen filling out forms. When I asked, Tamsin was evasive in the particular way of someone composing something important, which is to say, evasive and slightly offended at being asked.

But one evening I climbed to the loft with tea and found the pages lying out, and could not help reading upside down, a vice I come by honestly, since my father read every galley upside down and claimed it caught errors better.

Tamsin had titled it, in that careful clerical hand, *An Account of the Counters: Being What the Census Saw.*

The first line read: *"Before I tell you what was counted, you must understand what it cost to count."*

I set down the tea very quietly and climbed back down the ladder without waking them, and lay awake in the dark below, listening to the river.

Because I understood, suddenly, what was happening — what had been happening all along, in taverns and tithe barns and shuttered schoolrooms and a jobbing printer's conscience in Ghent-market. The record was not traveling *outward*, the way Crane had feared, like a contagion to be contained. It was traveling *inward*, deeper, into more hands, more mouths, more memories — multiplying the way water multiplies when the ice breaks, finding every channel at once.

And now it was claiming its counters, too. Its enumerators. Its Tamsins. Everyone the machinery of erasure had ever employed was beginning, one by one, to write themselves into the account.

The lamp scratched on above me until nearly dawn. In the morning, Tamsin came down with ink to the elbow and an expression I had learned to recognize as the beginning of an argument I would enjoy losing.

"There's a fair at Harsmere next month," they said. "Pigeon-fancier's going. He's offered to carry a parcel."

"What parcel?"

Tamsin set the first finished quire of manuscript on the bench between us, squared its edges with a compositor's precision they had absolutely learned from me, and said:

"The one we're going to print."
