## 37. What Tamsin Carries

I have carried a great many things in my life, and most of them were paper.

A census taker's satchel, when properly maintained, holds forty blank household schedules, a ledger of districts, two pencils (one spare, because the ministry does not trust its counters with ink — ink can be blotted, altered, forged; pencil is honest in its erasability), a tin of straight pins for pinning schedules to doorframes when the wind rises, and, if the taker is conscientious, a small stone for weighting pages on porches. Mine also held, for eleven years, a boiled sweet I never ate. It had gone soft and fused to its wax paper sometime during my third season, and I kept it the way sailors keep a coin under the mast: not for luck exactly, but as proof that one could carry a thing without needing it.

I mention the sweet because last night I finally threw it away, and it felt like amputation.

* * *

You should understand what a census schedule looks like, since you will otherwise imagine something grander than it is. It is a sheet roughly the size of a hymnal page, ruled into columns: Name of Household Head. Occupation. Number Resident Under This Roof. Ages. Place of Birth. And then, in the lower margin, in letters so small you might mistake it for a printer's flourish if you didn't know better, a single line added by hand in every district office two years ago:

*Notes on production capacity of premises.*

Production capacity. As if my father's bakery were a question of loaves. As if the Eastwick print shop were a question of anything but presses.

I filled in that line four hundred and eleven times.

I know the number because I counted, afterward, the way you count anything when counting is the only penance available to you. Four hundred and eleven households where I stood on a doorstep with my pencil hovering over that margin line, deciding — in the space between one heartbeat and the next — what a family was worth on paper. For most of them I wrote *none* or *nil* or *domestic only*. The cooper on Tanner's Row had a lathe; I wrote *lathe, domestic*. The widow Hollis took in sewing; I wrote *sewing, domestic*. A whole city of people reduced, by my small careful handwriting, to nothing worth burning.

And then there were the others. Nine of them. Printers, mostly, or near-printers — a bookbinder whose glue pots might hold paste or might hold something stickier, a stationer who sold more blank paper than any stationer needs. Nine households where I wrote down the truth about what they owned, because the truth was my job, and because at thirty-one years old I still believed, in the bottom drawer of myself where I kept such beliefs, that a record honestly kept was a neutral thing. That the ledger did not kill. That it only *counted*.

The bookbinder was named Ferro. He had ink-stained cuffs too — that's how we'd talked, the first time, cuff to cuff like two mules recognizing each other's harness. When the soldiers came for him in the second week of the purges, his wife told the neighbors he'd been taken for debt. Debt. As though the ministry would trouble itself over eleven crowns.

I filed his schedule on time. Neatly. In alphabetical order by street, per the manual, section twelve, subsection three.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about complicity: it does not arrive wearing a black coat. It arrives wearing your own coat, which fits you perfectly, and it asks you to do the next small thing, and the next small thing is always reasonable. *Log the presses.* Well, someone will log them anyway; better a careful hand than a careless one. *Note which shops are active at night.* Well, I'm walking those streets regardless; observation costs the observed nothing. *Flag the households producing seditious material.* And there it is — the word *seditious*, doing its work quietly, like damp working through plaster, until one day the whole wall comes down on somebody's head and you are standing in the rubble holding your pencil, saying, *but I only wrote what was true.*

Crane understood this. That was the horror of him. He never once asked me to lie. He built an entire machine out of true statements — names, addresses, capacities, dates — and let the arithmetic do the killing. A census, properly weaponized, is a firing squad that never has to look anyone in the eye. You aim by alphabetizing.

For years I told myself I was a counter, not a judge. The numbers passed through me the way water passes through a pipe, and pipes are not responsible for where the water goes. It's a comfortable theology, pipe-thinking. It lets you sleep. It lets you eat your supper while somewhere across the city a man named Ferro is being asked questions in a room without windows, questions formulated from a margin note in your own handwriting, in your own neat clerk's script, the script your mother praised when you were nine years old — *such a steady hand, Tamsin, you'll go far* —

I burned my originals in a grate on Tanner's Row, the night before the port sealed. Forty-one schedules from the river districts. It took less time than I expected. Paper burns faster when it's thin, and a human life, reduced properly to columns, is very thin indeed.

* * *

But here is the thing I have been turning over like a stone in my pocket ever since, the thing I finally understand well enough to say:

Burning them wasn't enough. Burning is easy. Burning is what Crane's historians do — it is their entire profession, fire with better funding. If I burn my lists and say nothing, then the story has only one shape: the victors wrote their version, the printers wrote theirs, and the people who stood between — the counters, the clerks, the ones who made the machinery run — simply vanish. No one will know there was a hinge there. No one will know that the door could have swung either way, and that some of us pushed.

Worse: no one will know that some of us *didn't* push, for years, and that our failure had a body count. A history that only contains heroes and villains teaches the next generation nothing except how to recognize themselves flatteringly. What the record owes — what I owe — is the middle. The pipe. The steady hand. The nine households I flagged and the four hundred and two I didn't, and the difference between mercy and cowardice, which is often just luck and weather.

So I am going to write it. Not the uprising — Beatrix owns that territory now, and God knows she earned it in lead and smoke. My account will be smaller and uglier: the view from the doorway. What the census wanted and why. How a list becomes a warrant, step by step, column by column, written plainly enough that a clerk in some future ministry could follow along and feel the floor tilt beneath them the way mine tilted under Aldous Crane's chandelier. I will name Ferro. I will name all nine. I will name myself first, and worst, and most precisely.

Authorship as penance. Beatrix says her father believed type was memory cast in metal. Then let me be remembered accurately, in my own characters: *Tamsin, who counted. Tamsin, who learned late what counting costs. Tamsin, who is writing this so you cannot pretend the middle of the story was empty.*

* * *

I started last night, on the back of a flour sack in Wren's borrowed room above the chandlery, using the only pencil I have left — my spare, the honest erasable kind. Fitting, I thought. Let everything I write be revisable until the day it isn't. I got through four pages before my hand cramped: a description of the schedule form, the margin line, the district office, the smell of the sealing wax they used on the quarterly returns. It reads like a manual. Perhaps it should read like a manual. Manuals are how atrocities travel.

What stopped me, around midnight, was not the writing. It was the sound of the river.

It has not stopped moving since the break — a full month now of high brown water, barges going down loaded with grain and, unknown to their bargemen, with other cargo sewn into oilcloth. Every time I hear it I think: that's what a record sounds like when it escapes. Not silence. Motion. The old ice never made a sound like that; ice only groans in place. Water actually *goes* somewhere.

My account needs to go somewhere too. Which means it needs what every document in this city has needed since the first pamphlet: a press, a compositor, and someone mad enough to set it.

There are sympathetic printers in the low country — the barge proved that. But printing my account here, in Vellham, before Crane's review closes the district entirely, would be suicide with excellent punctuation. And yet leaving it unprinted feels like another kind of filing: the schedule completed, sealed, and shelved in a drawer where no one will ever read it. I did not survive all this to become my own archive's closed record.

There may be a third way. There is talk — Wren hears everything, the child is a listening device disguised in a smock — of a small press upriver, past the toll bridges, run by a widow who sets type for hire and asks no questions unless she likes your answers. Half the circle thinks it's a rumor. The foreman swears it's real; he carried her type cases once, years ago, and says her galleys are the cleanest on the river.

Tomorrow I mean to find out. I will carry my four pages, my spare pencil, and my boiled sweet — no. The sweet is gone. I will carry what remains: a story about counting, which is also a story about what counts, and the steady, ruined, honest hand that wrote it.

If the widow exists, and if her press runs, then perhaps by spring there will be one more document on the river, going where the water goes.

And if she doesn't exist — if the foreman's memory has finally worn smoother than the truth — then I will set the type myself, somewhere, somehow. I have watched Beatrix's hands long enough. Some things you learn by carrying them.
