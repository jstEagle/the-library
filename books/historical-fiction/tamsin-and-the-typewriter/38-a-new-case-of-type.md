## 38. A New Case of Type

The low country smells of mud and cut grass and, in the mornings, of someone else's bread baking — a smell I had forgotten existed, having spent the last years of my life smelling only of ink and fear in roughly equal measure.

The press shop on the Oster canal is called De Gouden Hand, which translates, I am told, to The Golden Hand, though the hand painted above the door is brown and peeling and missing two fingers. The proprietor is a woman named Marta Voss who is built like a beer barrel and has the eyes of someone who has counted every coin ever passed through them and found most wanting. She took me on as an apprentice at fifty-one years of age, which she informs me weekly is the funniest thing that has happened to her since her second husband died.

"Apprentice," she said the first morning, looking me up and down as I stood in her doorway with everything I owned in one bag. "You've ink under your nails from before the war, by the look of it."

"There wasn't a war," I said. "There was an uprising, a suppression, and a long winter."

"In the low country we call all of it 'the troubles,' because it saves time." She jerked her thumb toward the interior. "Can you set type or do you just talk about it?"

"I can set type."

"Then you're not an apprentice. You're cheap labor pretending to be one so I don't have to pay you what a journeyman costs." She stuck out a hand like a boat cleat. "Welcome to the trade, Eastwick. Don't die on me; finding help is murder."

So I am an apprentice in name only, which suits me fine. I have spent enough of my life being invisible by necessity. Being invisible by contract is a considerable luxury.

* * *

Wren is teaching better than she was taught, which is to say I am teaching her properly this time, and she hates it, which means it is working.

In Vellham she learned the way children learn anything during bad years — by theft, by guesswork, by watching through doors. She could set a page faster than any compositor I've known, and half of it would be wrong: letters reversed, spaces too tight, an em where an en belonged. Her father's circle had valued speed over beauty because speed kept them alive, and no one had ever stood over her shoulder and said, *No. Do it again.*

I say it now. Daily. Sometimes hourly.

"You're doing this out of spite," she said last week, pulling a forme apart for the fourth time because she'd spaced a headline as if the words were afraid of each other.

"I'm doing this because when your hands know the work without thinking, your mind is free to think about other things. Like whether the man reading your broadside is going to arrest you."

"He's not going to arrest me here. There's nothing to arrest anyone for. We print almanacs."

"We print almanacs *now*." I took the composing stick from her and showed her the difference between her spacing and mine. "But printing is a craft that assumes trouble. Why do you think the good presses have false bottoms? Why do you think every printer in Vellham knew three ways out of his own cellar? The craft carries its own survival in it. Learn it slow and it will be there when you need it fast."

She grumbled. She re-set the headline. And when she finished it — clean, even, breathing properly between the words — she looked at it the way I used to look at my father's pages, and something in her chest visibly unclenched.

That is the whole trade, if you ask me. Not the press, not the type. It's that moment when the forme comes right and you understand that you made order where there was none, and that the order will outlive your bad temper and your good days alike.

Marta watches these lessons from behind her ledger and pretends not to. But she has started giving Wren the harder jobs, which is Marta's way of saying she has noticed the girl is becoming a printer instead of a pair of hands.

* * *

We live above the shop, Wren and I, in rooms that smell of paper storage and river damp. Wren sleeps like the dead and eats like the living dead, and has developed opinions about low-country cooking that she expresses with the tactlessness of the truly young.

"The fish here is a rumor of fish," she announced our second month. "It's fish-adjacent."

"It's herring," I said.

"It's a suggestion of herring. In Vellham the river trout would laugh at this."

"In Vellham the river was frozen solid for four months and full of things we didn't discuss."

She shut up about the fish. She does not talk much about Vellham, actually, except in flashes — the tavern council, the fire, the night the ice broke with a sound like the sky splitting. When she does mention it, it's usually about the people. She writes letters to the tavern-keeper, Gerrit, care of addresses that change monthly. She asks about the foreman, Bram, who ran decoy errands until the roads opened and then walked south with nothing but a knapsack and a guilty conscience he refuses to put down. She has never once asked what became of the shop itself, and I have never offered, and we both understand this silence as a form of kindness.

Some things you rebuild by talking. Others you rebuild by setting type beside someone every day until the old country stops being the only thing you have in common.

* * *

The news came on a Tuesday, the way news does — ordinary day, extraordinary contents, delivered by a boy on a bicycle who nearly rode into the canal.

Marta subscribes to papers from four cities, on the theory that every paper lies differently and the truth lives in the gaps. She reads them aloud sometimes of an evening while we clean the press, translating as she goes, her voice flat as a proof sheet.

"On Tuesday the Ministry of Historical Review in Vellham was dissolved by order of the Provisional Assembly," she read, and my hands went still in the solvent pan. "Its director, Aldous Crane, has been dismissed following what the Assembly terms 'irregularities in the custody of confiscated materials.' A minority faction is calling for a fuller inquiry. Crane has declined comment and left the city. His current whereabouts are unknown."

The rag dropped from my hands into the solvent. Wren stopped sweeping.

"Read it again," she said.

Marta read it again, slower, savoring it the way she never savored anything else, and I understood that underneath the ledger-keeper's flint, Marta Voss had been waiting months for exactly this paragraph, the way a compositor waits for the last line of a page to come out even.

"Dismissed," Wren said. "Not arrested."

"Not yet," I said.

"Do you think they'll arrest him?"

I thought about Aldous Crane standing in my shop with the finished stack of fifty true accounts between us, offering me mercy with one hand and holding erasure with the other. *History will find you,* he'd said. I had offered him a copy. He hadn't taken it. I had thought, in the moment, that it was pride. Later I wondered if it was something worse — the certainty of a man who believed history was a thing one directed rather than a thing one answered to.

"They'll try," I said. "Whether they succeed depends on how many of his own filings survive. He was careful. Careful men are hard to hang."

"But the record came out anyway." Wren was smiling now, that fierce unguarded smile she'd had the night we loaded the barge. "It surfaced. Where it couldn't be suppressed — that's what it says. That's us, Beatrix. That's the barge. That's the copies sewn into everyone's coats. That's—"

"That's a newspaper paragraph," I said, because somebody had to be the cold water, and I had seniority. "One paragraph. In a paper nobody in Vellham reads."

Marta lowered the sheet and looked at me over her spectacles with an expression I have come to recognize as her version of tenderness, which resembles contempt the way a gull resembles an albatross — related, but smaller.

"Eastwick," she said. "You spent a winter hiding plates in a coffin and burning your own shop, and now you want the victory delivered with a seal on it and trumpets?"

"No. I want it delivered *permanently*. A ministry dissolves, another forms. Directors are dismissed and reappointed under other names. I've watched it happen from a print shop window my whole life. What I want to know is whether the account itself keeps moving after the paragraph is forgotten."

"And how would you find that out?" Marta asked, dry as sandpaper.

And that was the question, wasn't it. Because for weeks — longer than I'd admitted, even to myself — I had been receiving letters. Small ones. Odd ones. A note from a bookseller in Ghelt saying a customer had asked, twice, for a pamphlet matching a description of ours. A letter from a journeyman printer downriver whose cousin swore he'd seen pages of "the true Vellham account" read aloud in a canal-side tavern, and whose handwriting shook slightly on the word *true*. And once, wrapped around a delivery of type metal, a scrap of paper bearing a single line set in type I knew anywhere — my father's type, the reversed *e* and all, printed on a press I did not own.

Someone, somewhere downriver, was reprinting it. Openly or almost-openly. The record had taken root the way seed takes root: invisibly, stubbornly, in the dark, until one morning there it is, green, undeniable, and everyone acts surprised.

* * *

That evening Wren and I walked the towpath, which is what passes for celebration in the low country. The canals were gold in the late light. Barges slid past loaded with cheese and bricks and one inexplicable cargo of harpsichords, and their wakes rocked the moored boats, and somewhere a woman was singing something tuneless and contented.

"Crane's dismissed," Wren said, for maybe the tenth time, testing it like a new letter in a stick. "Dismissed."

"Don't get comfortable. Dismissed men write memoirs. Memoirs are just counterfeits with better paper."

"You are incapable of being happy for a full hour, do you know that?"

"I'm capable of being happy," I said. "I'm just also capable of remembering. They're not mutually exclusive. My father used to say joy and vigilance share a bed in every printer's house."

"Did he really say that, or did you make it up just now because it sounds wise?"

I considered. "He said something like it. Probably about taxes."

Wren laughed — a real laugh, head back, startling a heron off the pilings — and I felt the cautious joy settle into me at last, the way type settles into a locked forme: not triumphant, not loud, but seated. True. Ready to print.

Because here is what I knew, walking that towpath with the girl I'd pulled out of a burning city, past barges going about the dumb peaceful business of carrying things: we had won. Not the war — wars aren't won by printers, whatever the pamphlets claim. But the long game, the one my father had started in secret and died before finishing, the one that cost a blind compositor his liberty and a tavern its peace and me a shop, a name, and a life I recognized — that game had gone to us. Crane could write his memoirs on vellum with a golden pen and it wouldn't matter. The account was loose in the world. It had been read aloud in taverns. It had been set in my father's type by strangers. Memory, it turns out, is portable. That was always the whole point.

"What are you thinking?" Wren asked.

"I'm thinking," I said, "that I've had three letters this month about a riverside town called Verlooy, where a printer named Anselm Dekker is running a new edition of the true account. Full text. Openly. With a colophon."

"A colophon? Like — naming the press? Naming—" She stopped. She looked at me sideways, and I watched her arrive at it. "Naming the printer."

"Naming the printers," I said. "Plural, if the stories are accurate. Apparently the edition carries two names at the foot of the title page. One belongs to a dead man from Vellham."

"And the other?"

I looked out at the water a while. A barge went by, low in the channel, heavy with whatever it was heavy with, moving steadily toward somewhere it intended to arrive.

"The other one," I said, "is mine."

Wren stopped walking. So did I.

"Verlooy's four days' travel," she said. "Three if we take the packet boat and pretend not to be seasick."

"Marta will never forgive us for leaving mid-contract."

"Marta," Wren said, "will charge us for the type we break learning to walk away. Beatrix — she's printing your name. Your *real* name. Under his. In an open edition, in daylight, where anyone can buy it."

"Yes," I said.

My voice came out steadier than I expected. Underneath it, something that had been clenched since the night soldiers torched my father's shop finally, slowly, let go.

"So," said Wren, already turning back toward the shop with her stride lengthening into the walk of a girl who has decided something. "Are we packing tonight, or are you going to stand there being cautiously happy until the boat leaves without us?"
