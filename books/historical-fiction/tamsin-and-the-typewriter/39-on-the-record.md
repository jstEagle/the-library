## 39. On the Record

The town was called Marrow's Bend, which I took at first for a joke and then, after three days there, understood to be a warning. It sat where the river widened and slowed, as if the water itself had grown tired of hurrying and decided to stay a while. A ferry landing, a grain market, two churches that disagreed about everything except the price of candles, and — the reason I had come four hundred miles on a barge, a cart, and finally my own blistered feet — a print shop with its windows unshuttered.

I stood across the lane from it for the better part of an hour before I went in. Old habits. There was a time when standing outside a print shop watching it would have gotten me arrested; now it only got me stared at by a woman selling eels, who eventually asked if I intended to buy one or merely court it.

"Neither," I said.

"Then move along. You're frightening them." The eels, presumably. I moved along, but only as far as the corner, where I could keep both the shop door and the eel woman in view. A person learns to watch doors. It doesn't wear off just because the soldiers have gone home.

The shop belonged to a printer named Hobb — a broad, flour-dusted man with forearms like mooring posts and a voice you could set type by. Wren had written to me about him in her careful, over-punctuated hand: *Mr. Hobb has agreed to run the full account. He says his press is big enough and his nerve is bigger. He has a daughter who reads everything twice.* Wren signed off, as she always did now, with a small printed ornament instead of a flourish — a fleuron, a little leaf of cast metal. She had learned to sign her letters in type. I don't think she knew what that meant to me. Or perhaps she did. She noticed more than she let on, that girl. It was the only thing she'd ever stolen from me worth keeping.

Inside, Hobb's shop smelled the way no shop had smelled to me since Vellham: ink and oil and hot metal and the particular dusty sweetness of paper stacked in quantity. My father's shop had smelled like that. For eleven months I had carried that smell only in memory, and memory, being a miser, doles it out in thimbles.

"You'll be Eastwick," Hobb said, not looking up from the forme he was locking. "You've got the look."

"What look is that?"

"Of someone counting my furniture." He tightened the quoins, then straightened and wiped his hands on an apron that had given up being white sometime during the previous reign. "Wren said you'd come. Said you'd stand outside first, too. Girl knows you better than you know yourself, I'd say."

"She said that?"

"No. But I'm saying it, and I'm older, so it counts double."

* * *

They were printing the account in signatures — sixteen pages at a time, folded and gathered, sewn into boards by a bindery up the street whose proprietor had initially refused the job and then, upon reading the first signature, worked through the night without being asked. That was how it went, Hobb told me, everywhere the book had landed. Refusal first. Then reading. Then the long sleepless arithmetic of conscience.

"Your father wrote a dangerous book," Hobb said. We were drinking tea in the back room while his apprentice — the twice-reading daughter, a solemn girl named Prue — fed sheets to the press. "Dangerous the way a lamp is dangerous. Doesn't hurt anyone unless they've been living in the dark and resent it."

"He didn't write all of it," I said. "There were others. A circle. He'd have been the first to say so."

"I know. Your name's not under his because he wrote it alone. It's under his because you carried it." Hobb shrugged, a landslide in miniature. "In this trade we know the difference between writing a book and delivering one. Delivery's the harder craft. Ask any midwife."

I looked into my tea. I had delivered it, hadn't I. Down a river full of ice, past a sealed port, inside a coffin, through fire. And yet hearing it said plainly — that the name beneath his was earned rather than borrowed — did something to my chest that I resented, because I had planned to get through this trip with my composure intact.

"The fifty copies," I said. "From the last night. Did any reach this far?"

"One did. Sewn into a coat lining. The coat belonged to a bargeman named Ferro, who sold it to a secondhand dealer in Lowmark, who found the pages, read them, and brought them here rather than sell them back." Hobb smiled. "People keep surprising you, if you let them."

* * *

On the third morning, Hobb hung the broadsheet in the window: **THE TRUE ACCOUNT OF THE VELLHAM UPRISING, AS SET DOWN BY THOSE WHO SAW IT. PRINTED AT LAST WITHOUT FEAR.** Underneath, smaller: *Josiah Eastwick, compositor and witness. Beatrix Eastwick, keeper and deliverer.*

I had known my name would be there. I had set it there myself, that last night, in my father's own ten-point roman, letter by letter, my hands shaking so badly I had to kern it twice. Knowing a thing and seeing it are different animals. Knowing is a thought. Seeing is a blow.

I watched from the corner — old habits again, though this time the eel woman was absent and nobody needed protecting from my presence — as the town came to read it.

They came the way people come to anything: slowly, then in numbers. A grain factor stopped first, because the broadsheet was large and free and men like him collected free things the way magpies collect foil. He read the first column. He went pale. He went inside and bought a copy, and came out holding it the way you hold something hot.

Then a clerk. Then two apprentices from the candle chandlery, elbowing each other. Then an old woman in a gray shawl who stood before the window for a long time, lips moving, and then pressed her hand flat against the glass — against the page, really, against the names — and stayed there until Prue came out and gently told her the books were for sale indoors and the reading was free either way.

I heard fragments, standing among them. A man reading aloud to his wife because she couldn't read, his voice going rough at the list of the disappeared. A boy asking his mother what a census taker was, and the mother saying, quietly, "Someone who counts us," and the boy saying, "Did anyone count what was done to us?" and the mother having no answer, and the boy finding it anyway, in the book, later, because the book was the answer.

That was the part I hadn't prepared for. In Vellham, the account had been contraband — a thing smuggled, hidden, defended. Here it was simply *for sale*. Two shillings, or one if you were poor, or nothing if you couldn't pay, because Hobb kept a stack behind the counter marked *gratis* and pretended not to notice when it shrank. Strangers walked out of that shop carrying my father's testimony under their arms like loaves of bread. Like bread. That was the word my mind kept returning to, and I suppose it's the right one. Something baked by many hands, broken and shared, gone into strangers and become part of them.

A woman asked me — me, a stranger on the corner — whether it was true, the part about the prison ships. I said it was true; I had set the type myself. She looked at my hands as if they might show it, and maybe they did. Ink gets into the creases and never entirely leaves. Tamsin used to say you could read a printer's palms like a fortune.

Tamsin. I'll come to Tamsin.

* * *

Hobb let me work the press on the fourth day. "Guests sit," he said, "but printers pull a lever," and he stepped aside with the magnanimity of a man confident that no one could ruin his press in an afternoon.

He was wrong to be confident, or right to be generous; the truth is my hands remembered everything even when my head forgot. The rhythm of it — feed, pull, point, feed — came back like a language you swore you'd lost and find spilling out of your mouth at a funeral. Prue watched me with the fixed, hungry attention I recognized from the other side of twenty years: the look of someone memorizing a future.

"You pull too hard," I told her, when it was her turn. "Let the press do the pressing. That's its whole personality. You're just introducing the paper to the type."

"Mr. Hobb says the same thing."

"Mr. Hobb stole it from someone too. Everyone steals everything in this trade. That's why it survives."

She grinned, and for a moment she was Wren, and before Wren she was every apprentice who ever leaned over a stone at midnight with ink to the wrists, and I understood — finally, fully, the way you understand a knot only once it comes loose — what my father had actually left me. Not the shop. Not the type. Not even the plates. He'd left me the chain itself: the long relay of hands, each teaching the next, none of them the owner of the craft, all of them its carriers. Belonging wasn't a place on a census roll. It was a link.

I pulled the last impression of the afternoon and held the sheet up to the window light. My father's words. My name beneath. Somewhere downstream, in a city that had burned my childhood, Aldous Crane was presumably composing a different account, one with fewer prison ships in it. Let him. Books travel faster than appointments. I had watched it happen.

* * *

Tamsin arrived on the sixth day, footsore and sunburnt, having walked the last forty miles after missing the ferry — "the ferryman wanted coin, and I have discovered I am rich in neither money nor patience" — and stood in the doorway of Hobb's shop looking around at the open windows, the displayed broadsheet, the customers browsing seditious history in daylight, with the expression of a person who has walked out of a locked room and keeps checking whether the door will swing shut.

"It's strange," Tamsin said, over supper at the tavern. "Seeing it *out*. I spent two years learning to make records disappear, and now I can't stop flinching at one sitting in a window."

"You burned yours," I reminded them. "The originals. All those lists."

"I burned mine. The ministry's copies exist somewhere. Crane's successor inherited the filing cabinets, if not the convictions." Tamsin turned their cup slowly on the table, a habit I'd never managed to break them of or wanted to. "Do you know what I keep thinking? Every name in that book — the disappeared, the prisoners, the ones who talked — half of them entered my ledgers first. Age, trade, household. I counted them before anyone erased them. I was the first draft of their erasure."

"And now you're the correction."

"That's very generous."

"It's very accurate. I'm a compositor. Accuracy is my religion."

Tamsin almost smiled. Then: "I've started writing. My own account. The counters as well as the counted. It's poor stuff — I keep wanting to file things instead of tell them — but Wren says poor stuff improves under revision, and Wren has become insufferably authoritative since learning to print."

"Wren is always right. It's her worst quality."

"I taught her that."

"No," I said, "you taught her to observe. Being right she learned on her own, out of spite."

We sat with that a while, the tavern noise washing around us — ordinary noise, unafraid noise, the sound of a town where nobody lowered their voice when the subject turned to government. I tried to remember the last time I'd heard that in Vellham and couldn't, which was answer enough.

"Tamsin," I said. "When you finish it — the account of the counters — I want to set it. Every page. My hands, your words."

They looked at me for a long moment. "You realize what you're offering. Your name next to mine. Again."

"I'm beginning to like the arrangement."

* * *

On the last morning, I went early to the shop, before Hobb, before Prue, and stood in the empty room among the smells of my former life. The press stood quiet. On the stone lay the forme for the next signature, locked and waiting, patient as bread dough rising.

And there, on the counter, a copy of the finished book, opened — someone had left it face-down, spine cracked, well-thumbed — to the title page. I picked it up. Read it. Read it again.

*Josiah Eastwick, compositor and witness.*
*Beatrix Eastwick, keeper and deliverer.*

My father's name I had known my whole life; it was carved on a stone in a Vellham churchyard that was probably still standing, probably still legible, probably visited by no one. But this — this was the better monument. Paper outlasts granite when enough hands pass it along. Granite just sits there being mourned. Paper gets *read*.

I had spent years believing I was invisible in a trade of loud men — hiding in routine, keeping my head down, signing nothing. It had taken a census taker with ink-stained cuffs, an apprentice with more courage than sense, a circle of broken survivors, a river full of ice, and one burning shop to teach me the opposite lesson: that invisibility was never my condition. It was my choice, and choices can be unmade. I had unmade mine in ten-point roman, and strangers in a town I'd never seen were carrying the evidence home under their arms.

The door opened behind me. Hobb, early, flour-dusted, unsurprised to find me holding the book.

"Well?" he said.

I closed it carefully, the way you close a door on a sleeping child, and set it back on the counter.

"When does the next signature go to press?"

His eyebrows went up. "This morning, if Prue ever drags herself out of bed."

"Good," I said, and reached for my apron. "Because I've had a letter from a typesetter in Corvel Bay who swears someone's been selling a *second* edition down there — and it isn't ours. Someone's printing the account with pages missing."
