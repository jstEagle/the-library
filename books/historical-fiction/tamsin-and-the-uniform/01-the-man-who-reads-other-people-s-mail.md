## 1. The Man Who Reads Other People's Mail

The rain came down on London that November morning like it had a grudge to settle, and Jonah Montrose sat in the driver's seat of the Fairweather motorcar with his collar up and his cap pulled low, watching the water bead on the windscreen and run in crooked lines toward the bonnet. The engine ticked beneath him, cooling from the morning's first errand. Somewhere above, behind the leaded windows of the house in Cheyne Row, Mrs. Fairweather was writing letters that would make men in Whitehall reach for their hats.

Jonah knew this because he read them.

Not all of them—not at first, anyway. It had begun the way most corruptions begin: innocently, with a letter left open on the seat beside him while he waited outside the Post Office in Kensington, its second page lifted by a gust and pressed against the glass of the side window like a moth. He had meant only to rescue it from the draught. He had meant to fold it exactly as he'd found it. But his eyes had gone to the page the way a man's eyes go to a lit window at night—without permission, without malice—and what he'd seen there was not gossip but history, being written in a sloping, impatient hand, three feet from where he sat holding the wheel.

*The deputation goes Thursday. If Asquith will not receive us, we shall make the receiving of us unavoidable.*

He had folded the letter. He had delivered it to the sorting clerk. And for two years now, on an average of four evenings a week, when the household slept and the garage lamplight made a yellow island of his workbench, Jonah Montrose steamed open other people's mail.

He told himself it was nothing. That was the trick of it—the lie was comfortable, worn smooth as the steering wheel, and he handled it daily without thinking. He was not a spy; spies wanted things. He wanted nothing. He was a spectator, a footnote, the man standing at the edge of the photograph with half his face cropped out. History happened in the rooms he drove people to, and he was the hinge of the door, noticed only when it squeaked.

He found, examining himself honestly by lamplight, that he did not mind. Or rather—he minded so rarely, and so faintly, that the minding scarcely registered. Like the ache of an old injury on damp days.

* * *

The rain thickened as the afternoon went on. At ten past three, Edith Fairweather came down the front steps under a black umbrella held by the parlourmaid, moving with the quick, deliberate stride of a woman who had spent a decade refusing to be hurried by anyone. She was fifty-one years old and built like a keel—narrow, deep, impossible to overturn—and she got into the car the way she got into everything, which was entirely.

"Kingsway," she said. "Then the Strand. And Jonah—if we pass a newsagent with the evening editions, stop."

"Yes, ma'am."

She settled her skirt, opened her satchel, and began reading as they pulled away. This was the arrangement between them, unspoken and therefore sacred: she did not make conversation, and he did not pretend she ought to. In six years of service he could count their personal exchanges on one hand. Once, in 1915, she had looked up from a pamphlet and said, "You drive very well, Montrose," which from her was the equivalent of a knighthood, and once, during a Zeppelin raid, she had gripped his arm through the blackout and said, "Keep going," and he had kept going, both of them watching the sky burn over the docks in the rear-view glass like men watching a distant war they were somehow already inside.

The city streamed past, grey and shining. A dray horse shook its head and sent water flying. Two boys in oversized soldiers' coats—home early, or home wrong—huddled under a railway arch sharing a cigarette. On a hoarding above them, a poster showed a wounded man reaching toward the viewer: *THEY GAVE—WHAT WILL YOU GIVE?* The paper had gone soft and bubbled with damp, so that the soldier seemed to be drowning rather than pleading.

Edith turned a page. "Stop here."

He stopped. She bought three papers without leaving the pavement, tipping the boy extra with a flick of the wrist, and got back in. For a mile she read in silence, and then, in the tone of a woman remarking on the weather:

"They're calling it the final push. Again."

Jonah said nothing. He was good at saying nothing. Six years of practice.

"The fourth final push." She folded the paper with a snap. "Do you know what I admire about our government, Montrose? Their stamina. Most men can only be wrong so many times before they tire of it."

He permitted himself the ghost of a smile, visible only in profile. "Very durable, ma'am."

That earned him a glance—one eyebrow raised slightly, reassessing him, the way she occasionally did, as if surprised to find the furniture capable of speech. Then she returned to her papers, and the moment closed over like water.

* * *

They were back at Cheyne Row by five, the light already failing, lamps coming on along the Embankment in smudged gold. Jonah put the car away, wiped it down, checked the tyres against the morrow, and performed the small liturgy of maintenance that was the honest part of his day. Then he ate in the kitchen with Mrs. Pym the cook and young Alf the boot-boy, endured twenty minutes of Mrs. Pym's theory that the Kaiser would be tried like a common criminal—"and hung, mark my words, and good riddance"—and excused himself to the garage with a tin mug of tea and the bundle of correspondence that had come into the house that day.

This was the other part of his duties, the official part: the post arrived mid-afternoon with the grocer's boy, and anything marked private was set on the hall tray for Miss Ashford, the stenographer, to sort in the morning. Anything else—circulars, invoices, the party newsletters—came out to Jonah to carry to the dustbin. He carried it to the dustbin. He simply did not carry it immediately.

The garage lamp had a greenish shade and threw his shadow huge against the far wall, a hunchback giant bending over a workbench. Jonah laid the letters out in a row, selected the thickest—a heavy cream envelope addressed in a small, precise hand to *Mrs. E. Fairweather, Cheyne Row*—and reached for the kettle he kept warm on a spirit stove, the steam rising off it in a thin white rope.

He was not proud of the technique. He had refined it over the years the way any craftsman refines his work: the envelope held at the right angle, the steam applied to the flap only, never the face; the patient lifting with a bone folder he'd bought for sixpence off a stall in Fetter Lane; the re-sealing done with a trace of flour paste so fine no secretary had ever remarked upon it. He had become, without ever intending it, an expert. There was something shameful in that—something worse than the reading itself. Any fool can sin. It takes application to get *good* at it.

The flap lifted clean. He drew out two sheets, closely written, and read.

It was from a woman named Constance Lytton—Jonah knew the name from the newspapers, knew the prison record, the hunger strike, the force-feeding that had damaged her heart. Her hand wavered on the page the way a convalescent walks. She wrote about a meeting planned for December, about funds, about a woman recently released from Holloway who could no longer hold a pen for more than ten minutes at a stretch. And then, near the bottom of the second page, a paragraph that made Jonah sit forward on his stool:

*You asked whether I thought the time ripe for the larger action. I think it is more than ripe—I think it is rotting. Every week we wait, another boy dies for a peace nobody means to describe honestly, and every week the men who run this war grow fatter on our patience. If the armistice comes before we have forced the question, they will bury everything—the mistakes, the lies, the names—in a victory parade. We must publish before they are allowed to celebrate.*

Jonah read it twice.

There was nothing in it, strictly speaking. No names, no dates beyond December, no plan a policeman could arrest. It was the kind of letter written by women who had learned, through hard instruction, what happened to letters that contained too much. And yet it hummed. He could feel the current running under the words, the way you feel a live rail through the sole of your boot. Something was coming. Something larger than a deputation, larger than a march. Edith Fairweather and her correspondents were building toward a thing, and they were timing it against the end of the war itself, and Jonah Montrose—footnote, hinge, cropped edge of the photograph—was the only man outside the conspiracy who knew it existed.

He held the letter over the steam again, pressed the flap down with the bone folder, smoothed it flat, and set it on the tray for Miss Ashford, precisely where it would have been had he never touched it. Then he sat for a while with his tea going cold, listening to the rain on the garage roof and the distant clang of a tram on the King's Road.

*We must publish before they are allowed to celebrate.*

He found he was excited, and he distrusted the excitement thoroughly. Excitement, in his experience, was the first symptom of involvement, and involvement was the disease that had killed better men than him. His whole survival—his whole *philosophy*, if a chauffeur could be said to have one—rested on the principle of the untouched glove: touch nothing, keep nothing, want nothing. You watched the game from the terraces and you went home dry. Men who climbed the wall ended up in photographs too, he'd learned that in France, in '15, for eleven weeks that had rearranged his ideas about glory permanently. The spectators lived. It wasn't cowardice, he told the dark. It was arithmetic.

The dark, as usual, did not argue. That was one thing to be said for the dark.

* * *

At half past nine there was a knock on the garage door—two raps, brisk—and Jonah's heart performed a small, traitorous lurch before the voice came: "Montrose? Are you still awake in there? The house telephone is dead again and I need a message taken tonight."

Clara Ashford.

He crossed to the door and opened it onto the wet courtyard, lamplight spilling out around his boots. She stood with an umbrella she hadn't bothered to open and a telegram form in her hand, her dark hair coming loose from its pins in the damp, her expression carrying the specific weariness of a woman who had been typing since eight that morning and had two more hours ahead of her. Twenty-three years old, sharp-eyed, with a way of looking at a person as though checking them for errors.

"The line's been bad all day," Jonah said. "I'll take it to the post office on the corner—they've a night counter."

"I know they do. I'm not simple, Montrose, merely tired." She handed him the form. "Mrs. Fairweather wants it confirmed sent within the hour. She's waiting up for the reply."

"Anything urgent?"

Something moved behind her eyes—a hesitation, quick as a blink, gone before it finished arriving. "Routine," she said. "Party business."

He nodded and pocketed the form. But as he turned, she spoke again, and her voice had changed—lighter, almost idle, the way a duellist's voice goes idle.

"You're up late yourself. Working?"

"Cleaning the plugs."

"In the dark?"

He gestured back at the lamp. "I've light enough."

"Mm." She looked past him into the garage, at the workbench, at the row of envelopes still lying there like patients in a ward, and for one long second Jonah felt the whole architecture of his quiet life stand exposed under the greenish light, every seam showing. Then Clara Ashford yawned, said, "Well. Don't catch your death," and walked back across the courtyard with her umbrella still furled, letting the rain land on her because it hardly seemed worth opening it for forty yards.

Jonah stood in the doorway until she had gone inside. Then he took the telegram from his pocket, read it under the lamp—it was routine, party business, a request for figures on a December meeting, nothing more, and yet he read it anyway, because he read everything, because he could not not—and he carried it out into the rain toward the corner post office, telling himself, as he told himself every night, that tomorrow he would stop.

Tomorrow he would be a footnote again. Tomorrow he would touch nothing.

Behind him, the lamp in the garage burned on, and the letters lay in their neat row, and somewhere across the sleeping city men were already drafting the story of the war they intended everyone to believe, and none of them—not one—had ever heard of Jonah Montrose.

He meant to keep it that way.

For eleven more days, he would succeed.
