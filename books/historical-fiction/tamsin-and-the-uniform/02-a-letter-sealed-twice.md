## 2. A Letter Sealed Twice

The letter came on a Thursday, in the grey hour between the milk carts and the first omnibuses, when London belongs to no one but the working and the waiting.

I was polishing the windscreen of the Daimler when it arrived—a small thing, cream-coloured, no larger than my palm, dropped through the brass slot of the Fairweather door by a boy who did not wait for a signature. I heard it land on the marble floor of the hall with a sound like a bird striking glass. Small sounds. That is how everything begins, though we never believe it at the time. Wars begin with small sounds. So do ends.

Mrs. Petch, the housekeeper, was still upstairs coaxing the range to life, so I crossed the hall and picked it up myself. This was not unusual. In four years of driving for Miss Fairweather I had become, without anyone deciding it, a kind of domestic tide—the man who carried coal up one flight, letters down two, who knew which stair creaked and which window stuck in damp weather. A house accumulates a man the way a riverbed accumulates stones, quietly and without ceremony.

I turned the letter over.

It was addressed to *Miss E. Fairweather*, in a hand that slanted hard to the right, as if the writer had been leaning into a wind only he could feel. The postmark was smudged—Paris, or somewhere that had passed through Paris—but the date was legible enough: three days old. And there, pressed into the flap of the envelope, was a seal of dark red wax bearing an impression I did not recognise: some kind of crest, a tower or a keep, cracked down its centre where the wax had been folded against itself.

And beneath it, visible along the edge of the flap where the paper had been opened and closed again with more care than honesty—

A second seal.

Or rather, the ghost of one. The residue of another wax impression, a paler red, half-scraped away, sitting underneath the newer crest like a scar beneath a bandage. Someone had sealed this letter once, and then someone else had broken that seal, read what was inside, resealed it under a different mark, and sent it on its way as though nothing had happened.

I stood in the hall with the letter in my hand and the smell of coal smoke coming down the stairs, and I felt something shift in my chest—not fear exactly, not yet, but the particular coldness that precedes it, like the air before snow.

Because I should explain something about myself, since no one else will. My name is Jonah Montrose. I am thirty-one years old, I drive a motorcar, and I read other people's mail.

Not all of it. Not cruelly, not for gain. But over four years I had developed a habit that I told myself was harmless and that I suspect, even then, I knew was anything but. Letters arrived for Edith Fairweather by the dozen—suffrage committees, pacifist circles, MPs too cowardly to be seen corresponding with her, women in Manchester writing about their sons at the Front—and every one of them passed through my hands before it passed through hers, because I collected them from the hall, sorted them into the japanned tray in her study, and sometimes, standing in the lamplight of the garage at night while the engine cooled beside me, I opened them.

I had a method for it. A thin knife, steam from the kettle, patience. I read them and I sealed them back and I delivered them untouched, and in four years no one had ever known—or if they had, they had never said. I told myself I was a spectator. A footnote. History happened above me, in those letters, and I merely watched it pass the way a man watches a procession from a side street: present, invisible, safe.

That is what invisibility buys you. Safety, and loneliness, and the strange comfort of never being asked what you think.

But this letter was different. This letter had been opened before it reached me.

Someone in the chain between Paris and Belgravia—a postal clerk, a censor's office, a man in a room I could not picture—had already done what I did. Had already held it to the light of his own curiosity. The difference was that he had wanted whoever received it next to know, or had simply been careless, or—worst thought of all—had wanted the letter to arrive looking exactly like this: twice-sealed, half-broken, a message within the message saying *we are watching this correspondence*.

I should have put it in the tray. That was the whole of my duty, the whole of my role: collect, sort, deliver. Instead I carried it out to the garage, because the house was waking now—I could hear Mrs. Petch clattering below, and above her, faintly, the piano scales Clara Ashford ran through every morning before work, five notes up and five notes down, patient as rain—and I wanted, I found, to be alone with it.

* * *

The garage smelled of petrol and wet wool and the ghost of yesterday's cigars, because Mr. Pemberton from the Peace Committee always smoked in the car and always denied it. I sat on my stool by the workbench, lit the lamp, and set the letter flat before me.

I want to say I hesitated. The truth is worse: I didn't hesitate at all. My hands went through the motions as they had gone through them a hundred times—the knife, the angle, the slow lift of the flap—and only afterwards, watching the inner fold of the paper come free, did I understand that I had stopped being a spectator somewhere between the hall and the garage door, and there was no road back.

Two sheets. Good stock, watermarked. The hand was the same hard-slanting hand as the address, but smaller here, compressed, as though the writer had been rationing his words the way the government rationed bread.

*My dear Miss Fairweather,*

*I write in haste and trust to your discretion. You will have seen the papers' account of events at Vireaux last spring; you will not have seen the truth of them. There are men in this city—our city, I am sorry to say, not theirs—who intend that you never shall. They speak of "settling" the record before the peace makes such settlements permanent.*

*I refer, of course, to the Fairweather business at Vireaux. Your brother's name is spoken of in rooms he never entered, blamed for orders he refused. I have seen the dispatches. I know what was written and what was later rewritten, and I know whose hand held the pen both times.*

*I can prove it. But not by post, and not to any address either of us would wish to name aloud. Burn this. When you are ready, send word through channels we discussed long ago. Time, madam, is shorter than either of us believed in April. The armistice will not end this war; it will bury it.*

*Yours in confidence and constancy,*
*—R.M.*

I read it three times.

The lamp hissed. Outside, a dray horse clopped past on the wet cobbles, and somewhere a whistle blew the quarter-hour, and none of it seemed real. I read it a fourth time, moving my lips slightly, the way the poor used to read scripture, and by the fourth reading I had stopped seeing the words and started seeing what stood behind them: Thomas Fairweather, dead two years in France, whom I had never met but felt I knew from the photograph on the study mantel—young, moustached, holding a riding crop he was too gentle ever to have used—and behind him a French village I could not find on any map in the house, and behind *that*, dimly, men in grey offices with sealing wax of their own.

*The Fairweather business at Vireaux.*

The phrase sat in my stomach like swallowed ice.

Here is what I knew, and all I knew: that Miss Fairweather's brother had died in the spring of 1916, officially of wounds received in action; that she had never spoken of the manner of his death, only of the fact, and then rarely; and that once, a year ago, coming in from the motorcar, I had heard her say to Clara, in a voice stripped of everything, *"They will not even tell me where he fell."* I had pretended not to hear. Pretending not to hear was my profession, alongside the driving.

Now a stranger was telling her—or trying to tell her—that the official account of her brother's death was a fabrication, that men in London were busy making the lie permanent, and that the proof existed and could be reached.

And I had read it first.

* * *

I did the thing I had never once done in four years. I put the letter back together carefully, resealed it with the pale wax stick from the drawer where I kept the tyre gauge and the fuses, pressed my thumbnail into it to mimic the crack in the crest, and then I did not deliver it.

I put it inside my coat.

I don't know how long I sat there after that. Long enough for the lamp to gutter when the oil ran low. Long enough for the morning to establish itself fully outside—carts, children, a Zeppelin raid's worth of whistles blown for no reason at all. I sat on the stool with a dead man's secret against my ribs and rehearsed lies.

That is what it came to, you see. That is the arithmetic I want to be honest about, because honesty is the whole point of setting this down. Every other time I had read a letter, my crime had ended when the reading ended. The letter went into the tray; Miss Fairweather read her own correspondence; my theft cost no one anything, or seemed to. But this letter could not go into the tray, because if it went into the tray, then Miss Fairweather would act on it—she would burn it, or answer it, or begin whatever dangerous business this R.M. proposed—and she would do it watched. Watched by whoever had broken the first seal. And the person standing closest to her correspondence, the person whose hands every letter passed through, was a chauffeur named Jonah Montrose who had just discovered that his invisibility might not be invisible at all.

If I gave her the letter, I protected the truth and exposed us both.
If I withheld it, I protected us both and betrayed the truth.

I chose, that first morning, without letting myself name it as a choice. I told myself I was buying time. Twenty-four hours, perhaps forty-eight, to learn who R.M. was, to see whether the household was watched, to decide. Duty, I called it. Prudence. The language of servants: *it isn't mine to interfere.* Dread wearing duty's coat, buttoned to the throat.

At nine o'clock I brought the Daimler round to the front steps as usual, and Miss Fairweather came down in her grey coat with the collar that made her look, people said, like a disapproving eagle, and she paused on the bottom step and looked at me with eyes the colour of gunmetal.

"Anything this morning, Montrose?"

"A few circulars, miss. Nothing pressing."

Four words. Four words, and the letter of a dead man's vindication sat six inches from my heart, and she nodded and got into the car and began talking about a deputation to Caxton Hall as though the world were ordinary, and I drove her through the wet shining streets and answered yes, miss, and no, miss, and kept my hands steady on the wheel while everything behind them rotted.

She talked about the armistice rumours on the way home. Everyone was talking about them; the whole city had the fever, shopkeepers and bus conductors quoting days and hours as though peace were a train due in from the coast. "They'll sign it soon," she said, watching the crowds thicken near Victoria. "And when they do, Montrose, there will be dancing in the streets, and half of what they're dancing for will be a lie." She said it lightly, almost, the way she said most true things—as if testing whether the world would punish her for them.

"Yes, miss," I said.

I lied to her twice more that day. Once when she asked if the post had come again—it had, a bill from the wine merchant, which I did give her—and once when she asked, pausing at the study door with her gloves half off, whether I thought Mr. Pemberton was a trustworthy man. "As far as I know, miss," I said, which was a lie, because as far as I knew nothing anymore, least of all myself.

By evening the letter had migrated from my coat to the false bottom of my toolbox, beneath the jack handle, wrapped in an oily rag. I told myself it was temporary. I told myself I would find R.M., or watch the house, or somehow place the letter in her hands without my fingerprints on the decision. I told myself a great many things, standing in the garage with the lamp turned low, listening to Clara Ashford lock up the study above me—her footsteps crossing the floor, the little chime of the typewriter cover coming down, and once, distinctly, the sound of a drawer opening and closing with the particular softness of someone hiding something.

I stood very still under the floorboards and listened to her hide something, and I understood that I was not the only one in that house with a secret, and that this should have frightened me more than it did.

What frightened me instead was simpler and colder: the memory of that second seal, that pale ghost of wax beneath the crest. Because I knew seals, I knew wax, I knew the whole small grammar of private correspondence better than any living man ought to—and the inner seal on that letter had not been broken by steam and a knife, careful and anonymous, the way I broke them.

It had been snapped. Opened roughly, in haste or contempt, by someone who did not care whether the recipient noticed. Which meant the sender had wanted it noticed. Which meant the warning was not only *they are watching* but *and we know who reads her mail*.

The lamp flickered. Somewhere across the mews a dog began to bark at nothing, and kept barking, and I stood in the dark of the garage with a dead man's letter in my toolbox and the terrible new knowledge that in whatever ledger the watchers kept—of correspondents, conspirators, drivers, maids—there was very likely already a line with my name on it, and that the next entry would be written by whatever I did tomorrow.

Above me, the house went quiet, one lamp at a time.
