## 3. Miss Ashford Takes Shorthand

There is a particular quality to the light in Edith Fairweather's morning room at half past nine in October — a thin, pewter-coloured wash that comes off the garden and lies across the writing desk like a sheet over furniture in a closed house. I have taken dictation in that light every weekday for eleven months, and I have learned to read Mrs. Fairweather's moods by the speed of her pen rather than her face, because her face gives nothing away. She was a suffragist before she was a widow's sister, and she learned early that a woman who shows what she feels hands her opponents a weapon.

That morning her pen was slow. Slow meant something had arrived by post that she did not like, or did not understand, or both.

"Take this down, Miss Ashford. To the editor of *The Vote*."

I had my notebook open before she finished the sentence. My pencil was sharpened to a point you could have performed surgery with; I kept six of them in a tin and sharpened them at seven each morning while the kettle boiled, because there is nothing worse than a stenographer caught blunt in the middle of a thought. A stenographer who misses a word is like a nurse who misses a pulse. The whole record goes wrong from there.

"*To the editor*," Mrs. Fairweather dictated, standing at the window with her back to me, which she always did when the subject was difficult. "*Regarding your request for comment on the Home Office circular of last week. You may print that I consider it an insult dressed as a courtesy—*" She stopped. Outside, on the gravel sweep, a motorcar engine coughed into life. "That wretched man," she said, not to me exactly. "He revs it like a farm tractor."

"Shall I go on, ma'am?"

"Yes. *An insult dressed as a courtesy, and I decline to be flattered.*"

I wrote it down clean. It was a good line. I allowed myself the small private satisfaction of knowing it would look well in print, and then I put the satisfaction away where I put everything else that wasn't strictly business, which is to say in the drawer of myself marked LATER.

The motorcar outside was Jonah Montrose's — Mr. Montrose, I should say, since we were not acquainted beyond nods and the passing of coats and once, memorably to me if not to him, his catching the office door when my arms were full of files. He drove for Mrs. Fairweather four days a week and was, as far as I could tell, a man constructed entirely out of quietness. Grey eyes, brown hair going grey at the temples though he couldn't have been more than thirty-two, hands that looked wrong on a steering wheel, too fine for it, a pianist's hands gone to petrol and crank handles. He spoke perhaps forty words a day and I had privately assigned most of them to weather.

I noticed him noticing my hands because he was bad at hiding it.

We had reached the second paragraph of the letter when I looked up from my notebook — one does, periodically, to check the dictation has landed — and saw him through the window, standing by the car with the bonnet up, ostensibly attending to the engine. Ostensibly. His head was turned three degrees too far toward the window. Toward the desk. Toward, if I am honest about the geometry, my hands.

People watch my hands. I'm used to it. They move fast and they make small precise shapes in the air when I write, and there is something faintly theatrical about shorthand that draws the eye — all those hooks and loops and dashes, a language that looks like birds' footprints and means everything. Clergymen watch. Colonels' wives watch. Once a bishop watched for a full hour and then asked whether it was Hebrew.

But they don't watch the way he watched. The colonels' wives watch with curiosity. Jonah Montrose watched the way a man watches a door he is not sure is locked.

"*The circular proposes*," Mrs. Fairweather went on, "*that women of my acquaintance refrain from public meetings until the war's conclusion, in consideration of national morale. You may inform your readers that my morale is excellent and requires no consideration whatsoever from Whitehall.*"

Pencil moving. Birds' footprints marching down the page. Through the window, Montrose closed the bonnet — gently, even the closing of a bonnet was gentle with him — and came round the side of the house toward the tradesmen's entrance, which would bring him past the morning room's French windows. Which he knew. Everyone knew the French windows stuck and were propped open in all but the foulest weather, and everyone therefore conducted their comings and goings within earshot of whatever happened to be said in the morning room, and no one, in eleven months, had ever remarked upon it except me, silently, filing it away in the drawer marked LATER along with everything else.

His footsteps came up the path. Mrs. Fairweather's voice dropped half a register without her appearing to decide to drop it — another thing I had catalogued. She lowered her voice near servants the way other women lowered it near invalids.

"*Further, the circular's author may be informed that I marched at Holloway*," she said, quieter now, "*and I do not require his permission to stand still.*"

And then the footsteps stopped. Just outside the French windows. Not pausing-to-pass stopping. Standing stopping.

Mrs. Fairweather's pen ceased mid-word. The silence lasted four seconds. I counted them, because counting is what I do instead of trembling.

Then the footsteps resumed, unhurried, and faded around the corner of the house toward the kitchen yard, and Mrs. Fairweather said, "Where was I?" in a voice of perfect glassy calm, and I read back her last sentence to prove it had been taken down whole, and we continued as though a man had not stood listening at the window to a sentence about Holloway prison.

I want to be clear: I did not distrust Jonah Montrose because he listened at windows. Half of London listened at windows; it is practically a civic duty in wartime. I distrusted him because of what he did afterward. When I carried the fair copy out to Mrs. Fairweather at luncheon, I passed him in the hall, and he looked at me — directly, briefly, the way one looks at a colleague — and there was a question in it. Not curiosity. A question with weight behind it, the specific weight of a man checking whether a thing he overheard had been written down.

It had been written down. Everything was written down. That was my entire profession and, as events would eventually prove, rather more than my profession.

* *

The war office correspondence began arriving in earnest that November, and with it, the handwriting.

I want it understood that I hold no grudge against individual clerks. No doubt some poor underpaid creature at the War Office sits in a draughty room producing that script with a nib worn to a hook, dipping it in ink the consistency of treacle, writing by gaslight after a day of it. But the collective output of that building constitutes, in my professional opinion, a deliberate act of hostility toward the English language. Every R looks like a drowned K. Every T crosses into its neighbour's territory like a disputed border. They abbreviate without system — *Recd*, *Rcd*, and once, magnificently, *R* — and they date things in three formats on a single page, as though time itself were optional.

"They do it on purpose," I said.

I hadn't meant to say it aloud. It was the fourth of December, sleet ticking against the morning-room glass, and I had been wrestling a single paragraph of a War Office letter for ten minutes, holding the page at arm's length, then at candle angle, then nearly to my nose, while Mrs. Fairweather waited with the patience of a woman who had endured worse than bad handwriting from worse institutions.

"Do what on purpose?" Mrs. Fairweather asked.

"The handwriting. Illegibility as policy. If a letter can't easily be read, it can't easily be quoted, printed, or held against them. It's the paper equivalent of a shrug." I smoothed the page flat. "My old mistress in Kensington had a correspondent at the Admiralty, same disease. She used to say the Navy wrote as though ashamed of itself."

A sound came from the doorway. Not quite a laugh. The ghost of a laugh, the sort of noise a man makes when a joke lands somewhere he didn't authorize it to land.

Jonah Montrose stood there with a leather dispatch case in one hand and sleet melting on his shoulders. "Beg pardon, ma'am. Telegram just come to the stationer's. I took it on the way in." He set the case on the hall table, produced the telegram, and hesitated — visibly weighed something — before adding, to the room in general but with his eyes nowhere in particular: "The Admiralty's worse. At the War Office they at least pretend the alphabet exists."

Mrs. Fairweather took the telegram without comment, but I saw the corner of her mouth move, and I confess mine moved too, and for one unguarded moment Montrose and I were two people sharing a small treasonous amusement over the heads of our employer, which is the oldest form of intimacy among servants and staff and probably among governments.

"The Admiralty," I said, because someone had to keep the flag flying. "You've seen Admiralty correspondence?"

"I drive letters. I don't read them." He said it evenly, mildly, a plain statement of the division of labour. And then — this was the part that lodged in me like a splinter — he said it a half-second too late, the way a person repeats a rehearsed answer after remembering it was the rehearsed answer.

"Mm," I said, and made a note of nothing, and noted it anyway.

He went out to the car. Mrs. Fairweather read her telegram, went very still in the way I had learned preceded either a campaign or a funeral, and dictated two letters and a wire in rapid succession. I took them all down clean, and all the while some efficient little clerk in the back office of my mind was stamping a file: MONTROSE, J. — driver. Reads? Unconfirmed. Watches hands. Funny about the Admiralty. Rehearsed.

I am not a suspicious woman by nature. I want that recorded too. Suspicion is exhausting and it curdles the blood and my mother, who suspected everyone including the milkman and the Almighty, died at fifty-one with her jaw set and nobody sorry. I had promised myself I would be an open-hearted woman living in a closed-hearted century, taking people as they came.

But I was also, by training and temperament, a keeper of records. And the record showed a man who claimed not to read letters, watching my hands during dictation like a man auditing a till.

* *

He tried again the following week, and this time I was ready, which is to say I had decided to find out what he wanted, which is a different thing from being ready and I knew the difference even then.

It was the seventeenth of December, the afternoon light dying at three o'clock, and Mrs. Fairweather had retired upstairs with a migraine and a hot-water bottle, leaving me to finish the fair copies alone in the morning room. I heard the car return, heard him come in through the side door — he used the side door when the household was abed or absent; I had charted his habits the way he apparently charted ours — and heard him stop in the hall outside, where the fire was lit and the tea things sat abandoned.

"You'll want the fire built up in here," he said through the door. Not entering. Announcing.

"It's built."

"It's smoking. The chimney draws badly when the wind's northeast." A pause, and then he came in anyway, knelt at the grate, and began doing something competent with a poker, and I returned to my copying and let the silence sit between us like a third person.

He was good at silence. I will grant him that. Most men of my acquaintance treat silence as a debt they must immediately repay with talk. Montrose wore it like an overcoat. For a full five minutes the only sounds were the fire catching and my pencil and the sleet, and I finished an entire fair copy, and I understood that he was waiting for me to speak first, and that he had calculated I would.

So I didn't. I started a second copy. Let him buy his own conversation; I wasn't selling cheap.

It took him another three minutes. "Miss Ashford keeps her pencil points like needles," he said at last, to the fire.

"Miss Ashford," I said, not looking up, "keeps them sharp because dull pencils lose words, and lost words have a way of turning up later wearing somebody else's meaning. You'd know something about words finding new meanings, I expect. Driving letters."

"I drive letters."

"Mm." I blotted a line. "You said. Last week. Very firmly."

The poker paused. In the firelight his profile was all planes and shadow, a face designed for portraits and employed in chauffeuring. "Is there a question in that?"

"No. There's an observation in it. Questions are extra." Now I did look up, and gave him the full force of what my last employer called my shorthand stare — the look I gave witnesses whose testimony didn't reconcile. "Here's what I observe, Mr. Montrose, and you may tell me I'm imagining it, people usually do. You listen at French windows that stick. You know the chimney draws badly from the northeast, which means you've sat in this room when no one asked you to. You claim not to read the letters you carry, but you knew the Admiralty writes worse than the War Office, which is knowledge available only to persons who've compared samples. And twice now you've watched my hands take dictation as though you were checking my arithmetic." I laid the pencil down parallel to the notebook, precisely, the way one lays down a card. "I don't care what a man reads. Reading is harmless. It's the reading-and-denying that makes me reach for my tin of pencils and wonder which one I'd use on a liar."

The silence that followed had texture. Sleet. Fire. Somewhere upstairs, Mrs. Fairweather's floorboard creaking as she turned over.

"I read the addresses," he said finally. Quietly. "To sort the post."

"That's the third version of the answer I've heard."

"It's the true one."

"Then it should have been the first one. Truth told on schedule is worth double." I picked the pencil back up. "Good evening, Mr. Montrose. Mind the chimney on your way out; it smokes when the wind's northeast."

He rose. He stood a moment with his cap in his hands, and I want to record honestly what I saw, because I misjudged it then and have had a long time to correct the record. I thought I saw a man caught out and retreating. What I actually saw — I understand now — was a man deciding, in real time, how much of himself it was safe to show, and concluding: none. A man so practiced at being furniture that being seen had begun to physically discomfort him, like sunlight to something that lived under stones.

At the door he paused. "The Vireaux spelling," he said. "In Mrs. Fairweather's letters. It's sometimes written Vireaux and sometimes Viaux, in the official post. If it matters to anyone someday, it shouldn't be assumed they're different places." And he was gone before I could ask how he knew it mattered to anyone, or why he thought it might, someday.

I sat very still for a moment with my pencil suspended over the page.

Then I opened the drawer of the writing desk — Mrs. Fairweather's drawer, the deep one beneath the blotter that she believed contained only ribbon and spare nibs — and lifted out the folder I kept there. My folder. The one I had been keeping since March, against all prudence and my mother's ghost, containing my own hand-copied duplicates of every sensitive letter that had crossed this desk since the spring. Insurance, I called it, when I let myself call it anything. A stenographer sees everything and owns nothing; the copies were the only property I had in the world that mattered.

I turned to the section marked F — FAIRWEATHER, THOMAS, CAPT., and found the two letters mentioning Vireaux, and set them side by side, and looked at the spellings, Vireaux and Viaux, sitting there in the dead captain's careful hand like two men photographed separately who are unmistakably the same man.

How did the driver know those letters existed? How did the driver know enough to correct my files?

I copied out his remark word for word — I can't help it, I record things, it is the whole of what I am — and dated it, and filed it under M.

Outside, the motorcar started up in the dark, and pulled away through the sleet, and I sat in the warm empty room with two folders of other people's secrets and the growing, unwelcome certainty that the quietest man in the household was keeping a third.
