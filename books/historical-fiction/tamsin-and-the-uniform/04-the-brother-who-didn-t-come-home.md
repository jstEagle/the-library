## 4. The Brother Who Didn't Come Home

The summons came on a Tuesday, in the interval between rain and more rain, when London looked as though someone had held it underwater and let it up gasping.

"Jonah." Edith Fairweather stood in the hall with her hat already pinned, which meant she had decided something before breakfast and the world would simply have to arrange itself around the decision. She was sixty-one years old and carried herself like a woman who had been arrested eleven times and found it tedious rather than frightening. "I'll want you at nine o'clock sharp tomorrow. Not for me."

"For whom, ma'am?"

She handed him a card, cream stock, printed in that grey bureaucratic ink the government used for everything from death notices to ration books: *Records Office, Department of the Adjutant-General, Storey's Gate.* Beneath it, in pencil, in a hand I did not recognise—smaller than Edith's, more careful—a reference number.

"My brother's effects," she said. "Captain Thomas Fairweather. He was killed in March of this year, at—" she paused, and I watched her choose not to say the place, "—abroad. The army has been holding his personal effects since April. They wrote to tell me they were ready for collection in June. It is now October." Her mouth did something complicated, a smile folded inside something harder. "You will appreciate, I'm sure, that the machinery of His Majesty's Government moves at the speed of grief, which is to say, not at all unless pushed."

"I'll push it, ma'am."

"I know you will. You're very good at pushing things quietly. It's why I keep you." She said it without malice, the way one praises a good lock or a discreet doctor, and went upstairs to her study, where the telephone was already ringing for her and the day's first crisis was waiting to be managed.

I stood in the hall a moment longer with the card in my hand. *Captain Thomas Fairweather.* I knew the name the way I knew all the names in that house—from envelopes, from the margins of letters I read by lamplight when no one was watching, from the careful past tense people used about him. *Thomas would have said. Thomas always thought.* A dead man spoken of in the conditional tense, as though he were merely delayed, a train running late somewhere behind the lines.

There was a photograph of him on the landing. I passed it every day going down to the garage. A young man in uniform with his mother's long face and Edith's stubborn chin, looking slightly off-camera as though something more interesting were happening just out of frame. I had looked at that photograph for two years without once thinking of him as a person who had owned things—boots, a watch, letters he'd never sent, a razor some clerk was now cataloguing in a box at Storey's Gate.

That night, in the garage, with the Daimler ticking as it cooled and the lamp turned low so the light wouldn't show under the door, I sat with my ledger of other people's words and realised I could not remember a single letter Thomas Fairweather had ever written home. There had been none through this house. Whatever he'd written had gone to his mother, dead before the war; then perhaps to Edith directly, or nowhere at all, into the great paper silence that swallowed most of what men felt in France.

For the first time since I'd begun reading the household's mail—four years, three months, and a habit I told myself was harmless—I felt less like a spectator and more like a thief. I did not examine the feeling closely. Examination is how you lose your footing.

* * *

Storey's Gate wore its war like a badge. Sandbags at the entrances, windows criss-crossed with paper tape against blast, sentries in greatcoats gone green-grey with weather. Inside, the smell was of cold tea, carbon paper, and floor wax—the particular perfume of the Empire at work.

I presented Edith's authorisation letter at the counter and was received by a clerk named Prewitt—or so his little brass desk-plate said—a narrow man of about fifty with the complexion of a mushroom grown in the dark, which is to say, the complexion of a man who had spent twenty-five years under artificial light among filing cabinets. He examined Edith's letter the way an apothecary examines a prescription, checking each word for poison.

"The Fairweather effects," he said at last. "Yes. Box forty-seven, sub-section C." He did not move. He looked at me over his spectacles with an expression I would learn, in time, to dread: the expression of a man deciding how much a stranger is entitled to know. "You are the driver?"

"I'm Mr.—I'm employed by Miss Fairweather, yes."

"Not family."

"No, sir."

"Hm." He rose, took a ring of keys from a hook behind him, and hesitated. That was the thing I noticed—not the hesitation itself, but its length. Two seconds is nothing. Three begins to be a sentence. He stood there four full seconds, keys in hand, looking at a point somewhere over my left shoulder, before he said, "Wait here," and disappeared through a door marked **PRIVATE**.

The waiting room gave me plenty to look at, because waiting rooms are where institutions keep their self-portraits. Framed photographs of dignitaries visiting the office. A notice board: *LOST PROPERTY MUST BE CLAIMED WITHIN 90 DAYS.* A poster of Kitchener, older now than the war, pointing his finger at men who had mostly stopped listening. And beneath the counter glass, a typed schedule of fees: *Certified copies, 6d per folio. Searches exceeding one hour, 1s.*

Everything had a price. Everything was counted. Somewhere in this building, I understood suddenly, was a piece of paper that said Captain T. Fairweather had existed, and another piece of paper that said what had happened to him, and every piece of it was filed, cross-referenced, feeable, and—for a price—certified true.

Prewitt returned with another man, older, softer, wearing the air of someone whose rank was too high for the work but whose discretion made him useful. This one introduced himself only as Mr. Callard, and he shook my hand with a dry, brief grip, like a man closing a drawer.

"Miss Fairweather's representative," Callard said, pleasantly enough. "Good. Good. We've been meaning to conclude this business." He gestured, and Prewitt went away again, and Callard made small talk about the roads, the rain, the difficulty of motorcars in November—though it was not yet November—and whether petrol rationing would tighten after the new year. He asked none of these questions as though he wanted answers. He asked them the way a doctor taps a knee, testing reflexes.

Then Prewitt came back carrying a box—pine, rope-handled, stencilled in faded khaki: *FAIRWEATHER, T., CAPT. — EFFECTS — 1 OF 1.* And set it on the counter between us with a carelessness that was somehow worse than reverence.

Callard produced a docket. "Sign here, acknowledging receipt complete and unopened. Contents inventoried per annex. Any discrepancy must be reported within fourteen days to this office, in writing, marked personal." He smiled. "We are thorough, Miss Fairweather will be glad to know. The army keeps everything."

"The army keeps everything," Prewitt echoed, from behind him, in a voice with no echo in it at all.

I signed. Callard shook my hand again—that same drawer closing—and wished me a safe drive back through the traffic, and the two of them watched me lift the box and carry it out to the car, and I felt their attention on my back the whole length of the corridor like a draught from a door left open somewhere.

It was not until I had the box on the seat beside me and was pulling out into Birdcage Walk that I understood what had been wrong with the exchange. Nothing had been said. That was it. In four years of driving Edith Fairweather through the corridors of officialdom I had learned the music of bureaucracy, its grumbles and delays and petty tyrannies, and this had been different. This had been *quiet*. Courteous, swift even—by their standards—and utterly silent on the one subject any normal clerk would have mentioned to fill the pause: the dead man himself. Not one word of condolence. Not one anecdote. Not *dreadful business, France*, or *he served with distinction*. Two men whose entire profession was paper about the dead, and neither had said a syllable that acknowledged there had been a man.

They hadn't been handling effects. They'd been handing off a problem.

* * *

Edith met me in the morning room, where she had laid a clean white cloth on the table like a surgeon preparing a tray. Clara Ashford was there too, seated at the small desk by the window with her notebook open and a pencil ready, because Edith wanted everything recorded—she recorded everything, our Edith, votes and parcels and grievances—and because, I think, having a witness made the opening of the box bearable.

Clara glanced up when I came in, and then, quickly, down. I had learned her glances the way one learns weather: hers were precise instruments, and this one said *you look wrong, driver, what happened?* I arranged my face into nothing and set the box down.

"Shall we?" Edith said, and cut the cord herself with Clara's scissors.

Inside: a kitbag smell of canvas and dried mud and something faintly chemical, the preservative they used. A razor in a cardboard case, rusted at the hinge. A cigarette case, dented—dented in a way that made Edith's breath catch, because dents in metal near a heart pocket tell their own story. A wristwatch, stopped. Letters, a bundle of them, tied with government string—letters *to* Thomas, returned unopened, from friends and one from a woman whose handwriting made Edith close her eyes for a moment before she continued. A Bible. A paybook. A small tin box of photographs, curled with damp. And at the bottom, wrapped separately in waxed paper, an identity disc and a leather folder containing what the inventory annex called, in Prewitt's upright typing, *personal correspondence, final dispatches (2), misc.*

Two dispatches. Thomas's own last letters home, the ones he'd got through before March. Edith read the first standing, then the second sitting down, and I will not record what was in them—they were hers, and I had stolen enough already—but I watched her go from a woman receiving a box to a woman receiving a wound, and I watched her bind the wound into purpose almost before our eyes, the way she bound everything. By the end she was not weeping. She was making notes.

"Clara, take this down. The paybook shows field service credit through the eleventh of March. The second letter is dated the ninth. I want both copied verbatim." Then, to me, without looking up: "Thank you, Jonah. You may go."

But as I reached the door, Clara spoke—lightly, too lightly. "The inventory," she said. "May I see the annex?"

I fetched it from the box. She ran her pencil down the list, item by item, ticking. Razor. Watch. Case. Bible. Paybook. Disc. Correspondence (2). Photographs (1 tin). She ticked each one against the contents of the white cloth. Then she stopped.

"There should be a file number," she said. "Here, at the top. See—'ref. V/47/C.' But there's a second reference struck through, here." She turned the annex toward Edith, and toward me, and her voice stayed perfectly level while her eyes did not. "Someone's crossed out a line and re-typed the header. Look at the ink. The strike-through is fresher than the typing."

Edith took the paper. For a long moment the room held still, and I heard the clock in the hall counting out the silence, and I thought: *they kept everything, the man said. The army keeps everything. So why does the paper say less than the box?*

"Jonah," Edith said at last, mildly, terribly mild. "When you collected this. Was anything said to you at the office? Anything at all?"

And here is the thing I must set down plainly, because everything after turns on it: I lied. Or nearly. I said, "Nothing of consequence, ma'am. Routine signatures. The clerks were courteous." I did not mention Callard's tapping questions, or the four-second hesitation with the keys, or the silence where condolence should have been. I buried it, the way one buries anything, smoothly and with a straight face, and I heard myself doing it and was astonished at how easily it came.

"Very good," Edith said, and returned to her notes, and dismissed us.

* * *

But I want to be exact about the rest of that afternoon, because exactness is all I have left to offer anyone, including myself.

I drove back to Storey's Gate alone at half past four. I told myself it was to return the annex—there was, I reasoned, a discrepancy procedure, *reported within fourteen days, in writing, marked personal*, and Edith would need the form. This was even true. It was also entirely beside the point.

At the counter, Prewitt was not there. Another clerk, younger, took my query about discrepancy forms and went hunting for them in a drawer, and while his back was turned I saw, on the ledge behind him, a trolley of files awaiting reshelving—one of them open-faced, and on its spine, in the same grey stencil as the box: *V/47/C.*

Fairweather. Sub-section C. The struck-through reference.

The young clerk turned back empty-handed—"Forms are with Mr. Prewitt, he's gone to the strongroom, can you come tomorrow?"—and I said yes, certainly, tomorrow, and thanked him, and turned to go, and stopped, because on the counter beside me lay a loose pad of requisition slips, the little triplicate chits by which files moved between departments, and the topmost slip was filled out in pencil and not yet torn free:

*Requisition: File V/47/C (Fairweather, T., Capt.). To be withdrawn from general circulation pending review. Authorised: H.C. — 12 Oct.*

*Pending review. Withdrawn from circulation.* On the twelfth of October. The day before Edith's letter had arrived requesting collection. Someone had pulled the file one day before a family came asking for it—as if the file knew we were coming, or as if someone had known, and wanted it elsewhere when we did.

H.C. Halloway? I didn't know the name then. I know it now, God help me.

I should have walked out. Every instinct I had built across four years of careful invisibility said walk out, you are a driver, drivers carry boxes, they do not carry knowledge, knowledge is heavy and gets men killed. And I stood there with my hand on the door and the rain starting against the glass, and I thought of Thomas Fairweather in the photograph on the landing, looking at something interesting just out of frame, and of Edith binding her wound into purpose, and of Clara's pencil stopping mid-tick.

I am not going to pretend it was courage. It was smaller and stranger than courage—it was the spectator's disease reaching its crisis, the realisation that watching has a cost too, that a man who sees everything and does nothing is not neutral but complicit in the slowest possible way.

While the young clerk bent to his drawer again, I tore the top slip from the pad and folded it into my glove, and walked out into the rain with the paper crackling softly against my knuckles like something alive.

A requisition slip. Worth perhaps a farthing as paper. It was the smallest theft of my life, and the first, and I knew even as I did it that it would not be the last, and that a man who steals once from the government has changed professions whether he admits it or not.

That night in the garage I read the slip by lamplight until I knew it by heart, then burned it in the coal scuttle and watched the ash settle, keeping only the words, which cannot be burned.

*Withdrawn from circulation pending review. Authorised: H.C.*

Whatever the Fairweather file contained, someone powerful had wanted it out of reach one full day before Edith asked for it. And I had held the evidence of that wanting in my hand for exactly as long as it took to commit a crime.

The next morning, Clara was waiting by the garage door before I'd finished winding the crank, her notebook tucked under her arm, her breath smoking in the cold.

"You went back yesterday," she said. Not a question. "To Storey's Gate. After you brought the box home."

I said nothing, which was answer enough.

"Miss Fairweather doesn't know," she said. "So either you went for yourself, or you're lying to both of us. And I've been wondering all night which would worry me more." She stepped closer, and lowered her voice to almost nothing, and what she said next I did not expect, and it changed everything.
