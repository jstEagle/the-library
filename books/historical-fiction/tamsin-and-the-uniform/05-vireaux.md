## 5. Vireaux

The letters arrived on a Tuesday, in a biscuit tin wrapped in brown paper and tied with butcher's string, and Clara Ashford knew before she opened it that the parcel would change something. She had developed, over two years of working for Edith Fairweather, a certain instinct about parcels—the way grief traveled through paper the way heat traveled through iron, warming whatever touched it without ever showing itself.

She carried the tin up to the morning room on a tray, past the portraits of Fairweather ancestors who had made their money in shipping and their reputations in Parliament, and set it down beside Edith's teacup.

"From Mrs. Prewitt," Clara said. "Thomas's landlady's sister, apparently. She kept his things."

Edith looked at the tin for a long moment. She was fifty-one that autumn and wore her widowhood of her brother like a coat she had never agreed to put on—always slightly too heavy, always visible. Her hands, when she reached for the string, were perfectly steady. That was the thing people misunderstood about Edith Fairweather: they mistook stillness for calm, when it was closer to a held breath.

"You'll read them to me," Edith said. It was not a request. "My eyes have been tiresome all week, and Thomas's hand was never legible even to himself."

"Yes, ma'am."

"I'd rather you didn't summarize. If he complained about the mud, I want the mud. If he lied about how well he was eating, I want the lie, whole." Edith lifted the lid. The smell of the tin reached them both—tobacco, boot polish, something faintly sweet that might have been the biscuits themselves, gone to ghost after three years. "He wrote to me every week until the last month. The army took those, I expect. Or he did. He was always tidying away the things that would worry me."

Clara untied the string with care, keeping the knot intact rather than tearing it. There were forty-odd envelopes inside, most of them soft-cornered from handling, a few still crisp as if they had been written and then reconsidered. At the bottom lay a dispatch case, canvas, with a strap worn shiny at the buckle.

She began at the beginning, because that seemed the honest way to do it, even though both of them knew where the story ended. The first letters were from training camp at Aldershot, full of jokes about boots and sergeants and a man called Duckworth who could not whistle. Thomas Fairweather wrote the way he had spoken, Clara gathered from Edith's face—which did nothing during the jokes except soften at the corners, a woman listening to a voice through a wall.

The rain came against the windows around eleven o'clock, a fine persistent drizzle that made the room feel underwater. Clara read. Edith drank tea that went cold in the cup and let it go cold without noticing.

* * *

By the twelfth letter they had reached France proper, and Thomas's prose had begun to change. The jokes thinned out—not disappeared, but rationed, spent more carefully, like cigarettes near the end of a pack. He wrote about the village of Vireaux in passing first, the way one mentions weather. A billet there. Good cider. A church with a roof holed by an early shell that nobody had bothered to repair, so that pigeons came and went through the open rafters, which Thomas said made services feel less like sermons than like departures.

"He liked that," Edith said quietly. "The pigeons coming and going. Freedom inside a ruin."

It was the twenty-ninth of October when the tone shifted for good. Clara felt it in the handwriting itself before she understood a word of it—the pen pressing harder, the lines crowding toward the bottom margin as though the page were running out of time. She read slowly.

*"Dearest Edie—I must write this while my nerve is good, because I don't know that it will be good tomorrow, and if anything happens to me I want someone besides myself to know what I know. You will want to burn this letter when you've read it. Don't. Burn nothing. Keep everything, even the dull ones, especially the dull ones. Paper is the only witness that can't be promoted or shot.*

*There is an order going round. I won't write its number; numbers get letters opened. But it concerns prisoners taken at Vireaux, and what is to be done with them, and it comes from further up than anyone here likes to say aloud. Captain Marsh showed it to me because I keep the company's correspondence in order—he says my hand is the only one in the mess that doesn't look like a spider drowned in ink—and I wish to God he hadn't shown me. Some knowledge sits in a man like shrapnel. It does its damage slowly, and always from the inside.*

*I signed the receipt for it. That's what I keep coming back to. My name is on a piece of paper saying I received a document, and somewhere in a file in France or London that paper exists, and I am frightened of it, Edie, in a way I was never frightened at the front. Bullets are honest. They come at you whistling. This other thing comes at you in triplicate."*

Clara stopped. Not because Edith asked her to—Edith had not moved—but because her own throat had closed. She took a sip of water from the jug on the sideboard, and the water tasted of the pewter, and she thought absurdly of Jonah Montrose down in the garage, who read letters by lamplight the way she was reading these now, aloud, sanctioned. Everyone in this house, it seemed, eventually put their hands on other people's words. The difference was what you did afterward.

"Go on," Edith said.

* * *

The next letter was dated the fourth of November, and it began mid-thought, as though the intervening days had been spent circling the subject without being able to land.

*"I have been trying to decide how much truth a civilian can carry, and I have decided the answer is: more than we think. We treat you all like china, Edie, and it's insulting, and it's also convenient for us, because china doesn't ask questions.*

*The order was countermanded. That is the official version and I will give it to you straight, because you deserve straight things: an order was issued regarding the prisoners, it was acted upon, and then word came down that no such order had been given—that the document I signed for had been misdated, or mistranscribed, or had never existed at all, depending on which officer is doing the explaining. And the men who carried it out—"*

Here the ink smeared, as though the page had been folded while wet, or held too long.

*"—the men who carried it out are dead. All of them. Duckworth among them, who couldn't whistle, who used to say the war would be over before he learned. The official account gives them the credit for acting without orders. Do you see? They died twice. Once in the field, and once on paper, where they were made into the authors of the thing instead of its instruments. Somewhere a clerk has already written 'excess of zeal' and underlined it.*

*I was not present. I thank God daily, hourly, that I was not present. But my signature received that order into company records, and I know what the order said, and I know that the men who obeyed it were told afterward that they had imagined it. One of them, a corporal named Hale, said to me the week before the action: 'They'll hang it on us if it goes wrong, sir, won't they.' And I told him no, that the army looks after its own. I have been trying to forgive myself for that answer ever since. It may be the worst thing I ever did, telling him that. Worse than anything I did with my hands.*

*If you receive this, keep it. If you never receive it, then I failed, and the pigeons will still be coming and going through the roof at Vireaux, and no one will know why the church matters."*

* * *

Clara read the final letter last, though it was not marked final—there was no farewell in it, no premonition dressed up as sentiment. That was somehow worse. It was dated the eleventh of November, 1916, and it was mostly domestic: socks received and appreciated, a request that Edith tell Aunt Prudence the marmalade was a triumph, a joke about a cat that had adopted the mess. Then, near the bottom, wedged between the marmalade and the cat:

*"P.S.—I've asked Marsh to move my papers to the regimental archive, the sensitive ones, under seal. He thinks I'm being fastidious. Perhaps I am. But if a man's signature can make him guilty of a thing he tried to stop, then a man's papers ought to be kept somewhere a court might find them. Tell no one. Especially tell no one helpful."*

*Especially tell no one helpful.*

Clara set the letter down on her knee and found that her hands were trembling slightly, and pressed them flat against her skirt until they stopped.

Edith had gone very still.

Not the ordinary stillness of listening—something else, something structural, as though the whole edifice of the woman had been reorganized around a new fact while her face remained a face. She sat with her spine straight and her hands loose in her lap and her eyes fixed on the middle distance, on some point beyond the rain-streaked glass, beyond the garden, perhaps beyond England. Clara had seen her sit thus exactly once before: at the memorial service, when the Last Post ended and everyone bowed their heads and Edith alone had raised hers, listening for something that had not yet sounded.

"Read the fourth of November again," Edith said. Her voice was level. Level the way ice is level.

Clara read it again. *"An order was issued regarding the prisoners, it was acted upon, and then word came down that no such order had been given."*

"And the men who carried it out are dead," Edith said, when Clara finished. "And blamed. Blamed posthumously. By men sitting in warm rooms writing the war the way they wished it had gone." She turned her head, slowly, and looked at Clara, and her eyes were dry, which frightened Clara more than tears would have. "How many letters are in that tin, Miss Ashford?"

"Forty-three, ma'am. Plus the dispatch case."

"The dispatch case is locked?"

"It appears so. There's a strap and a small lock. No key came with the parcel."

Edith rose. She crossed to the window, stood looking down into the street where a costermonger was shouting the price of apples through the rain, an ordinary sound, an obscene sound, proof that the world continued to conduct its commerce regardless.

"My brother was murdered twice," Edith said to the window. "Once they took his life, which was their right, God help us, that was the bargain every family in this country accepted. But then they went back and took the manner of it. They took his death and rewrote it, and they used the dead men who obeyed their order as the ink." She turned around. Something had happened in her face—not softening, not breaking. Hardening. Like metal quenched. "Do you know what I marched for, Miss Ashford? Before the war took even my brother from me? I marched because I believed that the truth could be made inconvenient to power. That was the entire theory. Inconvenience. You cannot hang a government on a feeling, but you can hang it on a document."

"Yes, ma'am."

"They think the record is theirs. They think paper obeys whoever files it." Edith came back across the room and stood over the tin, over the forty-three envelopes and the locked dispatch case, and Clara watched her place one hand flat upon the lid, gently, the way one touches a sleeping child. "Thomas asked for a court. He asked in the only language available to him—a sealed case and a postscript and a sister he trusted to be stubborn enough. Well. He chose the right sister."

"What do you mean to do?"

Edith smiled then, briefly, and it was the coldest expression Clara had ever seen her wear. "First, I mean to open that dispatch case. Locks are arguments, Miss Ashford, and I have never yet lost one. And then—" she paused, and the pause had weight, like a foot testing ice, "—then I mean to find out whether my brother's papers still exist, or whether someone has been tidier with them than he hoped. There is a records office on Queen Anne's Gate. Everything the army cannot destroy ends up there eventually, misfiled, which is nearly as good as hidden."

A motor drew up outside. Through the window Clara saw the dark green Fairweather car, and Jonah Montrose stepping around to the door, umbrella in hand, waiting with the patience of a man whose profession was waiting. She thought of him reading letters in the garage by lamplight—she had seen him at it once, through a gap in the doors, and had said nothing—and felt a strange, sudden certainty that the tin in front of her was not safe merely because it sat in a respectable morning room in Belgravia.

"Ma'am," Clara said, "if these letters say what they appear to say—if there are men who falsify records for the government—then this house is being watched. Houses like this one always are. And there are four people in this household who know what's in that tin now, counting yourself."

"Three," Edith said.

Clara hesitated. "I count the driver, ma'am. He carries your correspondence. He's in and out of the ministries and the post offices and the houses of every MP you write to. Whatever moves through this house moves through his hands first."

For a moment Edith studied her, and Clara had the uncomfortable sensation of being measured—of being found either more or less than she appeared, and of not being told which.

"You notice things," Edith said. It was neither praise nor accusation. "Very well. Say nothing to Montrose. Watch him, if you like—watching costs nothing. But understand this, Miss Ashford, and understand it completely: I did not spend thirty years fighting for a voice in how this country is governed only to learn that my brother's death belongs to whoever holds the filing cabinet." She picked up the eleventh-of-November letter, the one with the marmalade and the pigeon-holed church, and folded it along its existing creases with terrible tenderness. "Thomas wrote *keep everything*. So we shall. And then we shall make them read it."

Down below, the kitchen door opened, and Clara heard footsteps on the gravel, and Jonah Montrose's voice asking Mrs. Prewitt's boy, cheerfully, whether the rain meant the roads to Richmond would flood again this year.

An ordinary question. An ordinary man.

She filed it away anyway, in the desk drawer of her mind where she kept everything—because insurance, she had learned early, was simply another word for attention.
