## 6. The Men from the Ministry

The frost came early that November, and with it a silence on the telephone line that lasted four days.

Jonah noticed silences the way other men noticed weather. Four years of driving had taught him that a motorcar spoke before it failed—a new vibration in the steering column, a hesitation in the carburettor at half-throttle—and he had learned to hear the same language in the Fairweather household. When the house was well it hummed: the clatter of Clara's typewriter through the study window, Mrs. Petch's arguments with the milk boy, Edith's voice rising and falling in dictation like a tide with somewhere important to be. When something was wrong, the hum stopped. People spoke less. Doors closed more gently than usual, which was worse than slamming.

For four days the telephone had rung and been answered by someone else, or not rung at all. For four days Edith had taken her letters from the morning post into the study unopened, which she never did—she was a woman who opened correspondence like a surgeon, immediately and without flinching. And for four days no one had asked Jonah to drive anywhere farther than Kensington, as though the city itself had agreed to keep them close to home.

He told himself it was nothing. He told himself this while polishing the Hupmobile's bonnet until he could see his own face in it, distorted and small, a dark smudge of a man in a grey cap. He told himself it while he checked the tyres, greased the axles, did all the small dutiful things that made a driver invisible and useful and safe.

It was Thursday, the seventh of November, when the men came.

* * *

They arrived at eleven o'clock precisely, which Jonah noted because everyone else in London arrived late—the war had eaten punctuality along with everything else—and because precision was its own kind of message. A black Wolseley drew up outside the house in Cadogan Gardens with the unhurried confidence of a vehicle that had never once been questioned by a policeman, and two men got out.

The first was fifty-ish, broad, comfortable, dressed in a good tweed suit cut for a man who expected to sit down a great deal. His face was pink and pleasant, the face of a county cricketer gone softly to seed, and he wore his bowler slightly back as though the war were an inconvenience he had personally declined to attend. The second was younger, thinner, with the colourless neatness of a man who ironed his own collars, and he carried a leather document case held square against his chest like a hymnal.

Jonah opened the door of the car for them before they'd reached it, because that was what one did.

"Morning," said the elder man cheerfully. "Mr.—?"

"Montrose. I'm Mrs. Fairweather's driver."

"Montrose. Good name. Biblical." He smiled as though this were a compliment they would now share between them forever. "Halloway." He produced a card—thick stock, engraved, no ministry named upon it, only *A. Halloway* and a Whitehall number. "And this is Mr. Pell. We've an appointment with Mrs. Fairweather. Eleven o'clock."

There had been no appointment that Jonah knew of. But Miss Petch, answering the door behind him, said with mild surprise, "Yes, we were expecting you," and Jonah understood that the appointment had been manufactured sometime in the last four silent days, manufactured so smoothly that the household believed it had always existed. That was the thing about these men, he thought later. They didn't break into your life. They arranged themselves inside it and let you assume you'd left the door open.

He should have gone back to the garage. That was the rule, his own rule, carved deep: *the driver drives; the driver does not listen; the driver is furniture with hands.* But Halloway paused on the step and turned back with that pink, easy smile, and said, "You'll be wanting to bring the car round front? No hurry. Have a look at our wheels first if you like—Pell's rather proud of her."

Pell was not proud of anything; his face suggested pride had been removed in childhood, like tonsils. But Jonah found himself walking around the Wolseley anyway, running a finger along a wing, nodding at the engine as Pell lifted the bonnet without being asked. It was a fine car. It was also, Jonah realized with a coldness that started in his stomach and climbed, a way of keeping him occupied. Of measuring how easily he could be steered.

"Petrol rationing must make your work interesting," Pell said. His voice was flat as blotting paper.

"We manage, sir."

"Long hours? Evenings sometimes?" A pause exactly long enough to seem accidental. "I understand Mrs. Fairweather works late."

"She keeps her own hours, sir. All hours."

"Mm." Pell closed the bonnet with a click that sounded, in the cold air, like a small lock turning. "Must be hard, keeping track of everything. Papers going in and out. Letters." He looked at Jonah then for the first time properly, and his eyes were the colour of dishwater and entirely without curiosity, which was somehow more frightening than sharpness would have been. Sharp eyes wanted something from you. These eyes only recorded.

"There's been some misplaced correspondence," Halloway called from the step, still cheerful, still smiling. "Official correspondence, I'm afraid. Government property. You know how it is in wartime—things wander. Files take little holidays of their own." He laughed, a warm, hospitable laugh. "We're here to help find them. That's all any of this is. Helping."

*That's all any of this is.* Jonah would turn the sentence over for days afterward, the way one turns over a coin found on a pavement, checking both faces.

* * *

He took the car round front and then, because sitting in the garage felt suddenly like sitting in a trap, he walked the length of the mews and stood smoking where he could see the house. This too broke every rule he had. But there was a letter in his inside pocket—heavier than paper had any right to be—and the weight of it had been riding against his ribs since Tuesday.

It had come in the Tuesday post. A thin envelope, official-looking, addressed to Edith in a clerk's careful hand, postmarked Rouen. Jonah had read it in the garage by lamplight, as he read everything, swiftly and shamefully and with the particular pleasure that reading other people's mail gave him—that sense of standing at a window looking into lit rooms, warmed by other lives. It was a short letter. A covering note from some records office, confirming that certain documents relating to Captain T. Fairweather had been "withdrawn for departmental review" and that enquiries should be directed to Whitehall. It gave a reference number. It gave a name: *H.*

He had read it twice, three times, his breath loud in the cold garage. And then, because he was what he was—a reader of letters, a keeper of things, a coward with good instincts—he had not put it in the tray for Miss Ashford's typewriter. He had put it in his inside pocket. He had driven Edith to a committee meeting and lied when she asked whether the post held anything of note, said *only circulars, ma'am*, and watched in the mirror as she looked out of the window with an expression he had never seen on her before, something stripped and young, and he had thought: *she knows something is wrong even without the letter, and I am the reason she doesn't know what.*

Now he stood in the mews with the letter against his chest and the Wolseley's engine ticking as it cooled, and he understood, with the clarity that fear delivers whole, that the letter was why the men had come—or that the men had come regardless, and the letter was simply the first domino he had stolen from a falling line.

Files take little holidays of their own. Someone at that records office had logged the withdrawal. Someone at Whitehall had traced the covering note. And somewhere in a ledger—Pell's dishwater eyes made Jonah certain there was a ledger—there might be a record of something else entirely: that letters passed through the hands of a Montrose, J., driver, before they reached their addressee. That Montrose, J., had a habit.

Four years of reading. Hundreds of letters. He had told himself it was harmless, that he was a spectator, a footnote, a man warming himself at history's fire without ever touching the coals. Standing in the freezing mews he understood for the first time that a spectator can be seen. That the window he looked through was glass all the way round, and someone had been standing outside it, taking notes.

* * *

The interview lasted forty minutes. Jonah knew because he sat in the car with his watch open on his knee like a man timing a delivery, and because he could see nothing of what happened inside and therefore imagined all of it, which was worse.

At ten past twelve the front door opened. Halloway came out first, bowler in hand, beaming, shaking Mrs. Petch's hand as though she'd offered him a sherry. Pell followed with the document case, square against his chest. Edith appeared in the doorway behind them, upright, immaculate, her face composed into the particular stillness she wore at funerals and public meetings—one mask for grief, one for battle, indistinguishable at a distance.

"Mrs. Fairweather, thank you. Most helpful." Halloway descended the steps. Then he stopped, turned back, and raised his voice pleasantly—not shouting, merely projecting, the way a man projects when he wants to be overheard by a person twenty feet away pretending to check a tyre pressure.

"Oh—and Montrose! If anything else should turn up. Stray papers, envelopes without their letters, letters without their envelopes—" the warm laugh again, shared with the street, shared with the bare plane trees and the passing costermonger's cart— "you'll let us know at once. You drivers, you see everything. Eyes and ears of London, eh?" He tapped the side of his nose. "We do rely on chaps like you. More than you'd think."

Edith's gaze came across the pavement and found Jonah's, and held it for one second, two. Her face did not change. But her eyes asked a question with terrible directness, the way her brother's eyes must have asked questions in France, and Jonah looked down at his watch and closed it and said, "Sir," which was not an answer to anything.

The Wolseley pulled away. At the corner Halloway raised his hat to Jonah, a small jaunty salute, and the car turned out of sight.

* * *

He didn't decide to return the letter. Deciding would have required admitting he had taken it, and there is a species of cowardice so refined that it cannot bear to look at itself directly; it acts sideways, through euphemism, through *putting things right*. What Jonah decided, alone in the garage at dusk with the lamps unlit, was that the letter had been in his pocket by accident, essentially, that a busy man collects papers the way a coat collects snow, and that the correct professional course was to place it in the study tray before Miss Ashford locked up for the night.

The house was quiet. Edith was at a lecture in Hampstead—Clara had given him the address in the hall without looking up from her notebook, and he had heard what she didn't say underneath it: *I know the post has been strange. I know you have been strange.* Or perhaps he only feared she knew, which amounted to the same thing; fear needs no evidence, it prints its own.

The study smelled of ink and coal smoke and, faintly, of her lavender water, which Edith denied using and which lived in the room like a ghost of pre-war summers. The correspondence tray sat on the desk's left side, a battered silver thing dented from a suffrage platform thrown at a meeting in Bristol in 1911. Jonah set the envelope in it, squared it neatly with the day's other papers—two circulars, a bill from the coal merchant, a card from the Women's Social and Political Union—and stood there breathing.

"You're putting it back."

He turned so fast he knocked the inkwell stand. Clara Ashford stood in the doorway with a folder of typescript against her chest and her spectacles pushed up into her hair, and her face was doing something complicated—anger and relief fighting for the same ground and trampling each other.

"I don't know what you mean," Jonah said.

"Don't." She came into the room and shut the door with a click, quietly, the way one closes doors when one doesn't wish to summon anyone. "Don't do that. Not to me." She set the folder on the desk. Her hands, he noticed—they were always what he noticed—were perfectly steady, which meant they had not been steady an hour ago. "Mrs. Fairweather asked me this afternoon whether anything had come from Rouen. She asked me twice. She has never asked me twice about the post in six years. And you're standing at her desk at seven o'clock in the dark, returning an envelope you took on Tuesday, and I'm meant to say *I don't know what you mean*?"

"It wasn't—it wasn't taken. It was misplaced."

"Misplaced." She looked at him the way Pell had looked at the engine of the Wolseley: assessing, recording. But where Pell's eyes had been blank, hers were alive and furious and, beneath it, frightened, and the fright was what undid him. "Into your pocket. Where things go when they're misplaced. Mr. Montrose—Jonah—" and the use of his Christian name, the first time, landed on him like a hand on the shoulder, "what was in it?"

"A covering note. Records office, Rouen. Thomas Fairweather's papers withdrawn for review. Enquiries directed to Whitehall." He recited it flatly, a confession disguised as a report. "Signed off by someone with the initial H."

Clara went very still. Stillness, in her, was louder than speech. Then she pulled the spectacles down onto her nose, which was what she did when she needed to see a thing clearly and did not want her eyes to show what she saw.

"H," she repeated. "The same H whose card is downstairs in the hall rack, which Miss Petch will forget to throw away because it's good card stock. The same H who spent forty minutes asking Mrs. Fairweather, very courteously, whether she had received any correspondence concerning her brother, and whether she kept copies of her letters, and—" Clara stopped. Drew breath. "She said no. She lied beautifully, better than you lie, and she was lying about the same thing you were, and neither of you would tell the other, and I sat there taking shorthand of all of it like a court recorder at a trial where everyone's guilty of nothing but being afraid."

"The letters," Jonah said. "Thomas's letters. Do you—"

"I type what she gives me." A beat. "What she gives me," she said again, softer, and he heard the seam in it, the hidden drawer, and filed it away without understanding it yet.

Outside, a bell began to ring—some church practising for peace, the papers full of armistice rumours now, peace like weather coming in from the east. Clara listened to it with her head tilted, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped its anger and become something quieter and more dangerous: care.

"They know the post was interfered with. Whether they know it was you, I can't say. But Halloway stood on that step and said *eyes and ears of London* loud enough for you to hear, which means either he suspects you or he wants you to think he suspects you, and I genuinely don't know which is worse." She picked up her folder. "Put nothing else in your pockets, Jonah. Whatever you are—footnote, spectator, whatever you tell yourself you are—from tonight you're in the file. There's a file. There's always a file."

She reached the door. Stopped. Did not turn around.

"And Jonah—the letter you returned. Was the inner seal broken when it came to you?"

The cold went through him all over again, a second time, deeper. "How did you—"

"Because I steam seals too," Clara Ashford said, and closed the door behind her, and left him alone in the lamplight with the dented silver tray and the sound of bells ringing for a peace that had not yet come, in a house that was no longer, and perhaps had never been, a place where a bystander could safely stand.
