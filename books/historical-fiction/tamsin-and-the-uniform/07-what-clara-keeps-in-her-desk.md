## 7. What Clara Keeps in Her Desk

There are three locks on the desk in the corner of Edith Fairweather's study, and I am the only person in the house who knows what the third one is for.

The first lock is ordinary—a brass catch that keeps the stationery drawer shut against dust and mice. The second is on the deep drawer where Edith keeps the accounts, because Mrs. Pellow the housekeeper has been known to snoop when she believes herself unobserved, and Edith's household accounts contain subscriptions to publications that would make a bishop faint. The third lock is mine alone. It was fitted in March by a locksmith off Gray's Inn Road who asked no questions, which cost me two extra shillings and taught me a lesson I have never forgotten: discretion is priced like everything else, and it is always more expensive than you think it will be.

Behind the third lock sit four exercise books, schoolroom-plain, their covers marbled grey and blue. In them, in my own careful hand—because no typewriter ribbon can be trusted to keep a secret; ribbons remember everything, every letter pressed into them like footprints in snow—are copies of perhaps sixty letters that have passed through this house since January.

I began copying in the second week of my employment, before I had any reason at all.

People imagine stenographers as furniture with fingers. We sit at the edge of rooms, heads bowed over our pads, and gentlemen talk across us as though we were lamps. But a stenographer hears everything, and more than that, she *keeps* everything—not in paper, which can be seized, but in symbols that look like nothing, that mean nothing to anyone but her. When Captain Halloway's men eventually came asking questions—and they did come, they came twice—I could truthfully say there was no correspondence in my possession. There wasn't. There was only shorthand, and shorthand is not evidence until someone can read it, and I had already burned the key to my own system twice and rebuilt it differently each time.

I did not begin out of loyalty to Edith Fairweather. Let me be honest about that, since honesty is about to become the most dangerous currency in this house. I began because my last employer, a solicitor in Lincoln's Inn, was struck off for concealing documents in a case involving shell contracts, and I watched from three feet away as men in good coats lied with perfect fluency and destroyed a widow's only claim. I sat outside that inquiry with my pad on my knee and realized that I had transcribed every word of the relevant letters months before, and that if anyone had thought to ask me—if anyone had imagined the furniture with fingers might matter—the solicitor would have swung for it, or at least gone to prison, and the widow would have her pension. Nobody asked. I resolved that nobody would ever again have cause not to ask me.

So when Edith Fairweather dictated letters that made her voice go flat and careful—the ones about her brother, the ones addressed to colonels and chaplains and a barrister whose name appeared nowhere else—I copied them. Not all of them. Only the ones where her dictation slowed, where she chose words the way a surgeon chooses instruments. Insurance, I called it, privately. You insure a house against a fire you pray never comes.

The fire, of course, was coming. It had been coming since the letter arrived in October bearing two seals.

* * *

I noticed Jonah Montrose reading the post in November, and I said nothing, because I wanted to know how long he would believe himself invisible.

He is better at it than most. He has the driver's gift of being *placed* rather than present—parked by a kerb, standing by the motor, holding an umbrella, and in each position becoming so much a feature of the street that eyes slide off him. I watched him once take an entire letter from the morning tray, unfold it against the garage wall by lamplight, read it, refold it along its original creases with a precision I confess I admired, and return it to the tray, all in the ninety seconds Edith spent pinning on her hat. He believed he was a ghost. He believed his reading changed nothing, that he was a spectator at a play performed entirely above his station.

He was wrong on both counts, but I understood the temptation. I had sat in enough studies being invisible myself to know the particular hunger of it—the way invisibility curdles after a while into something like grief, though the invisible man is the last to name it.

On the fourth of December, I caught him properly.

Edith had gone up to change for an evening meeting at Caxton Hall. The hall clock stood at ten past six, and the house had gone into that dimmed, held-breath state it keeps between daylight and dinner. I came down the back stairs to fetch my gloves from the study and found the door ajar and Jonah Montrose standing at Edith's desk with a letter half-unfolded in his hands, lit by the green-shaded lamp, his face doing something it never did while driving: thinking openly.

He didn't hear me. That was the remarkable part—that a man so careful could be so careless—but then I have observed that people are always careless with the thing they secretly want most. He wanted to know. He had wanted to know since October, since the double-sealed letter, since whatever he had seen or half-seen that made him lie to Edith about the delivery. Yes, I knew about the lie. A stenographer knows about lies the way a dressmaker knows about seams: they're where the garment actually holds together.

I let him finish. I stood in the doorway with my gloves in my hand and watched him read to the end of the page, watched his thumb smooth the fold, watched him turn toward the tray—

"Second paragraph," I said. "You skipped it. It's the one that matters."

I will give him this: he didn't drop the letter, didn't startle like a guilty schoolboy, didn't attempt the transparent fiction of claiming he'd been straightening the tray. He went very still, the way a man does when he steps off a kerb and hears the motor horn too late, and then he turned around and looked at me with the letter still in his hand, and what was in his face was not fear exactly. It was relief wearing fear's coat. As if he had been waiting months for someone to catch him, and the waiting had been worse than any punishment.

"Miss Ashford," he said.

"You've read eleven letters that I know of," I told him. "Since the twenty-third of October. You always refold along the original creases, which is clever, but you put them back rotated a few degrees clockwise, every time, without fail. Left-handed men do that." I crossed the room and took the letter from him—gently; there seemed no purpose in cruelty—and set it on the desk. "Edith thinks the maid misdelivers her post. She blames Nora's cold."

"I never—" He stopped. Whatever defence he'd begun to build collapsed under its own weight, and he stood there in the lamplight looking younger than his thirty-odd years and considerably more tired. "How long have you known?"

"Since the third week of October. You want to know why I didn't say anything."

"Yes," he said, and then, honestly: "No. I'm not certain I want to hear the answer."

"The answer is that I wanted to see whether you'd do anything with what you read," I said. "A man who reads other people's mail is one of two things. He's a thief, or he's a witness. Thieves I report. Witnesses—" I looked at him, at this man who drove a suffragist through a city that hated her and carried her letters like unexploded shells and had never once, in eleven opportunities, sold one, leaked one, whispered one to a publican or a pretty girl. "Witnesses I keep."

* * *

We did not trust each other. That must be understood. What happened next in that study was not friendship; it was two armed nations discovering they share a border and deciding, provisionally, not to shell it.

He asked me what was behind the third lock, of course. Or rather he didn't ask—he was too well-mannered and too frightened—but he looked at the desk with a question in his face so loud I could practically read it aloud, and I decided that if we were going to be co-conspirators of any kind, even the silent kind, he should understand the shape of the thing he'd blundered into.

"Lock that door," I said.

He did. Then I unlocked the third drawer and took out the top exercise book and laid it open on the desk between us, under the lamp, and turned pages while he leaned in and his face went through recognition, disbelief, and something close to awe in the space of four pages.

"That's—" he began.

"The Vireaux material, yes. Thomas Fairweather's last dispatch, copied out entire, the night Edith dictated it to me in September and then locked the original away. Every letter she's written to the War Office about it. Halloway's two visits—yes, I transcribed those too; I take notes in church, Mr. Montrose, I take notes everywhere, it's an affliction. And the requisition slip you lifted from the records office in November." I watched him flinch. "Don't worry. I'm not going to ask where you keep it. I'm telling you that if anything happens to me—to either of us—there is a complete duplicate account of everything this house knows, written in a cipher that exists in exactly one head, and that head is currently attached to my shoulders."

"Why are you showing me this?" His voice had dropped to almost nothing. Outside, somewhere down the mews, a cart rattled past and faded.

"Because you're going to be caught," I said simply. "Not by me. By them. Men like Halloway don't visit a house twice out of courtesy. They'll find out about you eventually—you rotate the letters clockwise, for God's sake, you leave fingerprints of habit everywhere—and when they do, they won't threaten you first. They'll offer you money. They always offer money first, because money tells them what a man is made of far faster than fear does." I closed the exercise book. "When that happens, you will need to know that someone else in this house already knows everything, so that lying to them costs you nothing. You can't betray a secret that's already shared. That's not blackmail, Mr. Montrose. That's armour."

"And if I refuse your armour?"

"Then tomorrow morning Edith learns that her driver reads her brother's death warrant by lamplight for pleasure, and you can find another position, and I will have learned what you're made of at very little cost to me." I said it evenly, without heat, because heat would have suggested I cared about his answer more than I was prepared to admit. "But I don't think you'll refuse."

"No," he said quietly. "Neither do I."

* * *

Here is the part I have never told anyone, and will set down now only because a record kept too long goes stale and begins to lie.

I tested him that night. Not with kindness—with a lie.

While the exercise book lay open between us, I mentioned—I let it fall casually, the way one lets a glove fall—that Edith had received a telegram that afternoon confirming a Colonel Maitland would call on Thursday regarding "the disposition of certain field records." None of that was true. There was no telegram. There was no Colonel Maitland. I invented the name on the walk downstairs, plucked it from nowhere, and watched it land.

Jonah nodded. Absorbed it. Asked one question—"Will you be taking the dictation?"—and answered his own worry aloud: "Someone should warn her to be careful what she says in front of him."

And there it was. Any man who had already sold information—who was reading for profit or reporting to a handler—would have repeated Maitland's name within the day, because invented details are the cheapest currency in espionage and handlers pay for novelty. A man who intended to warn Edith instead would hold the name like a hot coal, wanting to put it down safely, wanting it to reach no one.

I gave him three days. On the Thursday, when no colonel arrived and no mention of Maitland surfaced anywhere in London or in this house, I brought him his tea in the garage—brought it myself, which caused Nora to gossip for a week—and said, "There was no telegram. There's no Colonel Maitland."

He looked at the tea. Then at me. I watched him work it through, the slow colour rising in his face—not anger, not yet; understanding first, and anger arriving just behind it like thunder after lightning.

"You lied to me."

"I tested you. There's a difference, and the difference is that I told you the truth afterward, which liars never do." I set the cup on the workbench beside the jack handles. "You passed, incidentally. Though you took eleven minutes longer than I'd have liked deciding what to do with it, and you chewed your lip the whole time, which in future will get you killed."

"You could have just trusted me."

"One doesn't trust, Mr. Montrose. One verifies, and then one trusts, and then one verifies again, forever, because the war didn't make the world dishonest—it only taught everyone the prices." I turned to go, and then stopped, because there was one thing more, and I found—annoyingly—that I wanted him to have it. "For what it's worth. The night you read the letter at the desk, the second paragraph you supposedly skipped—it concerned a chaplain's ledger. Edith wrote to a Reverend Aldous about it in September. I have his reply behind the third lock. If you're going to risk your neck reading other people's post, you ought at least read the right parts."

His mouth opened and closed. For a moment he looked less like a driver and more like a man standing at the edge of deep water, judging the cold.

"Miss Ashford," he said finally, "why are you helping me?"

I considered several answers on the stairs back up to the study—professional solidarity, contempt for Halloway, simple loneliness dressed in better clothes. All of them true. None of them sufficient. The sufficient answer I kept, and it was this: because I had spent eighteen months copying the secrets of brave women into schoolroom notebooks, hiding them behind a brass lock like a girl keeping diary, and I was tired—bone-tired—of being the only invisible person in the house. Two ghosts can hold a door open for each other. That is not love. It may, in time, become something adjacent to it.

"It's not helping," I said over my shoulder. "It's insurance. I told you."

He drank his tea in the cold garage, and upstairs Edith pinned on her hat, and the third drawer stayed locked, and the city went on burning its coal and burying its boys, and neither of us yet knew that within a fortnight one of us would be offered thirty pounds a month to destroy the other—or that the offer would come, as such offers do, smiling, in a warm kitchen, from a man who genuinely believed he was being reasonable.
