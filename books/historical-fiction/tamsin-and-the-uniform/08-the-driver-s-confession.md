## 8. The Driver's Confession

The house had a way of emptying itself at half past six. Mrs. Quill went home to Camberwell on the tram; the cook left by the garden door with her coat over her arm like a woman escaping a fire; and Edith Fairweather withdrew to her study for the evening hours she called *correspondence* and everyone else called the war. By seven the only sounds were the clock in the hall and, if the wind was right, the distant thud of guns across the water—a sound so far off now it had become almost domestic, like a neighbour hammering shelves.

Jonah stayed in the garage because it was his.

Not legally—legally it was Mr. Fairweather's garage, and before that it had been a stable, and before that perhaps a place where people stored coal or grief—but in every way that mattered, it was the one room in London he could close a door on. He kept it swept. He kept the Daimler under canvas even though Mr. Fairweather would never notice dust. And on the shelf above the workbench, behind a tin of brass polish, he kept the letters.

Not all of them. He wasn't a thief. That distinction mattered to him more than it should have, and he knew it, and it made no difference to anything he did. He copied them. Longhand, in a schoolboy's copperplate he'd learned from a parish priest who'd beaten the flourishes out of him until only the discipline remained. He copied them and then he returned the originals to their envelopes, licked the gum, pressed the flap flat, and carried on as though paper could forget being opened.

It couldn't. Paper remembered everything. That was rather the point of him.

Tonight there were three letters on the bench under the shaded lamp: one from a Manchester suffragist branch complaining about hall hire; one from a barrister whose handwriting looked as though it had been written during an earthquake; and one—the third—he hadn't touched yet. It sat square in the lamplight, addressed to Miss E. Fairweather in a hand Jonah did not know, and beneath it lay the requisition slip from the records office, four days old now, going soft at the folds where he'd worried it between thumb and finger like a rosary.

*File withdrawn. Refer to Ministry.*

Four days of not knowing what that meant, and the not-knowing had begun to behave like something alive. It ate first thing in the morning when he checked the tyres. It sat beside him at lunch. At night it climbed into bed on the cold side and lay facing him.

He heard the side door before he heard the footsteps—that particular complaint the hinge gave when someone didn't bother to lift it—and then Clara Ashford came down the three steps into the garage with her coat buttoned wrong and her hat pinned at an angle suggesting haste or defiance, and with her, Jonah thought, the two were frequently indistinguishable.

"I saw your light," she said. "Everyone else has gone home."

"Yes."

"You're sitting in a garage reading letters."

"Checking the route for tomorrow." The lie arrived smoothly, which was its own kind of evidence against him.

Clara looked at the bench. At the lamp angled the way a lamp is angled over a page, not over a map. At the tin of brass polish with its lid slightly askew, though of course she couldn't know what lived behind it, and he watched her decide not to know it, and understood that this was a mercy with a bill attached.

"The Manchester letter is about hall hire," she said. "I typed it myself on Tuesday."

"Then I'm checking it thoroughly."

Something happened to her mouth—not quite a smile, more the place a smile goes to wait—and she crossed the concrete floor and stood at the edge of the lamplight the way people stand at the edge of water they've decided to enter but haven't yet.

"Mr. Montrose."

"Miss Ashford."

"Do you know what I did before this?" she asked. "Before I came to the house?"

"Stenography. shorthand. You're very good."

"Before that. During." She unbuttoned her coat—one motion, decisive, the wrong-buttoned coat finally surrendered to gravity—and hung it on the nail by the door as though she'd been invited, which she hadn't, and which somehow made the room smaller and warmer at once. "I worked for a firm off Finsbury Pavement. Import agents. And my employer read the post. All of it. Business post, private post, letters from his clerks' wives asking after money. He'd steam them open over the kettle with a pair of tongs and a look of profound boredom. Everyone knew. Nobody said. Because saying it meant admitting you'd noticed, and noticing meant you might be next."

Jonah said nothing. The clock in the hall struck the quarter-hour through two floors and a brick wall, patient as artillery.

"He died last spring," Clara went on. "Heart, at his desk, mid-letter. And when they cleared the office they found forty years of correspondence filed twice—once by addressee and once by subject. Every affair, every debt, every quarrel. Catalogued. A man spent his whole life building a museum of other people's secrets and never once appeared in it himself." She turned and looked at Jonah directly, and the lamplight did something honest to her face, taking away the office composure and leaving just a tired, sharp young woman standing in a cold room. "I think about him more than I'd like."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you have ink on your fingers."

He looked down. His right hand, resting on the requisition slip, wore a small blue smudge along the second knuckle—the ghost of the copying he'd done at midnight, two nights past, Thomas Fairweather's last dispatch rendered in copperplate by lamplight while the house slept. He could deny it. He was good at denying things; he'd had years of practice and no audience worth the effort.

Instead, he found, he was already talking.

"It started in Belgium," he said, "or it started before Belgium, depending on how you count. My father drove for a shipping agent in Antwerp. Same trade—letters, parcels, seals. When the war came we came to England, and I took whatever work kept me near engines. Then Mrs. Fairweather hired me, and—" He stopped. Started again, lower. "She trusts me absolutely. That's the terrible part. She leaves letters on the hall table because Jonah will take them to the post, Jonah is reliable, Jonah sees nothing. And I see everything."

The words came out with the strange ease of a splinter leaving skin—pain and relief in the same motion, indistinguishable.

"Two minutes," he said. "That's all it takes. A kettle, a knife, patience. I read them in the garage and copy the ones that matter and put them back and drive her wherever she wants to go the next morning as though I'm a set of hands attached to a steering wheel and nothing else." His laugh came out wrong, too short. "I told myself I was nobody. A spectator. History happens to other people and I hold the door. Four years I've told myself that."

"And now?"

"Now there's a file missing from a government office and a letter sealed twice and men who ask polite questions, and I find that I'm not a spectator at all. I'm evidence."

Clara was quiet long enough that he could hear the Daimler cooling somewhere under its canvas, the small metallic tick of contracting metal, like a slow clock counting down something.

"Coward," she said at last.

The word landed exactly where she'd aimed it.

"Yes," he said.

"But also—" she came fully into the light now, pulled the other stool from the corner, and sat down across the bench from him so that the requisition slip lay between them like a border crossing, "—a witness. Do you understand the difference? A coward hides because hiding costs nothing. You hid and it cost you four years of pretending to be furniture. But a witness—" she tapped the slip once, sharply, "—a witness keeps records. You copied Thomas's dispatches. Don't look surprised; I type everything she receives, I know which envelopes come back lighter than they went out, I've known since March. Your copies are better than hers. Hers are in her hand and her hand shakes now."

"You knew." It wasn't a question. It barely qualified as speech.

"I knew," Clara said. "And I said nothing, and you said nothing, and we have spent four months passing each other in corridors like two spies who've each correctly identified the other and each decided the other isn't worth arresting." She almost smiled. "Which is either cowardice or diplomacy, and I've been unable to settle which."

"What changed?"

"The requisition slip." She nodded at it. "You stole a piece of government paper, Mr. Montrose. Small, grey, worthless-looking, and entirely illegal. And you didn't do it for yourself—I've watched you for months, you don't do anything for yourself—you did it because a dead man's file has gone missing and somebody official wanted it missing, and that means the file mattered, and if it mattered then the letters matter, and if the letters matter—" She spread her hands. "You're not a spectator. Spectators don't steal."

The engine ticked. Somewhere outside a cart went by on the wet road, the horse's hooves making that soft sucking sound London pavements make in November.

"If Halloway's men learn what I've been doing," Jonah said slowly, "reading, copying—it's not dismissal. You understand that? It's not even prison, necessarily. Men who can lose a war file can lose a chauffeur. There are roads at night and motors that go fast and inquests that record *misadventure* without anyone ever asking a question twice."

"I know."

"Knowing it, you're still sitting here."

"I'm sitting here," Clara said, "because I keep copies too."

The garage seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a ship does when it changes heading.

"In my desk," she went on, calm as a timetable. "The sensitive correspondence. Vireaux. The names the ministry men pretend they've never heard of. I began in June, after Thomas's third-to-last letter came, and I couldn't tell you why except that it felt like the letters were trying to be destroyed and someone ought to argue. Carbon paper is cheap and nobody counts sheets." She met his eyes. "So. You read everything and copy some. I type everything and copy most. Between us, Mr. Montrose, we are the most complete record of this household in existence, and neither of us is supposed to exist at all."

Jonah felt something loosen in his chest that had been tight so long he'd mistaken it for his skeleton. Shame, yes—shame was still there, would always be there, the priest's voice from Antwerp saying *what is hidden will be shouted from rooftops*. But underneath it, threaded through it like wire through wax, was relief. She knew. The worst fact about him was known, and spoken aloud, and she was still sitting on the stool with her coat on the nail, calling him a coward and a witness in the same breath, which was, he was beginning to suspect, simply the truth wearing two coats.

"Why tell me?" he asked. "Really. You could have gone to Mrs. Fairweather weeks ago. 'Your driver reads your mail.' One sentence. I'd have been gone by breakfast."

"Because she wouldn't believe it," Clara said. "Or worse—she would, and it would break something in her that she needs whole. She trusts you the way she trusted Thomas. Do you know what that's worth?" She leaned forward, forearms on the bench, close enough that he could smell typewriter ribbon and rain. "And because the men from the ministry will come back. Not for her—for the edges of her. The staff. The mail. They always start with the people nobody looks at. And when they do, I want the person sitting across from them to be the man who reads everything, not the man they think does."

"They'll offer money."

"Of course they'll offer money."

"And if I take it?"

Clara studied him for a long moment, and whatever she was looking for, she appeared to find, because she nodded once, businesslike, and stood, and reached for her coat, and the interview was evidently over—which was its own kind of answer.

"Then take it," she said, buttoning it correctly this time, top to bottom, unhurried. "Take their money and give them nothing and keep your copies dry. Just remember, when you're standing in your kitchen deciding what to feed them—" she paused at the steps, hand on the rail, lamplight catching her profile, "—that the difference between a double agent and a traitor is only whether the second side wins."

She went up the steps. The hinge complained. The side door shut, and Jonah sat alone in the yellow circle of the lamp with a stolen requisition slip, a tin of brass polish full of other people's lives, and the extraordinary, terrifying knowledge that for four years he had believed himself invisible, and had instead been observed—watched, catalogued, and apparently judged worth keeping.

He blew out the lamp.

In the dark, the requisition slip felt heavier than paper should. *Refer to Ministry.* Tomorrow he would drive Edith to a committee meeting; tomorrow evening the ministry men might return; and somewhere between those two appointments he would have to decide what kind of coward he intended to be—the kind who hides, or the kind who takes the enemy's coin and keeps his own account books.

He was still deciding when the front bell rang—late, urgent, three pulls—and through the garage's small window he saw, at the end of the drive, a motorcar he did not recognise, idling, its headlights cutting two white holes in the fog.

They hadn't waited for morning after all.
