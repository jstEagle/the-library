## 9. An Offer of Invisibility

They came back on a Thursday, when the rain had settled into the streets like a tenant who intended to stay.

Jonah was in the garage wiping down the bonnet of the Napier when he heard the front bell, and then Mrs. Kettle's footsteps crossing the hall, and then—this was what stopped his rag mid-circuit—the low, unhurried voices of two men. He knew one of them by its texture before he heard a single word. It was the voice from the study three weeks earlier, the one that had asked about misplaced correspondence with the courtesy of a man discussing the weather. Halloway. The name had come to Jonah later, unbidden, from somewhere: a mention in a letter, a signature half-glimpsed. *A. Halloway.* It sat in his memory now like a splinter worked under the skin.

He did not go up. He stayed where he was, hands in the engine's warmth, and listened to the house absorb them. Doors opened and closed. Edith's voice, cool and distant as the Thames in January. Then silence, long enough for tea to be poured and not drunk.

It was nearly an hour before the footsteps came down instead of out.

"Mr. Montrose." Halloway stood at the top of the garage steps with his hat already on, which was somehow more menacing than if he'd taken it off and settled in. He was a grey man, grey-suited, with the kind of face that would be impossible to describe to a police artist because it was precisely like every other face. Beside him, silent as furniture, stood the younger one—the one who had done all the listening last time, whose name nobody had offered and Jonah hadn't dared invent. "We wondered if we might trouble you for a moment."

"I'm meant to have the car round at half past," Jonah said. "Miss Fairweather's going to Hampstead."

"She's gone to her sister's in Kensington this morning," Halloway said mildly. "Telephone message. You'll have been told."

Jonah hadn't been told. The realization arrived coldly, itemized: they had rung the house; someone had answered; Edith's morning had been rearranged or invented without his knowledge. He was being handled, and the handling had begun before he'd even known there were hands.

"I'll check my orders," he said.

"No need." Halloway came down the steps into the garage. The younger man remained at the top, positioned neatly against the light, so that Jonah would spend the whole conversation squinting past him into the grey. "It isn't Miss Fairweather we've come about. It's you."

*You.* The word dropped through the oil-smell and the damp like a stone through pond water. Jonah kept his cloth in his hand. There is an art to being invisible, and he had practiced it for years, and the first rule of it was never to look surprised. He looked like a man waiting to hear how much longer he'd be needed.

"I'm just the driver," he said.

"Just the driver." Halloway smiled without any movement above the cheekbones. He walked slowly along the Napier, trailing one gloved finger along the wing as though inspecting it for sale, and Jonah understood that the inspection was of him, that every step was calibrated to be watched. "Do you know what I admire about your profession, Mr. Montrose? A man drives. He sees everything. He hears everything. Every appointment, every visitor, every telephone call conducted in the hall. And no one remembers he was present. It's rather remarkable, when you think of it. The most complete intelligence officer in London, sitting behind a wheel, and nobody thinks to brief him or debrief him or even learn his surname."

"You know my surname."

"We make it our business to know everyone's surname." Halloway stopped by the car's open door and looked in at the seat where Edith Fairweather rode every day, where her gloves left their impression on the leather, where letters rode in her bag within reach of the man at the wheel. "Tell me, do you read?"

"Road signs."

The smile again. "I'm told she dictates correspondence in the motorcar sometimes. On the longer runs. That she works through her post while you drive her to meetings, because she says a moving car is the only place in London a woman can finish a sentence."

"Women talk in cars," Jonah said. "It passes the miles."

"Mm." Halloway drew off one glove, finger by finger, and Jonah found himself watching the small ritual with the attention of a man watching a surgeon prepare. "Here is our difficulty, Mr. Montrose. Miss Fairweather is a distinguished woman. She marched, she was imprisoned, she has earned a certain—latitude. But the war has strained her. Her brother's death has strained her further. Grief does strange things to the finest minds, and lately her correspondence has become—" he selected the word with visible pleasure— "*unstable*. Accusations. Conspiracies. References to matters that are quite properly the business of the War Office and quite improperly the business of a grieving lady with a printing press and too many influential friends. We should hate to see her reputation suffer."

"And I'm to protect her reputation," Jonah said, "by telling you what she writes."

"I'm to *offer* you the opportunity to serve your country in a capacity suited to your talents." The second glove came off. From inside his coat Halloway produced an envelope—not thick, but of good paper—and set it on the Napier's running board with the care of a man laying a card on a table. "There's five pounds there. A retainer, if you like. Ten more each month, delivered wherever you prefer. For information. Nothing dramatic. Who calls. What she burns. Whether her letters go out by post or by hand, and to whom. Whether anyone in this household assists her with copies."

*Whether anyone in this household assists her with copies.*

Clara. The name arrived whole, with her sharp grey eyes attached, and Jonah had to turn toward the workbench as though considering, because something in his face wanted very much to move.

"There's a girl does her shorthand," he said. Neutral. Testing.

"There is indeed. Miss Ashford. Clever, apparently. Rather too clever for a stenographer, some say." Halloway let that hang, then took it gently back. "But then, that isn't your concern. You're a driver. You watch roads. You happen also to watch people, because you can't help it—it's in your nature, we understand. All we ask is that you write down, once a week, what you can't help noticing. Address here." He produced a card and set it beside the envelope. "No name required after the first. Just the initials J.M., and a date. Like a diary. Men keep diaries."

"And if I say no?"

Halloway sighed, and for a moment—and this was the worst of it, this was the thing Jonah would lie awake turning over—he sounded genuinely kind. "Then nothing happens tomorrow. Or next week. But a man with no trade references beyond a chauffeur's character note, a man whose mother lives alone in Camden Town on what his wages supplement—I believe there's a sister as well? In service in Edinburgh?" He put his glove back on, unhurried, the transaction concluded in advance. "Nothing happens to any of you, Mr. Montrose. That's the point. Nothing ever has to happen. One simply remains available to being helpful. And men who remain helpful live quiet lives. Men who decline to be helpful—" he glanced around the garage, the tools, the petrol cans, the pit dark beneath the car— "well. Motorcars are dangerous things. Petrol. Brakes. So much machinery in the world, and all of it indifferent."

The rain ticked on the roof. Somewhere above them, in the house, Clara's typewriter began—a burst of quick strokes, a pause, another burst—steady as a pulse in the ceiling.

"The car will be wanted at half past," Jonah said.

"It will." Halloway replaced his hat. At the foot of the steps he paused, and did not turn fully around, and spoke to the wall. "You'll notice the envelope contains five pounds. If it's still there tomorrow, we'll draw our conclusions. If it's gone—" he shrugged, a small economical motion— "we'll draw different ones. Either way, we'll know by tonight. Good afternoon, Mr. Montrose."

The younger man moved aside to let him pass and followed without a word, and the front door opened and shut, and the house exhaled.

* * *

Jonah stood in the garage for a long time.

The envelope lay on the running board, white as a wound dressing. Five pounds. More than a week of his wages. Enough to keep his mother in coal through February. Enough for the sister's Christmas parcel. He thought of the terrible ease of it—that they had done their arithmetic on him the way he now must do arithmetic on himself, and theirs was simpler: everyone has a price, and mostly it isn't money. Mostly it's a mother's cold rooms. Mostly it's the word *nothing happens*, repeated softly enough.

He could take the envelope to Edith. Lay it on her desk, tell her everything—the first visit, the questions about misplaced correspondence, the file at the records office with the clerk's frightened warning, the requisition slip folded flat in his waistcoat pocket these three weeks like a confession he couldn't finish. Tell her and be dismissed, probably. Be sent away, certainly, because a master cannot keep a servant who has been turned, whatever he turned back again. And then the next driver would be a man who didn't read letters, didn't care, didn't love—

He caught at the word. Set it down. Picked it up again. *Didn't love* whom? Edith? Yes. Yes, Edith. Certainly Edith.

And Clara's typewriter went on overhead, patient as rain.

Or he could take the money.

He saw it clearly then, the shape of the thing they'd built for him: not a bribe but a *position*. They didn't want information, or not chiefly. They wanted him owned. A man who takes the king's shilling reports to the king whether he means to or not, because the taking itself is the crime, and a criminal may be spent whenever convenient. They would own him the way they owned the clerk at the records office, the way they perhaps owned someone else in this house already—because surely they did, surely that was how they'd known his mother's coal bill, his sister's situation, the geography of his whole small invisible life.

Unless.

Unless a man took the shilling and gave them nothing. Unless a man sat in their machine and fed it sand. He pictured it: the weekly letter, unsigned, initialled, full of weather. *Miss Fairweather received callers Tuesday. She burned papers Wednesday, quantity unknown, likely kitchen refuse.* A double agent in his own kitchen, reporting faithfully on cabbage stalks. It was absurd. It was suicidal. They would see through it in a month, and then the machinery they'd hinted at—petrol, brakes, indifference—would stop hinting.

And if he refused outright, they'd simply find another pair of eyes. The cook's new nephew. The boy who delivered the laundry. The house had more windows than he could watch.

The arithmetic ran itself, cruel and clean. Refuse, and abandon the field to whoever they installed next. Accept, and hold the ground himself—hold Clara's desk, hold Edith's letters, hold the single point of view from which everything passed through his hands. Be the traitor they thought they'd bought and be, in fact, the only guard on the gate.

*You're a coward,* he told himself, and heard Clara saying it, and knew she'd say it again if she ever learned. *And a witness.*

He picked up the envelope. It weighed almost nothing. That was the horror of it—five pounds, a family's safety, a woman's reputation, a dead captain's honor, and the whole bundle lighter than a folded map.

He put it in his inside pocket, over his heart, where he kept the requisition slip. Two documents now, pressed together: one stolen for the truth, one accepted for the lie. He was becoming a filing cabinet for other people's secrets, and the cabinet had begun keeping secrets of its own.

* * *

That evening the house was quiet. Edith had dined early and retired with a headache; Mrs. Kettle had gone home through the rain; and the study lamp burned past eleven because Edith, headaches notwithstanding, always burned it past eleven.

Jonah came in through the side door to bank the scullery boiler, and found Clara in the servants' hall with her coat still on, drying her hair with a towel, laughing at something in a magazine—and stopping the laugh when she saw his face.

"Well?" she said. Not *what's wrong*. She never wasted words on the obvious. "You've the look of a man who's been given change he doesn't trust."

"They came today." He kept his voice below the boiler's mutter. "The ministry. Not upstairs—to me. Here, in the garage."

Her eyes sharpened, and she set down the towel, and he watched her decide to give him her whole attention, which he was learning was the most expensive thing Clara Ashford owned. "About the letters."

"About me." He told it plainly, because there was no other way to tell it: the offer, the envelope, the mother in Camden, the sister in Edinburgh, the gentle inventory of everything he loved laid out like goods at auction. He did not tell her about the slip in his waistcoat, or about what he'd read in the sealed-twice letter, or about the nights he'd spent with her copied pages in the lamplight of the garage—she had caught him once and tested him once, and between them lay an understanding built of mutual blackmail and something warmer neither of them named. But he told her the rest.

When he finished, she was quiet for a moment. Rain ran down the little window. Above them, faintly, the study floor creaked as Edith crossed and recrossed her territory.

"You took it," Clara said. Not a question. She'd seen his coat, maybe. Seen the shape in the pocket.

"I took it."

"And what are you going to give them?"

"Nothing. Weather. Cabbage stalks. A weekly letter so dull it stops a clock."

She looked at him for a long moment, and he braced for the word *coward*, ready to take it, half wanting it. Instead she said, "They'll test you. Early. Something small, something they can verify. And when you fail the test—and you will, Jonah, because you can't help being honest in your face—they'll know what you are. And then the envelope becomes evidence instead of wages."

"Then I'll need to pass the test."

"With lies?" Her mouth curved, without warmth. "You lied to Edith once, over a letter, and it's been eating you alive for weeks. You're going to lie weekly, in writing, to men who kill people with machinery?"

"Yes," he said. And heard how it sounded. And said it again, quieter, because some things only become true the second time. "Yes. Because if I don't stand where they put me, someone else will, and he won't be standing there for us."

"*Us*," Clara repeated softly.

"The household," he said. "The cause. Whatever we're calling it."

Whatever we're calling it. She rose, hung the towel precisely on its rail, and at the door she paused with her hand on the latch, not looking at him.

"For what it's worth," she said, "I'd have taken the money too. And I'd have fed them cabbage stalks. But Jonah—" now she did look at him, and the lamplight made her eyes the colour of wet slate— "write your first letter carefully. Because I have a feeling they're reading it over your shoulder already. Someone in this house told them your mother's coal bill. And it wasn't me."

She went out, and her footsteps climbed the stairs, and Jonah stood alone in the warm dark of the scullery with five pounds of somebody else's intentions against his heart, understanding suddenly that the trap had never been the envelope at all.

The trap was the informer. And the informer was already inside.
