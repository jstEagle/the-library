## 10. The Armistice Rumor

The morning the rumor came down Fetter Lane and into every kitchen in London, Clara Ashford was already awake.

She had been awake since four, in fact, lying in her narrow bed in the house on Cheyne Row with the gas lamp turned low, listening to the wind work at the window frames and thinking about carbon paper. This was what her life had become: a woman of twenty-six, educated at a good school in Cheltenham, trained to take dictation at a hundred and twenty words a minute, lying awake at four in the morning thinking about the grain of carbon paper and which brand held a cleaner impression—Eagle, or the German stuff Edith Fairweather had laid in before the war and guarded like contraband ever since.

It was not an entirely unpleasant way to live. It was simply not the life she had imagined for herself, back when she had imagined such things.

She rose, dressed in the dark, pinned her hair with the efficiency of long practice, and went downstairs past the sleeping house. In the hall, the morning papers had been pushed through the letter slot and lay fanned across the tiles like dropped cards. She gathered them up out of habit—she was always the first down; Mrs. Petch did not come until seven—and carried them into the morning room to read by the light of the one bar of the electric fire she allowed herself.

Even before she unfolded them, she knew. You could smell it in newsprint now, the way you could smell rain coming off the river. The papers had begun to smell of celebration, the way a theatre smells different before the curtain than after it. The *Express* had gone in for headlines like a drunk goes in for sentiment: PEACE NEAR — GERMANY'S ANSWER EXPECTED WITHIN DAYS. Inside, a cartoon of a dove perched on a shattered gun carriage, drawn by someone who had never seen a gun carriage except as a subject.

Clara read all four papers, cover to cover, because reading was her profession and also, increasingly, her armor. If you knew what everyone was saying, you knew what no one was saying. What no one was saying was anything about Vireaux. What no one was saying was anything about Captain Thomas Fairweather, late of the 9th Somersets, killed in the autumn advance of 1917 and buried in the official record under a version of events that Edith Fairweather had spent three weeks and most of her savings trying to dig out from underneath.

What no one was saying would fill a supplement, if anyone ever let it be said.

She heard the front door at half past six—the soft double click of the latch that meant Jonah Montrose, back from whatever errand he ran at dawn two mornings a week and never explained. She heard his boots in the passage, the small pause he always made outside the morning room door when he saw light under it, and then, instead of moving on, a knock.

"Miss Ashford. You're up early."

"The papers were early." She looked up at him over the top of the *Chronicle*. He stood in the doorway with his cap in one hand and rain still beaded on the shoulders of his coat, and he had the look of a man who has been holding a breath for some hours. "Is something wrong?"

"Why would something be wrong?"

"Because you ask questions like a man rehearsing innocence."

Something moved behind his eyes—she had learned to read him the way she read handwriting, from pressure and slant—and he almost smiled. "There's a crowd at the corner of the King's Road," he said. "Singing. At six in the morning, singing. Someone's told them it's nearly over."

"Someone tells them that every week."

"This time they're singing like they believe it." He set his cap down on the hall table, straightened it twice, and said, without looking at her, "Miss Fairweather will want the car early today. There's a meeting."

"I know about the meeting," Clara said, which was true, and also a lie by omission, because what she actually knew about the meeting was considerably more than that, and Jonah Montrose's face when she said *I know* suggested he suspected as much. They had reached a strange country, the two of them, a country with no name and no map, where each of them knew the other kept secrets and neither could say so aloud. She found she did not entirely hate it. That frightened her more than the ministry men did.

He went out to the garage. Clara folded the newspapers into a neat stack, squared their edges, and went up to wake Miss Fairweather.

* * *

Edith Fairweather was fifty-one years old and dressed, as always, as though the house might be raided before luncheon: plain grey wool, sensible boots, hair pinned so severely it looked like an act of discipline. She was at her desk before Clara knocked, and there was a document spread out before her that Clara recognized at once and pretended not to—the chaplain's ledger copy, the one with the date column that didn't match the dispatches, the discrepancy around which the whole terrible question of Vireaux turned like a key in a lock.

"You've seen the papers," Edith said. It was not a question either.

"I have."

"Sit down, Miss Ashford." Clara sat. Edith laced her fingers together on the desk and regarded her stenographer for a long moment with those pale, unblinking eyes that had stared down cabinet ministers and magistrates and, once, memorably, a mounted policeman at Westminster who had made the mistake of smiling at her.

"In three weeks," Edith said, "perhaps less, this war will end. And when it ends, every man in this government who has anything to hide will take advantage of the noise. Bells are very convenient things, Miss Ashford. They drown out a great deal." She turned the ledger page with one finger. "I have decided that I am done waiting for permission."

"Permission from whom?"

"From anyone." Edith said it flatly, without drama, the way she said everything. "I am going to publish Thomas's letters. All of them. The dispatches, the chaplain's record, the order given at Vireaux and denied within forty-eight hours and its blame transferred to men too dead to object. I am going to print it while the war is still technically running, so that it cannot be dismissed as a peacetime grievance, and I am going to do it knowing precisely what it will cost me." She paused. "I want you to understand what I'm asking, because I'm asking you rather than telling you, and that distinction matters to me."

Clara's hands had gone cold in her lap. Not from fear, exactly—or not only fear. It was the coldness of a thing becoming real, the way a sketch becomes a person. For eight months she had copied Edith's correspondence in secret, sheet after sheet into her locked desk drawer, insurance against fire and seizure and the quiet ministry men with their courteous voices, and through all of it a part of her had believed—had needed to believe—that the copies were the end of it. That the truth would stay in the drawer like a photograph of a dead man, looked at privately, mourned privately, safe.

"And my part?" she asked.

"Your part is the same as it has always been, only faster and without the shorthand. When we go to press, no machine-written copy must exist that can be seized and read. Shorthand is a cipher; mine is idiosyncratic enough to be useless to strangers. You will transcribe the Vireaux material in full—every dispatch, the ledger pages, Thomas's last letter home—into fair copy, and you will keep the fair copy on your person until the moment it reaches the printer. Not in your desk. On your person."

"On my person," Clara repeated.

"Yes." Edith met her eyes. "I am aware of what that means. If they stop you, it is you they stop, not me. I won't pretend otherwise, and I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. But I will tell you this: I did not march for fifteen years so that I could stand aside at the end and let the victors write the peace however they please. A peace built on a lie is just the war wearing different clothes. I did not march for a peace that lies."

The words went into Clara somewhere below the ribs and stayed there, warm and heavy, like a coal passed hand to hand.

"You're assuming," Clara said carefully, "that they don't already know. About the letters. About—" she hesitated over the word— "*us*. The household."

"They know something," Edith said. "They have known something since September, when those two gentlemen came asking about misplaced correspondence with faces like undertakers. Knowing and proving are different animals, Miss Ashford, and I intend to keep them different animals until the ink is dry." She drew a folder from the drawer and laid it flat between them. "We begin this morning. Take it down."

* * *

The Vireaux dispatch ran to nine pages in Thomas Fairweather's hand—a beautiful hand, Clara thought, a hand that had been punished at some expensive school, upright and clear even in its final days—and she took it down in shorthand at full speed while Edith read aloud in a voice that never varied and never softened, and both of them understood that the voice was a structure, load-bearing, and that if either of them acknowledged what was in the pages the other would have to leave the room.

*Order received from Brigade at 0410 to advance on the eastern salient regardless of artillery support, support having failed to arrive. Order countermanded at 0900 by same authority. Countermand received too late; companies already committed. Casualties as attached.*

And then the second document, dated eleven days later, the one that had made Edith go very still the first time Clara had read it aloud, back in the spring, back when all this had still seemed like grief rather than war:

*Am instructed that the action of the 14th is to be recorded as resulting from misapprehension of orders at battalion level. Am further instructed that the officer concerned cannot defend himself. I protest this instruction. Protest noted and filed. T.F.*

*Protest noted and filed.* Five words. Clara had typed them a dozen times since, in secret, in her room at night, and every time they landed like five blows. A man protests the falsification of the circumstances of his own death, and the universe replies: noted and filed.

Edith read to the end without stopping. Then she took off her spectacles, polished them on her sleeve with great thoroughness, put them back on, and said, "Again, please, from the third page. I want to check the dates against the ledger while you take it."

They worked through the morning. Rain came against the windows. Somewhere below, the motor started and pulled away—Jonah taking the decoy route, though neither of the women said so aloud; there were arrangements in the house now that everyone maintained by refusing to mention them, the way a family maintains silence about a drinking father. At noon Mrs. Petch brought tea and left it untouched. At one o'clock Clara finished the ninth page, blotted it, and sat looking at her own shorthand filling the sheet edge to edge, dense and illegible as birdsong written down.

"Miss Ashford."

Edith had not moved. Her hands were still laced on the desk, but Clara saw now that the knuckles were white.

"When this is printed," Edith said, "there will be a day—it may be a week, it may be an hour—when men come to this house with authority. Real authority, not the courteous kind. They will take papers. They may take me. They will certainly try to take you, gently at first and then not gently." She looked up. "I have arranged for money to reach you through the WSPU office if that happens. I want you to promise me you'll take it and go. Not to fight them. Go."

"No," Clara said.

Edith's eyebrows rose a fraction—an earthquake, by her standards.

"With respect, Miss Fairweather—you taught half the women in this city not to run, and I was one of them, and I decline to be excused from the lesson now because the exam has gotten difficult." Clara heard her own voice and was surprised by it, steady and rather louder than intended. "I will carry the copies. I will go to the printer. And if they stop me, they will find a stenographer carrying nothing but a shopping list, because the fair copy will be where no one thinks to look, and I will be back at my desk by teatime to take down whatever you dictate next. That is my promise. Not yours."

For a moment the two women looked at each other across the desk across the nine pages of a dead man's protest, and then Edith Fairweather did something Clara had never seen her do: she laughed. Briefly, quietly, but really.

"Very well," she said. "Where will you keep it?"

"That," said Clara, "is my department."

* * *

Her department, as it happened, began with Eagle carbon paper.

That evening, after dinner, after Edith had retired and the house had settled into its creaking night rhythms, Clara locked her door, drew the blind, and worked by lamplight with the patience of a woman defusing something. She made fresh carbons of the entire Vireaux sequence—dispatches, ledger pages, Thomas's final letter—two copies, each impression checked against the original under the lamp for completeness. One copy she sewed into the lining of her winter coat, inside the hem, flat as a second skin. The other she considered for a long time.

A drawer was a drawer, wherever it was. A mattress was a mattress. Every hiding place was a hiding place only until someone decided to look, and the ministry men had a way of making people decide to look. No. The second copy needed to travel. It needed to be somewhere that moved, unpredictably, on wheels, in the custody of someone whose whole talent was being overlooked.

Which raised the question of Jonah Montrose.

She thought about him while she threaded the needle. She thought about the way he watched her hands during dictation—not rudely; she had understood months ago that he wasn't watching *her*, he was watching the act, the words becoming marks, a man starving at a banquet window. She thought about the false detail she had fed him in October, the invented reference to a "Mrs. Callow" in the correspondence, and how it had surfaced a week later in a question he asked her, innocent as milk, proving he read everything that crossed this house and proving, equally, that he had protected her false detail instead of reporting it—which meant he lied to somebody, regularly, systematically, and she still didn't know for whom.

She thought about the morning, and the singing crowd, and how he'd stood in the doorway wanting to tell her something and swallowing it instead.

Peace was coming. Three weeks, perhaps less. And when it came, the bells would start and the papers would turn to triumph overnight and everything ugly would be swept into the cellar of the national mood, and either the Fairweather letters went to print in the narrow window before that happened or they went into the cellar too, and stayed there, and Thomas Fairweather remained forever a man who had misunderstood his own orders.

Hope and terror, Clara thought, sewing the final stitch flat. They shared a typewriter, those two. They shared everything.

At midnight she went down the back stairs, past the kitchen where Mrs. Petch's cat watched her with proprietary yellow eyes, and out into the yard where the garage stood black against a sky the color of dirty wool. The door was unlatched. Inside, by the light of a shielded torch, she found Jonah asleep in the driver's seat of the Hispano-Suiza, upright, cap over his eyes, a newspaper folded open on his knee to a story headed ALLIES PRESS ON — ARMISTICE TALKS ENTER FINAL PHASE.

She stood in the doorway a moment longer than she needed to.

"Wake up," she said. "I need to talk to you about a delivery."

The cap came up. In the torchlight his face was pale and watchful and, she noticed, already braced—as if he had been waiting days for exactly these words, and dreading them, and was relieved past all sense to hear them at last.

"What delivery?" he said. "When?"

"Tomorrow," said Clara. "And Jonah—someone will try to stop it. I need to know whose side you're on before the wheels start turning, because there isn't time anymore to find out the slow way."

Somewhere across the dark city, faint as memory, the crowd at the King's Road had started singing again.
