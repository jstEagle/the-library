## 11. Betrayal in the Motor Pool

The trouble with secrets, Jonah Montrose thought, is that they make you fluent in a language nobody else can hear.

He had been living in that language for weeks now—the doubled meanings, the pauses with furniture inside them, the way a question about petrol could be a question about loyalty. He had thought himself its only speaker in the Fairweather house. He had been wrong, and he was about to learn it in the worst possible place: his own kingdom, the motor pool, where the oil smell was honest and the tools were numbered and nothing had ever lied to him before.

It began with a ledger.

* * *

Rain had come to London again on the Thursday, the fine grey kind that didn't fall so much as hang, beading on the canvas roof of the garage and dripping off the ribs like slow punctuation. Jonah stood at the workbench with the Rolls' near-side lamp dismantled in front of him, polishing the reflector with chamois because Edith Fairweather would be speaking at a memorial service for fallen nurses on Saturday and she would arrive lit up like a small cathedral or not at all. He liked this work. He liked it more than he could say. There were perhaps six things left in England you could still fix completely, and a headlamp reflector was one of them.

The ledger lived on a shelf by the door—petrol, oil, mileage, repairs. Every man who touched the cars signed against an entry. It was a habit of Jonah's own devising, back when he'd taken over the pool in '16, and he kept it out of vanity as much as order. A well-kept ledger was a mirror. You could look into it and see a household running properly.

At half past four he went to enter the day's mileage and found the ledger open.

That was the first wrong thing. He always closed it. He closed it the way some men locked their doors—without thinking, without exception. The cover lay back and the pages fanned in the damp air, and someone had turned it to November.

Someone had also written in it.

Not forged entries—nothing so crude. A single word in the margin of the October page, beside the entry where Jonah had recorded a run to the records office in Kew. The run he had made alone. The run during which he'd pocketed a requisition slip bearing Thomas Fairweather's name.

The word was *Kew?*

Small pencil letters. Neat. Unhurried. The handwriting of a man who had all afternoon.

Jonah did not touch the ledger. He stood very still and let the garage tell him the rest. There were three men besides himself with regular access to this room: young Perkins, the mechanic's boy, who was seventeen and couldn't spell *November*; old Cobb, who came twice a week to turn the grindstone and whose hands shook too badly to write that small; and Albert Fenner.

Fenner. The second driver. Quiet as a coat on a hook. Fenner, who polished brass nobody asked him to polish. Fenner, who remembered every errand Jonah ran and never wrote anything down—so far as Jonah knew.

Fenner, who had once, months ago, mentioned that the ministry men had come asking after correspondence, and had done it with the particular smoothness of a man rehearsing a line he'd already delivered somewhere else.

Jonah looked at the pencil mark a long time. Then he took out his handkerchief, wiped his hands though they were clean, and thought about what he actually knew versus what he was now afraid of, and discovered the two lists had quietly become the same length.

* * *

He did not confront anyone. Confrontation was for men with clean sides, and Jonah had understood, standing there with the rain ticking on the canvas, that his side hadn't been clean since a Wednesday evening in September when two courteous officials had sat in Edith Fairweather's drawing room drinking her tea and asking about misplaced letters, and Jonah had said *no, sir, I only drive* — which had been, depending on how you counted, either his first lie to power or his first act of war.

Instead he watched.

Watching, it turned out, was a skill he'd been training all his life without knowing it. Reading other people's mail had taught him to see the shape of a secret before its contents—the way a letter carried itself in a hand, thick with news or thin with apology. People were the same. And over the next two days, Fenner moved through the house like a man reading from a script Jonah suddenly had sight of.

On Friday morning Fenner asked Perkins, casually, whether Mr. Montrose had been out late on Tuesday night. On Friday afternoon Jonah saw Fenner standing at the end of the mews talking to a grey man in a grey hat—a conversation of ninety seconds, no handshake, the grey man walking on without looking back, which was exactly how professionals said goodbye. On Saturday, while Jonah changed a tyre, Fenner drifted past the workbench and let his eyes rest—not on Jonah, not on the car, but on the drawer where Jonah kept the requisition slip. Two seconds. Then away. Then a pleasant remark about the weather, which had gone cold and clear and smelled of smoke.

Every instinct Jonah owned told him to move the slip. His hands actually started toward the drawer, and he stopped them, because moving it now would tell Fenner that Jonah had read the margin note, and if Fenner was reporting to the ministry, then everything Jonah did next would be read aloud in a warm office in Whitehall within the day.

So he left the slip where it was, and slept—or lay—in his room above the coach house, listening to the city and doing arithmetic he hated.

If Fenner had been reporting from the beginning, then the ministry had known about Kew almost immediately. They had known Jonah pocketed the requisition slip. They had known and done nothing—which meant they were watching what Jonah would do with it, which meant—

Which meant the visit in September had not been a fishing expedition. It had been a test. *Have you seen misplaced correspondence, Mr. Montrose?* And Jonah had failed it beautifully, politely, saying *I only drive,* and they had noted the failure and filed it and waited to see whether the little chauffeur would dig himself deeper.

And then Halloway had come with his offer of money—report on Mrs. Fairweather's unstable correspondence—and Jonah had accepted with his heart hammering and his plan already forming, feed them nothing, string them along, buy time. And he had walked out of that interview feeling like a chess player.

When had Halloway made that offer? After Kew. After the slip. After the ledger.

After the test he already knew Jonah had failed.

The money had never been an offer. It had been the second examination. *Will he take our coin?* And Jonah, meaning to deceive them, had handed them the answer on a salver: yes, he will take the coin, therefore he is corruptible, therefore whatever he reports to us is worth nothing, therefore we may stop pretending he is a spectator and begin treating him as a participant.

Jonah lay in the dark and heard his own cleverness being taken apart bolt by bolt, the way he'd dismantle an engine, except this engine was him, and someone else held the spanners.

They had let him believe he was playing them. That was the elegant part. That was the cruelty of it. Men like Halloway didn't need to catch you; they only needed to let you think you were free long enough to commit yourself fully, and then present you with the record.

* * *

Sunday evening, the proof arrived, and it was worse than proof—it was paperwork.

Jonah had gone down to the garage after dark to fetch a rug for the car; Edith was attending a late sitting at a friend's house in Kensington and wanted the Daimler warmed against the fog. The garage lamp threw his shadow huge across the wall, and he was reaching for the rug chest when he heard voices outside—the low exchange of men who are not surprised by each other.

Through the gap between the doors he saw Fenner and the grey-hatted man, standing by the mews entrance this time, out of the lamplight, in the blue fog.

"You're certain it's the same file?" Fenner said. His voice was different outdoors—looser, less servant, more clerk. "Montrose kept the slip, but the file itself—"

"Gone since Tuesday," the grey man said. "Destroyed under authority. All the Vireaux material—dispatch copies, casualty returns, the chaplain's endorsement. Everything that touched it." A pause. Jonah could hear paper shifting. "Fairweather's sister has been asking questions through her solicitor. Someone senior decided the questions would go better unanswered."

"Then what's left?"

"What's left is what she publishes. Rumour. A dead man's word against the War Office's clean books." The grey man's voice was almost gentle, the gentleness of a man describing weather to a farmer whose crops have failed. "You've done well, Fenner. Better than the other one."

"The other one" — Jonah felt those words land in his chest like a dropped wrench.

"The other one took the money," Fenner said. There was something like satisfaction in it, and something like pity. "Him accepting—that wasn't recruitment, was it. I heard Halloway tell Brand it was a test. He failed it. They know whatever he says now is—"

"It doesn't matter what he says." The fog swallowed the grey man's shrug. "What matters is the file. It's ash. Let them print their rumours. Nobody hangs a general with a rumour, and by Christmas nobody will remember Vireaux at all. There'll be bells, Fenner. Bells drown everything."

Jonah stood in the dark of his own garage with his hand on the rug chest and did not breathe.

Ash. The file was ash. Thomas Fairweather's last dispatches, the casualty returns that showed the order given and then denied and then blamed on the dead—all of it, burned by authority, on a Tuesday, while Jonah had been polishing a headlamp reflector and believing himself a player.

He had thought time was the enemy. Get the truth into print before the armistice, he had told himself, before the victors bury it. But the burial wasn't coming; it had already happened. The grave was dug and filled while he'd been driving a woman through the rain, reading her mail by lamplight, congratulating himself on his invisibility.

And Clara's copies—Clara's desk full of copied correspondence, the insurance she'd spoken of with that dry light in her eyes—suddenly became the only original documents left in the world. If Fenner learned of them. If Halloway learned of them. If the household was searched—

He heard the grey man leave, footsteps fading into the fog. Fenner lingered a moment, struck a match—Jonah saw the flare through the gap, the brief orange carving of Fenner's face, calm, untroubled, a man finishing a shift—and then the match died and the mews was empty.

Jonah stayed in the dark a long while. When he finally moved, he did not go to the drawer. He went to the ledger instead, took out his pencil, and beneath Fenner's neat marginal *Kew?* he wrote, in his own steady hand:

*Yes.*

Let Fenner report that. Let them chew it. Whatever game they thought they were playing with him, he was done being the piece that moves only where it's pushed.

Then he climbed the stairs to wash the grease from his hands, and knocked—softly, twice—on the door of the room where Clara Ashford kept her desk.

She opened it with her sleeves rolled and ink on her middle finger, and took one look at his face.

"They've burned the file," Jonah said. "All of it. And Fenner's theirs—he always has been. And my taking Halloway's money was the test, and I failed it, and there's no version of me anymore that they haven't measured." He heard his own voice shake and let it. "Everything now depends on what's in your desk. And I think—I think they may already know that too."

Clara looked at him for a moment longer than comfort allowed. Then she stepped back from the doorway and said, quietly:

"Then you'd better come in, Mr. Montrose, and help me decide what we burn and what we keep."

Behind her, on the desk, the carbon copies of Thomas Fairweather's last dispatch lay in a neat stack—and on top of the stack, folded once, was a sheet of paper Jonah had never seen before, addressed in a hand he recognized from two years of other people's letters.

It was addressed to him.
