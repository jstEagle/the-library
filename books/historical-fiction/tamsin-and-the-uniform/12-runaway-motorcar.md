## 12. Runaway Motorcar

The brakes had been singing for three days.

I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the one thing I have left to offer anyone, and I hoarded even it for so long that giving it away now feels like bleeding. The brakes had been singing—a thin metallic whine whenever I pressed the pedal going down into Holborn, a sound like a small animal caught somewhere in the workings of the car. And I had told myself what I always told myself: *I'll mention it to the garage. Next week. When there's time.*

There was never time. There was only the war ending or not ending, and Edith Fairweather's letters moving through my hands like contraband, and Clara Ashford's typewriter ticking upstairs like a second heartbeat in the house, and me driving through a London gone grey and giddy at once, past shop windows papered with flags waiting to be unfurled the moment someone official said the word. Everyone in the city was holding their breath. I suppose I was too. It's a poor excuse. A man who reads other people's mail ought to know better than anyone how often the important things arrive quietly, without fanfare, sealed twice.

It happened on Swain's Lane.

Highgate is a climb and then a fall, and the house Edith wanted visited sat near the top—an old suffragist, bedridden, who received Edith's visits the way the dying receive communion. On the way up the car grumbled but held. It was on the way down, with the evening coming on violet behind the plane trees and the cemetery wall running along our left like a dark river, that I pressed the brake pedal and felt it go soft as bread.

Not dead. Soft. That's the horror of it—the interval. The pedal sank and sank and the car did not slow, and for one full second I believed I had imagined it, the way you believe you've imagined the telegram boy's face when he stops at your door. Then I pumped it. Nothing came back up the lever to meet my foot. Just that empty travel, all the way to the floorboards, and the hill pulling us faster, and Cemetery Gate swinging toward us out of the dusk with its iron teeth bared.

"Jonah?" Edith said, from beside me. She'd seen my hands on the wheel do something they'd never done before.

"Brakes," I said. It sounded very far away, my own voice. "Madam, hold onto—"

I don't remember deciding anything after that. This is what nobody tells you about instinct: it isn't thought at speed. It's the absence of thought, a handover of the body to some older tenant who has been waiting years in the cellar of you for exactly this tenancy. The old tenant took the wheel and hauled it left, away from the gate, aiming us at the one thing on that hill softer than glass and steel—a heap of road-menders' sand and gravel piled against the wall where they'd been tarring all week, God keep their memory of leaving it there. The old tenant cut the engine. The old tenant turned the car broadside so we would hit lengthwise instead of nose-first, which is the difference between a crushed cab and a crushed skull, though I didn't know I knew that until I knew it.

We hit the sandpile doing perhaps thirty miles an hour.

The world went white, then sideways, then still. Gravel rained on the roof like applause from a very large and hostile audience. A smell of petrol and hot metal and, underneath it, tar. Somewhere a dog was barking with tremendous authority, having witnessed nothing.

I was aware first of pain in my hands—both of them, palms and fingers, skinned and burning as if I'd plunged them into a fire—and then of Edith's voice saying my name, once, twice, with a steadiness that shamed every panic in me. She had cracked her cheekbone on the windscreen edge; blood ran down her jaw in a neat line, and she wiped it away with her glove as though it were rain.

"Well," she said, surveying the ruined car, the scattered gravel, the cemetery wall we had missed by the width of a prayer. "That was instructive."

* * *

I walked her the half-mile to the doctor's on the corner of the lane, refusing her arm and offering mine, and she accepted the fiction of it with the grace of a woman who had spent fifty years letting men preserve their dignity while she dismantled everything around them. By the time we got there my hands had begun to shake. Not from cold. From the delayed arrival of the knowledge that I had nearly killed us both, or been killed, or—

Or.

The word arrived on the walk, between one lamppost and the next, and stopped me mid-stride so that Edith looked back at me with one eyebrow raised.

The brakes hadn't failed. Brakes fail. Brake linings wear through over weeks, cables fray, and the failure announces itself gradually—which it had, I told myself, which it had, the singing for three days. But I had serviced that car myself eleven days ago. I had checked those linings with my own eyes and my own lamp, because checking things was the one form of thoroughness my invisible life permitted me. Eleven days ago they had been good for another thousand miles.

And in those eleven days, the car had stood in the Fairweather garage. Where any man of the household staff might go at any hour, unremarked, unwatched, because no one watches a driver. No one watches the man who reads other people's mail.

Halloway's men had not needed to make me disappear. They had only needed to let Highgate Hill do it for them, and call it grief, and send flowers.

"Mr. Montrose," Edith said, not unkindly, "whatever calculation you are performing, I advise you to finish it indoors. You look as though you've seen the sum total of your life."

I finished the calculation walking. It came to this: someone had opened my car while I slept and done something to the mechanism—loosened a fitting, nicked a line, something a competent man could do in four minutes with a spanner and leave no signature. And the list of men with access to that garage was short enough to memorise. Shorter than I wanted it to be. Because at least two names on it belonged to people whose faces I saw at breakfast.

* * *

Clara was on the front steps when we returned, which meant she had already heard—news travels through a neighbourhood faster than ambulances—and the look on her face was not the composed stenographer's look. It was naked, furious relief, quickly folded away, but I saw it. I have made a life's study of seeing things people fold quickly away.

"You're bleeding," she said.

"It's the madam's car that suffered."

"Both of you, inside." She took Edith's coat, then mine, and when her fingers brushed my wrist she flinched as though the heat of the skin had surprised her. "Your hands are a ruin."

Upstairs, in the small room off the kitchen that served as her office, she sat me down under the lamp and fetched iodine and lint and a basin of water, and began, without asking permission, to take my hands apart.

I will tell you what I learned in that hour, since it is the nearest thing to a confession I can manage cleanly: that being tended to is unbearable. That I had spent nine years making myself the man no one tended to—the man who drives, who carries, who stands by the motorcar in the rain with his collar up—and that having Clara Ashford hold my wrist firmly and say, "This will sting and I won't apologise," undid something in me more thoroughly than the sandpile had undone the car.

She cleaned grit out of my right palm with a wet cloth, working methodically, her mouth set in a line of concentration, and I watched her hands the way I had watched them during dictation months ago—those quick, certain hands that had taken down a government's lies at ninety words a minute and filed them in a desk drawer against the day they'd be needed. Insurance, she called it. We were both collectors now, she and I. We kept other people's words in drawers.

"The singing started Tuesday," I said. "Going down into Holborn. I meant to have it looked at."

"And instead?"

"Instead I drove on it for three days like a man ignoring a cough."

"That sounds familiar," she said, and did not look up, and I understood she was not speaking of the brakes.

The iodine went on. I hissed through my teeth. She wrapped the lint with the brisk competence of someone who had bandaged strikers' heads outside Holloway prison and did not romanticise wounds. But when she reached the left hand—deeper-cut, gravel still lodged along the heel of the thumb—her pace changed. Slower. Her thumbnail careful. And she said, quietly, to the bandage rather than to me:

"I checked the car yesterday."

The lamp hissed. Downstairs, a clock struck the quarter-hour.

"You what?"

"Yesterday morning. While you were at the records office with Madam's letters. I went down to the garage." She tied off the wrapping, tucked the end flat, precise as a typesetter. "Because I've been frightened for a fortnight, Mr. Montrose, and frightened women check things. The linings were sound. The cable fittings were tight. I don't know engines, but I know when something has been *interfered with*, and nothing about that car was interfered with yesterday morning."

"So it was done after—" My voice came out wrong. "After yesterday morning."

"After you drove Halloway's clerk to Charing Cross. After you told me you'd accepted their money." She looked up then, finally, and her eyes were bright and hard and terrified in equal measure, and I understood that she had done the same arithmetic on the walk home that I had done between lampposts, and had reached the same sum, and had been carrying it alone for eighteen hours because telling me would mean admitting she'd been down in that garage on my behalf, which would mean admitting—

Which would mean admitting.

"Why?" I asked. It was the only word left.

"Because you drive that car," she said, "and I ride in it. Because if they're willing to loosen a brake fitting over a driver's loyalty, then they're willing to do worse over copies in a desk drawer, and I wanted to know what state our escape route was in." She straightened the roll of lint on the table, squaring its edges, buying herself a moment. "And because I couldn't sleep. Choose whichever answer lets you keep your composure."

"Miss Ashford—Clara—I accepted their money to protect—"

"Don't." She said it fast, low, a hand raised. "Don't tell me why. If I know why, then I'm part of it in a way I can't undo, and I'm already part of it in every way that matters, and I would like, just for tonight, to be a woman bandaging a man's hands and not a conspirator reviewing operations." She took a breath. Her voice dropped further. "But I'll say one thing, and then we'll never say it again unless we must."

I waited.

"They tested you," she said. "Halloway's acceptance—that wasn't recruitment, it was examination, and you passed it by taking the money, and now they think they own you, and the men who own you still sent you down a hill with no brakes. Do you understand what that means? It means it doesn't matter what you feed them. It means the decision about you was made before you ever decided anything." Her voice cracked, just once, on the last word, and she pressed her lips together until it steadied. "You were never going to be safe by being useful. Neither was I. There's no cleverness left that buys safety. There's only finishing it—publishing it—before they finish us."

Outside, Highgate was settling into its evening noises: a milk cart, children being called in, a wireless somewhere playing dance music with the volume of a city determined to be happy the moment it was given permission. Ordinary sounds. Civilisation, resuming. And in the little lamplit room, my hands wrapped in white, Clara Ashford looking at me as though I were a document she was afraid to open.

"There's a copy of the Vireaux dispatch," I said slowly, "in your desk."

"Yes."

"And Halloway believes the file was destroyed."

"He believes a great many restful things."

"If they'd kill a driver for hesitating—" I stopped. Said it plainly, because plainly was all I had. "They'll burn this house to get those pages, Clara. And they'll do it politely, with paperwork."

"I know." She untucked the end of my bandage, checked the knot, tucked it again—a fidget disguised as nursing. "So we move them. Tomorrow. Out of the house, out of my desk, into places no one thinks to search. And Jonah—" she had never used my name before; it went through me like warm water through cold, "—you can't drive the car again until it's seen to properly. And you can't be alone in the garage. And you can't—"

She stopped herself. Whatever rule she was about to issue concerning my continued existence, she withdrew it, and what replaced it was smaller and worse and true.

"You could have died on that hill," she said, "and I would have found out the way one finds out everything in this house. Second-hand. In someone else's account of it. Like reading about a battle." Her chin came up, defiant against her own sentence. "I am tired of learning things about people I—" 

Again she stopped. But this time the stopping was different; it was the stop of a woman deciding not to retreat, and she looked straight at me across the basin of reddened water and finished it:

"—about people I love. There. It's said. In a house full of intercepted correspondence, I've said it aloud, unencrypted, to a man with no brakes."

The clock downstairs struck again. Neither of us moved.

I should have answered with something worthy of the moment. What I did was reach across with my bandaged hand—the left, the deeper-cut one—and turn my palm up on the table between us, an offering so clumsy it barely qualified as language. She looked at it for a long moment. Then she covered it with both of hers, carefully, as though it were a page she'd promised not to crease.

"We'll need to be quick," she whispered. "And quiet. And we'll need help I don't trust yet."

"Who?"

She glanced at the door, then at me, and lowered her voice to almost nothing.

"The gardener," she said. "Pike. He's been reporting to someone—I've seen him posting letters in a hand that isn't his. But this afternoon, when the car came back wrecked, he looked at you the way a man looks at a dog he's fond of that he's been ordered to drown." Her grip tightened on my bandaged hand. "I think we may have misread which side he's on. Or I think he's about to choose. Either way, Jonah—he asked me this evening, casual as you please, whether Madam keeps spare keys to the records office."

The lamp flickered. Somewhere below, the front door closed softly—someone come home, someone going out—and we sat with our hands joined over the basin of bloody water, listening, while the house counted its footsteps.
