## 13. The Archive at Night

Jonah had driven the car to the corner of Queen Square and left it there with the lamps doused and the bonnet still ticking, and now he stood in the black mouth of an alley across from the records office feeling like a man about to commit his first real crime after a lifetime of practicing smaller ones.

Reading other people's mail was theft, he'd told himself once. But it had never felt like theft. It had felt like listening through a wall—shameful, perhaps, but passive, the sin of a spectator. This was different. This was a crowbar in his coat pocket, wrapped in sacking. This was Clara beside him in the dark, breathing fast, her collar turned up against the October drizzle. This was intent.

"You're certain about the window," she whispered.

"The coal chute feeds the boiler room. The boiler room has a window onto the yard, and the yard gate latch is rusted open—I checked this afternoon while the clerk was at his supper." He'd spent two hours pretending to adjust a fan belt within sight of the building, memorizing it the way he memorized everything: the lamplighter's rounds, the constable's beat, the hour when the last light went out on the second floor. "Half past eleven, the watchman does his first round of the perimeter. He'll be gone twenty minutes if he keeps to his habits."

"And if he doesn't keep to his habits?"

"Then we'll be very interested in coal."

Clara made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. In the dark he could only see her by the faint grey wash of the sky—a sharp profile, a strand of hair escaping from under her hat. She'd dressed as a woman going to an evening lecture: dark coat, sensible shoes, a canvas satchel that clinked faintly when she moved because it held a torch and a tin of sandwiches and, absurdly, a pencil.

"Why the pencil?" he asked.

"Because," she said, "if we find what we're looking for, we won't have time to copy it properly. I take shorthand faster than anyone in London writes longhand. Edith says my hands are wasted on her correspondence." A pause. "I intend to prove her right."

Somewhere across the city a clock began striking eleven. They stood still until it finished, as though the sound were a signal neither had agreed upon. Then Jonah took the crowbar from his coat.

"I should go first," he said. "The chute is a drop."

"I'm not fragile, Mr. Montrose."

"No one in this business is fragile," he said. "That's rather the problem."

He went over the yard wall first, dropped into wet gravel, and listened. Nothing but rain on slate and, far off, the endless mutter of artillery that even now, in the war's last weeks, never quite stopped—the city's pulse, everyone had stopped hearing it. He waved Clara over. She came down the wall less gracefully than he had, landed hard, swore under her breath with a fluency that startled him into smiling, and then they were both crouched at the rusted gate like children playing at burglars.

Except no children had ever played at this. Somewhere inside that building was the file on Vireaux—or its ashes, or its ghosts—and somewhere inside Jonah was the certainty, new and terrifying, that he was done standing outside history with his ear to the wall.

* * *

Clara had rehearsed this in her head all evening, lying on her bed with her eyes open while the house ticked and settled around her. She had rehearsed the climb, the dark, the fear. What she had not rehearsed was how steady Jonah's hands were, or how much that steadiness would matter to her.

The coal chute was exactly as vile as promised. She went down it feet-first in a controlled slide that ended in a heap of slack coal dust, and when Jonah lit the torch behind his cupped hand she saw herself reflected in his expression—grey-faced, blackened to the elbows, hat askew—and something in her chest loosened. If you must do an insane thing, she thought, do it with a man who looks at you covered in coal dust and doesn't flinch.

"Boiler room," he murmured. "Stairs at the end."

The building breathed around them. Pipes knocked somewhere above; paper shifted in rooms full of filing cabinets, thousands of them, she thought, acres of cabinets, the empire reduced to its paperwork. That was the thing she understood now that she hadn't understood a month ago, before the word *Vireaux* had entered their lives and refused to leave: wars were fought twice. Once with guns, and once afterward, with files. The second war decided which version the schoolchildren learned. Thomas Fairweather had died in the first war. Tonight they meant to fight for him in the second.

At the top of the stairs Jonah killed the torch and they waited, ears pressed to time itself. Then, distantly, the front door's lock turning—the watchman beginning his round—and the sound of boots moving away along gravel toward the far side of the building.

"Now," Jonah breathed.

They came up into a corridor lined with doors, each stencilled in white paint: REGISTRY — DISCHARGES — COURT MARTIAL — FIELD RECORDS. Field Records. Clara's heart was going so hard she could feel it in her teeth. Jonah tried the door handle; it turned without protest, and she understood he'd oiled it on some pretext days ago, during the errand for Thomas's effects, during the visit when a clerk had looked at him with pitying warning in his eyes. He had been planning since then. The man who read other people's mail had apparently also been learning locks.

Inside, the torch found rows of green steel cabinets, and the smell of dust and paste and old ink. Jonah went straight to the drawer marked F and ran his finger down the index cards. Fairweather, T. E., Capt. Clara watched him stop.

"Gone?" she whispered.

He pulled the drawer fully out and tilted it so the torch could rake the gap where the folder should have been. Not gone recently. The card remained; the space behind it wore the pale rectangle of absence, undusted, outlined in grime. Destroyed, then, and not yesterday.

"They burned it," Clara said. Her voice came out flat. "Halloway said—" No. Halloway hadn't said anything to her; Halloway spoke only to Jonah, courteous offers in motor cars. But the shape of it was obvious. The file was dead; therefore they had come for its ghost.

"Carbons," Jonah said quietly. "Every dispatch goes out in duplicate. The field copies come here, but the carbon stays wherever the original was typed." He was thinking aloud, she realized, thinking the way Edith thought aloud, and she found she liked watching it. "And there's a chaplain's ledger. Thomas wrote to Edith about the chaplain—Reverend someone—who kept a private register of burials at Vireaux. Names, dates, circumstances. The ministry can destroy a file. They can't burn every parish notebook in France unless they know it exists."

"So we need to know what the file contained before it was destroyed." Clara set down the satchel. "Which means requisition slips, transit registers, the registry of movements. Files don't vanish into air—they're signed out. Someone signed for it."

She saw it land in him, the same cold arithmetic landing in both of them at once: whoever signed for the destruction had put their name in a book, and books were patient.

* * *

Jonah had never stolen anything before tonight except knowledge, and knowledge, it turned out, had been practice. His hands knew how to be quiet. He worked down the row of cabinets in Registry while Clara attacked the transit ledgers on the shelf, her shorthand pencil flying across a memorandum pad whenever she found an entry worth keeping, and the strangest part—the part he would remember years later, telling it to strangers or to no one—was that he was happy. Actually happy. Terror and exhilaration had braided themselves together somewhere between the coal chute and the corridor, and what remained was a clarity he had never once felt driving Edith through the rain, reading letters by lamplight. For ten years he had been a witness. Witnesses watched. Tonight he was touching the machinery.

"Montrose." Clara's whisper had a blade in it. "Transit register, March. 'File 4471/V — withdrawn by direction, W. Halloway, Ministry representative. To be destroyed under supervision.' Under supervision. He signed it himself. Vanity or stupidity, he signed it."

"Or confidence," Jonah said. "Men who sign things believe no one will ever read the book." He felt something hot move through his chest—not anger exactly, older than anger. "Get the page. Date, reference, signature, all of it."

Her pencil scratched. Outside, faintly, boots on gravel—the watchman returning. They froze among the cabinets, Clara's hand flat on the open ledger, Jonah's palm hovering over the drawer he'd pulled, and the boots passed, paused at the far door, jangled keys, moved on. Twenty minutes, he'd promised her. The promise was bleeding away.

"There's more," Clara breathed. "Here—'Enclosures removed prior to destruction, retained: chaplain's ledger extract, Vireaux parish, ref. CL-9.'" She looked up at him, and even in the shuttered torchlight he saw her eyes go bright and fierce. "They kept a piece of it, Jonah. They kept the one piece they couldn't afford to have disappear entirely, because some clerk needed proof the burial record existed. It'll be filed under CL. It will be *here*."

He crossed to the cabinet marked EXTRACTS — MISC. and pulled the drawer. And there it was: a thin folder, third from the front, labelled in a clerk's indifferent hand. He drew it out with both hands, the way you lift a wounded bird.

Inside: four pages. A carbon of the chaplain's register—names, ranks, dates of death at Vireaux, and against eleven of them, in the chaplain's own copied hand, a single recurring notation: *died subsequent to surrender.* Eleven men who had already given up their arms. Eleven men whose deaths the official record had attributed to enemy fire, to confusion, to the fog of war—to the dead themselves.

Below the carbons lay a covering minute, typed, unsigned: *Retained for reference pending final disposition.*

"Final disposition," Clara read over his shoulder, and her voice shook with something that wasn't fear. "Pending. It's been sitting here for months, waiting for a signature nobody bothered to give. We are looking at the last copy of the truth, Jonah, and it survived because of clerical laziness."

"Copy it," he said. "All of it. Every name."

She was already working, torch gripped in her teeth, pencil racing. He stood guard at the door with the crowbar loose in his hand and listened to her work and understood, finally, what Edith had been trying to teach him for years without ever saying it: that history was not a parade you watched from the curb. It was a room like this one. Dark, dusty, full of paper. And somebody had to carry the paper out.

* * *

Clara's fingers were cramping and she did not care. Shorthand was the one magic she owned—years of Edith's dictation, of committee minutes, of angry MPs' letters taken down at speed—and tonight the magic was for Thomas Fairweather, whom she had never met, whose sister paid her wages, whose last letter home she had read aloud in a drawing room while Edith went still as poured wax. *An order was given. Then it was denied. Then it was blamed on the dead.* She wrote the names. Corporal Ames. Private Dunmore. Lance-Corporal Okafor. Eleven names, and each one went onto her pad like a nail driven into a coffin lid—except that these nails pried the lid off.

She finished the fourth page as Jonah touched her shoulder. Time. The watchman's round would bring him back past the yard within minutes.

They descended through the building the way they'd come, faster, less careful, the folder buttoned inside Jonah's coat against his chest and the shorthand pad inside hers. At the coal chute Clara hesitated, looking up at the narrow square of drizzle-grey sky.

"Ladies first," Jonah murmured.

"It's twelve feet of vertical coal shoot."

"I'll push."

"You'll do no such—" But his hands were already at her waist, impersonal and absolutely reliable, and he boosted her up the chute rung by rung until her fingers found the yard grate, and then she was out, gasping in clean cold air, hauling herself over the wall's wet brick while below her Jonah rose out of the ground like something exhumed, coal-black and grinning in the dark.

They did not speak until Queen's Square, until the car, until London had swallowed them back into its ordinary midnight traffic of trams and taxis and distant sirens. Clara sat in the passenger seat with the pad on her knees, and Jonah drove with the folder's weight against his heart, and when they stopped for a red lamp near Holborn—an absurd courtesy, stopping for traffic at one in the morning after burglary—he heard her laugh. Small, disbelieving, joyful.

"We have it," she said. "Part of it. Enough of it."

"We have enough to make them very frightened," Jonah corrected. "Which is different, and worse, and better." The lamp changed. He let in the clutch. "Edith will want to publish immediately. Halloway will want us buried alongside the file. Between those two appetites, Miss Ashford, we now live."

"Then we'd best live quickly."

The car slid south through the sleeping city, carrying its stolen ghosts home, and Clara bent her head to transcribe the night's shorthand into fair copy by the dashboard's glow, eleven names marching down the page, while ahead of them, unseen in the dark, the machinery of the ministry was already beginning to turn—because somewhere in that records office, sooner or later, a clerk would notice a drawer left a finger's width open, and would reach for a telephone.

* * *

Jonah saw the light first.

Edith's study window, burning yellow against the wet brick at three in the morning. He braked harder than necessary and sat staring at it, the engine idling, dread arriving late to the party like a guest who'd missed nothing.

"She knows," Clara said quietly.

"She can't know."

"Jonah. The housekeeper sleeps with her door open, the kitchen clock chimes wrong, and Mrs. Pym counts the silver. Of course she knows. She's known since the moment you asked her for the spare key to the garage 'for inventory purposes.'" Clara gathered her papers. "The question isn't whether she knows. It's why she's awake."

They came up through the service door into a house that smelled of banked fires and cold tea, and found Edith Fairweather standing at her desk in dressing gown and spectacles, not reading—standing, upright, both hands flat on the blotter, in the attitude of a woman who has been waiting and has decided to stop pretending otherwise. On the desk lay Thomas's photograph, face up. Beside it lay a telegram, opened.

"You've been out," Edith said. Not a question. Her eyes went from Jonah to Clara to the bulge of paper inside Jonah's coat with the unhurried precision of a woman who had organized supply lines and prison inspections and once, famously, talked her way past a military governor. "Sit down, both of you. Whatever you have, I want it on this desk, and I want it now."

Jonah reached into his coat—and stopped, because Edith lifted the telegram and read aloud, her voice level as a surveyor's line:

"*Regret to inform you Capt. T. E. Fairweather's service record subject to administrative review. Advise discretion. Halloway.*"

She set it down. She removed her spectacles.

"He sent me a warning dressed as a courtesy at half past two in the morning," Edith Fairweather said, "which means he knows what you have, which means he wants me frightened. So." Her chin came up, and Jonah saw in her face, suddenly and completely, the brother she had lost—same jaw, same unyielding set. "Show me what my brother died trying to tell me."

Jonah laid the folder on the desk and opened it to the first page, and the lamplight fell on eleven names, and the clock in the hall began, wrongly, to chime four.
