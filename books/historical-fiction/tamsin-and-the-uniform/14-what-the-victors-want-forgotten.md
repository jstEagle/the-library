## 14. What the Victors Want Forgotten

He came for me on a Thursday, when the fog had settled over London like a lid on a pot, and he came politely, the way all dangerous men do.

I was wiping down the bonnet of the Daimler in the mews behind Cadogan Gardens when I heard footsteps on the wet cobbles—measured, unhurried, the walk of a man who has never once needed to run. I knew before I turned around. You learn to hear it, after weeks of looking over your shoulder: the difference between someone passing through and someone arriving.

"Mr. Montrose." He was a broad man in a good grey coat, fifty or so, with the kind of face you forget while you're looking at it. Weathered, reasonable, faintly amused. He held his hat in one hand as though the fog were a light rain. "Halloway. We spoke on the telephone. I wonder if I might trouble you for ten minutes."

"We spoke on the telephone," I said, "for about ninety seconds."

"Yes. I find longer conversations go better in person." He glanced at the motorcar with something like genuine appreciation. "A 1913. She's held up well."

"She's been maintained."

"So have you, by all accounts." He said it pleasantly, and let it sit there between us like a coin placed on a bar. Then: "There's a teahouse off the King's Road. Quiet at this hour. Unless you'd rather talk here, in which case I'll try not to take offence at the smell of petrol."

The teahouse. Not the ministry offices, not some black motorcar with the curtains drawn. A teahouse. That was the first lesson he taught me without seeming to teach anything at all: men like Halloway didn't need to threaten you. They only needed to be seen with you somewhere warm.

"I'll drive," I said.

"No need. It's four minutes' walk, and I promise no one is following either of us." He smiled. "If anyone were following us, Mr. Montrose, I should be very disappointed in my own department."

* * *

The teahouse was called Merrilees's and it smelled of toast and tannin, and there was a girl behind the counter who called Halloway by name. *Mr. Halloway, the usual?* The usual. A man who had a usual at a Chelsea teahouse was a man who had made himself part of the furniture of ordinary life, and I understood that this too was technique. He ordered tea for two without asking me and we sat by the window where anyone passing could see us clearly, and that was also technique, and knowing it did nothing to make me feel safer.

"You've been with Mrs. Fairweather how long?" he began, unwrapping his napkin.

"Two years."

"And before that?"

"Cabs. Before that, the army—until they decided my knee was worth more to them flat than standing."

"Gallipoli?"

"Salonika. Less glamorous, more mosquitoes." I looked at him. "You'll have all of this already. In a file. Why ask?"

"Because files are dead things," he said, "and I prefer to meet people alive." He stirred his tea, two turns, set the spoon down precisely across the saucer's lip. "Jonah. May I call you Jonah? Thank you. I'm going to speak plainly with you, because I think you're a plain speaker yourself, despite a certain—let's call it a talent for silence. You've been reading her correspondence."

My hands went still around the cup. Not cold. Not hot. Just still, the way a rabbit is still.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Of course you do. And I want you to notice that I'm not angry about it. I'm not here to reprimand you. Frankly—" and here he leaned forward a fraction, confiding, almost kind— "it's the reason we're having tea instead of something less pleasant. A man who reads other people's mail is a man who understands information. Who values it. Who respects its weight. Most drivers, Jonah, would have read those letters for gossip. You read them for meaning. I've seen your file annotations—the ones from the intercept summaries, before your transfer. Very tidy mind."

I hadn't known there were annotations. I hadn't known there was a file. But of course there was; there had always been a file; I had simply never been the sort of man who imagined the state kept one on him. That was the vanity of the invisible. We believe our invisibility is our own accomplishment.

"What is it you want?" I said.

"To offer you a choice. And I mean that sincerely—a genuine choice, with both options laid out honestly, because I think honesty is owed to a man who's about to make a decision that will shape the rest of his life." He drank his tea. Outside, a woman hurried past the window with her collar up, a newspaper folded under her arm, and I thought: everyone in this city is carrying paper, and most of it lies.

"The Fairweather woman," Halloway said, "intends to publish her brother's letters. You know this. You've driven her to the printer's district twice this week—Tuesday to Fetter Lane, Wednesday to a compositor's shop near Clerkenwell that I'd wager she believes is secret. The letters concern an engagement at Vireaux in the spring of '17. They contain a written order, subsequently denied, subsequently attributed to the dead. If they are published, three things happen, and I want you to hear them in order, because the order matters."

He raised one finger.

"First: Captain Thomas Fairweather is posthumously disgraced. Not cleared—disgraced. Because the record we hold tells a different story than the letters do, and the public will believe the record, because the public always believes the record. His name goes into history attached to an atrocity, and his sister spends the rest of her life known as the woman who tried to smear a dead officer to advance her politics. Every suffragist cause she's touched becomes contaminated by association. The movement she's given thirty years to—set back a decade, perhaps more. The vote, Jonah. Women lose years of the vote because one grieving woman couldn't leave the past buried."

Second finger.

"Second: the men who gave that order at Vireaux—the ones who wrote the denial—are decorated officers now. One of them holds a position in this government that matters enormously in the peace negotiations. Publishing the letters doesn't touch him. It touches everyone around him. It touches the delegation. It touches the armistice itself. Do you understand what's being signed in eleven days? The shape of the next twenty years is being drafted right now, in rooms you will never see, and you propose to throw ink into the machinery because of one dispatch from one village nobody had heard of before the war."

Third finger.

"Third—and this is the one I'd ask you to consider most carefully—we know about the girl."

* * *

I had prepared myself, in the weeks since the men from the ministry first came to the house, for many things. I had rehearsed denials in the dark of the garage. I had composed, mentally, entire speeches of wounded innocence. What I had not prepared for was the smallness of the sentence, the way five words could reach into my chest and close around the warmest thing in it.

"Miss Ashford has done nothing wrong," I heard myself say, and my voice sounded far away, a stranger's voice on a bad telephone line.

"No," Halloway agreed mildly. "She's copied private correspondence, which makes her technically liable under the Defence of the Realm Regulations, but nobody cares about that, and neither do I. What I care about is that she's implicated. When this breaks—if it breaks—she isn't a bystander. She's a conspirator. Typewriters produce evidence, Jonah; that's what they're for. Her fingerprints are on every page. And she would go into the same room where we put people whose names never appear in newspapers, and unlike you, she hasn't spent a lifetime learning how to disappear. She's a stenographer. She's visible. Visible girls in rooms like that—" he shook his head slowly, regretfully, a performance so perfect I almost admired it— "they come out changed. Sometimes they come out old. Sometimes they come out not at all, and their families receive a story about a position abroad."

"You're threatening her."

"I'm describing consequences. There's a difference, and the difference is that I would genuinely prefer none of it to happen." He set down his cup and turned his full attention on me, and for the first time the pleasant mask shifted, just slightly, and I saw the thing underneath—not cruelty exactly, but scale. The indifference of a very large machine to anything small caught in it. "Here is the choice."

He drew an envelope from his inside pocket and laid it on the table between us, squared to the table's edge, precise as everything else about him.

"In this envelope is a railway warrant, first class, valid to any port in Europe, and papers that will get you across any border without question. There is also a letter of introduction to a transport firm in Rotterdam that pays its drivers very well and asks no questions about their pasts. And there is cash—fifty pounds, which is more than a year of your wages, and you may regard it as whatever you like: severance, gratitude, or the price of a clean conscience, though I'd caution against the last interpretation, because consciences aren't for sale, they're only for redirecting."

"You're paying me to leave."

"I'm offering you a future. There's a difference, and the difference is that I mean it." He tapped the envelope once, lightly. "Take it. Leave tomorrow. Mrs. Fairweather publishes her letters or doesn't—that decision was never yours, and you'll notice I'm not asking you to stop her, because I know you can't, and asking would insult us both. All I'm asking is that you're not standing beside her when the bill arrives. Because here is the truth of it, Jonah, and I give you this truth freely: the victors write the history. That's not cynicism, it's bookkeeping. Eleven days from now the guns stop and the world exhales and nobody—nobody—will thank the man who tried to sour the peace with an old horror from a French village. The public wants to forget. Governments exist to help them. And the people who stand in front of that forgetting—" he spread his hands, almost apologetic— "they become footnotes. Unflattering ones."

Footnotes. The word landed harder than he could have known. I had called myself a footnote for years, worn the word like a coat against the cold, and here was this man handing it back to me as a threat, as if he'd reached into my own private vocabulary and turned my armor inside out.

"And if I stay?" I asked. "If I refuse?"

"Then nothing happens today. Nothing happens tomorrow. Nothing happens for a while, in fact, and that's deliberate—you'll spend every day waiting for it, which is its own punishment, and I won't apologise for it because it works. Eventually Miss Ashford is invited to answer some questions. Eventually Mrs. Fairweather's charitable registrations develop irregularities. Eventually her driver's references turn out to be insufficient and no garage in London will hire him. None of it is dramatic. None of it is illegal. It is simply what happens to people who stand where you're standing." He finished his tea. "And I should mention—because you'll wonder—the file on Captain Fairweather. The original. The requisitions, the chaplain's ledger references, the carbon of the denied order. It no longer exists. It was destroyed last week, properly, with witnesses. So even if you stay, even if you publish, you'll be publishing accusations against the state's official memory with nothing but the sister's word and a dead man's letters. Which is to say: nothing."

Except, I thought, that we had been to the archive at night. Except that Clara's desk held copies he didn't know about, and my coat lining held pages he believed burned, and except that a printer on Fetter Lane was at that very moment setting type by hand because machines left records and hands left only ink. But I sat there with my face arranged into disappointment, because the first rule of surviving a man like Halloway is to let him believe he has shown you his whole hand.

"I'd like to think about it," I said.

"Of course." He rose, put on his hat, and dropped a coin beside the untouched cup he'd ordered for me. "But Jonah—think quickly. The armistice is eleven days away, and after the guns stop, mercy gets very expensive." He paused at the edge of the table, and for a moment the reasonable face did something almost human. "For what it's worth—and it isn't worth much—I read your annotations too. Before your time. You wrote, regarding a soldier's letter home that had been flagged for suppression: *'This man's mother deserves the truth more than the censors deserve their wages.'* I've remembered that for three years. It was well put. It's also why I'm sitting here instead of sending someone else." He settled the hat brim. "The offer stands until Sunday."

Then he was gone, into the fog, unhurried, and I sat alone at the table with the envelope squared neatly in front of me and my tea going cold, and outside the window London went about its business—buses, bicycles, a boy hawking evening papers shouting something about Wilson and fourteen points—and every single one of them, I thought, was betting their life on the idea that the war ending would mean the lying stopping.

I picked up the envelope. It was heavier than paper had any right to be. Inside, I knew without opening it: the ticket, the papers, the money, the whole reasonable architecture of escape, built by a man who understood that the strongest walls are made of kindness.

I could take it. God help me, in that moment, part of me wanted to take it—wanted Clara safe in a boarding house in Bloomsbury and Edith furious but intact and myself on a boat watching Dover recede, alive, unmarked, invisible forever. That was the temptation, and I want to be honest about it now, since honesty is the only thing I have left to trade: it was the most reasonable offer anyone had ever made me. Every argument Halloway had made was half true, and the halves were chosen with surgical care. Thomas disgraced. The movement damaged. The peace imperiled. Clara in a room without windows.

All of it plausible. All of it priced.

And underneath it, the one thing he had not counted on—or perhaps he had, and misjudged its weight, the way powerful men always misjudge the small things: I had read the letters. Not the file copies, not the summaries, not the redacted versions. Thomas Fairweather's actual hand, the pressure of his pen changing when he described Vireaux, the way a man's writing trembles when he is writing around a horror he cannot name. I had carried those pages in my breast pocket against my heart for three days. You cannot unread a dead man's trembling.

I stood, left the envelope on the table—it felt wrong to carry it, wrong to let it touch my coat where Clara's bandages had been—and walked out into the fog toward Cadogan Gardens, taking the long way, checking reflections in shop glass, doing all the paranoid arithmetic of a hunted man. By the time I reached the mews the gas lamps were coming on, yellow coins in the grey air, and the house above the garages had its downstairs lights lit, and in the lit window of the morning room I could see Clara at the typewriter, bent over the keys, her hands moving in the lamplight like something alive.

She looked up as I came in—she always knew my step on the stairs, God knows how—and her eyes went from my face to my empty hands and back again.

"Well?" she said. "What did he offer?"

"How did you know it was an offer?"

"Because you look like a man who's been told he can save himself." She took the carbons out of the machine and squared them, and her voice was steady, but her hands weren't, quite. "Did you take it?"

I stood in the doorway with the fog still on my shoulders and told her the truth, or the beginning of it: "He offered me a ticket anywhere in the world, fifty pounds, and your safety. All I had to do was leave before Sunday."

Clara set down the pages. For a long moment she looked at me the way she'd looked at the war office correspondence—decoding, weighing, finding the lie if there was one.

"And?" she said.

And I opened my mouth to answer her—and that was when we both heard the front bell ring, sharp and urgent through the whole house, and Edith's voice calling down the stairwell, thin and strange, a voice I had never heard her use:

"Jonah! Clara! Come quickly—he's here again, and he's brought soldiers."
