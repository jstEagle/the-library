## 15. Edith's Decision

Edith Fairweather had spent forty-six years learning to read a room the way other women learned to read scripture, and so she knew, before either of them spoke, that the morning of the twelfth of November would be the morning everything was said aloud.

She sat in her study with the curtains half-drawn against a grey drizzle that had settled over London like damp linen. The fire had been laid but not lit—Clara always lit it at nine, and Clara was late, and Clara was never late. That alone told her something was wrong. The second thing was Jonah Montrose standing at the edge of the Turkish carpet as though it were the edge of a cliff, his cap crushed in both hands, looking for all the world like a man come to surrender a city.

"Sit down, Mr. Montrose," she said. "You look like a footman at a hanging."

He didn't sit. "Mrs. Fairweather—I've done something you should know about. Several things. I'd rather you heard them from me than from anyone else."

"And Miss Ashford?"

"She's coming. She asked—" His jaw worked. "She asked if I wanted her here when I told you, or after. I said I couldn't do it without her."

Edith studied him. He was not a large man, but he had the stillness of one; he drove through London traffic like a man threading a needle in a gale, and he had never once, in three years, flinched from anything she'd asked him to drive into. Suffrage marches, hostile crowds, that awful afternoon in Whitehall when the police had waded in with truncheons and she'd stood her ground on the running board—he had been there, steady as a wall.

Now his hands shook.

"Then wait for her," Edith said, "and while we wait, you may begin."

* * *

It took him eleven minutes. She counted, because counting kept her hands quiet in her lap.

He began with the lamplight in the garage and the letters he had opened and read—every letter, or nearly every, for years. He described it without ornament, which she respected: how he had first unfolded an envelope out of nothing worse than boredom, waiting for her outside some committee meeting, and how the reading had become a habit, then a compulsion, then something he could no longer honestly call innocent. He was, he said, a spectator. A footnote. He had told himself that reading history secondhand hurt no one, that he never acted on what he learned, that his invisibility was a kind of virtue.

"I know about the seals," Edith said quietly, when he paused. "The one broken before it reached me. The Vireaux letter."

Jonah went white. "You—"

"I have eyes, Mr. Montrose, and servants are people, and people leave traces. The wax was wrong. It had been re-sealed with a cheap stick, and whoever did it pressed too hard." She let that sit. "I said nothing because I wanted to see where your conscience would take you. I confess I expected it to take you further away than it has. You're telling me yourself. That is worth something."

"It isn't worth enough," he said. "There's more."

There was more. There were the two courteous men from the Ministry—the ones who had visited in September asking about misplaced correspondence—and there was Halloway, whose name Edith had not known until this moment but whose face she had seen before, at a distance, at a function last spring where he'd watched her across a room the way a man watches a fire he intends to put out. There was the money offered to Jonah in his own kitchen, and the acceptance, and the plan—half-formed, desperate—to feed them nothing. There was the motorcar and the brakes that failed on Highgate Hill, and Jonah's hands held up as evidence, still scabbed along the knuckles, and Clara's bandaging of them, and what had passed between them in that room that neither of them would name aloud but both of them, standing now in Edith's study, wore openly like new clothes.

And there was the file. Thomas's file, destroyed. Jonah said the words and Edith watched him watch her, braced for her grief, and she gave him none of it—not because she felt none, but because grief had become, since the telegram in 1917, a country she lived in rather than a place she visited. One did not weep at one's own weather.

"They burned it," Jonah finished. "Halloway told me plainly. Publish the letters and they'll ruin you as a traitor and disgrace Thomas posthumously. He offered me a ticket anywhere in Europe and a clean exit." His voice cracked on the last word, and he hated himself for it; she saw him hate himself for it. "I didn't take it. But I'm not telling you this because I'm clean, Mrs. Fairweather. I'm telling you because I'm not, and you deserve to know exactly what is standing in your house."

* * *

The door opened. Clara came in with rain on her shoulders and a folder under her arm, and Edith saw at once that the girl had been crying—recently, furiously, and then stopped herself with that iron discipline Edith had hired her for and come to love her for. Clara set the folder on the desk.

"You'll want to see these," Clara said. "Before anyone decides anything."

Inside were copies. Months of them, typed and handwritten both—Edith's correspondence, copied in Clara's careful hand, dated and filed. Insurance, Clara called it, when Edith looked up at her with one eyebrow raised.

"I know," Clara said. "I've been waiting months to say it out loud and I still don't know how. I copied your letters. The sensitive ones. The ones that could hang someone. Because I watched what happened to women who kept only one copy of things—what happened at Holloway, what happened to the files they confiscated. I decided somebody in this house would keep a record that couldn't be burned." Her chin came up. "I'm not sorry. I'll resign if you want."

"Sit down," Edith said, "and don't be theatrical. If I dismissed every woman in this movement who'd gone behind my back for defensible reasons, I'd be holding meetings in a telephone box."

But her heart was doing something complicated, because there it all was, laid out on her desk like instruments on a surgeon's tray: a driver who read her mail, a stenographer who stole it, a ministry that burned her brother's name, and a copy of the Vireaux dispatch—Thomas's own hand, transcribed—sitting on top of the pile as though it weighed nothing at all.

She picked it up. She had read it before; Clara had read Thomas's letters aloud to her weeks ago, and Edith had gone very still in her chair and then very busy, because stillness was a luxury. But reading it silently, alone, with the rain ticking at the glass, was different. *Order given. Order countermanded. Blame assigned to the dead.* Thomas had written it like a soldier writing home, trying to make sense of a thing that made no sense, and somewhere in Whitehall a man named Halloway had decided that the sensible thing was to make certain no one else ever tried.

"My brother," Edith said, mostly to herself, "spent his whole life believing that records mattered. He was the honest one in the family. I marched and shouted and got myself fed in prison, and Thomas just—did his work, and wrote the truth down, and believed the truth would hold." She set the page down. "They burned his file. They will burn him twice unless we print him first."

* * *

Jonah spoke first, and it cost him visibly. "Mrs. Fairweather—don't. Publish, and they won't stop at letters. Halloway told me what they'd do. They'll call you a German sympathiser. They'll dig through everything, every march, every arrest, every penny you've ever sent to the cause, and build a case. And Clara—" he glanced at her, and Edith saw the whole of it pass between them, unarmored, "—Clara gets detained. Possibly worse. You can publish after the war. After the inquiry. Let the armistice come first, let the country breathe, and then—"

"No," Edith said.

It was not loud. She had learned long ago that volume was for people who hadn't prepared.

"Mr. Montrose. Do you know why I chained myself to railings? Not for the vote itself—I could have lived without the vote, unpleasantly. I chained myself because I had spent thirty years watching men write the record and women die unwritten. My sister died in childbirth and the coroner's report blamed her own weakness. My mother died in a workhouse infirmary and the ledger listed her as indigent, no next of kin, though I sat beside her for six days. Everything that happened to women happened twice—once to our bodies and once again in the paperwork, where men wrote us out of our own lives." She rose, because some sentences required standing. "I marched for the record. For the right to appear in it. To sign my own name to my own existence."

She came around the desk. The fire, at last lit by Clara's trembling hand, threw her shadow long across the floor.

"And now you tell me the government is doing to my brother precisely what this whole rotten system has always done to everyone it finds inconvenient—it is editing him. Erasing him. Deciding what the future will be permitted to know. And you ask me to wait for a peace treaty to be signed first. A peace built on their paper, in their ink, with my brother's death laundered through it." Her voice did not rise but it hardened, the way steel hardens, cooling. "I did not march for a peace that lies."

The silence afterward had texture. Rain. Fire. Somewhere below, the kitchen maid singing to herself, oblivious, seventeen years old, singing about a soldier boy coming home.

"If we publish," Edith continued, turning to face them both, "we do it properly. Not a leak, not a rumor—a document. Full text. Thomas's dispatch, the chaplain's evidence, whatever remains in that archive, printed and dated and distributed widely enough that destroying it becomes a scandal in itself. Halloway can ruin me only if the story is mine alone. So we make it everyone's. We make it impossible."

"The printers," Clara said slowly. Edith watched the girl's mind move—quick, mechanical, beautiful. "Fetter Lane. Mrs. Pankhurst's old contact—Mr. Ellery. He owes the movement more than he's paid. He could set it by hand overnight."

"He could," Edith said, "and he will. And the police will watch the street while he does, and we will need decoys, and drivers, and someone willing to be invisible in plain sight moving paper across London." She looked at Jonah. "Someone, perhaps, with experience carrying things nobody knows he carries."

Jonah's throat moved. "You're asking me—"

"I am telling you," Edith said, "that the man who read my mail in secret for three years is the only man in London I trust to carry this in the open. Because you already know what it costs, and you chose to walk in here anyway. Cowardice doesn't knock on my door and hand itself over." She softened—one degree, no more; she was not a woman given to more. "You were never a bystander, Mr. Montrose. You merely hadn't signed your name yet."

Clara was already pulling paper toward her, already dating a fresh sheet in her small precise hand, and Edith thought: *there*, that is what it looks like when fear and purpose occupy the same body and purpose wins.

"One thing more," Edith said. "Halloway offered you money and a ticket. He will offer again, and next time it will not be money. Men like him escalate. Before we go any further—before Clara types a single word—is there anything else? Any thread they hold? Any promise you made?"

Jonah was quiet for a long moment. When he answered, his voice was steady and it was the steadiest thing in the room.

"There's one more thing. The man who's been informing on this household—the one who told them about my reading, who tested my loyalty. I know who he is. I found out yesterday." He met Edith's eyes. "And tomorrow morning, I'm going to do something about it. Something you won't like."

Outside, the rain thickened, and somewhere across the city, in a warm office smelling of tobacco and wet wool, a man named Halloway was already writing the next line of the record.

Edith Fairweather intended to write the one after it.
