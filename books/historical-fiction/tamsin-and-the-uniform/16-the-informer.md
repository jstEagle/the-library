## 16. The Informer

I had known for three days before I did anything about it, and those three days were the longest of my life, because knowing a thing and being certain of it are separated by a distance no motorcar can cross at any speed.

It was the boots that told me. Not metaphorical boots—the actual boots, blacked and lined up outside the scullery door each morning, belonging to a man named George Pell, who had been the Fairweather house's odd-job man since before I arrived. Pell cleaned the lamps, carried the coals, wound the hall clock, and for four years had polished my shoes without once looking me in the eye. He was a grey man, Pell. The kind of man who is so thoroughly part of a house that you stop seeing him, the way you stop seeing wallpaper. I of all people should have understood how much a nearly invisible man could see.

What told me was this: on Tuesday, Pell polished the brass on the motorcar's bonnet while I worked beneath it, and he asked me, casually, whether Miss Fairweather kept her correspondence locked up now. On Wednesday, he asked whether I'd noticed the two gentlemen from the Ministry taking tea with Mrs. Croft, the housekeeper, as though they'd been old friends. And on Thursday morning I came down early to check the tyres and found the coal shed door ajar when I had locked it myself, and inside, on the shelf where the kindling sat, a folded slip of paper that had not been there the night before.

I didn't touch it. I left it exactly where it lay and went back into the kitchen and made myself tea with hands that would not entirely steady, because I knew what a collection point looked like. I had spent six years reading other people's letters by lamplight; I had learned how secrets travel, and they do not travel by ghost. They travel by grey men carrying coals.

Pell had a family. That was the thing I kept circling back to, the way your tongue returns to a broken tooth. A wife in Camberwell with the rheumatism, two daughters—I'd heard him speak of them perhaps twice in four years, always in the same flat, apologetic tone, as though having people to love were a small embarrassment. A son at the Front until the spring, when the son had come home with half a lung and a discharge paper and no trade but what his father could find him. Pell earned nineteen shillings a week and a room over the coach house, and men like Halloway did not recruit men like Pell with patriotism. They recruited them with twenty-five shillings and a quiet word about what happened to families whose fathers proved unhelpful.

I knew all this and still, when I saw him that Thursday evening setting out the boots, something in me wanted to put my hands around his throat.

* * *

I waited until Saturday. I told myself the waiting was strategy—letting him grow comfortable, choosing my ground—but the truth is uglier: I needed the time to decide what kind of man I intended to be, and I had never had to decide that before. For six years I had been a spectator. Spectators don't have to choose anything. They stand at the rail and watch the race and go home clean.

But I had signed Halloway's ledger with a false promise, and Halloway had tested me and found me wanting—or believed he had—and Thomas Fairweather's file was ash somewhere in a Ministry incinerator, and Clara had copied the Vireaux dispatch in her careful shorthand, and Edith had said *I will publish*, and there was no rail left to stand behind. The race was running through me now. Whatever I did next, I would be in it.

Saturday night the house went quiet early. Edith had gone to a committee meeting in Hampstead with Clara; Mrs. Croft was abed; the cook had left for her sister's in Peckham. I found Pell in the coal shed, filling a scuttle by lamplight, and I stepped in after him and shut the door and turned the key I had cut for that lock three days earlier, and set the lamp on the kindling shelf between us.

He looked at the lamp, then at me, then at the door, and I watched him understand all three in sequence, and I confess there was a part of me—a part I am not proud of—that enjoyed it. Enjoyed watching the invisible man become visible. Enjoyed being, for one moment, the one holding the lamp instead of the one reading by it.

"The collection point," I said. "The shelf. How often?"

Pell set down the scuttle very slowly, as though sudden movement might cost him something. "Mr. Montrose—"

"How often, George."

"Fridays." His voice was smaller than I expected. Grey voices are like that when you finally make them speak—they hold nothing in reserve. "Friday mornings, before the household's up. There's a man comes on a bicycle. Doesn't come past the gate. I leave it in the gap in the wall by the mews, and it's gone by seven."

"And what do you leave?"

He wet his lips. "Notes. Who comes to the house. What she says at meetings, when I carry her bag to the cab. Whether there's—visitors." His eyes flicked to mine and away. "Whether the driver reads the post."

There it was. I had braced for it and it still went through me like cold water. "You told them about me."

"I told them what anyone can see." And here Pell straightened slightly, and for the first time in four years he looked at me properly, and there was something in his face that wasn't fear. "You sit in that garage with other men's letters open on your knee, Mr. Montrose. You think nobody notices because nobody says anything. But the whole house knows. Mrs. Croft's known for years. She thinks it's harmless. I couldn't afford to think anything."

"You could have warned me."

"Why would I?" It came out almost gentle, which was worse than cruelty. "You're not my friend. You've never once asked after my boy's lung. None of you down the house ever ask. You're good people, I expect, the way rich people are good—carefully, at a distance. But when the gentleman from the Ministry sat in Mrs. Croft's parlour and said *we need eyes*, there was only one pair of eyes in this house that came cheap, and they were mine." He looked down at his coal-blackened hands. "Twenty-five shillings a week. Do you know what that buys? It buys the difference between my girls walking to school in shoes with holes and shoes without. It bought my boy a winter's worth of the special medicine. You'd have done the same. Don't tell me you wouldn't."

And the terrible thing—the thing I have never told Clara, though I suspect she has guessed—is that he was right. Not about everything. But standing in that coal shed with the lamp between us, I could feel the arithmetic of his life pressing against mine, and it balanced. Twenty-five shillings. Two daughters' shoes. A half-lung. Against it: a dead soldier's file, a widow's grief, a sister's truth, and my own skin. Men like Halloway built their whole machine out of that arithmetic. They never needed torturers. They needed only to know the price of every man in England and be willing to pay it.

"I'm not here to hurt you," I said.

"No," Pell said. "You're here because you want to know if I'll report tonight's conversation. Which I will, unless you give me a reason not to." He said it without bravado, simply, the way a man states the weather. "That's how it works, sir. You've seen the machine from the top, reading your letters. I've seen it from underneath. It's the same machine."

* * *

We stood there a long moment, the two of us, and I thought about the letter I had returned to Halloway's men in October, the one I should have kept, the coward's tithe I'd paid to keep my invisibility. I thought about Clara calling me a coward and a witness in the same breath, and how both things had been true, and how I had promised her—without saying it in words, in the space between bandages—that I would stop being one and start being the other.

"Who else reports?" I asked. "In this house."

Pell hesitated. Then, and I believe it was because I hadn't struck him, because I had listened, because mercy disarms more thoroughly than violence—he told me. Mrs. Croft reported on visitors' names and car registrations, believing she was protecting the household from scandal rather than serving the Ministry; she gave her information to a different man entirely and would have fainted to know the two streams met in the same office. The milk boy noted which papers were delivered and which were burned. And there was one more, a name that made me go still: a clerk at the records office where Thomas's effects had been kept, the same office whose missing file had started all of this, who received a retainer to flag any inquiry touching the Fairweather name.

"They knew within a day," Pell said quietly, "when you pocketed that requisition slip. That's why they came to the house. Not suspicion. Confirmation."

All of it laid out on a kindling shelf between a lamplight and a locked door: the whole nervous system of the thing, drawn in coal dust. I memorized it. I had a good memory; six years of reading other people's mail had given me that.

"Now," I said, "here is what happens next."

* * *

Here is what happens next, and I set it down plainly because I want the record—if there is ever a record—to show it was chosen, not stumbled into.

I let George Pell go.

Not merely unlocked the door and walked away—I let him go entirely. I took from my inside pocket the envelope I had prepared that afternoon, containing every shilling I had in the world beyond what Clara and I would need for the days ahead, and I held it out to him.

"There's eleven pounds there. It's not twenty-five a week. But it's enough to take your family to your sister's in Norwich until Christmas, and enough to say you lost the position because the household's breaking up—which, by Monday, may even be true. You send no more notes. The bicycle man finds his gap in the wall empty, and eventually he finds it empty long enough that someone else gets written into the ledger in your place."

Pell stared at the envelope and did not take it. "And when they ask why I stopped?"

"They won't punish you for stopping. Stopping isn't betrayal—it's just a man gone stale. They'll write you off. That's what men like Halloway do with tools that dull." I pushed the envelope into his coat pocket myself, because he would not reach for it. "But hear the rest. If you write one more note—not tomorrow, not ever—one more note, and it reaches London before I'm finished, then I will put your name in print. Not theirs. Yours. With the amounts and the dates and the gap in the wall. The newspapers won't care about the Ministry's methods, George, but Camberwell will care about yours. Your girls' schoolmistress will read it. So will your boy."

His face did something complicated then—shame and relief and fury all fighting for the same few inches of flesh. "That's blackmail."

"Yes," I said. "It is. Welcome to how the war is actually fought. I learned it from your employers."

For a moment I thought he would swing at me. His fists actually closed. And then they opened, slowly, and he took the scuttle up in both hands instead, the way a man takes up the only work he has, and he said, "You're going to get yourself killed, Mr. Montrose. And probably the young lady too. And maybe the missus. And it won't change one thing about Vireaux, because the men who buried it own the burying."

"Perhaps," I said. "But I've spent six years being certain nothing I did mattered. I find I prefer the other mistake."

He unlocked nothing—I did that—and as he stepped past me into the dark of the yard he paused and said, without turning round, "My boy's name is Alfred. If it goes in print someday, spell it right." And then he was gone across the cobbles, a grey man dissolving into a grey evening, and I stood alone in the coal shed with the lamp and understood that I had just done the most dangerous thing available to me.

Not mercy. Mercy was only the shape of it. What I had done was vacate a post.

Halloway's ledger had an entry where George Pell used to be: *eyes inside the Fairweather house, Fridays.* A machine like that does not tolerate vacancies. Either the entry would be filled by some new pair of cheap eyes, closer and colder than Pell's—or it would be filled by me. And I had decided, somewhere between the envelope and the threat, which name I preferred in that line.

* * *

Monday morning I telephoned the number Halloway had pressed into my hand in November, the one I had accepted along with his money and his poisoned offer, and asked for him by name, and was put through with a promptness that told me he had been waiting.

"Montrose." His voice was as courteous as ever, a voice like a well-brushed hat. "This is unexpected. Have you something for me?"

"As it happens, yes. My arrangement with Pell is finished. He's leaving—family reasons. Norwich, I believe. Which means your Fridays are vacant."

A pause, brief and thoughtful, the pause of a chess player reassessing a board he'd thought static. "That's unfortunate. Pell was reliable."

"Pell was cheap," I said. "Reliability is a different commodity. I'm offering you a replacement. Someone inside the house, inside the garage, inside Miss Fairweather's confidence—or near enough. Someone who already knows what your other sources are worth, so he can price himself accordingly. Someone who has already shown—" I made my voice dry as dust "—a certain flexibility."

Another silence, longer this time. Somewhere on the line I could hear the vast paper shuffle of Whitehall, the sound of an empire doing its filing. When Halloway spoke again there was something new in his voice, something almost warm, and I understood with a lurch that warmth from a man like that was the most frightening register he owned.

"You understand what you're proposing, Montrose. This isn't the casual arrangement we discussed in the autumn. A place in the ledger is a place in the ledger. Names are recorded. Obligations accrue. If you prove unreliable, the record of your unreliability will be—" he considered "—archival."

"I should hope so," I said. "I'm a great believer in records. In keeping them accurate."

"Are you," said Halloway softly, and for a moment neither of us pretended anything, and the line hummed between us like a wire under load. Then the courtesy closed over again, smooth as water. "Very well. Come to the office Thursday, eleven o'clock. Bring your signature. We are great believers in paperwork ourselves."

He rang off, and I stood in the hallway with the receiver in my hand and my heart going hard, listening to the house around me—the clock Pell used to wind ticking on regardless, Clara's typewriter sounding faintly from the study like rain on a window, Edith's voice rising and falling on a telephone call to her printer. Three days until Thursday. And on Friday, if the plan held, the presses on Fetter Lane would begin to turn.

I had signed my name to danger, properly, in the Ministry's own ink. Now there was nothing left to do but make sure the truth reached the page first.
