## 17. The Printer on Fetter Lane

The print shop had no name above its door. That was the first thing Clara noticed when Jonah let her out at the kerb and pulled away without a word—no name, no plate, nothing but a brass bell worn smooth by decades of hands and a window soaped over from the inside, as though the shop were ashamed of itself or hiding something precious. Perhaps both.

She knocked twice, paused, then twice again—the rhythm Mrs. Pankhurst's women had used in the old days to find one another in crowds—and the door opened on a chain before it opened all the way.

"You'd be Miss Ashford." The woman behind the gap was sixty if she was a day, with ink stains up both forearms like old bruises and spectacles pushed up into grey hair cropped short as a soldier's. "I'm Nell Barrow. I set type for the *Suffragette* through the whole of the Cat and Mouse Act, and I've been raided four times, so you'll forgive me if I look at you like a customs officer."

"I'd think less of you if you didn't," Clara said.

Nell Barrow smiled with half her mouth, unhooked the chain, and pulled her inside into air that smelled of ink and hot metal and lamp oil—a smell Clara would remember for the rest of her life, though she couldn't have known it then.

* * *

The shop was smaller than it sounded from the street: one long room, low ceiling, a hand press that looked older than the building, cases of type along the walls in ranks like soldiers at attention, and a single gas mantle turned down to a whisper. Every surface was paper. Galleys, proofs, quires of stock wrapped in brown paper and stacked to the ceiling. Somewhere beneath the ordinary work—the wedding invitations, the funeral cards, the trade bills that kept a radical printer fed—there was room for what they had come to do.

"Show me what we're setting," Nell said.

Clara had brought the copies in three pieces, distributed across her person the way Jonah had insisted: the Vireaux dispatch folded into the lining of her coat, Thomas Fairweather's letters sewn flat inside the waistband of her skirt, Edith's covering statement rolled into the handle of her umbrella. She had felt ridiculous doing it, dressing herself like a smuggler in her own city. She did not feel ridiculous now. Now every seam felt like a small fortification.

She laid them out on the stone bench by the press, and Nell read them standing, lips moving slightly, one ink-stained finger tracking the lines. It took perhaps ten minutes. When she finished, she took off her spectacles, polished them on her apron, put them back on, and read the Vireaux dispatch again.

"They gave an order," she said. "Then they denied giving it. Then they shot the men who carried it out and wrote it down as their own cowardice." She said it without inflection, the way one reads a laundry list, and somehow that was worse than any outrage could have been. "And this brother of your Mrs. Fairweather refused?"

"He refused, he reported it, and he was killed within the month. Officially by shellfire."

"Officially." Nell set the pages down with great care, aligning their edges, and Clara understood she was looking at a woman buying time to keep her hands steady. "I've printed a lot of things, Miss Ashford. Lies about myself, mostly, when the magistrate asked. But I've never printed anything that could hang a man who's already dead."

"It won't hang him. That's rather the point."

"No." Nell looked up, and behind the smudged lenses her eyes were very bright and very hard. "It'll hang whoever buried him. That's why they'll come. Right. How many copies?"

"As many as we can set before morning."

"And how much morning do we have?"

"The armistice is expected any day. Possibly tomorrow. Possibly the day after." Clara heard her own voice go flat and official, the voice she used for dictation, and made herself soften it. "Edith wants it in the streets while the guns are still warm. After eleven o'clock, after the bells, nobody will want to hear that the peace is built on a lie. They'll hate us for saying it."

"They ought to hear it anyway."

"Yes," Clara said. "That's rather the point too."

* * *

They began with the Vireaux dispatch, because it was the shortest and the sharpest and because Nell said, with a printer's pragmatism, that if the raid came early they should lose everything except the thing that mattered most.

Setting type by hand was not, Clara discovered, like typing. Typing was thought translated directly to paper, fast and forgiving; a mistake could be struck over, corrected, denied. Setting type was thought made physical letter by letter, each character picked from its case with a steel-tipped composing stick held in the left hand, each word built backwards and upside-down, mirror-writing in lead. A compositor read the world reversed. There was something in that which Clara found almost unbearable and almost holy: the truth had to be assembled backwards, in secret, in the dark, before anyone could read it forwards in the light.

Her first line took four minutes. Her tenth took two. By midnight she could find the letters blind, her fingers learning the geography of the case the way they had once learned the keyboard—e and t and the vowels in their crowded rows nearest the hand, the rare letters exiled to the edges like poor relations. Nell worked beside her in silence broken only by the small bright clicks of lead on lead, and once, when Clara dropped a capital F and had to hunt for it among the furniture, Nell said, "Fairweather. Fitting," and neither of them laughed, quite, but something in the room loosened.

Outside, Fetter Lane went about its business. Boots on pavement. A drunk singing somewhere toward Fleet Street, badly, a song about Kitchener that had been out of date for two years. And under it all, more than once, the slow deliberate passage of men walking together—not hurrying, not loitering, just passing, the way police pass when they are being paid to pass. Each time, Nell turned the gas down until the shop was lit only by the glow off the type, and they stood still among the cases like furniture themselves, and waited for the footsteps to fade.

"They've been watching since six o'clock," Nell said the third time. "Two of them, rotating with a third. They think I don't know their faces. I set a handbill for the local station last Christmas; I know half the coppers in Holborn by their mothers' names."

"What do we do?"

"We keep setting. They can't raid without a warrant, and warrants for Fetter Lane get signed slowly on a Friday night. Pray for bureaucracy, Miss Ashford. It's the only friend we've got."

* * *

Jonah came back at half past one, and Clara knew the car before she saw it—that particular tired cough of the engine, the way it idled a half-tone lower than any other motorcar in London. He tapped the pattern on the door, and Nell admitted him through the chain, and he came in smelling of cold fog and petrol with his hands shaking so slightly that only someone who had spent months watching them carry correspondence would ever have noticed.

"Well?" Clara asked.

"Three routes. I drove past the house, past the office, and past here, in rotation, with stops to buy cigarettes I don't smoke. If they're following me, they're following me in circles." He unwound his scarf. His face in the low light was grey with fatigue and lit underneath by something Clara recognized from her own mirror these past weeks: terror wearing purpose like a borrowed coat. "There's a car parked on Charterhouse Street with two men in it who aren't taxi drivers. They've been there since midnight. I counted."

"Watching the street or watching you?"

"Does it matter?"

It didn't, and they both knew it, and the knowing sat between them for a moment like a third person in the room.

"There's coffee on the brazier," Nell said, not looking up from her stick of type. "Drink it and hold your tongue, both of you. Sentiment slows the hand."

But when Clara poured the coffee and brought it to him, Jonah caught her wrist—gently, the way he did everything, as though afraid the world would break if he gripped it—and said, very quietly, under the click of Nell's composing stick: "If they come, you go out the back. There's a yard and a gate into the alley and Nell's boy waiting with a bicycle. You take the second copy set. Not me. Not Nell. You."

"Jonah—"

"You're the only one of us they haven't got a file on yet. That's worth more than my driving."

She looked at his hand around her wrist, ink-smudged where hers had marked it, and wanted to say a dozen things and had time for none of them, so she said the truest one: "Set type with me. Your hands need occupying."

And he did. He stood beside her at the case, and she taught him the layout—upper case above, lower below, the big wooden bay of capitals looming like a church organ—and his long driver's fingers were clumsy at first and then less clumsy, and somewhere around two in the morning they fell into a rhythm, Clara reading aloud from Edith's statement in a low voice while Jonah hunted the letters, and it occurred to her, with a clarity that frightened her more than the police outside, that this was the most intimate hour of her life: a man and a woman building a sentence together out of lead, in a hidden room, while the state listened at the door.

* * *

Toward three o'clock the fog thickened, coming off the river the way it did in November, and Fetter Lane dissolved into a white blindness that swallowed even the lamplight. Nell pronounced it a gift from God and opened the street door an inch to listen. The footsteps were gone—or muffled past hearing. The car on Charterhouse Street, Jonah reported from a scouting run to the corner, had either moved or drowned.

They worked faster. The forme grew on the stone bed of the press: column after column of Thomas Fairweather's last dispatches, the order given at Vireaux, the denial, the execution of the carriers, the blame laid on the dead. Clara locked up the chase with quoins and a key Nell guided her hands through, and when the first proof came off the galley—damp, grey, the ink still shining—she held it under the gas mantle and read her own work in its proper direction for the first time.

It looked so small. That was the terrible thing. All those weeks of copying, smuggling, fearing, and here was the truth reduced to eight inches of newsprint, ordinary as a grocer's bill. Anyone might crumple it. Anyone might line a drawer with it or wrap fish in it.

"Every revolution starts looking like that," Nell said, watching her face. "A bit of paper somebody in power would very much like back."

The press itself was another education. It took two people and a lever and a violence Clara hadn't expected—you fed the dampened sheet, pulled the bed under the platen, hauled the bar down with your whole body weight, and the machine answered with a sound like a door slamming in a cathedral. Jonah took the bar. Clara took the feed. Nell inked the forme with a roller that hissed against the lead, and the three of them found a tempo: ink, sheet, pull, release, ink, sheet, pull. Forty sheets an hour, maybe fifty. The pile of printed pages grew on the bench, and Clara stacked them with the reverence other women of her acquaintance reserved for communion wafers.

Somewhere in the fourth hundred, her hands began to ache, and she realized she was crying—not sobbing, nothing so useful, just tears running steadily down her face while her arms kept working, and neither Jonah nor Nell said anything about it, because there wasn't time, and because some work deserves to be done weeping.

* * *

At a quarter past five, the fog thinned, and the footsteps came back.

Nell heard them first—she had ears tuned by four raids—and killed the gas with a snap of her fingers on the tap. In the sudden dark, Clara heard her own heart and the tick of the cooling press and, outside, boots stopping. Not passing. Stopping. Two sets, then a third joining, the low murmur of voices too quiet to parse, and then the worst sound in the world: a fist being rubbed slowly against a coat, the sound of a man keeping his hands warm while he waits for permission.

"The back," Nell breathed. "The boy. Go."

Jonah's hand found Clara's sleeve in the dark. "Second copy set," he whispered. "You promised."

The second set lay locked in its chase on the side bench, wrapped in sacking, ready. Clara lifted it—it weighed nothing and everything—and moved toward the yard door with Nell guiding her by the elbow, and she was halfway across the floor when the knocker came, three blows, official and unhurried, followed by a voice through the wood:

"Open in the name of the Commissioner of Police. We have a warrant."

Nell Barrow stood very straight in the dark beside her press, and Clara heard her exhale the way she imagined soldiers exhaled before going over, and heard her say, conversationally, almost kindly:

"Miss Ashford. The gate sticks. Lift, then push."

The knocking came again, harder. Somewhere beyond the wall, in the alley, a bicycle bell rang once—soft, urgent, the most beautiful sound Clara Ashford had ever heard—and she lifted the gate, and pushed, and went out into the freezing white dark with the truth in her arms.
