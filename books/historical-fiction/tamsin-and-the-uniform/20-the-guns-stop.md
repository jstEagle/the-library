## 20. The Guns Stop

### Clara

They released her at twenty minutes past ten.

Clara would remember afterward that the clock in the corridor of the police station had been wrong for years—everyone knew it, the constables joked about it—and so she did not trust the hour it gave her. She walked out through the swing doors into Whitehall with her hat crooked and her wrists still marked by the chair arms, and the morning was so ordinary it felt like an insult: grey light, wet cobbles, a milk cart, gulls arguing over something near the river.

A constable held the door for her. "Mind how you go, miss."

"Thank you," she said, because what else was there. Eleven hours in a room with a man who asked the same six questions in different orders, and the sum of her freedom was *mind how you go*.

She had told them nothing worth telling. That was not courage; she had examined it all night by the light under the door and concluded it was arithmetic. She knew where the second set of letters was hidden only in the sense that Jonah knew, and Jonah was somewhere she could not reach, and even if they'd broken her—and they might have; her hands had shaken so badly by four o'clock that she'd pressed them flat against her knees to still them—the letters were no longer in any one person's keeping. That was the whole design. Edith had scattered them like seed. You cannot arrest a field.

But they had taken her notebook, and her typewriter ribbon from the house on Chiltern Street had been seized that morning before dawn—she'd learned this from the sergeant's bored questions—and there were pages in that ribbon, impressions pressed into the cloth, that could be read by anyone patient enough to hold it to the light. She had thought of that around five o'clock and had to ask for water.

Now she stood on the pavement and the bells began.

Not church bells first—it was the ships. A sound came up the river like the city itself exhaling, deep and rolling, and then St. Martin-in-the-Fields caught fire with noise, and St. Clement Danes, and every steeple in London seemed to have been waiting for a signal, and the signal had come, and Clara stood with her hand on a iron railing while the whole capital rang like a struck bowl.

"Eleven o'clock," someone shouted from a window. "It's over! It's eleven o'clock and it's OVER!"

A woman dropped her shopping bag and ran into the road. A soldier lifted her clear of a motorcar with one arm and set her down and then he was crying, laughing, both at once, his mouth wide open under the bells. Men poured out of offices with their hats off. Someone had brought a gramophone onto a balcony and it scratched and warbled "Keep the Home Fires Burning" into the roar. Paper began to fall—confetti cut from ledger sheets, ticker tape, whole documents shredded into snow—and Clara watched a page spiral down past her face and land on the wet stones, and it was a requisition form, War Office letterhead, and a boot stamped it into the gutter without ceremony.

The guns had stopped. Four years, and they had stopped, and the sound of their stopping was the absence of a sound she had lived inside so long she had forgotten it was there, the way you forget your own pulse.

And underneath the bells, underneath the joy that made her throat close and her eyes sting whether she willed it or not, ran the other thing, cold as the rail under her hand: *the supplement*. Somewhere in this mad city Edith Fairweather's printer—if he still stood, if the raid hadn't gutted him too—was supposed to be pushing two thousand copies of *The Vireaux Dispatches* into a current of celebration so loud that the truth might slip through unheard, or heard too late, or heard and drowned. They had planned it for this hour precisely because this hour would bury it. And now the hour had come, and Clara was standing free in Whitehall with no way to know whether the plan had lived or died, and the bells were so beautiful and so treacherous, ringing peace over a record that might, at this very minute, still read *died of wounds, honourably*, over men who had died of an order given and denied.

She started walking. Then running. Toward Fetter Lane, because there was nowhere else in the world to go.

The crowd carried her half the distance. Strangers gripped her arms; a man in a railwayman's cap kissed her cheek and apologized and was swept away; children sat on shoulders waving little flags that had appeared from nowhere, as though London kept them folded in drawers against exactly this morning. She passed a newspaper boy already hoarse—"Armistice! Signed! Guns silent on the whole Front!"—and bought a paper she could not read while walking, and shoved it in her coat, and ran.

Fetter Lane smelled of ink and smoke. The print shop's windows were dark. Her stomach dropped through the pavement—

—and then she saw the side alley, and the barrow, and old Mr. Penhaligon the printer in his shirtsleeves, ink to the elbows, hurling bundles off a handcart to a ring of suffragists who caught them and dispersed into the crowd like water into sand. He saw her and his face cracked open.

"Miss Ashford! Thank God—they let you go!" He thrust a copy at her, hot off the press, the ink still smelling of the night. "We got most of 'em out before they came back for the rest. Miss Fairweather said—she said put them in people's hands, don't leave them on doorsteps where they can be collected. So we're handing them out like salvation, girl, like salvation!"

Clara looked down at the sheet in her hands.

THE FAIRWEATHER SUPPLEMENT, said the header. THE TRUTH ABOUT VIREAUX. Published this eleventh day of November, in the name of Captain Thomas Fairweather, and of the men who could not speak.

Below it, in type she herself had set by lamplight while soldiers sang in the street outside, ran Thomas's final dispatch. *I write this so that the order will exist somewhere besides my memory.* Her own hands had made those words permanent. Her own hands.

She looked up. Above the lane, the bells went on and on, and confetti fell through the smoke-grey light, and somewhere in the crowd a band struck up "Pack Up Your Troubles," and Clara Ashford stood holding the truth in the middle of the greatest celebration England had ever seen, laughing and weeping and utterly unable to tell which she was doing, or for which reason.

Then a boy pushed through the ring of distributors, waving a scrap of paper. "Message for the lady what sets type! From the gentleman what drives the motorcar!"

Her heart stopped, started again wrong.

The note was in Jonah's slanted, careful hand, the handwriting of a man who wrote as though someone might always be reading over his shoulder. Six words:

*I am at the Ministry. Come.*

### Jonah

The room they put him in had one table, one chair, one high window, and a radiator that ticked like a slow clock and gave no heat. Jonah Montrose sat in the chair with his hands flat on the table—he had learned in the last twelve hours that flat hands read as honesty, and honesty was the only currency he had left—and waited for the consequences of having done the single loudest thing of his invisible life.

He had dictated for forty minutes. His own testimony, start to finish, into the stenographer's machine while Halloway stood by the door with his face composed into something between pity and contempt, saying at intervals, *Montrose, you may stop this at any point, you understand, no one will think less of you.* Jonah had not stopped. He had given them dates, registration numbers, the names of the men from the motor pool, the sequence of the file's destruction, the offer of money and the ticket anywhere, the brakes on Holly Hill. When the stenographer—a young woman with quick eyes and a chapped thumb—had finished, he had asked her to read it back, corrected two dates, and signed every page. Then he'd asked for a copy.

"There won't be a copy," Halloway had said pleasantly.

"Then I'll want the original witnessed and lodged somewhere that isn't this building. There's a solicitor's office—I'll give you the address—who holds instructions from me. If I'm not seen by Friday, it goes to the *Manchester Guardian* with a covering note." This was perhaps sixty per cent true. Halloway would check. But checking took time, and time was what Jonah was buying, page by page, with the only thing he owned outright: himself.

"You understand what you've done," Halloway had said at last, gathering the pages with distaste, as though they were soiled linen. "You've tied your name to a dead man's scandal and a hysterical woman's crusade. In a week the country will want nothing but parades. No one will thank you."

"I'm not doing it to be thanked."

"No," Halloway had agreed, almost gently, at the door. "You're doing it because you spent four years reading other people's mail and discovered you had opinions. That's the tragedy of your sort, Montrose. Spectators who mistake themselves for actors." He'd paused. "Enjoy the bells. You can hear them from here, I'm told."

Now Jonah heard them. Through the high window, muffled by glass and brick, London was making the sound of a cathedral-sized heart beating with joy, and it was the loneliest music he had ever listened to.

He thought about the garage on Chiltern Street, the lamplight, the letters opened with a steam kettle and resealed with a practiced thumb. Four years of other people's wars and other people's griefs passing through his hands like water, and him believing that watching was a kind of safety. He had been wrong about that in every particular. Watching had been the crime. Witnessing, it turned out, was the punishment, and he had finally consented to serve his sentence.

His hands ached where Clara had bound them—she'd used strips of her own petticoat, tearing them with her teeth in the kitchen at three in the morning after the hill, her fingers deft and furious, saying *hold still, you idiot, hold still*—and he flexed them now against the tabletop and found he could not regret anything, not the theft of the requisition slip, not the confession, not the signed pages upstairs, not even the cold. Because the letters were loose in the world. Because Thomas Fairweather's words were in strangers' hands all over London this very minute, being read by people who owed the dead nothing and therefore might believe them.

Because when Clara read his note she would come, or she wouldn't, and either way she would do it freely, which was more than he'd ever dared hope from anyone.

Footsteps in the corridor. Not Halloway's measured tread—lighter, quicker. The door opened without a knock and the young stenographer stood there, cap already on, a paper parcel under her arm.

"They've sent me home—all typing suspended, everyone's gone to the celebrations." Her voice was low and fast. "I typed up your testimony this morning. Before I handed it in." She set the parcel on the table. Carbon copies. Every page. "You asked for a copy. Nobody gave you one. That's an oversight, that is." Her chin came up, defiant, daring him to make it strange.

Jonah stood. "What's your name?"

"Doris Wicks. And don't make a speech, Mr. Montrose, I haven't got time—the bells'll stop eventually and my mum's expecting me at the parade." At the door she turned back, and her voice dropped further. "My brother was at Amiens. He used to say the worst thing wasn't dying, it was being written up wrong afterwards." She looked at the parcel, then at him. "Get it right. That's all. Get it *right*."

The door closed. Jonah stood alone in the ticking cold with four years of the truth in carbon duplicate under his arm, and through the high window the bells of London thundered on, triumphant and terrible, and somewhere below, in the streets gone mad with peace, he heard—or imagined he heard—a familiar voice calling his name.

### Edith

Edith Fairweather had not slept, and did not intend to begin now.

She stood at the drawing-room window of Chiltern Street with the telephone receiver cradled against her shoulder, listening to the third impossible conversation of the morning. Below, the street had become a festival; someone had dragged a piano out of a boarding house and parked it on the pavement, and a soprano nobody had invited was giving out "Roses of Picardy" to three hundred strangers, some of whom were weeping, all of whom were singing the chorus back at her.

"No," Edith said into the telephone, with the patience of a woman addressing a child. "You may not send a car for me. If the inquiry wishes to interview me, it knows where I live. Yes. Yes, I understand the supplement is *irregular*. I understand that publishing private correspondence during armistice celebrations is regarded in certain quarters as *opportunistic*. Let me save us both time, Mr. Pemberton." She drew breath, and Clara's voice sounded in her memory—*tell them plainly, ma'am, it's the plainness they can't bear*—and she obeyed it.

"My brother was blamed for an order he refused. The men who arranged that blame are presently employed by His Majesty's Government. The document proving it is in the hands of several thousand Londoners as we speak, and their hands, unlike yours, are not for sale. Good morning."

She set down the receiver before the poor secretary could rally, and allowed herself thirty seconds at the window, hands clasped, watching the piano and the crowd and the paper snow.

Thomas had loved days like this. Not the war-days—the small days, leave-days, the afternoon in 1916 when they'd walked along the Serpentine and he'd said, *when it's over, Edie, I shan't want glory, I shall want a garden and an argument about politics every Sunday.* He had wanted a garden. He had gotten a dispatch, an order, a denial, a grave with the wrong story carved on it.

Well. The story was out now. It flew through the streets on cheap paper, in her dead brother's own voice, and whatever they did to her—ruin, arrest, the slow polite strangling of a difficult woman's reputation—could not call it back. She had marched for the vote and been fed in prison through a tube, and she had learned the one lesson that mattered: they can take everything except what you have already put into other people's keeping.

The front-door bell rang. Not once—repeatedly, urgently.

Edith descended. Through the frosted glass of the front door she could see two figures, and her heart performed a complicated manoeuvre, bracing for grey suits—

—but when she opened the door it was Clara, hatless, coat torn at the seam, ink-stained to the wrists, with Jonah Montrose behind her carrying a brown-paper parcel and looking like a man who had walked out of his own funeral and couldn't decide whether to apologise.

"We got the carbon copies," Clara said, breathless, holding up a printed supplement as a knight might present a sword. "Penhaligon got hundreds into the streets before the police regrouped. Ma'am—it's *everywhere*. People are reading it standing up in the crowds. A clergyman took six, for his parish. There are women outside the *Globe* office demanding they verify it." Her voice broke, just slightly, on the last word. "It's everywhere."

Edith took the supplement. Her brother's handwriting stared back at her in cold type, immortal now, unkillable. *I write this so that the order will exist somewhere besides my memory.*

It does, Tom, she thought. It exists in ten thousand memories this morning, and they are singing in the streets.

"Ma'am," Jonah said quietly. He looked older than his years and steadier than she had ever seen him. "There's something else. The testimony I gave this morning is going to force their hand—they can't bury me quietly with witnesses and carbons loose. Which means they'll need an inquiry, public, official. And an inquiry means—" he hesitated, and Edith saw him choose the plain word, Clara's word— "it means subpoenas. It means they'll summon everyone. Including you. Including—" his eyes went to Clara, and back — "including whoever testifies first, and loudest. It isn't over. Today was the shot across the bow. Tomorrow they start trying to sink us properly."

Outside, the bells were still ringing, and the piano had moved on to "Tipperary," and the crowd was roaring the refrain at the sky.

Edith Fairweather squared her shoulders, and smiled the smile that had survived Holloway Prison and the Daily Mail and thirty years of being told to sit down.

"Then we had better decide," she said, "who speaks first."
