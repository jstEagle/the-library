## 1. The Weight of Lead

The type cases woke before Gussie did, or so it seemed. In the grey half-light of a November morning she could hear them calling from the shop below — not literally, of course; lead had no voice — but the memory of them was already in her fingertips when she opened her eyes, that faint tackiness the metal left on the skin after a long day's sorting, as if the letters themselves were reluctant to be put down.

She dressed in the dark. The room above the print shop was small enough that she could touch both walls standing in the middle of it with her arms out, and the window gave onto the bridge itself — the only bridge, the mill district's single ribbon of stone and iron flung across the Irwell, and beneath her window the water ran brown and quick with last night's rain. She watched it while she pinned up her hair. The river never stopped moving. Sometimes, on days when the cases were quiet and her father dozed at his desk and no customers came, she envied it.

Downstairs, the shop smelled of ink and lamp oil and the cold-iron breath of the press. Her father was already at his stool by the composing stone, though he should not have been. August Vane rose before his daughter the way he rose before everything — before his own failing hands, before his debts, before the plain arithmetic of a trade dying slowly in a district that wanted its words printed faster and cheaper than any hand press could manage.

"You've let the fire go down," he said, without turning. He knew her step. He knew everyone's step. He claimed he could tell a customer's trade by the sound of their boots on his floorboards — weavers came in soft-heeled and hesitant, mill owners' clerks came in sharp and entitled, and the Irish labourers from the cellar dwellings came in heavy, apologising for their own weight even when they carried money.

"I'll build it up." She crouched at the grate, feeding in coal the size of knuckles. "You should still be abed, Father. Your hands were bad yesterday."

"My hands are my own business."

They were. That was the cruelty of it. His tremor had come on gradually over two years, a fine shivering in the fingers that no willpower could still, and every morning Gussie watched him fight it the way a man fights a rising river — with sandbags, with stubbornness, with an increasingly hopeless faith in his own defences. He could still set type. He set it beautifully, in fact, better than she did, because thirty years of practice lived somewhere deeper than the shaking. But he dropped things now. Twice this month she had heard the particular bright scatter of a case of type going down on stone, and twice she had come running to find him kneeling among the letters like a man gathering spilled seed, saying nothing, his jaw tight as a locked door.

She built the fire and put the kettle on and began the day's work, which was sorting.

The job was called distributing, properly — returning the used type from yesterday's formes to its proper place in the cases, each letter to its compartment, so the compositor's fingers could find it blind later, the way a musician finds notes. It was the dullest work in the trade and the most necessary, and Gussie had done it since she was six years old, standing on a stool, her father's big hand closed over her small one, teaching her that the lower case held the common letters and the upper case held capitals and that a printer who mixed them was worse than a liar.

*Lower case*, she thought, sliding a handful of *e*'s home. *That's where they keep us.*

She was twenty-two years old and she could set a page faster than any journeyman within three miles, could impose a forme so true the impression came off velvet-smooth both sides, could read reversed type as easily as reading — and none of it showed. The shop sign said **VANE & SON**, which was a joke her father refused to correct because there was no son and correcting it would mean admitting there was only a daughter, and daughters, in the estimation of Manchester, were furniture. Good furniture. Useful furniture. Furniture that kept a shop clean and a ledger straight and asked for nothing.

The bell over the door rang at half past seven.

"Morning, missus Vane." It was old Hobson from the roving mill, cap in his hands, bringing in the cold with him. Behind him, through the doorway, she could see the street: the mill gates just opening, the hands streaming in under the tall chimney's shadow, women mostly, shawled and clog-shod, their faces in the lamplight looking older than their years. "Is your father about? I've a bill wants printing. Burial club notice."

"Come in out of the draught, Mr. Hobson." She took his paper. The handwriting was a clerk's, neat enough. "This'll be twopence the hundred, same as ever. Ready by tomorrow noon."

"Tomorrow." He said it oddly, weighing it. Then, lowering his voice as if the very walls might carry tales to the masters: "There's talk, you know. At the mill. Talk of a turn-out."

"Talk's cheap in Ancoats," Gussie said, and immediately wished she hadn't, because Hobson's face went careful and he looked at her a moment too long, deciding what she was.

"Aye," he said. "Well. Cheap talk's all some of us can afford." And he touched his cap and went out into the stream of workers, one more grey figure swallowed by the grey morning.

Her father had heard. Of course he had heard; he heard everything. "Mind yourself, Gussie," he said mildly, not looking up from the forme he was locking. "A print shop is like a pulpit. People say things near it they'd never say elsewhere, and then they remember who was listening."

"Who'd I tell? The wall?"

"The wall tells nobody. You might." Now he looked up, and his eyes — hers exactly, grey as Thames-river slate, though he'd have said the colour came from the Vane side — were tired and kind and frightened all at once, the way they always were when the world pressed too close against the shop's thin walls. "We're neutral here. Neutrality is the whole of our living. The masters trust us because we print their bills of sale and never ask what's sold. The hands trust us because we print their burial notices and never charge full price. Take away the neutrality and you take away the shop, and take away the shop—" He spread his trembling hands, palms up, an argument complete in itself.

"And take away the shop and we're nothing," Gussie finished, because she knew the sermon by heart. She'd been hearing it since she could hear at all. "I know, Father."

"Do you?" He turned back to his stone. "Your mother used to say—"

He stopped. He always stopped there, at her mother, the way a man stops at the edge of a stair in the dark. Gussie waited the length of four sorted letters and then let it go, as she always did. Her mother had been dead eleven years and in all that time her father had spoken of her perhaps a dozen sentences altogether, each one cut short, each one locked behind the same expression he wore now: a man guarding a drawer he would not open.

The morning filled up with work. A grocer's handbills. An auction notice for a deceased joiner's tools, which Gussie set with particular care because the deceased had been a customer once and his widow had paid in eggs. By ten o'clock the shop was warm and smelling of hot metal, and the light through the big front window lay across the composing stone in a long pale bar, and Gussie forgot, in the way that work lets you forget, that she had ever wanted anything except to be good at this.

It didn't last. Nothing in Ancoats lasted past noon.

At half past twelve the mills turned out for dinner, and the bridge became a river of its own — four thousand hands pouring across the Irwell toward the cheap eating-houses on the far side, or home to cellar kitchens if they had coal, and those who had neither stood about in knots on the bridge itself, blowing on their fingers, sharing what there was to share. From the print shop window Gussie could see them. She could hear them, too, because the window glass was thin and November had stripped the street of every muffling leaf.

"—not a farthing these two year, and rent up—"

"—my Ellen says the new frames'll throw half the girls out before spring—"

"—they had mutton at the master's table Sunday, my cousin saw it carried in—"

Ordinary grumbling. Winter grumbling. Every winter brought it, the way it brought ice on the lock gates and coughing in the cellars, and every winter it passed. Gussie sorted type and listened with half an ear, keeping her head down as her father taught her, letting the voices wash past the glass like the river washed past the piers.

But today the voices had something underneath them. She couldn't have named it. It was less a thing said than a thing withheld, a coiled quality in the way the groups stood — closer together than usual, facing outward rather than in, the way men stand when they expect to be moved along. One of them, a big weaver with frost-reddened knuckles, kept glancing at the print shop window as he talked, and each time his eyes crossed hers through the glass, Gussie looked down at her case and felt the strange heat of being seen doing nothing.

She was still feeling it an hour later when she stepped out to fetch the afternoon's paper stock from the warehouse on the corner, and saw the bills.

They were pasted on the bridge parapet, three sheets of them in a row, fresh enough that the paste still shone wet at the corners. Cheap paper, coarse printing, the kind of job done in haste on somebody's borrowed press. But the words were set well. Whoever had composed that forme had known his trade.

**TO THE WORKING MEN OF MANCHESTER**, the first line ran, and beneath it, in type a size larger:

**VOTE BY BALLOT — NO PROPERTY QUALIFICATION — PAY OF MEMBERS — UNIVERSAL SUFFRAGE — ANNUAL PARLIAMENTS**

And below that, smaller, the line that made her stop walking altogether with the paper stock forgotten under her arm:

**THE PEOPLE'S CHARTER IS THE PEOPLE'S ONLY REMEDY. WHO WILL CARRY THE NEWS?**

Gussie stood in the middle of the bridge, in the wind off the Irwell, and read it twice. Three times. Not because the words were unfamiliar — the Charter was everywhere in the newspapers, mocked in the *Times*, denounced from pulpits, debated in the coffee rooms her father's customers frequented — but because here it was, pasted on *her* bridge, in type that someone had set by hand, letter by letter, in the dark probably, quickly, with cold fingers, and then walked away from knowing what it would cost if they were caught.

Someone had made this. Someone with ink under their nails.

She found she was smiling. She corrected it at once, arranged her face into the blank civility the street required of her, and walked on — but the smile stayed folded up inside her like a proof pulled and hidden in the apron pocket, taken out again and again through the rest of the afternoon. *Who will carry the news?* As if news were a parcel. As if it needed carriers, runners, a whole secret traffic of ordinary people passing it hand to hand. As if print — her print, her trade, the dull respectable weight of lead she handled daily — could be a live thing instead of a dead one.

By closing time the wind had picked the corner of the nearest bill loose, and it flapped against the parapet like a bird trying to get free. She watched it from the window while her father banked the fire and counted the till, his coins clicking against each other in the rhythm she knew better than any psalm.

"Those bills won't last the week," he said, without turning around. He had seen them too. He saw everything. "The constables will scrape them off, or the rain will. And whoever pasted them will think twice before spending good silver on paper the weather eats."

"Unless people want more."

"They don't. People want bread and quiet." He set the last coin in the till and closed the drawer with his hip. "Gussie. Come here."

She came. He was holding something out to her: the evening's last job, a proof sheet, fresh from the stone. She took it. It was the burial club notice for Hobson, set fair, no errors she could find.

"Well?"

"It'll do."

"'It'll do.'" He shook his head, but he was almost smiling. "Twenty years I've waited to hear you say *it's good*. You've got the best eye on this bench and you spend it saying *it'll do*, like a woman apologising for existing. Why is that?"

Because good is dangerous, she nearly said. Because if I'm good at something worth doing, someone will notice, and then everything changes. Because invisibility is warm, Father, like a coat you never take off, and I've worn it so long I've forgotten whether it fits or whether I've simply grown into the shape of the space everyone leaves around me.

"Because it does do," she said instead. "That's what matters, isn't it? That the job goes out and the customer pays and the shop stands another week."

"That's what matters," he agreed, and there was something in his voice she couldn't read, some old grief wearing patience as a disguise, and he turned away and began covering the type cases with their boards, one by one, the little wooden lids going down over the letters like coffins over faces.

Later — much later, when he had gone up to bed and the shop was dark and only the banked fire breathed red in the grate — Gussie sat alone at the composing stone with a candle, and did nothing but sit. This was her hour. She never told anyone about it. Once a week, sometimes twice, when the house was asleep, she would come down and set something for nobody: a verse she liked, a sentence from a book, once — shamefully, thrillingly — a whole paragraph of Tom Paine copied from a smuggled pamphlet a sailor customer had left behind, set, proofed, admired, and then distributed back into the cases letter by letter until the sedition was scattered into ten thousand harmless compartments and no evidence remained anywhere in the world except in her hands.

Tonight she reached for the case and stopped.

Through the floorboards, faintly, she could hear the Irwell. It had been raining up-country; the river was high and talking to itself under the arches, a low continuous mutter like a crowd waiting for something. And out on the bridge, in her mind's eye, the Charter's words stood printed in the dark: *Who will carry the news?*

She dipped her stick of type — no, she caught herself, she wasn't setting anything, she was only sitting — and looked at her hands in the candlelight. Ink in the creases of the knuckles, always, no soap entirely removed it. Lead-grey traces under the nails. Her father's hands, thirty years younger, doing her father's work, saying her father's *it'll do*.

*Who will carry the news?*

Somewhere out there, tonight, someone was setting type by candlelight for strangers, for a cause, for the sheer unbearable purposefulness of it — and getting paid in silver, maybe, or maybe paying in risk, and either way they were *alive* in a way Gussie could feel in her chest like a second heartbeat.

She blew out the candle. She went up to bed. She slept badly, and dreamed of rivers, and in the morning the first thing she saw through her window was a constable on the bridge, scraping the bills off the parapet with a long knife, and the second thing — because the world has a sense of humour, and it was November, and anything might happen — was a stranger coming up the street toward the shop, a gentleman by his coat, with cuffs so clean they seemed lit from within.

He paused at the door. He read the sign. **VANE & SON**.

Then he smiled, and pushed it open, and the bell rang.
