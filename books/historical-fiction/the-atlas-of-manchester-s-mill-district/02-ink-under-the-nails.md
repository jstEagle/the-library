## 2. Ink Under the Nails

The bell above the shop door had a cracked voice — it had had one for as long as Gussie could remember, a thin, splintered note like a coin dropped down a well. On Tuesday the eleventh of October, 1842, it rang at half past four in the afternoon, and she knew before she looked up that the man coming through it was not one of theirs.

She knew it by his boots first. The mill men who came for bills of fare and funeral cards wore boots gone soft at the toe, patched at the heel, black with grease that no lye would lift. This man's boots were brown leather, uncracked, and they struck the floorboards with the deliberate weight of someone accustomed to being listened to. Then his cuffs: white linen, starched, clean as new snow in a town where nothing stayed white past nine in the morning.

"Mr. Vane?" he said, and his accent was Manchester through and through — the flat vowels, the swallowed consonants — but there was something laid over it, something polished, the way a clerk learns to speak so his masters forget where he came from. "Augustus Vane, printer?"

"My father," Gussie said, without looking up from the case. She was sorting twelve-point long primer, the e's into their compartment, the a's, the thin spaces, her left hand moving on its own arithmetic while her right held the composing stick. "He's at the press."

"Then I'll trouble him." The man set his hat on the counter — a good hat, beaver felt, brushed — and folded his hands atop it. His fingers were inkless. That was the strangest thing, Gussie thought. A man who wanted printing done and had never once got ink under his nails.

Her father came up from the back wiping his hands on his apron, and she watched him take the measure of the stranger the way he took the measure of everything now: slowly, a half-beat behind, his eyes doing a small uncertain drift before they settled. The tremor had been getting worse since spring. Some mornings he couldn't hold the stick steady enough to justify a line, and he'd stand there with his jaw tight, hating the type for moving, hating his hand for failing, until Gussie reached over without a word and finished it for him. He never thanked her. Thanking would have made it real.

"Sir," her father said. "What can we do for you?"

"Printing." The stranger smiled. It was a practised smile, warm at the edges and empty in the middle, like a shop window dressed for a sale that had already ended. "Five hundred handbills. Single leaf, one side. I've the copy here." He drew a folded paper from inside his coat and smoothed it flat upon the counter with two fingers, an oddly tender gesture, the way a priest smooths a page of scripture.

Gussie kept her eyes on the type case. But her ears were better than her father's, and her father's were better than most.

"Five hundred," her father said. "Size of paper?"

"Half-sheet will do. Something legible. This is meant to be read aloud, Mr. Vane. In rooms. At meetings." A pause you could have balanced a penny on. "Public meetings."

There was a law about public meetings now. There had been laws all year, each one tightening like a screw — the meeting banned, then the procession banned, then the very reading of certain documents declared a thing a magistrate might call sedition if the mood took him. Gussie's hands did not stop moving. E, a, thin space. But something in her chest went very quiet and very awake, the way it did when the mills' great wheels stopped all at once and the whole district seemed to hold its breath.

"May I see the copy?" her father asked.

"You may read it. You needn't keep it." The stranger stepped aside, courteous, giving the old man room, and Augustus Vane bent over the paper with his spectacles pushed up his forehead and his lips moving.

Gussie could not see the page. She didn't need to. She'd been setting Chartist phrasing in her head for a year, ever since the first bills had gone up on the bridge railings last spring — *the Charter and nothing less*, *fair wages or no wages*, *we are many, they are few*. She knew the cadence the way a musician knows a key. And when her father read aloud, softly, because he always read aloud when the words mattered — "*Fellow-countrymen* — no. Fellow-*workers*. 'Your masters have told you the trade is bad. Your masters have told you the times are hard. We say to you: the times were never so hard that the masters dined worse—'"

"That'll do," the stranger said pleasantly.

Her father looked up over the spectacles. For a moment the shop was silent except for the tick of the stove and the river going under the bridge below them, a sound so constant you only heard it when everything else stopped.

"This is Chartist matter," her father said.

"It is a handbill," said the stranger, "commissioned and paid for."

"The magistrates—"

"—are not standing in this shop." The smile was still there but it had changed temperature, cooled several degrees, become the smile of a man discussing the weather with someone too foolish to bring an umbrella. "I am told, Mr. Vane, that yours is a respectable house. That your work is clean and your discretion is cleaner. I am told your late wife—" and here his eyes flicked, just briefly, toward Gussie, taking her inventory: young woman, ink to the second knuckle, left-handed, saying nothing — "kept a tidy shop, and that tidiness survives her."

Gussie set down the composing stick. Very carefully, the way you set down a thing you want to throw.

"We don't do political work," she said.

The stranger turned to her fully for the first time. Up close he was younger than she'd guessed — thirty, perhaps, or a little more — with a face built for committees, regular and forgettable, the kind of face you'd struggle to describe to a constable an hour after seeing it. Only his eyes stayed with you: pale grey, patient, and quite without warmth, though they pretended otherwise.

"And your name, miss?"

"She speaks for the shop," her father said quickly. "We don't do political work, sir. Not since— we don't do it."

"Since your wife died." The stranger nodded, unsurprised, unhurried. From his coat pocket he produced a purse — not a wallet, a purse, soft leather, the kind that jingles — and set five coins upon the counter in a row, touching each one into place like a man laying a table. Silver. Half-crowns. Gussie's eye did the sum before she could stop it: two pounds ten. Enough to buy the winter's coal twice over. Enough to settle the account at Prosser's for paper stock they'd been owing against since July. Enough, God help them, for the doctor her father refused to see about his hand.

"Half now, half on delivery," the stranger said. "The other half-crowns wait for the work. Five hundred copies, half-sheet, legible at six feet, delivered Thursday night to the yard behind the Wheatsheaf on Great Ancoats Street. No wrapper, no bill of parcels, no name of any kind on the job. I believe you call it work done *quietly*."

"We call it nothing," Gussie said. "Because we don't do it."

"Gussie." Her father's voice, low, worn. Not angry. Worse than angry. Tired, the way stone is tired, the way a wall is tired, holding itself up out of pure habit. "Leave us a moment, love."

"I'm sorting type."

"Leave us a moment."

She went to the back room, but she did not close the door — the door stuck in damp weather and closed with a bang that would have announced her eavesdropping to the whole street, and besides, she reasoned, if they meant to talk privately they'd have asked properly. So she stood among the reams of paper with her arms crossed and heard every word, and hated herself a little for how carefully she listened.

"Mr. Vane." The stranger's voice had changed register again — lower now, almost gentle, the voice of a man who had learned that some doors open to force and others to sympathy. "I know your circumstances. I make it my business to know the circumstances of every tradesman on this bridge. I know the coal is dear. I know your landlord has raised the rent twice since Lady Day because he knows you cannot move — the bridge is shut for repairs come November and no cart can carry a press across the river till spring. I know you've a daughter who works like a man and gets paid like a ghost. Five hundred handbills, Mr. Vane. Two pound ten. No names. By Thursday the paper's off your premises and the silver's in your pocket, and whatever comes of it comes to me."

"And what comes of it?" her father asked quietly. "What comes to you?"

"What always comes of paper and ink, sir." A pause; she could hear the smile in it, feel it like a draft under a door. "Reading."

Silence. The stove ticked. Below, the Irwell went on talking to itself under its first skin of ice.

Then her father said, in the voice of a man stepping off a ledge because the ledge was going anyway: "Thursday night. Half the money now, as you say. My daughter sets the form. She's the best compositor between here and London, whoever tells you different is a liar, and I'll have it said plainly: if the work is crooked, it's hers, and if it's clean, it's mine. Is that understood?"

"Perfectly," said the stranger, and Gussie heard the coins being swept off the counter into a palm, the small bright music of complicity.

She went back through as the man took up his hat. He paused beside her at the door — near enough that she caught his smell, which was wrong somehow, soap and clean wool and under it something faintly chemical, sulphur maybe, or the sharp ghost of a print-shop of his own somewhere, some larger shop, some shop that belonged to men whose cuffs never needed cleaning because other hands did the work.

"You sort type," he said. It wasn't a question. "Left-handed. Unusual. Quicker that way, they say — the left hand lifts while the right places. Like rowing." He put his hat on. "Good evening, miss."

The bell gave its cracked note and he was gone, his footsteps crossing the boards, descending to the bridge, fading into the noise of the district — the mill wheels starting up the night shift, a woman calling a child home, the far iron groan of something being loaded at the wharf.

Gussie stood looking at the space where he'd stood. On the counter, next to the folded copy her father still held, lay one of the half-crowns, fallen or left, she couldn't tell which. It caught the lamplight and threw it back at her, a little cold moon.

"He dropped that," she said.

"No," her father said. He was looking at the copy in his hands as if it were a bird that might startle. "He didn't."

* * *

They worked on it Wednesday, and worked on it badly, which was to say they worked on it like guilty people.

Her father wanted to lock the shop door and set the form himself, alone, as if solitude were the same thing as secrecy. Gussie pointed out that locking the door in working hours was more conspicuous than leaving it open; that Mrs. Ogden next door noted such things the way other women noted fashions; that the whole street would know the Vanes had something to hide before the kettle boiled twice. Her father snapped at her — *then what would you have, girl?* — and she snapped back — *openness, Father, like honest shops* — and then they both fell silent, because honesty was precisely what this job was not, and they both knew whose idea it had finally been.

It had been his. That was the thing she kept turning over, like a coin of her own. All her life he'd preached neutrality the way other printers preached religion: *we set what they pay for, we ask no questions, ink is ink*. It was the creed her mother had died outside of, in a sense — dead eleven years now of the fever that came up from the cellar dwellings along Store Street, dead with a woodcut block unfinished on her bench and her husband spending a decade afterward making sure the Vane name touched nothing sharper than a christening card. *Ink is ink.* And now Augustus Vane had taken Chartist money in his own hand and given his own terms for the work, and Gussie could not decide whether this was weakness, or weariness, or the first crack in a wall she'd believed was load-bearing all her life.

Wednesday night they set it. She set it, rather; her father distributed, handing her the type piece by piece from the case because he said it steadied him to be useful, and she saw that it did, and let him believe she believed it.

But setting the copy meant reading it, word by word, letter by letter, backwards and forwards and upside down, the way compositors read everything — and so she read it more closely than its author had ever read it, more closely than any reader in any room in Ancoats would read it, and by midnight she knew it the way you know a scar.

*Your masters tell you the trade is bad. Ask them what their dividends said last quarter. Your masters tell you the times are hard. Ask them what they paid for their daughters' pianofortes.*

It was good copy. That was the shame of it. It was plain, muscular, built to be shouted in a cellar room by candlelight with forty hungry men listening — no Latin, no flourishes, every sentence ending in a blow. Whoever wrote it knew the district, knew the mills, knew exactly which nerve ran closest to the skin. And underneath the craft of it Gussie recognized the skeleton beneath: the People's Charter, the six points, universal suffrage wearing its work clothes. The stranger hadn't bothered to disguise it much. He hadn't needed to. The disguise was in the buying, not the selling — a handbill printed by a respectable house on the bridge, unsigned, deniable, worth ten pounds to whichever magistrate finally decided to care.

Ten pounds. The reward for seditious libel, if they called it that, was ten pounds plus costs. Two pound ten sat in the till downstairs like a sleeping animal she could hear breathing through the floorboards.

"You're pressing the e's too hard," her father said. "You'll batter the face."

She looked down. She was. Every e in the last line stood slightly bruised, its counters filled, its serifs chipped — her left hand bearing down on the type as if it owed her money.

"They'll read it anyway," she said.

"Aye." He took the stick from her gently, turned it to the light, squinted at her damaged e's. "Aye, they will." He did not correct them. She noticed that. He locked up the chase around them, battered letters and all, and said nothing, and she understood that he had decided the flaws were safer — a job with faults in it looks like a job done fast and cheap, a job done by a poor man, a job no magistrate troubles to trace to good stock. Her father was protecting them in the language of type, and she loved him for it and despised the necessity, and could not have said which feeling was stronger if her life had depended on it.

* * *

Thursday came grey and smelling of coal smoke and river. Gussie worked the day job with her sleeves pinned back — a hundred visiting cards for the draper on the corner, dull as porridge — and all day the five hundred handbills waited in the back, tied in bundles of fifty, wrapped in waste paper, smelling of fresh ink and oil, alive. She caught herself listening to them. That was the madness of it. They made no sound, and still she listened, the way you listen to a clock in an empty room.

At seven, dark having come down early as it does in October, she carried the first three bundles out herself. Her father wanted to hire the boy from the chandlery, but the boy talked, everyone's boy talked, and besides — she argued, and heard even as she said it that the argument was a lie with good posture — besides, a woman with a shawl full of washing draws no eyes on Great Ancoats Street.

The yard behind the Wheatsheaf was mud and barrel staves and the sweet-rotten stink of spent hops. A cart stood waiting with its lamp hooded. Men moved around it without speaking, quick and economical, and took the bundles from her arms as if they were infants, as if they were ammunition — and she supposed, watching them stow them under sacking, that the difference hardly mattered anymore. One of them, a big man with a mill-hand's shoulders and a clerk's careful voice, pressed the remaining half-crowns into her palm, counted them aloud in a whisper, touched his cap.

"Much obliged to the house," he said. "Tell your father the Charter remembers its friends."

And there it was. Said plain, in the open air, in the yard of a public house: *the Charter remembers its friends.* Not a handbill. Not neutral ink. A debt, entered in a ledger she hadn't known she'd opened.

She walked home fast with the empty shawl clutched to her chest like something stolen, which it was, in a manner of speaking. The gas lamps were lit along the bridge. Under the third one she stopped, without deciding to stop, and looked at her hands in the light. Ink in the cuticle of every finger, black as always, black as it had been since she was eight years old — but tonight it looked different. Tonight it looked like evidence.

She had said nothing. That was the sentence she kept returning to as the bridge planks rang under her boots. Through the whole of it — the silver on the counter, the copy in her father's trembling hands, the bundles going out the door into the dark — she had recognized the work for what it was and she had said nothing. She could have stopped it. One word to her father, one refusal, one honest sentence spoken at the right moment, and the stranger would have taken his dirty commission to some cellar press in Angel Meadow where it belonged, and the Vanes would have remained what they had been for eleven years: nobody, harmless, invisible.

Instead she had sorted the e's.

And the terrible thing, the thing she would not say aloud to anyone, kneeling later to bank the stove while her father slept upstairs — the thing that sat in her chest like a swallowed coal, burning comfortably — was that she was not ashamed. Or rather: she was ashamed, thoroughly, specifically, ashamed of the lie to her father's creed and the danger to the shop and the battered e's and the silver warming in the till — and underneath all of it, beneath the shame like a vein of ore beneath topsoil, ran something else entirely. Something that had lifted when the big man said *the Charter remembers its friends*, something that had lifted when the bundles left her arms, something with a pulse.

For eleven years she had been the invisible daughter of an invisible shop, competent and quiet, sorting other people's words into order. Tonight five hundred pieces of paper with her bruises in every letter were moving through the district in the dark, going into cellars and chapels and kitchens, going into hands, going into mouths to be read aloud. Somebody would stand up in a crowded room tomorrow and read a line she had set, and forty voices would answer it, and not one of them would ever know her name.

That was the thrill. And the shame was that it was a thrill.

She raked the stove dead and washed her hands at the basin, scrubbing at the ink the way she did every night, the way that never worked, the stain living down in the skin where soap couldn't reach. In the dark glass of the window her reflection hung faint and doubled over the lamplit street, and she looked at it for a long moment — a young woman, ink-stained, twenty-two years old, standing in her father's shop on the only bridge out of Ancoats.

Through the glass, down at the far end of the bridge, a figure stood under the last lamp. Clean cuffs caught the light when he shifted his weight. He was not looking at her window; he was looking at the shop; and he was waiting, patient as type in a case, for something.

Gussie blew out the lamp.
