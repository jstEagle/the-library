## 3. The Bridge Closes

The first scaffolding pole went up on the Tuesday, and Gussie felt it in her feet before she saw it — a tremor through the floorboards of the shop that was not the press, not a cart, not the mills. She set down her composing stick and went to the window over the bridge, where the frost ferns on the glass had melted into a clear oval from the heat of the stove below.

They were driving oak piles into the near abutment. Four men with mauls, another two hauling on ropes, a foreman in a coat too good for the work standing with his hands in his pockets like a man watching his own funeral procession. The poles rose one by one against the grey sky, black and straight as the fingers of something enormous reaching up out of the river to take hold of the bridge.

"Gussie." Her father's voice came from behind the press, thin as a pulled thread. "What's the noise?"

"Scaffolding," she said. "On the bridge."

He appeared at her shoulder, wiping his hands on his apron though they were clean; it was what he did instead of thinking. He looked out at the poles for a long moment, and Gussie watched his face do the arithmetic she had already done — the winter repairs announced in September's *Guardian*, buried on page four between an auction notice and a sermon summary. The bridge would be closed from the first hard frost until spring. Timber decking over the arches, new stonework on the cutwaters, the whole structure shored up like an old man before a long illness.

"They'll close the road," her father said.

"They'll close everything but a footway, if that."

"The mail—"

"Will go round by the New Bridge. Two miles east."

He nodded slowly, the way he nodded at bad news about strangers, and went back to his press, and Gussie stood at the window a while longer watching the mauls fall, each blow landing somewhere under her ribs.

* * *

By Thursday the district knew, the way a body knows a wound before the mind admits it. The bridge was the only way across the Irwell for a mile in either direction — the only cart road, the only coal road, the only road the mail coach took, the only road a man could walk out on with his wages in his pocket and come back with food. The Ancoats side held the mills and the cellar dwellings and the chapel and the shops, theirs among them, wedged at the bridgehead like a keystone nobody thought about until it cracked. The far side held everything else: the market proper, the coal yard, the magistrates' court, the world.

The coal carts stopped first. Gussie heard them turn away on the far bank — the drivers shouting, the foreman waving them off toward the eastern crossing, the horses' breath steaming in the cold like little locomotives denied their line. Then the carrier's van, which had come every week since she was six years old, bringing paper stock and ink and the parcels her father ordered from London and took three months to pay for. The carrier did not even slow down this time. She watched him cross the far end of the bridge, tip his hat to the foreman with the good coat, and roll away east, and she understood that the map of her life had been redrawn without her consent.

"We have stock?" her father asked that evening, counting sheets by candlelight because the gas was a luxury he rationed like medicine.

"Eleven reams of foolscap. Six of the good wove. Ink enough for a month if we're careful, longer if we stretch it."

"And after?"

"After we buy dear, or wait till spring."

He said nothing to that. The candle flame leaned when the door draft found it, and in its light his hands looked older than the rest of him — spotted, scarred with lead, the right thumb carrying the permanent grey crescent of a man who had set type for thirty years and never once washed it fully clean. She had loved those hands her whole life. That winter she began to be afraid of them.

* * *

It was Ellen Brack who named the thing plainly, the following week, when she came in for the mill notices.

"They've made us an island," she said, laying her halfpenny on the counter. Her knuckles were split from the cold; she'd been washing in water from the river because the pump nearest her court had frozen. "Island folk, all of us. And islands starve, Miss Vane, when the ships stop coming."

"That's dramatic, Mrs. Brack."

"That's sums." She counted on those split fingers. "Coal comes over the bridge. Bread flour comes over the bridge. The relief committee's money comes over the bridge, when it comes at all, and it comes slower every week. You can walk across the scaffolding planks if you're brave and the foreman likes your face, but you can't carry a sack of coal across a plank with four thousand families queuing for it." She took her change and paused, not quite looking at Gussie. "My Tom says come spring there'll be fighting over the first cart through."

"Your Tom says a great deal."

"My Tom is generally right and always loud." But there was no humor in it, not really. She gathered her shawl and went out into the wind off the river, and Gussie watched her go, small and straight-backed, crossing the bridge on the narrow timber footway the workmen had left open, walking as if the river below were a thing she refused to acknowledge existed.

* * *

Gussie had spent twenty-three years believing that walls were safety.

She had built them herself, brick by deliberate brick: the shop with its smell of oil and lead, the routine that never varied, the customers who knew her as "the Vane girl" and asked nothing else of her. Invisibility had been her inheritance along with the type cases — her father's whole philosophy of survival compressed into a single instruction, delivered once, when she was fourteen and had asked why he wouldn't print a letter for the workingmen's association that other shops in town were taking. *"We print what's paid for and nothing else, Gussie. A printer who takes sides drowns with the side that loses, and there's no side that wins forever."*

She had believed him. Or she had believed in the life his belief had built — the warm shop, the ordered cases, the small competence of a woman who could justify a page of scripture to the last em quad without measuring twice. The district around her might seethe — wages falling, rents rising, the mills running their engines day and night while the operatives ate bread and onions — but inside the shop the worst news was a late payment, and the loudest voice was her father reading aloud the shipping reports.

Now the scaffolding rose, and she saw what she had actually built.

Not a wall. A cage. The difference being that a wall keeps things out, and a cage keeps things in, and she had chosen the bars herself, rung by rung, year by year, mistaking them for shelter.

She felt it most at night. The shop slept above the works, as always, and in the old days the night sounds had been the sounds of traffic — wheels on stone, the mail horn at eleven, drunks singing coming back from the pubs on the far bank, the whole breathing commerce of a bridge that connected them to everything. Now the nights were silent except for the river and the workmen's watchman coughing in his hut, and the silence pressed on the windows like deep water. Once, waking at three, she lay listening and could not shake the conviction that she could hear the ice forming on the Irwell — a faint crystalline ticking, like type being set somewhere beneath the water by hands slower than hers.

* * *

The mail was the worst of it, though she couldn't have said why at first.

The coach had been a ritual: eleven o'clock, the horn, the guard tossing down the canvas bag to her father at the door because the Vane shop was the district's receiving house, had been for forty years. Letters from sons in America. Bills. The radical papers her father pretended not to read and read cover to cover. Every evening the bag, and every morning the little wooden drawer beside the counter where the neighborhood collected its news of the outside world.

Now the bag went east, and the letters came back days late, sorted at some distant office, carried in by a boy on foot over the scaffolding planks for a penny a trip. The drawer filled slowly, then emptied slowly, and the neighborhood noticed the delay the way a body notices a failing heart — not at once, but as a general dimming.

"It's not just letters," Gussie said one night, sorting the delayed post by candlelight. "It's time itself. We're running behind the rest of the world now. Whatever happens out there, we'll hear of it late, and answer later, and by the time our answer arrives the moment will have passed."

"What moments does a print shop miss?" her father asked, mild, not looking up from his ledger.

She almost laughed. Almost told him. The pamphlets she'd been reading — the ones passed hand to hand in the chapel yard, the ones with the People's Charter demands printed in type so like her own that she'd caught herself checking the letterforms for family resemblances. The meetings. The talk of a general strike when the new poor-law commission report landed. The sense, everywhere in the district that November, of a kettle beginning to murmur before the boil.

"A printer who misses the moment prints nothing worth the ink," she said, which was safe, and true, and not the tenth part of what she meant.

Her father looked at her then, over the top of his spectacles, with an expression she couldn't parse — something between pride and premonition. "Your mother used to say the bridge was the best and worst thing that ever happened to this family."

"She said that? Why?"

"Best, because everything passes over it and a printer sees it all. Worst—" he turned back to his ledger, and his pen scratched, and he did not finish the sentence. Gussie waited. He did not finish it ever.

* * *

December came in on a north wind that smelled of snow, and the island tightened.

The price of coal doubled in ten days. The relief committee opened a soup kitchen in the chapel schoolroom, and Gussie watched the queue form past her window each morning — four hundred strong, then six hundred, women first because the men were ashamed, children tucked into shawls like bundles of rags. The mill agents posted notices turning away even casual labor. On the far bank, visible through the scaffold poles like scenery beyond prison bars, the market went on: carts, barrows, the coal yard steaming, ordinary life conducted in full view of people who could no longer reach it.

She caught herself testing the distance. Standing at the window, measuring the span of the river with her eyes — eighty feet, perhaps ninety, dark water going grey at the edges now with the first skin of ice. A swimmer could cross it in summer. In January it would be a death. The footway over the scaffolding remained open, a plank path with a handrail, guarded by a watchman who charged a halfpenny and decided by his own lights who looked respectable enough to pass.

She crossed it once, deliberately, to buy ink from the oil-and-colorman on the far side rather than wait for the boy. The planks flexed under her boots. Below her, through the gaps, the river ran green-black and quick, carrying scum and sticks and one drowned rat, rolling toward Manchester and the sea and everywhere else in the world. Halfway across she stopped, both hands on the rail, and looked down, and felt the vertigo rise in her like sickness — not fear of falling, but the sudden terrible knowledge that she had lived her whole life within sight of this water and never once considered it, never once wondered where it went, because the shop had been enough, the walls had been enough, the small warm room of her competence had been the whole of the world and she had called it peace.

The watchman coughed. She walked on.

On the far side she bought her ink and stood in the market square among the carts and the noise, and found that it didn't feel like freedom. It felt like a visit. The island had gotten into her already. She kept calculating the hour, the walk back, the plank path, the shop, the drawer of delayed letters, the father with his unfinished sentence — kept feeling the pull of the cage even from outside it, the way a bird that has known the bars will return to perch on them when the door stands open.

That frightened her more than anything the winter had yet done.

* * *

The snow came on the fourth of December and stayed. It filled the scaffolding joints, outlined every pole in white, turned the district into a woodcut of itself — and Gussie, setting a jobbing bill for the chandler, realized with a lurch that she was thinking in images again, framing the bridge as a composition, the black poles and white snow and the queue of women below like elements waiting for a block to be cut. Her mother had drawn woodcuts. She knew this the way she knew the shape of her own hands: absolutely, and never once had examined it.

That evening she asked her father, casually, over supper: "What happened to Mother's blocks? Her engravings."

The spoon stopped halfway to his mouth. For three full seconds the shop above the street was silent enough that Gussie heard the ice ticking on the river, real this time, unmistakable.

"Furniture," he said finally. "Packing crates. Some things burn better than they keep." He ate his supper. The subject was closed with the finality of a forme locked up tight, quoins driven home, nothing to be moved without breaking the whole apart.

But he had said *burn*, not *lost*, and Gussie had spent her life reading other people's words closely enough to know the difference between a man telling the truth and a man choosing the least damaging lie.

She climbed to bed past the locked drawer in the old bureau on the landing — the drawer her father had kept locked since the funeral, the key gone wherever such keys go — and lay listening to the river freeze, tick by tick by tick, the island growing heavier around her, the water closing over inch by inch.

Somewhere out beyond the scaffolding the world was moving on without them. Somewhere the Charter was being signed in thousands of names. Somewhere a kettle was murmuring toward the boil. And here she lay in the warm cage she had built herself, invisible, safe, and — for the first time in her twenty-three years, lying awake in the dark with her heart going like a press at speed — unbearably restless.

In the morning, the first Chartists' bills would appear pasted to the scaffold poles themselves, close enough to touch from the shop window.

She would read them through the glass, and pretend otherwise.
