## 4. The Lockout

The bell went at half past six, as it always did, and Ellen Brack was through the gate of Ashworth's Mill before the last of it died away, her shawl pinned tight against the November damp and her clogs striking sparks off the cobbles. She had been a piecener at eleven, a spinner at fifteen, and at twenty-six she knew the sound of that mill the way she knew her own breathing — the groan of the shafts waking floor by floor, the smell of oil and cotton dust and the sour steam off the canal. She knew it was wrong the moment she stepped inside.

The engines were cold.

Not banked — cold. No fireman's boy hauling coal to the boilers, no hiss from the pipes, no tremor in the floorboards that every hand in Ancoats felt in their boots before they felt it anywhere else. The spinning room stood dark as a cellar, four hundred mules silent under the gas brackets with their glass chimneys still sooty from the night shift. Overhead, the line-shafting hung dead, belts slack as hanged men.

Mr. Ogden, the overlooker, stood at the foot of the stairs with his hat in his hands, which told Ellen more than any notice could have. Ogden had not taken his hat off indoors since the year the Queen came.

"Turned out," he said, when the crowd of them had pressed close enough. "All hands. Every mill on the estate. Master's orders — no cause given, no date given. You can collect what wages are owing at the counting house, and then —" He looked at the hat instead of at them. "Then that's all there is."

Nobody shouted. That was the strange thing Ellen remembered afterwards, when she had time to remember anything: nobody shouted. Four thousand people across the district, and in that first hour the loudest sound was a woman two floors up weeping without any noise at all, just her shoulders going, going, like a loom out of true.

Ellen walked home the long way, past the other mills, because she wanted to see it with her own eyes. Turned out at Brookfield's. Turned out at the New Mill, where the great gates were chained though there was nothing inside worth chaining but silence. At the corner by the bridge she stopped, because there was a bill pasted fresh on the hoarding, its paste still shining, and she read it twice to be sure she had it right.

NOTICE. — The undersigned manufacturers will receive no hands into employment who shall decline to accept such reduction of wages as may be found necessary...

She did not finish reading it. A man beside her read it aloud for her anyway, slowly, in the voice men use for scripture or bad news, and then he spat, carefully, so as not to miss.

"Reduction," he said. "They've turned us out before we could refuse it. That's the trick of it. We're locked out and they'll write it down as if we struck."

Ellen's brother Joseph was waiting outside their cellar door on Canal Street when she got back, arms folded, jaw set in the way that meant he had already been drinking or was thinking about it. Their mother sat inside on the one chair, shelling nothing, just moving her hands over an empty bowl because her hands needed work the way lungs need air.

"Well?" Joseph said.

"Four thousand," Ellen said. "Maybe more. They say it's every mill from Great Ancoats Street to the river."

"And the relief committee?"

"Will open tomorrow, I should think. There's talk of subscriptions, of bread scales."

Joseph laughed, one hard note. "Bread scales. We'll be measured out our dinner like meal for cattle." He kicked at the step. "I've got Mary and the babby, Ellen. Eleven shillings a week, and now nowt. What am I to do, stand in a queue with my cap in my hand?"

"Yes," Ellen said. "That is exactly what you're to do. And I'll stand behind you, and so will Mother, and we'll hold our heads while we do it, because if we go begging to any man on our knees we'll never get off them again."

He stared at her, and something in him settled — not agreement, but the temporary truce of a man who has no better plan. Inside, their mother put down the empty bowl.

* * *

The relief committee opened in the schoolroom off Miller Street, and by seven in the morning the queue reached the corner and doubled back on itself, a slow grey river of people standing in the sleet with their collars up and their children tucked inside their coats. Ellen had counted the first fifty yards out of habit — you learned to count things in a mill — and then stopped, because the number made her sick.

They moved forward a yard at a time. The hours went by. A woman fainted two places ahead and was carried to the wall, and the queue closed over the space where she'd stood the way water closes over a stone. Men who had worked twenty years at the same frame stood holding their caps and saying nothing, and their wives did the talking, because wives were used to asking for things in a voice that didn't crack, and the shame of it sat on all of them like wet wool.

When Ellen finally reached the trestle table, a chapel deacon with ink on his fingers looked up her name in a ledger and asked her questions in a low voice — married? children? rent? how much owing? — and wrote the answers down as though writing them down were help. It was help, of a kind. It was also being weighed, and she knew it, and she stood straight under the weighing.

"Half-stone of oatmeal, quarter-pound of tea, and there's soup at the chapel Thursday," the deacon said. "Next."

She took the parcel and stepped aside, and that was when she saw the girl.

She was standing near the wall with a canvas satchel slung across her body, the kind printers' errand-boys carried, and she had the look of someone not queuing but watching — dark hair escaping a plain bonnet, sleeves rolled to the elbow, and on the fingers of her right hand, unmistakable even at ten feet, the stains. Blue-black crescents under the nails, along the cuticle, worked into the knuckle creases. Ink. Not washerwoman's bluing, not a clerk's single blot. The deep ground-in blackness of a person who lives with type.

Ellen knew her. Of course she knew her. The print shop on the bridge — Vane's — small windows, gold lettering gone brown, the smell of hot metal drifting out onto the span on summer evenings. Ellen had taken bills there to be printed for a chapel tea once, years ago, and the father had quoted the price and this girl — younger then, fourteen maybe — had stood at the cases sorting letters with a speed that had seemed almost like music, her hands never still, her eyes never lifting. Ellen had thought, watching her through the glass: *there's a trade that keeps a body clean.* Now she saw the truth of it written on those stained fingers.

The girl was looking at the queue. Not at the parcels, not at the soup tickets — at the faces. Reading them the way she'd read type, Ellen thought, one character at a time.

Ellen crossed to her. It was a mad thing to do; she had a parcel to carry home, a mother waiting, a brother spoiling for trouble. She crossed anyway.

"You're Vane's daughter," she said. "From the shop on the bridge."

The girl's chin came up a fraction — wariness first, then something else, something that might have been relief at being known. "Augusta. Gussie." She glanced at the relief table, at the ledger, at Ellen's stained apron with its spinner's wear at the knee. "You've been hours in that line."

"Three and a half." Ellen shifted the parcel to her hip. "There's four thousand of us turned out, miss, and not one of them asked whether we'd take the cut before the gates were chained. They'll call it a strike in the papers. You'll know, won't you — what gets printed. Your trade prints everything in this town."

Gussie said nothing, but she didn't look away either, and Ellen had spent enough years reading overlookers' faces to know that silence was not the same thing as refusal. Silence was a door still shut. Refusal was a door slammed.

"I'm not begging for myself," Ellen said, and heard her own voice go flat and steady, the voice she used for the mule headstock when the carriage kicked. "I'm not begging at all, if you want the truth of it. I'm asking. There's a difference, and I mean to keep the difference while I've got a mind left to keep it with. But there are four thousand people on these streets who cannot read the notices that turned them out, and half the rest can only read what your trade chooses to set for them. The masters have their newspapers. The magistrates have their proclamations. We've got—" She stopped. Looked down at her own rough hands, red at the knuckles, cracked at the tips. "We've got nothing printed. Nothing at all."

A constable passed the end of the street, unhurried, and both of them watched him go.

"The workers would pay what they could," Ellen went on, quieter now. "Coppers, mostly. Maybe not even that, not yet — but there'd be a debt owed, and working folk pay their debts if it takes years. Handbills. Notices of meetings. Whatever tells the truth of this lockout, plain, in words a piecener can read after a twelve-hour day — if she ever gets a twelve-hour day again." She held the girl's eyes. "Will the Vanes print for the workers?"

It was a plain question, and she had meant it to be. She watched it land. Watched the girl glance toward the bridge — the shop sat square on the span itself, you could hear the presses through the floor of the roadway — and then glance away again, quick, and Ellen saw the whole arithmetic happen behind those eyes: a father, a livelihood, a window that could be broken as easily as any mill window, a name that could be entered in a magistrate's ledger next to the word *seditious*.

"It's not mine to promise," Gussie said at last.

"No," Ellen agreed. "It's yours to ask. That's better than most of us get."

For a moment neither of them spoke, and the sleet ticked on the canvas of the satchel between them, and somewhere down Miller Street a baby started crying in a way that meant hunger rather than temper, the thin relentless cry that had become, in three days, the ordinary music of Ancoats.

"My mother died before I knew her properly," Gussie said, suddenly, as if the queue and the sleet and the constable had all stepped out of the room. "My father doesn't speak of her. There's a press in that shop older than me, and type I've been told never to touch." Her stained fingers curled around the satchel strap. "I don't know why I'm telling you this."

"Because I asked you a plain question and nobody's asked you one in a long while," Ellen said. "People ask printers for prices. Nobody asks them what they think."

Gussie laughed — a short, startled breath, quickly swallowed. "You don't know what you're asking of us."

"I know exactly." Ellen lifted her chin. "I'm asking you to risk what we've already lost."

* * *

She walked home along the canal with the oatmeal heavy against her hip and the girl's face fixed in her mind — not the answer, which had been no answer, but the hunger underneath it. Ellen knew hunger of every kind by now: the belly kind, which she had known since childhood, and the other kind, which was worse, the hunger of a woman who could work twelve hours and still be nobody, whose name would appear in no book except a relief committee's ledger, entered and ruled off and forgotten.

*That's what she is,* Ellen thought, watching the frozen scum of the canal slide past beneath the towpath rail. *Invisible. Even to herself.*

At Canal Street she found the crowd before she found her own door — thirty or forty turned-out hands gathered in the rain, and Joseph in the middle of them, flushed, gesturing at the shuttered corn mill across the way.

"—sitting there full of grain while our childer go hungry, and we stand here like—"

"Like what?" Ellen pushed through. "Say it, Joseph. Say what we stand here like, and then tell me what smashing windows puts in the babby's bowl."

"It puts fear in *them*, Ellen—"

"It puts constables in *our* streets." She said it loudly enough for the whole crowd, and the crowd, God help them, listened — because she was Brack's sister, because she had stood three and a half hours in the line and come out with her back straight, because there was nothing else yet to listen to. "They turned us out hoping we'd break something. Broken windows are worth money to them, and broken heads are worth a lesson. Every brick we throw gives the magistrate a reason and the *Mercury* a paragraph. We've got one weapon they haven't priced yet, and it isn't stones."

"What then?" someone called.

"Numbers," Ellen said. "And the truth, written down and passed hand to hand, till every soul in this district knows it wasn't us that struck — it was them that locked the door. Let them read about *that* in London. Let the Parliament men read it."

The murmur ran round the crowd like fire in a cotton heap, and Joseph looked at her with an expression she hadn't seen on him since they were children — as if she had grown a head taller in the rain.

Afterward, alone, she let herself admit what she had really done in that crowd: she had promised them words. Printed words. Words that did not exist, from a press whose owner she had never met, whose daughter she had cornered in the sleet and asked to gamble everything.

She had no right. She knew she had no right.

But she had seen the ink under the girl's nails, and she had seen the look beneath it — the look of a woman being offered, for the first time in her life, the chance to matter — and Ellen Brack had spent her whole existence reading machines, and she knew a mechanism ready to turn when she felt one.

Two days later, passing the bridge on an errand for her mother, she saw the shop windows dark, the door shut, and a bill freshly pasted beside the lintel announcing that the premises remained open for lawful business of every description. Lawful. Underlined in the setting, almost, like a man raising his hands.

And beneath the bill, chalked small and crooked on the doorpost where only someone looking would find it, a single figure: a number, and a street.

Miller Street. The relief committee's number.

Ellen stood in the falling dusk with the ice thickening on the canal below and her heart going like a shuttle, and understood that the question had not ended — it had only changed hands.
