## 5. What Father Cut

The tremor came back on a Tuesday, in the middle of setting a funeral bill for the Wesleyan chapel on Great Ancoats Street.

Gussie saw it first in his hands. Her father stood at the composing stone with the word *RESURRECTION* half-built before him, and his right hand — the good one, the one that had set type for thirty years without so much as a crooked letter — began to shiver against the brass rule like a bird deciding whether to fly. The *R* went down true. The *E* went down true. Then the *S* slipped from his fingers and rang against the stone, and he stared at it as though it had spoken to him.

"Father." She crossed the shop without hurrying, because hurrying would make it real.

"I'm well." He said it the way he said everything now: too loudly, too firmly, a man nailing a door shut from the inside. "Cold morning. That's all."

It was not a cold morning. It was the third week of October, mild as milk, and outside the bridge windows the Irwell ran brown and slow under a sky the colour of unbleached calico. She picked up the *S* — long s, they'd need two of them for *RESURRECTION*, the chapel liked the old style — and set it herself, and then the rest of the word, her fingers moving in the dark of the case without looking, the way his had taught hers to move when she was six years old and could barely reach the lower trays.

He watched her do it. She felt him watching, and she knew what he was feeling, because she'd have felt the same: that mixture of pride and grief that has no name in any language she knew. His hands were leaving him. Her hands were what remained.

"The chapel can wait a day," he said. "Mind the shop. I'm going to lie down."

He never lay down at midday. He climbed the stairs to the room above the shop where he'd slept alone these nine years, and Gussie heard the floorboards take his weight, step by careful step, and then the creak of the bed, and then nothing.

The funeral bill sat unfinished on the stone. *RESURRECTION* gleamed in its frame of furniture, waiting for the smaller lines below — the name, the date, the chapel's stern invitation to weep decorously. She looked at it for a long moment. Then, instead of finishing it, she did something she had never done in all her years behind that counter.

She left it.

* * *

There was a drawer in the stone that did not open.

Everyone who worked type knew the anatomy of a composing stone: the galley tray, the furniture rack, the drawers of leads and reglets and quoins. This drawer — low, at the knee, beneath the furniture rack — was swollen shut, painted over so many times it had become part of the wood, a rectangle of shadow in the grain that no one remarked on. Customers didn't see it. Apprentices hadn't stayed long enough to ask. Gussie had noticed it as a child, tugged it once, been told by her father — quickly, more sharply than he ever spoke about anything — *"That's dead wood, Gussie. Leave it be."*

Dead wood. In a house where every stick of timber earned its keep, where even the shavings went into the stove, her father had kept a piece of dead wood at the heart of his trade for thirty years.

Her mother had died in 1833, when Gussie was eleven. What she remembered of Margaret Vane was mostly hands — quick hands, ink-stained at the cuticle in a way Gussie's were stained now — and a voice that sang hymns slightly ahead of the congregation, as though impatient for the verse to arrive. She remembered being lifted onto a stool at the stone and told, *the letters live in their houses, child; you only have to knock.* She remembered her father after the funeral, silent for a month, working double shifts at the press as though he could print his way out of grief. And she remembered that from the week of the burial onward, the drawer had been shut.

Nine years. And today her father's hand had shaken, and he had gone upstairs at midday, and the shop was empty, and the funeral bill waited, and Gussie stood at the stone with her heart going hard against her ribs like a fist on a locked door.

She knelt.

The drawer didn't budge. Paint had welded it to the frame; she worked a quoin knife around the seam, gently, the way you'd ease a splinter from flesh, and felt the blade find the gap and travel. Dust sifted into her lap. Somewhere above her, the floorboards stayed silent. She put both hands on the drawer face and pulled, and with a sound like a tooth coming from a jaw, it opened.

Inside was paper. A great deal of paper, laid flat, protected by sheets of waste newsprint gone amber with age. And on top of the pile, wrapped in a square of oilcloth tied with string, a wooden block the size of a prayer book.

She untied the string with fingers that had suddenly become clumsy.

The block was boxwood, end-grain, polished dark by handling. Carved into its face — cut deep and clean, every line confident as a signature — was an image she knew. Not from memory. From history. From the engravings in the radical papers her father burned but she sometimes rescued from the stove before the flames took them.

A field. A crowd of thousands rendered in strokes fine as hair, banners rising over their heads, a hustler's cart at the centre with small figures upon it. And riding down on the crowd from the right, sabres out, the yeomanry — hussars, their horses' manes flying, cut with such fury that the blades seemed still moving, still falling, frozen at the instant before impact.

Peterloo. St Peter's Field, August 1819. Sixty thousand people come to hear about the vote, and eleven of them dead on the grass, and the name given afterward in mockery turned to iron: *Peterloo*, because the men who rode them down had learned their soldiering against Napoleon and found easier quarry at home.

Gussie turned the block over. On the back, scratched into the wood with a graver's point, in a hand she recognized with a jolt that went through her like cold water — a hand she had seen on the flyleaves of the family Bible, on the labels of her mother's preserve jars, on the note tucked in her school satchel — were words:

*M.V., 1819. They may cut the crowd small, but the cutting is true.*

Her mother. Her mother had cut this. Her mother, who sang ahead of the hymns and scalded jam and died at thirty-four, had stood at a bench somewhere in this city twenty-three years ago and carved the sabres falling with her own hands.

Gussie sat back on her heels on the dusty floor of her father's shop, holding the block in both hands, and felt the ground shift under everything she knew.

* * *

Beneath the block, the papers.

She carried them to the window where the light was best — the same window through which she'd watched the Chartist bills go up on the bridge railings three weeks past, the bills whose phrasing she'd known in her bones before her mind caught up. She unwrapped them one by one and spread them across the stone, and the stone, which had spent thirty years setting other people's words, became for an hour a table of evidence.

Woodcuts, mostly. Proofs pulled on thin paper, some printed in red — dangerous, deliberate, unforgettable. A hand pressing a ballot into a box, cut so the hand was large and the box small, the vote a seed too big for its pot. A mill rendered as a machine that ate children at one end and excreted cloth at the other, the little figures inside it cut with terrible tenderness, each face distinct. A woman standing over a loom with her arms folded, and beneath her, in letters that marched like soldiers: *WE WOVE THE WEALTH. WHERE IS OUR SHARE?*

And pamphlets. Quarto pages, stitched, unsigned or signed with initials that meant nothing to the magistrates and everything to those who knew. *An Address to the Operatives of Manchester, 1819.* *On the Unlawfulness of the Six Acts.* One, its title page torn half away, bore only the surviving words *—TRIAL OF HUNT* and a woodcut of a courtroom in which every juror's face was the same face, repeated, the same smug cut features stamped again and again — a satire so sharp it made Gussie laugh aloud, alone, in the empty shop, a startled bark of sound she hadn't made in weeks.

Her mother had written these. Or set them. Or both. The prose had a voice — plain, furious, precise, the sentences short and load-bearing like bridge girders — and Gussie read a paragraph of the *Address* and heard, unmistakably, the cadence of the woman who used to say grace at speed because God could keep up.

*They tell you that you have no grievance. Count your hours. Count your wages. Count your children. Arithmetic is a kind of speech, and they cannot forbid you to count.*

Arithmetic is a kind of speech. Gussie read the line twice, three times, and something moved in her chest that was not quite tears and not quite laughter — recognition, maybe, the shock of finding your own thought already lived in, already furnished, by someone you had mourned as a stranger.

She had grown up believing the Vanes were neutral. It was the family creed, repeated like grace itself: *we print what's paid for, we take no sides, the press serves whoever pays.* Her father said it to customers, to constables, to himself. It had kept the shop alive through the bad years, through the trials of other men, through the decade when printers went to gaol for opinions and the Vanes' opinions were, officially, none. Neutrality was the wall she had hidden behind all her life — the invisibility she wore like an apron, useful and suffocating in equal measure.

And underneath the wall, literally underneath the stone where the family's bread was earned, her mother had hidden the truth: the Vanes had taken a side in 1819. Had cut it into end-grain boxwood with their own hands. Had printed it in red.

The question that rose in Gussie then was not *why hide it*. She understood why; she'd watched her father burn enough papers to understand fear as a trade skill. The question was: *why keep it?* Why not burn the lot, melt into true neutrality, bury the past clean? Why keep a drawer of dynamite under the hearth for nine years?

Because you cannot burn your own hand's work. Because the blocks were Margaret Vane's hands, cut and dried, and her husband had not been able to destroy them any more than he could have destroyed her grave. So he'd done the next thing: he'd sealed them behind paint and called them dead wood, and kept them the way you keep a wound — closed, but yours.

Gussie gathered the proofs together with great care. At the bottom of the pile she found one item more: a single sheet, unfolded, covered front and back in her mother's handwriting — not print-copy for the compositor but private writing, fast and slanted, the ink faded to the colour of weak tea. A draft. An unfinished thing. The heading read:

*ON THE AGENT BRIGGS, AND HOW THE TURNOUT AT TODMORDEN WAS MADE TO ORDER*

Below it, three paragraphs, ending mid-sentence. *The men who own the mills do not lock their gates in anger; anger is expensive. They employ a man for it, and pay him quarterly, and his name is—*

His name is. And then nothing. The sentence stopped, the page stopped, and Gussie held it and understood she was holding a weapon her mother had forged and never fired — or been prevented from firing; there was no telling which — and that the blank at the end of that line was the loudest thing in the whole shop.

She heard the floorboards overhead. Her father, awake, moving. Coming down.

She had perhaps a minute. Long enough, barely, to choose.

She could repack the drawer, smear the dust, kneel elsewhere, be innocent. The old life. The apron of invisibility, the lowered eyes, the *we print what's paid for*. Five hundred handbills for a stranger with clean cuffs, and say nothing, and say nothing, and say nothing until saying nothing was all she was.

Or she could leave the proofs lying on the stone in the window light, and be standing among them when he came down the stairs, and let him see what she had found, and let whatever happened next happen in the open, between the two of them, for the first time since her mother died.

Above her, the stairs creaked. First step. Second.

Gussie Vane did not move. She stood amid her mother's scattered work with the unfinished page in her hand, and when her father appeared in the doorway — pale, steadying himself on the frame, his trembling hand pressed flat against his chest as if to hold his heart to its post — she met his eyes across the shop and did not look away.

He saw. She watched him see: the proofs, the red ink, the block with its falling sabres, the draft in his dead wife's hand. He closed his eyes briefly, the way a man does before a wave breaks over him, and when he opened them he said, in a voice worn down to the grain:

"You went through the dead wood."

"It wasn't dead," Gussie said. "You knew it wasn't."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Outside, on the bridge, a coal cart rattled past — one of the last, she thought, before the scaffolding came and the road shut and the district became an island. Inside, her father crossed the room slowly, took the unfinished page from her hand, and read the broken line — *his name is—* — with his lips moving, though he knew it by heart. She could tell he knew it by heart.

"He's still here," her father said quietly. "The man whose name she wouldn't print. He's older now. Richer. And this winter—" He stopped. His hand, the good moments of it, closed around the page. "This winter, Gussie, the whole district is going to learn what a lockout is. And when they come asking who will speak for them—"

He looked at the drawer. At the blocks. At her.

"—they'll be asking you."

The bell over the shop door rang. Someone had come in off the bridge — Gussie turned, the proofs still spread behind her like a dealt hand, unable to hide them and no longer certain she wanted to — and in the doorway, hat in hand, cuffs immaculate as ever, stood the stranger with the Manchester accent, smiling as if he'd been expected.

"I hope I'm not interrupting," Nathaniel Quill said. "I've brought you something to read."
