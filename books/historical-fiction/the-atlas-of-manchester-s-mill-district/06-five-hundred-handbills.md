## 6. Five Hundred Handbills

He did not bring a pamphlet after all. He brought a letter — three pages of close, clerkly script, signed by eleven men whose names Gussie half-knew from the relief committee's notices — and he laid it on the composing stone as if it were a specimen of rare type, smoothing the creases with two fingers.

"Read it aloud," her father said. His voice was steady in the way it only was when someone else was watching. "My eyes are not what they were."

Gussie read it. It was a petition, of a sort — not to Parliament this time but to the mill owners themselves, itemising the wages promised and the wages paid, the fines docked for broken threads and dirty frames, the rents taken straight from the pay before it ever reached a working man's hand. The figures were precise. The phrasing was Quill's; she could hear him in every balanced clause, every rhetorical question set down like a stone in a wall. But the grievance in it belonged to the district entire.

"They mean to have five hundred copies," Quill said when she finished. "Struck tonight or tomorrow, before the owners can answer with something sweeter than they've said in person. Paid for in advance." He set a small canvas bag on the stone, and silver shifted inside it with a sound like sleet on a window.

Her father looked at the bag for a long moment. Then at his daughter, who had proofs of their mother's woodcuts spread behind her like evidence.

"Five hundred is nothing," he said at last. "We've run ten thousand bills for the theatre on Deansgate." He paused, and Gussie heard the tremor come back into his voice, faint as a hairline crack in glass. "But a petition against the masters, printed in Ancoats, on the bridge — August, tell me you understand what that is."

"It's a bill," Gussie said. "It's work."

Quill smiled at her, and she looked away first, which she resented.

They struck the bargain in four sentences. Type would be set by Wednesday; the copies collected Thursday, after dark; no names on the bill, no printer's line at the foot, though every magistrate in Manchester could read an imprint like a signature and knew the Vanes' founts the way a shepherd knows his sheep. Her father insisted on that much: *no name.* Quill agreed too easily, and Gussie understood, watching him fold the letter away, that the absence of a name was itself a kind of signature — one that pointed, politely, at them.

When the door had closed behind him and the bell's last shiver died in the rafters, her father stood a while with his hand flat on the stone, as if taking its pulse.

"Your mother printed worse things than that," he said finally, not turning round. "Worse, and better written. Go and see if the coal scuttle needs filling, August."

There was no coal left to fetch; there had not been since November. She went anyway, and stood in the yard in the falling dark, letting the cold press against her like a hand, trying to decide whether what she felt was dread or appetite. It was both. That was the trouble. It was always going to be both.

* * *

She set the type herself.

Her father wanted to do it — she saw him reach for the case twice on Tuesday, saw the palsy catch his wrist halfway, saw him pretend he'd been reaching for something else — and on Wednesday morning she simply had the stick composed and locked in the chase before he came downstairs, standing over it like a cook guarding a risen dough.

"You might have let me check your spacing," he said, hurt in it somewhere under the gruffness.

"Check it now, then."

He bent to the forme, reading the reversed letters with the fluency of forty years, and Gussie watched his lips move and waited for correction. It came slowly.

"The leading's tight in the third paragraph," he said. "And your em-quad after *Ancoats* has crept out of line. Otherwise—" He stopped. Straightened. Looked at her with an expression she couldn't file anywhere. "Otherwise it's clean work. Better than mine was at nineteen."

She locked up the forme and did not say thank you, because neither of them could have survived her saying it.

The press was the old Columbian, black as a beetle, older than she was, and she worked it alone that night because her father's hands could not be trusted with the tympan and he knew it and hated knowing it. She lit two candles and one lamp, shuttered the window that faced the bridge, and began.

*TO THE MILL OWNERS OF MANCHESTER*, the first impression said, coming up grey because she hadn't inked the rollers evenly, and she pulled that sheet and the next and burned them in the stove rather than waste a thought on them. By the fourth pull the ink lay true, black and even, and the words stood off the page with the authority of something already true — which, she thought, pulling sheet after sheet into the growing stack, was the whole secret of printing, wasn't it. Ink made a thing real. Not the fact. The fact could be shouted down a cellar grate and forgotten by morning. But a thousand identical sheets, all agreeing, all in the same upright letters — that was a chorus, and choruses frightened people who lived by the solo voice.

Five hundred. She counted in dozens, then in reams of fifty, laying them flat under boards to keep the edges square. The stove ticked. Outside, the river muttered under its new lid of ice, and once — far off, toward the mills — a dog barked itself hoarse at something invisible, and stopped.

Somewhere around the three hundredth sheet her arms ached and her mind went strange and clear, and she found herself thinking about the drawer. About the blocks. *No more chains, no more masters.* Her mother had cut those lines into pearwood thirty years ago with the same hands that had — what? Held her, fed her, died. Gussie had been nine. She remembered the funeral and almost nothing else, and now she owned a drawer full of her mother's anger and had never once been told it existed.

*They'll be asking you,* her father had said.

Well. Someone had asked. And here she was, at midnight, inking her mother's press with her mother's fury, printing other people's grievances because her own had nowhere else to live.

She pulled the last sheet at a quarter past three, wiped the forme, distributed the type back into its case — each letter home to its compartment, the whole argument dismantled into silence — and sat down on the stool amid five hundred sheets of sedition and felt, overwhelmingly, nothing but tired.

Then she heard the knock.

Three raps, spaced, deliberate, at the yard door — the door that faced the alley, not the bridge. Gussie blew out the candles first, instinct outrunning thought, then crossed the dark shop and put her eye to the crack.

A woman stood in the snow with two empty shawls over her arm. Even in the lamplight spilling through the gap Gussie knew the breadth of her shoulders, the way she stood with her weight even on both feet, planted, a woman built for standing in queues without flinching.

She opened the door six inches. Cold poured in like water.

"You're Brack," she said. "Frame-tenter, second mill. You came in for the funeral bills."

"Aye." Ellen Brack's breath smoked between them. Her eyes went past Gussie's shoulder into the dark shop, reading it the way her father read a forme. "And you're Vane's lass, though I'll not say which one, seeing as there's only ever been the one." A pause. "Is it done?"

Gussie should have said done with what. She heard herself say, "How are you carrying them?"

"In what God gave me." Brack lifted one shawl. "Folded small, against the body. Two trips. There's women walking behind me at intervals, ordinary as going to chapel, so if anyone asks I'm carrying bread."

It was absurd and it was flawless and Gussie felt laughter rise in her throat, dangerous, giddy, and pressed it down. "Wait here."

She counted out two hundred and fifty, folded them into packets of twenty-five, tied them with string, and passed them through the doorway into arms that took the weight without comment. Brack stowed them under the first shawl, against her chest, and became instantly, completely, a woman carrying washing or bread or anything at all. Gussie stared. She had grown up believing print made things invisible — turned living speech into dead, portable marks. But Brack made paper disappear entirely, folded it into the ordinary until it weighed nothing.

"Second trip at four," Brack said. "Same knock. And missus—" She hesitated, and for a second the queue-standing steadiness flickered and something rawer showed underneath. "I know what this costs. We know. It's not charity, whatever you tell yourself to sleep. It's the first thing anybody's done for us that wasn't a sermon."

She went. Gussie closed the door and stood in the cold she'd let in, her heart going hard and fast, telling herself — actually forming the words in her head, arranging them like type — *it's charity, we're paid, it's a job like any other.*

She did not believe a word of it. The lie wouldn't justify. It sat in the stick crooked, and no amount of planing would bring it level.

* * *

The second collection went at four o'clock precisely. At half past, wrapping the last of the silver in oilcloth to return to Quill — he'd overpaid, deliberately, she was sure, another small hook — she saw the light.

Not a lantern. A pipe-bowl glow, low and orange, at the mouth of the alley where it met the bridge road. It glowed, dimmed, glowed again. Somebody was standing in the snow smoking, in weather that froze a man's whiskers stiff in ten minutes, and nobody stood in that weather for pleasure.

Gussie pinched out the lamp and moved along the wall to the window slit.

A constable. Municipal coat, the tall hat, the truncheon hanging like a third arm. Young, by the way he kept shifting his weight, stamping one boot then the other. He wasn't looking at the shop. He was looking at the bridge — or pretending to — in the manner of a man paid to look at one thing while attending to another.

She watched him for a quarter of an hour until the cold drove him off up the road toward the mill gates, unhurried, a man with nowhere particular to be. Then she sat down in the dark among the empty boards and the smell of ink and tried to think.

It meant nothing. Constables walked rounds. The district was full of idle men and smouldering tempers; of course they posted a man near the bridge, near the mills, everywhere. It meant nothing.

Except that the Vanes' shop was the only lit window on the lane past midnight, and lit windows got remembered, and remembered windows got named, and named windows got visited by gentlemen with warrants who asked cheerful questions like *who does your printing, Mrs Vane, and for whom?*

Invisibility was a craft, like setting type. You maintained it daily, impression by impression: the shutters closed at nine, the bills printed only for the respectable, the opinions kept in the drawer with the woodcuts. She had held that craft together for nineteen years without knowing she was holding anything.

Now there were five hundred sheets of her work walking through the district under women's shawls, and a constable smoking outside her door, and a stranger's silver in her apron pocket, and her mother's anger in her hands.

You could distribute a forme back into the case and the argument vanished, letter by letter, into silence. But there was no case big enough to put the last three days back into. The type was out. It was walking around Ancoats in other people's clothes, and it would not come home.

* * *

Thursday evening her father came down late, moving carefully, and found her cleaning the press. He watched her a while from the bottom step.

"The constable," he said. "I saw him from the bedroom. Twice this week, August. Once is weather. Twice is employment."

"I know."

"Do you." He came into the room properly, and she braced for the sermon — the legacy, the name, the fragile edifice of forty years' respectability that a man builds by never once giving the magistrates cause — but what he said instead was: "Was it worth it?"

She dried her hands. Chose truth, for once, because lying to a compositor is wasted labour; they can read any face at arm's length.

"When the first good pull came off," she said, "the words stood up off the page and they weren't yours and they weren't Quill's. They were mine. My spacing. My leading. My hands. Nobody has ever—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I've been invisible my whole life, Father. Invisible and competent. Last night I was visible for four hours and terrified the whole time and I have never in my life been less sorry."

Her father looked at her for a long moment. The palsy moved in his cheek like something swimming under ice.

"Your mother said almost exactly that," he said, "in almost exactly those words, the night before they came for her blocks the first time." He turned away, busying himself pointlessly with a pile of clean sheets. "There's a man been asking after us at the Roebuck. Asking what the Vanes will print, and for how long, and who holds the purse. Not Quill — a bigger fish, they say. A committee man. If he calls, August—"

"If he calls?"

Her father didn't answer. He climbed the stairs slowly, one hand on the rail, and left her alone with the press, the smell of ink, and the sound of the river groaning under its ice — long, shifting, restless, like something enormous turning over in its sleep beneath the bridge.

Outside, at the mouth of the alley, a pipe-bowl glowed once in the dark, and went out.
