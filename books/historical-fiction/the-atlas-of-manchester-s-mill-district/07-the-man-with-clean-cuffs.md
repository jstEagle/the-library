## 7. The Man with Clean Cuffs

He came back on a Thursday, when the fog off the Irwell had thickened to the colour of dishwater and the gas had to be lit at three in the afternoon.

Gussie saw him first through the shop window — that walk she would have known anywhere now, quick and precise, a man who crossed streets as if they were ledgers to be balanced. The fog swallowed his legs and left only the torso moving, and then he was pushing open the door with its bell of cracked brass, and the cold came in behind him like an unpaid debt.

"Mr. Vane." He doffed his hat. Snowmelt beaded on the brim; his cuffs, when he raised his arm, were still immaculate. "You'll recall me. Quill. I had five hundred handbills of you in November."

"I recall the silver," her father said, not looking up from the stone where he was distributing type with hands that shook less in the mornings, more by evening. It was past four. Gussie watched the tremor come into his fingers and go out again, watched him fight it the way a man fights sleep on a night coach.

"And I've come about the quality," Quill said. "It was very good work."

"We do good work for everyone who pays."

"So I've heard." Quill smiled — a small, dry smile, the smile of a man who has already counted the room. His eyes went over the shop the way Gussie's went over a page: the two presses, the elder one her grandfather's, its iron ribs furred with dust; the cases ranged along the wall, California job case above, lower cases below, every compartment labelled in her father's cramped copperplate; the composing stone worn into a shallow valley by sixty years of elbows. Then his eyes found hers, and stayed a half-second longer than was comfortable, or perhaps exactly as long as was intended.

"You'll want the back room," her father said heavily. "Gussie, mind the counter."

But there was no one at the counter. There had been hardly anyone at the counter since the mills turned out their hands, since the bridge closed and the district became an island with a print shop at its centre like a heart nobody had asked for. The men who came in now came in slowly, hats in hand, asking after notices — relief committee meetings, chapel hours, whether Mr. Vane could run off fifty copies of a petition for sixpence and could the sixpence wait until spring.

Gussie minded the counter anyway, and listened.

She heard Quill lay something on the back-room table — paper, several sheets, the particular whisper of a folded quarto. She heard her father's chair creak under him.

"A weekly," Quill said. "Eight pages. Set and printed here, distributed through the strike committee. We'd want it named plainly — *The Bridge*, we thought, given where you sit."

"No."

The word came so fast it seemed to have been waiting by the door all along. Gussie's hand stopped over the type case she was dusting.

"Mr. Vane—"

"You know what I am, Mr. Quill? I'm a man who prints wedding invitations and bills of sale and the Ancoats chapel calendar, and I have done for thirty years without once seeing the inside of a magistrate's room. My wife is dead. my daughter is unmarried. My hands—" he paused, and Gussie heard him admit it, quietly, as if confessing to a debt, "—my hands are not what they were. You are asking me to hang a sign over this door saying *sedition printed here*."

"I'm asking you to print the truth about a lockout."

"The truth is a broadsheet, sir, and the magistrate reads every column of it."

There was a silence. In it Gussie heard the river under the bridge, the ice ticking against the piers like a clock counting toward something.

"Four thousand people," Quill said then, and his voice changed — lost its clerk's polish, gained something rawer underneath. "Four thousand with no wages since the week before Christmas. I stood in the relief queue yesterday behind a woman with three children and a shawl that wouldn't close. The owners' agent tells the papers it's a rest, a holiday, that the hands will return when they're reasonable. Reasonable." A short laugh with no humour in it. "Who speaks for them, Mr. Vane? The *Guardian* prints what the mill owners pay it to print. The chapels pray for them, which is kind and costs nothing. Somebody must set down, in plain type, week after week: this is what is being done, and this is who is doing it, and these are the names."

"Names get men transported."

"Silence gets them starved slower."

Gussie heard her father rise. The chair scraped. When he spoke again his voice had gone thin and high, the voice he used when the tremor was worst and he was most afraid of being seen afraid.

"Take your papers and go home, Mr. Quill. Tell your committee the Vanes are printers, not martyrs. There's a difference, and it's measured in hanging rope."

Footsteps. Quill came out into the shop with the folder under his arm, and for a moment the three of them stood in the gaslight among the smells of ink and oil and coal smoke, none of them looking quite at any other.

Then Quill put the folder down on the counter. Not offering it. Just setting it there, deliberately, the way a man sets down a card.

"For when you change your minds," he said. "Committee meets Sundays, the old weavers' hall off Redhill Street. Anyone may come. That's rather the point of us."

And he looked at Gussie — fully, openly, the look she'd caught the half-second of before, now held steady — and touched his hat brim, and went out into the fog.

* * *

Her father did not touch the folder for two days.

It sat on the counter like a live thing, and both of them stepped around it, and neither of them moved it, and on the second evening Gussie found her father standing over it with his reading glasses pushed up on his forehead, looking down at it as a man looks at a grave he dug himself.

"You should burn it," she said.

"I know what I should do." He didn't turn. "Your mother used to say I knew what I should do and did the other thing. She said it fondly, mostly."

Gussie kept her voice level. "Did she?"

"She did." Now he turned, and took the glasses off, and folded them away into his waistcoat with great care, the way he did everything lately, each motion rationed like bread. "Gussie. Sit down a moment."

She sat on the customer's stool. He lowered himself into his own chair behind the stone, and for a while he said nothing, and she let him have the silence because she knew how much a silence cost a proud man.

"When your mother died," he said at last, "she made me promise something. Not the usual things. She made me promise to keep you ordinary."

"Ordinary."

"Safe. Small. A printer's daughter who marries a printer's son and lives above a shop and never once stands in front of anything moving fast." He rubbed his trembling hand with the steady one, absently, as though it were another man's. "I promised her because she was dying and because I believed her. She'd seen what happens. She was at Peterloo, Gussie. Sixteen years old, on her father's shoulders, and she carried it her whole life in her — not the fear. The anger. And I thought, better she be angry alone than my girl be angry too. So I kept you at the cases. Kept you setting other people's words. I told myself it was training. It was a muzzle, and I built it myself."

The gas hissed. Somewhere below, the ice shifted and groaned along the pier foundations, a long animal sound.

"Why are you telling me this?" Gussie asked.

"Because that man will come back. Men like that always come back. And when he does—" her father looked at her, and his eyes in the gaslight were wet and fierce and terribly clear, "—I need to know which of us he's come back for."

* * *

Quill returned on Sunday night, after the chapel bells, when the fog had frozen into rime on the windowpanes and the shop was lit by one candle because coal, too, was dearer on an island.

He did not knock properly; he tapped twice, softly, the tap of a man who has learned doors can be listened behind. Her father was already abed — Gussie heard his tread cross the room overhead and stop, and knew he was lying awake listening too. She opened the door herself.

Quill stood in the blue dark with snow on his shoulders, and this time his cuffs were not clean. They were smudged grey to the wrist, printer's grey, and Gussie understood before he said a word that he had been somewhere with a press tonight — some cellar, some chapel vestry — running off handbills on borrowed type with borrowed hands, badly.

"They smashed our press," he said, before hello. "The one in Hulme. Constables found it inside a day. A boy of seventeen arrested, and his father's a shoemaker with no trade to lose but the boy himself." He stamped the snow from his boots without entering. "I shouldn't have come. I told myself I wouldn't. But the committee meets tomorrow and we have nothing to give them but resolutions, and resolutions don't feed anyone, Miss Vane. Paper does. Paper in ten thousand hands does."

"My father said no."

"I heard what your father said." He glanced up at the ceiling, at the shape of the listening house. "I'm asking someone else."

The cold came through the doorway and found her throat and she let it, and in the space it opened she heard herself say — not deciding to say it, discovering it already said, the way type discovers itself in the stick when the compositor's hands know the copy better than his head does:

"Eight pages is too many for a first number. Make it four. Four pages, weekly, set at night, printed between midnight and four when the constables change shift at the bridge. Distribution isn't mine to promise — that's your problem. Composition and presswork are mine."

Quill stared at her.

"You'd do it without your father's leave?"

"My father taught me the trade. He didn't ask my leave for thirty years of keeping me at the small jobs." She heard her own voice and marvelled at it — level, unhurried, a stranger's voice, a voice with authority in it like a coin sewn into a hem. "One condition. Nothing goes to press that I haven't read and set myself. No names invented, no numbers exaggerated. If we print a lie, we die for a lie, and worse — we deserve to. Truth or nothing. That's the price."

"That's a strange price for a criminal."

"It's the only price for a printer."

For a moment he simply looked at her, and the candle behind her threw her shadow out into the street between them, long and dark across the snow, and she thought absurdly that it was the first time her shadow had ever reached further than her own doorstep.

"Miss Vane," Quill said, and then stopped, and started again, and whatever the middle sentence had been going to be he buried it. "The committee will want a name for the imprint. Papers need a printer named. It's how the law finds you, but it's also how the readers trust you. Anonymous sheets read like ghosts. Named sheets read like witnesses."

"Gussie Vane," she said. "Print it plain."

"Plainly." Something moved through his face, quick as the tremor in her father's hand — excitement, or fear, or the place where the two share a border. He pulled off his glove and held out his hand bare, and she took it, and his grip was cold and hard and honest as a type-high piece of steel.

"First copy Monday fortnight," he said. "Copy delivered Sundays, after dark. I'll bring it myself."

"You'll bring it in disguise. Clean cuffs are a mark against a man in this district just now."

He laughed — startled out of him, real — and pulled the glove back on, and touched his hat, and was gone into the frozen fog. She watched until the dark took him, and then she shut the door and stood with her back against it and felt her own heart going like the press at full tilt, platen falling, frisket lifting, sheet after sheet after sheet.

Above her, the floorboards creaked. Her father, awake. Had he heard?

She waited, counting. Ten. Twenty. Thirty.

The boards creaked again — and then she heard, unmistakable even through the floor, the sound she had grown up beneath all her life and never once thought about: the sound of her father getting out of bed in the dark, crossing to the drawer where he kept the bankbook, and opening it.

Not to stop her, she realized. To count what they had. To learn how long a press could run before the money ran out.

She climbed the stairs past his door without stopping, though her name was almost spoken behind it — she heard the breath gather, and let it go unspent, because some things once said cannot be unset, like type locked in a forme, like a name printed plain on the last page of a forbidden sheet.

In her room she lit no candle. She sat in the dark by the window with the rime ferning the glass, and below her the black river carried its ice toward the sea, and she practised in her head, line by line, the first leader of the first number: *To the working people of Ancoats, this paper is addressed...*

Monday fortnight. Twelve days. She had twelve days to become, in secret, in the dark, in her own shop, the person she had spent twenty-three years pretending not to be.

Somewhere out in the island, in a cellar she'd never seen, a boy of seventeen sat in a cell for the crime of a handbill. She pressed her palm flat against the cold glass, ink-stained even now, always, and made him a promise in the only language she trusted:

We will set you in type. And type, unlike boys, cannot be arrested.
