## 8. Ice on the Irwell

The river froze on a Tuesday, and by Friday Ellen Brack had learned to hate it.

She stood at her window in the cellar dwelling on Canal Street, watching the Irwell go still beneath a skin of grey ice, and thought: *that's our coffin lid.* Not because the cold would kill them — though it might — but because frozen water meant frozen trade, meant no barges, no coal, no work for the few men who'd found piece-labour hauling on the wharves. The mills had been shut four weeks. The lockout was no longer an event. It was a season.

Behind her, in the one room that held all seven of them — herself, her brother Joseph, his wife Martha, their three children, and the damp that never left the walls — the morning was arranging itself into its new shape. No factory bell to rise for. Instead: the queue at the relief committee's cellar on Great Ancoats Street, where you stood two hours in the cold for a loaf, a quarter-stone of oatmeal, and the particular humiliation of having your need weighed by men in good coats who wrote your name in a ledger as though hunger were a debt.

"We've nowt for the fire but what's left o' the chair," Joseph said from the hearth. He said it lightly, the way he said most hard things, snapping off the last leg and feeding it to the flames.

"Then we'll stand," Ellen said.

Martha laughed — a short, cracked sound with nothing in it. "We'll stand at that committee till doomsday, and they'll weigh us like pig-iron."

"The baby's coughing again," Joseph said, and the lightness dropped out of his voice all at once, the way a stone drops through thin ice.

Ellen turned. Little Sam, not two years old, lay in the drawer-bed between his sisters, his breath coming quick and whistling. His cheeks were flushed too high, too bright, the fever-colour she'd seen on other children in other winters and had always been able to walk away from, because they were somebody else's children in somebody else's cellar.

Not this winter. This winter the whole district was one big cellar, and everybody in it was somebody else's child.

* * *

She went out before eight, wrapping herself in everything she owned, and joined the queue.

It moved the way queues move when nobody has anywhere else to be — slow, shuffling, patient in the way of cattle. Around her the talk was low and constant, a kind of communal breathing. Wages. Turn-outs. The masters' agent, Mr. Prowse, who had gone through the districts in October telling hands that if they wouldn't take the reduction there were plenty who would, and then — when the mills stopped anyway — told the newspapers the men had struck for the Charter, wild-eyed revolutionaries, when half of them couldn't have spelled the word.

"They're saying at the chapel," the woman behind her said — Mrs. Ogden, whose husband had been a piecer at Burton's until the turn-out — "that London's taken up a subscription for us."

"And how much of it'll get past the committee's fingers?"

"That's God's business, isn't it."

"That's arithmetic," Ellen said, and Mrs. Ogden snorted, which was as close to agreement as either of them could afford.

By the time she reached the table — a plank across two barrels, a clerk behind it with ink-blackened fingers and a face like a closed ledger — the cold had got into her knees and wouldn't leave. She gave her name. The clerk wrote it. He looked at her over his spectacles the way a man looks at goods he's pricing.

"Brack. Ellen. Widow?" 

"Sister of Joseph Brack. He's living."

"Not working, though." It wasn't a question. Nothing about any of them was a question anymore; they were all entries in the same column.

She took the loaf and the oatmeal and the ticket for coal that might or might not be honoured at the yard, and she walked back along the canal path, and that was where she saw the shop.

* * *

Vane's print shop sat on the bridge itself, the only building on the only road out of the district, its upper windows leaning over the frozen river like a man peering down a well. In daylight it looked exactly what it was: a small, respectable trade, gold letters going dull on the glass, the smell of ink and oil reaching even into the street. Ellen had passed it a thousand times without thinking of it at all.

She thought of it now constantly.

Because three weeks ago she had walked in off the street, straight up to the counter where a young woman was sorting type with quick, sure, left-handed movements, and asked — plainly, because dignity had left her nothing but plainness — whether the Vanes would print for the workers. And the young woman had looked at her for a long moment with eyes that gave away nothing and everything, and had said only, "Leave the copy tonight. Under the door."

And the handbills had come. Five hundred of them, printed clean and true, calling the hands to hold together, naming the reduction for what it was. Ellen had carried them away wrapped in her shawl with her heart going like a mill-engine, and they had been read aloud in cellars and chapels and beer-shops until the words were worn smooth as pebbles.

Nobody knew who had set them. Nobody except Ellen, and the young woman with the ink under her nails, and whatever silence lived between them.

She stopped across the road now, snow gathering on her shoulders, and looked at the lit window. Inside, a figure bent over the composing stone. Even at this distance she knew the stance — the slight forward lean, the left hand moving faster than the right ever bothered to.

*You don't know her name,* Ellen reminded herself. *You know her hands.*

But hands were enough. Hands were all anybody had to give this winter.

* * *

Sam's fever climbed through the afternoon.

Martha did everything Martha knew to do: cool cloths, broth he wouldn't take, the child propped upright against her chest so the phlegm could find its way somewhere that wasn't his lungs. The two girls, four and six, sat silent in the corner with their dolls made of rags, understanding with the terrible instinct of children that quiet was wanted and providing it.

By dusk the doctor came — not because they could pay him, but because the relief committee kept a surgeon on retainer for the dwellings, and the surgeon came round with his bag and his tired eyes and did what he could, which was nothing much. He listened to Sam's chest. He wrote something on a paper. He said the word *congestion* the way other men said *weather*.

"Keep him warm," he said, standing to go, and Joseph laughed — one bark, ugly.

"Warm. Aye. We'll burn the house down, doctor, that should warm him."

The doctor paused at the door. He was not a bad man; that was the worst of it. Badness you could fight.

"There's fever in the next court," he said quietly. "Three families. If it comes here—" He didn't finish it. He put on his hat and went down into the dark, to the next cellar, and the next.

That night Ellen sat up with Sam while Martha slept beside him, exhausted, and Joseph paced the seven feet of floor that pacing allowed. Outside, the wind came off the frozen river with a sound like something vast turning over in its sleep.

"He'll be better by morning," Joseph said, for the fourth time.

"Aye," Ellen said, for the fourth time, and neither of them believed it, and both of them knew the other didn't, and that shared disbelief was somehow the only warm thing in the room.

* * *

Sam died an hour before dawn.

It was very quick at the end — a long rattling breath, a smaller one, a stillness that seemed almost gentle after the struggle of it, as though the child had simply stepped out of a crowded room. Martha made a sound Ellen had never heard a human being make and hoped never to hear again, low and continuous, like the earth itself grieving. Joseph knelt by the drawer-bed with his head bowed, and his shoulders shook, and he did not make any sound at all, which frightened Ellen more than the noise.

What Ellen felt, sitting rigid in the corner while the parents broke open around her, was not grief first. Grief came. But first — first, hot and immediate, before sorrow could organise itself — was rage. Clean, total, directional rage, aimed with perfect precision at every master in Manchester, every magistrate, every clerk with his ledger and his weighing of need, every man in a good coat who had eaten that night.

*Aye,* she thought, holding the rage like a coal in her cupped hands. *Now we're getting somewhere honest.*

Then, underneath it, quieter, colder: *He died of damp and hunger and a winter with no wages. Nobody smashed a window for him. Nobody will.*

And that thought — the futility of it, the terrible arithmetic of a death that changed nothing — frightened her more than the rage did. Because rage without effect doesn't burn out. It turns inward. It looks for something to break, and if the right thing is out of reach, it takes the near thing instead.

She saw it happen to Joseph within two days.

* * *

They buried Sam on the Saturday — a pauper's burial, the parish cart, six little coffins that week alone from the Canal Street dwellings — and Joseph did not cry at the graveside, and that was wrong, everyone knew it was wrong, a man ought to cry at his own son's grave, but Joseph stood dry-eyed and rigid with his hat crushed in his fists, and Ellen watched the grief compress inside him like steam in a boiler with the valve soldered shut.

On Sunday evening he came to her where she sat mending by the candle, and he had a strange brightness about him, a purposeful lightness, and she knew before he spoke that she wasn't going to like what he said.

"I've been thinking," he said.

"Don't hurt yourself."

He didn't smile. That was wrong too; banter was their currency, and he'd gone off it entirely. "There's talk going round. At the George, and down the courts. That Vane's shop — the printer's on the bridge. That they've been printing for the masters' side. Handbills against the strike. Scab-work."

Ellen's needle stopped.

"Who says?"

"Men say." He shrugged, elaborate, careless. "A shop sits there fat and safe on the only bridge out, taking money off both sides while we starve. Somebody said — and I don't say it was me, Ellen, I'm only telling thee what's said — somebody said the windows want breaking. Traitors' property. Let 'em feel a bit of what we feel."

The candle flame leaned in the draught and steadied.

"Joseph." She kept her voice level. "Thou knows who printed the handbills. Our handbills. The ones thou read out at the George till thy voice went."

"I know what men say."

"Men said the turn-out would be over in a fortnight, an' all." She put down the mending. "Listen to me. Whoever set those bills risked the rope. Seditious libel — they hang for less, or transport. Thou thinks a master's man risks that? For us?"

"Maybe it were a trap," he said, but the certainty had gone out of it, replaced by something sullen and hungry. "Maybe it were bait to make fools of us. Anyhow—" and here it came, the real thing, rising up through the grief like gas through marsh water— "anyhow, what's it matter? Sam's dead. Four thousand of us are starving, and the masters sit by their fires, and the magistrates send dragoons when we so much as look sideways, and thou wants me to care whether some printer's windows crack? They're glass, Ellen. Glass mends. My lad doesn't."

The room was very small and very quiet. Martha had taken the girls into the alcove. The wind worried at the ill-fitting shutter.

"No," Ellen said. "It doesn't mend. And I know better than most what it is to want summat broken."

He looked at her then, really looked, and for a moment the boiler-pressure showed through — the naked, scalding thing underneath — and she understood that she was looking at the movement's own soul in miniature. All that fury, earned honestly, with nowhere honest to go. Pointed outward, it was a weapon. Pointed inward, at the shop that had helped them, at the chapel that counselled patience, at each other — it was a knife with no handle.

"So what does thee want?" he said. "Patience? Prayer? Another month at that committee while they weigh us like pig-iron?"

"I want the handbills again," Ellen said. "I want a paper. I want our names in print and our case in print, week after week, till Manchester can't look away. And I want it printed by whoever's willing to risk their neck doing it — and I'll tell thee now, brother, it's nobody at the George talking brave over small beer. There's one shop on that bridge with ink on its conscience already, and if we smash it, we smash the only press in Ancoats that's ever been ours."

He stared at her. "Ours? It's a tradesman's shop. Vane's. Old man Vane's."

"It's ours," Ellen said, "because we paid for it. In risk. In trust. And if thee breaks those windows, thee tells every hand in this district that we can't protect what's ours — that we eat our own the minute we're hurting. Is that the solidarity thou's been shouting about at the George? Because I've heard thee shout it, Joseph. I've heard thee shout *united we stand* till the glasses shook."

He turned away. Stood with his back to her, fists opening and closing.

"If it comes to a crowd," he said finally, without turning, "I can't answer for it. Men are past reasoning with. Sam's dead, and they're past reasoning with."

"Then somebody'd best get ahead of the crowd," Ellen said, "and give it summat better to do than break glass."

* * *

She went out that same night, though the streets were black and the cold had teeth in it.

The bridge was deserted, the river below locked in ice that gleamed faintly under a torn sky. Snow squeaked under her clogs. The shop was dark, all but one lamp glowing low behind the ground-floor glass, and she stood across the way a long moment, gathering what needed gathering.

Then she crossed, and took the pencil stub from her pocket, and on the back of the coal-ticket the clerk had given her — fitting, that; the committee's own paper — she wrote in her blunt, unlettered-looking hand, pressing hard:

*They know. The press is ours. Keep it safe till we need it. — A friend*

She folded it once and slid it under the door, into the dark where the type cases waited, and where the young woman with the ink under her nails would find it come morning.

Then she stood back and looked up at the dark windows, at the bridge, at the frozen river running silver and dead beneath it, and she understood — with a clarity that felt almost like fear — that the district had become a siege, and the shop had become a garrison, and the anger out there in the courts and beer-shops was a flood with no channel yet cut for it.

Give it a channel, she thought. Give it a name in print, a page, a voice. Or watch it turn on everything near it, starting with the one place that had dared to help.

Inside the shop, faintly, a lamp moved.

Ellen pulled her shawl tight and walked home through the snow, and behind her, above the black ice, the single light burned on — and she found herself wondering, with the strange tenderness the weary feel toward the brave, just how many nights it had burned like that already, and what in God's name they were setting in there that took so long, and whether either of them would live to see it finished.

She did not yet know that the answer to the third question was being written, page by page, in a cellar by candlelight — and that the whole district would soon be reading it.
