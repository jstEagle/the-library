## 9. The First Number

The type was cold before dawn.

That was the thing nobody told you about lead — that it held the temperature of the room it slept in, and the cellar where her mother's cases had waited twenty years for a hand to lift their lids was colder than any grave Gussie had ever stood beside. She had carried them up in three trips through the trapdoor behind the press, wiping each case with a rag before she dared look into it properly, as though twenty years of dust were a shroud and she ought to be gentle about pulling it back.

Her mother's type. Not cast new — no foundry in Manchester would sell to a woman, and her father had never had the silver — but bought secondhand from a jobbing printer off Deansgate who was emigrating to America and selling his shop by the pound-weight. Her father had carried it home on a barrow the winter after Peterloo, Gussie later worked out, which meant she had been an infant or not yet born, and her mother had set pamphlets with these very letters while Gussie slept in a drawer lined with sacking beneath the composing stone.

She ran her thumb along the compartments now. *A* in its great box, *E* fullest of all, the little sorts worn shiny at the shoulders from a dead woman's fingers. The face was old-fashioned, a bit heavy in the serifs, nothing like the crisp modern faces her father ordered from the type-founders for respectable work. It looked like what it was: printing from another age of anger, come out of storage.

"You'll want to justify your lines tighter than you're used to," her father said from the doorway. He hadn't slept. She could tell by the way he held his left arm against his ribs, the way he did when the tremor had kept him awake. "That face runs wide. And mind the *w*s — half of them are cracked at the base and they'll pinch if you crowd them."

He had come down at four o'clock, she realized. He had been standing in the dark watching her work, saying nothing, for how long?

"I know about the *w*s," she said. "I've set two pages with them already."

"I know." He came forward into the candlelight and looked at the forme laid out on the stone — page one of something that had never existed before, four columns, a masthead she had cut herself from a block of pear wood because they could not afford an engraver and she had not wanted one anyway. THE BRIDGE, in letters she had drawn freehand and then hated and then redrawn eleven times until they stopped looking like anyone else's letters and started looking like hers. Beneath it, the motto: *We are many; we are here.*

"Your mother's phrase," he said quietly.

"I know." She hadn't known until it was out of her pen and onto the block, and then she had known instantly, the way you know a voice through a wall. "It's in the drawer lining. Under the sacking. She wrote it on a scrap and never printed it."

"She was saving it." He touched the edge of the forme, not quite touching the type. "For something worth saying."

The press took the first pull at ten minutes past five.

Gussie had inked a thousand formes in her life, but never one like this — never one where every word had come out of her own head and through her own hands, where if a sentence was crooked it was crooked because she had written it crooked, and if it rang true it rang because she had struck it true. The blanket came down over the paper. She leaned her weight on the bar. The screw turned, the platen pressed, and somewhere under her palms a page became real.

She pulled it up and held it to the candle.

The ink was still wet enough to shine. THE BRIDGE. No. 1. Price one penny, given free to the locked-out. And below, in her mother's heavy old-fashioned letters, the words she had written at midnight three nights ago while her father slept and the frost climbed the cellar glass:

*Four thousand hands are idle in Ancoats this week, and every one of them is idle because a handful of men decided they should be. This paper exists to say so, plainly, every week, until the mills open or the truth does. We will print what the relief committee counts and what the magistrates won't read aloud. We will print the names of those who give and those who refuse. We will print what happens on this island, because we live on it too, and there is no other paper that will.*

*— The Printer.*

She had argued with herself for two days over that last line. *The Printer.* Not Vane. Not even a false name, really — just the truth wearing a plain coat. Her father had read it and said nothing, which from him was a kind of blessing, and then had gone upstairs and come back down with the good rag paper, the stock he saved for wedding invitations, and put it beside the cheap stuff without a word.

They printed four hundred copies between five o'clock and noon. Her father worked the press and she worked the stone, setting page two while page one ran, and neither of them spoke much except about leads and quoins and the weather of the ink, which was the language they had always spoken best. Twice her father stopped, flexed his right hand, and watched it shake, and twice Gussie took the brayer from him without comment and inked the forme herself and gave it back when the tremor passed. They were past the age of pretending, the two of them. The lockout had burned that off like fog.

By noon the copies lay stacked in bundles of fifty, wrapped in waste paper, smelling of oil and lampblack and — she thought, though she knew it was nonsense — of 1819. Of her mother's year. Of a winter when someone else had stood at this same stone and set words that frightened men.

"They'll hang you for less," her father said, looking at the stack. He said it without drama, the way he'd say *the ink's gone thick.* "You understand that."

"I understand it."

"And you're doing it anyway."

"Yes."

He nodded once, slowly, and went up to open the shop, because the shutters had to come down regardless of revolutions, and customers mustn't smell anything but lampblack and business.

* * *

Ellen Brack came at dusk, with three mill girls and a chapel deacon named Mr. Ogden who smelled of tallow candles and looked like a man who had spent thirty years being disappointed by the Church of England and had made his peace with it.

They came through the back, off the yard, in ones and twos, the way Ellen had arranged it — Gussie didn't ask how she'd arranged it, only noted that a woman who'd spent twelve years in a spinning room knew how to move four people across a yard without a soul seeing. Ellen's face was thinner than it had been in October. There was a bruise-yellow shadow under one eye she didn't explain and Gussie didn't ask about. Hunger sat on all four of them like a coat they'd stopped noticing they wore.

"Is it done?" Ellen said.

Gussie lifted the top bundle onto the stone and unwrapped it.

There was a silence while four people who had never seen a newspaper be born looked at the first copy of The Bridge. Mr. Ogden picked it up, held it at arm's length, and read the whole front page standing, his lips moving slightly, his eyebrows climbing higher with each column. One of the mill girls — a tiny creature called Sally with chilblains on both hands — couldn't read at all, and stood close to Ogden's shoulder following his finger as though the words might be caught by proximity.

"*We will print the names of those who give and those who refuse,*" Ogden read aloud. He lowered the paper. "Missus Vane. Do you mean that last part? Names?"

"Every one."

"The refusers too?" His voice had an edge of something — hope, or its opposite. "Because there's gentlemen on the relief committee giving bread and taking obedience along with it, and there's others giving nothing at all and calling it principle. You'll print both?"

"Both. Fairly. With what evidence I have."

Ogden looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw there seemed to settle some question he'd carried in with him. "Then it's a newspaper," he said, "and not a placard. God help us all." He tucked his copy inside his coat with the tenderness of a man pocketing a love letter. "Deacons distribute better than constables do. We've got chapels from Ancoats to Collyhurst and every one of them has a poor-box and a grudge. Give me sixty copies."

Ellen took eighty for the mill rows — "the girls can carry them in their aprons, folded, nobody searches a girl carrying washing" — and the third bundle went with Sally and the other girl to the cellar dwellings, where, Ellen said flatly, there were people who could not walk to a chapel anymore and needed the news brought to them like medicine.

It was over in a quarter of an hour. That was the astonishing part, Gussie thought afterward. Four hundred copies, a month of fear, a lifetime of hiding — and the actual moment of it passing out of her hands took fifteen minutes and sounded mostly like coats being buttoned.

At the door, Ellen paused.

"It's your writing," she said. Not a question. "On the front page. *This paper exists to say so.* That's yours. I can tell."

"How can you possibly tell?"

"Because it doesn't sound like a man wrote it." Ellen said it simply, without flattery, the way she'd state the price of bread. "Men write *we demand.* You wrote *we will.*" She pulled her shawl up. "First number of anything is the easy one, Missus Vane. Everybody's brave before they've been caught. Second number's the test."

Then she was gone into the dark, and Gussie stood alone in the pressroom with the ink smell and the empty stacks and the wild, terrifying lightness of having done a thing that could not be undone.

* * *

She should have gone to bed. Instead she walked out.

It was a madness, and she knew it, and she went anyway — out the back, down the frozen yard, along the river path to the middle of the closed bridge, where the scaffolding made a cage of poles against the sky and the ice on the Irwell groaned and shifted in the dark like something enormous turning over in its sleep. Nobody was about. The district was an island full of hungry people and patrolled by watchmen who were paid by the very men the paper named, and she stood in the middle of the only road out, holding nothing, having just committed a crime, feeling — for the first time in her adult life — entirely visible.

Not safe. Not hidden. *Visible.* Seen, from this night forward, whether she was recognized or not. Somewhere out in the dark, sixty copies were moving through chapel vestries and eighty more through mill rows, and every one of them carried words she had made, and every reader who read them would know, without knowing her name, that somebody in Ancoats was watching the watchers and writing it down.

She had spent her whole life being the invisible one. The daughter at the type case. The girl who sorted the *e*s and said nothing. She had thought invisibility was safety, and then she had learned it was only a slower kind of erasure, and now she stood on a frozen bridge in the black cold and understood that she had traded one for the other permanently, tonight, with four hundred sheets of paper.

Her hands were shaking. She told herself it was the cold.

From the far bank, faintly, she heard singing — one of the strike songs, roughened and slowed, coming from the direction of the cellar dwellings. She couldn't make out the words. She didn't need to. Somewhere in that dark, someone was reading her sentences aloud to people who couldn't read, the way Mr. Ogden had, finger under line, and the words were walking around the district on other people's breath.

She stayed until her feet went numb, and then she went home.

* * *

The trouble arrived on Thursday, as trouble does: politely, and in daylight.

A boy came into the shop wanting a funeral card printed — ordinary work, ordinary pay — and while her father quoted him prices, the boy's eyes went around the shop slowly, shelf by shelf, window by window, the way a man looks at a room he intends to describe to someone later. Gussie watched him from the type case and felt the hair rise on her arms. He was perhaps fourteen. His boots were too good for a family that buried its own on cheap card.

After he left, she found the reason she'd been uneasy: he had looked longest at the press. At the press, and at her hands.

She was washing ink off them at the sink when the second thing happened. A gentleman stopped outside the window — well-dressed, gloved, a clerk's collar and a magistrate's air — and stood reading the notice her father had posted years ago about charges for jobbing work. He read it for a long time. Far longer than the notice deserved. Then he took out a small book, wrote something in it, and walked on toward the bridge without coming in.

Gussie dried her hands and went upstairs and told her father, and her father sat very still for a while with his trembling hand pressed flat against his knee as if to hold it down by force.

"A penny weekly," he said at last. "Seditious matter, unsigned, distributed through dissenting chapels. They'll not send constables first, Gussie. Constables are for thieves. For printers they send the magistrates' clerk, and the clerk sends informers, and informers cost ten pounds a head these days." He looked at her. "How many people know it's us?"

"Ellen. Ogden. Two mill girls. Nathaniel Quill."

"That's five."

"That's five *friends*, Father."

"Informers eat friends first," he said. "It's how they get introduced."

But that evening, when she came down to begin setting No. 2 — Ellen's list of relief distributions, Ogden's report on the soup kitchen, and a paragraph she had drafted about a mill agent named Harrop who had been seen counting heads at the gate and marking names in a book — she found something waiting on the composing stone.

A proof sheet. Of page one, No. 1. And pinned to it with one of her own brass nails, a note in her father's careful upright hand:

*Your mother printed nine numbers before they came for her. She always said the ninth was the best thing she ever wrote. I am the only person alive who knows that, because the other eight were seized and burned and she never spoke of them again. Print carefully, my girl. Count your friends twice. — F.*

Beneath the note, in smaller writing, added later, the ink a different shade:

*And keep the ninth number in your head, not in the case. Whatever happens, the last one lives in your head.*

Gussie stood a long time in the lamplight, the proof sheet in her hands, the cellar cold rising around her ankles like water. Then she set the type for page one of No. 2, and began to count.
