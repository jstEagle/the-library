## 10. A Reward for Names

The clerk's name was Mr. Hobbes, and he had held his post under the Manchester magistracy for eleven years without once being thanked, promoted, or noticed. He occupied a narrow room behind the sessions house on Bridge Street that smelled of tallow, damp plaster, and the particular sourness of old law paper. He liked it there. Nothing in that room had ever surprised him, and he had arranged his life so that nothing ever would.

On the fourth of February, 1842, something surprised him.

It came in the shape of a printed sheet, four pages, sewn at the spine with thread pulled from somebody's petticoat by the look of it, and it was delivered to him not by the post but by a constable named Daker, who set it down on Hobbes's blotter with the delicacy of a man laying down a dead rat.

"Found this nailed to the chapel door on Every Street," Daker said. "Third one this week. There's one on the canal bridge and one inside the gate of the Ancoats mill yard — that one the watchman swears wasn't there when he made his rounds at ten and was there when he came back at two."

Hobbes put on his spectacles. The sheet announced itself as *The Bridge*, "Printed within the district, for the hands locked out of the mills," and beneath the masthead ran three columns of type so clean and even that Hobbes felt a small, involuntary professional admiration before he remembered to be alarmed.

He read it twice. The first time as a magistrate's clerk, cataloguing offences: incitement, sedition, conspiracy, the usual litany. The second time as a man who had spent thirty years among documents, because the type troubled him.

"This isn't done on a jobbing press by some alehouse scribbler," he said. "Look at the spacing. Look at the justification. Whoever set this learned their trade properly."

Daker shrugged, which was his answer to most things.

"And the ink is fresh but the formes were locked with care." Hobbes turned the page. There, at the foot of the last column, was the thing that would occupy him for weeks afterward: a printer's device, a small bridge over water, cut in wood, and beneath it no name at all.

"No imprint," Hobbes murmured. "Which means they know exactly what we'd do with one."

* * *

By the following Monday, copies of *The Bridge* had been found in fourteen locations: pinned inside privy doors, slipped between loaves at two bakeries, folded into the pay packets of women still employed at the calico works, and — this last item reported to Hobbes in a state of some agitation by the Reverend Mr. Calloway of the Wesleyan chapel — placed deliberately upon the pulpit cushion before morning service, so that the minister had read half a column aloud before understanding what he held.

Hobbes laid all fourteen copies side by side on his long table and studied them as another man might study butterflies. Fourteen copies, and every one identical down to the kerning. One press. One compositor. One mind arranging the words.

The words themselves were careful. That was what unsettled him most, when he let himself think about it honestly, which was rarely and only after dark. There was nothing in the pages he could point to and say *there, that is treason*. The handbills of the summer had shrieked; this thing conversed. It printed the wage figures from the relief committee's own books, accurate to the farthing. It printed the names of families turned out of cellar dwellings, spelled correctly. It asked questions in the tone of a reasonable man across a table, and it signed its editorials only with the wooden bridge.

"You can't arrest a question," said Superintendent Larch, when Hobbes brought his findings upstairs. Larch was a large man with a large man's patience, which is to say he had none, and he expressed it by moving slowly.

"You can arrest the printer."

"Then find me the printer." Larch jabbed a finger at the spread sheets. "Four thousand hands locked out and every one of them a potential author as far as the town's concerned. Half the magistrates think it's the Chartists. The other half think it's the Methodists. Calloway wants it to be the Catholics. I want it to be nobody, and quickly."

"There's a way to make it somebody," Hobbes said. He had thought about this through Sunday dinner while his wife talked about her sister's health. "A reward. Ten pounds. Not more — more looks like fear. Ten pounds is what you'd pay for a lost dog, and that's the point. We're saying this printer is worth no more than a lost dog, and someone will sell them for exactly that price."

Larch considered. "Ten pounds buys a lot of informers in Ancoats just now. There's not a family over there eating regular."

"That," said Hobbes, "is rather the intention."

The notice was drafted that afternoon in Hobbes's careful copperplate:

*WHEREAS certain seditious publications, styling themselves THE BRIDGE, have been circulated within the township of Manchester: NOTICE IS HEREBY GIVEN that a REWARD OF TEN POUNDS will be paid to any person giving such information as shall lead to the apprehension and conviction of the PRINTER or PUBLISHER thereof. Apply to the Magistrates' Clerk, Sessions House, Bridge Street.*

He wrote the word *conviction* himself, after Larch had left, adding it in where the draft had said only *apprehension*. It was a small cruelty, professionally speaking. A man might be informed upon out of hunger and forgiven by his neighbours; a man whose information led to conviction had sent someone to prison, and Ancoats did not forgive that. Hobbes wanted the reward to work slowly. Fear worked better than money, and it kept better.

By Wednesday the notices were pasted on the toll house, the mill gates, the walls of the gasworks, and the shutters of every public house in the district. By Thursday evening, Hobbes had received his first visitor.

* * *

His visitors, in the weeks that followed, fell into types, and Hobbes catalogued them as he catalogued everything.

There was the hungry man, of whom there were many: men who stood in his doorway turning their caps in their hands, offering rumours about brothers-in-law and lodging-house neighbours, none of it worth the candle. There was the malicious woman, of whom there were two: one who wished to be revenged on a rival for a man's affections, one for a debt of ninepence. There was the patriot, a retired soldier who believed every radical movement since Waterloo was directed from Paris, and who offered, for his ten pounds, a theory involving Napoleon's nephew that Hobbes listened to for forty minutes out of a kind of professional courtesy.

And then there was the third kind, whom Hobbes feared, though he would never have used that word: the ones who knew something true.

A mill girl came on a Tuesday, still in her shawl, and said she'd seen bundles of printed papers carried out of the district wrapped like washing. She could not say from where. She took her shilling for attendance and went away, and Hobbes wrote in his daybook: *bundles, wrapped as linen, carrier unknown.*

A publican from the George came and said a man had been seen buying lamp oil in quantities beyond domestic use, in a street with no lamps. *oil, unusual quantity,* wrote Hobbes.

And on the last Friday of February, a young man in good boots and a coat cut above his station sat down opposite Hobbes's desk and said, pleasantly, "You're wasting your ten pounds, Mr. Hobbes."

Hobbes looked up. "I don't believe we're acquainted."

"We aren't. My name doesn't matter." The young man set his hat on his knee. He had the Manchester vowels softened almost to nothing, the cuffs of an office, and eyes that measured everything in the room including, Hobbes suspected, Hobbes himself. "But I'll tell you what your reward has bought you, since you've paid for it. It's bought you forty lies, and it's taught every fool in Ancoats that the magistrates are frightened of a four-page pamphlet. Frightened men print notices. Confident men find printers."

"And how does a confident man find them?"

"He stops looking at the poor." The young man smiled. "Paper costs money. Type costs money. Ink, oil, candles, thread — a press eats like a horse. Somewhere in that district is a household whose consumption has changed since January. Find the shop that buys its candles differently. Or better—" he rose, settling his coat, "—wait. Whoever prints a weekly must print next week's. And the week after. They'll make a mistake eventually. Everyone does. Your notice will still be on the wall when they do."

At the door he paused. "One piece of advice, gratis. When you do find them, you may find you've caught something smaller than you expected, holding something larger. It happens." And he was gone into the grey afternoon before Hobbes could ask a single question.

Hobbes sat for a long while with his pen dry in his hand. Then he opened his daybook and wrote: *visitor unknown, young, educated, warned us off. Either mad, or protecting the press, or running it.* He underlined the third possibility twice, then, after a moment, crossed it out — not because he disbelieved it, but because he believed it entirely, and some conclusions are safer unpublished.

* * *

In Ancoats, the notices did their work the way frost does theirs: silently, everywhere, killing the tender things first.

Ellen Brack tore the first one she saw off the mill gate and burned it in her mother's grate, and made her brother Joe swear he'd never touch the reward, and Joe swore, and meant it, mostly. But she saw the way men read it in the queue at the relief committee — the way eyes slid sideways, weighing neighbours — and she understood that the magistrates hadn't spent ten pounds on information. They'd spent ten pounds on doubt, and doubt cost nothing to manufacture and spread itself free.

She carried the warning to the shop on the bridge the same night, taking the long way along the canal, checking the ice-glazed windows behind her.

* * *

Gussie Vane heard Ellen out standing by the composing stone, her sleeves rolled, the smell of ink and lye on her hands. No. 3 of *The Bridge* was half-set in the forme; her mother's woodcut bridge stared up from the chase like a small black eye.

"Doubt spreads faster than the paper does," Ellen finished. "That's what the ten pound's for. Not to catch you. To make everyone wonder who'd sell you."

"I know." Gussie wiped her hands. "We'll move distribution again. Chapel deacons take the east side, mill girls the west, nothing carried twice by the same pair."

"That's not what I came to say." Ellen hesitated, and Gussie saw that the hesitation cost her. "Your da. Has he read it? The notice?"

"He reads everything. He's read my proofs since I was six."

"Aye," Ellen said. "Well." She did not say the rest, and did not need to. She touched the edge of the stone, once, the way a joiner touches good timber, and went out into the cold.

Her father was in the back room, and he had read the notice. Gussie knew it before she saw anything else, because he was sitting very straight at the table with both hands flat in front of him, and August Vane had not sat straight since the tremor began in his right hand two winters ago. Straightness was effort, and effort meant fear.

"Gussie." His voice was level, which was worse than shouting. "Sit down."

She sat. On the table between them lay a proof sheet — the corrected galley of No. 3, pulled that afternoon, still smelling faintly of the press — and beside it, folded, a copy of the magistrate's notice that someone had brought him, or he had gone to read on the toll house wall himself. She guessed the latter. Her father had walked out to read his own indictment.

"Ten pounds," he said. "Do you know what ten pounds is, in Ancoats, in February?"

"It's less than we cleared on wedding bills in October."

"It's a coffin bought outright. It's a winter's coal and a winter's bread and the arrears besides." He turned the notice over with one finger, carefully, as though it might go off. "There's not a soul over there wouldn't weigh their own child against ten pounds and call it arithmetic. You'd have done the sums yourself if you weren't the sum."

"I'm not asking anyone to love me. I'm asking them to carry paper."

"You're asking them to risk the sessions house." Now his voice did rise, and the tremor came into it, and he pressed both hands flat again. "Gussie. I set type for forty years and I never once printed a word I couldn't stand behind in daylight. Your mother—" He stopped. The room held its breath around the stopped sentence. "Your mother paid for her daylight," he finished quietly. "Nine numbers. Do you know what I did with the ninth?"

Gussie's throat tightened. "You told me it was burned."

"The eighth and the rest were burned by the constables. The ninth I burned myself, the night after they took her, because I'd read it, and it was the best thing she ever wrote, and I knew what it would do to whoever was holding it when they came." He looked at her then, and his eyes were wet and furious. "I have been waiting twenty years for this exact evening. Every night you've come up from that cellar with ink to your elbows, I've been sitting here doing this arithmetic. And now there's a price on it, in print, on every wall, and my daughter's name is written on every page whether the magistrate knows it or not — because I know it. I know that justification. I taught it to you."

The fire ticked. Down below, faintly, the river moved under its ice.

"Da—"

"No." He picked up the proof sheet of No. 3. He held it a moment, reading it — she watched his lips move slightly over the columns, and saw the terrible pride come into his face even now, the craftsman's involuntary pleasure at clean setting — and then he leaned toward the fire and fed it in.

The paper caught at the corners first. The type showed through the flame a moment, black letters swimming up out of the burning white like fish rising, and then it was all orange and curling and gone. Her father watched it until the last corner broke apart, and when he turned back his face had closed like a ledger.

"The press goes quiet," he said. "Tomorrow you'll tell whoever carries for you that the number's delayed. Next week you'll tell them it's stopped. In a month they'll have forgotten, or nearly, and we'll still be here, and the shop will still be here, and that's the whole of what I have left to protect. I won't lose you to ten pounds' worth of other people's hunger. Is it understood?"

Gussie looked at the grate. A fleck of ash still glowed there, pulsing faintly, going dim.

"Yes, Da," she said.

He nodded once, satisfied, exhausted, and reached for the lamp to turn it down. And Gussie sat very still in the failing light, obedient in every visible part of herself, while underneath — in the place where her mother's ninth number lived, in the place where the forme of No. 3 lay locked and waiting in the cellar, distributed nowhere yet, known to no one but Ellen and the deacons, already committed to memory page by page in case of exactly this evening — underneath, where her father had never once thought to look, the press did not go quiet at all.

She kissed his cheek. He held her hand a moment longer than usual, his thumb finding the ink stain at the edge of her palm, and neither of them said anything about it.

Upstairs, she banked the fire and waited for the house to settle into his uneven breathing. Then she took the candle down the cellar stairs, lifted the lamp to the forme locked in the chase — her mother's bridge, her own words, whole and unburned — and began, softly, in the voice her father used for counting friends, to recite page one aloud to the dark, committing it deeper, against fire and seizure and ten pounds, into the one case no magistrate could raid.

She had reached the third paragraph when she heard, above her, the front door of the shop open — quiet, deliberate, someone who knew the latch — and the candle flame bent toward the stairs.
