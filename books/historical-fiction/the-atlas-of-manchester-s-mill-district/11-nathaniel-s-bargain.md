## 11. Nathaniel's Bargain

The river had stopped pretending to be a river. From the shop window Gussie could see the Irwell lying under its lid of ice, grey-white and seamless, stitched at the edges with frozen foam. A week ago it had still groaned and shifted. Now it was silent, and silence, in her experience, was never neutral.

She was standing at the composing stone with a stick of type in her hand — the third number of *The Bridge*, half-set, when she heard the bell above the door and knew before she turned that it would be him. He had a way of entering rooms like a clause inserted into a contract: precise, unannounced in intent, altering everything downstream of himself.

"Miss Vane." Nathaniel Quill removed his hat. His cuffs were clean as ever — that was the first thing she'd noticed about him, months ago, and it still struck her wrong, a man with clean cuffs walking through a district where every sleeve was black with coal and grease. "Your father in?"

"No."

Something flickered across his face — not disappointment. Calculation, quickly folded away. "Then perhaps you'll hear me out instead."

"I'm working."

"So I see." He crossed to the stone without invitation and looked down at the galley, at the columns of type waiting under her fingers. He read upside down; she'd noticed that about him too, the second time he came. Solicitor's clerks read everything upside down. It was a profession built on knowing what other people thought they were hiding. "*On the Wages Question,*" he read aloud. "Number three. You're ahead of schedule."

"We're behind," she said. "Ellen Brack wants five hundred by Friday."

"Ellen Brack wants five hundred by Friday," he repeated, tasting the name. "And will get them. You do good work, Miss Vane. The best in the district, I'd venture, and I've seen most of it."

"Flattery costs you nothing."

"On the contrary." He set his hat on the corner of the stone, which no one did, which no one dared, and looked at her directly. "It costs me exactly what I've come to spend. Miss Vane — Gussie — the magistrates have a description now. Young woman, ink-stained, left-handed. It's been passed round three public houses and one very nervous relief committee. Ten pounds is a fortune to a starving weaver. Sooner or later someone hungry enough will walk through that door, or stand outside it long enough to watch you bank your fire at night, and mark how the smoke rises from a shop that claims to print only funeral bills and price lists."

Her hands went still on the type. She had known this was coming — had felt it coming the way you feel weather in an old wound — but knowing and hearing were different animals.

"You've been to the magistrate's office," she said.

"I work there."

"You *work* there." She set the type down carefully, because throwing it at him would cost her more than it cost him. "You sit at their desks. You sharpen their pens. And every week you carry their secrets across the bridge to me."

"And every week they carry nothing back," he said. "That's the arrangement. That has always been the arrangement."

"The arrangement," she said, "was handbills. Five hundred handbills, paid in silver, and my father took the job because silver buys coal. Nobody agreed that I would—" She stopped. The sentence had a shape she didn't want finished aloud.

"That you would write it," he said quietly. "Yes. I know. I've known since the first number. No compositor alive sets prose like that by accident — the rhythm gives you away. Your mother's pamphlets had the same pulse. I've read them. In the magistrate's own archive, as it happens. They kept her work as evidence."

The room seemed to tilt slightly. Her mother's words, filed in the enemy's drawers, catalogued and numbered, kept warm all these years in a government cupboard while the woman herself rotted in a pauper's grave. Gussie found she had to grip the edge of the stone.

"What do you want, Mr. Quill?"

"A cellar," he said. "Off Canal Street, under a disused warehouse. Dry, sound-floored, one entrance through a coal chute, another through the warehouse above, which belongs to a man who owes me more than he can pay. There's room for the press, the cases, the stock. There's a chimney that draws. And there is no bridge between it and anywhere else — no single road in or out for a constable to sit beside, smoking his pipe, counting who passes."

She said nothing. The offer was good. That was what frightened her. Bad offers she could refuse on principle; good ones got inside you.

"And the terms?" she asked, because there were always terms, and a man like Nathaniel Quill respected you for asking.

He smiled — not the smile he used on clerks and constables, something smaller and more honest, which was somehow worse. "Editorial control."

"There it is."

"Gussie—"

"*Mr. Quill.*" She picked up her composing stick again, mostly to have something in her hands. "Say the rest. You'll want to say the rest eventually; better now, while I still think well of you."

"The rest is this." He spoke evenly, the way he must speak to witnesses. "I move the press. I pay for the move, the rent, the paper — paper, Gussie, which you cannot buy in this district anymore without being marked. I place each number with the mill girls and the chapel deacons, I time the distribution so it never touches the same hands twice running, and I keep the magistrate's clerk — myself — supplied with just enough false trails to keep the hounds running at rabbits. In exchange, I see every page before it's printed. Nothing goes out that I haven't approved."

"Nothing goes out that you haven't *approved*."

"It's a war, and I'm the quartermaster."

"It's a newspaper," she said, "and I'm the one who writes it."

"And if you're taken?" His voice didn't rise, but it came closer, the way cold does. "If they take you, they don't take a girl with ink on her hands. They take the press, the type, the whole apparatus, and four thousand people go back to having no voice at all until spring. Every word you print puts Ellen Brack in the dock beside you. Every name in every column — the deacons, the mill girls, the men on the strike committee — every one of them hangs from your discretion. My discretion is the only wall between your writing and the assizes."

"My mother wrote without a wall."

"Your mother died of it."

The words landed like a fist, and she saw immediately that he knew they would — that he had carried them here deliberately, wrapped in silk, the way he carried everything. For a moment neither of them moved. Somewhere outside, a cart creaked over the frozen ruts of the bridge road, the only traffic left in the world.

"My mother," Gussie said at last, very quietly, "died of childbed fever, Mr. Quill, after eleven years of printing truth in a district that pretended not to know her. Don't you dare make her death into an argument."

"I apologize." And he did look sorry — genuinely, humanly sorry, which was the most dangerous thing about him. "But I won't withdraw it. Because it's true, and because you know it's true, and because you are setting type right now with her fire and none of her caution, and one of us in this room has to be cautious enough for both."

"One of us," she repeated. "So that's the bargain. My press moves into your cellar, my words pass under your eye, and in return I get to stay invisible. Is that it? You're selling me my old life back?"

"I'm offering you the chance to keep doing the only thing in this district that matters."

"The only thing that matters," she said, "would be worthless if it belonged to you."

* * *

Nathaniel walked out without another word, and stood on the bridge in the wind off the ice, and hated himself with a fluency that was almost professional.

He had rehearsed the speech eleven times. In the office, over the ledger, walking the canal towpath at dawn. Eleven times, and in ten of those rehearsals she had said yes — because the logic was sound, because the danger was real, because he had built the argument the way he built all his arguments, load-bearing and airtight. And in the eleventh rehearsal, the one he'd never admitted to, she had said no, and walked out of the shop, and been arrested within the month, and he had attended the trial in his clean cuffs and watched her transported, and woken every night since in a cellar of his own devising.

The truth — the truth he would have sworn to no one, not under oath, not under torture — was that the editorial control was real and also irrelevant. He would have approved every line she wrote. He had read her numbers against his master's briefs and found her more accurate than the government's own informants. If she handed him a page tomorrow naming the Home Secretary himself, some traitorous chamber of his heart would already be drafting the distribution plan.

No. The control was the price, but it wasn't the point. The point was the wall. The point was that she should never stand where her mother had stood, alone, in a doorway with the law coming up the street. He could not stop her printing. He had tried — God knew he had tried, sitting across from her father with his hat in his hands while the old man trembled and refused and meant it. But he could put himself between her and the consequences. That much, at least, a solicitor's clerk could do. That much he could buy with other people's money and his own ruin, both of which were already spent.

The trouble was that she had looked at him across the composing stone and seen straight through the architecture to the foundation, and the foundation was not strategy. The foundation was that he had crossed that bridge thirty times to watch her hands move among the type, and had told himself each time it was reconnaissance.

A sound made him turn. She had come out onto the bridge — coatless, which she would regret, arms crossed, breath smoking — and stood at the far end, at the boundary of her side and his, as though the planks themselves were jurisdiction.

"You followed me," he said.

"I live forty feet from where you're standing. This is my bridge." She came closer, slowly, boots ringing on the frozen boards. Below them the river lay locked and white, going nowhere. "I've been thinking about your cellar."

"And?"

"And I'll come." She held up a hand before he could speak. "On terms. Mine. You'll hear them all before you agree to anything, because unlike you I say what I mean the first time."

He waited. The wind pulled at her hair and she let it.

"First. The press moves, but the type stays mine. Every letter of it — including what I reclaimed from my mother's case. You don't inventory it, you don't catalogue it, you don't touch it. Second. I show you each number before it prints, as you ask. But showing isn't asking. I don't need your approval. I need your silence. If you object to a line, you say so once, plainly, and then you abide by what I decide. Third." She paused, and the pause had weight in it, deliberate as a full stop in type. "Third. When this is over — when the lockout breaks, or we're taken, whichever comes first — you tell me the whole truth of what you are. Not the version you give the magistrates and not the version you give Ellen. The whole of it. Whatever it costs you."

The river ticked beneath the ice, some vast slow shift far down in the dark water, like a thing turning over in its sleep.

"And if I refuse your terms?" he asked.

"Then I print from this shop until they carry me out of it, and you can explain to your masters why their clever clerk lost the only press in Ancoats."

"They wouldn't blame me. They'd never know I was anything but their clerk."

"That," said Gussie Vane, "is the loneliest thing anyone has ever said to me."

He looked at her then — really looked, past the argument and the architecture, at the woman on the frozen bridge with ink under her nails and her mother's fire banked in her chest — and understood that he had already lost, had probably lost the day he first commissioned five hundred handbills from a trembling printer and his quiet daughter, and that losing felt nothing like he'd expected. It felt like being let out of a cell.

"Agreed," he said. "All three."

"All three?"

"All three. Though I warn you — the third term may bankrupt you. Truths are heavy cargo, Gussie. Some sink the boat that carries them."

"Then we'll swim." She turned toward the shop, then stopped, her back to him, and for a moment the wind filled the space where a softer person might have said something tender. What she said instead was: "Thursday night. After the mills' whistle. Bring a cart and two men who can hold their tongues."

"And the third number? Ellen expects five hundred by Friday."

"The third number comes off the press tomorrow, in this shop, under the old arrangements." She glanced back at him, and there — just for a heartbeat, just long enough — was something in her face that was not strategy at all. "One last run in the open air. After that, we go underground."

She went in. He stayed on the bridge a while longer, listening to the ice groan below him, thinking about cellars and cargoes and the weight of truths, and wondering — with the particular dread of a man who has just made a bargain he intends to keep — what his masters would make of the empty shelf in the evidence room where her mother's pamphlets used to be.

Because he had taken them. All of them. Three weeks ago, on the night the reward notice was drafted, he had carried her mother's radical woodcuts and pamphlets out of the magistrate's archive in his satchel, meaning — he had told himself — to destroy them before they could hang her daughter.

They were at this moment in the bottom of that satchel, wrapped in oilcloth, in his lodgings on Canal Street. Directly above the cellar he intended to offer her.

And tomorrow, when the cart came, he would have to decide whether the mother's words went down into the dark with the daughter's press — or whether some hungrier part of him kept them, yet a while, as surety. As leverage. As the one card even Gussie Vane didn't know he held.
