## 12. The Raid That Missed

The knock came at the relief committee door on a Tuesday that had already gone wrong twice before noon.

Ellen Brack was counting coppers into paper twists — four ounces of bread a twist, two ounces of oatmeal for families with children under three — when the boy from Angel Street burst in with the news: constables at the canal, six of them and a sergeant, moving down the towpath toward Brindley's Row.

"Whose cellar?" she asked, and her hands kept counting while her mind went somewhere else entirely. That was the trick of hunger months; it taught a body to do two things at once, because neither could be spared.

"Don't know yet," said the boy. "They've got a warrant. Somebody's word on paper."

Somebody's word on paper. Ellen set down the copper she held, wiped her palms on her apron — Gussie Vane's apron, actually, borrowed three weeks past and never returned, still stiff in the seams where the other woman's starch had been — and went out into the street.

* * *

She found them at the mouth of Brindley's Row, and the first thing she saw was not the constables but the crowd. A raid drew people the way a fire drew moths, except here the moths were starving and had nothing left to lose but their curiosity. They lined the walls of the row in silence, which was worse than jeering; silence meant they were learning something, filing it away, passing it hand to hand like bread.

Six constables stood before the cellar door of number nine, and out of that door came a boy of perhaps fifteen, his wrists bound before him with a strap, his face grey as the February sky.

Ellen knew him. Everyone on the row knew him. He was the Molyneux lad, Joss, who swept cuttings at Tulketh's mill before the lockout and had since made himself useful about the district — carrying messages, hauling coal-sacks of type up from the low cellars, doing the hundred small errands that a secret press required the way a lamp required oil. He wore an apron. It was too big for him. It was ink-stained down the left side.

It was, Ellen realized with a coldness that started in her stomach and climbed, *Gussie Vane's spare apron* — the one that lived in the cellar under the chapel schoolroom, the one Gussie wore when she pulled proofs by candlelight and sent the boy out after with the wet sheets wrapped in sacking.

They had taken Joss because he fit the description. Young. Ink-stained. Left-handed — he was wiping his nose with the back of his left wrist even now, hands bound, and the sergeant saw it too, and nodded to himself like a man whose arithmetic had come right.

"Stand aside, missus," said the sergeant, catching her eye. He had a broad red face and the manner of a man enjoying his work. "Nothing here concerns the likes of you."

"Everything on this row concerns me," Ellen said. "Half these families eat off my committee's charity, and this lad's mother is one of them. What's he charged with?"

"Seditious printing." The sergeant said it grandly, tasting the words. "Producing and distributing a scandalous weekly publication calling itself *The Bridge*, against the peace of our sovereign Lady the Queen, inciting the mill operatives to unlawful combination." He recited it the way the magistrates' clerk had written it, and Ellen thought: so they have a copy. So someone read it aloud to them, slowly, twice. Someone in the relief committee or the soup kitchen or the chapel itself had sat down with a candle and copied out the words that would sell for ten pounds.

Ten pounds. She looked at the faces along the wall and wondered which one of them it was. Not the widow Hale, who had wept at the child's funeral. Not old Sam Croft, who could barely walk. But somebody. Somebody had needed ten pounds badly enough.

"You've got the wrong lad," she said.

The sergeant smiled. "That's what they all say."

"He can't write," Ellen said, and it was true, and it was the truth she had chosen without knowing she'd been saving it. "Joss Molyneux hasn't got his letters. Ask his mother. Ask the Sunday school mistress. I keep the relief lists — I know every soul on this row, what they earn, what they read, what they can't." She let that settle. "You want your printer? Your printer sets type with both hands and reads Latin margins for pleasure. That's no fifteen-year-old sweeping-boy."

It was a risk, saying even that much. Every word gave away shape. But the alternative was watching them carry Joss Molyneux off in Gussie's apron, and Ellen Brack had decided, somewhere between the committee room and the canal, that she was not going to stand in a silent crowd and learn something.

"Who are you?" the sergeant asked.

"Ellen Brack. Secretary of the Ancoats Relief Committee. My name will be known to Mr. Ellice at the town hall — he signs our grain requisitions."

He did not like that. A woman with papers behind her name was a different animal from a woman in the crowd. He looked at Joss, at the apron, at the boy's bound wrists, and Ellen watched him weigh it: the easy arrest, the tidy report, the ten pounds likely shared — against the bother of a magistrate asking why they'd taken a lad who couldn't spell *sedition*, let alone set it.

"There were type in that cellar," the sergeant said.

"There were potatoes in that cellar till Christmas," Ellen said. "There's a broken loom-frame now, and my committee's store of candles, and if there's type among it then half the district's children have been learning their letters off loose sorts, which is more learning than the mills ever gave them." She did not blink. She had learned steadiness from her mother, who had stood just so before a millowner's agent and lied about her age to get work at eleven years old, and had never once trembled.

Inside the cellar — she could see past the constable's shoulder into the dark — the press was gone. Gussie had moved it two nights since, down the canal by skiff, Ellen herself hauling on the rope at the lock gate with her palms blistered through her mittens. What remained below was a broken loom, candle-boxes, potato sacks, and — Ellen prayed — nothing else. They had swept it. They had swept it well.

But Joss had been caught coming out of it with ink on his cuffs, and that was enough for a warrant written by a man who had never seen a composing stone.

The sergeant chewed on it. Behind him, one of the younger constables shifted and said, quietly, "Sergeant — there's a woman two doors down swears she saw a press carried out of here Tuesday last. On a boat."

"And where'd it go?"

"Down-canal. Toward the basin."

Toward the basin. Toward Canal Street, though the man didn't know it, and Ellen felt the words land in her chest like dropped stones. The trail ran straight to the cellar Nathaniel Quill had offered Gussie, straight under the lodgings where — though Ellen didn't know the half of that either — certain papers lay wrapped in oilcloth.

"Well then," said the sergeant, brightening. "We'll follow the ink, won't we. And this lad comes along to tell us what he knows."

"He knows nothing," Ellen said, "because he cannot read what he carries, and no one tells a carrier more than the parcel."

"Then he's lost nothing but an afternoon." The sergeant took Joss's shoulder. The boy looked at Ellen — one look, quick, over his bound wrists — and whatever was in his face, it wasn't fear. It was apology. As if he were sorry to be taken, sorry to be a trouble, sorry that his arrest might cost *her* something. Fifteen years old, hungry since October, and apologizing.

Something in Ellen's chest turned over hard.

"His mother," she said. "Someone fetch his mother. Mrs. Molyneux, number fourteen. Tell her to come to the town hall, and tell her I'll meet her there."

The sergeant snorted. "Committing yourself to the prisoners' interest, missus? That can be noted too."

"Noted all you like," Ellen said. "My name's on every list in this district. You'll find it easier to spell than his."

* * *

The town hall was warm, which was its own kind of obscenity in a winter when children died of cold in cellar dwellings three streets away. Ellen sat in the corridor outside the magistrate's room on a bench polished by fifty years of waiting backsides, next to Mrs. Molyneux, who smelled of the dye-works and could not stop her hands from folding and unfolding in her lap.

"They say he set type," Mrs. Molyneux whispered. "Our Joss. He can't set *table*, Ellen. He can't write his own name."

"I know."

"Then why—"

"Because someone sold a description," Ellen said, "and the description was of a young person with ink-stained fingers, and Joss was the nearest thing in the district that matched it." She did not say *and the description was of Gussie Vane*. She did not need to. Mrs. Molyneux looked at her, and understood, and looked away, and folded her hands.

That was the thing Ellen could not stop turning over as the minutes crawled: that Gussie's risk had become other people's sacrifice, and would keep becoming it, and that Gussie herself would tear herself apart knowing it. Ellen had seen it happen before — not in printers, in strikers. One bold one acts, and the quiet ones pay. The bold one always believed they were spending only themselves. They never were. Courage was a coin that spent everyone in the purse.

And yet.

And yet the handbills had come, and then *The Bridge*, and the week the first number circulated, the queue at the relief committee had changed. Not shorter — longer, God knew, hungrier — but changed. People stood differently. They read the bills aloud to each other instead of whispering. Old Sam Croft had pinned a copy inside his coat, over his heart, like a scapular. The paper had done what Ellen's committee, with all its grain and coppers, could not do: it had told four thousand people that their hunger had a cause, and a name, and that names could be printed.

So she would not wish it undone. She only wished — sitting on the hard bench in the warm corrupt-smelling corridor — that the bill for it weren't being presented to Joss Molyneux.

The door opened. A clerk beckoned. "Mrs. Brack? The magistrate will hear you regarding the prisoner's character."

* * *

Mr. Hargreaves, magistrate, was a small dry man with ink-brown teeth and the air of one perpetually interrupted from better things. Joss stood beside the desk, wrists still strapped, and when he saw Ellen his chin came up — trying to look brave for her, God help him, at fifteen, in a stolen apron.

"Mrs. Brack." Hargreaves consulted a paper. "You administer the relief committee."

"I do, sir."

"You state the prisoner is unable to write."

"Entirely, sir. I keep the school registers crossed against the relief lists — it's how we sort the households. The Molyneux boy has never attended above four Sundays in his life. His mother can show you the register. There's no mark against his name after Michaelmas 1839."

Hargreaves looked at Joss. "Can you write your name, boy?"

Joss's jaw worked. Ellen saw him understand — saw the whole shape of it land on him — that if he said yes, if he scratched out a shaky *JOSS MOLYNEUX* and proved himself capable of letters, the lie collapsed and took Ellen down with it. Fifteen years old, and she was asking him to stand there and be ignorant on command, to wear his ignorance like the apron, because his ignorance was the wall between the constables and the press.

"No, sir," Joss said. "I never learnt."

"Read this, then." Hargreaves pushed a printed sheet across the desk. It was *The Bridge*. Number three. Ellen's stomach tightened; she knew that number; Gussie had been proud of it, had set the whole front page in her mother's reclaimed type.

Joss looked at it upside down. He turned it around slowly, frowning, moving his lips over shapes that plainly meant nothing, and Ellen thought: he doesn't even have to act. That's the bitter mercy. Fourteen years of mill and poverty had done the acting for him.

"I can't, sir," he said. "It's just marks."

"Take it away." Hargreaves withdrew the paper and laced his fingers. "Mrs. Brack, you will appreciate that appearances must be answered. The boy was found leaving a cellar at night with printer's ink upon his person."

"He was found leaving a cellar at night with a message for me, sir, which I will account for freely." Ellen heard her own voice go flat and official, the voice she used with the grain merchants. "The relief committee uses runners. On the night in question I sent Joss Molyneux to the cellar at number nine to fetch a box of our committee candles — we store surplus there since the break-in at the chapel — and he brought them up, and any ink on him came from the type sorts the district children practise with. I'd sooner the children learnt letters off loose type than learnt nothing off the mill gates, sir, but I'll answer for it if answering's wanted."

It was a good lie. She had built it crossing the town on foot, testing every plank. The break-in at the chapel was real. The candle storage was real. The children practising with loose sorts was real — she had seen them herself, Gussie's doing, teaching letters in the schoolroom with a case of worn-out type like a dame-school toy. Only the runner's errand was invented, and the errand was the kind of thing committees did a dozen times a week.

Hargreaves studied her. She met it steadily, and thought, absurdly, of Gussie's hands — those quick stained clever hands — and of what it cost, this steadiness, this lying with dry eyes while a child stood strapped beside her. Her mother's inheritance, same as Gussie's woodcuts: the women of this district had always known how to stand still in front of powerful men and give them nothing.

"The sergeant reports a press was removed from the premises by boat," Hargreaves said. "Tuesday last."

"Tuesday last the committee moved its candle stock by boat, sir, because the cart hire was up and the towpath was the cheaper road. If the sergeant counted candle-boxes as a press, I can show him candle-boxes."

A silence. Somewhere upstairs a door banged; coal smoke ticked in the grate. Joss swayed slightly on his feet, and Ellen wanted desperately to put a hand on his shoulder and did not.

Hargreaves sighed, and the sigh was the sound of a man declining a fight he suspected was beneath even his wages. "The charge is preferred on a warrant signed on information received. The information describes a person of the prisoner's general appearance engaged in the production of the weekly in question." He looked down at the description — young, slight, ink-marked, left-handed — and Ellen watched him notice, as she had noticed, that the description fitted a great many people in Ancoats, because poverty dressed everyone alike and ink marked anyone who worked. "However," he said, "in view of the testimony as to the prisoner's illiteracy, and the absence of type or press upon the premises, I am inclined to discharge the prisoner on his own recognizance, pending further inquiry. Bound over. If further evidence emerges—" he raised a finger at Joss "—you will be sought. Do you understand, boy?"

"Yes, sir," Joss whispered.

"Mrs. Brack stands surety for his appearance. Take the strap off him."

The constable untied the wrists. Joss rubbed them, and walked to Ellen, and she put her arm through his — propriety be damned — and steered him out past the bench where his mother rose up like a spring tide.

* * *

Outside, the cold hit like clean water. Mrs. Molyneux seized her son and wept into his neck, and Joss endured it with the stiff patience of boys, and Ellen stood on the town hall steps looking down the hill toward the district, at the smoke standing over the locked mills, at the canal running grey between its ice-ledges toward Canal Street.

"Gussie," she said quietly, when the Molyneuxes had gone. Just the name, to the air. Then, louder, to Joss, who had paused at the bottom of the steps: "Walk on home by Deansgate. Not the towpath. Not today."

He nodded, understanding more than she'd credited, and went.

She should warn her. That was the plain duty of it: walk to the shop on the bridge, or send the word down-canal to Canal Street, and tell Gussie Vane that the description was sold, that the net was drawn, that the sergeant meant to follow the ink to the basin and every cellar within a mile of it. Warn her tonight, before the constables worked out that the candle-boxes had been a press and the boat had gone down-canal.

But even as she turned up her collar and started walking, Ellen found the other thought waiting, patient as debt: that warning Gussie meant warning the press, and the press meant the pamphlet — the one the whole district had begun to breathe about, the one naming the agent, the one that would come off the stones soon if the type held and the luck held. And that every hour Gussie stayed free and unwatched was an hour the truth got closer to a thousand hands.

Joss had paid today. Ellen had lied today. The Molyneux household had stood in a magistrate's room today. And tomorrow it would be someone else — unless the thing was finished, quickly, and scattered wide enough that no single arrest could gather it back in.

At the corner of the bridge road she stopped. Below her, at the far end, the print shop windows threw their small yellow light onto the snow, and a figure moved behind the glass — bent over the stones, working late, left-handed, ink-stained, exactly the woman the warrant described.

Ellen stood in the falling dark and chose which way to walk.
