## 13. What the Type Remembered

The stroke came on a Tuesday, in the grey hour between the gas being lit and the last of the daylight dying, and it came without drama — which was, Gussie would think afterward, the cruellest thing about it.

Her father had been standing at the composing stone the way he had stood at it for forty years: shoulders slightly rounded, left hand flat on the marble to steady himself, right hand moving through the case with the blind certainty of a man who had stopped needing his eyes for this work decades ago. He was setting a funeral bill for the chapel on Great Ancoats Street — old Mrs. Pilling, aged seventy-one, *she hath done what she could* — and he had got as far as her Christian name when his hand simply stopped.

"Father?" Gussie said, from the imposing frame where she was locking up a chase of jobbing type. "The S is two trays down. You've gone past it."

He didn't answer. His hand hung above the case, fingers still curled as though around an invisible piece of fourteen-point, and his face — she saw it when he turned toward her, slowly, the way a door turns on a rusted hinge — his face had gone slack on one side, the left eye drooping, the mouth pulled down as if the flesh itself had given up its argument with gravity.

"Father."

She caught him before he hit the stone. He was not a heavy man — thirty years of stooping over cases had folded him up like a letter — but he went all at once, the whole weight of him surrendering, and they came down together against the cabinet, her knees on the floorboards, his head against her shoulder, and the funeral bill's loose type scattering across the stone with a sound like rain on a slate roof.

*E-a-n*, said the letters under her hand, where they'd fallen. Half a name. Half a woman.

* * *

Doctor Hargreaves came within the hour, summoned by Ellen Brack, who had been passing the shop on an errand for the relief committee and who heard the crash through the open door and did not hesitate — did not ask whose shop it was, or what was printed there, or whether helping might cost her. She simply put her shoulder to it, helped carry August Vane upstairs to his bed, sent a boy running for the doctor, and then stood wringing her shawl in the parlour below until Hargreaves arrived, because she would not leave Gussie alone in the house with a dying man and a press full of sedition.

That was the thing about Ellen that Gussie was only beginning to understand. She never asked whether a kindness was safe. She asked only whether it was needed.

"Apoplexy," Hargreaves said, coming down the stairs with his bag under his arm and his face arranged into the particular blankness doctors wear when the news is bad and they have delivered it too many times to enjoy delivering it. "A seizure in the brain. The left side is affected — you'll have seen that. His speech may come back partially. It may not." He set his hat on his head. "Keep him warm. Keep him quiet. And Miss Vane—" he paused at the door, and his eyes went, just for a moment, to the shutters of the print shop below, to the faint smell of ink that lived in the very plaster of the house, "—keep him away from anything that agitates him."

*Anything that agitates him.* As if she could keep agitation out with a bolted door. As if the district outside were not one long low note of hunger and anger, four thousand hands idle, the river frozen solid from here to Salford, constables counting heads at the bridge like sheep-counters at a fold. As if there were not a pamphlet hidden under the floor of her mother's old workroom with a man's name in it that could set the whole winter alight.

"I'll keep him quiet," Gussie said.

Ellen stayed while Gussie fed her father broth he could barely hold in the right side of his mouth, most of it running down his chin into his beard, and Gussie wiped it away and pretended this was ordinary, this was nursing, this was a thing daughters did and had always done. When the candle had burned down to a thumb's width, Ellen touched her arm at the stairhead.

"He set my marriage notice," Ellen said quietly. "Eleven years back. Charged me tuppence and threw in the border free, because I'd no father to pay for it." She looked toward the closed bedroom door. "You'll want help. With him, with the shop, with whatever it is you're not telling me you do down there at night. You'll want help, and I'm offering it, and I don't want thanks."

Then she was gone, down the stairs and out into the blue cold, and Gussie stood at the window watching her cross the bridge — walking now, since the carts had stopped, everyone walking now, the whole district reduced to foot-traffic and foot-soreness — until the dark took her.

* * *

Her father slept for two days.

Gussie kept the shop shut. She hung the card — CLOSED ON ACCOUNT OF ILLNESS — in the window, in her own setting, her own even spacing, and hated how steady it looked, how professional, as if the world were a place where cards explained things. Mill hands paused outside to read it. Some of them knew her father by sight if not by name; he had printed their union cards in the good years, their funeral bills in the bad ones, their children's reward tickets for Sunday school. A woman in a Paisley shawl crossed herself. Two men took off their hats.

On the third morning he woke lucid.

Not entirely — the right side of his face still answered late, and his words came slow, dredged up one at a time from somewhere deep, like stones hauled on a rope out of a well. But his eyes were his own again, clear and brown and appalled at the ceiling of his own bedroom, and when Gussie leaned over him he fixed them on her with an intensity that made her breath stop.

"Gussie," he said. The G cost him. "Girl."

"I'm here."

"The shop."

"Shut. Card in the window. Nothing's touched."

His hand — the right one, the good one — moved on the blanket, restless, feeling for something. She took it. His fingers were ink-stained at the cuticle even now, after three days abed; the stain would be in his coffin with him, she thought, however many years off that was, ground so deep into the whorls of his skin that death itself couldn't lift it out.

"There's a tray," he said. Each word arriving separately, expensively, like goods paid for on delivery. "In your mother's room. Under the—"

"I know," Gussie said. "I found it. Last month."

Something moved across the ruined half of his face — not quite surprise. Relief, maybe. Or resignation. The expression of a man who has been carrying a sack uphill for twenty years and is told, at last, that someone else knows what's in it.

"You found," he said, "the cuts."

"And the blocks. And the half-set forme." She kept her voice level. It was strange, how calm she sounded to herself, when inside her chest everything was beating hard and fast, a press at full tilt. "Father, why didn't you ever tell me? All these years — the way you flinched every time a Chartist came near the door, the way you made me swear we'd stay small, stay dull, stay *nothing* — and the whole time your wife was cutting woodblocks about Peterloo and hiding them under her own floor?"

His eyes closed. For a moment she thought she'd lost him again, that the effort of her voice had been too much, and terror went through her like cold water — but then his mouth moved, and what came out was almost a laugh, cracked and thin as old varnish.

"She hid them," he said, "from *me*. At the last."

Gussie sat down on the edge of the bed. Downstairs, faintly, the house ticked and settled around its own timbers, the way old houses do in frost, contracting, holding themselves together.

"Tell me," she said.

* * *

It took him an hour to tell her what would have taken ten minutes in the old days, and she learned to wait, to watch his throat work, to pour sips of barley water between sentences, and in the waiting she understood that the telling was itself a kind of labour for him, that he was setting these words one at a time in the composing stick of his ruined mind, choosing each one, spacing it, locking it up, and sending it out to her.

Her mother, it emerged, had not stopped in 1819. That was the history the family had always told — Martha Vane, radical woodcutter, silenced by Peterloo like so many others, married a printer, put down her knives, raised a daughter. But that was only the first half of the sentence.

"She kept cutting," August said. "Small things. Bills. Squibs. Things that could pass as jobbing work. And then—" his hand tightened on Gussie's, the grip shockingly strong, strength surfacing from somewhere below the ruin, "—then, two years past. Before she was ill. A man came. From the mills. An agent for the masters — a man whose trade is provoking. He goes among the hands, Gussie. Stirs. Agents provocateurs, the French call them. He gets men to swear oaths, to break windows, to give the magistrates their excuse. Then the masters wash their hands, and the soldiers come, and the men are transported, and the masters get their obedient town."

Gussie felt the floor of the world shift slightly, the way ice shifts when you step wrong. "Here? In Manchester?"

"Every mill district has one," her father said. "Ours—" and then he stopped, and she watched him try, jaw working, the word rising and failing, rising and failing, and she wanted to shake it out of him, wanted to shout *say the name, say the name* — and did not, because his face had gone grey with the strain.

"He has a name," she said carefully. "Mother wrote it."

"In the pamphlet." His breathing had gone shallow. "Half finished. Her last forme. She was setting it when she took ill — the fever — and she hid the proofs from me because—" the ghost of that laugh again, bitter as chicory, "because I begged her to burn it. I did beg her, Gussie. I'd seen what happened to printers who named names. I'd stood in the crowd at Peterloo and watched what happened to people who only *listened*. I wanted us small. I wanted you alive. God forgive me, I chose fear, and she—" his voice snagged, tore, "—she chose the truth, and she died with it under the floorboards, and I let it lie there because digging it up meant admitting what a coward I'd been for thirty years."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a cart passed on the bridge — rare now, the coal carts having found other roads — and the shop windows hummed faintly in their frames.

"It isn't cowardice," Gussie said at last, "to want your child alive."

"It is," he said, "when the price of her living is everyone else's dying." He turned his head — slowly, with effort, the neck stiff on its damaged side — and looked at her full, and his eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "I've watched you this year, girl. Thinking I didn't see. Setting type at midnight with the shuttered lamp. Coming up with ink to the wrist and lies on your tongue. Your mother's hands, on my daughter. I haven't the strength left to stop you, and I find—" the words came harder now, each one hauled, "—I find I don't want to. That's my blessing, Gussie. Take it. It's the only thing I have to give that's worth the carrying."

"Father—"

"Finish it." The grip on her hand was painful now, and she didn't pull away. "Finish her pamphlet. Set it true. Print it clean. And when they ask you who set it — and they will ask — you tell them the truth. Tell them it was Vane's. Both Vanes'. Let the name stand for something better than a shut door."

*Both Vanes'*, and her throat closed, because she understood suddenly what he was doing: he was putting his name under hers, ahead of hers, a father signing for his daughter the way he'd signed for her school bills, taking his share of the debt before it came due. The blessing and the burden arriving in the same sentence, inseparable, the way they always are.

"The forme is locked up," she managed. "Half of it. There's a page missing — the last page, where the evidence goes. The ledgers, the dates, the payments. Mother had sources. I don't know who."

"Quill," her father said.

Gussie went still.

"The clerk. The clean cuffs." A flicker at the corner of the ruined mouth that might, in another face, have been a smile. "You think I didn't know what he was when he walked in? Forty years on this bridge, girl. I know every man who crosses it and what he's carrying. He's playing a game with more boards than you can see. But he has access to the masters' papers, and your mother trusted him, and—" the strength was going out of him now, the tide going out, his eyelids sinking, "—trust her judgement. She was never wrong about people. Only about me."

He slept. Not the frightening sleep of the first day but an ordinary sleep, breath rattling gently, hand still curled around hers, and Gussie sat in the fading light and did not move for a long time, watching the frost climb the inside of the glass in feathers.

* * *

Down in her mother's workroom, past midnight, with the shuttered lamp turned low and her breath smoking, Gussie unlocked the old forme and read her mother's page again.

The woodcut at the head was new to her each time, though she had looked at it a dozen times now: a mill front, cut with savage economy, its hundred windows rendered in black rectangles — and in one upper window, small as a fingernail, a figure holding a lantern, lighting the way for men with bars in their hands. Below it, in her mother's upright, uncompromising roman, the title she had chosen:

*THE PROVOKER: Being a True Account of How the Lockout of Ancoats Was Made.*

And beneath the half-set columns, in the space where the final page should close the argument, Martha Vane had pencilled — in her beautiful, impatient hand, the pencil lines still sharp after two years in the dark — a list. Names of ledgers. Dates of payments. And at the bottom, underlined twice:

*Get the agent's name in ink. Not rumour. INK.*

The wind moved along the frozen river and the house creaked, and somewhere overhead her father slept with half his body gone silent, and somewhere across the black water Nathaniel Quill slept too, presumably, in whichever of his lives he was keeping there — the solicitor's clerk, the strike committee's man, the stranger with clean cuffs who had walked into her shop eight months ago and paid in silver and set all of this turning like a chase in the press.

*Playing a game with more boards than you can see.*

Gussie blew out the lamp and stood a moment in the dark, her mother's pencil list pressed against her sternum, folded small, warming against her heart.

Tomorrow, she thought, she would go and find Nathaniel Quill. And she would need to learn — quickly, before the thaw, before the magistrates, before her father's clock ran down — exactly which side of the forme he was really standing on.

Because the last page was still empty. And someone was going to fill it.
