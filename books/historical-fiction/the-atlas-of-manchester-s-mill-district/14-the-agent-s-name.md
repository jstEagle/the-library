## 14. The Agent's Name

The ledger smelled of his master's office before I even opened it — pipe smoke and sealing wax and the particular sweetness of money kept too long in a warm room.

Nathaniel had left it where he said he would: inside the false back of the composing stone cabinet, wrapped in an oilcloth that had once covered the press. He had come to the cellar door at dusk, not knocking, only standing there until I lifted the latch myself. Snow in his hair. His cuffs, for once, not clean — ink on the left one, though it was printer's ink, not clerk's, and we both knew whose.

"You shouldn't have come here," I said.

"Probably not." He set nothing down. He carried the ledger against his chest like a hymnbook. "Your father's name is in the magistrate's morning book now. Not as a charge. As a question. There's a difference of perhaps three days between them."

"Then give me what you brought and go."

He looked past me into the cellar, at the candle burning behind the stone partition, at the forme locked in its chase with my mother's type gleaming like frost. "Page six," he said. "The agent's name. You've set it."

"I've set it."

"And you mean to print it without knowing whether it's true."

I held out my hand for the ledger. "That's why you're here."

He gave it over, and our fingers touched around the oilcloth, and neither of us pretended it was nothing. That was the cruelty of Nathaniel Quill: he never pretended. He simply arranged things so that pretending became unnecessary, and then let you discover, too late, what the arrangement had cost.

* * *

I worked by candlelight through the coldest hours, when even the rats went quiet and the river under the cellar wall made sounds like a great animal turning in its sleep.

The pamphlet — my mother's pamphlet, finished now by my hand, my father's last instruction still warm as a coal in my chest — named one man. **Josiah Craven**, collecting-agent for the firm of Ackroyd, Whitmore & Craven, cotton spinners. It claimed that Craven had gone among the mills in October, before the lockout, carrying word from the masters' committee: that wages would be reduced a further fifteen per cent, that any hands who objected would be turned off without character, and that this was to be announced *before* the relief committee was formed, so that want would do in a fortnight what argument could not do in a year. Break the workers' spirit first; starve them pliant.

It was a terrible claim. It was exactly the sort of claim that gets a printer transported if she cannot prove it.

So I proved it.

The ledger was Ackroyd & Craven's own — disbursements, October 1841 through January 1842. Nathaniel's master, Mr. Farrow, was solicitor to the firm, which meant every payment the firm wished to make quietly passed across Farrow's desk first, and every entry Farrow copied into his own books, Nathaniel copied into his cuffs, his memory, and now, into my hands.

I found Craven within the hour.

*Oct. 9th — J. Craven, per order, visiting the several works, expenses* — four pounds eleven.

*Oct. 11th — J. Craven, per order, "the Ancoats communication," disbursements* — nine pounds. The words *the Ancoats communication* were in quotation marks in the original, which meant they were someone's euphemism, which meant they were a lie wearing gloves.

And then, dated the fourteenth of October — five days *before* the lockout, five days before the first empty mill gate — this:

*J. Craven, per order of W. Ackroyd, in consideration of services rendered and to be rendered in relation to the present difficulty of the trade* — fifty pounds.

*Services rendered and to be rendered.* Before the lockout. Paid like a bill for coals.

I sat back on the stool and let the candle flame steady itself, because my hands would not. My mother had known. In 1819 she had cut woodblocks of the yeomanry riding down St. Peter's Field; she had learned then what I was learning now, that the violence is never spontaneous, that somewhere there is always a ledger, always a man paid *in consideration of services rendered*, and that the whole architecture of respectable Manchester rests on papers no respectable person ever reads.

But there was more. There is always more; that is the first lesson of ledgers.

Because beneath Craven's payments, in a later hand, in entries Farrow had apparently thought too dull to hide, ran a second column of business. Fees. Retainers. Little sums marked *per N.Q., attendance.*

Nathaniel Quill had been attending. On the magistrates' committee. On the relief committee's rival, the masters' association. On two occasions — and I read the entries four times to be sure — *attendance upon the stipendiary, re: the anonymous press.*

Re: the anonymous press. Re: me. Re: *The Bridge*, re: the handbills, re: the pamphlet lying locked in its chase not six feet from where I sat.

I told myself it proved nothing. A solicitor's clerk attends everything; attendance is what clerks are for. Perhaps he sat in those rooms taking notes for the strike committee. Perhaps his presence there was itself a kind of espionage in our favour, and these dry entries were the record of his double life, the price of the intelligence he'd been feeding us since the night he stood in my father's shop with silver in his fist.

Then I found the third thing.

Dated the eighth of January — the day after the raid on the wrong cellar, the day Ellen Brack had lied to a magistrate with steady eyes while a boy of seventeen went down for wearing my apron — a single line:

*N.Q., per agreement, in consideration of information useful to the peace of the district* — twenty pounds.

*Information useful to the peace of the district.*

They had raided a cellar full of harmless barrels because someone had told them a press was there. Someone who knew a young woman kept irregular hours. Someone with ink on his cuffs who could describe her left-handedness, her hours, her face, to men who paid twenty pounds a time for such descriptions.

Or — and I turned this over until dawn thinned the candle — someone who had fed them just enough falsehood to keep them hunting cellars instead of finding mine. The raid missed. The boy arrested was no printer. If Nathaniel had sold us, why sell so badly?

Unless selling badly was the deeper sale. Unless the magistrates' confidence that they were being informed upon — and misinformed — was itself the information, purchased to make them complacent while the real work went on below a bridge.

I could not tell. That was the discovery that mattered, the one I wrote in my own private book beside the ledger's cold figures: *I cannot tell what he is, and the not-knowing is not innocence. It is design. He has built a man whom both sides can believe, and I have been believing him.*

* * *

My father woke at noon, as he did now, his right side slow and his speech arriving in fragments, like type dropped from a case and gathered up in the wrong order.

I read him the ledger entries. All of them. I had decided in the night that I would keep nothing from him — he had spent his life keeping things from me, my mother's blocks, her politics, her whole hidden self, and look what the keeping had bred. So I read him Craven's fifty pounds and Farrow's fees and Nathaniel's twenty, and I watched his face do what faces do when a hope dies quietly.

"He played us," I said. "Or he's playing them. And either way he's been paid out of both tills."

My father's good hand moved on the blanket. Words came slowly. "Read — the — dates again. The twenty pounds."

"The eighth of January. The day after the raid."

"Not — after." His eyes, the only part of him still entirely his own, fixed on me with an urgency that frightened me more than anger would have. "Ask — Farrow's clerk — what *day* the entry — was *written*."

"What does that matter?"

"Ledgers lie — by *order*. Dates — are how." He closed his eyes, exhausted by the effort, but his hand still moved, patting the blanket, patting, insistent. "Fees — are entered — when *paid*. Not when — *earned*. Find — what happened — *before* the eighth. What he — *did* — before the eighth — that they paid him — *after*."

I kissed his forehead and went back down, and I am not ashamed to say I ran.

* * *

What happened before the eighth of January took me two days to find, because it was not in the ledger at all. It was in the district.

Ellen Brack found me first — came to the shop's back door with flour on her shawl and her brother's shadow long and resentful behind her in the yard, and asked, plainly, as she always asks, "Has Quill been near you?"

"Why?"

"Because he was near *us*." She stepped inside uninvited, which Ellen Brack has never once done, which is how I knew the news was bad. "Tuesday last he comes to the committee room. Says the magistrates mean to search every cellar on the canal side within the week, and that the warrant names a woman printer. Says we should move the press or sink it. Says he'll arrange a diversion."

"A diversion."

"That's his word. Two nights after, constables hit Dyer's cellar by the lock-keeper's walk. Wrong end of the canal entirely. Our lad Tom Ashworth in your apron." Her jaw worked. "Tom's mother came to me yesterday. Somebody's paying his fine and his keep and telling her it's charity from friends of the cause. She wouldn't say who. She didn't need to. Nobody else in this district pays in guineas."

I felt it settle over me like the cold coming off the river: not rage, not yet, but the terrible clarity underneath rage, the thing my mother's woodcuts must have been carved out of. Nathaniel had engineered the raid himself. Had chosen the wrong cellar, had dressed a boy in my apron, had bought the constables their arrest and the movement its warning in a single stroke — spending Tom Ashworth's liberty the way he spent everything, precisely, profitably, and without ever once asking leave of the person whose skin it cost.

And he had done it, I reminded myself, to save the press. To save me. The raid had worked. Every fact was true and the shape of it was monstrous, and that, I understood at last, was Nathaniel Quill entire: a man made entirely of true facts arranged into lies.

"Ellen," I said, "how much of the committee's business goes through him?"

"All of it, now. Since the reward went up, folks won't carry word themselves. He offered, and he's quick, and he's never once been caught." She looked at me sharply. "You see it too."

"I see everything too late. But yes."

"So what do we do? Chase him off? Half the girls trust him with their lives. My brother says smash his windows and be done." Her voice dropped. "Gussie. If you're wrong about him, and we cast him off, we lose our courier, our warning system, and our purse in one afternoon. If you're *right* about him—" she didn't finish it. She didn't have to.

If I was right about him, then every route the pamphlet would travel on Saturday was a route he controlled, and the agent's name — Craven, fifty pounds, *services rendered and to be rendered* — would reach the magistrates' morning book before it reached a single pair of working hands.

"We don't chase him off," I said slowly, and the plan assembled itself in my mouth even as I spoke it, the way a page assembles in the forme, piece by reversed piece, backwards all the way through, reading true only once it's pulled. "We use him."

Ellen waited.

"He wants to know everything and tell everyone. Then he shall. Every detail of Saturday's distribution will pass through Nathaniel Quill's clever hands — times, routes, names. Except none of it will be true. We'll give him a distribution he can sell, and run the real copies along roads nobody's told him exist. Chapel deacons. The barge women. Your mill girls with the shawls." I heard my own voice go flat and cold, my mother's voice, 1819's voice. "Let him report our ghost to his masters. Let them stake out bridges and cellars for a pamphlet that's already in Stockport. He bought information with Tom Ashworth's freedom; I'll buy ours with his reputation. When the truth surfaces and his reports are shown to be ash, they'll stop trusting him — and stop paying him — and he'll have nothing left to sell but the side he actually chose. And then, Ellen, we'll finally learn which side that is."

She stared at me a long moment. Something moved through her face that might have been grief — for the version of us that hadn't needed to learn this — before it hardened into something older and harder that I recognized from the queue outside the relief committee: dignity under humiliation, sharpened.

"You've changed," she said.

"The river's frozen. Everything changes or cracks." I took up my coat. "There's one thing I have to do first, and I'll do it alone."

* * *

I found Nathaniel at dusk on the towpath, where the canal runs level with the Irwell and both are sealed under ice. He was watching the water as if reading it. Perhaps he was. I no longer assumed there was anything a man like him did idly.

I put the ledger against his chest the way he had put it against mine, and held it there, and did not let go.

"Fifty pounds to Craven on the fourteenth of October," I said. "Nine pounds for 'the Ancoats communication.' Twenty pounds to you, entered the eighth of January, for information useful to the peace of the district — earned, by my father's arithmetic, some days earlier. Days that contain a cellar raid, a boy in my apron, and a diversion arranged, I'm told, by a helpful friend of the cause."

He didn't flinch. That was the worst of it. He looked at me with those patient grey eyes and let me finish, and when I had finished he said only, "Your father is better than they say."

"Don't. Don't charm me, don't manage me, don't arrange me. I know what you are now, Nathaniel. You're a broker. You sell certainty to people who can't afford doubt — to them, to us, to anyone — and you've sold me so smoothly I mistook it for love, and I'll not mistake it twice."

"No," he said quietly. "You won't. That's why I chose this way."

"Chose—"

"Every other way ended with you in a cell before the pamphlet was finished. This way ends with them certain they know your moves. I have fed them a Gussie Vane who distributes by bridge and canal-side, who trusts me utterly, who can be taken whenever they please. While that Gussie exists, the real one prints in peace." He paused, and for the first time since I'd known him something unarranged crossed his face — quickly, like ice shifting. "I meant to tell you after the run. There's never been an after yet. There's only ever the next thing that must survive the week."

I wanted to strike him. I wanted, worse, to believe him — and that wanting frightened me more than the constables, more than the ten pounds on my head, because he knew it was there. He had built his whole double life on the certainty that I would want to believe him.

So I did the one thing he had not arranged.

"Saturday," I said, "the distribution goes by the bridge road at seven, twelve carriers, and you'll take the list yourself to the committee. Every name. Exactly as I give it."

His eyes narrowed — the first miscalculation of his careful life, because I saw him weigh it and accept it, believing I was surrendering my network into his hands.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me," I said, and walked away along the frozen canal with the real list folded against my heart and the false one already forming in my head, letter by letter, backwards, waiting to be set.
