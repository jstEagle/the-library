## 15. Ten Pounds

The word came to Ellen Brack in the queue outside the relief committee, as all bad news came now — sideways, in a whisper, passed from mouth to mouth like a coin too hot to hold.

"Ten pounds," said Martha Ogden, her breath steaming. "For the printer of *The Bridge*. Ten pounds and no questions asked. That's half a year's wages for some of them."

Ellen kept her eyes on the committee door. "There'll be no one takes it."

"There's always someone." Martha pulled her shawl tighter. "There's someone already."

She learned who by Thursday. Not a name at first — a shape of a thing, a wrongness she could feel the way you feel a draught under a door. The relief committee had been asked questions, official questions, by a man with a notebook who claimed to be gathering evidence of distress. How many hands had turned out? How were the families fed? Who brought the printed sheets that kept up spirits — because surely, the man said, with his sympathetic smile, such things did not print themselves?

And the committee had answered. Most of them honestly, some of them eagerly, and one of them — one — with an eye to the reward.

It was Mrs. Catterall who told her, in the end, behind the chapel on a raw afternoon when the frost had come early and hard. Mrs. Catterall, who had buried two children since the lockout began and whose face had gone thin and grey as newsprint.

"It's Josiah Pell," she said. "He's been seen going into the magistrate's clerk's office twice this week. Twice. And coming out counting."

Josiah Pell. Ellen turned the name over and felt nothing but a cold sort of confirmation. Pell sat three places down from her at every committee meeting. He had wept openly the night little Sam Whitlow died of the fever. He had signed the petition, carried the subscription list, praised *The Bridge* in a voice that carried to the back of the room. And all the while he'd been learning the thing he needed to learn: who moved among them that didn't belong.

"He told them about the handbills first," Mrs. Catterall went on. "Months back, they think. But now — now he's given them more. He's given them a description."

"What description?"

Mrs. Catterall hesitated. She was a woman who had learned, through long practice of grief, to deliver hard things gently, and she delivered this one as gently as she could.

"A young woman. Small hands. Ink-stained fingers — the left hand especially. Left-handed, they say. She comes and goes at odd hours near the bridge. That's what Pell gave them, or most of it. The rest they've pieced together themselves."

Ellen looked down at her own hands, chapped and red at the knuckles, ink worked so deep into the cuticles of the left that no scrubbing with lye would shift it. She thought of Gussie Vane — small, quick, left-handed, her fingers stained black to the second knuckle, standing at the stone in the cellar below the shop with the candle guttering and the type gleaming like a field of wet beetles.

"They know it's her," Ellen said.

"They suspect. There's a difference, and the difference is worth ten pounds to somebody." Mrs. Catterall gripped her arm, her grip fierce despite its frailty. "You warn her, Ellen Brack. You warn her tonight."

* * *

She went at dusk, along the frozen towpath where the canal lay locked under ice like a road of dirty glass. The mill district wore its winter look — chimneys cold, windows shuttered, smoke standing straight up in windless columns like the district itself holding its breath. Half-starved dogs trotted the frozen ruts. A knot of men stood outside the gates of Marsland's mill, not picketing anymore, just standing, the way men stand round a grave.

In the shop above the bridge the lamp burned low. Gussie let her in, and Ellen saw at once that something had changed in her — the father's stroke had done it, she supposed, though nobody spoke of it plainly. The girl moved quieter now, older, as if she'd been carrying something heavy up a long stair and had learned to set her feet carefully.

"Pell's sold you," Ellen said, without preamble. She laid it out flat, the whole of it: the reward, the office, the description. Ink-stained. Left-handed. Young woman, near the bridge.

Gussie took it standing very still, the way she took everything. Then she looked at her own left hand, turned it over in the lamplight, and laughed — a short, dry sound with no humour in it at all.

"I might as well have signed my name in the corner of every sheet."

"You could stop."

"No."

The word came out level and immediate, and Ellen respected it even as it frightened her. She had come expecting to argue; there was nothing to argue with.

"Then you can't stay here," Ellen said. "They're watching the shop. They don't need proof to take you, Gussie — they need only to be sure enough, and Pell's making them surer by the day. The press has to move."

"Move it where?"

"The cellar under the old dye-works, off the canal. It's empty since summer. Dan knows the caretaker. It's dark and it's cold and no magistrate's man would think to look." She paused. "It has to be tomorrow night. And it has to be us, and it has to be quiet, and it has to be soon, because I don't trust anyone past Martha and Mrs. Catterall and my brother, and my brother I trust with a rope round my own neck."

Gussie was silent a moment. Through the floorboards overhead came the small sound of her father shifting in his chair — the drag of it, the uneven rhythm of a body learning its new limits.

"He can't know," Gussie said at last. "If it goes wrong, he must be able to say truly that he didn't know where it went."

"And you?"

"I'm the ink-stained left-handed young woman," Gussie said. "I stopped being able to say anything truly some time ago." She looked up, and in the lamplight her face had the set look Ellen had last seen on women queuing for bread they knew wouldn't stretch. "Tomorrow night, then. Bring the skiff."

* * *

The barge skiff belonged to Dan, and had belonged to their father before him, and had not been in open water since the canal froze in November. They dragged it out of the shed behind the cellar dwellings just before midnight, runners of old iron lashed beneath the keel with tarred cord — Dan's idea, borrowed off the coal sledges — so that it would slide where it could not row.

The press came apart into seven pieces. Ellen had watched Gussie do it once and memorized it, the way she memorized everything: the screw and the platen, the carriage, the frame in two halves, the stone, and the type — the type wrapped in sacking and crated like something living, because it was, because it was the whole voice of the movement and everyone knew it. The forme of the mother's pamphlet, half-set, went into a bread box. The ink cakes went into a flour sack. Gussie worked without hurry, wiping each piece, checking each fit, and her breath hung white in the cellar air and her fingers never once fumbled.

They went out the coal hatch at the back, onto the frozen yard, and the sky was clear and bitter and full of stars that seemed close enough to cut yourself on. The cold had a sound to it, a fine ringing, like wire under strain. Ellen took the front handles of the crate and Dan the back, and they carried it down the frozen yard and onto the towpath, and the ice of the canal received the skiff with a sound like a struck bell.

They pushed and ran, pushed and ran, the skiff hissing along on its iron runners, the crates lashed down and muffled in sailcloth. Three miles of black ice between the bridge and the dye-works, and every yard of it exposed. Anyone at any window. Any constable on any bridge that crossed the water. Once, passing under the gaslight at Turner Lane, Ellen saw their shadows thrown enormous against the snow-banked wall — four figures, bent and straining, dragging something big — and thought, with a strange calm: there we are. That's what guilt looks like, lit up.

Dan swore softly at the turns. Gussie walked beside the type crate with one hand on it the whole way, as if it might roll overboard, as if the ice might open under it, as if it were a child.

At the halfway lock the ice was rougher, ridged where the water had shifted beneath, and the skiff juddered and slewed and nearly went over. They got it righted in silence. Somewhere across the canal a dog began to bark, then another, and they stood still in the starlight with their hearts going like hammers until the dogs tired of them.

It was Gussie who spoke, barely above the wind.

"Why are you doing this?"

Ellen wiped her nose with the back of her glove. "Because it needs doing."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind there is." She set her shoulder to the skiff again. "My father had a saying. When the mill's eating you alive, find the one job nobody else can do, and do it so well they can't replace you. It's how he survived thirty years. Well — I can't set type. I can't write what you write. But I can haul a boat across the ice at midnight without getting caught, and I can lie to a magistrate with a steady face if it comes to it, and there's not five women in this district can do either." She glanced at Gussie, and the stars were in her eyes. "That's the sisterhood of it, if you want it plain. Not speeches. Just everybody finding the one job, and doing it."

Gussie was quiet a long moment. "Your father sounds like mine."

"Your father built a press. Mine broke his back on one. Same trade, different wages." She grinned, quick and crooked. "Come on. Two miles yet, and my toes are going."

* * *

They reached the dye-works as the sky in the east had begun, very faintly, to change — not dawn yet, but the promise of it, the colour of pewter. The cellar door opened to Dan's knock, three and two, and the caretaker's boy stood aside with a lantern, saying nothing, having been paid nothing, owing them everything.

Down the steps, piece by piece, the same order reversed. Gussie found the stone's level with wedges and a bowl of water, tested the screw, fitted the frame, and by the time the light through the grating had gone from pewter to pearl the press stood assembled in the dark cellar as if it had always been there, waiting out the centuries. She set the forme on the stone and covered it and stood looking at it, and Ellen saw that her hands were shaking now — now, when the danger was past, which was the way it always went.

"Ten pounds," Gussie said quietly. "I keep thinking. Ten pounds is four months' food for a family of six. I'd give me up for less, if I were them. If I were hungry enough."

"You're not them."

"No. But I might have been." She straightened, and rubbed her stained fingers absolutely clean of nothing. "Pell will tell them the shop's quiet now. That the girl's gone to ground or given up. And when the last page is set—" She stopped herself.

"When the last page is set, what?" Ellen asked.

But Gussie only shook her head. "Nothing. Go home, Ellen. Sleep. And don't come here again — send Dan, or the boy, or no one. You've been seen enough with me."

"I'll come when I come," Ellen said. "You don't get rid of me by printing pamphlets."

She left her there, a small dark figure in a dark cellar with her mother's type and her father's press and a river of ice between her and the world, and walked home along the towpath as the sky went pale, and felt no fear, which frightened her.

* * *

Two days later the notice went up on the toll-house at the bridge, pasted beside the winter repair schedule, where every soul crossing into or out of the district had to pass it. Ellen read it in the queue at the relief committee, heard it read aloud twice more, watched it travel down the line like a spark down a fuse:

TEN POUNDS REWARD
for information leading to the apprehension of the printer or printers of the seditious weekly paper calling itself THE BRIDGE. Apply to the Magistrate's Clerk. All information strictly confidential.

Strictly confidential. Josiah Pell sat three places down from her that evening, head bowed over the subscription list, the picture of a broken man, and Ellen looked at him and felt the coldest clarity of her life. Let him sell his description. Let them hunt their ink-stained, left-handed girl.

But the description had a hole in it, and Ellen saw the shape of the hole and knew the magistrates would see it too, sooner or later, and smile. The girl who wrote *The Bridge* had to cross the river sometime. Everyone did. The bridge was shut for repairs, the mills were shut for spite, the district was an island, and islands had exactly one road off them.

Which meant, Ellen understood, standing in the freezing queue with ten pounds hanging over all their heads, that the trap, when it came, would not come to the cellar or the dye-works or the frozen canal.

It would come to the bridge.
