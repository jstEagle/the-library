## 16. The Trap on the Bridge

They came on the third morning of February, two men in drab coats who had no business standing in the cold with nothing to do.

Gussie saw them first from the upper window while she was shaking out her father's blankets — one leaning against the parapet at the far end of the bridge, hat pulled low, hands in his pockets like a man waiting for a friend who would never come; the other across the river by the coal wharf, ostensibly studying the ice as though it were the first frozen water he had ever seen. Neither moved for an hour. Neither had moved, she suspected, since before dawn.

She dressed without lighting the lamp and went down through the shop in the dark, past the composing stone where her father had fallen, past the empty frame where the press had stood until Ellen's brother and three other men carried it out wrapped in sailcloth ten nights ago. The shop looked gutted now, honest about its emptiness. A print shop without a press was only a room full of drawers, and drawers full of nothing but type — and even the type was mostly gone, packed into flour sacks and rowed up the frozen canal in a skiff by a woman with strong arms and no sentiment.

But they didn't know that. Or they did know it and wanted to see who came and went anyway. That was the thing she could not settle: whether the men on the bridge were evidence of failure or evidence of success.

Her father sat in his chair by the stove, his left hand curled in his lap like a sleeping bird. The stroke had taken the whole left side of him and most of his speech, though what remained came clear enough when it mattered. He watched her cross the room and look out through the shutters at the bridge.

"How many?" he said. The word cost him.

"Two I can see. There'll be a third somewhere comfortable." She let the shutter fall. "They're guessing we can't leave. Nobody leaves the island without crossing."

"Then don't leave."

"Not tonight," she agreed.

He held her gaze a moment longer than he could hold most things these days, and there was something in it — not fear exactly, but the particular gravity of a man who has spent sixty years putting words in order and knows precisely how many are left to him. He raised his right hand, the good one, and touched the edge of the table where the proof had burned, where he had burned it himself with his own trembling match before the stroke took him, back when terror still had use of his limbs.

"I meant it," he managed. "What I said. Before."

"You meant it then," she said gently. "You've changed your mind since. You told me to finish it."

His mouth worked. "Both," he said at last. "I meant both. That's what it is to be a father."

* * *

Nathaniel Quill came at dusk, which was itself a message. He had come at night all winter, hooded, careful; to arrive in the last grey light, openly, walking the length of the bridge past a constable who watched him do it, was either recklessness or permission.

She met him in the doorway rather than letting him in, arms folded against the cold, and studied his face for the tell she needed and could not find. Clean cuffs, still. Even now, even with half the district starving and the other half informers, Nathaniel Quill's cuffs were immaculate, and she had come to understand them as she understood type — a face cut deliberately, readable from a distance.

"You've seen them," he said quietly.

"Since Tuesday. You?"

"Since Monday." He glanced down the street. "There's a man lodging at the Wheatsheaf who pays for his ale in new coin and asks after barges. And a woman on the relief committee has been seen talking to the magistrate's clerk's boy. Ellen knows who. She won't say yet. She wants to be sure."

"Ellen is sure," Gussie said. "Ellen's always sure. She just likes a day or two to decide what she'll do about being sure."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile. He was very good at those almosts. It was one of the hundred things about him she no longer trusted, and one of the hundred things she loved, and the fact that the lists were identical kept her awake at night.

"Gussie." His voice dropped. "The warrant's drawn. I've seen it. Seditious libel, and the description matches you — young woman, ink-stained, left-handed. They mean to take you crossing the bridge, because they believe everything in this district must eventually cross the bridge. They're right about that much." He paused. "There will be a search of the shop within the week. Perhaps tomorrow."

"And the press?"

"Gone. Hidden. I know where, if you'll tell me I'm wrong."

"You're not wrong." She hadn't confirmed it aloud to anyone but Ellen, and hearing herself confirm it to him felt like laying type on a tilted stone — one slip and the whole line scattered. "Why are you telling me this? Truly. I'd like the true version, this once, because I've stopped being able to sort the others."

He didn't flinch. She wished he would flinch. A flinch she could read.

"The true version," he said slowly, "is that I am a solicitor's clerk who has been stealing documents from his master for four months, lying to a strike committee about how he got them, and lying to the magistrate's office about where his evenings go, and that every lie has kept someone safe until now, and now the lies are running out on both sides at once. The true version is that I don't know anymore which side I'm betraying when I walk through a door, including this one." He looked at her, and for once there was no polish in it. "That's the true version. It isn't a good one. It's just true."

"And if I'm taken tomorrow — does your master's office profit? Does the inquiry die with me? Is that the arrangement?" She heard her own voice go flat and cold, compositor's voice, voice for measuring ems. "Because I've thought about it, Nathaniel. Every way you might have played us. Every way the ledgers could have been bait. Every reason a man might teach a printer where the magistrate drinks."

"And have you found one that fits?"

"That's what frightens me. No. Every version ends with you ruined too. So either you're honest, or you're playing a game so long and so deep that I can't see the board." She exhaled, breath smoking between them. "I used to think trust was a thing you decided. Now I think it's a thing you spend, like silver, and you never get change."

"Then spend it or don't." He said it simply. "But spend it tonight, because tomorrow the shop gets searched, and if they find so much as a comma out of place in this building, six months becomes transportation, and I will not be able to lie fast enough to stop it."

* * *

After he left she stood a long time in the dark shop, listening to the river.

The ice had begun to talk. Everyone in Ancoats heard it now in their sleep — a groaning, cracking language under the floorboards, the Irwell shifting its winter weight, thick plates grinding against the piers of the bridge. The old men said it meant a break within the fortnight. The thaw had come early and hard, rain on the hills, and the millowners' agents were saying publicly that God Himself was clearing the roads so trade could resume, and the mill girls were saying privately that God had better hurry, because the relief bread was half chalk already.

Gussie laid her palm flat on the composing stone, cold as the river, and tried to think the way her mother must have thought in 1819, woodcuts hidden in a drawer, a city closing around her, deciding night after night whether the work was worth what it would cost. Her mother had never written down the answer. She had only left the drawer.

"Father," Gussie said. "If they come tomorrow, they find a shop that sells ballads and billheads and nothing else. If they ask where the press went, we sold it for scrap. If they ask where you were on the nights in January—" she stopped. He was watching her from his chair, firelight on the ruined side of his face, and his right hand lifted, slowly, and pointed — not at her. At the drawer.

Her mother's drawer.

"No," she said. "Not the pamphlet. Not here."

His finger stayed where it was. Then, with the terrible patience of a man spending words like his last coin: "Finish. It. Here. Your hand. Not theirs. Not the cellar." He breathed. "A page set in this shop. Once. Before they take the room."

And she understood him, finally, the way you understand a line of type only when you lift it out of the stick — that he was not asking her to be brave. He was asking her to be a Vane, in the room where Vanes worked, one time, with the constables on the bridge and the warrant drawn and the ice groaning like something waking up. He wanted the last page of Margaret Vane's pamphlet to be composed above the river that had carried their name for forty years, whatever came after.

"They'll search," she whispered.

"Then," he said, and the effort of it shook him, "be. Finished. First."

* * *

She sent word to Ellen by a mill girl carrying washing — three knots in a red thread, meaning *come by the water, midnight*. Ellen came along the frozen canal towpath with her shawl over her head and her brother's anger trailing behind her like a smell of smoke.

"He wants to burn the shop," Ellen said without greeting, crouching beside Gussie at the water stair where the skiff was tied. "Not tonight. But if you're taken, he says, and the girls' names come out of you, he'll put a brick through every window on the bridge and let the magistrates blame whoever they like." She said it evenly, reporting weather. "I told him if he touches your father's glass I'll break his head with the mallet we moved the press with. He believes me. He should."

"Ellen." Gussie turned to face her. On the black water the broken ice clinked and shifted, plates knocking together like cups in a drunkard's hand. "If I'm taken—"

"Don't." Ellen's voice cracked like the ice, sharp and final. "Don't you dare give me the speech. I've buried a child this winter and queued for chalk bread and watched my brother turn traitor in his own head, and I have no room left for farewell speeches from a woman who is standing in front of me alive." She gripped Gussie's wrist, hard, ink-stained wrist, left-handed wrist, the description on the warrant made flesh. "Tell me what you need done and I'll do it. That's the whole of what we have to say to each other. It always was."

Gussie told her: the pamphlet's final form, locked in the cellar with the press; the last page unset, deliberately, because her father had asked, because some inheritance could only be claimed in the room it was made in. Tomorrow, in daylight, while the constables watched the doors for a fleeing woman, she would sit at the stone in her own shop and set the final lines of her mother's unfinished charge against the men who had starved four thousand people — set them in plain sight, behind unshuttered windows, trusting to the one hiding place no one ever searches: ordinary work, done ordinarily, in view of everyone.

Ellen listened without blinking. When it was done she said only, "You're mad," in the tone she used for things she intended to help with.

"And you'll be on the water?"

"When the ice goes," Ellen said, "everything goes with it. Constables, warrants, the lot. All eyes on the river when it breaks — it'll sound like Judgment, and every soul in Ancoats will run to look. That's your boat. That's our minute." She stood, pulling the shawl low. "Get your page set, Gussie Vane. Get it set, and get yourself off that bridge, because I have lost enough people to this winter and I am not losing the one who writes."

She went up the towpath and was gone into the dark, and Gussie stood alone at the water stair with the ice grinding beneath her, listening to the river rehearse its breaking.

Behind her, up the street, a lantern came alight at the far end of the bridge. Then a second. The watchers, settling in for the night shift, patient as stones, certain that everything in Ancoats must cross the bridge in the end.

Let them watch the doors, she thought, turning back toward the dim window of her father's shop, where the candle had been lit and his shadow waited on the wall, bent toward the composing stone as if he could already see the line forming. Let them count the windows. Let them guess at boats and cellars and barges.

Tomorrow she would set the last page in plain sight, above the groaning water, and the only question left — the question that would follow her down the stairs and keep her hand steady on the type all through the daylight hours — was whether the man who had warned her had also, with those immaculate cuffs and that honest ruin of a face, shown the watchers exactly where to stand.
