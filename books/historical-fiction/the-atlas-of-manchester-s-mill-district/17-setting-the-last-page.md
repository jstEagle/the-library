## 17. Setting the Last Page

The ice began to speak at dusk.

Gussie heard it first as a sound she could not name — a long, low groan beneath the floorboards of the print shop, as though the building itself had grown a voice. She stood at the top of the cellar stairs with the candle in her hand and listened, and understood: it was the river. The Irwell, frozen solid for six weeks, was beginning to move under its white lid, the way a sleeper turns in bed before waking. The whole mill district lay on that ice — the bridge, the coal yard, the warehouses, the shop itself — and now the river wanted its bed back.

She went down the stairs anyway.

The cellar had been her world for eleven days. Since Ellen Brack and two barge men had rowed the press down the frozen canal by night, sliding it on greased planks across the ice to this old dye-house cellar with its bricked-up windows and its smell of madder and wet stone, Gussie had lived here more than above ground. Her father's bed was carried down too, because he could no longer manage stairs, and because he refused — with the only violence left in him — to be anywhere but beside the composing stone while the last pages were set.

He sat now in the corner where the candlelight reached him least, wrapped in two blankets, his left side slack as wet paper since the stroke. His right hand rested on his knee. It trembled still — it had always trembled — but it was his good hand, and tonight, God willing, it would have one more piece of work to do.

"Is it time?" he asked. His speech came slow, each word set like type itself, chosen and placed with effort.

"Nearly." Gussie hung the second candle from its hook over the stone and turned up the wick. "I want to read you the last column before I lock the form."

* * *

Her mother's pamphlet lay open on the composing stone, the manuscript worn soft at its folds from six weeks of handling. *A True Account of the Turn-Out in Ancoats*, written in a hand Gussie knew better than her own face — the slanted, impatient letters that had filled the hidden drawer under the case, the woodcuts of Peterloo crowds and sabres and a woman holding up a child above the charge. Forty-three years old, that manuscript. Her mother had been nineteen when she wrote it, and she had never finished it. There was a gap of three pages near the end, and then a final paragraph, begun and abandoned mid-sentence, and after that nothing but blank paper and, folded into it, a single line in different ink, added later, drier:

*The agent's name is* —

And the space after it empty. Her mother had died with the name unwritten, or had chosen to leave it so, or had simply run out of courage — Gussie would never know which, and had stopped asking the dead questions they could not answer.

But Gussie had the name now. She had it from the ledgers Nathaniel Quill stole out of his own master's office, copying the entries in fair hand by lamplight while his employer slept upstairs: payments, dated to the week of the lockout, made by the masters' association to a Mr. Josiah Clegg, agent, *for services rendered in provoking and prolonging said dispute*, itemized like candles or carriage hire. Provoking. Prolonging. Four thousand hands turned out into a hard winter because one man was paid to make sure they could not go back.

She had checked it twice against the relief committee's records Ellen brought her wrapped in shawls, and twice against her own memory of the man — Clegg, the smooth-voiced gentleman who had walked the picket lines in his fur collar the first week, telling the hands the mills would never open for such ungrateful wages, telling the magistrates the opposite. The name was true. That was the terrible thing about it. It was true, and printing it would ruin them, and there was no honest way to leave it out.

"You're stalling," her father said.

"I'm reading."

"You're stalling. Your mother stalled the same way. She'd sweep the floor twice before a difficult page." He shifted against the wall, and the effort showed in his face, grey under the stubble. "Read me the last column."

Gussie drew the candle closer and read.

It was the ending she had built herself, out of her mother's fragment and her own four months of nights: the account of the lockout, the queue at the relief committee, the child in the cellar dwelling whose name Ellen had given her and which Gussie had insisted on setting in full — *Martha Brack Hulme, aged five years, of Angel Meadow, who died of fever on the 14th of January, in the fourth month of the turn-out, within sight of a full warehouse.* Then the ledger evidence, laid out plain, entry by entry. Then the agent's name. Then a last paragraph, her mother's abandoned sentence completed at last in Gussie's own words, the sentence her mother had started forty-three years ago and which had waited all that time for a daughter to finish it:

*Let it be known, then, that we are not a mob, nor a riot, nor a misfortune of trade, but a district of working people who can count, and who have counted what was taken from them, and who ask now only that the reckoning be printed —*

"—and be read," her father finished, when she paused for breath. "That's how she'd have ended it. *Printed, and be read.* She always said the press was worth ten thousand speeches because speeches die in the air and print sits in a man's pocket and argues all night."

Gussie smiled despite herself. "You told me once you hated that saying."

"I hated it because it was true and I'd married it." He looked at the manuscript with his good eye; the other drifted now, and would not come back. "Set it exactly so. Don't polish it. Her sentences were better rough."

* * *

Then there was nothing left to do but the work.

She washed her hands — an old ritual, pointless in a cellar where ink lived under her skin year-round, but her father had taught it to her as a kind of prayer before any important forme, and prayers do not need to be sensible. She tied back her hair. She checked the type cases by candlelight: the body text of long primer, her mother's reclaimed type, bought secondhand in 1819 and hidden in the drawer through every raid and inspection since, lead that remembered everything it had ever printed. The display sizes for the title and the agent's name. The furniture, the quoins, the composing stick with its worn brass edge that fit her left hand the way a tool fits the hand that has earned it.

She set the last page standing at the stone, the way she had stood every night of her life, and the work did what work does — it took her out of herself and gave her back to herself changed.

There is a silence inside composition that is unlike any other silence. The mind holds the sentence whole and breaks it into pieces, letter by letter, upside down and backward, and the hands move without instruction, and outside the head the world may burn or freeze but inside it there is only the next word. Gussie had found that silence at nine years old, standing on a stool at her father's stone, and had kept it secret ever since — the one room in her life no one else could enter. Tonight she entered it deliberately, like going home.

*Martha Brack Hulme, aged five years.* The capital M was easy; there were three left in the case. The lowercase she took by feel, thumb and forefinger reading the nick of each sort in the dark beyond the candle's reach. *Josiah Clegg.* She set his name in the same size as everyone else's — no larger, though her hand wanted to make it large, wanted black-letter and fury — because her mother's draft had taught her better. A name set plainly among facts is more damning than a name set in flames. Fury fades. Facts sit in a man's pocket and argue all night.

Outside, the ice groaned again, longer this time, and something cracked — a sharp report like a pistol shot, echoing off the warehouse walls. Candle flame shivered. Somewhere overhead, on the street, a dog began to bark and would not stop.

"The thaw's come early," her father said.

"It's not the thaw. It's rain upstream, maybe. Warm days in the hills." But she had grown up on this river and knew its voices, and this was not the small talk of January cracks. This was the deep shifting of a thing too heavy to hold much longer. Two days, perhaps. One. The ice on the Irwell had broken every spring of her life, and when it broke it broke all at once, with a roar, taking fences and coal yards and anything else that stood too near its temper.

Two days was enough. It had to be enough. Tomorrow night the run would go out by skiff through the breaking channel — Ellen had the boatmen promised, three of them, paid in flour — and the pamphlet would scatter up and down the valley faster than any constable could walk, and by the time the river gave the island back to the law there would be nothing left to seize but a girl and a scattered case.

If the constables came first, there would be nothing at all.

She shut the thought away and lifted the next line into the stick.

* * *

It was past midnight when she reached the end.

The last column stood complete on the stone except for one line — the final line, the sentence she had rewritten eleven times in her head and could not improve and could not finish, because finishing it meant the pamphlet was finished, and finished meant the world would start moving again, and some cowardly part of her wanted to stay here forever in the candlelight with the work almost done, safe in the country of almost.

She stood before the stone with her hands empty.

"Gussie."

She turned. Her father had worked himself upright somehow, blankets fallen, his ruined side propped against the cellar wall, and he held out his right hand to her. Trembling. Open.

"Bring the stick," he said. "We'll set it together. Like when you were small."

"I can set it, Father. You should lie down."

"I know you can set it." For a moment his voice came clear, the old voice, the voice that had called measurements across the shop for thirty years. "That's not why. Come here."

She brought the stick and knelt beside him, and he closed his trembling fingers around hers around the brass, and together, slowly, painfully, letter by letter, they set the last line of her mother's pamphlet:

*Set and printed by A. Vane & Daughter, Ancoats, in the year the ice broke.*

Not her mother's words. Not her father's either. Hers — and his blessing on them, spelled out in type, in the open, with no false imprint, no London address to hide behind, their own name on the work that would hang them.

"Father," she whispered. "They'll know it's us."

"They've known it was us since October." He let go of her hand and sat back, exhausted, but his eyes were wet and steady. "Your mother signed her woodcuts with a little bird. I always told her it was vanity. It wasn't. It was this." He touched the line of fresh type with one finger, careful as touching a sleeping child. "A thing isn't true unless you'll stand behind it. Finish the form."

She locked the quoins. She lifted the finished page — the whole last forme, heavy as a paving slab, light as a held breath — and carried it to the press and laid it in the coffin, and inked the rollers, and pulled the proof, and held it to the candle.

The agent's name stood in the middle of the page, plain as bread. Martha Hulme's age and street stood beneath it. And at the bottom, in the same modest type as everything else, their name: *Vane & Daughter.*

Above them, through the bricked-up window slit, the ice spoke again — and this time it did not stop speaking. The groan rose and rose, and somewhere far off down the valley another crack answered it, and another, a conversation rolling toward them through the dark, and the candle flames leaned all together toward the river as if bowing.

Her father heard it too. He looked at her across the press, and there was no fear in his face, only the calm of a man whose forme is locked and whose work is done.

"How long?" he asked.

Gussie watched the candle flame bend, and listened to the river gathering itself beneath them like a fist closing.

"A day," she said. "Perhaps less. We pull the run tomorrow night — ready or not."
