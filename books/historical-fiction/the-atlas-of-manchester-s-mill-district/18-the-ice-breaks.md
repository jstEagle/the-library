## 18. The Ice Breaks

**Gussie**

The river spoke first.

She heard it through the cellar floor at half past two in the morning — a long, groaning syllable, like a giant clearing its throat under the cobbles of Ancoats. The ice on the Irwell had been talking for three days, muttering and ticking as the thaw worked at it, but this was different. This was a sentence. A declaration.

Gussie set down her composing stick and listened with her whole body.

Above her, the shop was dark. Her father slept in the room behind the press, propped on pillows, his left side still slack from the stroke, his right hand — the good one — curled on the counterpane as though even in sleep it was reaching for a quoin. In the corner stood the last crate of type, packed in straw: her mother's letters, the ones that had named Josiah Craven and his agent Silas Petch, the ones that would be in a thousand hands by Friday if the river did its work.

The ice groaned again, longer this time, and somewhere upstream something cracked with a report like a pistol.

She climbed the cellar stairs and let herself out into the yard. The cold hit her lungs like water. The sky over Manchester was the colour of wet slate, and the air smelled of thaw — mud and coal smoke and the green, mineral breath of moving water. After weeks of iron stillness, the world had begun to breathe again.

Down at the bridge, lanterns moved. She counted four. Constables, keeping their watch, as they had every night for a week. They knew no one could leave the island district except across that bridge, and they meant to catch the printer of *The Bridge* walking into their arms.

They had not thought about the river.

* * *

**Ellen**

Ellen Brack stood at the lock gate on the canal cut with her boots frozen to the towpath and her heart going like a shuttle.

The skiff sat low in the black water, gunwales barely clear, loaded with oilcloth bundles. Inside each bundle: fifty copies of the pamphlet, sewn in twelves, the ink still smelling faintly of lampblack and linseed. Four hundred copies in all. Nathaniel Quill's man had brought the barge skiff up from the basin before midnight, poling it along the channel where the canal ice had been broken by the coal boats all winter and had only just refrozen thin enough to smash with an oar.

"Once she goes," the boatman had said, jerking his chin at the river, "every constable in the parish will run to the banks to watch. Nobody looks at a canal when the Irwell's breaking up."

Now they waited. Ellen, her brother Sam, and six mill girls from the India House sheds, each of them sworn to silence and none of them entirely trusted, because fear made liars of everyone and they all knew it. The child from the cellar dwellings — little Mary Ann Hobbs — had been dead nine days, and her funeral had turned into a march, and the march had turned into a riot, and the riot had taught them what numbers were worth. Four thousand locked-out hands. Not a mob. An army that hadn't been issued weapons yet.

Sam crouched at the lock, chipping at the skim ice with the flat of an oar. "It'll hold us," he muttered. "Barely."

"It doesn't have to hold us." Ellen watched the river below the weir, where the great white sheet of the frozen Irwell shuddered against the piers of the bridge. "It has to hold the paper. We walk."

She thought of Gussie Vane then — small, ink-stained, left-handed, the girl who'd stood in the print shop doorway in November and said, *I'll do it*, when her father wouldn't. Ellen had wrapped those first five hundred handbills in her own shawl and carried them past a constable who'd looked straight through her, because a mill girl carrying washing was invisible. That had been the whole art of it, winter long: invisibility, borrowed and lent among women who were never seen anyway.

Tonight the borrowing ended. Tonight the words went out in the open, on the water, under every lantern in Ancoats.

Upstream, the ice gave a sound like a cathedral splitting.

"Now," Ellen said. "Load her now."

* * *

**Nathaniel**

Nathaniel Quill walked the bridge with his collar up and his hands in his pockets and the constable's warrant burning a hole in his memory where he'd read it over his master's shoulder three days ago.

*Augusta Vane, or such person as shall be found in possession of the press and materials.* Ten pounds reward. His own master, Mr. Harrop, solicitor, had drawn up the affidavit for the magistrate without knowing — or perhaps knowing; Nathaniel no longer knew what anyone knew — that his clerk had been carrying proofs between the print shop and the strike committee since December. That he had stolen ledgers out of Harrop's own office to prove what Silas Petch had done. That he had —

He stopped at the middle of the bridge, where the parapet gave onto the black running water, and made himself look at it plainly, one more time.

He had used her. From the first night, clean cuffs and silver coin and a Manchester accent polished just rough enough, he had known exactly what the Vane girl was and what she could be made into. A press. A name for the pamphlets that wasn't his. And when the trap closed — and it was closing tonight, he'd seen the orders, constables summoned from Salford, plainclothes men posted at both ends of the island — she would be taken, and the committee would be safe, and the machine he'd built would roll on over her.

Unless the river broke first.

He felt it through the soles of his boots before he heard it: a deep concussion, transmitted through stone, as if the earth itself had struck the ice with a hammer. Downstream, past the weir, a seam of black water opened in the white field, wide as a cart road, and the whole sheet began to move.

"God," said the constable beside him, forgetting himself. "Look at that."

Every lantern on the bridge swung toward the sound. Every face. Nathaniel turned with them, one man in a crowd of watchers, and behind him, up the street, a door opened and shut without a sound, and a small figure in a man's coat went quickly down toward the water.

He had warned her. Or led her to it. He had told himself both stories all winter until neither was true anymore.

There was only one story left to tell, and it would be told in a magistrate's room, in his own voice, damning himself — unless the boats beat the constables to the weir.

He began, casually, to argue with the constable about the safety of the parapet, loudly, drawing two more men into it, buying minutes the way he bought everything: with words.

* * *

**Gussie**

The skiffs went out into the breaking ice like needles into cloth.

She stood on the sluice steps below the print shop and watched Ellen Brack pole the loaded boat out into the channel, the oilcloth bundles stacked like loaves, Sam Brack smashing the skim ice ahead of them with slow, rhythmic blows. Behind them, the Irwell was tearing itself apart — plates of ice the size of barn floors grinding against the bridge piers, rearing up, collapsing, the black water surging through in floods. The noise was enormous. It swallowed the dogs, the church bell, everything.

A constable appeared at the top of the sluice steps, lantern held high, and Gussie did the only thing there was to do.

"Here!" she shouted, waving both arms. "Help! The water's coming up the yard!"

It was even true; the thaw-flood was lapping at the cellar grate. The constable came down toward her, and above, on the street, she heard boots — many boots — and saw lanterns pouring off the bridge toward the shop. Toward her father. Toward the empty crates and the scattered galleys she'd spent the night arranging into a lie: a shop mid-job, innocent, interrupted.

The boats slid past her in the dark. As Ellen's skiff crossed the lamplight spilling from the bridge, Ellen looked back — one glance, finding Gussie on the steps — and raised her hand from the pole. Not a wave. A salute. Then the current took them, and the ice took the light, and the pamphlet that named Silas Petch went downriver toward Salford and the city and the thousand hands waiting beyond.

Gussie turned and climbed the steps to meet the law.

* * *

**Ellen**

The river fought them the whole way to Salford and Ellen loved it for fighting.

Ice ground along the hull like millstones. Twice a floe rose under them, tilting the skiff until the bundles shifted, and Sam swore and shoved it off with the oar, and the mill girls in the stern — Betsy Hobbs, Mary Ann's sister, thirteen years old and grey-faced with cold — bailed with their aprons and sang under their breath, some chapel hymn beaten into time by the oar-strokes. The water was rising fast now, the thaw come down off the hills all at once, and the channel that had been a ditch all winter was a young sea.

At the Salford stairs a crowd had gathered on the bank to watch the ice run — hundreds of people, drawn out of their houses by the roar of it — and when the skiff grounded and the bundles came ashore, dripping, there was no need for secrecy at all. Ellen unwrapped one bundle in the torchlight and held the pamphlet high.

"Read it!" she called. "Read it and pass it on! It names the man who starved you!"

The crowd took it like fire takes dry thatch. She watched a copy go from a bargeman's hands to a woman's to a boy who ran off up the hill with it held overhead, and she understood, standing in the freezing spray with her hands bleeding from the rope, that it was already too late for any magistrate in Lancashire. You could burn a shop. You could not burn a river.

Behind them, across the black water, Ancoats glowed with lanterns converging on one small building by the bridge.

"Hold her bow line," Ellen said to Sam, and did not look back again.

* * *

**Gussie**

They came through the door of the print shop at ten minutes past three.

There were six of them and her father was sitting up in his bed demanding to know the meaning of it, his speech slurred but his fury intact, and Gussie stood between the constables and the press with her hands at her sides and let them see her exactly as she was: ink to the second knuckle, lead dust in the creases of her palms, a woman who smelled of her trade and always had.

"Is this your press?" asked the sergeant, a red-faced man with snow melting on his shoulders.

"It is my father's press," Gussie said. "And I am the only soul in this house who can lift a forme. So if you're hunting a printer, Sergeant, you've found one."

They turned the shop over anyway. They pulled the drawers and spilled the cases — her mother's type, the reclaimed letters, scattering across the boards with a sound like hail on slate. They found the empty crates, the oilcloth shreds, the damp of fresh ink on the stone. They found nothing that named her, because the proof of her lay on a boat halfway to Salford, but they didn't need proof. The sergeant looked at her ink-stained hands, and at her left hand holding the candle, and something in his face went almost apologetic.

"You'll come along quiet, miss?"

"I'll come along quiet," Gussie said.

Her father tried to rise. The stroke had taken his left side and the bed took the rest, and it was Gussie who reached him, Gussie who eased him back against the pillows, his good hand gripping hers with terrible strength.

"The drawer," he whispered. "Under — the stone. Her last page. She never —"

"I know, Father." She bent and kissed his forehead, which smelled of camphor and lead. "Finish the run. That's my inheritance. Keep it for me."

Behind her the constables waited with their lanterns, and outside the ice went on breaking like the end of the old world, and Gussie Vane picked up her cloak, and put it on, and walked out of her father's shop into custody with her head up — surrendered, staged, deliberate, free.

* * *

In the magistrate's lock-up on Bridge Street, past midnight, a clerk recorded the prisoner's name — *Vane, Augusta, printer, aged 22* — while outside the window the Irwell carried its shattered winter seaward, and under the last of the ice, riding the flood toward a city waking to find itself papered in truth, the final copies of her mother's pamphlet went out into the dark like seeds on a thaw wind.

The trial was set for Thursday.

Nathaniel Quill was not yet in the room. But he was coming, and everyone in Ancoats already knew whose testimony would break it open.
