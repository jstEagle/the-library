## 19. The Magistrate's Room

The room smelled of tallow, wet wool, and the particular staleness of men who had sat in judgment too long. Gussie counted the things she could see, because counting kept the fear at a distance where it could be studied rather than felt: four candles guttering in brass sconces; a map of the mill district pinned to the panelled wall, its ink faded to the brown of dried blood; a mahogany table scarred with rings from a hundred tankards; and three faces arranged behind it like figures on a coin — the magistrate Alderman Prosser in the centre, heavy-jowled and red about the nose; a stipendiary to his left whose name she didn't know and whose eyes never left her hands; and to the right, a clerk with a quill poised over paper, waiting to make her silence into a record.

Her wrists ached inside the irons. They had not bothered to remove them for the interview — a detail she noted with something close to professional interest. Type was set tight or loose depending on what the page must bear. These men had set her loose. They did not believe she was dangerous.

They were wrong, but not in the way they feared.

"Augusta Vane." Prosser pronounced it as though tasting something he suspected of being spoiled. "Printress, of the shop upon the bridge."

"Printer," Gussie said.

A pause. The stipendiary's gaze lifted from her hands to her face for the first time.

"We have here," Prosser continued, tapping a sheet before him without looking down at it, "the final number of a seditious weekly called *The Bridge*, together with a pamphlet of some two-and-twenty pages entitled *An Inquiry Into the Late Lockout of the Ancoats Mills*. Both printed upon an Albion press. Both set — we are informed — in type of a distinctive and aged cut." He looked up. His eyes were pale, watery, and utterly without mercy, but there was something else beneath them, a tiredness that surprised her. "You will confirm these particulars."

"I will not confirm anything," Gussie said. Her voice came out steadier than she had expected. Somewhere below, through the floor, she could feel the river — not hear it exactly, but feel it, a low shifting weight against the bridge's foundations, still carrying the last of the broken ice seaward. It had carried other things too, by now. She let herself think of that: the press run gone out by skiff in the grey chaos of the thaw, Ellen Brack at the tiller with her jaw set like iron, bundles wrapped in oilcloth and bound for cellars and chapels and the back rooms of public houses from Ancoats to Salford. A thousand copies. More. Gone before the constables' boots had come up the stair.

"You will confirm nothing." Prosser repeated it flatly. "You understand, Miss Vane, that your silence is not the protection you imagine. We have witnesses. We have a description — young woman, left-handed, ink about the fingers. We have the press itself, seized this morning, and the type scattered across your father's floor like—" he paused, and something flickered in his face, "—like a spilled drawer."

*Like shrapnel,* Gussie thought, and said nothing.

"The pamphlet makes allegations," the stipendiary said. His voice was drier than Prosser's, more careful, the voice of a man who dealt in documents. "Serious allegations. Against Mr. Josiah Craven, agent to Messrs. Ashworth and Lees. That he provoked the lockout deliberately. That wages lists were altered under his hand. That he conspired with certain parties to starve the district into submission ahead of the repeal agitation." He laid a folded ledger page on the table, smoothed it once with his palm. "Where did you obtain corroboration for these claims?"

Gussie looked at the ledger page. Nathaniel's handwriting — no, her master's handwriting, copied out in Nathaniel's careful clerk's fist, the figures squared and ruled as only a solicitor's office could rule them. Stolen from the offices of Quill & Marlor by Nathaniel Quill himself, who had been playing both sides since October, who had commissioned five hundred handbills in silver and then sold information to the magistrates and then stolen the proof of his own treachery and laid it on her composing stone like a bouquet. She had used him. He had used her. And now the figures sat on a magistrate's table, and there was no version of events in which they had arrived honestly.

"I set what I was given," she said. "I'm a printer. Printers don't author. Ask any man in this room whether he blames the press for what's written on it."

Prosser snorted. "We blame the press for a great deal, Miss Vane."

"Then you understand my trade better than most."

The clerk's quill scratched. Outside, muffled through shutters, the town went about its noise: cart wheels, a dog, the bell of St. Matthew's striking eleven. Somewhere in the building a door slammed and footsteps passed and faded. Gussie found she could catalogue all of it, the way she catalogued sorts in a case — b, d, p, q, each in its compartment, nothing out of place. Fear, she discovered, was a compositor's emotion after all. It demanded order. It rewarded precision.

"Your father," Prosser said, and watched her. It was the first true thing either of them had done all morning, that watching. "Thomas Vane. He is reported to have suffered a seizure. He is reported unable to speak."

"He speaks," Gussie said, before she could stop herself. Then, because she would not take it back, she added: "Slowly."

"And yet the pamphlet is finished. Set, printed, distributed — in the space of days, while the river broke up and every constable in the district was watching the ice." The stipendiary leaned forward. "A half-paralyzed old man did not set twenty-two pages, Miss Vane. Someone did. Someone with ink under her nails."

Gussie looked at her hands. The ink was in the creases, in the whorls, worked so deep into the skin of her left palm that soap had given up on it years ago. She turned her palms upward, deliberately, and held them out across the table like an offering or an accusation.

"Yes," she said. "There's ink under my nails. There has been since I was six years old. My father taught me to set my name before I could write it with a pen. If that is evidence, arrest the alphabet."

For one unguarded instant — less than a breath — the stipendiary's mouth twitched. Then it was gone, and he was a wall again, and Gussie understood that she had seen the whole shape of her situation in that twitch. They did not want her. They wanted the thing she represented, contained, and they wanted it cheaply. A fine. A sentence. A name in the gazette. The town had watched the mills stand empty all winter, had watched children queue at relief committees, had read the pamphlet — everyone had read the pamphlet, it was in a thousand hands, it was being read aloud in chapel vestries and beer shops from here to Stockport — and the town's sympathy had turned like weather. Even these men knew it. Justice, she was learning, rarely arrived straight. It arrived crooked, sideways, wearing whatever coat fit, and it was paid for always by somebody's ruin. The question was whose.

"Take her down," Prosser said wearily. "Hold her for trial. Sessions open Monday."

As the constable took her arm, Gussie paused at the door. She did not plan it. It simply rose in her like type rising under the press's pull.

"Alderman," she said. "When the inquiry comes — and it will come, because you cannot unprint a thousand copies any more than you can unring a bell — ask Mr. Craven why the wage lists changed the week before the lockout. Ask him whose signature countersigned them. You'll find the answer in your own records. You've been sitting on the truth for months. All I did was print it."

She was gone down the stair before anyone answered, which was just as well, because no one did.

* * *

They held her three days in the New Bailey before the trial, in a cell with a straw pallet and a window high enough to show only sky. Ellen Brack came on the second day, having bullied her way past a turnkey with a basket of bread and cheese and a manner that suggested she had faced worse obstacles than law officers in her time.

"They're saying Craven's fled," Ellen said through the bars, low and quick. "To Liverpool, then maybe America. Ashworths are disowning him — putting notices in the papers, as if paper ever washed anybody clean. There's to be an inquiry. A real one, not a whitewash. Reverend Wray says even the *Manchester Guardian*'s writing about it careful-like." She pushed the bread through. "You did that."

"We did that," Gussie said. "How's my father?"

Ellen hesitated, and the hesitation told Gussie everything before the words did. "He sits at the window. He watches the bridge. He can't say much, but when the neighbours pass he lifts his hand." She stopped. "He's proud of you, Gussie. Whatever else. Whatever comes."

On the morning of the trial the sky was the colour of unprinted paper, and the courtroom was full past standing. Gussie was brought in between two constables and saw faces she knew in the crowd — mill girls in their shawls, deacons from the chapel, the publican from the Old Navigation, a boy who had run pamphlets for Ellen all winter. Nobody shouted. That was the discipline Ellen had imposed, and it held: a silence more eloquent than cheering, four hundred people watching a nineteen-year-old girl in irons the way congregations watch scripture.

The case was brief. Seditious libel, three counts. The evidence was produced: seized copies, the testimony of two informers, the description circulated among the police. Gussie answered the questions put to her with the same economy she had spent on the magistrates — she confirmed nothing, denied nothing, and when asked directly if she had composed *The Bridge*, she said only, "I am a printer. Words pass through my hands. What stays in them is between me and God."

It might have ended there, quickly, with a verdict already agreed in some private room over claret. It might have. But in the gallery, unhurried, a man rose.

Nathaniel Quill wore his clean cuffs. Of course he did. Even now, ruined, he had dressed with the terrible precision of a man arranging his own headstone. He descended to the floor, presented himself to the bench, and announced, in a carrying voice pitched for the back of the room, that he wished to give evidence.

The stipendiary recognized him with visible reluctance. "Mr. Quill. Clerk to Quill & Marlor, solicitors. Your firm acts for Messrs. Ashworth and Lees."

"It did," Nathaniel said. "I am, as of this morning, dismissed. Or perhaps resigned. The distinction seemed important to my former master and unimportant to me." A rustle went through the room. "I wish to testify regarding the provenance of the pamphlet's financial claims."

"You understand," the stipendiary said slowly, "that such testimony may implicate yourself."

"I understand precisely what it implicates," Nathaniel said. "I have been employed for some months by persons connected to the strike committee to gather information regarding the conduct of the mill agents during the late lockout. In the course of that employment, I removed certain documents from my employer's offices — wage lists, correspondence, a memorandum in the hand of Mr. Josiah Craven dated the eleventh of November last. Those documents corroborate, item by item, the claims made in the pamphlet *An Inquiry Into the Late Lockout*. I supplied them to the printer of that pamphlet. I did so knowingly, willingly, and in the full understanding that doing so constituted theft, breach of trust, and — I should imagine — conspiracy." He paused, and looked directly at Gussie, and what was in his face was not love exactly, or not only love. It was the look of a man setting his last line of type, knowing the press must come down on it. "The lady did not steal anything. I did. Every document, every figure, every damning entry — mine to take, mine to give. If the court seeks the author of this mischief, let it look to the man who opened the safe."

"That's not—" Gussie started, and stopped, because the constable's hand was on her shoulder, and because finishing the sentence would undo everything he was spending himself to build.

Nathaniel smiled at her then — small, private, the smile from the frozen river, the night she couldn't tell whether he was saving her or drowning her. Now she knew. He was doing both, and paying for the privilege.

"Mr. Quill," Prosser said heavily, "you do comprehend that you are confessing to offences punishable by transportation?"

"I comprehend it perfectly, Alderman. I have excellent comprehension. It is my besetting virtue." He folded his hands. "Proceed."

The courtroom made a sound — not a cheer, nothing so useful, but a long exhale, the sound of four hundred people realizing the story had turned. The stipendiary conferred with Prosser in a murmur. The clerk wrote fast, quill flying, recording ruin in a beautiful hand. And Gussie stood in the dock with irons on her wrists and understood, with a clarity that hurt worse than any sentence could, what she was watching: justice arriving, crooked as promised, outside the law entirely, bought and paid for with Nathaniel Quill's career, his liberty, quite possibly his skin — offered up on the altar of a pamphlet, for her, and she could neither accept nor refuse it. It was already done. He had set the type himself.

The verdict, when it came that afternoon, was almost gentle, and gentle the way a trap is gentle. Seditious libel, convicted. A fine of fifty pounds and six months' imprisonment in the New Bailey. No transportation. No pillory. Prosser read it out in the tone of a man closing a ledger he was glad to be rid of, and added, off the record, in a voice pitched for her alone as the constables moved her away:

"You'll find the town has decided you're a heroine, Miss Vane. Heroines are cheaper alive. Mind you don't cost anyone anything further."

The crowd parted for her going. As she reached the door she looked back once, up at the gallery, but Nathaniel was already gone — taken down another stair, into another custody, toward a hearing of his own. She would not see him again for a long time. She would see his letters sooner than that.

Outside, snow was falling on the bridge, softening the scaffolding scars, erasing the winter's record stroke by stroke, the way snow does — patiently, thoroughly, and never for good.

Ellen Brack was waiting at the foot of the steps with her arms folded against the cold, and her face, when Gussie read it, held a message she would not deliver until visiting day: that the mill girls were already passing round a hat.
