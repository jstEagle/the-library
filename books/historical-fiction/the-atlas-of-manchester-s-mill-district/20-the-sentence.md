## 20. The Sentence

They tried Gussie Vane on a Tuesday in March, and the town came out to watch as if it were a hanging.

Ellen Brack stood at the back of the courtroom on Minshull Street with her arms crossed over her shawl and counted heads. Not the magistrates — she'd stopped counting those long ago; they were six grey stones in scarlet and black, arranged behind the bench like coppers in a till. She counted *her* people. Mill girls who should have been at the gates queuing for whatever work had opened since the settlement. Weavers with their caps crushed in their fists. Two deacons from the chapel who'd sworn they'd never be seen within a mile of seditious printing and were now wedged shoulder to shoulder against the wall like men sheltering from rain. Even old Mrs. Catterall, who could barely walk from the cellar dwellings to the privy, had come, propped between two granddaughters, her face set like a woman attending a funeral she intended to disapprove of.

The room was too small for all of them. The overflow spilled down the steps and into the street, and when Ellen went out at midday to fetch bread for a girl who'd fainted, she saw them standing in the road in the drizzle, four deep, silent, looking up at the shuttered windows of the courtroom as though the verdict might be posted there like the price of flour.

*They've come for her*, Ellen thought. *Not to gawk. To witness.*

She'd have said it was impossible, three months ago. In December, when the child died of fever in the cellars and her brother Sam had stood outside the print shop with a brick in his fist calling the Vanes traitors' property, she would have said Manchester would watch August Vane transported with the same face it wore for a cockfight. But that was before the pamphlet. Before the agent's name went round the district like a fever of its own — before the inquiry was announced and the mill owners' man was made to stand in a room and answer questions he'd never dreamed he'd be asked. The lockout hadn't ended because the masters grew kind. It had ended because they grew *careful*, and careful men do not starve four thousand hands in winter with a magistrate's clerk keeping their ledgers honest.

The town remembered who had told them the truth. The town had come to see what the truth cost.

* * *

The charge was read out in language designed to make a printer's daughter sound like Bonaparte. Seditious libel: the publication of certain scandalous and inflammatory writings, to wit, a pamphlet entitled *A True Account of the Late Lockout in the District of Ancoats*, containing false and malicious imputations upon respectable persons—

"Respectable persons." Ellen heard a weaver behind her breathe the word out like smoke. "That's what we were starving under. Respectable."

Gussie stood at the bar in a plain dark dress, her hair pinned back, her hands folded in front of her. Her left hand, Ellen noticed. The ink had never entirely come out from under the nails; you could see the blue-black crescents even across the width of that room, and Ellen felt an absurd surge of pride at the sight of them, as if the stains were medals.

They had cleaned her up for trial. They'd taken her from the cells that morning and given her — Ellen learned this later, from the turnkey's wife, for a shilling — a wash and a comb, because the magistrates wanted no spectacle of a martyr dragged in chains. What they got instead was worse. What they got instead was a young woman who looked like a schoolmistress and answered questions like one.

Did she compose the pamphlet? "I set type, sir. Composition is a different trade."

Did she deny setting the type? A pause here, measured out like medicine. "I don't deny my hands did the work."

Did she know that the writings were seditious? Another pause, longer. Ellen watched her from behind — watched the straight back and the still shoulders and thought: *she is choosing every word the way she chose every letter, one at a time, out of the case.* "I know the pamphlet named a fact that certain gentlemen wished buried. If facts are sedition, then I am guilty of nothing but arithmetic."

There was a sound in the courtroom at that — not laughter exactly, not cheering, something between the two, a low rumble like the river before the ice goes. One of the magistrates rapped for order and his rap was lost in it. Ellen found she had her teeth bared, not in anger, and made herself close her mouth.

Nathaniel Quill testified after the noon adjournment.

Ellen had hated him for a season — hated him on Gussie's behalf through the whole tangle of the bargain by the frozen river, the stolen ledgers, the question of whether he'd warned or led her into the trap on the bridge. He walked to the witness box like a man going to his own execution and gave his name, his master's name, his position, and then, without being asked in quite so many words, he gave them everything else. He had copied documents from his employer's office. He had carried them to the printer's shop on the bridge. He had known the contents of the pamphlet before it was printed and had done nothing to prevent it, and more — he had *assisted* it, because the documents proved the agent had provoked the strike to break the unions, and a man who knows a thing like that and keeps it in a drawer is a greater criminal than any printer.

"You understand," the chairman said heavily, "that you implicate yourself."

"I understood it when I copied the first page, sir."

"And why would a solicitor's clerk ruin himself?"

Quill looked up then, and found Gussie at the bar, and the whole room saw him find her. "Because I was asked to choose, sir. Between my position and the truth. I chose late, and badly, and at cost to others who deserved better. But I chose."

Ellen watched Gussie close her eyes — just for a moment, just a blink held too long — and when they opened again they were wet and furious and she did not look at him again for the rest of the day. But Ellen saw. Ellen had spent twenty-nine years reading the faces of women being cheated, and she knew what that blink was. It wasn't forgiveness. It wasn't even gratitude, not yet. It was the look of a person discovering that someone has paid a debt they didn't know was owed to them, and having to decide, right there, in front of six grey stones, whether to let it stand.

The sentence came at four o'clock. Fine of fifty pounds and six months' imprisonment in the House of Correction at Kirkmanshulme Lane.

Fifty pounds might as well have been five thousand. Six months might as well have been six years. But Ellen had sat through enough sessions in that room to know what a heavy sentence sounded like — transportation sounded like thunder, two years' hard labour sounded like a door slamming — and this was neither. This was the sound of magistrates who had counted the crowd outside their windows and decided that making a martyr of August Vane would cost more than letting her sit out a winter where no one could see her. The town's sympathy had turned, and turned things float.

Still, when they took her down, Gussie paused at the door of the dock and looked back — not at the bench, not at Quill. At the crowded back of the room. At Ellen. And she lifted her ink-stained left hand, just slightly, just enough, the way a compositor lifts a finished page off the stone to carry it to the press.

Ellen's throat closed. She nodded once, hard, like a woman agreeing to terms.

* * *

The fine was announced at fifty pounds, and by Friday evening the district had begun paying it.

Not the committee — the relief committee was dead and buried, its accounts frozen, its informer exposed and driven out past the boundary markers with his hat knocked over his eyes. No committee organized this. It organized itself, which was the thing that frightened the masters, Ellen suspected, far more than any pamphlet. A committee can be bought or broken. A habit cannot.

It started with the mill girls. Ellen went to the gates of the Atlas works on Monday to ask after hours for her sister-in-law and found a girl she knew — Betsy Hulme, seventeen, half her wages docked since the restart — standing by the gatepost with an old jam jar full of coppers, collecting. Not begging. Collecting. There was a paper pasted inside the jar's glass, written in a fair schoolroom hand: FOR THE PRINTER. A PENNY FROM EACH WHO READS.

"Betsy," Ellen said, "who set you onto this?"

"Nobody set me." The girl said it with the flat pride of the young, daring Ellen to argue. "We read it, didn't we? All of us, out loud, in the lodging house, taking turns, like scripture. My mam can't read and she made me say it twice." She shook the jar; the coppers rattled. "She put in tuppence."

By Wednesday there were jars at three mill gates and the chapel door and the corner of the bridge — the reopened bridge, its new scaffolding pale as bone, traffic rolling over it again as though nothing had ever shut. By Friday a journeyman spinner Ellen had never spoken to in her life stopped her in the street and pushed half a crown into her hand, saying only, "For the press," and walked on before she could thank him. Somebody had written to London — Ellen never learned who — and a parcel arrived at the shop containing five pounds and a note signed *A Well-Wisher to the Liberty of the Press*, which everyone correctly translated as *some radical gentleman who wants his name kept out of it.*

The shop itself was shut, chained, its windows papered over by the constables after the raid, waiting to learn whether the Crown would seize the press or sell it for costs. Ellen went and stood in front of the papered windows on Saturday and found she was not alone. There were perhaps thirty people gathered along the frontage, quiet, some of them touching the boards as they passed, the way you touch a church wall.

Sam came to find her there. Her brother, who had stood outside this same shop in December with a brick in his fist. He'd not come to apologize — Sam apologized the way ice melts, slowly and from the edges — but he stood beside her and read the notice pasted to the door about seizure for costs, and after a while he said, "Fifty pound for telling the truth about Starling's agent. And how much did they fine the agent?"

"Not a farthing," Ellen said. "Inquiry's still sitting."

"Aye." He worked his jaw. "I'll bring summat Tuesday."

That was all. That was everything. Ellen watched him go and understood that the movement — her brother in it, Betsy Hulme in it, the deacons and Mrs. Catterall and the spinner with his half-crown — had learned in one brutal winter what no speech could have taught them: that they were not alone, that they could count, that a penny from each who reads comes to more than fifty pounds faster than any master would believe. Chastened. Broken open. And discovering, with the wonder of children finding their own hands, their own strength.

* * *

On the last Sunday of March, Ellen carried the money to the magistrates' clerk herself.

It took three trips, in the end — fifty pounds in coppers weighs a great deal, and dignity requires that you not hire a cart. She went with Betsy Hulme and two of the chapel deacons, and they brought it in canvas flour sacks, and they poured it out onto the counter of the clerk's office coin by coin until the man behind the desk stopped counting and simply stared. Coppers worn smooth by ten thousand pockets. Halfpennies saved from the coal money. A few silver pieces wrapped in paper with names written on the papers — names Ellen read aloud as they came, deliberately, so the clerk would hear them: HULME. BRACK. CATTERALL. OGDEN. WHITTAKER. On and on, hundreds of names, each one a household that had eaten less so that a stranger's fine could be paid.

"What is this?" the clerk asked at last, though the notice pasted inside every jar had told him plainly enough.

"It's the fine," Ellen said. "Paid. Count it or don't."

"The prisoner is convicted of seditious libel—"

"And the town," Ellen said, "has read the libel. Every word. Out loud. Twice, some of us, for our mothers." She set the last sack down and flattened it with her palm. "You fined her fifty pounds for telling four thousand people why they starved. You'll want to write this down, sir, for your own protection: there are more than four thousand of us, and we can each spare a penny, and next time there won't be a fine big enough to hold what we know. That's not a threat. It's arithmetic."

Betsy made a noise that was almost a laugh and strangled it into a cough.

The clerk counted. It took him and his boy until dusk, and the total came to fifty-one pounds, seven and sixpence, and Ellen told him to keep the seven and six toward Gussie's keep in the House of Correction, so that her bread that winter would be bought, in however small a part, with the district's own money and not the Crown's. Then she walked out past the bridge in the last of the light, the river running loud and free below with the thaw, and stood a while at the rail where the print shop's dark windows faced the water.

Six months. October, then, or near it. Gussie would come out to a shop that was hers — her father gone from the composing stone for good, his hands finished, his blessing given — to a district that had paid her debt in coppers and would not forget it, and to a movement that had learned its own weight. And Nathaniel Quill would be waiting somewhere in it, struck off his master's books, ruined and upright, owing what he owed.

Ellen pulled her shawl tight and allowed herself, for the first time since the arrest, to think past the sentence to the spring beyond it — to presses running openly, to names in print that no magistrate could unname, to the atlas of everything that had been done to them, set in type by a left-handed woman with ink under her nails.

Then she turned toward the House of Correction, because visiting day began at nine, and she meant to be first through the gate with news, a shilling for candles, and word of the seven and sixpence — and because there was a letter in her pocket from Nathaniel Quill, marked *to be delivered only into Miss Vane's own hand*, and Ellen Brack had decided she would very much enjoy watching Gussie's face when she read it.
