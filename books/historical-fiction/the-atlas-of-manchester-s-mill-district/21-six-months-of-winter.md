## 21. Six Months of Winter

The New Bailey did not smell of anything so honest as ink. It smelled of lime wash over old damp, of boiled mutton and wet wool, of the carbolic they swabbed into the corridors each morning as though cleanliness could be pressed into a place like type into paper — hard, and leaving an impression whether you willed it or no.

Gussie learned the prison by its sounds before she learned it by its rooms. The bolt-rack at six, each lock speaking its own note, a scale descending the length of the wing. The tread-wheel's slow groan from the men's side, like a mill that ground nothing but hours. And beneath all of it, under the floor, the Irwell — still running, even now, even here. She had been inside three weeks before she understood that what she heard at night was not her own blood in her ears. It was the river. She had let it carry her mother's pamphlet away, page by page, and it had carried her sentence instead to this stone room, and still it went on talking to itself below, patient as a compositor reading proof.

Her cell was eight feet by six, with a plank bed, a stool, a slop pail, and a window set so high and so narrow that she only knew the weather by its colour on the ceiling. Six months. The magistrate had said it almost gently, the way you'd quote a price for mending a shoe. *A fine of fifty pounds and six months' imprisonment for seditious libel.* The courtroom had made a sound when he said it — not a cheer, nothing so foolish, but a shifting, a settling of four hundred people who had come expecting transportation and got arithmetic instead. Ellen Brack had been in that crowd. Gussie had seen her face go through surprise and then something harder, something that looked like planning.

They took everything from her at the gate: her stays, her boots, the ribbon off her hair. They left her the calluses. A matron ran her thumbs along Gussie's fingers, frowning at the ridges on the first joint of each — the compositor's marks, earned over ten years at the case — and asked what trade she followed.

"Printer," Gussie said.

The matron wrote it down without comment. Somewhere in the ledgers of the New Bailey there was now a line that read, in some clerk's indifferent hand: *Vane, Augusta. Trade: printer.* It struck her, even then, even frightened, that this was the first official document in her whole life that named her by her work and meant it. Her father's rate-books called her *assistant*. The parish register called her *spinster, daughter of*. The newspapers had called her *a person unknown, described as young and left-handed*. Only the prison had gotten it right.

* * *

The chaplain came round the second week with his list of women who could read and women who could not, and found Gussie sitting on her plank bed doing what she always did when her hands were empty: composing. Her fingers moved against her knee, picking invisible letters from an invisible case, setting and distributing, setting and distributing, the way another woman might worry beads.

"You're Mrs. Vane," he said. Not a question. "The printer."

"Miss Vane."

"The one from the bridge." He said it carefully, the way a man walks past ice. Then, because he was a decent man doing an impossible job with no money and less help: "We've forty women in this wing and I shouldn't wonder if thirty of them can't read their own names. The matrons haven't time. I haven't time." He looked at her knee, where her fingers were still moving. "Could you teach them?"

She should have said no. Every instinct her father had ever drilled into her — keep your head down, mind the shop, be the quiet one behind the glass — said no. But her father had not written to her in five weeks, and his silence sat in her chest like a piece of type fallen into the wrong box, rattling whenever she moved. And besides, she had spent twenty-three years being useful in secret. It was a strange, dizzying thing to be asked to be useful out loud.

"I'll want chalk," she said. "And whatever slate we can get."

He gave her both, and the long room under the chapel where the women took their exercise on wet days, and that was how the school began.

* * *

There were thirty-one of them by February. Debtors mostly, and thieves, and two women in for the same crime as half the district — taking relief while their husbands were on strike, which the magistrates had decided was riotous assembly. There was Mrs. Ogden, sixty years old, who could read the large print of the Scriptures but nothing else and wept with frustration when the small print defeated her. There was Sally Nield, seventeen, quick as a shuttle, who learned her letters in nine days and then wanted to know everything at once. There was Bridget Flannery, who spoke Irish faster than English and discovered, to her own astonishment, that she could spell better than any of them once she understood that letters held still on a page.

Gussie taught them the way her father had taught her: not letters first, but words. *Mill. Wage. Bread. Home.* Words already living in their mouths, so that when the shapes resolved on the slate they recognised them like faces across a street. Then names — their own names, spelled out letter by letter, and oh, the look of a woman seeing her own name written for the first time, holding the slate at arm's length as though it might fly away.

But the lesson they loved best, the one that drew even the women who pretended indifference, was the soap.

It began because Gussie missed type the way other prisoners missed tobacco or gin — a physical ache in the hands. Prison soap came in blocks, grey-white, dense enough to hold an edge if you were patient. With the blunt end of a spoon and a good deal of smuggling between cells, she began to carve. A lowercase *a*, body and bowl and the little stem, cut in reverse as every compositor cuts it, mirror-wise. An *e*. A *g* with its descender curling like a fern. She carved them the size of dice and laid them out on her bed-board in a line:

**B R E A D**

and showed them how you read it in a looking-glass, or turned the board around, and the word stood up out of its own reflection and became itself.

"It's backwards," Sally said, turning the soap letters in her palm, "so it comes out forwards."

"Everything worth printing is backwards first," Gussie said. "You work it wrong to make it right. That's the whole trade."

She built up a font over the weeks, hoarding her rations, trading bread for soap until the matrons noticed and chose — God bless them, she thought then and thought after — to notice slowly. By March she had forty-odd pieces, enough for short lines, and the women would queue to set their own sentences, arranging the little grey bodies on the board, checking them in the tin mirror, reading themselves into print.

Bridget Flannery set: **MY NAME IS BRIDGET FLANNERY AND I CAN READ IT NOW.**

It took the whole font and two days. When it was done she would not let Gussie distribute the letters for a full hour. She just sat looking at it, turning the board, reading it forward and backward, forwards again. Then she kissed the soap — actually kissed it, tasting of tallow and prison lime — and gave it back.

"Break it up," she said. "Set someone else's name next."

That, Gussie thought, watching the letters scatter into new hands, is more than most printers learn in a lifetime.

* * *

Nathaniel's letters came twice a month, on the second and fourth Tuesday, passed through whatever channel he had purchased — she never learned which, and he never told her, though she guessed it cost him most of his clerk's wages. They arrived on good paper, folded twice, addressed to *Mrs. Augusta Vane, prisoner*, which made her laugh aloud the first time; the matron who delivered it raised an eyebrow and said nothing.

The early ones were careful. News of the shop: *your father keeps the shutters half-closed but the smoke from the chimney says the press is warm.* News of the district: *the inquiry sits on Thursdays; the agent's ledgers are being read aloud in a room he cannot leave.* News of Ellen: *she asks after you every week and sends word that the fine stands at eleven pounds, four shillings and threepence, collected.*

He never wrote *I love you*. He wrote around it, the way you space out a tight line, easing the letters apart so the sense fits. He described the river in spring thaw. He described a window with light in it. He described waiting. Once, only once, near the bottom of a page otherwise filled with committee business, he wrote: *I have been thinking about what you said about trust being the last currency. I am spending mine slowly and hope to be solvent when you return.* She read that line perhaps a hundred times. She was not certain she forgave him — the bargains, the half-truths, the weeks on the bridge when she could not tell warning from trap. But she had decided, somewhere in the third week of her sentence, lying awake listening to the river, that certainty was a luxury for people who had never composed a page under threat of the magistrate. What she felt for Nathaniel Quill was not simple and would not be made simple by six months of stone. She kept the letters in her Bible, in order, and answered them in a hand she disguised slightly, out of habit, though no one in the New Bailey cared about hands.

Her father's letters did not come.

She told herself reasons, and the reasons were all plausible: he could not hold a pen since the stroke; he was proud; he was ashamed; writing was the one thing the tremor had taken from him entirely, the last tool lifted out of his reach. She composed his letters herself, at night, on the plank bed — she could see his hand perfectly, the old copperplate going shaky at the downstrokes — and the letters he wrote in her imagination were kind and oblique and full of type-metal metaphors, and they were worse than silence, because she had invented them, and invention was her trade, and she knew exactly how much a made thing could be trusted.

In the sixth week, Sally Nield, who had a genius for knowing things that travelled under the prison walls faster than any permitted channel, put her mouth close to Gussie's ear in the washroom and said: "Your da's shop has a boy in the window now. Setting type. Left-handed."

Gussie stood very still with the prison soap in her hands.

"Left-handed," she repeated.

"That's what they say." Sally wrung out her shift and twisted it, matter-of-fact. "Maybe he's teaching somebody."

*Maybe he's teaching somebody.* Gussie carried those four words back to her cell and turned them over all night like a piece of doubtful copy. It could be ruin — a stranger at the stone, her father's craft leaking out through untrained hands. Or it could be the opposite of ruin: her father, half-paralyzed, refusing to let the case go silent, doing the one brave ordinary thing available to him. She thought of him guiding her hand on the last line of her mother's pamphlet, his tremor stilled against hers, and decided — chose, deliberately, the way you choose a face size for a job — to believe the second thing. Belief, she was learning, was also a composition. You set it yourself.

* * *

By April the school had outgrown the exercise room. The matrons sent their own daughters' copies for correction. The debtors' wives on visiting days pressed scraps of paper into Gussie's palms with questions about spelling written on them. The chaplain, emboldened, brought her a battered spelling book missing its first forty pages, and she taught the first forty pages herself, out of her head, standing at the end of the room with thirty-one women watching her the way she had once watched her father watch the press — that particular hunger, the hunger of people who have discovered that a thing can be known and intend to know it.

She had spent her whole life believing that invisibility was safety, and that safety was enough. Now she stood in a room full of women the world had made invisible — locked up, struck off, unnamed — and she watched Sally Nield read her first complete sentence aloud in a voice that shook, and she understood that she had never once been hidden. She had simply been waiting, all those years at the case, cutting type by day and setting truth by night, for her life to find its proper measure. The prison, of all places, had given it to her. Eight feet by six, and yet the widest room she had ever worked in.

On the last Tuesday of April, Nathaniel's letter contained a single line at its foot, below the news and the careful spacing and the patience:

*The bridge reopens on the first of May. Your father has not crossed it. He waits on the far bank every morning, they say, facing the town.*

She read it four times. Then she folded it into the Bible with the others, took out her soap font, and began, one last time, to set a line on the bed-board. She chose it slowly, checking each letter in the tin mirror, and what she set was not a verse or a slogan but a date and a name, like the imprint at the foot of a title page — the line every printer sets last and means most:

**PRINTED BY AUGUSTA VANE.**

Outside, far under the floor, the Irwell ran high and brown with the thaw, carrying winter away toward the sea. In six days the gates would open. In seven, she would stand on the reopened bridge with the whole of Manchester on either side of her, and a father on the far bank, and a shop with a left-handed boy in the window, and an atlas waiting to be begun.

She distributed the type back into its soap-box rows, letter by letter, ready for the next edition.
