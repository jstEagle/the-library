## 22. The Atlas

The bridge opened on a Tuesday in April, and the whole of Ancoats came out to watch the barriers come down.

Gussie stood among them at the shop window — her shop window now, the words still strange as new type in her mouth — and watched the carpenters knock the last wedge from the last plank. The scaffolding had stood since November, grey with frost and coal smoke, and beneath it the river had done its slow work: freezing, breaking, carrying away everything that floated. The Irwell ran high and brown and ordinary, as if it had never groaned under ice, never carried a skiff by moonlight, never swallowed five hundred sheets of seditious paper in a single night.

A boy ran across the planks first, daring them to hold. They held. A cheer went up from the crowd, ragged and thin after the winter, but real.

*The only bridge out,* she thought. *And I am still here.*

She had crossed it twice already — once going into Manchester to be released, once coming back — and each time her feet had found the worn hollows in the stone without her asking, the way her hands found the type cases in the dark. Six months in Kirkmanshulme Gaol had not unlearned her body's knowledge of this place. It had only sharpened it. Everything she touched now she touched deliberately, the way you touch something you have been told you might lose.

Behind her, the shop smelled of ink and cold tea and the particular dust of lead that she had breathed since she was seven years old. Her father's chair sat empty by the composing stone. She had not moved it. She did not know if she ever would.

* * *

They had turned her out on the first fine morning, a constable holding the door, a wardress pressing her bundle of belongings into her arms — apron, shawl, the soap-carved letters wrapped in a scrap of linen like relics, which is what they were.

"Six months for paper," the wardress had said, not unkindly, at the gate. "You'll do it again, though."

"Yes," Gussie had said. "I expect so."

The wardress had almost smiled.

Now, standing in the shop with the door open to the spring air and the noise of the reopened bridge, Gussie took down the shutters one by one and let the light in. It fell across the press — Nathaniel's press, technically; the one Ellen had moved by barge along the frozen canal while Gussie waited to be arrested — and across the composing stone where her father had fallen in January, his hand still resting on a half-set line when they carried him out.

He was at Ellen Brack's sister's house in Bradford now, in a room with a south window, being fed broth and read to from the Bible he had never once opened in health. He could not write. His right side would not answer him, and the tremor that had been creeping into his hands for years had become a permanent shaking, as if the stroke had merely made visible what grief had always been doing to him.

But in his last lucid hour before they took him away, he had gripped her left hand — her good hand, her working hand — with his ruined one, and said, in a voice like paper tearing:

*"Your mother cut the block. You set the page. Finish it."*

And then, when she thought he had slipped back into silence: *"It was never my shop, Gussie. It was always hers. I just kept the door open."*

She kept the door open now. That was the whole of her inheritance, and it was enough.

* * *

The first thing she set, on the first morning of her freedom, was not a pamphlet and not a bill and not an issue of *The Bridge*, which had continued in other hands during her imprisonment — badly, Ellen reported, the deacons arguing over every comma — but a single line, set large, in the case her mother had reclaimed:

**VANE & VANE, PRINTERS.**

Her father's name above hers. She had considered leaving off the ampersand, considered putting her name alone, considered a dozen small revenges and renunciations. But standing at the stone with the stick in her hand, she found she wanted none of them. *Vane & Vane.* Two names, joined by a character that meant *and* — the most honest word in any language, and the hardest to live up to. Let the district read it how it liked. She knew what it meant: the dead woman who had cut woodcuts against the yeomanry, the broken man who had kept the shop through thirty years of compromise, and the daughter who had gone to gaol rather than let the truth be burned. One name, three bearers.

She locked the line into the chase and printed fifty impressions on the proofing press, and pasted the first one in the window where the whole bridge could see it, where the magistrates could see it, where anyone at all could see it, openly, legally, without fear.

Her hands shook setting it. Not from the tremor — that was her father's affliction, not hers, though some mornings now she woke with her fingers twitching and had to hold them flat against the stone until they stilled — but from the strangeness of printing something she was not afraid of. For six months, every sheet had been contraband. Every word had been a sentence waiting to be served. Now the sun came through the window and lay across the forme like a blessing, and no one was coming, and the not-being-afraid felt almost like falling.

She would get used to it. She intended to get used to it.

* * *

Ellen Brack came at noon, with flour and a leg of mutton that Gussie suspected had been "found," and stood in the doorway looking at the shop as if checking whether it had survived the winter the way its owner had.

"You're thin," Ellen said.

"You're not."

"I'm feeding four on relief wages and a husband who thinks striking is a religion." Ellen set the basket down and looked around, cataloguing — Gussie could see her doing it, the weaver's eye taking stock of warp and weft — the press, the cases, the empty chair. "You're keeping it, then."

"It's mine. Father signed it over before—" She stopped. Before the trial, when it looked like she might hang instead of serve six months, and old Mr. Quill — Nathaniel's father, that other Quill, the respectable one — had drawn up the papers without fee and without comment, which was its own kind of message. "It's mine."

"And what does a printer print," Ellen asked, "when there's no strike to print?"

Gussie reached under the counter and brought out the bundle she had been working on in her head for six months: sheets of foolscap covered in prison pencil, lists and sketches and columns of figures, soft at the folds from handling.

"An atlas," she said.

Ellen looked at her. "Of where?"

"Of here." She spread the sheets on the composing stone. Street by street, mill by mill, cellar dwelling by cellar dwelling — every name she had gathered, hers and Ellen's and the chapel deacons' and the mill girls' who had passed *The Bridge* hand to hand under their shawls. "Every street in the district. Every mill, and who owns it, and who works it, and what the wage is, and what it was in 1839, and 1826, and 1819. Every house that lost a child in the fever winter. Every man blacklisted after the lockout. Every name on the relief committee and every name that informed." She tapped the pages. "Not a map of streets, Ellen. A map of what happened. Nobody's written us down yet. We've been in every newspaper in England this winter, and not one of them has printed our names except as *the operatives*. So I'll print them. All of them. Ourselves, in our own book."

Ellen picked up a sheet and read it the way she read everything — slowly, completely, lips moving. Then she put it down.

"They'll call it sedition."

"Some of it will be. The true parts." Gussie smiled, and found that the smile did not hurt anymore. "But most of it is just names and numbers, and names and numbers are legal. That's the beauty of it. They can't prosecute a census. And even if they could—" she laid her hand flat on the stone, on the wood of it, on all the work that had passed across it — "it would be too late. That's what I learned in gaol. You can seize a press. You can burn a run. But once a thing is printed and given away, it lives in people's heads, and there's no magistrate alive who can raid a memory."

Ellen was quiet a moment. Outside, the sound of carts crossing the newly opened bridge — coal carts, mail carts, the world's traffic resuming — filled the pause like a held breath let out.

"My brother'll want his name in it," she said at last. "For the window-smashing he didn't do. He's very proud of that."

"He can have a footnote. *Distinguished himself in the matter of the glazier's bill.*"

That got the laugh out of her — the short, hard laugh that had gotten them both through the worst months — and then Ellen did something Gussie had never seen her do: she took off her shawl, hung it on the peg by the door, and rolled up her sleeves.

"Show me your case," she said. "I can set a line if you'll teach me the measure. Four thousand hands turned out this winter, and half of them can't read what they struck for. If you're making an atlas, I'm not letting it be a book for folk who already have books."

* * *

Nathaniel came at dusk, when the light went long and gold across the water and the bridge threw its shadow clean across the river for the first time in a year.

She heard his step on the boards outside — she would have known it anywhere now, that quick clerk's step — and did not turn from the case.

"The shop's open," she said. "I'm told the proprietor takes commissions."

"So I hear." He came and stood beside her at the stone, close enough that she could smell the road on him — he'd walked from Manchester, she guessed, rather than take the omnibus like a man with his prospects intact. His prospects were not intact. Everyone in the district knew by now what he had done in the magistrate's room: stood up in his master's presence and testified that he had copied the ledgers himself, that the agent Hobson's provocation of the lockout was documented in his own employer's hand, that he had passed them to the printer of *The Bridge* knowingly and would pass them again tomorrow. They had dismissed him within the hour. His father had not spoken to him since Christmas. He had lost everything a solicitor's clerk could lose except his liberty, and he had spent those six months writing her letters — two a week, steady as a press — that she had answered with one for every three, because she was learning, slowly, what she wanted and did not want, and she refused to want anything out of gratitude or guilt.

"How is the unemployment?" she asked, still sorting type.

"Chronic. Incurable. My father says I may come home when I renounce 'that woman and her filthy paper.'" He glanced at the window, where the *VANE & VANE* impression hung. "I've taken rooms over the chandler's on Redhill Street. There's a view of the canal and a landlady who thinks I'm a martyr, which is better than my father thinks I am."

"Nathaniel."

"Gussie." He said her name carefully, the way he'd said it across the frozen river in January, when she couldn't tell whether he was warning her or walking her into the trap — and she had learned since that he genuinely did not always know himself, that a man raised to serve two masters could spend his whole life unsure which hand his own belonged to. "I'm not here to bargain. I promised myself I wouldn't. Whatever I say next, remember that."

"Then say it."

He looked at the atlas sheets spread on the stone, and she watched him read them — really read, the way he read everything, hungrily, as if information were bread — and she watched the idea arrive in his face before he spoke it, because she had known it was coming for six months, had perhaps known it since the night he first stood in this doorway with clean cuffs and silver in his palm.

"A gazetteer of the district. Owners, wages, agents, rents. Cross-referenced." His voice had gone quick, the organizing voice, the voice that had built a strike committee out of nothing in a frozen winter. "Do you understand what that is? Not a pamphlet — a pamphlet burns. A *reference*. Every negotiation, every grievance committee, every future strike for fifty years will open that book first. You wouldn't be writing history, Gussie. You'd be writing the tool history gets made with."

"I know," she said. "That's why I'm doing it."

"And you'll need—"

"Distribution. Capital. Someone who knows every ledger in Manchester and can't be bought because he's already lost everything buyable." She finally turned and looked at him. He was thinner too. The clean cuffs were gone; his shirt cuffs were frayed, and there was ink on his fingers — real ink, workingman's stains, and she saw that he had noticed her noticing, and did not hide it. "I don't need a partner, Nathaniel. Partners split things. I need a correspondent. Someone who sends me what's true and lets me decide what gets printed. Can you work under those terms?"

Something moved through his face — hurt, and then the harder thing underneath it, which was respect. On the frozen river he had offered her alliance with terms attached: his control of the press in exchange for his protection. She had refused, and survived anyway, and been convicted anyway, and come out free anyway. He knew now that she did not need rescuing, and a lesser man would have resented it.

"Yes," he said. "Those terms suit me exactly. I've had enough of deciding what's true for other people. I'd rather just find it."

"Then find it," she said, "and bring it here."

He stayed while she locked up the forme for the day, handing her furniture and quoins when her hands were full, moving around the small shop as if he had always belonged in it — and perhaps he had, she thought; perhaps every conspirator belongs, afterward, wherever the conspiracy lived. At the door he paused, and for a moment she thought he would say something about them — about the charged, restrained, unfinished thing between them that six months of letters had neither built nor demolished — and she found she was glad when he didn't. There would be time. They had both learned the value of a thing set slowly, line by line, in its proper order.

"Thursday," he said. "I'll bring the toll records from the bridge office. They list every cart that crossed in November and December — including the ones carrying the mill owners' goods while the workers starved. Names and dates."

"Names and dates," she agreed. "My favourite kind of sedition."

He laughed, and went out across the bridge, and she watched him go until the dusk took him.

* * *

That night she worked late.

She lit two candles — extravagance, but she was done with economizing on light — and pulled her mother's case down from the shelf: the old type, reclaimed from the drawer of woodcuts, letters cast in 1819 and older than she was. She had printed *The Bridge* from these letters. She had set her mother's unfinished pamphlet from them, the final page guided by her father's shaking hand, and seen the run go out on the breaking ice. The letters were worn, some of them, faces rounded by a century of impression, and they printed soft, with a faint halo, like words remembered rather than spoken.

She set the opening of the atlas in them. Not the title page — that would be modern type, clear, legal, undeniable. This was for the inside. The dedication, though dedications to oneself were vanity, and she was not vain, only determined:

*To the memory of Margaret Vane, who cut the truth in wood when there was no press to print it; and to Ellen Brack, who moved it along the frozen canal; and to the four thousand.*

She read the line over twice. Changed nothing. Locked it up.

Through the window, the bridge lay pale in the moonlight, its new planks silvered, its old stones dark beneath. Somewhere across the water a mill whistle sounded the hour — eleven — and the sound went out over the district like a stone into still water, touching every street she meant to write down. Redhill Street and Canal Street, Bengal Buildings and the Old Mill, every court and entry and cellar stair. Every name. Hers to gather, hers to set, hers to give away.

They had tried to make her invisible, and then they had tried to make her an example, and they had failed at both, because a person who prints cannot be either. Ink under the nails. Her mother's phrase, from the drawer, from the beginning. Wear it like a wedding ring.

Gussie Vane dipped her ball, wiped the forme, and began to print the first page of the atlas of Ancoats — openly, in daylight tomorrow, and every day after — and outside her window the river ran free, carrying the spring away downstream, keeping its secrets, needing none.
