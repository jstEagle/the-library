## 1. The Weight of Salt

The tide went out before the sun did its work on the water, and I was in the shallows off the ama divers' beach with my skirts pinned up and my feet in the cold.

I had learned to hold my breath the way my mother must have held hers — not bravely, but economically. A breath is a currency. You spend it where it buys the most. Down among the rocks at low tide, in the green half-light where the loose pearls hide in the cracks like coins dropped by a careless god, I spent mine carefully, three seconds, five, seven, and came up with salt in my teeth and nothing in my fist. The third dive gave me one: small, misshapen, the color of weak tea. Not a fortune. A breakfast, if the broker at the fish market was in a generous mood, which he never was before six o'clock.

My mother dove for pearls. That much was true of her, and true of the water, and true of the morning; it was the only inheritance I could claim openly, because no clerk ever asked a question about a dead diver's daughter that began and ended at the waterline. They asked other questions instead. Those were the ones I answered with paper.

I dressed behind Sato-baasan's drying racks, wrung the sea out of my hair, and became someone else by degrees — stockings first, then the collar buttoned to the throat, then the forged papers folded flat against my ribs where they sat all day like a second heart, beating wrong. My name on those papers was Rosalind Amberdale, English father, English mother, born under a flag I had never seen. It was a good forgery. Kenji had made it, and Kenji made nothing carelessly. But every document is a door, and every door can be knocked upon.

The Meridian Trading Company's counting house stood above the harbor like a man who has never once been wet. White stone, green shutters, a clock that was always four minutes fast because Mr. Pell believed punctuality was a form of prayer. I arrived at five minutes past eight, which was four minutes late, and he noted it in the margin of his day-book without looking up.

"Miss Amberdale."

"Mr. Pell."

"The Kowloon manifest. Column four. Reconcile it before noon."

I reconciled columns the way I dove: economically, breath held, eyes moving over the figures for anything that glittered wrong. Column four did not reconcile. Forty crates of ceramic ware, consigned to Shanghai — but ceramic ware weighs light, and the freight charged against it weighed heavy, heavy as men. I said nothing. I wrote the discrepancy down nowhere, and remembered it everywhere.

That is the rule I live by. Busy hands, closed mouth. The hands copy figures; the mouth stays shut; the heart, folded flat beside the forged papers, keeps its own ledger, in its own cipher, and waits.

At noon I ate rice from a lacquered box and thought of my mother's hands, rough as rope and gentle as water, and of how she had gone into the sea off Iojima and not come back, and how the sea keeps what it takes without issuing receipts.

At four o'clock Mr. Pell locked the ledgers away. At four o'clock I walked home along the quay with the tide coming in, erasing everything, the way tides do — except what is written down, and what is remembered, and what waits beneath the water for a hand foolish enough to reach for it.

I reached for it again the next morning. That is also a rule.
