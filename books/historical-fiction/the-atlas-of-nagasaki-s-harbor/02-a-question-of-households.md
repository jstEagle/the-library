## 2. A Question of Households

The notice went up on the tenement's rain-warped board on a Tuesday, pasted there by a boy from the ward office who ran through his rounds the way boys run everywhere in Nagasaki — as though the whole city were a game he was losing. *Household registry inspection. All residents must present themselves.* The ink had already begun to bleed at the edges before anyone bothered to read it.

Rosalind Amberdale read it twice, standing in the alley with her market basket on her hip and mackerel scales drying silver on her knuckles.

She had known this day would come. Every year it came, and every year it passed her by like a ship that did not stop at her pier. But this year the paper looked different — heavier stock, an official seal she didn't recognize, and beneath the printed lines, a handwritten note in a small, precise hand: *All persons of foreign or mixed descent will be recorded separately.*

The basket handle bit into her fingers. She made herself breathe out slowly, the way Kenji had taught her when she was seven and learning that fear was a thing you could hold in your lungs and release on your own terms.

*Busy hands, closed mouth,* he always said. *A woman whose hands are full has no time to tremble, and a woman whose mouth is closed gives them nothing to write down.*

She bought what she needed — rice, dried kelp, a twist of cheap tobacco for Sato-baasan, who smoked it rolled in newspaper and claimed it kept her eyes sharp enough to see pearls through six feet of green water. Then she climbed the tenement stairs two at a time, as she always did, because climbing slowly gave a body too much time to think.

* * *

Their rooms occupied the third floor's north corner: two chambers separated by a paper screen gone soft as cloth with age, a cooking alcove, and a window that looked out over a wilderness of rooftops toward the harbor, where the masts rose and swayed like a forest deciding whether to walk into the sea. It was not much. It was everything.

Kenji Watanabe sat at his low desk when she came in, as he sat every evening, copying letters for merchants too proud to write their own English correspondence and too poor to hire the big firms. His brush moved with the patience of a man who had learned, long ago, that speed invited error and error invited ruin. He was sixty-one now and looked older in the lamplight, his spine curved like a drawn bow, his hands spotted and precise.

He glanced up when she entered. He always knew, before she said a word, whether the day had been kind.

"The notice," she said, setting the basket down. "On the board."

Something shifted behind his eyes — not surprise; Kenji was rarely surprised — but a settling, like a man adjusting his weight on ground he suspected might give way. "So they've come to our street at last."

"It says mixed descent will be recorded separately."

"I read it." He laid down his brush and squared it on its rest with a small, deliberate click. "Ishida."

"You know him?"

"Everyone knows Ishida." Kenji's voice was flat in the way she had learned meant deep water beneath. "He is not a cruel man. That is what makes him dangerous. A cruel man can be paid. A careful man cannot."

Rosalind began unpacking the basket, because her hands needed occupation. Rice into the crock. Kelp hung by the stove. The tobacco wrapped in cloth and set aside for the morning, when she would carry it down to Sato-baasan's room at the end of the hall. "When?"

"Tomorrow, most likely. They work the hill streets first, then come down. Ours is a poor ward; we are never first." He said it without bitterness, the way one remarks that rain falls downward. "You'll answer him plainly, Rosalind. You'll be tired, polite, slightly stupid about paperwork. Stupidity is armor. Cleverness draws the brush."

"And my name?"

"Your name is Rosalind Amberdale." He recited it the way a priest recites scripture — worn smooth by repetition. "Daughter of Thomas Amberdale, clerk, deceased, of the firm of Glover & Company. Your mother—" the pause was barely perceptible, a hairline crack across the sentence, "—was Japanese. She died when you were four. You were raised by your father until his death, then by me, his old colleague, out of Christian charity and friendship. Your papers say so."

"My papers lie."

Kenji looked at her then, fully, and in the lamplight his face was all the years she had ever known him. "Your papers tell the truth badly," he said. "That is different. Now eat. Tomorrow you will want the strength of looking well-fed and unremarkable."

* * *

They came at mid-morning, which she had not expected. Census men, she supposed, were like tides — no use predicting them by yesterday's habit.

There were two of them: a junior clerk carrying the ledger box, and behind him a man of perhaps forty-five in a good but unfashionable dark kimono, Western shoes despite the mud of the alleys, and spectacles that caught the thin light and turned his eyes into two pale coins. He carried nothing except a notebook bound in oilcloth, and he carried it the way other men carried swords.

"This is Mr. Ishida," the junior clerk announced, in the tone of someone presenting a superior he himself found slightly frightening. "Ward registry office."

"We've had the notice," Rosalind said, stepping back from the doorway. "Please come in. Forgive the mess — I was just going out to the wash."

She was not just going out to the wash. There was no wash. But the lie sat easily on her tongue these days, worn like a stone worn smooth, and it performed its office: it made her a busy woman interrupted, a little flustered, entirely ordinary.

The rooms took them in. Kenji had gone out at dawn — to the temple, he'd said, to make arrangements — and his absence was a held breath in the corner where his desk stood empty, brush squared precisely on its rest, everything in order, everything arranged to say: *nothing here is hidden, because nothing here exists worth hiding.*

Mr. Ishida removed his shoes without being asked, aligned them neatly beside hers, and stepped up onto the worn tatami. He did not look around the room. That was the first wrong note. Men who meant only their business looked around; men who meant more than their business did not need to.

"You are Rosalind Amberdale," he said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"Born where?"

"Nagasaki. Oura district." This much was true, or true enough — born in a room above a boatyard that had since burned, to a woman whose name appeared on no document Rosalind had ever been permitted to touch.

"Father?"

"Thomas Amberdale. Clerk. Deceased." She let her shoulders droop a fraction, let her voice take on the flatness of grief long since packed away in boxes. "I don't remember much of him, sir. I was young when he passed."

"Mm." Ishida opened the oilcloth notebook. The junior clerk crouched by the door with the ledger box open at his knees, pen ready. "Raised then by...?"

"Watanabe Kenji-san. My father's colleague. He's been more a grandfather to me than anything else." She gestured to the empty desk. "He's at the temple this morning. If you require him—"

"Not yet." The word *yet* lay on the floor between them like a dropped coin. Ishida made a small notation — she couldn't see what — and continued in the mild voice of a man reading a shopping list. "Occupation?"

"Bookkeeper's assistant. Meridian Trading Company, Dejima offices. Three years."

"Three years." He wrote it. "And before?"

"Diving work. Seasonal. As a girl." She watched him carefully and saw the pen hesitate — a fractional stall, the nib pressing a half-beat too long so that the ink bloomed. Diving work. Women who dove came from certain families, certain beaches, certain names. She felt the trap close a joint at a time, elegant as a crab's claw, and understood she had been maneuvered into mentioning the sea herself. *Slightly stupid about paperwork*, Kenji had said. But she had answered the diving question too readily, too honestly, because honesty was the deepest disguise she owned and she reached for it the way a drowning woman reaches for driftwood.

"Which waters?" Ishida asked mildly.

"The usual. Iojima side, mostly. Whatever paid."

"Your mother's family were divers, then."

It was not a question either. That was the second wrong note, and this one rang.

Rosalind laughed — a short, tired sound, pitched perfectly between embarrassment and indifference. "Sir, my mother died when I was four. I have her face in my own mirror and nothing else. Whatever her family was, it ended before I could ask."

Ishida regarded her over the rims of his spectacles. Up close, without the light catching them, his eyes were not pale coins at all but a warm brown, almost soft, and that softness frightened her worse than coldness would have. Coldness she could fight. Kindness in a man doing this work was a hook dressed as bread.

"One last matter," he said. "The registry lists your household under Watanabe Kenji, retired interpreter, single occupant, amended 1891 to include a dependent minor female, foreign father, unnamed." He turned back a page or two with a dry whisper of paper. "The amendment requires a surname. Someone wrote 'Amberdale.' But the original column — the column for the dependent's parentage — is blank. Blank columns are against regulation. Regulations were tightened this year. So I am required to ask formally, and to record formally:" he looked at her directly, and his voice remained mild as bathwater, "the name of your father. Full name, place of origin, and the date and manner of his entry into Japan."

The room became very quiet. Somewhere below, a fishwife was screaming cheerfully about the price of squid, and a gull argued with her, and the harbor bells marked the quarter hour, and all of it happened in another country.

"Thomas Amberdale," Rosalind said. Her voice did not shake. She had spent three years learning to make numbers sit still in columns that refused to balance; she could make a voice sit still too. "Of Liverpool, England. Entered Japan — I believe in 1874, through Shanghai, with the tea trade. He died in '86. Of fever. There was a doctor — Dr. Hansen, off the Hizen-cho — if you wish to verify."

She had rehearsed this. She and Kenji had rehearsed it a dozen times over five years, refining dates, anchoring lies to verifiable stones. Dr. Hansen existed. The fever year existed. Thomas Amberdale existed — once, briefly, a real clerk of a real firm who had really sailed home in '85 and drowned, by terrible coincidence, in the Mersey, leaving behind a name that a desperate man had borrowed for an orphaned child with a diver's black hair and a foreigner's grey eyes.

But Ishida did not write any of it down immediately. He sat with the pen poised, watching her, and in that pause Rosalind felt something she had not felt since childhood: the sensation of being *read*. Not her papers, not her forged registry line, not her careful costume of tired respectability — but her. The set of her shoulders, which Sato-baasan swore was a diver's shoulders even in wool. The way her hands had gone still instead of busy. The grey eyes that no Japanese father and no English mother of record should have produced in quite that shade.

"You have your mother's coloring," Ishida said quietly. "In the cheekbones."

"Everyone tells me I favor my father."

"I'm sure." And then — finally — he wrote. The pen scratched for a long time, longer than the answers warranted, and Rosalind stood with her arms folded and her heart beating somewhere high in her throat while a stranger's brush decided things about her life she had spent a decade keeping unwritten.

He closed the notebook. Wrapped it. Rose.

"Thank you for your cooperation, Amberdale-san." He stepped down and put on his shoes, aligning them again, and paused with his hand on the doorframe. He did not turn fully around; he spoke to the alley outside, as if to the morning itself.

"For what it is worth — and it may be worth nothing — the registry bureau does not invent questions. Every question I asked today was given to me to ask. When a blank column troubles men above me, it is usually because they already know what should fill it, and they wish to learn who else knows." He straightened his collar. "Keep your accounts clean, Amberdale-san. All of them."

Then he was gone, down the stairs, his junior clerk trotting after, and the fishwife below had moved on to arguing about eels, and the ordinary morning poured back into the room like water into a diver's emptied lungs.

* * *

She did not move for a long time.

Then she crossed to the window and looked down into the alley, and saw him standing at its mouth, talking to no one in particular — just standing, the oilcloth notebook held loosely at his side — before walking unhurriedly toward the harbor road. Toward Dejima. Toward, she realized with a slow cold spreading under her ribs, the direction of the Meridian counting house.

*They already know what should fill it.*

Kenji returned at dusk. She heard his step on the stairs — slower than it used to be, but even, always even — and she had tea ready because her hands demanded work and tea was honest work. He sat, accepted the cup, and studied her face for the length of one breath.

"He came early," Kenji said.

"He asked my father's name. Full name. Entry date. Manner of entry." She kept her voice level, reporting figures. "I gave him the story. All of it. He wrote it down." Her hands tightened around her own cup. "But Kenji-ojiisan — he asked about my mother's cheekbones. And he said the questions came from above him. And then he walked toward the harbor."

The old man set his cup down with great care, as though the saucer might otherwise break, or as though he might.

"Thirty years ago," he said at last, "there was a census in this city, and a man asked careful questions about households on the waterfront, and within a season three diving families had lost their boats, their licenses, and two of them their daughters — sold south with debts no one could produce the papers for. The man who asked those questions was not wicked. He was thorough. And thoroughness, in the wrong ledger, is worth more than wickedness, because wickedness can be bribed and thoroughness can only be fed."

"Are you saying he works for them? The company?"

"I am saying," Kenji said, "that I do not know, and that not-knowing is the most expensive thing a person like us can carry." He looked at the empty desk where his brush waited, at the girl he had raised lying to officials in a voice steadier than his own had ever been, and his old hands folded together on his knees. "We assumed the wall was holding because no one knocked. Today a man knocked politely, wrote something down, and left. We do not know what he wrote. That is how walls fall, Rosalind. Not with fire. With a pen."

Outside, the harbor lights were coming on one by one across the black water, and somewhere among them the Meridian counting house still burned its late lamp over its beautiful, balanced books — and somewhere in a ward office, in an oilcloth notebook, in a hand small and precise as a surgeon's, a line about a girl named Amberdale was drying in the ink.

Rosalind washed the teacups. Busy hands. Closed mouth.

But that night, for the first time in years, she took her mother's one relic from its hiding place beneath the floorboard — a scrap of silk, salt-stiff, embroidered with a pattern of waves — and held it a long while before the banked coals, listening to the tide she could not see, counting the hours until dawn, when she would walk down to Dejima and unlock the counting house doors and smile at Mr. Pell and open the ledgers as if the world were still the shape she had built it into.

As if no one had written anything down.
