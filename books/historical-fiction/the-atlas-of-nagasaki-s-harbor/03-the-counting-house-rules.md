## 3. The Counting House Rules

The counting house of the Meridian Trading Company smelled of ink and camphor and old money — a smell Rosalind had come to think of as the smell of other people's certainty. It rose from the floorboards themselves, from the waxed cedar of the long desks, from the leather spines of ledgers that went back further than she had been alive. Outside, the harbor worked and shouted and stank of fish and coal; inside, everything was quiet as a chapel, and twice as devout.

She arrived at half past seven, before Mr. Pell, as she did every morning. This was rule one, though it had never been written down: be there first, so that no one ever sees you arrive, only finds you already present, already bent over your figures, already harmless.

She hung her coat on the peg by the door — the plain grey coat, deliberately shapeless, chosen to make her seem smaller than she was — and lit the lamp at her desk in the corner, the one with the worst light and the best view of the door. Then she opened yesterday's page and checked her own work against her memory of it, because rule two was: trust no arithmetic you did not do twice, including your own.

By the time the clerks began filtering in — Yamada with his cough, Oka-san with his hair oil, the two junior boys who whispered about her when they thought she couldn't hear — she was a fixture, part of the furniture of numbers. Nobody looked at furniture. That was the whole architecture of her life, built plank by plank since she was fourteen years old: busy hands, closed mouth, eyes down. A woman who wrote beautiful figures and asked for nothing. A woman whose papers said *Amberdale, R., assistant bookkeeper* and whose papers were lies, but lies so carefully constructed, so patiently aged with tea-staining and borrowed seals, that even she sometimes caught herself believing them.

Mr. Pell came at nine exactly. She heard him before she saw him, because his boots had a particular sound on the boards — not the shuffle of the Japanese clerks in their tabi, but the firm English heel-and-toe of a man who believed floors existed to be commanded. Thomas Pell, second supervisor of accounts, forty-one years old, thin as a mast, with a face like a closed ledger himself: ruled lines, no margin for feeling.

"Amberdale." He never used her given name. She suspected he didn't know it.

"Good morning, sir."

"The Kwantung consignment came back with queries from Shanghai. You'll sit with me this morning. Bring your pen and don't bring your opinions."

"Yes, sir."

That last instruction was, in its way, a kindness. Pell gave it to everyone. *Don't bring your opinions* meant *your opinions are worth nothing here*, which was the one thing in Nagasaki Rosalind fully agreed with.

* * *

They sat together at the high desk by the window, and for three hours he drilled her the way he drilled all his assistants — which is to say, without mercy and without malice, like weather.

"A shipment leaves here marked ceramic ware. It arrives in Shanghai weighed, and the weight is entered against the manifest. If the weights disagree, what does that tell you?"

"That someone made an error."

"Someone always makes an error. Errors are the salt of commerce. What do you *do* about it?"

"You reconcile. You trace the entry back through the daybook to the original tally, you find where the error entered, you correct it, and you initial the correction."

"And if you cannot find where it entered?"

"Then you report it upward."

"To whom?"

"To you, sir."

He almost smiled. It was the closest thing to warmth the counting house produced, and it happened perhaps four times a year. "And if I tell you the error is nothing? A rounding of tonnage, a clerk's fatigue, a matter beneath the company's attention?"

Rosalind kept her eyes on the column. "Then I would write *reconciled* and initial it."

"Why?"

"Because the company's books belong to the company."

"Because," Pell said, tapping the desk once, "a ledger is a machine for producing agreement. That is all it is. Not truth — agreement. Truth is above my pay grade and yours. Now: take the October folios and check them against the Shanghai returns. Every line. I want discrepancies flagged in red, and I want you to understand—" he paused, and something crossed his face too quickly for her to read it, "—I want you to understand that some discrepancies are simply noise. Don't fall in love with them."

"I don't love anything, sir," she said, deadpan, and was rewarded with the ghost of the smile again before he walked away.

But she had been raised by Kenji Watanabe, and Kenji had taught her that when a man tells you not to look at something, the looking has already begun.

* * *

The October folios were thick as bricks. She carried them to her corner desk and began, and for two hours the work was only work — dates, tonnages, marks, initials, the small honest tedium of commerce. Ceramic ware, ceramic ware, cotton piece goods, ceramic ware. Meridian shipped porcelain out of Nagasaki the way other houses shipped coal; it was their respectable trade, their flagship cargo, and every entry was beautifully kept, every figure aligned, every sum correct.

It was the correctness that finally snagged her. Not an error — the opposite of an error. A perfection too smooth, like a pearl with no grit at its heart.

The freight charges, she noticed, were calculated on displacement. Standard practice; everyone billed by weight or measure. And the manifests recorded weight, and the weights reconciled against the Shanghai returns, and all of it matched, matched, matched — except that the *charges* told a different story than the *weights*. A crate listed at 340 pounds paid freight calculated on 640. Another at 410 pounds, charged on 780. Always nearly double. Never exactly double. Never so far off that a lazy auditor would blink, and always, always reconciled somewhere downstream by a balancing entry — a discount applied, a surcharge reversed, a figure adjusted and initialed until the columns kissed each other and agreed.

Errors, Mr. Pell had said, are the salt of commerce. But these weren't errors. These were seams, stitched shut by expert hands.

She sat very still and let the counting house go quiet around her. Somewhere behind her Yamada coughed; somewhere a pen scratched; outside a gull screamed over the masts. She turned back through the folio, then forward again, and began keeping her own private tally on a scrap of paper she kept beneath blotter — not the figures themselves, she was too careful for that, but a count. How many crates of ceramic ware paid freight on weight they did not have.

Fourteen, out of sixty-one entries in October alone.

She thought of a hold. She couldn't help it; the arithmetic drew the picture whether she wanted it or not. A crate of porcelain at 340 pounds is a large crate but not a vast one — it stacks, it ships flat, it takes up room. Whatever else was being carried under those names took up nearly twice the room. Nearly twice the space. Nearly twice the —

She stopped the thought at the waterline, the way her mother must once have stopped a dive, turning back before the lungs gave out. But the shape of it stayed with her anyway, the way a diver sees shapes below her that she chooses not to name.

Human beings weigh about the same per person, give or take, as a good crate of porcelain. That was the obscene arithmetic of it, and once it was in her head it would not leave. Crouched bodies, packed close, breathing through gaps between boxes marked *KUTANI WARE*. Freight billed on flesh at ceramic rates plus a little extra, because the syndicate — whoever they were — charged for the risk.

*Rosalind Amberdale,* she told herself sternly, *you are a bookkeeper's assistant with forged papers and a talent for silence. You balance columns. That is all you do.*

She wrote *reconciled* in the margin of the first discrepancy, as instructed. Her hand did not shake, and she was proud of that, and ashamed of the pride, and did both simultaneously while her face showed nothing at all.

* * *

At midday she ate rice and pickled radish at her desk, as always, and used the quiet hour to think, as always.

Kenji would know what it meant. Kenji had been an interpreter for a British trading house before Meridian, before whatever scandal had broken him — he never spoke of it, but she had grown up watching him flinch at certain words, certain letterheads, certain ships' names spoken aloud in the market. He had taught her English from smuggled novels and figures from a slate, and above all he had taught her caution: *A number is a confession, little one. Every ledger confesses eventually. Your task is to be elsewhere when it does.*

She was not elsewhere. She was at the center of it, holding the pen.

She looked across the room at Pell. He stood at the high desk with his back to the window, checking the November daybooks, and in the flat white light he looked less like a commander and more like a man standing watch — tired around the mouth in a way she'd never let herself notice before. When Yamada brought him a folio, Pell took it and said something quiet, and Yamada nodded and went away comforted. Pell noticed things. That was his profession. Which meant either that Pell did not see the seams in his own ledgers — impossible, the man could smell a misaligned digit through leather — or that Pell saw them every day and had learned, as she was learning this very hour, to write *reconciled* and initial it and keep his opinions off the page.

Which made him what? A coward? An accomplice? A fellow prisoner?

She didn't know, and the not-knowing frightened her more than the figures did. A villain you can fear cleanly. A man who is kind to you and might be damned is much worse company.

* * *

The afternoon passed in the rhythm of drills. Pell tested her on exchange conversions, on the difference between the Yokohama rate and the Shanghai rate, on how to enter a loss so that it appeared as deferred profit without technically lying — "because technically lying," he said, "is what we have lawyers for." She answered well. She always answered well. It was the safest way to be invisible: be excellent, so no one ever has a reason to look closer at the girl behind the excellence.

Near four o'clock he paused beside her desk with the October folio under his arm — the very folio, she realized, her stomach tightening — and stood looking down at her red flags.

"You found fourteen," he said.

"Yes, sir. All reconciled downstream. I noted the balancing entries."

"Mm." He turned pages slowly. His finger stopped at one — the fourth of October, the crate at 340 paying on 640 — and rested there a moment longer than reading required. "You counted them."

"They seemed worth counting."

"Why?"

She chose her words the way she chose her steps on the ama divers' rocks: testing each one before bearing weight. "Because a pattern isn't an error, sir. Errors scatter. Patterns point."

Something moved behind his eyes — she would spend many nights afterward trying to name it, and would settle finally on *recognition*, the look of a man hearing his own secret said back to him in a young voice. He closed the folio.

"Rule seven, Amberdale," he said quietly. "Patterns point. That's true. And when a pattern points at something above your station, you note it, you file it, and you forget it. The men who own those patterns own ships, banks, and several officials whose names you'd recognize from the newspaper. They are not errors. Errors cannot ruin you." He set the folio on her desk, square with its edge. "Redo the flags in pencil. We'll call them rounding variances."

"Yes, sir."

He walked away. At the high desk he stopped, half-turned, and added, in a tone so ordinary it might have been about the weather:

"You're the best I've had in eleven years. That's why I'm telling you the rules instead of just enforcing them. There's a difference, and someday — not now — you may understand why I bothered."

Then he was gone into his office, and the door clicked, and Rosalind sat with her heart going like a hammer in a locked room.

* * *

She erased her red flags at five, obediently, and rewrote them in pale pencil, and initialed the variances, and closed the folio, and did everything correctly. Rule three: comply visibly, completely, immediately. Compliance is camouflage.

But rules, she knew, were for the surface. Below the surface, divers had other methods.

While the office emptied — Yamada first, then the boys, then Oka-san locking his ink drawer with his little ceremony of keys — she copied, from pure memory, into the deep back pages of her personal notebook where she kept her mother's diving songs written in romanized Japanese disguised as French vocabulary exercises: not figures, not dates, not a single incriminating character. Only a shape. Fourteen crates. October. Weight doubled, freight doubled, reconciled downstream. A pattern, filed nowhere, owned by no one, carried out of the building inside the only vault no clerk could confiscate — the one behind her eyes.

Outside, the harbor had gone gold and violet with evening. Fishing boats were coming home past Deshima, and somewhere across the water the ama boats were beaching, the divers walking up out of the sea with their baskets, their dark hair slicked flat, their bodies brown and strong and free in ways her forged papers would never permit her to be. One of them might be carrying a pearl worth a year of a clerk's wages in a pouch against her skin. One of them had been her mother.

She stood a moment at the top of the stone steps, notebook against her ribs, and watched the water.

Fourteen crates of ceramic ware that weighed like people. A supervisor who warned her away from patterns and called it kindness. And below it all, somewhere under the tidy surface of the Meridian's books, a hold full of breathing cargo, sailing on schedules she could read but not yet name.

Tomorrow she would carry the question to Kenji — not the figures, never the figures, just the shape of them, the way one diver describes a bottom to another. Tonight she would walk home through the tenement streets as Rosalind Amberdale, assistant bookkeeper, harmless as furniture.

Behind her, as she left, the counting house lamps went out one by one, and the ledgers slept in the dark, confessing nothing — yet.
