## 4. Interpreter's Daughter

The rain that night came down the mountain in ropes, and Rosalind Amberdale — who was then nine years old and called only Kume-chan by everyone who loved her — sat on the wet stones of Dejima's seawall with her mother's diving weight across her knees.

It was a lump of lead the size of two fists, worn smooth at the grip where her mother's hands had held it ten thousand times. The rope was gone; someone had cut it free from the drowned woman's belt when they pulled her up off Iojima, and what remained was this: the weight itself, cold as the sea it had come out of, and smelling faintly of brine no matter how many years passed or how many rains fell upon it.

Kume-chan did not cry. She had learned already, in the four days since the boats came back without her mother, that crying made the aunties look at her with soft eyes and then turn away to whisper, and whispering was worse than anything. So she sat in the rain holding the weight and watching the harbor lamps smear themselves across the black water, and she counted the lights because counting was something her mother had taught her.

*One pearl for the boat,* her mother used to say, laying the day's take out on the mat inside their one room above the ama drying-sheds. *Two pearls for the rice. Three pearls for the roof.* She would move each shell across the tatami like a general moving soldiers. *Four pearls for your school, little fish. Five pearls for luck, because the sea gives nothing freely.*

Six pearls, her mother had never said. There had never been a sixth pearl. And now there would never be one, because the sea had taken its payment all at once, the way it sometimes did, folding a diver into its grey green dark between one breath and the next, between the surface and the bottom of the world off Iojima where the oysters grew in rows like graves.

"You will drown yourself sitting there."

The voice came from behind her, in English — which was strange enough to make her turn — and then again in Japanese, gentler: "You'll catch your death, child."

A man stood under the eave of the shuttered customs house, holding an oil-paper umbrella he had not opened. He was tall for a Japanese man, thin as a brushstroke, dressed in a Western coat gone shiny at the elbows, and his hair was going grey at the temples in a way that made him look less old than weathered, like a post left standing after the house around it had burned.

"I know you," Kume-chan said, because she did. Everyone on the waterfront knew Kenji Watanabe, once interpreter for the Meridian Trading Company, once a man who could speak five languages and walk through any door on the bund. He had been dismissed two years before — she was too young to know why, though she'd heard the word *disgrace* said behind fans and hands — and now he lived alone above a chandler's store near Ohato, translating letters for sailors for coins, and nobody bowed very deeply to him anymore.

"Do you," he said. "Then you know I am not worth knowing." He crouched, joints cracking, until his face was level with hers. Rain ran off the eave between them in a beaded curtain. "That is your mother's weight."

"Yes."

"Kume-san dove the Iojima banks. Thirty years, some say. She could hold her breath longer than any woman working the water, and she could count faster than any man on the quay." He looked at the lead in her lap with an expression Kume-chan had no name for yet, though later she would learn its name was grief wearing the mask of memory. "I saw her once, from a ship's rail, rise out of the water with her hair streaming and a shell in her hand, and I thought — there is a person the sea respects. Do you understand? Most of us, the sea does not even notice. But the sea noticed her."

"She's dead," Kume-chan said. Saying it aloud, flatly, in front of a stranger, was easier than she expected. It was like setting down the weight.

"Yes," Kenji Watanabe said. "She is. And you are sitting in the rain guarding a piece of lead, which is faithful of you, but useless. Lead does not need guarding. Lead is the one thing in this world that keeps."

She looked at him. Her mother had taught her about men — not the warning mothers usually give but a different kind of lesson, delivered over shellfish and lamplight: *Watch what a man does with his hands when he talks, little fish. If his hands are busy and honest, listen. If his hands are still and his mouth is busy, run.* Kenji Watanabe's hands were folded around the furled umbrella like a man holding something he was afraid to open.

"Where do you live?" she asked.

"Above the chandler's at Ohato. It smells of tar and I have one kettle and more books than floor space." He paused, and something moved behind his eyes — hesitation, or perhaps the ghost of a decision being made. "There is a second kettle. My wife brought it from Osaka before she died, and I have never used it, because using it would mean admitting she is gone, and I have been a coward about many things but not, I think, about that much longer."

Kume-chan did not fully understand this speech. But she understood that a man who spoke of kettles and dead wives and cowardice in the same breath, to a child, in the rain, at midnight, was either mad or lonely, and she had been told by three aunties in four days that she was going to the orphanage at Urakami, where the nuns were kind but the porridge was thin, and where no one would teach her to count pearls because girls who counted were trouble.

"My mother said the sea takes nothing freely," she said. "So it took her instead of taking me. That means I owe it something. But I don't know what yet."

For a long moment Kenji Watanabe said nothing at all. Then he opened the umbrella, and the rain drummed on it like applause from a very small, very polite audience, and he held it out over both their heads.

"Come and owe it somewhere dry," he said. "You can bring the weight. It can sit by the door. Every house should keep one thing that remembers."

*

The room above the chandler's store at Ohato was smaller than the one she had shared with her mother, and it was full — full in a way that frightened her at first, because every wall was papered with pages. English words in columns, Japanese characters beside them, numbers running down margins like ants marching in disciplined lines. Dictionary pages, shipping manifests, half-translated contracts, a page torn from a prayer book pinned next to a page of tide tables as if salvation and arithmetic were cousins, which perhaps they were.

"There are rules," Kenji told her on the first morning, setting a bowl of rice before her and a pencil beside it. "Rule one: we eat together. Rule two: you ask me anything and I will answer honestly, even if the answer shames me. Rule three—" he tapped the pencil— "busy hands and closed mouth. A girl with ink on her fingers and nothing in her ears hears everything. A girl with gossip on her tongue and nothing in her hands hears nothing, ever, all her life. Which do you want to be?"

"The first one," said Kume-chan, whose mother had taught her the same rule in fewer words: *dive deep, speak shallow.*

"Good." He pulled a manifest across the table — a real one, from his days at Meridian, kept out of spite or sentiment or both. "Then we begin. Your mother could count pearls in the dark. You will count everything else."

He taught her English the way divers teach children to swim: by throwing her in. No alphabets recited in order, no patient drilling of *this is a cat*. Instead he read aloud from the shipping news while she ate, then asked her questions in English, and when she stared at him blankly he simply repeated himself, slower, day after day, week after week, until one morning in her third month under his roof she answered him in English without noticing she had done it, and he put down his teacup and looked so stricken with joy that she laughed for the first time since Iojima.

Numbers came faster. They had always lived in her, inherited like her mother's lungs. By the time she was eleven she could reconcile a column of freight weights against a manifest faster than Kenji could read them aloud, and he began bringing home harder things — bills of lading from other companies, tariff schedules, the dense bilingual contracts of the treaty ports — and teaching her not merely how the figures worked but how they lied.

"This is the most important lesson," he said one winter evening, laying two documents side by side. On the left, an official manifest declaring forty chests of ceramic ware bound for Shanghai. On the right, the port authority's tonnage record showing the ship drawing six inches deeper than forty chests of cups could ever account for. "Paper says one thing. Water says another. When they disagree, believe the water — and then find out why the paper lied, because the lie is always the true cargo."

"What was the true cargo?" she asked.

He was quiet a long time. The lamp hissed. Outside, the tide turned in the channel below the window with a sound like a long slow exhale.

"That," he said finally, "is a story for when you are older, and it is also the reason I live above a chandler's store instead of a counting house, so let us leave it in its box for now." But his hand, she noticed, had gone still on the table, and his mouth had gone busy with silence, and she filed the moment away the way she filed everything — in the deep cold place where she kept her mother's voice and the smell of the sea off Iojima.

*

She was twelve the first time she understood why he had been dismissed, or part of why, or at least enough of why to stop asking.

It happened on a Sunday, when the harbor was quiet and Kenji drank a little more sake than usual — not often, but on Sundays, and only on Sundays, as if grief kept office hours. He had taken out a lacquered box she had never seen him open, and from it a photograph: a younger Kenji in company dress, standing beside a British merchant captain on the deck of a barque, both men smiling the stiff smiles of men posing. Behind them, partially visible, a line of people on the deck who were not smiling at all.

"Who are they?" she asked, because rule two said she could.

"Passengers," he said, and the word had a crack in it.

"They don't look like passengers."

"No." He set the photograph face-down on the table with great care, the way a man lays down a card he knows loses him the game. "They don't, do they."

He told her some of it that Sunday, in fragments, the way a man tells a story he has rehearsed so many times it has worn smooth as a stone: that thirty years before, the companies had shipped laborers south and west — indentured men, contract women, people whose papers said one thing and whose debts said another — and that an interpreter who translated those contracts faithfully, who wrote down what was actually agreed rather than what was convenient, became a very inconvenient man. That he had written a report once. One report. That it had gone up the chain of the company and come back down as his dismissal, and the man he had tried to help — a boy of fifteen sold onto a ship like the one in the photograph — had sailed anyway, and Kenji had stood on the quay and watched the sails go, and done the arithmetic every night since of what one report had cost and bought.

"One report," he said, turning his cup slowly on the table. "I was young. I thought truth was a weapon you could just pick up and swing. Truth is a ledger entry, little fish. It has to balance, or it ruins the wrong people. I ruined myself and balanced nothing."

"And the boy?"

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. The harbor outside the window answered for him, breathing its tide-breath, keeping its accounts.

But here is the thing Rosalind remembered all her life afterward, the thing she returned to on the nights when the copying work felt impossible and the danger felt enormous: Kenji Watanabe never taught her caution as cowardice. He taught it as craft. *Busy hands and closed mouth* was not a rule for hiding — it was a rule for surviving long enough to act. Watch, he said. Record. Wait. The sea takes nothing freely, and neither does justice; you pay in patience, in precision, in years if it demands them. But you pay, and it balances, or you make it balance, and that is the whole of the work.

When she was fourteen he gave her a notebook — cheap paper, cloth cover — and told her to begin keeping accounts. Not money. Accounts of what she saw. Ships' names, tonnages, the discrepancy between draft marks and manifests, the faces of men who visited the Meridian offices after dark. "Don't show anyone," he said. "Don't tell anyone you keep it. Just keep it. A record kept in the dark is a lantern waiting for morning."

"Why give me this if it's dangerous?" she asked. "If it ruined you?"

He looked at her for a long time across the lamp-lit table, this gaunt man with his papered walls and his unused second kettle, and his eyes were wet, though his voice never wavered.

"Because it ruined me alone," he said. "And I have thought for thirty years that if there had been two of us — if one person, just one, had believed me and written it down beside me — the boy might be alive, and I might be able to sleep. I cannot go back and stand on that quay again. But I can raise someone who will stand on it better than I did." He pushed the notebook across the table. "Your mother paid the sea. Now you will collect."

She took the notebook. She wrote her first entry that night: the date, the name of a Meridian collier, and the six inches of unexplained draft she had watched the pilot note and ignore. Her handwriting shook. It was the first line of a ledger that would take fifteen years to fill.

*

On the last night of that year — she was fifteen now, working mornings at the chandler's, studying nights — she woke past midnight to a sound from the main room and found Kenji standing at the window with the photograph box open in his hands, looking down at the harbor where the winter stars lay reflected on the black water like spilled salt.

He heard her and did not startle. He never startled; twenty years of disgrace had burned that out of him.

"I dreamed about Kume-san tonight," he said. "Your mother. First time in years. In the dream she was diving off Iojima and she surfaced and handed me a shell, and inside the shell was a pearl, and the pearl had writing on it, and I couldn't read it, and she laughed at me. She had a laugh like gulls." He closed the box. "Do you remember her voice?"

"Every day," Rosalind said. "Less clearly every day. That frightens me."

"It shouldn't. Memory isn't a warehouse, little fish. It's a ledger. Things get copied forward and the copies change slightly, and that's not loss — that's interest." He smiled, tired and real. "Someday you'll write it all down. Her, and me, and the boy in the photograph, and every name this harbor swallowed. That's the only atlas that matters. Not charts of coastlines. Charts of people."

Outside, far across the water, a ship's bell rang the hour, and beneath it — so faint she might have imagined it — came the sound of the ama singing on some distant bank, the rising falling work-song of women pulling breath and pearls out of the dark.

Kenji listened too. His face in the starlight was unreadable, and then, briefly, entirely readable.

"Go back to bed," he said gently. "We have figures tomorrow."

But she stood a moment longer in the doorway, watching him watch the harbor, and she thought: *he is waiting for something. He has been waiting thirty years. Someday the thing he is waiting for will knock on our door, and I want to be the one who answers it.*

Three years later, almost to the week, a census taker named Ishida climbed the stairs to their rooms above the chandler's store with a leather folio under his arm, and knocked.
