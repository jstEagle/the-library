## 5. Ishida Returns

He came back on a Tuesday, when the rain had settled over Nagasaki like a lid on a pot and the whole harbor steamed with it.

Rosalind saw him from the counting-house window before she knew who he was — a dark figure picking his way along the wet cobbles below the Meridian Company's steps, umbrella tilted against the drizzle, moving with the unhurried patience of a man who has never once in his life been late for anything. There was something familiar in the set of his shoulders, the careful angle of his hat. It took her a full minute to place him, and when she did, her pen stopped mid-stroke and left a small blot of ink on the freight column like a period at the end of a sentence she hadn't meant to write.

The census taker.

Mr. Ishida.

He had come to the tenement eleven days ago, on a gray morning much like this one, with his ledger of households and his polite questions and his way of looking at a person as though they were a form to be filled in. She had answered everything correctly. She had given him the name on her papers — Amberdale, R., clerk's assistant, father English deceased, mother — and here was where she had performed the small sleight of hand she had performed perhaps two hundred times since Kenji first taught it to her at the age of nine: mother Japanese, deceased, name unrecorded. A blank, honestly reported. Blanks were safe. Blanks were what the world expected of a girl like her, and no one ever pressed a blank, because pressing a blank required effort, and effort was a currency most officials kept hoarded.

But Mr. Ishida had pressed. He had asked about her mother's family name — *one question too many*, Kenji would have called it, if Kenji had been there, which he had not, because Kenji never came near the tenement in daylight, and Rosalind had begun to suspect that some part of him believed that whatever he could not witness could not be taken from him. She had lied smoothly. She always lied smoothly; it was the one inheritance from her father that she valued, an Englishman's talent for looking bored while constructing a fiction. And yet the man had written something down. Not in the household register but in a smaller book, a private one, slipped from his sleeve and returned to it in a motion so fluid she might have dreamed it.

Now he was climbing the Meridian Company's steps, shaking the rain from his umbrella with three precise strokes, and asking the doorman for — she watched the doorman's mouth move, watched him turn toward the interior stairs — the records office. The records office where the shipping manifests were kept.

Her heart did not pound. That was the strange thing, the thing she had learned about fear in her nineteen years: it did not announce itself. It arrived quietly, took up residence behind the sternum, and began doing its work with the diligence of a bookkeeper, entering each small debit in its proper column until one day the balance came due and you discovered how much of yourself had already been spent.

"Miss Amberdale." Pell's voice, from the doorway of his office. Her supervisor stood there with his spectacles pushed up into thinning hair and his waistcoat straining at a button he refused to admit was too small. "There's a gentleman from the prefectural census bureau downstairs asking after manifests. Shipping documentation." He paused, and something flickered across his face — not suspicion, exactly. Concern wearing suspicion's coat. "He says it concerns a household inquiry. Yours."

"Mine," Rosalind repeated.

"He asked for you by name." Pell's eyes, mild and brown and usually so tired, held hers a moment longer than was comfortable. "I told him our manifests are company property. But he seems a patient sort of gentleman. I've put him in the side room. You may speak with him or you may send him away — that is your affair, Miss Amberdale, and the company takes no position on census matters." He said this last with a slight emphasis that she could not parse, and then he retreated into his office and closed the door, which he almost never did before noon.

* * *

The side room was a narrow chamber used for inspecting sample goods: a table scarred by crate-edges, a window fogged with harbor damp, a lamp burning yellow against the gray morning because the room faced north and got no light worth the name. Mr. Ishida stood at the window with his hands folded behind his back, watching the cranes swing their slow arcs over the water, and he did not turn around when she entered. He let the silence sit between them like a third person.

"You found me," Rosalind said.

"You are easy to find. That is the trouble with being diligent — people always know where to look for you." He turned then. Up close he was older than she'd first thought, fifty perhaps, with a scholar's stoop and a face like a dried persimmon, all creases and patience. His small notebook was already on the table. Beside it, square and deliberate, he placed a second object: a strip of paper, soft with handling, covered in brush-written characters.

She did not look at it. Looking would be surrender. She looked at his face instead, and waited, because waiting was also a kind of arithmetic and she was very good at arithmetic.

"You wrote your mother's name as unrecorded," Ishida said. "On the eleventh of last month, at the tenement on Hama-machi lane. Unrecorded. Deceased." He tapped the strip of paper once, gently, as a man taps a sleeping child. "That is a very clean answer, Miss Amberdale. Too clean. In thirty years of taking censuses I have learned that people who give clean answers have usually cleaned them."

"My mother died when I was four. I don't remember—"

"Kume." He said the name quietly, without triumph, without cruelty — and that was the worst of it, that he said it the way one says the name of someone mourned, with respect, with even tenderness. "Kume of Iojima. An ama diver of considerable reputation, they tell me. Lost off the point in the autumn of 1884, in water too deep and too cold for the season, diving for pearls that were not hers to dive for." He paused. "Your mother's registry was never closed properly. When a diver dies, there is a form. Her village filed it late, and incompletely, and the bureau's copy went missing in a reorganization. So officially, Kume of Iojima was never recorded dead at all. She simply ceased to appear." His eyes were very steady. "A woman who ceases to appear leaves a daughter who appears too cleanly. Do you see my difficulty?"

Rosalind's mouth was dry. She made herself say, evenly: "I don't know what you're suggesting, sir. My mother was a Japanese woman. She died. I've never hidden it."

"No," Ishida agreed mildly. "You've never hidden it. You've simply declined to mention it, which is different — a distinction your father's countrymen would appreciate, being great ones for distinctions. Half-caste children of indentured clerks do not become assistants to the chief bookkeeper of the Meridian Trading Company on honest paper alone. Forged papers, then. Well-made ones — Watanabe Kenji's work, I should imagine, though I would not say so aloud, because saying so aloud would ruin him and I am not here to ruin anyone." He said this with such matter-of-fact gentleness that it took her a moment to understand he had just spoken Kenji's name, and her whole body went cold and still, the stillness of a diver hanging in green water listening for what moves below.

"I think," Rosalind said, "you should leave."

"I think I will, presently. But first—" He slid the strip of paper across the table toward her. "This is a fragment of the Iojima registry. Recovered. It lists the divers of the 1884 season. Kume's entry is here. And beside it, in the margin, someone wrote a notation — the price paid for her final dive, and to whom the pearls were consigned. Do you know what the notation says?"

She looked down despite herself. The brushwork was faded, hurried. She read it twice because the first time made no sense.

*Meridian.*

"The pearls my mother died for," Rosalind heard herself say, "went to the Meridian Company."

"To a broker acting for the Meridian Company, through a channel that does not appear in any ledger the company shows to the customs house." Ishida folded his hands. "You keep their books, Miss Amberdale. Tell me — in all those beautiful columns — have you ever seen a purchase of raw pearl from the Iojima beds? Any line, any entry, any weight of shell?"

She had not. She thought of the ceramic ware that weighed wrong, the crates whose manifest tonnage exceeded any kiln's output, the columns that refused to reconcile no matter how many times Pell made her check them. She thought of these things and kept her face as smooth as a closed ledger, but she knew — she saw in the faint settling of the old man's expression — that he had read her anyway. Census men, she understood suddenly, were trained readers of faces. Their whole trade was knowing what people were and writing it down.

"What do you want?" she said. "If this is blackmail, ask for money. I haven't any, but I can—"

"I don't want money." He sounded genuinely pained that she should think it. "Money is the smallest thing a man can want. Listen to me now, because I will only say this once, and then we will never speak of it again unless you choose otherwise." He leaned forward slightly, and the lamplight caught the deep lines around his eyes. "Twenty-six years ago I was a young clerk in this same prefectural bureau, and I noticed something in the shipping records. Divers registered in spring, absent by autumn. Villages whose able women thinned year over year while the harbor grew richer. I wrote a memorandum. It was passed upward. The memorandum disappeared. Two years later my superiors changed, my post was moved to household enumeration — the least consequential desk in the building, the one where they park men who ask questions — and I have sat at that desk for twenty-four years, counting kitchens and chimneys, keeping my mouth shut, growing old." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, gentle and ruined. "So you see, Miss Amberdale, I know precisely what it costs to notice things. I paid the price young. You are paying it now, or soon will be, whether I exist or not."

"Then why—"

"Because last month a girl gave me a clean answer about her dead mother, and something in me woke up after twenty-four years and would not go back to sleep." He straightened. "Here is what I want. Nothing more, nothing less. Access. When the Meridian Company ships cargo, its manifests pass through channels — customs, harbormaster, the consul's office for the foreign entries. Copies exist in the prefectural archive, sealed, catalogued, gathering dust, because no one has ever requested them. No one official, that is. A census verification of household shipping-declarations — it is a real category, obscure, never used. With it I may lawfully request the manifest copies for review. What I cannot do is read them. They are written half in trade English, half in commercial cipher, and my English is a bureaucrat's English, good for treaties and useless for ledgers." He looked at her, and for the first time something moved beneath the patience — something hot and banked, like coals under ash. "You read ledgers. You keep them. You are, I am told, remarkably quick."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I write in my report that the household registry contains a discrepancy requiring further investigation, and it goes up the chain, and somewhere above me sits a man — I do not know his name; I have spent years failing to learn it — who makes problems disappear. Your papers. Your interpreter. Perhaps your own name added to some autumn shipment, bound for a port where no census taker will ever count you." He said it without heat, the way one reads out a weather forecast. "I am not threatening you. I am describing to you what happens. There is a difference, and I ask you to believe the difference matters to me, though I know how little belief is worth in a conversation like this one."

The rain ticked against the window. Out on the harbor, a crane swung a net of crates over the Kaiyo-maru's hold, and Rosalind watched it descend into the dark of the ship and thought about weights that did not reconcile, and about her mother's name in a margin, sold with her final breath of air, and about Kenji at the kitchen table with his tea going cold, teaching her long division when she was eight years old because, he said, numbers were the one language that could not lie to her.

Numbers could not lie. But men could make numbers lie, and she was beginning to see the machinery of it, vast and quiet, turning beneath the harbor like a second tide.

"Why me?" she asked. "Why not go to the consul, the police, the newspapers?"

"Because you are the only person in Nagasaki who stands inside the counting house and outside it at once. Because you owe the Meridian nothing — less than nothing — and love no one the company can hold hostage, except perhaps an old interpreter whom I will protect with whatever is left of my career." He gathered his notebook, his umbrella, himself, with the economy of a man accustomed to leaving rooms without regret. At the door he paused. "One more thing. Kinship, I suppose you would call it — though kinship between a bureaucrat and a bookkeeper is a thin soup." Something almost like humor crossed the dried-persimmon face and was gone. "My sister dove the Iojima beds. Three seasons, before she married. She knew your mother. She said Kume sang better than any woman on the water, and held her breath longer than anyone dared time." He bowed, small and exact. "When your mother drowned, the bureau recorded it nowhere. I am offering to record her daughter properly instead. Think about which of us is doing the blackmailing, Miss Amberdale. I will call at the archive office Thursday. If you are not there, I will file my report, and we will both learn to live with what follows."

Then he was gone, his footsteps precise and unhurried down the stairs, and Rosalind stood alone in the sample room with the strip of paper in her hand and the rain coming down on the harbor like the sky itself taking inventory.

She read the marginal notation again. *Consigned, Meridian channel. Price of one dive.* Beneath it, in a different hand, a date: three days before her mother went into cold water and did not come up.

They had priced the dive first. They had sent her down knowing the season, knowing the depth, knowing — perhaps — that the price was cheap because the diver was expendable, one line item among many, a cost of goods that would never appear in any column Rosalind had been hired to keep.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Later, walking home through the wet streets past the fish stalls and the shuttered bathhouse, past the ama divers' beach where old Sato-baasan sat mending nets under an awning and lifted one hand in her usual wordless greeting, later still, climbing the tenement stairs to the rooms where Kenji would be waiting with rice and questions he had learned not to ask — later, her hands stayed steady, and she understood that something had ended. The wall of anonymity, the rule of busy hands and closed mouth, the long safety of the blank space where her mother's name should be — Ishida had walked through all of it in twenty minutes, politely, with references, like a creditor calling on a debtor.

Thursday. The archive office. Manifests in cipher, and the one person alive who could teach her the old commercial shorthand was the man who had forged her papers, and whose terror of the Meridian name ran deeper than any well.

She climbed the stairs. Through the door, the smell of charcoal and miso, and Kenji's silhouette bent over the stove, and beyond the window the harbor lights coming up one by one in the rain — each one, she thought now, possibly a column in a ledger she had not yet learned to read.

"Rosalind." Kenji glanced back, and his eyes narrowed at once, the old interpreter's instinct reading her face the way she read figures. "What happened today?"

"Kenji-san," she said, setting the strip of paper on the table between them, "tell me about the Iojima registry."

And she watched the color leave his face like tide going out.
