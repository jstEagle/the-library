## 6. The Divers' Landing

The tide was going out when Rosalind came down the shingle path to Iojima's western beach, and it went out the way her mother must have known it, the way every diver on that shore knew it — not as a schedule but as a voice, a long slow exhale of water drawing back from the rocks and leaving them bare and glistening like the ribs of some enormous patient animal.

She had not come here in eleven years.

The path itself had changed, or she had. As a child it had seemed a road; now it was barely a scar in the hillside, switchbacking down through wind-bent pines and scrubby azalea gone over with rust-colored blooms. Her sandals found the old ruts without her permission. Her body remembered this place even where her mind insisted on forgetting, and that frightened her more than anything else about the morning: that she could spend eleven years becoming someone else — Miss Rosalind Amberdale, assistant bookkeeper, third desk from the window at Meridian Trading — and still her feet knew the way home.

*Not home*, she corrected herself, stopping where the pines thinned and the sea opened out below her, vast and pewter-colored under a sky rinsed clean by last night's rain. *A place. Only a place.*

But the word would not stay corrected.

Below, the beach curved in its crescent of grey sand and black stone, and the divers' huts stood along the tideline exactly as they always had — low-roofed, tarred dark against the weather, smoke rising thin and blue from two or three morning fires. The drying racks were up, kelp hung in long brown curtains that turned slowly in the breeze. And beyond the racks, out on the flat shelf of rock that ran into the shallows, women were already wading, their white cotton work-clothes ghostly against the green water, laughing and calling to one another in the particular shorthand of women who have shared cold and danger for years and no longer need full sentences for anything.

Rosalind stood in the shadow of the pines and watched them, and something in her chest turned over like a boat in a swell.

She had told Kenji she was going to visit an acquaintance in Nagasaki proper. She had told herself she was going to check the tide tables for the harbor office, which was true in the sense that any lie is true if you carry a real errand inside it like a stone in your pocket. The truth underneath both statements was simpler and worse: she had read those ledger columns for three nights running — *ceramic ware, 40 crates, 2 tons 3 hundredweight* — and the weight had sat in her stomach like swallowed seawater, and she had needed, with a need that felt almost physical, to see the faces of the women whose trade her mother had died in. To see whether they looked like cargo yet.

She started down the path.

* *

The beach received her without ceremony, which was the first mercy. No one looked up from the fires or the racks; a stranger walking the tideline at low tide was nothing remarkable, a fish buyer perhaps, or a census man, or one of the shrine pilgrims who occasionally wandered through taking notes on local customs. Rosalind kept her stride even and her eyes forward, the way Kenji had taught her twenty years of lessons boiled down to a single sentence: *Walk as though you belong, and belonging will be forced to agree with you.*

The smell hit her halfway across the sand and undid a decade of careful forgetting in a single breath — woodsmoke and salt and kelp rot and the sweet iron tang of the tide flats, and under all of it, faint as a watermark, the particular smell of diver: brine cured into skin, iodine and cold stone. It was her mother's smell. It was the smell of the first four years of her life, before the fever took Kume off Iojima and the world contracted to Kenji's rented rooms above the chandlery with their ink-and-tatami quiet.

Her eyes were stinging. She blamed the wind off the water.

"Careful there, girl, you'll walk into the rack."

The voice came from her left, dry as driftwood, and Rosalind stopped just short of colliding with a woman coming up from the waterline with a basket of abalone balanced on her hip. The woman was small and immensely solid, built like something the sea had tried repeatedly to remove and failed — broad hands, forearms corded with muscle, a face so weathered it had gone past red and brown into the color of old teak. Her white diving clothes were soaked to the thigh. Her hair, iron-grey, was bound up under a towel turban in the old style, and her eyes were the palest brown Rosalind had ever seen, almost amber, set in folds of sun-creased skin.

Those eyes went over Rosalind once, unhurried, top to bottom, the way a diver reads the surface before going under.

Then they stopped. And stayed.

"You," the old woman said. Not unkindly. Not kindly either. Just with a terrible, gathering attention, like a tide beginning to turn. "Stand still a moment."

"I'm sorry, auntie, I didn't see—"

"Stand still."

Rosalind stood still. It occurred to her distantly, with a bookkeeper's horror, that she had obeyed instantly, completely, without a flicker of the smooth deflection she deployed daily against Mr. Pell and the census taker and every other man who asked questions. Some voices you did not deflect. This was one of them.

The old woman shifted her basket to her free hip and reached up — she had to reach; she stood barely to Rosalind's chin — and turned Rosalind's face toward the light with two fingers under the jaw, rough and gentle at once, the way one handles a pearl one is deciding whether to string.

"Hm," she said.

"Auntie?"

"Your mother," the old woman said. "Who was she?"

The question went through Rosalind like a harpoon through water — fast, silent, striking deep. Every rehearsed answer rose at once and jammed in her throat. *I don't know what you mean. My family is English. My papers are in order.* But the old woman's fingers were still under her jaw, and the sea was behind her, and the smell of her mother was in the air between them, and none of the answers would come.

"I don't—" Rosalind began.

"Don't lie to me on my own beach." There was no anger in it. That was the frightening part. It was said the way one says *the tide comes in*, a fact of the place, older than argument. "You've got city paper in your pocket and city manners on your back, and you walked down that path like your feet knew it better than your head did. And you've got—" the amber eyes narrowed, reading her again, closer now, "—Kume's chin. Kume's brow. You've got her face with the color washed out of it. Who was your father, girl? An Englishman?"

The world tilted very slightly. Rosalind heard the surf, the gulls, the far laughter of the divers in the shallows, all of it arriving from a great distance.

"How do you know my mother's name?" she whispered.

The old woman let go of her jaw. She looked at her for a long moment more, and then something moved through the seamed face — not a smile, nothing so simple, but a loosening, like ice going out of a river.

"Sato," she said, tapping her own chest with a fist. "Sato-baasan, if you're being polite, which you aren't, but we'll get to that. I dove beside Kume for nineteen years. Nineteen years, two rocks apart, breathing when she breathed. I pulled her out of the water twice and she pulled me out once, and she owed me for the difference until the day the fever took her." She picked up her basket as though the matter were settled. "And you have her face. So. You'll take tea with me, and you'll tell me what name you're wearing these days, and why a girl with a dead diver's face walks around pretending she hasn't one."

* *

The hut was low and dark and smelled of charcoal and dried fish, and Rosalind sat on the worn mat by the fire with her knees drawn up like a child, holding a clay cup of tea too hot to drink, while Sato-baasan moved about the small space with the economy of someone who has lived alone a long time and liked it.

Outside, the morning's diving was ending; the white figures were coming up the beach, wringing out their hoods, calling results to one another — *two! three! a good one!* — and somewhere among those calls was the count of abalone and sea-urchin that would become, further up the chain of the world, a line in a ledger. Rosalind thought of Mr. Pell's columns. *Ceramic ware.* She wrapped her hands tighter around the cup.

"You haven't answered me," Sato-baasan said, settling opposite her with a grunt. "Name."

"It's complicated."

"Nothing is complicated. Things are only long." She sipped her own tea. "Say it long, then."

So Rosalind said it. She said it in fragments at first, watching the fire rather than the old woman's face — the fever, the orphanage year, Kenji Watanabe finding her, the rooms above the chandlery, the lessons, the forged registry, the name *Amberdale* taken from an aunt in England who existed mostly on paper, the counting house, the ledgers. She did not say the words *ceramic ware* aloud. She could not make herself say them yet, even here, even to this woman. But Sato-baasan listened the way divers listen, without interruption and without blinking, and when Rosalind finished, the silence held for a long time, filled only by the fire's small talk and the sea outside.

"Amberdale," the old woman said at last, tasting it. "You dive with that name over you like a hood."

"It keeps me safe."

"Mm." Sato-baasan set down her cup. "Do you know what happens to a diver who keeps her air too long? She feels strong. Stronger than she's ever felt. The bottom looks friendly. And then the light goes strange, and then she doesn't come up." She leaned forward, elbows on her knees, and the amber eyes fixed Rosalind with the patience of deep water. "You've been holding your breath eleven years, child. I can see it in how you sit. Like you're waiting for someone to tell you it's safe to exhale."

Rosalind looked down into her tea. "It isn't safe. To exhale. Where I am."

"No," Sato-baasan agreed placidly. "Probably not. Now tell me what brought you here today, because girls from counting houses do not wander to Iojima for the scenery."

And there it was — the second harpoon, thrown before she'd recovered from the first. Rosalind opened her mouth to give the tidy half-truth she'd prepared, and instead heard herself say:

"There are shipments leaving the harbor. Heavy ones. Listed as ceramic ware." Her voice sounded very far away to her own ears. "The weights don't reconcile. I keep the books, or I help keep them, and I know what two tons of porcelain weighs, and this isn't it. I came to see—" She stopped. Started again, lower. "My mother sold pearls to the company boats. All of you did, I think, in those days. I wanted to know if the buying had changed."

Something passed over Sato-baasan's face then that Rosalind would remember for the rest of her life. Not surprise. Not fear. Something older and wearier than either — the look of a woman hearing a bell toll for something that happened years ago and was never mourned properly because no one dared hold the funeral.

"Changed," the old woman repeated. She rose, stiffly, and went to a chest in the corner, and knelt at it with her back to Rosalind for a long moment before opening it. When she turned around she was holding a bundle of bamboo tallies tied together with frayed cord, each stick notched and marked with characters burned into the grain.

"Do you know what these are?" she asked.

"Tally-sticks. Debt records. One notch per unit owed."

"Per unit," Sato-baasan said. She untied the cord and laid the sticks out on the mat between them, and her hands — those indestructible hands — were not quite steady. "That's what the company men called it, yes. Units. When I was young, a tally meant rice advanced against the season's catch, and you worked it off, and everyone knew everyone, and the arithmetic was honest even when the prices weren't. Then the Shanghai men came, seven years ago now, with their contracts and their brokers, and the arithmetic stopped being honest." She drew one stick from the bundle and held it to the light. "This one is O-Tsune's. Forty-one notches. She went north three winters ago to work a pearl concession in the straits. Good wages, they told her. Her debts carried over and cleared within two years, they told her." She set the stick down. "No letters come back from the straits, child. The boats go north heavy and come back empty, and the notches keep growing on sticks for women who aren't here to argue with them."

The fire crackled. Out on the beach, a diver laughed at something, bright and unconcerned, and the sound fell into the hut like a stone into deep water.

"How many?" Rosalind asked. Her voice was nearly steady. "How many have gone north?"

"On this beach? Eleven in seven years." Sato-baasan began tying the sticks back together, notch by notch disappearing into the bundle, and Rosalind understood suddenly that she was looking at the only true ledger those women had ever possessed — kept not in ink but in bamboo and memory, hidden in a chest, counted by a grandmother who could not write. "Some went for wages. Some went because their husbands drank. Some went because the broker stood in their doorway and mentioned their daughters. And some—" the cord tightened, "—went because their debts were sold. You understand? Not the women. The debts. A debt is a thing, like a barrel, like a crate. It can be shipped. And where a debt goes, the debtor follows, because that is how debt works, everywhere in the world, in every language."

*Ceramic ware,* Rosalind thought. *Two tons three hundredweight.* The number surfaced in her mind and floated there, and she could not look away from it.

"Is the Kaiyo-maru one of theirs?" she heard herself ask. "The ship that loads at the Meridian workshop?"

For the first time, the old woman looked genuinely startled — a quick widening of the amber eyes, quickly mastered. "Where did you hear that name?"

"I keep the books," Rosalind said. It was the truest sentence she had spoken all morning.

Sato-baasan studied her for a long moment. Then she took the tallest of the tally-sticks — the one with the most notches, forty-one, O-Tsune's — and pressed it into Rosalind's hand, closing the girl's fingers over it with both of hers, hard enough to hurt.

"Take it," she said. "Counters should count. Whatever it is you're counting in that counting house, count it truly, and when the numbers tell you what I think they're telling you —" she paused, and her grip tightened, and her voice dropped to the register divers used underwater, low and carrying, meant to cross distance and danger both, "—you come back to this beach. Because there are eleven names on my sticks, and I am too old to swim after them myself. Do you understand me, girl? Count truly, and come back."

Rosalind closed her hand around the tally-stick. It was warm from the old woman's palm, and lighter than it had any right to be, and heavier than anything she had carried out of this bay since the day she'd left it at six years old, holding Kenji Watanabe's hand.

"I understand," she said.

* *

She climbed the shingle path as the tide began its slow return, filling in the marks of her footsteps behind her one by one, and at the top, where the pines closed over the view of the beach, she stopped and looked back once.

The divers were going out again with the turning water, white figures scattering across the green, and among them, small and indomitable, Sato-baasan waded toward her rocks as though seventy years were a rumor and the sea an old friend with whom one had merely quarreled.

Rosalind put the tally-stick inside her jacket, against her ribs, where the forged papers also lived — the two falsest things and the truest thing she owned, lying together over her heart.

Forty-one notches. Eleven names. Two tons, three hundredweight.

She had come to the beach to look at faces, and she was leaving with arithmetic, and she knew — with the cold certainty of a column finally balancing — that by the next dawn tide she would be standing in Mr. Pell's office with his ledger open before her, matching his neat figures against a dead woman's debt-stick, and that whatever she found there, there would be no unfinding it.

Behind her, far below, a diver's song rose off the water, high and wordless, and followed her all the way up the hill.
