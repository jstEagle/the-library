## 7. Figures That Lie

The counting house after dark was a different animal entirely.

By day it breathed with the rhythm of the company — the scrape of chairs, Pell's dry voice correcting a decimal, the rattle of the abacus beads like rain on a tin roof, clerks coming and going with their arms full of manifests that smelled of tar and tea chests. By night it was only Rosalind and the lamps and the ledgers, laid open like sleeping animals she had been told not to touch.

Mr. Pell had begun leaving her late on purpose. *You have an eye for reconciliation, Miss Amberdale,* he'd said three weeks ago, tapping her column of freight tallies with his pencil stub. *Most clerks see numbers. You seem to see what the numbers are trying to hide.* He had meant it as praise. She had felt it land somewhere between her shoulder blades like a cold fingertip.

So now she stayed past seven, past eight, alone in the long room above the harbor, copying fair entries into the master books while the gaslight hissed and the ships below shifted against their moorings with sounds like great beasts turning over in sleep. It was honest work, mostly. It was also the perfect cover for dishonest work, and she knew it, and some nights the knowing sat in her stomach like swallowed sea water.

Ishida's request was simple enough on its face: copy nothing, steal nothing, only *look*. Find where the Meridian's shipping figures refused to agree with themselves, and mark the pages in memory — never in ink — so that he might know which manifests to request through official channels. The census bureau, he said, had authority to audit commercial registries when household records suggested irregular labor arrangements.

*Irregular labor arrangements.* She had turned that phrase over all the way home from their meeting beneath the camphor tree, tasting each word. She knew what irregular labor looked like. It looked like her mother, waist-deep in cold water at dawn, diving for a company that owned her debts down to the last sen. It looked like Sato-baasan's hands, knotted worse than driftwood. Irregular labor was a polite phrase for people who could not leave.

She had promised herself she would be careful. She had promised Kenji, who did not know yet that there was anything to be careful about. And most of all she had promised herself the one rule that mattered: *look, remember, but do not take.* Paper left a trail. Memory left nothing but the shape of a thing behind your eyes.

For eleven nights she had looked.

* * *

The trouble with ledgers, Rosalind had learned from Kenji at their kitchen table years ago, is that they are written by men who assume no one will ever read them twice. *A lie is easy to enter,* he'd said, wetting his brush to demonstrate a false sum for her amusement. *But a lie must then be carried forward, page after page, year after year, and every time it is carried it must agree with itself. Truth agrees with everything. Lies agree only with each other, and even then, grudgingly.*

She had thought of him tonight as she pulled the crew registries for the coastal runs — the small steamers and lorchas that ferried goods between Nagasaki, Shanghai, and the intermediate ports whose names blurred together like watercolors left in the rain. The Meridian ran nine such vessels under British flag and Japanese registration both, whichever suited the cargo. That much she already knew. What she wanted was the crews.

It began as tedium. Name, age, port of origin, wage rate, signed-for date of engagement. The same columns repeated in the same copperplate hand — Pell's hand, or the senior clerk Yamane's, or the younger boys' more careless script. She worked through the *Kaiyo-maru*'s book first because it lay nearest the lamp, and because something about that ship had snagged at her weeks ago in the freight schedules — ceramic ware, declared again and again, crates too heavy for porcelain and insured for too little to be jade.

Name by name she copied into her working memory the crew lists for the Kaiyo-maru's last four voyages. Forty-one names per voyage, standard complement. She set them side by side in her mind, then — because memory was beginning to blur and this was arithmetic, not recollection — she risked a single sheet of scrap paper from the wastebasket, writing only initials and tally marks, the kind of scrawl any clerk might make checking a headcount.

That was when the pattern surfaced, the way a drowned thing surfaces when you stop looking straight at it.

Eleven names appeared on all four voyage rosters. Eleven men who signed articles in Nagasaki, drew their advance wages — she checked the wage ledger, and there they were, advances paid out, sums entered neatly against each name — and then simply *were not recorded again*. No discharge entry. No return advance. No death benefit, no desertion notice filed with the harbor office, none of the paperwork that followed a sailor who jumped ship, which was common enough that the company kept a rubber stamp for it.

Eleven men who took money and vanished. On paper.

Except that paper does not vanish men. Men vanish men.

Rosalind sat back in her chair and the gaslight hummed and somewhere below a halyard clinked against a mast, patient as a clock. She made herself go further. If these eleven were phantom crew — wages drawn by someone else, names borrowed to balance a manifest — then the ship had sailed light-handed, or else the berths those eleven should have filled had held something else entirely. Something that weighed like grown men and was declared as ceramic ware.

Her hands were cold. She noticed them distantly, as though they belonged to another girl across the table, and watched that other girl pick up the wage ledger again and check the disbursement signatures. Every advance for the eleven phantoms had been countersigned by the same man. Not Pell. Not Yamane. A signature she did not recognize — a tight, downward-slanting hand, initials *T.M.*, and beside it, in the margin of the oldest voyage book, a small annotation in Japanese that someone had thought no English-reading auditor would ever parse:

*配分済 — 分与先は上海。*

*Distributed. Recipient: Shanghai.*

The room tilted very slightly, the way a deck tilts. Rosalind pressed her palms flat against the desk until it steadied.

Forty-one names entered as crew. Thirty who sailed. Eleven who existed only as sums of money traveling east to Shanghai, buying — what? Passage for others? Or passage *of* others? Her mother's world rose up in her mind unbidden: the ama divers' beach at low tide, the women wading out with their knives and their wooden tubs, the brokers in good coats walking the sand with their little books. Sato-baasan had once spat at the mention of the Shanghai houses, and old Ume had gone silent in a way that silence itself had been information, if you knew how to read it.

*Ishida wants the manifests,* she reminded herself. *Not your theories. Give him the pages and let him do his office's work.* But even as she thought it, her hand had already pulled the Kaiyo-maru's current manifest toward the lamp — the one for the sailing three weeks hence — and her eyes went down the crew list, and there they were again. Eleven names. Some the same. Some new, young ages, seventeen, eighteen, nineteen. Fresh phantoms, freshly paid.

And beside two of the new names, in that same slanting hand, a notation she had not seen before: a small circle, and inside the circle, a character — *女*.

Woman.

* * *

She did not remember deciding to copy the page. That frightened her afterward more than anything else — that the decision had made itself somewhere below her thinking, in the place where her mother's blood lived, the diver's blood, the blood that went down into the dark holding its breath because what it needed was down there and fear was a luxury.

One page. The current manifest's crew roster. She had a sheet of good paper in her sleeve — she always did now, folded small, a habit she hated herself for — and she worked fast, transcribing the forty-one names in a compressed clerk's shorthand of her own devising, initials and numerals, illegible to anyone but her. Two minutes. Three. The gas hissed. Down in the harbor a bell buoy tolled against the swell.

She heard the stairs when he was six steps from the top.

There was no hiding the page — no time. The desk drawers locked and she had no key; the stove stood cold; the window overlooked the yard where Pell would see her drop it plain as a falling leaf. So Rosalind did the only thing her hands knew how to do without instruction. She swept the transcription off the desk into her lap, dragged the actual manifest on top of the scrap paper still lying there, and bent over both with her pencil moving, a clerk mid-correction, absorbed, blameless, boring.

Mr. Pell came around the corner of the stacks with his coat over his arm and his hat in his hand, and stopped.

He was not a frightening man to look at. Fiftyish, thin as a mast, with a face like a closed account and spectacles that caught the lamplight so you could rarely see his eyes. But Rosalind's heart slammed against her ribs so hard she was sure he must hear it, sure the lamp flame itself flinched with each beat.

"Still here." Not a question. His voice was mild. It was usually mild. "I forgot my gloves."

"Good evening, Mr. Pell." She did not look up. Looking up was a confession; clerks caught doing nothing look up. She kept her pencil moving along a line of the manifest, murmuring numbers under her breath the way she genuinely did when reconciling — a trick Kenji had taught her, *hum while you count and the numbers stick like burrs* — and let him watch her be exactly what she appeared to be.

He did not move. Ten seconds. Fifteen. Long enough that she counted her own breaths and found she had stopped taking them.

"The Kaiyo-maru," he said at last. "You're checking her complement?"

"The wage advances don't reconcile against the discharge rolls." She kept her voice flat, clerk-to-clerk, and prayed the half-truth sounded like the whole truth. "I flagged it for morning. Probably Yamane's posting error."

Silence again. Then Pell crossed the room — unhurried, his footsteps precise on the boards — and stood at the edge of the lamplight, close enough that she could smell the tobacco and camphor of his coat. He looked down at the manifest. At her pencil. At the scrap of paper protruding perhaps a quarter-inch beyond the manifest's edge, bearing her compressed initials and tally marks.

His gloved hand — no, he had forgotten his gloves; his bare hand, pale and blue-veined — reached out and slid the scrap free.

Rosalind did not breathe. Somewhere very far away, on the water, the bell buoy tolled again.

Pell studied the scrawl. Turned it over. Studied the blank reverse. And then, in a voice as mild as ever, almost gentle: "Sloppy work, Miss Amberdale. If you're going to check a headcount, check it cleanly. Yamane will think you can't add."

He set the paper back on the desk, precisely aligned with the manifest's edge, picked up his hat, and put it on.

"Young women burn bright and gutter early," he said, adjusting the brim in the dark glass of the window. "Working these hours. Take the tram home tonight. I insist." At the stairhead he paused, one hand on the rail, and without turning around added: "The Kaiyo-maru's accounts are in excellent order. I'd hate to see you worry yourself over them."

Then his footsteps descended, unhurried, precise, and the street door opened and shut below, and Rosalind sat in the ringing lamplight with the transcription crushed in her fist and her heart going like a diver's pulse at depth — slow, enormous, counting out the seconds of air remaining.

He had seen. Of course he had seen. A man does not forget his gloves and climb two flights of stairs for them; a man checks. And he had looked at her scrawl — her initials, her tally marks, eleven marks too many for any honest headcount — and he had called it sloppy and told her the accounts were in excellent order, which was either a warning, a dismissal, or the strangest mercy she had ever been handed.

She unfolded the transcription with trembling fingers and checked it whole. All forty-one names. Both circled characters. The Shanghai notation, committed to memory twice over now, ink and mind.

*Look, remember, but do not take.* She had broken her own rule, and within minutes been nearly taken herself. Tomorrow Pell would wonder. Tomorrow he might pull the manifest himself, or set someone to follow her home, or — worse, far worse — begin pulling at the loose thread of *Miss Amberdale*, whose papers were beautiful and forged, whose father existed nowhere but ink.

Kenji's face rose before her, stern and tired across thirty years of his own ruined caution. *Burn it,* he would say. *Whatever you've found, burn it, child. Men who carry proof are men who drown carrying it.*

But beneath the terror, steady as bedrock under loose sand, was the other thing. The thrilling, terrible, luminous thing.

She *knew*. Not suspected, not wondered — knew, the way she knew a sum balanced, the way she knew salt water from fresh with her eyes shut. Eleven names that bought and sold human beings while wearing the respectable costume of a payroll. A ship loading now, in her harbor, under her company's flag, bound for a house in Shanghai. A woman's character in a circled field, marked like a bale of goods.

Rosalind folded the page small, tucked it deep in her sleeve against the skin of her wrist, and blew out the lamps one by one. In the sudden dark the harbor showed through the tall windows, black water pricked with riding lights, and among them — she found it without searching, because she had known she would — the brighter cluster of lanterns at the Meridian pier, where the Kaiyo-maru lay taking on cargo she could now name.

Three weeks until she sailed. Eleven names, and one of them perhaps a woman, perhaps a girl, perhaps someone's granddaughter sitting right now on the divers' beach watching the tide come in, not knowing she had already been sold and entered and countersigned.

Rosalind pulled her shawl tight and went down into the night, past the sleeping warehouses, along the quay where the water slapped the stones. She would have to tell Ishida. She would have to decide about Kenji. And she would have to be faster than Mr. Pell's suspicions, whatever mixture of threat and kindness they truly held.

Behind her, high in the dark counting house, one lamp she had thought extinguished flickered briefly at the window — and went out.
