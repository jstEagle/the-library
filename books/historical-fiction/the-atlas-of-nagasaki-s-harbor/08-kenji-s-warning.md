## 8. Kenji's Warning

The rain came off the harbor in the late afternoon, the way it always did in the season of plums, and Kenji Watanabe heard it change the sound of the street before he saw it — the drumming on the awning of the tofu seller below, the hiss of it finding the gutters, the softening of footsteps so that he could not tell, from his chair by the window, whether anyone was climbing the stairs until they were already at his door.

He knew her step anyway. He had known it since she was four years old and climbed these same stairs on legs still unsteady from the sea, salt in her hair, her mother's amulet knocking against her collarbone. Rosalind climbed stairs like a person going somewhere important. She had never once arrived at his door looking like a child with nothing to do.

Tonight she arrived looking like a woman carrying a corpse.

"Kenji-ojisan." She did not use the word lightly. She used it now, which told him how frightened she was, because fear made her formal, the way pain made some people quiet. She stood in the doorway with the rain steaming off her shoulders and a paper wrapped in oilcloth held against her chest the way one holds a thing that might still be breathing.

"You're wet," he said. "You'll catch your death."

"I'm already dead," she said, "if this is what I think it is."

He put the kettle on because that was what one did, because his hands needed something older than thinking to occupy them, and while the water muttered he watched her unwrap the oilcloth on his low table with the care of a surgeon and the guilt of a thief.

A single page. Copied in her small, exact hand — he knew that hand better than his own; he had built it, stroke by stroke, year after year, drilling her in figures the way other fathers drilled daughters in music. English numerals down the left margin, kanji transcriptions along the right, her annotations in pencil in the gaps: *weights do not reconcile. 41 names. no discharge records.*

Kenji sat down across from her and read it once quickly, the way a man reads a letter that might be a summons. Then he read it again slowly, and the room grew colder around him, though the kettle was beginning to sing.

He had seen this pattern before.

Not this page. Not these numbers. But the shape of them — the architecture of the lie. Shipments entered as ceramic ware whose tonnage matched nothing any kiln in Kyushu could produce. Crew manifests with names that appeared and vanished like carp surfacing in a pond. Debts recorded as assets, people recorded as freight. The columns balanced beautifully, which was itself the tell, because honest ledgers are ragged things, full of corrections and crossed-out entries and the small human mess of real commerce. These numbers were too clean. They had been laundered, pressed, and folded like linen for a coffin.

Thirty years ago he had been a young interpreter for a British trading house — not Meridian, but its grandfather, its ancestor, the same money wearing different clothes — and he had found exactly this pattern in the shipping books of Dejima and the foreign concession. He had been proud of it. He had been twenty-six years old and fluent in three languages and certain that arithmetic was a kind of honesty, that if you could show a man the numbers, the numbers would speak for themselves.

He had taken his findings to his employer. And his employer had thanked him gravely, praised his diligence, promoted him into a position with fewer duties and more silence, and then, when the house's affairs were quietly reorganized, dismissed him with a character reference so carefully worded that no other firm would touch him. *Conscientious but indiscreet.* Four words that had followed him for thirty years like a dog that would not stop following. Every company in the port had read those words and understood them perfectly. He had ended up teaching English to dockworkers' children and keeping accounts for men who paid in rice wine, and it had been, he told himself for decades, a small life, an honest life, a life he had chosen.

It was only tonight, looking at a girl's copied page in lamplight, that he understood he had spent thirty years telling himself a story as clean and false as anything in a Meridian ledger.

"This is Meridian's?" he asked, though he knew.

"The coastal manifest. Third quarter." Her voice was steady. She had learned steadiness the way divers learn breath-holding — not as absence of fear but as discipline imposed upon it. "Forty-one names entered as crew who never sailed. I checked the harbor master's rolls. Those men don't exist, Kenji-ojisan. Or rather—" her jaw tightened— "they exist. They just never signed on as crew."

"They're cargo," Kenji said.

The word came out of him flat and final, and he watched it land on her, watched her flinch and then refuse to flinch, and he thought: *she is her mother's daughter after all.* Kume had had that same quality — the refusal to look away from the water no matter what the water showed her.

"You have to burn it," he said.

She looked up sharply.

"Burn it. Tonight. Burn the original notes too, all of them, every scrap in your handwriting that touches this. Tell me you kept copies nowhere else."

"Kenji-ojisan—"

"Do you hear me, Rosalind? This is not a game of figures. You are a bookkeeper's assistant with forged papers in a city where the census bureau can end you with a single entry, and you have stolen a page from the private ledger of a company that sells human beings. If they discover this page is missing — if they trace the copy — they will not go to the police. The police are theirs. They will go to the harbor at night, and there will be a story about a clerk who slipped on the stones, or a fire in a tenement, and everyone will shake their heads and say what a pity, she was so clever with numbers."

"And thirty years ago," she said quietly, "you showed them the numbers, and they ruined you instead of the crime."

The kettle screamed. He rose to take it off the flame more because he needed to move than because the tea mattered, and his hands were not quite steady, and he hated that she could see it.

"It isn't the same," he said to the stove.

"Isn't it?"

"It was a different house. A different era. There are treaties now, consuls, a modern police—"

"There are forty-one names," she said, "and somewhere north of them, women who dive for pearls with debts around their necks that they will never pay off because the debts are not meant to be paid. Sato-baasan's beach is half empty, Kenji-ojisan. Did you know that? The young ones are gone. Everyone says they went to Shanghai for work. Nobody asks why their families stopped receiving letters."

He poured the tea. His hands had steadied themselves by force of will, the way they had steadied themselves thirty years ago in a director's office while a man with kind eyes explained to him the difference between conscience and career.

"If you burn it," she said, "then it happened and no one will ever know. Again. You will be safe, and I will be safe, and the ledger will balance, and the ship will sail, and in thirty years some other child will find a page like this and bring it to some other old man, and he will tell her to burn it too. Is that the life you taught me figures for? So I could keep beautiful accounts of other people's drowning?"

He set the cup before her. His own he held without drinking, warming his palms against the clay, and for a long moment neither of them spoke, and the rain filled the silence with its patient, indifferent sound.

"You think I am a coward," he said finally.

"I think you are afraid," she said. "And I think you have been afraid so long you've mistaken it for wisdom."

*Yes,* he thought. *That is exactly what I am.* He looked at her across the low table — this girl who was not his blood but was more his than any blood, whom he had fed and schooled and guarded and lied to about the world in order to protect her from it — and he felt the old ruin open inside him like a wound that had never truly closed. He had lost everything once for speaking. He could lose everything again for staying silent, because what he could not survive losing was her, and she had already decided, he could see it in her face, she had decided the way her mother used to decide, standing on the rocks with the tide coming in.

"Promise me one thing," he said.

Her eyes narrowed. She was too clever to promise blind.

"When you do whatever it is you are going to do — and I can see that you are going to do it, so spare us both the argument — promise me you will not carry it alone. Promise me that if the time comes, you will let an old man's disgrace be useful. I know these houses, Rosalind. I know how they write, how they hide, how they lie in three languages. My name is already ruined; there is nothing left in me for them to take." He set down the cup. "Except you. Don't make my silence cost you twice."

She reached across the table and took his hand — her fingers cold from the rain, ink-stained, calloused from the abacus — and gripped it hard.

"I promise," she said.

They drank their tea. She stayed long enough to dry herself and eat the rice he pressed on her, and they spoke of small things afterward, deliberately, the way people do when the large things have grown too heavy to lift again — the price of charcoal, a leak in his roof, whether the persimmon tree in the temple yard would fruit this year. When she left, the rain had thinned to mist, and he stood at the window and watched her descend the stairs and turn toward the harbor, toward the counting house district, toward the lion's mouth, walking with that purposeful stride that had always terrified him because it never wavered.

Then he turned back to the table.

The oilcloth lay where she had left it. She had repacked the page — he had watched her do it, folding it with those precise hands — but she had left the cloth itself behind, and beneath it, half-hidden under the edge of the teapot, a corner of paper he had not seen before.

He pulled it out. It was another copy. The same manifest, the same forty-one names, in her hand — but annotated differently, dated a week earlier, with a note in the margin: *first draft — check against autumn rolls.*

Two copies. She had made two from the beginning. She had given him one to burn and kept the other, and she had let him believe — no, she had not even troubled to deceive him thoroughly; she had simply arranged matters so that his order to destroy the evidence would be obeyed in the letter and defeated in the fact.

Kenji stood holding the page for a long time. He should have laughed. Instead he felt something loosen in his chest that had been clenched for thirty years — grief and terror and, underneath them both, unwelcome and unmistakable, a fierce and rising pride.

"She hid it from me," he said aloud to the empty room, and his voice cracked in the middle like a boy's.

He looked at the page. He looked at the stove, where a single coal glowed dully, close enough to reach, hot enough to end it.

Then he folded the page along her own creases, wrapped it in the oilcloth, and carried it to the back of the cedar chest where his mother's funeral kimono lay folded thirty years untouched, and buried the paper beneath the silk, beneath the camphor, beneath the weight of everything else in his life that he had preserved instead of burned.

Some things, he thought, closing the chest, are kept not because they are safe but because they are true.

Outside, the rain moved out over the harbor, and far down the waterfront, past the darkened warehouses and the sleeping cranes, the workshop lamps of the Meridian Company burned late into the night — and somewhere among them, a ship's schedule was being written that would give them, though none of them yet knew it, less than three weeks.
