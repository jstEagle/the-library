## 9. The Morning Tide Schedule

The rain had passed by morning, leaving the harbor scrubbed and shining like the inside of an oyster shell, and Rosalind Amberdale walked to work through a city rinsed clean of its own secrets — or so it liked to pretend.

She came down the stone steps from the Dutch slopes early, earlier than she needed to, because the counting house opened at seven and Mr. Pell had begun, in the past fortnight, to arrive at half past six, and she had learned that the surest way to keep a supervisor's favor was to make him feel observed in his diligence. Her umbrella was furled but carried anyway; Nagasaki skies changed their minds the way tide changed sand. The harbor lay flat and pewter-bright, dotted with junks and lighters and the masts of foreign ships riding at anchor off Dejima, and everywhere the smell of wet rope and coal smoke and the green salt breath of the sea that had raised her mother and would, one way or another, have her too.

She had not slept well. Ishida's face kept surfacing behind her eyelids — that mild, patient, terrible face, the face of a man who asked questions the way water asked questions of stone: slowly, without malice, and with absolute confidence in the outcome. *Your mother was Kume. Kume of Iojima.* He had said it not as an accusation but as a gift, and gifts were harder to refuse than threats.

*Three weeks,* he had said of the manifests. He wanted access to Meridian's shipping papers, and he wanted her to be the hand that brought them to him, because a census taker could knock on any door in Nagasaki but could not open a single drawer of the Meridian Trading Company without setting off alarms that reached all the way up to Tokyo.

And she had not yet answered him. That was the trouble. Every day she did not answer was itself an answer, and both of them knew it.

*Busy hands,* Kenji had taught her. *Closed mouth.* But there was a third rule he had never needed to say aloud, because she had absorbed it with her mother's milk or with the harbor's salt air: *when the current turns, you cannot argue with it. You can only decide what you will carry and what you will let go.*

The current was turning. She felt it in her ribs like a change in pressure before weather.

* * *

The counting house smelled of ink and cold tea and the particular dust that lived in paper — a dry, sweetish smell, like old leaves. Mr. Pell was already at his desk when she arrived, his coat hung on the stand by the door, his sleeves rolled to the forearm in the manner of a man preparing for surgery rather than arithmetic. He was forty-odd, thin as a ledger-rule, with sandy hair going grey at the temples and a pair of spectacles he cleaned so often they had worn two small polished moons into their rims.

"Amberdale." He did not look up. "The Kaiyo-maru papers came in on the last launch. Top of the pile. We'll want the manifest abstracts entered by noon."

"The Kaiyo-maru," Rosalind repeated, and heard how carefully neutral her voice came out, like a column total that balanced to the penny.

"Syndicate charter out of Shanghai." Pell drew the bundle toward himself and tapped it twice with one finger, a habit of his, as though waking the documents. "Cargoes of ceramic ware, mostly. You'll find the entries duller than ditchwater, which is exactly how we like them."

Something turned over in her chest — the same slow cold turning she had felt the week before, entering figures for crates marked *porcelain* whose declared weights had run three hundred pounds heavier than any crate of porcelain she had ever seen lifted, and lighter by far than the cranes' logs suggested they weighed at loading. She had copied those figures faithfully and said nothing, per the rules, and had gone home and lain awake doing sums against the dark.

"Duller than ditchwater," she agreed, and took the bundle.

But Pell did not release it immediately. He held it a moment longer, looking at her over the spectacles with an expression she could not file under any heading she knew. Not suspicion. Something more tired than suspicion.

"You'll want to note the sailing date prominently," he said. "Twenty-third. Morning tide. There are penalties written into the charter if she misses it, and the company is — attentive — about penalties."

"The twenty-third." Three weeks and two days. She wrote it at once, in her small even hand, at the head of the page, where dates belonged, and watched her own pen make the numerals as though they belonged to someone else's life.

Pell let go of the bundle then and returned to his own work, and for an hour the counting house was nothing but pens scratching and the harbor sounds coming through the shutters — gulls, hammers from the yard, a steam launch coughing somewhere out toward the mole — and Rosalind entered manifest after manifest and told herself she was only doing her job.

Except that her job had changed shape without anyone announcing it. Because now, under every line she copied, another line wrote itself, in the invisible ink of everything Ishida had told her and everything she had guessed alone at night: *ceramic ware, 412 cases, gross weight consistent with human freight; ceramic ware, 388 cases; ceramic ware* — case after case, voyage after voyage, a porcelain trade so vast it must have filled every cupboard in Christendom twice over, except that no porcelain ever seemed to arrive anywhere. Crates went aboard heavy and came ashore elsewhere empty, or never came ashore at all, and the divers of the ama beaches went on diving, season after season, fewer of them each year, and nobody in the counting houses ever asked why.

She had grown up on those beaches. She knew what happened to divers who dove too deep too often, chasing pearls for men who weighed them like cargo. And she knew — the knowledge sat in her stomach like swallowed seawater — what a ship chartered for *human freight disguised as ceramic ware* would be carrying out of Nagasaki Harbor on the morning tide of the twenty-third.

People. Sold. In lots of four hundred cases.

Her pen did not waver. That was the discipline Kenji had ground into her through ten years of drills and corrections and cold-eyed kindness: *the hand does not tell the heart's business.* But beneath the desk, out of sight of Mr. Pell and God and the whole reconciled world of double-entry bookkeeping, her knee had begun, very softly, to shake.

* * *

At noon she carried the finished abstracts up to Pell's desk, and found him standing at the tall window with his back to the room, watching the harbor, and for a moment he did not turn around, and she stood with the papers in her hands and looked past his shoulder at what he was looking at.

The workshop.

It stood on pilings at the edge of the waterfront, downstream of the great bonded warehouses, a long low building of dark timber with a corrugated iron roof gone rust-orange at the seams, where the Meridian Company did its rough work: rigging repaired, crates built, cargoes broken down and reassembled. From here it looked ordinary, almost humble, a working building among working buildings. Smoke rose thinly from its stovepipe. Lighters bumped against its water-stairs.

"That's where they're staging the Kaiyo-maru cargo," Pell said, still not turning. "Everything goes through the workshop first. It always has." A pause, and then, lightly, in the tone of a man discussing the weather: "Company records too, some of them. The shipping masters keep their own books over there — running tallies, draft copies. Things move back and forth. It's all quite proper."

"It all sounds quite proper," Rosalind said.

"Yes." He turned at last, took the papers from her, squared their edges against the desk with three precise taps. His eyes behind the worn spectacles were pale and unreadable, and there was something in his face that she had never seen there before and would spend many sleepless nights afterward trying to name: not guilt, exactly, and not fear, but the specific gray exhaustion of a man who has just checked a lock he already knows is locked. "You've done good work this morning, Amberdale. Better than good. You have a talent for seeing what a set of figures means, not merely what it says. Don't lose that." He smiled, briefly, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "And don't, whatever else you do, go wandering down to the workshop. It's no place for clerks. It smells of tar and sailors, and the shipping masters are territorial about their books."

"No place for clerks," she repeated.

"None whatsoever." He sat down and pulled a fresh sheet toward him, and the interview was over, and she walked back to her desk with her heart beating in her ears like oars against a hull.

Because she had spent her whole life learning to read the space between what people said and what they meant — a half-caste girl survives on that literacy or does not survive — and what she had just heard, translated out of the careful language of ledgers and supervisors, was this:

*The master ledger lives in the workshop. Do not go near the master ledger.*

He might as well have drawn her a map.

* * *

She ate her lunch on the sea steps below the counting house, where the clerks were not supposed to linger but always did, sitting on warm stone with her sandals off and her feet in the shallow wash, and she made herself eat every grain of rice although her appetite had gone somewhere and drowned.

Three weeks. Twenty-three days, precisely, which was worse than three weeks, because precision was a thing she respected and numbers did not lie to her the way people did. On the morning tide of the twenty-third, the Kaiyo-maru would take on her final loading and stand out of the harbor for Shanghai, and whatever evidence existed of what she carried — and evidence existed, Rosalind was certain of it now, certain in the way she was certain of tides and interest rates, certain with the whole weight of every misaligned column she had copied these five months — would leave with her, beyond any consul's reach, beyond any law, dissolved into the syndicate's vast indifferent machinery like sugar into tea.

And the master ledger — the true tally, the shipping masters' own books, the thing Pell had warned her away from with all the eloquence of a signpost pointing at treasure — sat across the harbor in a building of tarred timber on pilings, guarded, apparently, by nothing but territoriality and the smell of tar.

She looked at it across the bright water while she ate. Small, brown, unremarkable. Gulls wheeled above its roof. A man was coiling rope on its water-stage with the unhurried movements of someone who had done it ten thousand times.

It would be easy, a voice said in her, in her mother's voice, in her own voice, in the harbor's voice. It would be easy to walk down there some evening and simply look. Looking was not theft. Looking was not treason. Looking was only the beginning of knowing, and knowing —

Knowing was what had gotten Kenji Watanabe ruined thirty years ago, dismissed from the company he'd served, his name scraped out of its rolls, left to patch together a living translating letters for merchants too poor to matter. Knowing was what got people drowned off Iojima. Knowing was a debt that came due at the worst possible hour, and she owed enough debts already — she owed Ishida, she owed Kenji, she owed the dead woman whose face she wore and whose name she could not say aloud in daylight without ending her life as she knew it.

She closed her eyes and let the little waves slap her ankles, cool and rhythmic, patient as bookkeeping.

Then she thought of Sato-baasan.

The old diver had been expecting her that evening anyway — it was the third Sunday custom, rice wine and dried squid and the long slow gossip of the beach, the one afternoon in Rosalind's fortnight when she could put down the forged papers of her life and sit among people who had known her mother's laugh. Sato-baasan had dived beside Kume for twenty years. Sato-baasan's granddaughter had gone to work the Shanghai route two seasons ago and written only twice since, and the second letter had been strange, short, wrong — *I am well, do not worry, the work is fine* — in a hand that trembled, and Sato-baasan had shown it to no one but Rosalind, holding it in her knuckled old fingers and saying nothing, saying nothing with an intensity that had frightened her more than weeping would have.

If anyone sold divers south, Sato-baasan would know, or would know who knew. If the beaches whispered — and the beaches always whispered, under the singing, under the splash — then the whisper would reach the oldest ears first.

Rosalind finished her rice, shook the water from her feet, and laced her sandals. Across the harbor the workshop smoked peacefully in the sun, keeping its books.

* * *

The ama beach at Ohato in late afternoon was all gold light and black rock, and the sound of the women coming up from the shallows — that long falling whistle, *isobue*, the song of the breath released — rose and fell along the shore like the harbor itself breathing.

Sato-baasan sat outside her hut on an overturned boat, mending a net with fingers that moved without her attention, the way Rosalind's pen moved. She was seventy if she was a day, small and hard as driftwood, with white hair cut blunt at the jaw and eyes the color of wet slate. When she saw Rosalind come down the path she did not smile — she had stopped smiling years ago, or perhaps had never started; her face saved its expressions the way a miser saves coins — but she shifted sideways on the boat's hull, making room, which was her way of embracing.

"You're thin," she said. "Sit. Eat."

"I ate."

"Eat again. Sitting with the worried burns rice." She pushed the wine flask over, and Rosalind drank, and for a while they watched the divers work the shallows and the net mended itself under the old woman's hands, stitch by stitch, and Rosalind understood that she was being given time, the way her mother's friend had given her time her whole life: silently, without accounting for it, as one gives bread.

Finally Rosalind said, "Obaachan. Tell me about the Shanghai boats."

The needle did not stop. The slate eyes did not lift. But the rhythm of the mending changed — fractionally, the way a diver's breathing changes before the dive, gathering itself — and Rosalind knew she had struck the rock under the water.

"What boats?"

"The ones the young women go on. Two seasons now. The contracts come through agents, the pay comes back irregular, the letters—" She stopped herself. Careful. Busy hands, closed mouth. But it was too late to be careful; Sato-baasan's head had come up, and the look she gave Rosalind was one she had never been given before: measuring her, weighing her, deciding something.

"Who have you been talking to?" the old woman asked.

"A census man."

"A census man." Sato-baasan spat, delicately, into the sand. "Government vermin. They count you like fish in a basket and report you to whoever pays for the count." But her eyes stayed sharp on Rosalind's face, reading it the way Rosalind read ledgers, and slowly, terribly, understanding began to move across her features like cloud-shadow over water. "No. Not vermin. You wouldn't bring me a vermin's question with your mother's face on. You'd bring me—" She stopped. The needle stopped at last. The whole beach seemed to hold its breath with her, the isobue falling silent for a moment, only the water talking.

"Sato-baasan," Rosalind said quietly, "I think they are selling the girls. I think the Kaiyo-maru carries them south as cargo, and I think your granddaughter may be—"

"Don't." One word, cracked down the middle.

They sat in silence. Down the beach a diver surfaced, whistled her long breath skyward, and dove again. The sun was going copper behind Iojima, the island where Kume had drowned, where the sea had taken the best diver of her generation and given back nothing, and Rosalind watched the light die on the water and waited, because she had learned patience from this woman and patience was a weapon that never dulled.

When Sato-baasan finally spoke, her voice was flat and quiet and older than the harbor.

"I signed nothing. Understand that. I said nothing, I reported nothing, I asked nothing. They came to me — the agent, the smooth one, the one who smells of camphor — after Mei went on the boat. They told me the girl was comfortable. Well fed. Learning fine work in a fine house. And they told me that girls who have aunties who ask questions sometimes get sent farther south, to places where letters don't come at all." Her hands had closed on the netting until the knuckles stood out like roots. "So I kept my mouth shut and my ears open, and every month I paid attention, and I counted."

"Counted what?"

"Boats." The old woman turned and looked at her then, full on, and Rosalind saw that behind the slate of her eyes there was a furnace banked thirty years deep. "I have counted boats since before you were born, child. I know which leave heavy and which come home light. I know the Kaiyo-maru takes on her people-cargo at the workshop stairs after dark, in crates marked porcelain, and I know it because I helped carry two of those crates once, before I understood, and I have not slept a whole night since the year of the ox." She drew breath. "And I know something else. The shipping masters keep their true tally over there — not the pretty books for the consuls and the tax men, the real one, the one with names in it, because the syndicate buys and sells by name. Names and prices. My Mei is in that book somewhere. Eleven girls from these beaches went south in two seasons, and eleven names are written in that workshop, and if that book ever burned, they would be no one. Sold and unwritten. Ghosts with no graves."

The tide was coming in around their feet, cold and insistent. Rosalind found she was gripping the rim of the overturned boat hard enough to hurt her hands.

"The twenty-third," she said. "She sails on the morning tide of the twenty-third."

"Then you have until the twenty-third," Sato-baasan said, "to become a thief."

Rosalind stared at her.

The old woman reached into the breast of her smock and drew out something wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it with the deliberation of a priest handling relics: a short bundle of wooden sticks, notched and bound with red cord, each notch a dive, a catch, a payment — the divers' own ancient bookkeeping, older than any alphabet, the tally of a people who trusted wood over paper because wood floated when the ships went down.

"These are mine and my sisters', forty years of them," Sato-baasan said. "Against their pretty columns, wood tells the truth and paper lies. Take them. Hide them with whatever else you are hiding — and don't insult me by pretending you hide nothing, you have your mother's face and your mother's face always meant trouble for somebody." She pressed the bundle into Rosalind's hands and closed the girl's fingers over it with hers, hard, the grip of a woman who had fought octopuses on the seafloor and won. "Three weeks. Bring me back my granddaughter's name out of that book, and burn the rest of it to the waterline."

Far out past the point, unseen yet, the evening launch was putting out from the town pier, its lights pricking the darkening water — and Rosalind thought of the workshop across the harbor, of the true ledger sleeping on its shelves, of twenty-three mornings between this one and the tide that would carry eleven living women out of the world of names forever.

The clock had begun. She could hear it now under everything, under the gulls and the isobue and the lap of the tide: steady, pitiless, keeping perfect time.

She stood, bowed deeply to the old woman, and started up the darkening path toward the town and the lamplit counting house windows — toward Kenji, who would have to be told, and would tell her to burn everything and save herself, and toward the answer she would give him.

Behind her, the sea breathed. Ahead of her, across the black water, the workshop lamps burned late.
