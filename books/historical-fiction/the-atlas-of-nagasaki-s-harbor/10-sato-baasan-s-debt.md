## 10. Sato-baasan's Debt

The tide went out at four in the morning, and Sato-baasan went out with it, as she had gone out with it for sixty years, her feet finding the slick stones of the landing below the divers' huts without the aid of lantern or moon. Her knees had opinions now. They complained on every stair and every crouch, but they had never once refused her, and she considered this a fair bargain between an old woman and her body: you may ache, but you will work.

The beach at Iojima was not a beach in the way foreigners understood beaches — no promenade, no bathers, no painted pleasure boats. It was a working shelf of grey sand and black rock where the ama boats were drawn up keel-skyward like a row of sleeping fish, their hulls patched with tar that shone in the first light like wet lacquer. The drying racks stood empty until midday, when the first dives came up and the abalone and the sea-slugs were laid out to cure in the wind off the strait. The smell was salt and smoke and iodine, and it had soaked into Sato-baasan's skin so deeply that no amount of washing would ever take it out. She did not want it taken out. It was the smell of the only country she had ever held title to.

She knelt at the water's edge and put her hands into the shallows.

Sixty years ago her mother had done the same thing. Fifty years ago Sato-baasan herself had been the one going down, lungs full of borrowed air, body sliding into the cold green dark with the weighted rope over her shoulder, hunting for abalone and, if the gods were generous, for pearl. She had been good. Not the best — there had been a girl named Kume who was the best, Kume who could hold her breath through two waves and come up laughing, Kume whose face was still beautiful even wrinkled from forty years of salt — but Sato-baasan had been good enough, and good enough had kept rice in the pot and a roof over a granddaughter's head.

She thought about Kume every morning at this hour. It was not a choice. The water made her think of Kume the way a bell makes you think of the hand that struck it.

* * *

The girl came at low tide, as Sato-baasan had known she would.

She had seen her twice before — once at the funeral for old Tome, standing at the back with her hat brim pulled down, once on the ferry, reading figures in a little book as though the numbers might escape if she looked away. Both times Sato-baasan had felt the recognition arrive in her chest before her eyes finished the work of confirming it. The face was Kume's face. Not exactly — the jaw was narrower, the eyes lighter, touched with something foreign, something pale-eyed that must have come from the father, the clerk, the Englishman whose name nobody on the beach spoke above a whisper because speaking it too loudly had once cost people their places in the boats. But the way the girl held her head, slightly forward, slightly wary, like a diver checking the surface before committing to the deep — that was pure Kume. That was the daughter of the water, whether she knew it or not.

Sato-baasan had said nothing either time. Caution was the first lesson of the diving life and the last lesson of the political one, and she had lived long enough to learn both. A mixed-blood girl passing as Japanese, working in a foreign counting house, carrying forged papers — such a girl was a lit lamp carried through a dry field. You did not call attention to the flame.

But the girl had come back. And this time she had come alone, walking down from the ferry road in shoes too fine for the rocks, stopping at the edge of the drying racks with the awkwardness of someone who has rehearsed a speech and forgotten all of it.

"Obaasan." The girl bowed. "I'm looking for — I was told you might remember my mother."

"I remember everyone," Sato-baasan said, not looking up from the net she was mending. "That is the trouble with living so long. Sit. You'll fall on those stones in those shoes, and then I'll have to carry you, and my back will file a complaint."

A pause. Then the sound of the girl sitting down carefully on an upturned boat, skirts gathered, and Sato-baasan permitted herself a small private satisfaction. Obedient. Kume had been obedient too, in the ways that mattered, and iron in all the others.

"You are Kume's daughter," she said, still working the netting needle. It was not a question, and she watched the girl's shoulders go rigid in the corner of her eye. "Don't look so frightened. Half this beach has known since the day you stepped off the ferry. We say nothing. That is also our custom. The sea keeps secrets; we learned it from the sea."

"How—"

"Your face. Your mother's face, with foreign weather blown across it." She tied off a knot and finally raised her eyes. Up close the resemblance was almost unbearable. There was a small scar above the girl's left eyebrow — the whole family got that scar, generations of them, from the low lintel of the hut that had stood where the third rack now stood. Sato-baasan herself had put that scar on Kume, sixty-odd years ago, hauling her out of the way of a falling spar during a storm. "You have her chin. You hold your breath when you're thinking. Did you know that? You stop breathing entirely, like you're under water. Kume did that."

The girl's hand rose halfway to her throat and stopped. "They told me she drowned off Iojima. When I was six."

"She drowned," Sato-baasan said. "That part they told you true." And because the girl's face did what Kume's face used to do — closed like a shell around the grief, keeping the pearl inside — Sato-baasan set down the net and gave her more than she had planned to give. "It was a bad squall, out past the point. The boats were recalled but she'd gone down on a promising bed and she was greedy for it, the way the best ones always are. Greedy for the sea, I mean. Never greedy for money. When she didn't come up, her partner went after her. Found her caught in a kelp line, tangled, her weight belt snagged. By the time they got her up—" She stopped. Some things did not need finishing. They sat in the air complete, like a knot already tied.

"I was told she was buried in the temple register under a number," the girl said quietly. "Not a name."

"The company kept the registers. The company counted us like cargo long before anyone thought to sell us." Sato-baasan heard her own voice go flat and hard, and was surprised by it. Sixty years of swallowing, and here was this girl, this child of a dead friend, pulling the swallowed things up one by one like a diver bringing shells to the surface. "Sit still. I'm going to tell you something, and then you're going to wish I hadn't, and then you're going to be glad, the way one is glad of a wound that turns out to have saved one's arm."

* * *

What Sato-baasan told her took a long time, because she told it in order, and order required her to begin thirty years back, in the years when the Meridian Company's agents first started coming to the beach with paper instead of cash.

"In the old days we were paid by the share," she said. "You dive, you bring up, you get your portion at the season's end, weighed out honest on the company scale. Then the company invented the book. Everything went into the book. Advances against the catch — rice in winter, medicine when your man coughed blood, charcoal, oil, the tax on your hut. All written down. At season's end the book was read aloud, and somehow the book always said you owed more than you'd earned. Every year. For everyone. My husband worked himself into the grave trying to clear our account, and when he died, the debt passed to me, and when I grew too old to dive, it passed to my son, and when my son drowned — the sea takes our men the way it takes our breath — it passed to his daughter."

The girl had stopped breathing again. Sato-baasan watched her do it and felt the old fury bank itself into something steadier.

"My granddaughter's name is Chiyo. She was sixteen when the book said she owed the company eleven yen and forty sen, which was a lie, because I had paid it, I paid it twice, but I had nothing in writing, because we are divers, not clerks, and the company's word was paper and ours was only memory. The agent came in the spring. He was polite. He wore a good coat. He said the debt could be cleared by service — that the company needed workers for its ships, that Chiyo could earn her family free in two years' contract, that she would be fed and housed and sent wages home." Sato-baasan's hands had found the net again without her permission; she made herself let it lie. "He said these things while two men stood behind him, and he said them looking at me, and I understood what he was not saying, which was that refusal had a price, and the price would be paid by her."

"What did you do?" the girl whispered.

"I let her go." The words came out of Sato-baasan like a stone coming up from the bottom, cold and covered in silt. "I stood on this beach and I watched the company boat take her north, and I told myself it was arithmetic — one old woman cannot fight a company, better one girl indentured than a whole family ruined — and I have repeated that arithmetic to myself every night for nine years, and it has never once made the sum come out right. Arithmetic is what liars use when the truth costs too much. Your Mr. Pell knows that. Whoever taught him knows that."

The girl flinched at the name. So. She had not hidden her employment well enough, or the beach saw more than she thought. It hardly mattered now.

"They write," Sato-baasan continued. "Or rather, letters arrive that claim to be from her. The handwriting changes every year. The first letter called me *obaasama*, which she never did — she called me *baaba*, rough, like a fishwife, because I raised her rough. The second letter thanked me for money I never sent. The third came postmarked from a port she should not have been anywhere near. Nine years of letters written by men who have never met her, each one a small proof that she is alive somewhere and unable to speak freely, because if she could speak freely, why would they need to forge her voice?"

The silence that followed was filled by the water, folding and unfolding against the stones.

"And your silence," the girl said at last, very carefully, "was bought."

"Do not dress it up." Sato-baasan's voice cracked like a whip and she was glad of it, glad to hurt herself with it. "My silence was sold. There is a difference, and the difference is that selling implies I received fair value. I received a promise. The promise was that as long as the beach stayed quiet — as long as no diver spoke to inspectors, no family petitioned, no one asked the census office inconvenient questions about households that appear and disappear from the rolls — Chiyo would be kept safe. Fed. Unbeaten. Perhaps even released someday, when her contract ran out, though the contracts have a way of growing new years the way crabs grow new shells." She looked out at the boats. "So I bought my granddaughter's safety with every other family's daughter, and I have been paying that installment for nine years, and I am here to tell you, girl, that compound interest works in hell as readily as in a bank."

* * *

The sun had climbed high enough to warm the black rocks. Down the beach, two young divers were pushing off in a boat, their white loincloths bright against the dark water, their voices carrying in the old work-song that had not changed in a thousand years. *Down, down, the sea is wide; up, up, the air is sweet.* Sato-baasan listened to it the way other women listened to temple bells.

"Why are you telling me this?" the girl asked. "You don't know me. Telling me puts you in danger. If the company learned you'd spoken—"

"The company learned everything within a week of your first visit. Old Tome's grandson drinks, and drinks cheaply, and talks while drinking." She waved away the girl's alarm. "Let them hear. I am seventy-three years old. What can they take from me? My eyes are going, my lungs are half salt-water already, and my debts are all paid except the one that matters." She turned and looked at the girl directly, and saw Kume looking back at her from under that foreign-touched brow, and made her decision the way a diver makes hers — all at once, on one breath, with no certainty of what lies below.

"Two nights ago a man came asking questions. A census man. Ishida. He came to the beach with his little book, asking which families had members 'in company service abroad.' Asking dates. Asking ports." She snorted. "Census. The government counts us now instead of the company, and calls it progress. But this one asked different questions than the others. He asked about ships' names. He wrote down *Kaiyo-maru* when I said it, and his hand shook when he wrote it, which interested me, because a man's hand does not shake over routine paperwork. A man's hand shakes when he is afraid of what he is writing down, or afraid of who will read it."

The girl had gone very still. "He came to you?"

"He came to everyone. He is building something. I don't know what — a case, a list, a coffin, it makes no difference to me. What matters is that for the first time in nine years, someone official is asking questions the company cannot answer honestly, and the company is nervous, and nervous companies make mistakes." She pushed herself to her feet, joints protesting, and limped to the third hut from the water — her hut, the one with the tarred roof and the wind chime made from a ship's bolt that Kume had given her forty years ago. "Wait there."

From beneath the floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth inside a cedar box that had once held incense, she brought out the tally-sticks.

There were forty-one of them. She had carved them herself, over nine years, one for every diver taken north under contract, each stick notched with the woman's age at departure, the year, the port, the debt figure entered in the company's book, and the payments — real or forged — recorded since. Bamboo, split and dried and rubbed with ash so the cuts would hold. On the reverse of each, in the old divers' cipher that predated any company ledger, she had cut what the family remembered: the woman's childhood name, her mother's name, the bed she loved best, the song she sang coming up from the deep. Things no clerk would ever think to record. Things that proved these were not entries in a book but women who had mothers and favorite songs.

Forty-one sticks. She had carved the forty-first the night before last, after Ishida left. Its notches were still sharp.

She carried them back and put them in the girl's hands, and watched the girl understand what she was holding — saw it move through her face like weather, disbelief and horror and then, slowly, terribly, purpose.

"These are—" the girl began.

"Proof. The kind your British consul wants, if the rumors are true, and on this beach rumors travel faster than tide. Paper can be burned. Paper can be forged. But these sticks match the company's own ledgers entry for entry, and no one knew I was cutting them, and no one can uncut them." She folded the girl's fingers closed over the bundle. "Nine years I kept them hidden, telling myself I was waiting for a safe moment, and safe moments are like fair winds — they exist mainly in the talk of people who never sail. Then you walked onto my beach with your mother's face and your dead woman's caution, and I understood that the moment had come wearing a shape I recognized."

"Why me?"

"Because you are the only person alive who is inside the counting house and outside the company's loyalty. Because your mother dove these waters and her bones are in them, which means the sea has a claim on you that no employer can cancel. And because—" Sato-baasan paused, and chose honesty, having spent so many years unable to afford it. "Because I need someone to hate properly on my behalf. I am too old for hatred. It requires a stamina I no longer possess. But you — you have your whole life, and her temper, and I saw it just now when I said the word *sold*. You went somewhere cold and quiet. Kume used to go there too, right before she did something reckless and magnificent."

The girl looked down at the tally-sticks, turning the top one over with her thumb, reading the notches like a page. Forty-one lines. Forty-one women. Somewhere among them, a stick marked with a childhood name — Chiyo — and a mother's name that was Sato-baasan's own.

"There's a ship," the girl said quietly. "The Kaiyo-maru. It sails in three weeks. I've seen it on the manifests — cargo bound for Shanghai, weights that don't reconcile." Her thumb stopped moving. "Obaasan. If some of those weights are—"

"Then you know what I have suspected since the letters changed handwriting," Sato-baasan said. "That my granddaughter may be closer than she has been in nine years. Close enough to see from this beach, perhaps, if a ship stands off the harbor waiting for tide." Her throat closed. She let it. An old woman was entitled to one crack in the wall per decade, and she was overdue. "I gave my silence to keep her safe, and my silence has kept nothing safe — not her, not the others, not me. So take the silence back. Take the sticks. Do whatever it is you and your census man and your consul are building. And when you read the names aloud, wherever you read them — read hers loudly. She was always shy. Someone ought to embarrass her, just once, in front of the whole harbor."

The girl bowed, low, forehead nearly to her knees, the way one bows to family at a grave. When she straightened, her eyes were wet and her jaw was set, and Sato-baasan thought: *there it is. The cold quiet place. Good.*

"I'll bring them back," the girl said. "All forty-one. And her."

"See that you do." Sato-baasan turned back to her net, because there was nothing else in the world to do with her hands, and because a diver's discipline was to watch the person leave the water without running to the edge. But when the girl had gone twenty paces up the strand, she called out, one last thing, the thing she had been holding under her tongue since the girl sat down:

"Girl. What name will you use, when you stand in front of the consul? Think on it before the time comes. A name is a breath — you can hold it, or spend it, but you cannot do both."

The girl stopped. Stood a long moment with the bundle of sticks against her chest, not turning around. Then she walked on, up toward the ferry road, smaller and larger than she had been an hour ago.

Sato-baasan mended her net until the light failed, and that night, for the first time in nine years, she slept without doing the arithmetic.
