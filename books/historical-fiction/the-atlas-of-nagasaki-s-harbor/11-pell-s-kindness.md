## 11. Pell's Kindness

The morning after Sato-baasan's words, Rosalind arrived at the counting house with salt still in her hair and a new weight behind her ribs. The tally-sticks were hidden at home, wrapped in oilcloth beneath the loose board under Kenji's bed — she had not slept well knowing they were there, nor slept well imagining them anywhere else. She had rehearsed her face on the walk down the harbor road: the pleasant blankness of a junior clerk, the small deferential smile, hands busy, mouth shut. Her rule. Her armor.

She should have known that Mr. Pell would notice something.

He was waiting for her inside the door of the Meridian Company's counting house, which was in itself an event. Pell was a man who arrived precisely at seven minutes past eight, no earlier, no later, a punctuality so absolute that the dockworkers used him to set their watches. He stood now among the clerks' desks like a lighthouse keeper who has found a crack in his lamp glass, holding two envelopes in one hand and a ledger in the other, and he watched her come up the steps with an expression she could not read.

"Amberdale." He never used her given name. "A word."

Her stomach performed its cold arithmetic — *he knows, he has seen the missing page, Ishida's visit has been reported* — before she could remind herself that there was nothing yet to know. She followed him into his office, the little glass-walled room at the end of the hall where the light came off the water and lay in pale bars across the desk. On the wall hung a framed print of Dover cliffs that must have crossed half the world to hang in this room; it had faded to the color of old teeth.

"Sit down, girl."

She sat. He sat opposite, laid the two envelopes on the blotter between them, and did not push them forward. For a long moment he looked out at the harbor, at the masts swaying gently against the grey morning sky, and Rosalind studied him sidelong as she had studied him for three years: a narrow man, dry as a ledger's spine, with thinning sandy hair combed with mathematical care over a pink scalp, and hands that were always slightly ink-stained no matter how often he washed them. Forty-five, perhaps fifty. A man who had been young somewhere else and had grown old here, among other people's numbers.

"Three years you've been with us," he said. "Two as assistant, one as — whatever we call what you actually do, which is most of my checking and none of my credit."

"Mr. Pell, I—"

"Hold your tongue until I'm finished. It's a rare quality in you, but exercise it now." He turned from the window. "I've recommended you for promotion. Assistant bookkeeper, proper position, proper wage. There's the letter confirming it." He tapped the first envelope. "And there is your raise. Effective the first of the month. You'll take the second desk by the window, and you'll sign the company register in your own hand."

Rosalind's mind, which had been braced for accusation, scrambled to catch up. "Sir — I don't understand. Why?"

Something moved across his face then, quickly, like a fish turning under green water. "Because you're the only person in this building whose columns I don't have to check twice," he said. "Do you know what that's worth? Do you have any idea how many men I've trained in thirty years of keeping books, and how many of them couldn't add a column of freight tonnage without inventing half of it?" His voice had risen slightly; he brought it down again. "You have the gift. The rare one. Numbers behave for you."

*Numbers behave for you.* No one had ever said anything like that to her. Kenji had said she had a good head; Sato-baasan had said her mother's hands were lucky; but no one had ever told her that a thing inside her was valuable, that it made the world easier rather than harder.

"Thank you, sir," she managed.

"Don't thank me. Thank yourself. And sign these when you've read them." He slid the envelopes across at last, and then, as if embarrassed by the whole transaction, busied himself straightening papers that were already square.

She read them. Her own name — the forged name, the paper name, *Rosalind Amberdale*, clerk's daughter of Portsmouth, deceased — written in Pell's careful copperplate, promoted and paid and entered into the company register as though she were real. There was a bitter comedy in it that she almost smiled at. The census taker questioned whether she existed; the Meridian Trading Company had just certified her existence in triplicate.

"You'll want to tell your family," Pell said, watching her fold the letters away. "Fathers like that sort of thing."

"I live with—" She stopped. Careful. Busy hands, closed mouth. "My guardian. An interpreter. Retired."

"Ah." Pell nodded slowly, and for a moment he looked very tired, older than his years, a man carrying sacks of rice up stairs all day. "The Japanese gentleman. Watanabe. I remember him, you know. Before your time. He worked the other side of this harbor once — for Hollingworth & Sons, back when I was a junior man myself. Clever fellow. Too clever for them, or they too stupid for him, one of the two." He paused. "They ruined him, I heard. Over a discrepancy in some manifests. Thirty years ago now."

Rosalind kept her face still, but her pulse knocked against her collar. *Thirty years ago.* The same pattern. The same ledgers. Perhaps even the same men, grown older and richer and more careful.

"Yes, sir," she said.

"He was right, you know."

She looked up sharply. Pell was looking out the window again, at the Kaiyo-maru riding at anchor in the outer roads, her black hull dark against the bright water, cranes already swinging cargo toward her sister ship at the coal wharf.

"What did you say, sir?"

"Watanabe. Whatever the story they told about him — falsified entries, embezzlement, whatever they put in the record — he was right about what he found. Everyone in the counting houses knew it and no one said it, because saying it was a way of ending your career." He turned back to her, and his eyes were pale and clear and terribly tired. "You're a careful girl, Amberdale. Careful girls go far in this trade, as long as they're careful about the right things." He held her gaze a moment longer than comfort allowed. "That's all. Back to work. The ceramic-ware invoices won't reconcile themselves."

---

The rest of the day passed strangely.

At her new desk by the window — the second desk, the one with the drawer that locked — Rosalind found a small tin of Chinese ink and a fresh blotter waiting, and beside them a paper of sugared plums wrapped in bamboo leaf, the kind sold by the old woman at the foot of Umegasaki lane. She stared at it a long while. In three years, Mr. Pell had never once given her so much as a kind word unconnected to arithmetic. Now there were plums and a locked drawer and a promotion that made no sense in any ledger she could construct.

Unless it made perfect sense.

The thought came to her around noon, while she copied shipping weights in the slanting light, and it would not leave. *Why now? Why promote me now, in the same weeks that the Kaiyo-maru takes on her strange cargo, in the same weeks that a census taker starts asking questions?* Either Pell suspected her — had seen her copying pages, had guessed at Ishida, and was buying her silence the only way a man like him knew how, with wages and titles and sugared plums — or Pell needed her closer. Needed her at the second desk, by the window, near the strong-room, where he could watch what she touched.

Or — and this thought frightened her more than either of the others — Pell was trying to protect her. Pulling her up out of the floodwater the way you'd pull a child onto a roof, because the flood was coming and he knew exactly when and how high.

She watched him all afternoon. That was the trouble with liking someone: you began to watch them the way you watched weather. And what she saw troubled every theory. She saw Pell take his midday meal alone at his desk, as always, eating rice and pickles from a lacquered box worn smooth at the edges by decades of use. She saw him correct a junior clerk's error with patience instead of contempt, and send the boy home early because his cough was bad. She saw him stand at the window a long time, looking down at the wharf, where a line of men in matching dark clothes were supervising the loading of crates marked CERAMIC WARE, and she saw his shoulders — narrow, stooped shoulders, a bookkeeper's shoulders — rise and fall once, deeply, like a man standing at the edge of cold water and making himself go in.

At four o'clock he came to her desk.

"The ceramic-ware accounts," he said quietly. "Month of September. Bring them to my office at closing. Not before. And Amberdale—" he glanced along the row of desks, where the other clerks bent over their work, "—come alone."

---

Closing time. The other clerks filed out into the blue evening, their geta clacking on the stone steps, their voices fading down toward the lantern-lit lanes. Rosalind gathered the September ceramic-ware folder with hands that were perfectly steady — she had trained them to be — and carried it through the dimming counting house to the glass-walled office.

Pell had lit one lamp. It threw his shadow huge against the Dover cliffs. He gestured her to sit, took the folder, and did not open it.

"Tell me what you see in those figures," he said.

It was a test. She understood that immediately — perhaps the only kind of test that mattered in this building. If she lied and said she saw nothing, she was either safe or doomed. If she told the truth—

Busy hands, closed mouth. But Sato-baasan's voice was in her ear now, too: *A name is a breath — you can hold it, or spend it.*

"I see eleven crates per shipment," Rosalind said quietly, "marked as ceramic ware, weighing between forty-eight and fifty-two kan each. Ceramic ware doesn't vary that little, Mr. Pell. Crates of porcelain weigh according to their contents. These crates weigh like—" She stopped.

"Like men," Pell said. "Like women. Say it. We're alone, and the lamp is small, and no one outside this glass can hear above the gulls."

"Like people," Rosalind whispered.

Pell opened the folder at last and looked down at the columns for a long time without seeing them, the way a man looks at a scar. "Forty-eight kan," he said. "A diver of average build, wet, is about that. I did the sums myself, years ago, before I let myself stop doing them." He closed the folder. "Now you've done them too. I'm sorry for that. It's a knowledge that doesn't wash out."

"Then why give me the promotion?" The question escaped her before she could cage it. "Why the plums, the desk, the raise — why now, sir? Are you buying my silence? Or are you—"

"Or am I what?" He almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, patient and sad. "Complicit? A prisoner? Both? Ask the question properly, girl. You're too good a bookkeeper to fudge an entry."

The lamp hissed softly. Out in the harbor, the tide was turning; she could feel it, the way her mother's people felt it in their bones.

"Are you a free man, Mr. Pell?"

He sat with that a long moment. When he answered, his voice was low and even, the voice he used for totals.

"No. I haven't been free since 1884. I owe them money — not gambling debts, nothing so honest. Debts they created, entry by entry, year by year, until my whole salary flows upward to men I've never met, and the interest grows faster than I can copy figures. They own my ledger the way a landlord owns a house, and I live in it." He rubbed his ink-stained hands together. "Every crate that goes down that wharf, I certify. Me. My hand. My initials in the margin. Fifteen years of initials. So yes — complicit. And yes — captive. The two aren't contradictory, Amberdale. That's the first lesson of this trade they don't teach apprentices."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you asked. Because no one has asked in fifteen years." He looked at her then, fully, and she saw in his face something that frightened her more than menace would have: hope. Faded, folded-up hope, like a letter kept too long in a pocket. "And because you have the gift, and they will use it if they find it, and I would rather they didn't. So I've moved you to the window desk. The one with the lock. Whatever you keep in that drawer, girl — and I'm not asking — keep it there, and keep your initials out of the margins."

Rosalind's throat was tight. "I don't know what you mean, sir."

"No," Pell agreed mildly. "You don't. And I never said it." He rose, took his hat from the peg, and settled it with the same precision he gave everything. At the door he paused, silhouetted against the last light on the water.

"One more thing. The Kaiyo-maru sails on the morning tide three weeks from Thursday. After that, the manifest closes and the account is settled, and nothing that happened in this harbor can be undone by anyone, however clever their head for figures." He did not turn around. "Whatever you're going to do — and I advise you, sincerely, as your superior and as the only friend you have in this building, not to do anything — do it before the manifest closes."

His footsteps went away down the corridor, precise as always, seven minutes past six.

Rosalind sat alone in the lamplit office with the September folder between her hands, listening to the gulls cry over the darkening water. She had come expecting an enemy and found a hostage. She had expected blackmail and received sugared plums and a warning wrapped in three layers of deniability. And worst of all — worse than suspicion, worse than capture — she liked him. Liked his patience with the coughing boy, liked his faded Dover cliffs, liked the terrible care with which he squared papers that were already square, a man keeping order in a burning house because order was all he had left.

*He was right, you know,* he had said of Kenji. *Everyone knew it and no one said it.*

She pressed her palms flat against the closed folder, feeling the crisp edges of the pages beneath, and thought: *the manifest closes in three weeks. The workshop falls soon after. And the only man inside the walls has just told me, in the language of our shared trade, exactly where the deadline lies.*

She snuffed the lamp and went out into the evening, where the harbor lamps were coming alight one by one, and somewhere beyond them, at the top of the hill, Kenji was waiting with a cold supper and questions she could not yet answer — and one answer, forming slowly in her chest like a pearl around a wound, that she was beginning to be afraid she could not refuse.

Tomorrow she would go to Ishida.
