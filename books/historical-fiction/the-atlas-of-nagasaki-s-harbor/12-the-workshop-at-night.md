## 12. The Workshop at Night

*Rosalind*

The workshop stood at the end of a timber pier like an old man leaning over water he no longer trusted. Rosalind had passed it a hundred times in daylight — one of the Meridian Company's smaller holdings, its boards silvered by salt, its windows furred with grime, a sign on the door reading *Meridian Shipping Annex No. 3* in characters so faded they seemed to be apologizing for themselves. In daylight it was nothing. In daylight it was furniture.

At night, with the moon laying a pale road across the harbor and the tide slapping the pilings in slow applause, it looked like what she had begun to suspect it was: a vault.

She came along the shore road first, not the pier, because Ishida had told her that anyone watching would watch the pier. She wore her darkest kimono and no shoes, carrying them looped around her neck on a cord as Kenji had once taught her to do when walking wet stones — *a barefoot woman is a beggar or a ghost, and no one stops either*. The gravel bit her feet and she was grateful for it. Pain kept the mind from wandering toward fear.

Behind the fish-salting sheds she stopped and counted to two hundred, as agreed. At one hundred and forty, a shape detached itself from the shadow of the shed wall.

"You're late," Ishida whispered.

"You're early," she whispered back.

"One of us should be."

He was thinner than she remembered from his visit to the tenement, or perhaps the darkness made everyone thin. He had traded his census bureau coat for a plain worker's jacket, but he still carried himself like a man holding a clipboard — spine straight, hands folded, everything about him suggesting a document about to be filled out. She found it absurd and slightly comforting. A world full of people pretending to be other things; at least he did it badly.

"The watchman," she said. "Is there one?"

"There is a dog," Ishida said. "The watchman comes at eleven and leaves at four. Between those hours the dog keeps his own counsel." He paused. "I fed it this afternoon."

"You bribed a dog."

"I bribed a dog. It was cheaper than bribing the watchman, and dogs keep receipts less carefully." Something moved under his voice that might have been humor, or might have been terror wearing humor's coat. She was learning to tell them apart on him. Terror spoke faster.

They crossed the open ground between the sheds and the pier in three short runs, the way divers cross a beach between waves — move when the water is busy elsewhere. The boards of the pier boomed under their feet no matter how softly they stepped, each plank a drum announcing them to the whole sleeping harbor, and Rosalind understood with sudden clarity why the company had chosen a place like this to keep its secrets: not because it was hidden, but because any approach to it was loud. The building didn't need walls so much as it needed acoustics.

The door was locked. She had expected that. What she hadn't expected was the second lock — a heavy brass padlock hanging below the ordinary one, bright and new against the grey wood, the kind of lock bought in the last month by men who had recently decided something inside mattered.

"New," Ishida murmured, touching it. His fingers were steady. Hers were not, and she put them in her sleeves where they couldn't be seen doing anything foolish.

"Since Pell's inventory," she said. "I copied the annex inventory myself, three weeks ago. There was no second lock then. There wasn't even a first lock worth picking."

"So whatever they're keeping safe, they decided to keep it safe recently."

"Or someone told them someone might come looking."

They looked at each other. The moon said nothing. Somewhere across the water a ship's bell rang the half hour, soft as a dropped coin.

"We're not getting in tonight," she said.

"No," Ishida agreed. "We're getting a look at what we can't get into. It's a different thing. Less satisfying."

But he crouched anyway at the base of the door, tilting his head to read by moonlight whatever could be read, and she crouched beside him, close enough to feel the warmth coming off his shoulder, which surprised her — she had begun to think of Ishida as a paper object, all surface and filing. Through the gap beneath the door the interior breathed out smells: tar, rope rot, lamp oil, and under it all something sweeter and older, ink. Fresh ink, and a great deal of it.

"They're writing in there," she said quietly. "Or storing what's written. Paper has a smell when it's new, and this is new."

Ishida straightened slowly. When he spoke again his whisper had changed register, dropping the dry clerk's irony for something plainer.

"My grandmother kept silkworms. You learn to smell a room that holds something alive." He looked at the door the way he must have looked at household registries, seeing through the wood to the columns behind it. "Paper is alive too, if you feed it. It grows. It breeds. It gets people dismissed, imprisoned, drowned. My grandmother never told me that part."

*Rosalind had asked him to meet her here, and he had come, and now they stood together locked out of the truth, and she thought: this is what trust looks like in its larval stage. Small, damp, easily crushed.*

"Come away from the door," she said. "If we stand here any longer we'll start believing the padlock is a person and try to reason with it."

They retreated to the dark seam between the workshop's seaward wall and the rail of the pier, where the shadow was thick enough to hide in and the water talked below them, covering their voices. From there Rosalind could see what daylight never showed: the loading crane above the workshop's water door, its arm folded like a sleeping bird, and beyond it the mooring where the Kaiyo-maru would tie up when the time came to load. Three weeks. Perhaps less. The harbor lay flat and black and patient, keeping every appointment it had ever been given.

"Tell me about the bureau," she said.

Ishida went very still. She had learned that about him already — that stillness was his version of another person's flinch. He answered questions quickly enough when they touched on ledgers, tides, locks, schedules. But turn the question toward himself and he became a closed registry, sealed with wax.

"It's a government office," he said. "It counts households. It's very boring."

"Ishida."

"Rosalind Amberdale." He said her false name deliberately, tasting it. "You see how easy lying is? One name for another. I've been thinking about how you do it. You don't hesitate. That's what gave you away, actually. Honest people hesitate. They search for the answer. Liars have it ready, polished, waiting in the pocket." He turned to look at her, and his face in the moonlight was younger than his manner, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "You want my confession. All right. But understand that once I give it, we are both of us standing in a boat with a hole in it, bailing together, and neither of us can ever say afterward that the other person made the hole alone."

* * *

*Ishida*

He began, as he began everything, with the paperwork.

"The Household Registration Act of 1871," he said. "Every soul in Japan entered in a book. Births, deaths, marriages, the names of fathers and the trades of grandfathers. The state's memory. A beautiful idea, if you believe the state should remember. I believed it. I was twenty-two and I believed it the way you believe in rain — it simply fell on you and you didn't question where it came from."

Below them the tide turned. He felt the change in the pier more than heard it, a long shudder through the timbers, the harbor exhaling.

"My section handles Nagasaki prefecture, foreign quarter and waterfront districts. The hardest registries in the empire. People arrive here from everywhere and become no one, or someone else, or two someones at once." He glanced sideways at her. "You'd be astonished — or perhaps you wouldn't — how many entries in my books are works of fiction. Whole neighborhoods of invented fathers. And my job, officially, is to catch the errors. Unofficially—" He stopped. Started again. "Unofficially, my job is to catch the errors that certain parties want caught, and to be blind to the ones they don't."

"Which parties?"

"Which do you think?" He laughed without sound. "Meridian has friends. Not merely friends — tendrils. A director here, a magistrate's brother-in-law there, a man in the customs house whose gambling debts are mysteriously light. The census bureau answers upward, Miss Amberdale. Everything in this country answers upward. And at the top of the line that leads down to me, there are men who receive reports on discrepancies in household registries, and who act on them."

"Act how."

"A woman in Dejima ward whose papers said she was widowed when her husband was seen alive in Shanghai. Reported. Investigated. Her registration revoked. Without valid registration you cannot rent, cannot work legally, cannot marry, cannot exist. She existed for exactly nine days after my report crossed the right desk, and then she stepped off the breakwater at low tide, and the entry in my book was corrected at last: *deceased*." His voice stayed level throughout, which was its own kind of horror. He had clearly rehearsed this account many times, alone, in rooms with locked doors. "I wrote the report that killed her. I didn't know it would kill her. I was being thorough. Thoroughness is praised, you see. It's the one virtue a bureaucrat is permitted, and they use it the way men use drink."

Rosalind was silent beside him. He could feel her silence working, though — sorting, weighing, filing him somewhere. He had the uncomfortable sensation of being an entry in one of her books.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you think I'm the danger," he said. "And you're right. I am. I'm the leash, and someone holds the other end, and the day I stop being useful they'll pull it. So before you decide how much of yourself to show me, you should know exactly what I am and who owns me." He drew a breath. "Three weeks ago I was ordered to audit the Meridian waterfront households. Ordered — do you understand? Not assigned. A list arrived with names already flagged, mine among the auditors, and instructions on what discrepancies to look for. Someone upstairs wants pressure applied to Meridian. Or wants Meridian's rivals armed. Or wants a scandal timed to ruin a specific man. I don't know which. I only know that I was handed you the way a man is handed a tool, and if I fail to use the tool properly, I will be replaced by a man who will."

"And the blackmail," she said quietly. "Your visit to my tenement. Your questions about my father's name. Was that the order too?"

"The order was to find pressure points." He said it flatly, refusing the mercy of euphemism. "You have excellent pressure points, Miss Amberdale. Forged papers. A dead mother with an inconvenient history. A guardian with a grudge against the company. You are, forgive me, a beautifully documented vulnerability." He turned fully to face her then, and made himself hold her eyes, because looking away now would cost more than any truth. "So. That is what stands next to you on this pier. A coerced instrument of men I've never met, who blackmailed a frightened girl because it was efficient, and who is telling her all of it because — "

"Because?"

"Because the woman in Dejima ward haunts me," he said simply. "Because I have decided I would rather fall on my own terms than be swung like a hammer on someone else's, and because when I read your mother's name in the old diving registers — Kume, lost off Iojima — I checked further. On my own time. With my own money. I bought tea for retired ama until their stories ran together, and every story matched: Kume Amberdale dove longer than any woman on that coast, and the company's boats took her pearls and paid her in rice, and when the sea took her, the company's books recorded a loss of equipment and nothing else. Not even her name." He paused. "They wrote *one diving suit, damaged beyond repair*."

Rosalind made a small sound. Not a sob — he would remember it differently, later, as the sound of a ledger closing. Of arithmetic arriving at its total.

"That," she said finally, "is the cruelest entry I have ever heard."

"Yes. Which is why I'm here at night, feeding a dog and picking at a padlock, instead of home asleep like a sensible instrument. Because somewhere in that building is the book where they write people as equipment, and I have spent ten years being the pen in other men's hands, and I am done." He spread his empty palms. "That is my confession. Coerced, complicit, compromised, and finished. Now you know the worst of me."

* * *

*Rosalind*

The tide had turned while he spoke, and the harbor's voice changed with it, deeper now, drawing breath. Rosalind sat with her back against the workshop wall and let his words settle into her the way silt settles in still water.

She had spent weeks assuming Ishida was the company's knife. It was simpler that way — one enemy, one shape, one set of footprints to avoid. The truth was worse and better: he was nobody's knife except circumstance's, and circumstance held a great many knives, and most of them were aimed at each other. She recognized the condition. It was hers. Two liars, then, both forged rather than born, both bent to shapes by hands they'd never seen.

"You could have lied better just now," she said at last. "You told me yourself — liars keep answers polished in their pockets. That confession was not polished. It had splinters in it."

"I'm out of practice at lying to people who matter."

"Doesn't your bureau teach anything useful?"

"Only formats." Almost a smile. "Confessions aren't covered in the manual."

She pulled her knees up and considered the padlock gleaming faintly at the end of the pier. Considered the smell of ink breathing under the door. Considered Sato-baasan's face at the ama beach, and the tally-sticks wrapped in cloth beneath her floorboards, and Kenji's white knuckles when she'd shown him the copied page — *burn it*, he'd said, love speaking in the grammar of fear. Everyone around her was afraid in a different dialect. Perhaps that was what a family was: people afraid in the same direction.

"If we do this," she said, "we do it by rules. I live by rules. Busy hands, closed mouth, and never sign anything you haven't read twice. Rule one: neither of us lies to the other again. Not about danger, not about orders received, not about fear. Fear especially. A lie about fear kills faster than any other kind."

"Agreed. Rule two?"

"Rule two: we assume we're watched, always, and we give the watchers nothing worth reporting. We meet irregularly. We speak of census matters in public. If either of us is taken, the other knows nothing — genuinely knows nothing, because we'll arrange it so there's genuinely nothing known. No notes between us. No names written down. Memory only."

"Rule three," Ishida said, "the copies stay separate from both of us. If they're found in your rooms or mine, we're both ended. They need a third place. A place no one connects to either of us."

"The harbor workshop is out, obviously."

"For the moment. Though mark this —" he nodded at the padlock — "they'll move whatever's in there soon. Loading begins within the fortnight, if the Kaiyo-maru keeps her schedule. A building full of crates going aboard means confusion, and confusion means opportunity. Whatever we intend to take, we take during loading chaos, not before and not after. Before, the security is too tight. After, the proof sails to Shanghai."

"And the demolition?"

His head turned sharply. "What demolition?"

She told him then — the rumor running along the docks like oil along water, that the waterfront annexes were slated for clearing once the season's shipping cleared, that the workshop would come down to make way for a coal depot backed by new money. She watched the news rearrange his face, subtracting time from it.

"Then the clock is shorter than the ship," he said quietly. "Ships can be delayed. Demolition notices, once posted, run like trains." He exhaled slowly. "All the more reason. We need eyes inside before the crates move. The padlock defeats us tonight, but doors defeat no one forever. There will be a delivery. There is always a delivery. Paper goes out as well as in — manifests, waybills. Someone opens that door for business, however secret the business."

"Someone," Rosalind said. "Or some night. Tides don't wait for office hours, and cargo that weighs like human cargo moves best at odd hours."

He looked at her with something that was nearly respect, and she realized she was doing the same — measuring him not as the census taker, the threat, the leash, but as a fellow creature of columns and caution, a man who counted things because counting was the only power he'd ever been given.

"We'll need to divide the work," she said. "You have access I don't — registries, shipping schedules, the customs friendships you despise. I have access you don't — the counting house, Pell's ledgers, the daily rhythms of Meridian's paper. And there's a third access neither of us has."

"Which is?"

"The divers." She said it plainly, letting him hear the weight of it. "Whatever is in that building, whatever crosses that gangplank onto the Kaiyo-maru, the women of the ama beaches will see it first and understand it best. They always have. The companies count on the divers being invisible — poor, female, unlettered. But invisible people see everything. Sato-baasan remembers faces and debts going back forty years. If we can win her — truly win her, not borrow her grief — we'll have watchers on every beach and every pier between here and Iojima."

Ishida was silent a moment. Then he took from his sleeve a small notebook, looked at it, and — deliberately, holding her gaze — tore out a blank page, and another, and put the notebook away. "No names written down," he said. "Memory only. So let us memorize. Tomorrow I'll walk the ama beach at Ohama as a bored official checking registrations, and I will be so tedious, so endlessly tedious about household details, that every woman on that beach will know within the hour that the census has come sniffing — and they'll bring word to whoever among them thinks fastest." He inclined his head slightly toward her. "Word travels from the beaches to the tenements faster than any official dispatch in the empire. I'm counting on it."

"You'll make them hate you."

"Hate is attention," Ishida said. "Attention is a door. I've been hated by better people than the ama of Ohama, and survived." He rose, brushing salt and old paint from his jacket, and offered his hand to help her up. She took it after a moment's hesitation — the first time they had touched — and his grip was dry and careful, the grip of a man handling a page he was afraid to crease.

They stood together a last moment at the pier's dark shoulder, two shadows with a locked building between them and the sea beyond it, patient and enormous, keeping its accounts.

"Two liars," Rosalind said. "Trusting each other anyway."

"Not trusting," Ishida corrected gently. "Verifying. Repeatedly. Forever. Trust is what people with clean hands can afford. We'll settle for verification." He paused, and added, quieter, "Though I find I hope you lie to me beautifully for years."

The bell across the water rang the hour — eleven — and far up the shore road a lantern bobbed: the watchman, right on schedule, his dog bought and his rounds unremarkable. They slipped away separately, she along the seawall, he through the fish sheds, two threads unpicking themselves from the same knot.

Behind them the workshop held its breath and its ink, and the tide came on, faithful as arithmetic, carrying everything toward morning.
