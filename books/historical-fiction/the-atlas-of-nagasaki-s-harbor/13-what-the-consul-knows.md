## 13. What the Consul Knows

The British Consulate stood on the Oura bluff like a man who had learned never to turn his back on the sea. Rosalind had walked past it perhaps two hundred times in her life — a girl with a fish basket, an errand-runner for Kenji, a clerk with a ledger under her arm — and in all those passings she had never once looked at it the way she looked at it now, on a bright Thursday morning in October, with the smell of the tide in her nostrils and a lie folded neatly in her pocket like a letter she had rehearsed too many times.

She had dressed for the part. That was the first rule of a good forgery, Kenji had taught her when she was nine and practicing her father's name on scraps of paper: *a lie must be dressed better than the truth, because the truth is believed for free.* So she wore her second-best blouse, the one with the high collar, and her good skirt, and she carried a leather folio that held nothing but a blank sheet of paper and her own heartbeat. Her hair she had pinned with the plainest of her mother's combs, the tortoiseshell one, because it made her look older, and older meant taken seriously, and taken seriously meant not looked at too closely. She had even practiced her walk. Not too quick. Quick was guilty. Not too slow. Slow was afraid. She walked the way Mr. Pell walked down the counting-house aisle on a Tuesday: a woman with a small, tedious errand, a woman nobody would remember by evening.

*Amberdale. Rosalind Amberdale. Clerk, Meridian Trading Company. Here about a passport.*

The steps up to the consulate were granite, worn smooth at the centers, and she counted them — fourteen — because counting steadied her the way prayer steadied other people. At the top, a garden of clipped camellias, and a door of dark wood with a brass plate, and a bell-pull that rang somewhere deep inside the building with a sound like a spoon dropped in an empty house.

The door was opened not by an Englishman but by a Chinese house-boy in a white jacket, who looked at her with the flat professional blankness of a man who had admitted a thousand petitioners and would admit a thousand more.

"I've come about a passport," she said in English, and heard with relief that her accent held — the careful, slightly formal English Kenji had drilled into her, the English of grammar books and shipping documents, an English that belonged nowhere in particular and could therefore belong anywhere.

The boy led her down a corridor that smelled of beeswax and cold tea, past a map of the world so large it covered an entire wall, its oceans a blue so confident it made the real sea outside look like something provisional. Nagasaki harbor was a small brown smudge on that map. Her whole life, the tenement, the divers' beach, the workshop, the counting house — a smudge, and not even a named one.

The clerk's office was small and cluttered and hot, the stove already lit against the autumn damp, and the clerk himself was younger than she had expected: a thin man of perhaps twenty-eight with sandy hair going early grey at the temples, wire spectacles, ink on the side of his right hand in the way of a man who wrote all day and thought about nothing else. His nameplate said **E. W. FLETCHER, ASSISTANT CLERK**, and beneath the nameplate, half-hidden, a Japanese woodblock print of a heron, which struck her as a small, private rebellion, or perhaps only a souvenir.

"Miss — ?" he said, standing, and there it was, the first test, the first door.

"Amberdale," she said. "Rosalind Amberdale. Of the Meridian Trading Company."

"Ah." He gestured her into the chair opposite his desk. "One of the big houses. You'll want the commercial section, I expect — Mr. Hargreaves handles trade papers."

"It's a private matter, actually." She set the empty folio on her knee and kept her hands folded over it, busy hands, closed mouth. "A passport matter. For a relative."

"Go on."

"My late father's sister. She was born in England — Manchester — but married a Japanese national, and after his death she wishes to return. She's been told she may have lost her British status through the marriage." She had built this story the way she built her ledgers, every figure resting on a figure beneath it: a sister, because a sister was plausible for a woman of her apparent age without inviting questions about parents; Manchester, because Kenji had a real acquaintance from Manchester and Rosalind could speak of it with borrowed detail — the rain, the mills, the chapel bells; a widow, because a widow asked for help rather than answers. "I'm her — well, I'm the one who does her paperwork. She trusts me with it. I thought I'd come and ask what she'd need."

Fletcher had picked up a pen while she talked, and now he wrote *Amberdale* on a slip of paper, and she watched him do it and thought: *that's how it begins. That's how Ishida began. A name on a slip of paper.*

"It depends," he said, "on the circumstances of the marriage. If she married before the Naturalization Act — no, you won't want the statutes. In plain terms: a British woman who marries a foreign national takes her husband's nationality. If he's dead, she doesn't automatically revert. There are provisions, but they're — " he made a small, apologetic gesture with the pen — "they're not generous. She'd want to petition. Evidence of birth, of course. Parish records if she can get them. It takes time."

"Of course." Rosalind let a small, rueful smile touch her mouth, the smile of a woman who had expected nothing better. "Everything takes time."

"Everything takes time," Fletcher agreed, and something in the way he said it — a tiredness under it, a man who had said the same sentence to a hundred faces — made her like him, which was inconvenient, because she had come here to use him.

* * *

She could have left then. The errand was done; the pretext had held; the clerk had been courteous and the door had opened and closed behind her without a sound. She could have walked back down the fourteen steps and along the bund and into the counting house and been at her desk by the half-hour bell, and no one — not Pell, not Ishida, not the men above them all — would ever have known she had climbed the bluff at all.

But she had not come for a passport. And Fletcher, gathering her blank folio to hand back to her, glanced at it, and glanced at her, and did the thing she had been hoping for and dreading in equal measure: he lingered.

"You said Meridian," he said. "You're in their offices?"

"I'm an assistant in the bookkeeping room."

"Ah." He set the folio down again, unopened, and leaned back. "Then perhaps you can answer something for me, since you've answered my questions about your aunt. We've had — " he paused, choosing — "correspondence. From a shipping concern in Shanghai. Asking after certain vessels. Whether they've cleared here, whether their manifests were countersigned, that sort of thing. Routine. But the same names keep appearing, and the same tonnages, and the figures don't — " he stopped himself, and she saw him do it, saw the clerk's instinct collide with the clerk's caution and lose. "It's not my department. Mr. Hargreaves says it's not anyone's department. But you're in the trade. You'd know if — " and then, more carefully: "Is the Meridian well-regarded? As an employer?"

The question hung in the hot little room between them, and Rosalind understood that she was standing at a second door, and that this one had no rehearsed answer waiting behind it.

She thought of the columns that did not reconcile. *Ceramic ware*, forty-one crates, weighed like sleeping men. She thought of Sato-baasan's face on the beach at low tide, the way the old woman had gripped her wrist and said *your mother's eyes, your mother's eyes*, and then had looked over her shoulder at the road before she said anything else at all. She thought of the tally-sticks she had not yet seen but had already begun to dream about, rows of notched wood in a box somewhere, each notch a breath.

"They pay on time," she said. "The ledgers are kept to a very high standard."

It was true, every word, and it was a lie, every word, and Fletcher looked at her for a long moment over his spectacles, and she could not tell whether he had heard the truth or the lie or both.

"High standards," he repeated. "Yes. I've heard that about them."

* * *

She should have gone. She knew it even as she stayed, the way a swimmer knows the current has taken her. But the room was warm and the man was lonely — she could see it now, the loneliness, in the way he had turned his chair a few degrees toward her, in the woodblock heron, in the fact that he had asked her a question at all — and lonely people open doors, and she had come all this way to find a door.

"Mr. Fletcher," she said, and made her voice light, made it the voice of a woman making conversation over the teacups, "may I ask something impertinent? It's about my aunt again, in a way. Suppose — purely suppose — a person in this city knew of something improper. Something in the shipping trade. Not a crime anyone had witnessed, you understand. Only figures that wouldn't sit straight. Names that didn't match faces. What would the consulate do with a thing like that?"

The change in him was immediate and total. It was like watching a lamp turned down: the warmth went out of his face by degrees, and what remained was official, and the official face was younger and harder and much less kind.

"Who have you been speaking to?" he said.

"No one. I told you — suppose."

"Miss Amberdale." He set the pen down with great precision, parallel to the blotter's edge. "I'm going to tell you something, and I'd take it as a kindness if you didn't make me regret telling it. This consulate is not a court. We are here to protect British subjects and British commerce, and to write letters. If a British subject is wronged, we protest. If British property is threatened, we request the protection of the Japanese authorities. If a person — " and here he looked at her, and she felt the look go through the collar and the combs and the careful walk and land on the girl from the tenement beneath, the diver's daughter, though she could not have said how he saw it — "if a person who is not a British subject comes to us with rumors about a British firm, do you know what happens?"

She said nothing.

"A letter is written. The letter goes to the governor's office. The governor's office contains men who dine with the principals of the Meridian Trading Company. And the person who brought the rumor — " he spread his hands, an elegant, terrible little gesture — "finds that her name has been written on a slip of paper, and passed along, and that the next ship to Shanghai carries a copy of it."

The stove ticked. Somewhere down the corridor a door closed. On the wall the enormous map held its confident blue oceans, and Nagasaki was a smudge, and the smudge was burning, and the map did not care.

"Rumor," Fletcher said, more quietly now, and there was something in his voice that was almost — not quite, but almost — an apology. "The consul acts on documents. Sworn statements. Original papers. Figures that can be checked against other figures, by men with no interest in the outcome. Anything less is gossip, and gossip in this town has a way of coming back to the gossiper. I've seen it. I've written the letters." He picked up her folio and held it out to her. "Your aunt should petition. The forms are on the table by the door. I'm sorry it isn't a better answer."

She took the folio. Her hands were steady. She had trained them to be steady across ten thousand columns of figures, and they obeyed her now, though inside she felt something she could only describe as the tide going out — the hope she had carried up the bluff in her pocket draining away through the floorboards, leaving the mud of what remained: *documents. Original papers. Men with no interest in the outcome.* And where, in all of Nagasaki, were such documents to be found? Locked in a harbor workshop. Guarded by a company that burned what it could not own. Three weeks from sailing out of reach on a ship named for the ocean itself.

"Thank you, Mr. Fletcher," she said. "You've been very clear."

"Miss Amberdale." He stood, and hesitated, and for one moment the official face slipped and she saw the young man with ink on his hand and a heron on his wall. "I never heard your name today. I mean — " he fumbled — "I mean that if a person were ever to bring documents. Real ones. Sworn, witnessed, original. To this office. The consul would have no choice. None. It's the one thing they can't write a letter about. But the person would want to be certain. Certain beyond argument. Because there wouldn't be a second letter, do you understand? There wouldn't be a second anything."

"I understand," Rosalind said.

"Good day, then."

"Good day."

* * *

The fourteen steps down were harder than the fourteen steps up. She counted them anyway, out of habit, and found that the numbers would not hold still — she got eleven, then fifteen, then thirteen, and gave up, and stood at the bottom on the road with the harbor spread below her, glittering and indifferent, the masts of a dozen ships leaning against the sky.

*Documents.* She had known it, of course. Some part of her had known it since the night she proved the discrepancy and sat alone in the counting house with her heart hammering, understanding that knowing was worth nothing, that the truth without paper was a pearl without a hole — beautiful, and impossible to string. But knowing a thing and hearing it said in a warm office by a tired young man with the weight of a hundred unwritten letters on him were two different knowings, and the second one sat in her chest like cold ballast.

They would need the master ledger itself. Not a memory of it, not a rumor of it, not a clever girl's arithmetic. The book, or a copy so exact it might as well be the book. From a workshop guarded by men with clubs, before a ship sailed, before a demolition order she could feel coming the way one feels weather in an old wound.

And the copy would have to be perfect. Every figure, every cipher, every countersignature — because Fletcher had told her, in the only language he could safely use, that there would be no second chance. The consul would act once, or never.

She looked out at the water. The tide was turning; she could see it in the changed angle of the moored lighters, in the slow swing of a sampan's wake. Somewhere out there, on a ship or in a depot or in a debt-book in Shanghai, were eleven names — no, she corrected herself, at least eleven, the eleven Sato-baasan had begun to whisper of on the beach, names attached to faces, faces attached to mothers — and the sea between here and there was patient, and the men who owned the sea were patient, and the only thing on Rosalind's side was paper, and the paper did not exist yet.

She turned toward the city, toward the tenement, toward Kenji.

Because there was one man in Nagasaki who had read those ledgers thirty years ago, who had learned their ciphers when the ciphers were new, who had been ruined for saying aloud what the figures said — and who had ordered her to burn the one page she'd copied, and had gone pale as paper when she showed it to him, and had not yet been told that she had hidden it instead.

He was going to have to be told. And more than told.

She walked. Behind her, up on the bluff, the consulate's windows caught the morning sun and threw it back at the harbor like a wall of small, closed doors.
