## 14. Kenji Breaks His Silence

The lamp in Kenji Watanabe's room had a cracked chimney, and through the crack the flame threw a thin line of shadow across the page Rosalind had copied — a seam of darkness running straight through the column of figures, as if the numbers themselves had been wounded.

He had not touched the page in an hour. He sat with his hands folded on the low table, the way he used to sit when he was teaching her long division and she was crying with frustration, patient and still, letting her come back to the work in her own time. But he was not waiting for her now. She had gone home an hour past, up the hill to the tenement, and the copied page lay before him like something washed ashore.

Forty-one names. He counted them again, though counting changed nothing. Forty-one names entered as crew of the *Kaiyo-maru*, each with a wage attached, each wage entered twice — once paid out, once pocketed. He knew the pattern. God help him, he knew it the way a man knows the face of the woman who ruined him: intimately, unwillingly, across thirty years of trying to forget.

Her name had been O-Tsuyu.

Kenji rose, joints cracking, and went to the window. Below him Nagasaki harbor lay in its bowl of hills, lamplit along the quay, dark on the water, the masts of a dozen ships stitched against the last grey of the sky. Somewhere out there the Meridian Company's warehouse lamps burned all night, the way they had burned in 1869, when he was a young interpreter with a British firm's seal on his letter-case and a conviction in his chest that paper could be made to tell the truth if a man was only stubborn enough to read it whole.

He had been wrong about that. Or rather, he had been right, and being right had cost everything, and the truth had drowned anyway, the way O-Tsuyu had drowned — no, that was not honest, even alone in his own head. O-Tsuyu had not drowned. O-Tsuyu had been put aboard a ship at Dejima wharf with eleven other girls whose names appeared in the ledgers as *ceramic ware*, and Kenji had stood on the quay with the proof in his satchel and let a company doctor convince him it was fever, that the girls were indentured laborers going willingly to Shanghai, that his figures must be mistaken because Mr. Hartwell's arithmetic was never wrong.

His figures had not been mistaken. He had learned that two years later, in Shanghai, standing at the door of a house on a street he would not let himself name, where a madam told him no girl called Tsuyu had ever come there — which was itself a kind of answer, because the houses always lied, but the ships' manifests did not, and the manifest said *sold, Shanghai, 1869*, and after that name there was nothing. No house. No grave. No entry anywhere in any book kept by any man that she had continued to exist.

That was what a ledger could do. It could unmake a person so thoroughly that even her disappearance left no mark.

And here it was again, thirty years later, in a child's careful hand — not a child anymore, a young woman of twenty-two who wrote figures the way her mother had dived, cleanly and without wasted motion. Kume's daughter. The girl he had taken in because Kume had once pulled him half-drowned out of the channel off Iojima when his ferry capsized, and because a debt like that does not die with the person who earned it.

He had taught her English, figures, caution. Caution above all. *Busy hands, closed mouth.* He had thought he was arming her. Now he understood he had merely taught her to be quiet in a house that was burning.

*Burn it,* he had told her, three nights ago, holding the first stolen page over the lamp flame until she snatched it back with a look he had never seen on her face before — not defiance exactly; something older and colder. She had hidden it inside the false binding of a hymnal, and she had looked at him then, and he had known that whatever he ordered next, she would do otherwise. She had Kume's chin when she set it that way.

He had spent three days telling himself that silence was wisdom. That he was sixty-one years old, that his lungs were bad, that a man once dismissed from the firm for *insubordination and falsified testimony* — their words; the falsification had been theirs — could do nothing against a syndicate that owned customs men, census men, half the consular clerks, and possibly more than half the police. That loving the girl meant keeping her small and invisible and alive.

But tonight he had taken out the old cipher key.

It lived at the bottom of a lacquered box beneath his winter clothes, wrapped in oilcloth, and it was not a key at all but a sheet of memorized substitutions he had never dared write down — the Meridian house cipher, the one the senior partners used for the true entries, the ones that ran parallel to the honest-looking columns. A date-cipher built on the founding year of the company, 1858, with the digits cycling through the fiscal quarters; simple enough, elegant enough, and invisible unless you already suspected the books were lying. He had carried it in his head for thirty years like a stone he could not spit out. Tonight he had laid it on the table beside Rosalind's copied page, and the two objects had seemed to recognize each other.

Look, the arrangement said. Look at what your forty-one names become when you read them through the old key.

He had looked. And the forty-one names had resolved into something worse than ghost-wages: they became a schedule. Departure dates. Transfer fees. A notation in the margin — *K-rō, 11 heads, balance owing* — that used the old trade abbreviation for *karamono*, goods from Kara, foreign goods, human beings entered as cargo. Eleven heads. The same number as the girls of 1869. Someone in the syndicate had a taste for symmetry, or perhaps the number simply repeated the way tides repeat, evil keeping its own calendar.

Kenji pressed his palms against the cold window glass until his fingertips went white.

He thought about the morning he had been dismissed. Mr. Hartwell had not shouted. Hartwell never shouted. He had folded his hands on the desk among the ivory paperweights and said, *Watanabe-san, you are a valuable man, but you have allowed sentiment to corrupt your figures,* and the phrase had followed Kenji down every year since, because it was true in a way — he had let his horror at what the figures meant make him careless, loud, visible, and they had destroyed him with paperwork, quietly, the way they did everything. Dismissed for cause. Blacklisted from every foreign firm in the treaty port. His wife had left him within the year, taking their son to Osaka, and Kenji had lived since on translation work and small debts and the fierce, unspoken penance of raising a dead diver's daughter.

For thirty years he had believed his punishment was poverty. It was not. His punishment was this room, this hour, this page — being shown that the thing he failed to stop had grown fat and old and respectable while he grew thin and old and poor, and that it was now preparing to sail away with eleven more O-Tsuyus, and that the only person willing to stand in its path was a bookkeeper's assistant with forged papers and his own cowardice for a teacher.

No. Not his only ally. There was the census man, Ishida — a creature Kenji distrusted on principle, for census men had been the instrument of the household registry that nearly exposed Rosalind, and yet the man had chosen to bite the hand that fed him, which was either courage or a longer game, and Kenji intended to find out which. There was Sato-baasan on the divers' beach, who had buried a husband and two sons to the sea and would not bury her granddaughter to a ledger without a fight. There was Pell, whose position Kenji could not yet read, and mistrusted accordingly.

And there was himself. Sixty-one years old, bad lungs, thirty years of silence behind him, and one thing no one else in Nagasaki possessed: the cipher, and the memory, and the standing — once, long ago — to authenticate what the documents said. A copy proved nothing. A copy authenticated by the man whose dismissal had been engineered precisely to bury this knowledge — that was a different instrument entirely. That was a blade.

*You have allowed sentiment to corrupt your figures.*

Perhaps. But there was another kind of corruption, and he had breathed it for thirty years, and he found tonight that he could no longer afford it.

* * *

She came the next evening at dusk, as arranged by a note passed through the fish-seller, written in the old shorthand he had taught her — ostensibly a lesson request, *sensei, I cannot reconcile the third quarter*, which was true in every sense.

He had cleaned the lamp chimney. He had laid out the table properly: inkstone, fresh paper, the hymnal with its false binding, and beside them, uncovered for the first time in her presence, the lacquered box.

Rosalind knelt opposite him and looked at the box, then at his face. She had Kume's face broadened slightly by her father's English bones, and just now it held the careful blankness she wore at the counting house, the one that had cost him a pang every time he saw it because he had taught her that too.

"You opened it," she said.

"I opened it." He lifted the lid. Inside, on black silk gone grey with age, lay nothing but a slip of paper covered in his own cramped hand — the substitution table, finally written down after three decades, the ink barely dry. "Before you say anything, understand what I am giving you. This is not a favor. It is a confession."

"Sensei—"

"Let me finish. When I am finished, if you still want my help, we will work until your hands give out, and I will authenticate every page with my name, and if they hang anyone for it, they will hang me first, which at my age is less a sacrifice than a convenience." He heard his own voice go dry and formal, the courtroom voice, the voice he had used at thirty-one and never needed again, and was surprised to find it still worked. "In 1869 I found these same entries. Eleven girls. I had the proof entire, and I let a man with ivory paperweights persuade me my arithmetic was wrong, and by the time I understood it was not, the ship was past Fukue Island and one of those girls was named O-Tsuyu, and she was sixteen years old, and she had once sold me persimmons on the Ohato quay and laughed at my accent."

Rosalind had gone very still. In the lamplight her eyes were wet but her jaw was set — Kume's jaw, the diver's jaw, the one that stayed shut against pressure.

"I told you to burn the pages," Kenji said. "I was wrong. Not because burning them would have been safe — it would have been safer, and safer is what I have chosen my whole life since, and look what it bought. I was wrong because the fire is coming anyway. They will burn the workshop themselves, and the ledger with it, and every name in it will be unwritten, the way she was unwritten. The only question is whether a true copy exists somewhere outside their reach when the smoke clears." He turned the slip of paper around and pushed it across the table to her. "So. We make the copy. All of it. And I teach you to read what the honest columns are hiding."

She picked up the cipher table in both hands, the way one receives a diploma or a death sentence, and read it top to bottom, and he watched her mind move behind her eyes — he had watched that mind for fifteen years and never tired of it — and then she looked up.

"The founding year," she said. "Eighteen fifty-eight. The digits cycle by quarter."

"And the quarter-mark shifts the substitution by the fiscal month. You'll see it in the marginal notations. The visible ledger is written to be audited. The true entries live in the margins, in dates that don't parse — a shipment dated the eleventh month of a year the company didn't trade in, that sort of thing. Once you know the key, the nonsense dates resolve into transfer records." He drew a fresh sheet toward himself. "We begin with what you copied. Read me the fourth entry."

She read. He translated, wrote, explained. Her pen moved; his pen moved; the lamp burned steady through its mended chimney. Twice she caught errors in his Japanese rendering and he corrected them without embarrassment, feeling something loosen in his chest that had been clenched since 1871 — the pleasure of working alongside a mind that met his own, which he had assumed was finished for him, the way love was finished, and music, and standing upright in a room without apology.

Near midnight she paused, pen lifted, and asked the question he had been waiting for.

"Why now? You've known thirty years. Why does my one stolen page change anything?"

He considered lying to her, and rejected it — she deserved better, and besides, she detected his lies the way gulls detect offal.

"Because for thirty years I told myself the past was sunk," he said. "Ships sink, cargo sinks, guilt sinks, and the water closes over, and a man learns to eat his rice above the place where it happened. Then you brought me a page with eleven heads noted in the margin, and the water did not close. It has never closed. I have been breathing through it all this time." He set down his pen. "Your mother saved my life once, and I repaid her by saving her daughter badly — I taught you to hide, which is not the same as teaching you to survive. Let me try to repay the rest of the debt while I am still above ground. That is why now. Not virtue. Arithmetic. My remaining years against their remaining crimes, and mine are fewer, so mine must be spent faster."

Rosalind bowed over the table, low, her forehead nearly touching the wood, and when she rose her eyes were dry and absolutely clear.

"Then teach me the rest," she said. "All of it. Before the tide turns."

They worked until the third watch. He taught her the quarter-shift, the fiscal-month rotation, the way the partners disguised transfer fees as insurance premiums and head-counts as tonnage. He showed her how to verify a ciphered date against the harbor tide-tables, because the syndicate scheduled its shipments by neap tides when the customs launches lay idle — a detail he had discovered in 1869 and never forgotten, and which meant, he realized as he said it aloud, that the *Kaiyo-maru*'s sailing date could be predicted from the almanac alone, and that the almanac gave them a number of days he did not like at all.

When at last she gathered her papers to leave, he stopped her at the door with the lacquered box in his hands.

"There is one more thing," he said. "A condition. When this is finished — if we are not all in prison or ashes — you will register under your true name. Both halves of it. No more forged papers, no more hiding. If I am to spend my last years buying you the right to exist on paper, you will use it."

Something crossed her face — fear, and beneath the fear, a hunger so old it had worn smooth, like a pebble in a streambed.

"We'll see," she said, which from her, he knew, was not refusal. It was a door left unlocked.

He watched her go down the hill toward the tenement, a slim figure with a scholar's satchel, moving through the lamplight like a woman carrying water across ice. Then he closed the door, sat again at the table, and did what he had not done in thirty years: he took a clean sheet and began drafting, formally, in both languages, a certified attestation — *I, Kenji Watanabe, formerly interpreter to the Meridian Trading Company, do solemnly affirm...*

Outside, far down in the harbor, a ship's bell rang the middle watch, and the sound came up the hill thin and cold, and Kenji found himself counting days against the almanac's neap tide, and the number the arithmetic gave him was seventeen — seventeen days until the *Kaiyo-maru* sailed, and the master ledger still waited in the harbor workshop, and somewhere in the city, men whose business was unwriting people had already begun to smell smoke.
