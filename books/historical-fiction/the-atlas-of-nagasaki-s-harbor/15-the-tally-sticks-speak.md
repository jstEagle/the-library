## 15. The Tally-Sticks Speak

The tally-sticks smelled of the sea even after thirty years shut away in a lacquered box that had traveled from Iojima to Dejima to a rented room above a noodle shop and back again, each migration leaving its sediment — a grain of beach sand in the corner of the lid, a hairline crack filled with salt that wept white whenever the weather turned damp. Rosalind laid them out on Kenji's floor mat in rows of ten, the way she had been taught to lay out figures, and the lamplight caught the notches cut into the bamboo so that each stick wore a little spine of shadows down its length, like the vertebrae of something small that had once swum.

There were forty-one sticks. Sato-baasan had counted them out into Rosalind's hands three nights ago on the ama beach, wrapping them in a scrap of oilcloth as though she were handing over a child.

"Every diver who ever signed with Meridian," she had said. "Notch for contract. Notch for season. The cross-cut at the end" — her thumbnail had found it without looking, the way you find your own door latch in the dark — "that is when they were paid off. Or when they stopped coming home."

Now, in Kenji's room, with the master ledger pages fanned across the tatami and Ishida sitting cross-legged by the window with his census book open as a prop for his elbows, Rosalind began the work of making bamboo speak arithmetic.

* * *

It was slower than she had expected, and harder, and she was ashamed of how long it took her to understand why. She had spent two years in the counting house reconciling columns; she could smell an error in a freight manifest the way Pell could smell a forged signature. But this was not reconciliation. This was translation between two languages that had never been meant to meet: the language of sticks cut by divers who might sign their names but could not write them, and the language of ledgers kept by clerks whose whole craft was the art of saying one thing while meaning another.

"Read me the line again," she said, and Ishida read it, his voice flat with the careful neutrality he wore like his census sash:

*"Ceramic ware, assorted, crated. Manifest weight, four hundred twenty kan. Vessel Kaiyo-maru, hold two. Consignee, Chōbei Trading Concern, Shanghai."*

"Now the stick." 

She held up the eleventh stick in the second row. Fourteen season-notches. A deep gouge near the top that Sato-baasan had called a shark-mark, cut the winter a fin had come too close off Takashima. And at the end, instead of the clean cross-cut of a paid-off contract, a strange double notch, shallow, almost shy, as if the hand that made it had been hurried or afraid.

"Four hundred twenty kan," Rosalind said slowly, "is the weight of about eleven women."

Ishida's brush stopped.

"That is what I keep arriving at," she went on, because now that she had said it aloud she could not unsay it, and the room seemed to tilt around the sentence. "A grown ama, wet, in her diving clothes with her weights — call it thirty-eight kan. Eleven of them is four hundred and eighteen. Round up for the crate, for the straw packing, for whatever they pack around a human being to make her look like porcelain. Four hundred twenty." She set the stick down with exaggerated care, the way Pell set down a page he did not want to crease. "The ceramics weigh nothing, Mr. Ishida. They never have. I told you in chapter— I told you weeks ago, when I first showed you the discrepancy. But I thought it was silk under the label. Silk, or tin, or opium. Something you could put down and walk away from."

"And now?"

"Now I think hold two of the *Kaiyo-maru* is going to Shanghai full of my mother's sisters."

* * *

Kenji came up the outside stairs while she was still kneeling over the rows, and she heard him pause on the landing the way he always did, gathering himself before entering any room where feeling might be waiting for him. He had been like that for as long as she could remember — the dismissed interpreter, the man who had once spoken five languages and now rationed himself to silence. He came in, looked at the floor covered in sticks and paper, and said nothing, which was his way of asking everything.

"The sticks are contracts," Rosalind said. "Sato-baasan kept them for every diver Meridian signed since before the Restoration. And I have been matching them to the shipping manifests all evening, and Kenji—" Her voice cracked on his name and she hated it and let it crack anyway. "They are not selling ceramics."

He lowered himself onto the mat with the care of a man twice his age, which he nearly was. He picked up a stick — the shark-marked one — and turned it in the lamplight, reading the notches the way he read English, left to right, top to bottom, his lips moving slightly.

"This is Kume's notation," he said at last. "The season marks cut on the left, payments on the right. Your mother used it. It comes from the old Tomoku divers, before the companies came. Each notch means a woman survived a season, and the depth of the cut means how much she was paid, and the cross-cut means the contract ended." His thumb found the double shallow notch at the end of the stick. "This is not a cross-cut."

"No."

"This is what they cut when a diver was transferred. Sold onward, but the company would not say sold. Transferred." He looked up, and in thirty years of knowing him — no, eleven years, she corrected herself; she had known him eleven years and had loved him for all of them without once finding the bottom of him — she had never seen his hands shake. They shook now, very slightly, the way water shakes just before it decides to boil. "Rosalind. How many of these transfer-cuts are there?"

She had already counted. She had counted three times, because the first two times she had hoped she was wrong.

"Nineteen."

"Nineteen women transferred."

"Nineteen women *transferred*, yes. But only some of those transfers match manifest dates I can find. Some go back thirty years, and those ledgers are ash or lie or Shanghai by now. What I can trace — what lines up, stick to manifest, notch to crate-weight, date to date —" She made herself breathe. "Eleven. Eleven transfers onto vessels bound for the Chōbei Concern within the last six years, and of those eleven, nine were transferred onto the *Kaiyo-maru*'s route in the last two seasons. Two more were moved to the Shanghai run last month, after the typhoon took the *Wakatake* off the schedule. That makes eleven alive, if they are alive, on ships that sail the same water the *Kaiyo-maru* sails. The other eight—" She stopped.

"You do not have to say it," Ishida said quietly.

"I am going to say it. Somebody has to say it. Eight transfers with no return manifests, no pay-off records, no settlement of wages owed. Wages owed, Mr. Ishida. Do you understand? The company still owes these women money. There are outstanding balances in the labor rolls — small sums, pitiful sums, a few yen each — carried forward year after year like bad debts, except they are not debts the company owes. They are proof the women existed. You cannot close a wage account for a dead woman unless she died, and there are no death notices. So the balances sit there. Eleven yen here, fourteen there. Ghosts on the books, kept alive by arithmetic because closing them honestly would require admitting what happened to them." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "I have been balancing those balances myself. For two years. I thought they were sloppiness. I thought Mr. Pell was simply a careless bookkeeper."

"Pell is many things," Kenji said, "but careless is not among them."

"No," she agreed. "No, he is not."

* * *

They worked until the third hour, the three of them, and the room filled with the particular quiet of people doing arithmetic that matters. Ishida copied. Rosalind matched. Kenji read the oldest sticks, the ones whose notation had drifted through dialects and decades, and translated notches into names.

Because that was the thing she had not anticipated — the thing no amount of ledger discipline had prepared her for. Sticks had names.

Sato-baasan had carved them herself, small characters near the butt end, half-erased by handling: *O-Hana. O-Sumi. Chiyo. Rin.* Names of women who had gone into the cold green water off Iojima and Takashima and the Goto islands two hundred times a season and come up with pearls and bleeding ears and, eventually, a transfer-cut where their freedom should have been.

Rosalind wrote each name beside its matching manifest line in her smallest, clearest hand, and with each name the ledger changed substance in her mind. It stopped being evidence. Evidence was abstract; evidence was ammunition; evidence was a thing you carried to a consul wrapped in oilcloth. This was becoming something else — a muster roll. A list of the living.

"Chiyo," she said. "Manifest line forty-one, spring consignment, four hundred twelve kan — that is ten women plus packing, and Chiyo's transfer-cut is dated nine days before the sailing, and her wage balance of thirteen yen forty sen has been carried forward for four years." She looked up. "Chiyo is twenty-three years old. Sato-baasan says she dives like an otter. She has a scar on her chin from a coral cut when she was a child."

"How do you know that?" Ishida asked.

"Because Sato-baasan told me. Because Sato-baasan knows every one of these women the way she knows the tide tables, and when she gave me the box she talked for two hours and I wrote it all down." Rosalind touched the notebook in her sleeve. "Names, ages, faces, families. Who has a mother living. Whose husband drowned. Which ones can swim the harbor channel at night if someone opens a hatch for them." She heard her own voice go low and hard, a voice she did not recognize as hers. "We are not carrying paper to the consul, Mr. Ishida. We are carrying people. We just happen to be carrying them on paper."

* * *

Near midnight Kenji lit a fresh lamp, and the new light fell across the rows of sticks and made their shadow-spines point all one direction, like compass needles. He had been silent for a long time — his thinking silence, which Rosalind knew from childhood was more dangerous than his speaking one — and when he finally spoke, he spoke in Japanese, slowly, the way he did when the words mattered too much to risk in either of his borrowed tongues.

"Thirty years ago I audited a shipment for Hollington Brothers. Ceramic ware, crated, heavy. I flagged the weight. My superior smiled and told me the crates held kiln furniture — the clay posts and saggers that protect porcelain in firing. Very dense. Very heavy. Perfectly plausible." His mouth did something that was not a smile. "I was young. I wanted to believe plausible things. I initialed the manifest. Three months later a diver's sister came to the office asking after a woman who had signed a transfer to Shanghai and never sent word home. I remembered the crates. I said nothing, because by then I had begun to suspect what saying something would cost, and I was a coward with a salary. When I finally did speak — two years later, too late, to the wrong man — they ruined me for it. Dismissed, blacklisted, name struck from the company rolls. I learned that a clerk's signature is the cheapest murder weapon in the empire. You kill with a pen stroke and no blood ever reaches your cuff." He set the shark-marked stick down with terrible gentleness. "I will not initial anything tonight. But I will tell you what I know, and what I know is this: the Chōbei Concern does not buy divers for pearl beds. Shanghai has no pearl beds worth the passage. They buy them for the holding houses. The places where women are kept until someone pays."

The word *kept* sat in the room like a stone dropped into a well, and they all listened for it to land, and it went on falling for a long time.

"Then eleven days is too long," Rosalind said. "Ten. However many are left. Every day that ship sits in the harbor loading 'ceramics,' those women are—"

"Are beyond our reach regardless," Kenji said, not unkindly. "You cannot storm a syndicate vessel with a box of bamboo."

"No." She gathered her papers, squared their edges, aligned them precisely — Pell's habit, drilled into her hands if nowhere else. "But I can do something else. If the consul needs documentary proof, then I will give him documents no one can argue with. Not just the manifests. Not just the weights. I will build him a reconciliation. Every transfer-cut matched to its manifest line, every line matched to its wage balance, every balance matched to a named woman with a family that can testify she boarded a ship and never came home. Tally-stick to ledger line to living witness. Three independent records agreeing. Even a consul cannot call that rumor." She looked at the eleven sticks she had separated from the rest, laid apart in their own row, each with its name showing. "And I will start tomorrow morning with the wage rolls, because those are in the counting house, and because I am the only person alive who knows exactly which drawer they sleep in."

* * *

Ishida left first, folding his census book closed with the click of a man resuming his disguise. Kenji banked the lamp. And Rosalind stood at the window with the eleven sticks wrapped again in oilcloth against her ribs, looking down at the black harbor where the *Kaiyo-maru* rode at anchor with a single riding light burning, patient as a spider.

Eleven women. She had entered thousands of figures in two years at the Meridian Company and had never once felt the numbers as flesh. Now every figure had a face, and the faces had names, and the names had mothers, and one of them — she had checked this twice, her heart hammering both times — one of them, *O-Rin*, transferred last spring at age nineteen, was listed in Sato-baasan's murmured genealogy as kin to the old woman herself. Kin. Family. The granddaughter Sato-baasan never mentioned and could not stop protecting.

Resolve did not feel the way she had imagined it would. She had imagined fire, and what she felt was colder and far more durable: the flat, clear certainty of a column that finally adds. Eleven names, eleven sticks, eleven living women, one ship, one consul, ten days.

She blew out the lamp.

And downstairs, faint through the floorboards, she heard Kenji turn his own lock — and then, a moment later, turn it again, and stand very still behind the door, listening to the street, listening for footsteps that should not be there, and hearing, perhaps, or perhaps only imagining, the soft sound of someone hurrying away down the hill toward the counting house.
