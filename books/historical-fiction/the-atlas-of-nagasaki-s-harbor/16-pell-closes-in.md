## 16. Pell Closes In

The page was gone.

Mr. Edmund Pell knew it the way a man knows a tooth has broken — not by looking, but by the sudden wrongness of his own mouth. He had been running his thumb down the shipping abstracts for the *Kaiyo-maru*, third quarter, cross-checking the crew manifests against the wage disbursements, when the ledger's spine gave that soft, hollow whisper beneath his fingers. He turned back to the binding. Turned forward again. Counted the folios once, then twice, then a third time with a pencil mark against each one, and the arithmetic did not care for his hopes: folio two hundred and fourteen was absent. Not torn out crudely — the stub was clean, cut close to the gutter with a blade or a razor — but absent all the same.

He sat very still in the counting house after everyone had gone. The lamps along Dejima wharf were coming up one by one across the water, little orange coins dropped into the blue dusk, and somewhere below a winch groaned through its last load of the day. Pell listened to the building settle around him the way he had listened to it for eleven years, and for the first time he heard it as what it was: a body holding its breath.

Folio 214. He could have recited its contents from memory, which was precisely the problem. Forty-one names entered as crew. Wages disbursed against signatures no harbor master had ever witnessed. A vessel's articles signed in a hand that repeated itself too faithfully, the same loop, the same loop, the same loop — because the men whose names they were had never held a pen, or perhaps had never been asked. He had entered those figures himself, four months ago, under instruction from above, and he had told himself the thing he always told himself: *I keep the books. I am not the book.*

Now someone else had read the book and taken a leaf of it, and the leaf was in the world, loose, like a spark on a dry roof.

* * *

He went home the long way, past the lantern stalls closing up on Shinchi, past the smell of grilled squid and cheap sake, because he could not bear the short way. The short way passed the window of the counting house's side entrance, and he kept imagining himself seen there, a man hurrying, a man with something in his face. A man must learn to have nothing in his face. That was the first lesson Nagasaki had taught him, eleven years ago, when he'd stepped off the P&O steamer with a clerkship in the Meridian Company and a head full of England, and it was the lesson he had paid most dearly to keep.

His rooms above the chandlery were cold. He lit the stove, put the kettle on out of habit, and did not drink the tea. Instead he unlocked the bottom drawer of his writing desk — three locks now, where once there had been none — and took out the packet that lived there wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it, though he knew every line of it the way a sinner knows his catechism.

A promissory note, dated six years past, drawn on a house called Yamamoto & Sons, merchants, Nagasaki. Two hundred Mexican dollars at interest that compounded like a disease. Beneath it, another note. Then a letter, unsigned, thanking him for his "continued discretion regarding certain manifest irregularities," and observing that a man with obligations was a man who understood the value of cooperation. Then a third paper, the worst one, the one he touched least often: a copy of a deposition, never filed, sworn by a Chinese stoker named Lin who had jumped ship in Nagasaki harbor and claimed — before he was quietly returned to his ship, and before the ship sailed, and before Lin was never seen again — that men were being carried north in the *Kaiyo-maru*'s hold as cargo, sold against their debts to a Shanghai syndicate, and that the company's own ledgers recorded them as free seamen drawing honest wages.

Pell had copied that deposition himself, in his own fair round hand, because the man who brought it to him had said, pleasantly, *You'll want to know exactly what you're protecting, Mr. Pell. It makes the protection more sincere.*

That was six years ago. The debt had been two hundred dollars. The debt was now unpayable by any arithmetic he permitted himself to perform, and he was a good enough accountant to know that this was the entire design. You did not chain a clerk with a single payment. You chained him with a sum he could never reach, and then you let him feel, every morning when he sharpened his pen, that the chain was of his own forging — that he had signed, that he had drunk the tea he should have poured out, that he had looked at Lin's deposition and copied it instead of carrying it to the consul two streets away. Two streets. Eleven years. He could walk it in ten minutes. He had walked it a thousand times in his mind and arrived nowhere, because the consul's door opened onto a world where Edmund Pell was not a man with obligations but a man with accomplices, and the difference between those two words was the whole width of the sea between here and home, and he had no ticket, and no name that would survive the crossing.

The kettle screamed. He took it off without pouring.

* * *

Who had taken the page?

He made a list, because lists were how he kept the world from dissolving. He was aware, even as he drew the ruled lines in his notebook, that there was something obscene about it — a man cataloguing his own ruin in double entry — but the habit held him upright, and he needed to be upright.

Not the syndicate's own people; they would sooner burn the folio than steal it, and burning left ash, and ash was evidence, and these men were far too careful for evidence. Not young Hobbes, the junior clerk, who could not find Tuesday in a calendar and whose only vice was the racecourse. Not the coolie staff, who could neither read the English entries nor afford the razor that had cut so cleanly along the gutter.

Which left, when he set down his pencil and looked at what he had written, one name on the page, and the name had been sitting there all along, patient as ink.

Miss Amberdale.

He turned the pencil over and over. Rosalind Amberdale, twenty-two years old, engaged eleven months, recommended by a reference that had checked out just well enough — he remembered now, with the unpleasant clarity of hindsight, remembering — that he had wondered at the time why a girl with her figures had been available at all. She reconciled columns faster than he did. She had found the error in the silk invoices in March that had saved the company forty pounds, and she had found it without being asked, quietly, the way one finds things one is not supposed to be looking for. She worked late without complaint. She asked no questions.

She asked no questions. In eleven years, Pell had learned that the dangerous ones were never the questioners. The questioners you could answer, or dodge, or flatter. The dangerous ones were the ones who watched, and said nothing, and went on watching — and Miss Amberdale watched the ledgers the way a cat watches a mousehole, with a stillness that he had mistaken, until tonight, for diligence.

And there was the other thing. The thing he had noticed in her second week and decided, deliberately, not to think about: that she handled Japanese figures — the old soroban reckoning, the merchant marks of the local houses — with a fluency no English governess imparts. That once, when a dockside supplier had shouted something angry across the yard, she had turned toward the sound half a beat before translation was possible, as if the words had needed no translating at all. Her papers were in order. Her papers were immaculate. In Pell's experience, immaculate papers were like immaculate ledgers: the tidier they stood, the harder someone had worked to make them stand that way.

He thought of her bent over the desk by the window, lamplight on her dark hair, her pen moving in that quick sure hand, and felt something twist in him that was not entirely suspicion. He liked her. That was the humiliating truth of it, the thing he would admit to no one: in eleven years of cold rooms and colder arithmetic, the girl had become the one part of the day he did not dread. When she looked up from her column and smiled — briefly, professionally, the smile of an employee — something in his chest unclenched that he had not known was clenched. He had raised her pay in the spring and told himself it was because she'd earned it, which was true, and had not examined the other reason, which was also true: that kindness was the only currency he had left that wasn't counterfeit.

And now she might be the razor at the gutter of folio 214.

* * *

Three days he watched her.

He told himself it was diligence, management, prudence; he used all the respectable words, and they fit as poorly as his Sunday coat. He arranged the office so that his desk commanded hers. He invented errands that took him past her table. He noted — noting was all he had left, noting was his trade — that she stayed late twice, that on the second night she locked her drawer before going to fetch water, which no innocent clerk does, and that she had begun wearing her shawl indoors, wound close, as though something might need concealing against her body even in a warm room.

On the fourth evening he dismissed Hobbes early, sent the coolies home, and waited until the wharf bells rang the hour, and then he did the thing he had rehearsed in front of the cloudy mirror that morning until his face learned it: he walked to her desk with a ledger under his arm and a mild expression, and he said,

"Miss Amberdale. Might I trouble you a moment?"

She looked up. Her eyes were steady. God help him, her eyes were steady, and some cowardly chamber of his heart whispered, *then it isn't her, it can't be her,* and another chamber, older and better schooled, answered: *the steady ones are always the ones.*

"Of course, Mr. Pell."

"I've been doing the quarterly audit." He laid the ledger open on her desk, to the abstracts, near the place where the gap lived. "Crew manifests against disbursements. Routine." He watched her hands. Hands betrayed people; faces could be taught, but hands were honest. Hers rested folded on the desk, quiet as sleeping birds. "You'll recall entering the wage abstracts for the *Kaiyo-maru* in the spring."

"I recall the work, sir. Not the individual pages."

"No. Of course." He turned a leaf. Another. Arrived at the stub of folio 214 and let his finger rest beside it, lightly, the way a man touches a bruise to confirm it still hurts. "It seems we're a page light. Folio 214. Cut out, not torn — someone was careful." He raised his eyes to hers. "Careful work. I'd know whose, if I saw it."

For a moment — he would replay this moment for weeks afterward, examining it like a flawed coin — for a moment something moved behind her composure. Not guilt. He knew guilt when he saw it; he shaved above it every morning. It was something swifter and more frightened and more resolute, a bird startled off water, and then it was gone, and she said, in a voice of perfect clerical regret:

"How strange. I enter what's put in front of me, sir. I don't audit the binding."

"No," he said gently. "You don't. You only count what other people are afraid of."

The silence that followed lasted three seconds and contained, he felt, an entire correspondence. Somewhere outside a barge hooted; gulls quarreled over the fish market leavings; the ordinary harbor went on being ordinary, indifferent to the small silent duel of two bookkeepers across a scarred oak desk.

"If you're asking me something, Mr. Pell," she said, "you might ask it plainly."

And there it was — the open door, the moment a man either walks through or closes forever. He could ask it plainly. He could say: *I know what's in that folio, and I know who else knows, and I know what they do to people who carry pages out of this building.* He could warn her, in the grammar of warning, without ever saying anything that could be quoted. Or he could do his duty, such as it was, and send word up the chain to the pleasant men at Yamamoto & Sons, and let the machinery do what machinery does.

He looked at her — young, alone, half a world from wherever she truly came from, wearing her forged neatness like armor he suddenly recognized because he wore its twin — and he found that he could not do it. Not because he was brave. He had settled accounts with his own courage long ago and the balance was pitiful. But because some last uncorrupted column in him refused to post the entry, refused to write *delivered one girl to the syndicate, received: continued discretion,* refused to be, in the great ledger of his life, quite that figure.

What he said instead was: "Pages go missing in old buildings. Damp, rats, careless rebinding. If it turns up, bring it to me directly — to me, Miss Amberdale, not to anyone else. And if it doesn't turn up—" he closed the ledger, softly, "—then I advise whoever may have it to weigh carefully what a page is worth against what it costs. Some goods, once shipped, cannot be recalled. I speak as a man of commerce."

"Yes, sir," she said.

"And Miss Amberdale." He paused at the door, his hand on the frame, and did not turn around, because he could not have said the next sentence to her face. "Whatever your papers say you are — be careful. This harbor keeps two sets of books. The one in ink, and the one in blood. Only one of them forgives."

He heard her chair shift, heard the small sounds of her putting on her shawl, gathering her things, and then her voice, level and clear behind him:

"Good evening, Mr. Pell. Mind the tide stairs — the lamp's out again."

He walked home the long way. At the top of Higashi-yamate he stopped and looked down at the harbor, black water stitched with riding lights, and among them the masts of the *Kaiyo-maru*, and he thought: three weeks, perhaps less, and she sails, and whatever is written in folio 214 sails with her memory, and if the girl has the page — when the girl produces the page, because he was an accountant and he believed in balances, and the page would surface, pages always surfaced — then everything would move very quickly, and the machinery he had fed for eleven years would turn its attention from ledgers to people.

He went inside and took out the oilcloth packet and, for the first time in six years, added a sheet to it: a plain note in his own hand, undated, unsigned, naming the house of Yamamoto & Sons and the route by which the *Kaiyo-maru*'s articles were falsified. He did not seal it. He did not decide what it was for. He only knew that a man who keeps two sets of books must, someday, choose which one is true — and that he wanted, when the choosing came, to have already begun.

Below, in the town, a lamp moved along the waterfront road — a small, quick light, going not toward the foreign quarter but toward the old harbor workshops, where the demolition notices had lately gone up like crows on a fence.

Pell watched it until it vanished, and his blood ran cold, because he knew that walk. He had seen it before, years ago, the night before Lin's ship sailed.

Someone else was closing in too.
