## 17. A Name of Her Own

The rain came in from the west that evening, the way it always did in October, and Rosalind Amberdale watched it through the counting-house window while Mr. Pell's pen scratched behind her like a slow animal in a cage.

She had learned to read weather on this harbor the way other girls learned to read faces. A grey sky over Iojima meant the divers would stay ashore and grumble. A green light under the clouds at dusk meant a hard blow coming, and the fishing boats would double their moorings. And a certain quality of stillness — like now, the harbor lying flat and silent as poured pewter beneath the first fat drops — meant that something was waiting. That the day was holding its breath.

She did not believe in omens. She believed in columns, in sums that reconciled or did not, in the small honest arithmetic of tides and wages. But she felt the stillness all the same, sitting there with her figures spread before her and her hands — busy hands, always busy hands — copying a page of freight charges that told no lies whatsoever about ceramic ware.

"You've been staring at that line for four minutes," Pell said mildly, without looking up.

"Sixty-two crates of porcelain," she said, "entered at forty kan each. The duty schedule for porcelain is twelve per cent."

"And?"

"And nothing, Mr. Pell. It's perfectly in order." She smoothed the page with her palm, a gesture she had practiced until it looked like nothing at all. "I was thinking about the weather."

"The weather," Pell repeated, in the tone of a man filing something away. He had been doing a great deal of filing lately. She could feel it, the way one feels a draft under a door.

He let it go. That was the trouble with Pell these last weeks — he let things go with such gentleness that she could not decide whether the gentleness was kindness or a net being laid out very carefully, very slowly, in front of her feet.

* * *

She walked home through the rain without an umbrella, because umbrellas marked a woman as having money, and money marked a woman as having something worth taking. The tenement lane smelled of charcoal smoke and wet stone and the brine that never left Nagasaki's streets no matter how far up the hill you climbed. Salt was in everything here. Salt in the air, salt in the rice, salt dried into the beams of the houses. Her mother had said once — Sato-baasan had said it, repeating what Kume used to say — that a diver carried the sea inside her body, that when they cut you open after death they would find you were made of the harbor itself.

Rosalind had spent nineteen years trying to be made of paper instead. Clean paper. British paper. A dead clerk named Thomas Amberdale, a birth in a consulate she had never entered, a name written in a hand she had practiced in Kenji's kitchen until the loops came naturally.

And every night the sea inside her pressed against the paper, patient as water.

Ishida was waiting under the eaves of the tofu seller's shop.

She saw him from twenty paces and did not break stride, because breaking stride was a confession, and she had not survived this long by confessing anything. But her heart performed its small treachery anyway, quickening against her ribs like a bird testing a cage.

"Amberdale-san." He inclined his head. Rain dripped from the shop's awning between them in a beaded curtain. "A word, if you have it."

"Census matters are concluded, I understood."

"Census matters are never concluded." His voice was mild, unhurried, the voice of a man who had learned that patience moved papers faster than force. "They are only ever filed. Files can be reopened. Amended." He paused. "Corrected."

There it was. The word she had known was coming since the morning he had stood in her doorway with his ledger of households and asked, so innocently, about her father's trade.

"Not here," she said.

"No. Not here."

* * *

Kenji's house sat below the terraced gardens above Oura, a narrow wooden thing gone silver with age, its eaves low enough that a tall man must bow to enter — which Rosalind had always suspected Kenji considered the house's finest moral feature. Ishida ducked under them without comment and knelt at the low table as though he had been expected, which perhaps he had. Kenji poured tea for three without asking why there were three. He had raised her too well to ask questions in front of strangers; he asked them only afterward, in the dark, where questions could be safely kept.

The rain thickened against the shutters.

"You have been to the workshop," Ishida said. It was not quite a question either.

Rosalind set down her cup with care. "You have been following me."

"I have been following everyone," Ishida said. "That is my profession. You would be astonished how much of a city can be read by watching who walks where after dark." He opened the satchel he carried and drew out — not the household registry she dreaded, but a single sheet, folded twice, which he laid flat upon the table and turned to face her.

It was a registry form. Blank except for two lines.

"I am going to speak plainly," he said, "because plainness is the only courtesy left to us. Your papers say Thomas Amberdale's daughter, born of an Englishwoman, registered in the foreign quarter. I have read those papers very carefully. I have also read the consular records, the mission school rolls, and the burial register of the temple at Iojima." He folded his hands. "Kume the diver was lost off Iojima in the winter of 'eighty-one. Her daughter was entered in the temple's death book the same season — a clerical kindness, some priest's mercy, a child erased so a child might live. There is no Englishwoman. There never was."

Kenji had gone very still across the table. In the lamplight his face was a mask of old wood, but Rosalind saw his thumb press white against the rim of his cup, and she knew he was calculating, as she was, exactly how many seconds remained before their lives ended.

"If you know this," she said, "then you hold my life in a folder. Why bring me tea instead of irons?"

"Because a file is a lever," Ishida said, "and I have already used mine. What I am offering tonight is something else." He turned the blank form around again, so it faced her, and slid it across with two fingers. "Your mother's family name was Kume. Her registration lapsed on her death, as registrations do, but the record exists, and records can be restored. I can restore it. Legally. Stamped, sealed, entered in the town office where no one may amend it without leaving a mark of their own. Rosalind Kume. Daughter of Kume the diver. Registered, real, untouchable — under your own name."

The rain filled the silence.

"Do you understand what I am telling you?" Ishida went on softly. "No more forgery. No more fear of every census, every inspection, every new clerk with sharp eyes. The thing you have built your life on is a lie with a crack down the middle of it, and men like me find cracks. This—" he touched the form "—is stone. I am offering you stone."

"And what does it cost," Rosalind said, "to buy stone?"

"To stand in the town office and sign yourself a diver's daughter? Nothing in yen." He met her eyes, and for the first time she saw that his mildness had a floor to it, and the floor was grief. "Everything else. The moment your name enters that book, you are half-Japanese in the eyes of every shipping agent, every consul's clerk, every company man on this waterfront. No more counting house. Perhaps no more Nagasaki at peace. You would be trading an invisible cage for visible chains — and choosing which weight breaks you."

Kenji spoke for the first time, quietly. "And what do you gain, Ishida-san?"

The census taker looked at him for a long moment. "A witness who cannot be discredited," he said. "A woman whose name is true cannot be dismissed as a liar when she testifies to what Meridian's ledgers contain. They will come for her forgery otherwise — one word, one comparison of registers, and everything she shows the consul becomes the fantasy of a criminal. I am not only offering you safety, Miss Amberdale. I am offering the case its foundation. But I will not pretend the two gifts are separate. They are the same gift, and both cut."

He rose, bowed to them both, and left the form on the table between the teacups.

At the door he paused. "You need not decide tonight. But the Kaiyo-maru sails on the morning tide three weeks from Tuesday, and the town office closes at four." The door slid shut behind him, and his footsteps went away down the wet stones, unhurried as ever.

* * *

Kenji banked the fire while she sat with the form, reading the blank lines as though they were deep water.

When he finally spoke, he spoke in Japanese, which he did only when the matter was too heavy for English. "Your mother signed her name with a fish-hook flourish on the final stroke. She was proud of her writing. Diver's daughter, taught letters by a temple priest, and prouder of it than any merchant's wife of her silks."

"I know."

"She hid nothing," he said. "That is what I keep returning to, all these years. She dove in cold water with sharks, and walked into the foreign quarter with pearls in her sash, and told anyone who asked exactly whose daughter she was. And it killed her as surely as the water did — not the diving, the standing there, visible, priced. When she died I swore I would teach you to survive, and survival meant invisibility, and I have never once asked whether I taught you freedom or only fear."

Rosalind looked up at him — the old man who had fed her, schooled her, ruined his own remaining prospects teaching a mixed-blood child his careful English — and saw that his hands were shaking.

"If you sign," he said, "they will see you. All of them. Every day, forever. Can you bear to be seen?"

She thought of the harbor, flat and waiting under the rain. She thought of sixty-two crates of porcelain that weighed like sleepers. She thought of Sato-baasan's face at the ama beach, and of eleven names somewhere in a ledger that had not yet become eleven living women.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "Ask me tomorrow."

But when she blew out her lamp she took the form with her, folded it into the lining of her jacket, over her heart, where the forged papers lived — and found, falling asleep to the sound of the rain, that she could no longer remember which of the two weighed more.

In the morning there would be ledgers, and Pell's mild eyes, and three weeks ticking down like water into a diver's lungs. And on the doorstep, when she rose, a demolition notice flapped against the post of the house opposite — fresh paste, fresh ink — dated six days hence.

Someone, it seemed, had decided the tide should come early.
