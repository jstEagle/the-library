## 18. Storm Over the Harbor

The typhoon came up from the south like a debt collector, and like a debt collector it gave warning only once.

Rosalind heard it first as a change in the harbor's breathing. She had gone down to the water at dawn, as she always did on her half-days, to stand where her mother had stood and watch the ama divers' boats come in before weather closed the strait. The sea was the wrong color — not green, not grey, but a bruised violet that no honest water wore — and the swell was coming in long and silent, wave after wave without wind behind it, which every child raised on this coast knew to be the sea holding its breath.

By noon the breath went out of it.

The wind arrived from the southeast all at once, not rising but *arriving*, flattening the fishing boats against their moorings and tearing the laundry lines off the tenement balconies in long white flags that whipped away over the rooftops toward China. Rain followed, horizontal rain, rain that did not fall so much as travel, stinging like gravel flung by an angry hand. The Meridian Company's counting house shut its shutters at two o'clock — Mr. Pell himself going along the gallery with a mallet, nailing each one fast, his thin coat soaked through in the thirty seconds between doors — and Rosalind walked home through streets running knee-deep, clinging to doorposts and lamp standards, while above her the awnings of Dejima's merchants cracked and tore like sails in battle.

The Kaiyo-maru would not load today. Nor tomorrow, if the storm held. Rosalind counted that arithmetic as she climbed the tenement stairs, wringing out her skirt at the landing: three days lost, perhaps four, and the workshop's demolition notice nailed to its own door like a sentence already passed. The typhoon was not merely weather. It was a hand pushing the two dates toward each other, departure and demolition, until they met somewhere in the middle with her unfinished ledger crushed between.

She opened the door of Kenji's rooms and found the found family already assembling itself, the way it always did when the world turned dangerous — drawn together not by plan but by gravity.

* * *

Kenji Watanabe had felt the storm in his bones for two days before the sky admitted it.

Thirty years of Nagasaki seasons lived in the old interpreter's joints now, an almanac written in ache. He had spent the morning laying in supplies with the methodical patience of a man who remembered the typhoon of 1867, when the harbor had swallowed eleven boats and the town had eaten candle stubs for a week. Rice, dried fish, pickled plum, oil for the lamps, candles if any could be bought at a sane price — none could, so he bought them at an insane one. By the time Rosalind came dripping through his door, he had the room converted: tatami cleared, shutters barred with the spare beam he kept under the eaves for exactly this purpose, the little iron stove lit against the damp.

"You look like something the sea rejected," he said, and handed her his driest cloth.

"The Kaiyo-maru can't load," she said. It was not a greeting; it was the only fact in her head. "Three days. Maybe four."

"Four." Kenji set the kettle on the stove and did not look at her. He had done his own arithmetic in the night, lying awake listening to the barometer fall. Four days of grace, and then the ship would take on its cargo in a frenzy of double shifts to make up the loss, and the workshop with its true ledger would come down on whatever day the company's engineers chose, storm or no storm. "Then we have four days we didn't have yesterday. A gift, even if it comes wrapped in a typhoon."

"It doesn't feel like a gift."

"Nothing worth having does." He poured the tea — coarse bancha, the kind he'd drunk since the company dismissed him and fine things stopped coming to his table unbidden. "Your hands are shaking. Copying cramp?"

"Wind cramp." She flexed her fingers. "I gripped every railing between here and the harbor."

"Good. You'll need the grip." He said it lightly, but they both heard what lay under it. The copying was halfway done. Halfway across the page, with the other half locked in a building scheduled to die. He watched her drink, this girl who was not his daughter and was more his daughter than anything else the thirty years had left him, and he thought about the last typhoon shelter he'd shared with someone he loved. Her mother had been six years old then, screaming with delight every time the roof shuddered, fearless in the way of children who had never yet seen the sea take anything they loved.

Kume had grown up. The sea had taken her anyway.

"Ishida-san will come tonight," Kenji said. "He sent word by a fishmonger's boy. And Sato-baasan refused to stay on the island with the weather coming — she crossed on the last ferry, shouting at the boatman the whole way, I'm told. They're putting her up in the drying shed until dark."

Rosalind looked up. "She crossed *in this*?"

"She'd have swum it if they wouldn't take her fare. That woman has been arguing with the ocean for seventy years and considers a typhoon a personal correspondent." Kenji smiled despite everything. "When she arrives, do not offer her help carrying anything. She will take it as an insult to her ancestors."

* * *

Sato-baasan arrived at full dark, soaked to the skin and furious about nothing except the ferryman's caution, which she described as "the cowardice of a man who has never once trusted his own arms."

The divers' matriarch stood in Kenji's doorway streaming seawater and rain, a bundle on her back wrapped in oiled paper, and surveyed the room with the eye of a general inspecting fortifications. Ishida the census taker was already there, huddled near the stove looking smaller than his office made him, his precious registry documents wrapped in waxed cloth inside his coat. Rosalind sat cross-legged by the lamplight with her fingers still moving absently, tracing figures on the floorboards — numbers from the ledger, rehearsed into memory, because paper could burn and minds were harder to drown.

"Move your stove ash bucket," Sato-baasan commanded. "It will spill in the night and you will all be burned alive and I will have crossed the strait for nothing." Then, seeing Rosalind: "Girl. Come here."

Rosalind came. The old woman took her hands — those hands, the bookkeeper's hands, ink-stained and salt-cracked both — and turned them over as she had done since Rosalind was small enough to be carried out past the reef line on a diver's back.

"Still writing," Sato-baasan said. "Still counting other people's money."

"Some of it is ours now."

"Ha!" The old woman released her and began unpacking her bundle with brisk authority: rice cakes, a jar of seaweed, a flask of shochu that she set down in front of Kenji without comment, because some debts between old people ran too deep for thanks. "I brought the sticks."

The room went quiet. Even the storm seemed to lean in through the shutter seams to listen.

The tally-sticks — the divers' ancient accounting, notches cut into palm-length slats of bamboo, one notch per dive, one deeper groove per pearl delivered, kept by the ama themselves because no company clerk had ever bothered to count them honestly — lay in the lamplight like a bundle of bones. Eleven of them. Rosalind had matched nine to the ledger lines already, in the long nights before the storm. But Sato-baasan had spent the crossing doing her own work, and what she unwrapped now included three sticks Rosalind had never seen: fresh-cut bamboo, the bark still green, notched in a diver's hurried hand.

"These came off the Kaiyo-maru's tender," Sato-baasan said. "A boy swam them across the anchorage two nights ago, from the women held aboard. They are still cutting tallies, girl. Still keeping count, even locked below decks. Because they know that someday someone will read them."

Rosalind looked at the fresh sticks and could not speak. Somewhere in the dark harbor, in the belly of a ship riding out the same storm that shook these walls, women her mother had worked beside were cutting notches into bamboo so that their names would survive them. The ledgers recorded them as cargo weight disguised as ceramic ware. The tally-sticks recorded them as what they were: divers. Sisters. Granddaughters. Counters of their own souls.

"We will read them," Rosalind said finally. "Every notch. I promise you."

"Don't promise me." Sato-baasan lowered herself onto the cushion Kenji offered, groaning theatrically at her knees. "Promise them. I'll be dead soon enough either way. Promise the ones still breathing."

* * *

Ishida spoke little during the evening. This was his habit and, Rosalind suspected now, his armor.

He sat apart from the circle of lamplight, ostensibly mending a torn sleeve, actually watching. He was a careful man in a profession that rewarded carelessness with dismissal, and he had spent the whole day watching the storm rearrange his plans. The census bureau's superiors — the men above him whose names he never said aloud, whose shadow he had confessed to on the night they scouted the workshop together — would use the delay. Loading halted meant schedules rewritten meant opportunities to move papers, lose pages, adjust the record before any consul could demand it. Ishida understood better than anyone in the room how records could be edited by patient men, because editing records was, in its gentlest form, his entire career.

"Your face is doing arithmetic," Rosalind said, settling beside him with the teapot.

"My face is always doing arithmetic. It's why the bureau keeps me." He threaded the needle by lamplight, missing twice. "The storm buys us days on the loading. But it also gives them days to search. When a port closes, officials get bored, and bored officials start asking why a census taker has requested shipping manifests through unofficial channels."

"Are you frightened?"

He considered the question honestly, needle suspended. "I have been frightened since the day you lied to me about your father's name," he said. "It was such a good lie. It frightened me more than a bad one would have. A bad lie means a careless person. A good lie means a person with something worth protecting." He pulled the thread through. "Since then I have been waiting to find out whether I am the kind of man who protects or the kind who is protected. The storm has decided nothing. But it has shortened the exam."

Outside, the wind rose another register. The whole tenement leaned and groaned like a ship, and somewhere below them a tile tore free of a roof and shattered in the courtyard, and no one flinched, because they were all of the harbor and the harbor's people did not flinch at its tempers.

* * *

They passed the night in shifts, though the shifts dissolved by midnight into simply everyone being awake together, because no one could sleep with the world ending politely outside the shutters.

It was Sato-baasan, in the smallest hours, who broke the silence with story. This was deliberate; Rosalind knew her well enough to know that the old woman told stories the way generals deploy troops, precisely where they were needed. The wind was at its worst, the lamp flame bending in drafts no shutter could stop, and fear was pooling in the corners of the room — fear of the ship, the demolition, Pell's men, the consul's indifference. So Sato-baasan talked into the fear the way she talked to the sea: steadily, without asking its permission.

"Iojima, forty years ago," she began. "A typhoon much like this one, and Kume's mother — your grandmother, girl, though you never knew her name — dove anyway the morning after, because the storm had stirred the seabed and storms move pearls the way thieves move gold." She nodded at Rosalind. "Your family has always gone down after the weather breaks. It's in you. You think your mother drowned because she was reckless? No. She drowned because she was *thorough*. She stayed down to be sure. It is the same quality that makes you copy a ledger twice to check your own sums. Your blood is thoroughness, girl. Be proud of it. Be careful of it. Both."

Kenji laughed softly from his corner. "You are describing an accountant as a deep-sea creature."

"What do you think an accountant is?" Sato-baasan demanded. "Someone who goes down into the dark where the valuable things hide and brings them up into the light. The only difference is the drowning takes longer." She drank her shochu. "Kume was the same. On her last day — I was on the boat, I saw it — the others came up and she waved them off. One more minute, she signaled. One more minute, always. She found things the rest of us missed because she gave the deep one more minute than anyone dared." The old woman's voice roughened, just slightly, just once. "The sea gave her no minute back."

Rosalind sat very still. She had heard fragments of her mother's last day her whole life, but never this — never the image of her mother signaling *one more*, choosing the depths, thorough to the end.

"That's why I check my columns twice," Rosalind said quietly. "I never knew why. Now I do."

"And when the storm passes," Sato-baasan said, jabbing a thumb toward the harbor, invisible beyond the shutters, "you will give that workshop one more minute. One more hour. Whatever it takes to bring up what she would have brought up." 

* * *

Toward dawn the storm's voice dropped a register, from shriek to growl, and by the first grey light it was only raining — enormous, exhausted rain, the sky wringing itself empty.

Kenji opened the shutters onto a transformed world. The courtyard below was carpeted with broken tile and torn bamboo; a fishing boat sat in the street two doors down, deposited there by the surge like an abandoned toy; and out in the harbor the masts leaned at drunken angles and debris rode the swollen water in rafts. But the sky was clearing from the west, and the sea, having spent its fury, lay heaving and glassy-eyed, already beginning to forget.

On the far side of the anchorage, the Kaiyo-maru rode at anchor, listing slightly, her decks crawling with men already assessing damage. Loading would resume within a day. Two at most. The storm had given them four days, and the storm had also shown the syndicate exactly how much time remained, and the men on that deck were counting too.

"They'll work double tides to make up the loss," Kenji said, reading the ship's activity with thirty years of harbor eyes. "And they'll want the workshop emptied before the engineers come. Which means—"

"Which means they may not wait for the demolition date at all." Rosalind finished it. Her hands, resting in her lap, had gone still — truly still, for the first time in days. "If they're hurrying, we have to hurry faster. Tonight. We finish the copying tonight, whatever's left, whatever it costs."

Behind her, inside the warm wreck of the room, she heard Sato-baasan begin rolling up the sleeping mats, and Ishida folding away his mended sleeve, and Kenji setting the kettle on for the tea that preceded all serious undertakings. No one argued. No one hesitated. The typhoon had stripped the roofs of the town and stripped something else besides — every remaining doubt, every lingering pretense that any of them might still walk away clean.

One more minute in the deep, Rosalind thought, her mother's signal rising in her like a tide. One more minute, and then another, until the ledger is done.

She reached for her coat. Outside, the harbor was already calling the workers back, and the work — theirs as much as the company's — would not wait for the water to settle.
