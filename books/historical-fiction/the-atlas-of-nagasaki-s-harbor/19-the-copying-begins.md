## 19. The Copying Begins

The lamp was a cheap thing, bought from the oil seller on the corner for twelve sen, and it burned with the smell of fish and kerosene that clung to everything in the tenement district after dark. Rosalind set it on the low table between them and adjusted the wick until the flame stood small and steady, no taller than her little finger. A taller flame drew attention. She had learned that much already: light itself could be a confession.

Kenji sat across from her with his reading spectacles perched low on his nose, the stolen ledger pages fanned before him like a hand of cards he did not want to look at. Ishida knelt at the third side of the table, his census bureau posture softened into something almost humble, his brush laid parallel to his knee as though awaiting inspection. Outside, the rain came down in the fine, patient way of November, ticking against the tiled roof of the Watanabe house, running off the eaves into the stone channel behind the kitchen where Sato-baasan had once taught Rosalind to wash ink out of cloth with cold water and salt.

"Rules first," Kenji said. His voice was quiet, but it had the particular flatness he used when he meant to be obeyed. He had been an interpreter for the Meridian Company for eleven years before they dismissed him, and even now, thirty years of careful speech sat in his mouth like stones worn smooth. "We do not copy in order. Never in order. If anyone counts the pages we take, the pages we return must not look like a sequence. Tonight we begin at entry forty-one. Next night, entry two hundred and six. You understand?"

"Because a thief reads what interests him," Rosalind said. "A thief doesn't read like an archivist."

Kenji looked at her over the rims of his spectacles, and for a moment she saw something move behind his eyes — pride, or its close cousin, grief. "Your mother couldn't write her own name," he said. "She would have made you stop this. And she would have been proud of you for refusing."

He turned the first page.

They worked until the oil ran low. Rosalind took the figures; Kenji took the ciphered marginalia, translating the old company shorthand — *ceramic ware, 40 crates* becoming, under his brush, *persons, 40, condition: debt-bound* — while Ishida wrote the fair copy in a hand so neat it looked printed. Three hours produced nine pages. Nine pages out of perhaps four hundred. When Rosalind flexed her fingers, they ached as though she had spent the evening wringing wet laundry, and there was a groove pressed into the side of her middle finger where the pen had rested, a groove she suspected would become permanent, a callus shaped like her crime.

"It's too slow," Ishida said at last, setting down his brush and pressing his eyes with thumb and forefinger. "At this rate we finish after the Kaiyo-maru sails. After the demolition, if the schedule holds."

"At this rate we finish without being caught," Kenji answered. He folded the stolen pages along their original creases, precisely, so that no new fold would betray handling. "Speed is how men hang. We have one advantage, Mr. Ishida, and it is that nobody believes a bookkeeper's assistant, a disgraced interpreter, and a census clerk are capable of this. Let us keep being unbelievable a while longer."

Rosalind blew out the lamp. In the darkness the rain sounded louder, and she felt the nine finished pages wrapped in oilcloth beneath the loose floorboard beside the hearth, present as a second heartbeat.

* * *

The second week, her hands began to fail her.

It started small. By day she kept the Meridian counting house ledgers as always, entering freight and duty and exchange rates in Mr. Pell's immaculate columns, and by night she copied theft, and somewhere between the two the muscles of her forearm decided they could no longer tell which work was real. On the fourth night her figures came out slurred, the sevens losing their crossbars, and Kenji took the page from under her pen without a word and set it aside.

"Again," he said.

"I'm tired."

"Yes. Again."

She looked at him. The lamplight caught the grey stubble along his jaw, the deep lines bracketing his mouth, the ink stains — permanent ones, decades old — on his right hand. She thought of all the nights he had sat like this when she was a child, drilling her on multiplication tables while the harbor foghorn moaned, telling her *a number written carelessly is a lie told politely*. She had hated him then, gently, the way children hate discipline. She understood now that the discipline had been armor, fitted for her years before she knew she'd need it.

"Tell me about the last time," she said, instead of writing again.

Kenji's brush stopped above the page.

"The last time someone copied these numbers," she pressed. "Thirty years ago. You knew. Chapter and verse, you told me — you saw the pattern. What happened to the person who tried?"

For a long moment the only sound was rain. Then Kenji set his brush down, aligned it with the table edge — a habit, she realized, of a man who had spent his life making small things orderly because large things would not stay ordered — and spoke.

"There was a clerk named Morioka. In the old company, before Meridian absorbed it. He found what you found: the ceramic ware, the weights that didn't reconcile, divers entered as cargo. He was older than I am now. Careful, decent, dull. He copied pages for four months." Kenji's voice did not change register; it stayed level, almost gentle, which made it worse. "He gave them to a journalist in Nagasaki who promised discretion. The journalist sold him. Morioka lost his position, his registry standing, and within the year his health. He died in the charity ward at Oura with no family to claim him. I attended the cremation. There were four mourners, and two of us were only curious."

"And you stayed silent afterward," Rosalind said. "You saw the same pattern again and again and you stayed silent for thirty years."

"I raised a child instead," Kenji said. "I taught her every cipher the companies use. Draw your own conclusions about whether that is silence."

Ishida, kneeling at his corner of the table, had gone very still. Now he spoke for the first time that night, his voice carefully stripped of anything that might be mistaken for judgment. "Watanabe-san. If the consul will only act on documentary proof, then the proof must be complete. Not nine pages. Not ninety. All of it. That means speed after all — or it means nothing."

"That means," Kenji said, picking up his brush again, "that we learn to work faster without working carelessly. Which is the entire art of the thing." He glanced up at Rosalind, and there was something wry in the tired lines of his face. "Your mother held her breath for two minutes in cold water. You can hold your pen for another hour."

So she held her pen. Night after night. Her handwriting deteriorated and recovered and deteriorated again, like a tide. She learned to copy standing up, to copy with her left hand when the right cramped, to rest her eyes by closing them and letting Kenji read entries aloud while Ishida transcribed. She learned that the master ledger's paper was thinner than the company's day books, chosen, she suspected, so more pages fit in less space, and that the ink used in the ciphered margins was a different vintage than the body entries — added later, she guessed, by a different hand, someone senior, someone who wanted the true cargo list legible only to insiders.

On the eleventh night she fell asleep over the table with her cheek on a fair copy and woke to find Kenji had draped his own coat over her shoulders, and Ishida was still writing, alone, his brush whispering in the dark like a small animal moving through grass.

"You should sleep," she mumbled.

"So should you," Ishida said, not looking up. "One of us sleeping is prudence. Both of us sleeping is negligence." He wrote on. In the lamplight his face had lost the bureaucratic smoothness she had first distrusted, and underneath it was simply a tired man in his forties doing labor he believed in, badly paid, badly thanked. "My wife left me eight years ago," he said suddenly, to the page rather than to her. "She said I was a man who counted other people's households because I could not keep my own. Perhaps. But I know what the registries hide, Amberdale-san. Every falsified household, every daughter entered as a niece, every widow erased to save a tax. The census is supposed to make people visible. Instead it teaches them to disappear." He set down his brush and looked at her directly, and did not flinch from what her face was. "I am not helping you despite what you are. I am helping you because of what they made you hide."

Rosalind found she could not answer. She bowed, instead, the small bow of a diver surfacing, and went back to her figures, and the three of them worked until the sky behind the shutters turned the grey of unpolished silver, and Kenji called the halt, and the pages went under the floorboard, and the ordinary city woke around their ordinary crime.

* * *

By the third week they had a rhythm, and the rhythm was its own kind of fortress.

Sato-baasan arrived each evening at dusk with food she claimed was leftovers — rice with pickled plum, miso thick enough to stand a spoon in, occasionally dried squid that Kenji pretended to dislike and ate entirely. She did not ask to see the pages. She sat in the kitchen with her hands folded over her knees and listened, and sometimes when the wind gusted hard against the shutters she would say, without opening her eyes, "Footsteps. Two men. They'll pass," or, "That's only Tetsu's dog," and she was never wrong. Thirty years of listening to the ama divers' breathing from the bottom of the sea had calibrated her ears beyond any ordinary instrument.

The tally-sticks lay in a bundle beside her, and Rosalind had begun matching them — notched bamboo, each notch a diver's contract, a debt, a season — against the ledger lines, and each match was a small detonation. Entry 118: *ceramic ware, 12 crates, consigned Shanghai.* Tally-stick of Umekichi, whose shoulders Rosalind had seen scarred by the rope harness. Entry 204: *ceramic ware, 8 crates.* Tally-stick of Haru, sixteen years old, sold against her grandmother's silence. The abstractions acquired faces the way a photographic plate acquires an image — slowly, in chemical darkness, until suddenly they were unmistakable and could never be unseen again.

"We have sixty-two entries remaining," Ishida announced one night, drawing his finger down the master index. "And I estimate eleven days of copying at our current pace."

"The workshop demolition notice says eight days," Rosalind said. "Contents disposed of in five."

The arithmetic hung in the lamplight, ugly and simple. Eleven days of work inside five days of opportunity. Kenji removed his spectacles and rubbed the bridge of his nose.

"Then we change the pace," he said. "From tomorrow we work two shifts. Rosalind copies the body entries; I translate cipher; Ishida makes fair copy. When Rosalind's hand gives out, Ishida copies body entries and Rosalind translates — her cipher is good enough now for routine notation. We sleep in shifts of two hours. Sato-baasan—" he raised his voice slightly toward the kitchen, "—will forgive us the smell of ink at dawn."

"I have smelled worse things come out of this family," Sato-baasan called back placidly, and Rosalind laughed despite everything, a short startled sound, and felt the strange truth of the room settle over her: that this was what a family was, perhaps. Not blood, but people who agreed to be exhausted together toward the same purpose.

They began the double shifts that very night. The oil seller on the corner began to recognize her, buying lamp oil in quantity, and she invented a sick aunt who needed light for her needlework, and the oil seller nodded with the merciful incuriosity of the poor. Her fingers split at the knuckles from the friction of the pen; Sato-baasan bound them with strips of cloth soaked in camellia oil, grumbling that a girl who dove for pearls should have tougher skin, and Rosalind copied on through the bandages, and the fair copies accumulated under the floorboard until the board no longer sat flush and Kenji redistributed them into three hiding places, none of which he disclosed fully to either of the others — and Rosalind noticed, and understood, and did not object, because trust among conspirators was not the absence of caution but the agreement to survive each other's caution.

On the fifth night of the double shifts, with thirty-one entries remaining and four days until disposal, Rosalind climbed the outer stairs to her own narrow room at half past three in the morning, her eyes burning, her shoulder a single knot of ache. The tenement corridor was silent. Below, in the yard, the neighbor's son Tadao — fourteen, hollow-cheeked since the opium had found him that autumn — sat on the step where no boy should be at half past three in the morning, smoking, though he did not smoke.

His eyes followed her all the way up the stairs.

She did not hurry. Hurrying was confirmation. She walked at the pace of a woman coming home late from honest work, closed her door, and stood in the darkness with her heart going like a hammer on a hull, listening.

After a long time, below, Tadao's footsteps crossed the yard — quick, light, headed not toward his own family's room but toward the lane, toward the direction of the harbor, toward the Meridian counting house, where a lamp burned late in Mr. Pell's office most nights now.

Rosalind stood in the dark with thirty-one entries still uncopied, and began, silently, to count the hours until sundown.
