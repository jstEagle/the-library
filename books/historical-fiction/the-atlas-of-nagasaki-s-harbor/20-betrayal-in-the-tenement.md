## 20. Betrayal in the Tenement

The trouble with a secret kept in a tenement was that tenements breathed. They inhaled through their thin walls and exhaled through their floorboards, and every sound made in one room traveled to another like gossip down a row of connected lungs. Rosalind had known this since she was seven years old and could recite which of her neighbors took their tea at what hour by nothing more than the scrape of a chair and the particular cough that followed it. She knew Mrs. Hara boiled cabbage on Tuesdays because the smell climbed the stairwell like something alive. She knew the Yamaguchi boy practiced his flute badly between six and half-past, and that old Mr. Endo shouted at him through the wall every single evening without fail, and that neither ever stopped.

She should have remembered that walls carried more than sound. They carried light.

It was on the fourth night of copying that she noticed it — or rather, on the morning after the fourth night, when she came down the outside stairs into the grey wash of dawn and saw the paper screen of Kenji's front room from the alley below. From inside, lamplight made it glow amber, soft as a lantern festival. From outside, it made them visible. Every silhouette. Her own bent shoulders over the table. Ishida's narrow back. Kenji's hand rising and falling as he translated line after line into English for her checking eye.

Anyone walking the alley at midnight — anyone at all — would have seen a shadow-play of exactly what they were doing: three people hunched around a forbidden document, copying it out against the tide and the demolition date and the whole weight of the Meridian Trading Company.

Rosalind stood in the cold and felt the mistake settle into her bones like harbor damp. We have been burning ourselves like beacons, she thought. Night after night. Come and see. Come and look.

She said nothing about it that morning. There was nothing to say that would not frighten the others, and there were only eleven days left before the workshop's contents were to be "disposed of," and fear spent time they could not afford. But she began, quietly, to watch.

* * *

Watching became its own occupation, layered over all the others. By day she sat at her desk in the counting house with her figures neat and her face smooth, and she counted other things besides columns. The way Mr. Pell paused over the shipping manifests a moment longer than usual. The way he looked at her — not with suspicion, precisely, but with a kind of careful fondness that was somehow worse, the look of a man handling something he suspected might be cracked and did not yet want to know.

"Amberdale." He stood beside her desk on Thursday afternoon with his hands clasped behind his back, rocking slightly on his heels. Pell rocked when he was thinking; she had learned this long ago. "You've been tired lately. Don't deny it, I can see it round your eyes. Burning the candle?"

"Family matters, sir. My uncle's health."

"Ah." A pause. His gaze drifted, quite naturally, toward the window, toward the rooftops of the foreign quarter and beyond it the jumbled grey slope of the tenement districts where people like her lived stacked upon each other like cargo. "Your uncle. The interpreter fellow. Watanabe."

Her pen did not stop moving. She had trained herself years ago never to let a pen stop moving when a question landed; a stopped pen was an admission. "Yes, sir. My mother's brother-in-law, once removed. He raised me."

"Mm." Pell rocked twice more. "Good man, they say. Knew his languages. Shame about the trouble he got into, back in the day." He said it lightly, the way one mentions weather, and then he patted the edge of her desk — an odd, paternal gesture that made her skin crawl even as some treacherous part of her warmed to it — and went back to his office.

She finished the column she was working on. Then she put down her pen and pressed both palms flat against the cool wood and breathed until her heart slowed. He knows where I sleep, she thought. Or near enough. He knows who raised me. And he has just told me, in the politest possible language, that he remembers what happened to men who crossed this company thirty years ago.

That evening she walked home the long way, twice doubling back through the fish market, watching reflections in shop glass for followers. She saw none. That proved nothing. The company did not need clumsy watchers; it owned half the eyes in Nagasaki, and most of them did not know whose wages they truly drew.

At the mouth of the tenement lane she stopped, as she had stopped every evening that week, and let her gaze travel up the crooked face of the building. Third floor, Kenji's rooms, screens closed. Second floor, the Haras', supper smell already escaping. First floor —

First floor, at the corner window where the Yamaguchis lived, the flute boy's grandmother sat shelling beans. Ordinary. Except that the boy himself was not in the room behind her, and it was nearly seven, and the boy was always in the room at seven, practicing his flute badly.

She found him twenty minutes later, quite by accident of habit, at the steps of the Ebisu shrine at the top of the hill, where boys sometimes gathered to smoke stolen cigarettes and dare each other toward mischief. He was sitting alone on the lowest step, knees drawn up, and when she came near he flinched so hard she knew everything before he said a word.

"Tetsu," she said gently, in Japanese, using his name like a hand laid on his shoulder. "You're not smoking. You don't smoke."

"I do sometimes."

"You hate it. It makes you sick." She sat down beside him, unhurried, arranging her skirts as though she had all the leisure in the world. Below them the harbor lights were coming on one by one, strung along the black water like a rosary being counted. "Who gave you money, Tetsu?"

He stared straight ahead. His jaw worked. "Nobody gave me anything."

"Then why are you shaking?"

"I'm cold."

"It's June."

For a long moment he said nothing, and the harbor breathed below them, and somewhere out on the water a ship's bell rang the hour. Then his shoulders crumpled all at once, like a folded paper giving way, and he turned his face away from her and said, very low, "I didn't tell them about you. I swear it, Amberdale-san. I didn't know it was you they wanted. I only said — I only said there were meetings. At the interpreter's house. Nights. Men and a woman copying things." His voice cracked. "I didn't know it was you."

The cold that went through her then was not the June evening's. Meetings, she thought. Copying things. Someone had asked a twelve-year-old boy to report on the comings and goings at Kenji's door, and he had answered, because someone had paid him, or promised him, or threatened him — and she already knew which currency it was, because Tetsu's elder brother had gone pale and hollow-eyed that spring and begun selling the family's possessions one small item at a time, and everyone on the street knew what kind of hunger did that.

Opium. The syndicate's oldest ledger-entry. Cheaper than bribing an official and more reliable than threatening one, because a boy who needed to feed his brother's craving would sell anything, including the truth, including his neighbor, including himself.

"Who spoke to you?" she asked, still gentle. Gentleness was a blade too, if held steadily enough. "A foreigner? A Japanese man? Was he from the harbor offices?"

"A clerk." Tetsu was crying now, silently, the way children cry when they have learned that noise brings punishment. "From the big trading house. He was kind to me, that's the worst of it, he bought me taiyaki and asked easy questions, and then last week he gave me money and said just write down the hours people come and go. Just the hours. That's all I wrote. Hours." He wiped his nose on his sleeve. "Amberdale-san, is my family in trouble? Is my brother —"

"Your brother is ill, and illness isn't crime." She heard herself say it and marveled distantly at how steady she sounded, how much like Kenji, while inside her everything was sliding. "Listen to me now. You will write no more hours. If the kind clerk asks, you will say the meetings stopped. Say the old interpreter locked his door and goes to bed early. Can you remember that?"

He nodded, quick and miserable.

"And Tetsu —" she waited until he looked at her — "you are not the first person in this city to be bought cheaply by bad men, and you will not be the last, and I am not angry with you. Do you hear? Not angry. But if you write another word for them, I cannot protect you from what comes after, and what comes after will not be kind like me."

He nodded again. Then, in a small voice: "They know it's you. The woman at the meetings. The clerk said so yesterday. He said —" Tetsu swallowed. "He said they'd known for days."

* * *

She did not go straight home. She walked the hill road above the town instead, past the shrine and up among the terraced graves, because she needed sky and needed to think, and thinking required motion the way a pump required water. The whole city lay below her in the blue dusk: the harbor, the counting houses, the masts of a hundred ships, and somewhere in that glittering sprawl a clerk with a taste for taiyaki writing down everything she was, everything she loved, everything she intended to destroy.

They had known for days. Which meant Pell had known for days — and Pell had patted her desk this afternoon and asked after her uncle's health with a smile. Either Pell was a fool pretending ignorance, or Pell was a cat playing with something in a corner, or —

Or Pell was afraid. She turned that third possibility over, examining it the way Kenji had taught her to examine a suspicious figure: turn it upside down, check the totals against the parts. A man with debts to the syndicate, ordered to watch a clerk he liked, reporting upward while hoping downward that nothing would come of it. She had seen such men all her life in miniature — the harbor policeman who looked away from small smuggling for a share, the landlord who reported tenant meetings to avoid trouble himself. Complicity rarely wore a villain's face. It usually wore an apologetic one.

But hope was a luxury, and she had been raised by a man ruined for trusting the wrong person's decency. Assume the worst arithmetic, Kenji always said, and be pleasantly surprised by any kinder sum.

By the time she descended the hill the lamps were lit in the tenement windows and supper smells filled the lane, ordinary and warm, and it struck her with fresh force how entirely this street had become the thing the company was hunting. Kenji here. Sato-baasan two doors down with her bundle of tally-sticks hidden under her floorboards. Ishida arriving after dark with his census rolls as cover. Herself, the forged papers, the copied pages wrapped in oilcloth beneath the loose plank under Kenji's tatami. Everything they had built was within forty feet of a boy who had been selling hours for opium money.

We cannot copy here anymore. The thought arrived fully formed, cold and certain. One more night, perhaps. No more.

Inside, Kenji was grinding ink, and Ishida was squinting over the master ledger's smuggled pages by lamplight, and the room smelled of rice and lamp-oil and industry, and she hated to break it, the way one hates to wake a sleeper before a storm.

She broke it anyway.

She told them everything — Tetsu, the hours, the taiyaki clerk, the words they'd known for days. She watched the color leave Kenji's hands where they rested on the inkstone, watched the old man's spine curve forward as though the air itself had grown heavy. Ishida set down his spectacles with great care, as if the gesture could hold the situation together by friction.

"They will not raid," Kenji said at last, slowly. "Not yet. Understand that. A raid makes noise, and noise makes questions, and the company survives on quiet. No — they'll do what they always do. They'll wait. They'll tighten the net inch by inch and watch who runs toward what." He looked up at her, and in the lamplight his eyes were wet and furious and terribly old. "Thirty years ago I made copies too. Did I ever tell you what happened to the man who carried mine to the consul?"

"You never told me what happened at all."

"He drowned," Kenji said simply. "Between the consulate steps and the harbor, in four feet of calm water, with his papers sewn into his coat. They ruled it drink." He pushed the inkstone away. "So we finish faster, and we move the pages tonight, and we stop meeting here. That is the whole of the strategy. Finish, move, vanish."

"Move them where?" Ishida asked. "Every safe place we have, they can find. Your name is in their books, Watanabe-san. So is hers. Mine especially — I'm a census taker; they need only ask the bureau where I sleep."

"Sato-baasan," Rosalind said.

Both men looked at her.

"The divers' beach. Her hut above the landing. Nobody from the company goes near the ama beach — the divers won't speak to their clerks, and their clerks know better than to be seen asking why." She was speaking quickly now, the plan assembling itself out of desperation the way a wreck assembles itself out of a storm. "And she has the tally-sticks. Put the pages with the sticks. If anyone finds one, they find the other, and the sticks make sense of the pages, and the pages make sense of the sticks. Let them find it all together and try to explain it away."

Kenji was silent a long moment. Outside, faintly, absurdly, the Yamaguchi boy began to practice his flute — the same stumbling phrase, over and over, climbing toward a note he could never reach. Something in the ordinariness of it nearly undid her.

"The old woman," Kenji said at last, "has already lost a granddaughter to these people. You would ask her to wager what remains of her household on our papers."

"I would ask her," Rosalind said. "And I believe she will say yes. Because they took the girl by buying silence, Kenji-sensei, and silence is the one debt she has left to pay."

* * *

They moved the pages that same night, in installments, the way one moves anything precious past watching eyes — a few sheets rolled inside Ishida's census cylinder, a few more sewn into the lining of Kenji's old coat, the rest in the false bottom of Rosalind's sewing basket, under thread and buttons, in plain sight of any thief who searched only for documents. They went separately, at intervals, by different lanes. Rosalind walked the last stretch along the sea-wall with the basket on her arm and the tide sighing below, and every shadow seemed to detach itself and follow her, and every follower dissolved into a mooring post or a drunk sleeping off his sake, and she could no longer tell vigilance from terror, and suspected they had become the same thing.

Sato-baasan met her at the top of the divers' path with a lamp held high, and read the whole catastrophe in Rosalind's face before a word was spoken, because old women who have survived sixty years of harbors learn to read faces the way sailors read weather.

"So," the old diver said. "The little fishes know our pond." She listened while Rosalind explained, standing in her doorway with the lamp steady, and when Rosalind reached the end and the request, Sato-baasan was quiet for the length of three waves breaking on the shingle below.

"My granddaughter is on their ship," she said finally. "My silence bought her breath, and I paid it, God forgive me, and now you bring me papers that will either free her or drown us all." She stepped back from the doorway, opening it. "Under the sleeping mat. Where I keep the sticks. And girl —" her hand closed briefly, fiercely, on Rosalind's wrist — "come early next time. Come in daylight. Come as a daughter visiting her grandmother, the way any girl in this district might. The company watches for conspirators. It does not watch for granddaughters."

Rosalind slept that night — or lay sleeping-shaped — in her own narrow bed in the tenement, listening to the building breathe around her, cataloguing every creak. Somewhere below her a floorboard settled. Somewhere across the lane a shutter moved in the wind, or did not move in the wind. The walls that had raised her, that had sung her to sleep with the sounds of other lives, now felt thin as paper screens with lamplight behind them, showing her silhouette to whoever cared to stand in the dark and look.

They know everything, she thought, staring at the ceiling. They know where I work and where I sleep, who raised me, who visits me, what nights we meet, that we copy. Everything except where the pages lie. One fact between us and the water.

In the morning she rose before dawn, as she always did, and went down the outside stairs into the grey — and stopped halfway, her blood going to ice.

The screen of the ground-floor corner room stood open to the morning air. Inside, the Yamaguchi grandmother sat shelling her beans as always. But on the sill, weighted by a stone against the breeze, lay a folded paper, white and deliberate, angled outward toward the stairs as plainly as a signpost.

Rosalind looked at it for a long moment. Then she looked up the street, and down, and saw no one — and understood, with terrible clarity, that whether or not anyone was watching mattered less than the certainty that they could be. She descended, lifted the paper, and unfolded it with fingers that refused to be steady.

Six characters, written in a clerk's tidy hand:

*The workshop burns tomorrow.*
