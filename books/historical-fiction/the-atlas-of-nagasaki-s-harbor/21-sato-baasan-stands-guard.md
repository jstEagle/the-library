## 21. Sato-baasan Stands Guard

The tide had turned before dawn, and Sato-baasan felt it in her knees the way she had felt it for seventy years — a slow dragging ache, as though the whole harbor were pulling at her bones and asking her to come back down into the cold green dark. She sat up on her futon in the loft above Kenji Watanabe's workshop, pressed her palms flat against the floorboards, and listened.

Below her, muffled through the old cedar planks, came the small industrious sounds of the night's work ending: the scratch of a pen running dry and being refilled, the soft thump of a page being turned, Ishida's low voice reading a figure back for confirmation, Rosalind answering in her careful half-and-half voice that was neither wholly English nor wholly Japanese but something like the harbor itself, where the two waters met and did not separate.

They had worked until nearly three. She had heard them. She heard everything now; sleep came to her in shallow cups, poured out and refilled, and between the pourings she lay awake cataloguing sounds the way she had once catalogued pearls — by weight, by luster, by the small telltale imperfections that told you whether a thing was true or false.

A cart passing on the stone road at an hour when no honest cart passed. Two sets of footsteps slowing outside the gate and moving on. The company was sniffing, she thought. The company always sniffed before it bit. She had watched Meridian's men come to the divers' beach for thirty years with their ledgers and their polite bows, and she knew the shape of their patience. It was the patience of a fisherman watching a netted cove: no hurry at all, because nothing inside could get out.

She rose, wrapped herself in her padded jacket against the autumn chill, and went down the ladder without making a sound. Seventy years of climbing wet rocks in the dark teaches a woman where every board complains.

* * *

The copying room was the back chamber of the workshop, the one that had once held coils of rope and barrels of pitch, and it smelled still of tar and hemp beneath the fresher smell of ink. Kenji had hung blankets over the window and set the lamp behind a screen so that no light showed from the street. Inside, the three of them were bent over the master ledger's stolen pages and the growing stack of copies, and the sight of them — the disgraced interpreter, the census man who had become something else, and Kume's daughter with ink to her second knuckle — made something tighten and loosen in Sato-baasan's chest all at once.

"You should sleep," she said.

Rosalind looked up. In the lamplight her face was hollowed with fatigue, but her eyes were clear, and there was ink on her cheekbone where she had pushed back her hair with a stained hand. "Two more pages," she said. "The Shanghai entries. If I stop now I'll lose the thread of the columns."

"The columns will keep. You will not, if you fall asleep into the inkwell."

Kenji laughed softly, a dry sound like paper folding. "She is right, child. We are past the point where one page matters more than a steady hand tomorrow."

Ishida capped his pen. "Tomorrow we finish the shipping abstracts," he said, and there was a new steadiness in him too, Sato-baasan noted. Fear had not left the man — he flinched still at every footstep on the road — but he had arranged his fear into shelves and compartments, the way he must once have arranged his census forms, and he worked beside it now as if it were merely another clerk.

"Then sleep," Sato-baasan said again, "and I will sit outside."

Rosalind frowned. "Baasan, there's nothing out there but the harbor."

"There is everything out there." She lowered herself onto the bench by the door, joints cracking like green firewood, and began winding her headcloth. "The neighbor's boy has been walking past twice a night instead of once. A rickshaw stood at the corner for an hour yesterday with its man asleep upright, which no rickshaw man ever is unless he is paid to be. And Pell's little clerk with the weak chin came asking at the chandlery whether the old diver woman had moved in here permanent." She tied the cloth off under her chin. "They know meetings happen. They do not yet know what happens in them. My job is to make sure they go on not knowing until Thursday."

"And how does an old woman sitting on a step accomplish that?" Ishida asked — not mocking, she judged, only tired enough to be blunt.

Sato-baasan considered him for a moment. Then she said, "Because no man in this harbor will put his hands on me, and every man in this harbor knows why."

* * *

She took up her post on the flat stone at the top of the workshop steps just as the sky went from black to the color of a mussel shell. Below her the harbor lay in its morning gray, masts and halyards emerging from the mist in pieces, as if the ships were being assembled out of fog by some patient invisible hand. The Kaiyo-maru rode at her mooring out past the breakwater, black-hulled, sullen, waiting on her tide. Sato-baasan looked at her the way she would look at a shark finning offshore while children swam — without hatred, because hatred wasted breath, but with a complete and unsentimental attention.

Her granddaughter was somewhere on that ship's route. Or had been. The tally-sticks hidden in Rosalind's keeping said eleven names, eleven women bought north with their debts, and among the eleven, in the old diver's cipher that only beach-women could read, was *Miyo*, third notch deepened, which meant pregnant or recently so, which meant they had taken her anyway, which meant —

She stopped the thought there, the way you stop a cut with pressure. Thinking forward along that line did no one any good. Thinking backward did no one any good either. What did good was sitting on this stone, awake, with her back straight, for as long as it took.

The divers' respect she had spoken of to Ishida was real, but it was not simple, and she did not fool herself about what it rested on. It rested on thirty years of her being the one who dove deepest and held breath longest; on the winter she had organized the beach women to refuse the company's new debt terms and had won, mostly, for a while; on the two funerals she had paid for out of her own pearl money when the company would not; on the plain fact that she was old now and beyond threat, and everyone knew it, and everyone therefore owed her the courtesy that is given to those who can no longer be punished. There was power in being unpunishable. It was the only kind the poor ever got to hold for long, and she meant to spend every coin of it.

The sun came up properly. Fishermen called to each other across the water. The smell of charcoal and grilling fish drifted from the tenement row. Ordinary life, going on around a room full of treasonous arithmetic.

At mid-morning the first of them came.

* * *

He was young, this one, broad through the shoulders, wearing the good gray coat that marked a company man of the second rank — not a laborer, not yet a manager, one of the hands that did the reaching. He came up the stone road at an easy walk, and Sato-baasan watched him the whole way, because the first lesson of the sea is that you watch everything, always, and the second lesson is that most things watch you back only when they think it profits them.

He stopped at the bottom of the steps and touched his hat brim. "Grandmother. Good morning."

"It is morning," she agreed. "Whether it is good remains to be seen."

"I'm looking for Mr. Watanabe. On company business."

"Mr. Watanabe is sleeping. He keeps late hours these days. His eyes are bad."

"His eyes seemed fine enough last month."

"Last month," Sato-baasan said, "you were not standing in front of me."

The young man shifted his weight. He glanced up at the workshop — at the shuttered windows, the blank door — and then back at her, and she saw him doing the arithmetic that company men did: one old woman, one door, orders to carry out. She let him look. She kept her hands folded in her lap, over the headcloth's knot, and she kept her breathing slow and even, the way you breathe before a long dive, and she let the silence stretch until it began to press on him like depth presses on a diver's ears.

It was the silence that broke him, in the end. They were never taught what to do with silence, these men. They were taught forms and threats and the names of superiors, but not how to stand in front of a woman who had spent forty years of her life in a place where speech was impossible and patience was the whole of survival.

"My instructions," he tried, "are to inspect the premises."

"For what?"

"That isn't — the company doesn't disclose—"

"Then you may tell your superiors that the premises contain an old woman, a lame cat, and forty years of rope dust, and that the old woman has already been inspected this year by the ward office, the temple registrar, and two very polite young men exactly like yourself, none of whom found anything worth writing down." She unfolded her hands and laid them open on her knees, palms up, empty. "Look at me, boy. Do I look like a woman who hides things? I have spent my life diving naked in cold water in front of the whole beach. There is nothing on my body or in my house that the light has not already seen."

Something flickered across his face — not quite shame, but the neighborhood of it. "You're the one they call Sato-baasan. From the Iojima boats."

"I am the one they call Sato-baasan."

"My mother dove the Nomo pens," he said, abruptly, as if the words had gotten loose without his permission. "Before she married."

"Then she'll have told you what happens to men who trouble old divers."

"She told me to be respectful of them." He said it stiffly, resenting his own mouth. But he stepped back off the bottom stair, and his hand came away from the gatepost where it had been resting. "I'll return when Mr. Watanabe is awake."

"He sleeps badly," Sato-baasan said. "You may find him difficult to catch."

He went. She watched him all the way down the stone road and around the chandlery corner, and only then did she allow her shoulders to drop a finger's width.

* * *

The second one came at dusk, and he was not young, and he was not polite.

She heard the rickshaw first — the same rickshaw, or its twin, stopping at the same corner — and then footsteps that made no attempt at quiet, because the man wearing them wanted to be heard. He came up the road in a dark Western suit with a watch chain, and he stopped at the foot of the steps and looked at her for a long moment with the frank appraisal of a buyer at market.

"Sato-san." Her name in his mouth sounded like something being weighed. "You've made yourself comfortable here."

"I visit friends."

"For six days."

"I am old. Old women move slowly. Six days is nothing to me. I once sat on a rock off Takashima for nine hours waiting for the water to clear."

"The water cleared?"

"The water always clears," she said. "That is the trouble with water. That is the trouble with everything, if you wait long enough. Everything clears, and then everyone can see what everyone has done." She looked at him steadily. "Some people find that comforting. Some find it otherwise. Which sort are you?"

His jaw tightened. Behind the blankness of his face she read the message he had been sent to deliver without delivering: *we know, we are patient, we are many.* She had seen that face on company men for thirty years, and she had learned its secret, which was that it was a mask worn by frightened clerks over the faces of more frightened clerks, all the way up, all the way to the top, where perhaps one man was genuinely unafraid and genuinely guilty and slept perfectly well.

"The workshop is scheduled for demolition," he said. "Six days. The notice is posted. Whatever Mr. Watanabe imagines he is doing in there, it ends soon."

"Buildings end," Sato-baasan agreed. "I have buried two husbands and both my sons and one granddaughter's mother, and I am still here. Buildings are easier to lose than that. Tell whoever sent you that I said so."

For a moment she thought he might actually say something human. His eyes went, briefly, toward the harbor — toward the black hull of the Kaiyo-maru — and there was something in them that was almost pity, or almost warning, or almost the wish to be elsewhere entirely. Then the mask came back down.

"Good evening, grandmother."

"Good evening," she said. "Mind the third step. It's loose. Has been for years. Nobody fixes anything in this town until it falls on someone important."

He laughed — one short bark, surprised out of him — and then caught himself and descended carefully, avoiding the third step despite himself, and she noted that too, filed it away: a man who obeys even a stranger's small warnings is a man who still believes the world has rules. Useful to know. Possibly useful later.

* * *

Full dark now. She remained on her stone a while longer, because the changeover hour was when watchers got careless, and because her bones had stiffened and she wanted to give them a reason. Below, the harbor lights came out one by one, doubled in the black water, and somewhere out past the breakwater the Kaiyo-maru showed a single riding lantern, low and red, like a coal that refused to die.

Rosalind opened the door behind her and knelt on the top step, close enough that Sato-baasan could smell the ink and the camellia oil the girl used on her hair, Kume's oil, bought from the same stall by the same bridge for twenty years.

"You should eat something," Rosalind said. "Ishida-san made rice. It's terrible, but there's pickles."

"In a moment." Sato-baasan nodded toward the harbor. "Your ship is showing her light early tonight."

Rosalind followed her gaze and was silent a moment. "Kenji-san says the agent boards tomorrow. Final inspection before the sailing. And Pell—" She stopped. Started again. "Pell sent word. Through the chandlery. One sentence." Her voice dropped. "*Thursday night, use the loading door.*"

Sato-baasan turned her head and studied the girl's face in the spill of lamplight from within — the exhaustion, the fear, and underneath both, banked and burning steady, the thing that had made Kume dive water no other woman on the beach would touch.

"So the counting-house man has chosen," Sato-baasan said.

"Or he's baiting a trap."

"Or both. Men can be two things. I have been married twice; I know." She reached up and patted the girl's ink-stained hand, once, hard, the way she had patted the shoulders of young divers before their first deep descent — not comfort, exactly, but transfer. "Eat your terrible rice. Sleep two hours. Tomorrow we finish whatever is left to finish, and Thursday night, I will be sitting on this stone again, and anyone who wants to reach that loading door will first have to explain themselves to an old woman who has buried everyone she loved and is not afraid of what remains."

Rosalind's throat moved. "Baasan — why? Why risk this? Miyo is my cause. The ledger is mine and Kenji's and Ishida's. You've given the tally-sticks. No one would blame you if you—"

"No one would blame me," Sato-baasan repeated. She looked out at the red riding light on the black water, at the ship that held her granddaughter's name in its hold like a stone in a net. "Child, I have been blamed my whole life — by the sea, by the company, by my own heart, every time I stayed silent to keep what little I had. Silence bought me sixty years and cost me everything else." She rose, joints announcing each inch, and stood above the girl on the step, straight-backed, headcloth white in the lamplight. "There is no one left to blame me now. So I have decided to be useful instead."

Inside, Ishida called softly that the rice was getting colder, and Kenji's pen scratched on, and the harbor breathed its long salt breath against the stones.

But Sato-baasan, turning to go in, paused with her hand on the door — because far out on the dark water, the Kaiyo-maru's single red light had gone out, and in its place, faint but unmistakable, rode the glow of work-lanterns on her deck.

Loading, in the dark. Ahead of schedule.

Someone aboard was in a hurry.
