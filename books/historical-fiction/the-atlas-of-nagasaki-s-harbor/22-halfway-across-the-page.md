## 22. Halfway Across the Page

The rain came in off the harbor at dusk, soft as a held breath, and Rosalind counted it among her small mercies. Rain meant fewer eyes on the streets between the tenement and Kenji's back room. Rain meant the company's men — Pell's men, she corrected herself, though she could no longer say that with certainty about anyone — preferred their doorways dry. And rain meant ink would flow smooth instead of dragging, if she kept the lamp close enough to warm the nib.

She sat with the ledger copy open before her, half of it finished, half of it still waiting like a shore across a strait. Two hundred and eleven pages copied in Kenji's careful hand or her own quicker one; two hundred and twelve more to go, if Ishida's count was right. She had taken to thinking of them not as pages but as crossings. Each entry was a boat going out: *Kaiyo-maru, manifest 14, ceramic ware, forty-one cases.* Each case weighed like a man kneeling. Somewhere on the far side of all that arithmetic were eleven living women whose names existed nowhere except in tally-sticks and an old diver's memory and now, line by line, in this book.

Her hand ached from wrist to knuckle. She flexed it, dipped the pen, kept writing.

*Entry for the twelfth of September. Divers transferred, port of destination Shanghai, per agreement with—*

The name again. The merchant's name, the one her mother's note had named, appearing in the margins of the master ledger like a watermark, patient and everywhere. She wrote it out fully this time, letter by letter, making it permanent, making it hers.

The rain thickened against the shutters. Sato-baasan had posted herself below at the mouth of the alley since nightfall, wrapped in oilcloth, a shape like driftwood that no one troubled to look at twice. Twice already tonight she had turned men away with nothing but her presence — once a Meridian runner claiming he sought directions, once a stranger whose boots were too good for the neighborhood. Rosalind heard the low exchange through the floorboards and did not stop writing. There was a strange steadiness in knowing someone stood guard over you. She had not felt it since her mother was alive.

Kenji slept sitting up in the corner, his translation notes fanned on his knees like a broken wing. He had worked until his hands shook — the cipher gave up its secrets slowly now, the deeper entries written in the old form, the one he'd been ruined for reading thirty years ago. *They never changed it,* he had said earlier, with something between contempt and grief. *Men who steal never change their locks. They only change who is allowed to carry keys.*

Ishida was out — walking the long way past the consulate, past the harbor office, gathering what he called *the weather*, meaning gossip, meaning the movement of officials, meaning anything that might tell them whether the ground was about to open beneath them.

So it was Rosalind alone when the knock came.

Three knocks. Even. Unhurried. Courteous, even, which was worse than pounding, because courteous knocks belonged to people who expected to be let in.

She closed the ledger copy and slid it beneath the floorboard with the others before she crossed the room. Through the paper of the shutter, lamplight behind her, she must have been visible as a silhouette — a woman alone in a rented room, doing nothing remarkable. She lifted the latch.

Mr. Pell stood on the step with an umbrella he hadn't opened and a paper-wrapped parcel under one arm.

He was not a large man. She had known that for two years, but seeing him outside the counting house made it newly strange — Mr. Pell diminished by the dark, by the rain beading his hat brim, by the fact that he had climbed to this neighborhood at all. His coat was good wool, English-cut, but the cuffs were frayed where no one at Meridian ever looked closely enough to notice. In the lamplight spilling past her shoulder she saw that he was wet to the shoulders despite the umbrella, that he had walked a long way in the rain rather than take a rickshaw, and that he had chosen a rickshaw-less route deliberately, so there would be no puller to remember him here.

All of which meant he had planned this visit the way she planned her lies: carefully, and with fear.

"Miss Amberdale." He glanced past her into the room, and she watched his gaze find the lamp, the cold rice, the folded bedding, the absence of anything incriminating because everything incriminating was under her feet. "May I come in? It's wretched weather."

"It is," she said, and stepped aside.

Kenji stirred in the corner but did not rise. He had met Pell once, years ago, across a negotiating table, and neither of them had pretended since then to have forgotten it. The old interpreter's eyes were open now, watchful above his folded arms, saying nothing — present as a witness, silent as stone.

Pell set the parcel on the table. He untied it with fussy precision. Inside: a tin of good tea, English tea, and a small paper sack of sugar candies, the kind sold near the foreign concession for children and homesick clerks.

"For the household," he said. "I'm told old Mr. Watanabe has a sweet tooth."

"You've been asking after my household," Rosalind said. Not a question. She kept her voice level, bookkeeper-flat, the voice she used for figures she didn't yet trust.

"I ask after all my staff." He lowered himself onto the stool she offered, arranging his wet coat with a small grimace. Up close, in this light, she saw how tired he was. Not working-tired. Something older than that, worn into him like a groove worn into a stair by decades of feet climbing reluctantly. "You've been promoted three weeks, Miss Amberdale. Raised pay. Given the shipping abstracts, the manifests, the whole spine of the company's business. Do you know why?"

"Because I don't make mistakes," she said.

"Because you don't make mistakes," he agreed. "And because I wanted you where I could see you." He took off his hat and set it on his knee, and for a moment he simply looked at his own hands, as though consulting them. "You've been working late. Coming in early. Asking questions about the Kaiyo-maru's route. Requesting the harbor workshop inventory — twice. Visiting the divers' beach on your half-day, which is your right, but which was noticed."

"By whom?"

"That doesn't matter." He raised his eyes to hers, and she steeled herself for menace and found none, and that frightened her more than menace would have. What she found instead was something like pleading, folded very small inside courtesy, the way a suicide note might be folded inside a letter of business. "Miss Amberdale. Rosalind." Her given name, for the first time. It landed strangely, almost tenderly, and she understood he had been saving it. "I'm going to speak plainly, and then we will never speak of this evening again, whichever way you answer me."

She said nothing. Busy hands, closed mouth. But her hands were still, and her mouth waited.

"There are men above me," Pell said. "There have always been men above me. When I came to Nagasaki I owed money — not gambling debts, nothing picturesque, just poverty, ordinary English poverty dressed in a collar — and the company paid it, and I have been paying it back for nineteen years at interest no honest bank would recognize. Every year the sum grows instead of shrinks. Do you understand? I am not a rich man stealing. I am a poor man owned."

In the corner, Kenji breathed slowly, deliberately, the way he did when he was memorizing.

"The ledgers you've been studying," Pell went on, "are not mine. The discrepancies you've found — and you've found them, don't insult us both by denying it, I taught you reconciliation and you're better at it than I am — they go back further than me. Further than the current men above me. It is a machine, and I am a gear in it, and gears do not choose what the machine makes." He leaned forward. The lamplight caught his face and she saw, truly saw, the sweat standing on his forehead despite the cool of the room. "Here is what I can do. Tomorrow I can transfer you to the Shanghai office. Full salary in advance, passage paid, excellent references, a life where nobody asks questions about households or registries or parents. Or—" and here his voice dropped, gentled, became almost kind, "—you stay, and you stop looking, and I protect you. Whatever comes. However it ends. You will be the last person in that building I let them touch. That is a promise I can keep, and it is the only promise I can keep, and I am offering it because—" he stopped. Started again. "Because you remind me of someone I failed to protect once, a long time ago, in another port. And because you are the best clerk I have ever trained, and I find I cannot bear—"

He stopped himself. Pressed his lips together. The sentence died unfinished, and its ghost hung in the room: *I cannot bear to watch you be destroyed for being right.*

Rosalind sat very still.

And she saw it — she couldn't help seeing it — the chains. Not metaphorically. She saw the young clerk arriving in Nagasaki with his debts and his good penmanship and his hunger to matter, signing papers he didn't read because the man across the desk had a kind voice. She saw the nineteen years of reconciliations he must have performed on his own conscience, column after column, entry after entry, always finding the balance slightly wrong and entering it anyway. She saw a man who had learned to call his cage a career, his captivity a position of trust. He was not evil. That was the terrible discovery of the evening. He was *owned*, and he had come out in the rain, unsheltered, spending whatever remained of his freedom on one attempt to save her from the same purchase.

She almost pitied him. She felt pity rising in her like the tide, warm and treacherous, and she understood in the same instant that pity was exactly the instrument being used — not cynically, perhaps, perhaps he even believed his own offer was kindness, but the machine did not care what he believed. The offer was the machine. Protection in exchange for silence was the oldest transaction in the world, and her mother had died in its arithmetic, and eleven women were at this moment riding at anchor in the dark with lanterns burning on their deck, loading ahead of schedule, while a clerk offered tea and candy and a ticket to Shanghai.

She thought of Sato-baasan standing in the rain below, driftwood-still, guarding a room full of paper with her body because her granddaughter was somewhere north of here in the hold of that ship. She thought of Kenji, ruined at thirty for reading what she was copying at twenty-three, choosing now, at the end of his courage, to help anyway. She thought of the pearl hidden in her mother's relics, perfect and cold, and the name written beside it.

"Mr. Pell," she said quietly. "Who was the person you failed to protect?"

His face went grey. "That isn't—"

"A diver?" she asked. "A girl? Someone whose columns didn't reconcile either?"

The silence stretched until it screamed. He looked down at the tea tin, the candies, the small domestic offerings he had brought, and she watched him understand that she had seen through the parcel to the bribe beneath it, and through the bribe to the fear beneath that, and through the fear to the man, and that seeing all of it had changed nothing in her answer.

"I can't accept," she said. "Not Shanghai, not protection, not silence."

"Rosalind—"

"No." Gentle, but final, the way she entered a figure she knew was correct and would not revise. "You think you're offering me safety. But the ship sails soon, and the workshop comes down after it, and when those things happen, everyone who helped will need protecting, and you know as well as I do what your protection will be worth on that day. You can't even protect yourself." She rose. "I don't hate you, Mr. Pell. I think you may be the saddest man I've ever met. But I'm not for sale, and neither are the eleven names I haven't finished copying yet."

For a moment something moved through his face that was not anger — something closer to relief, terrible relief, the look of a man who has tested the lock on his own cell every night for nineteen years and finally been proven right that it holds. He stood. He put on his hat. At the door he paused, his back to her, rain-hemmed in the lamplight.

"They'll torch it before demolition now," he said, barely above the sound of the rain. "Loading early means burning early. Days, not weeks. Finish your accounts." And he stepped out into the dark and was gone, umbrella still furled, letting the rain fall on him as though he deserved it.

* * *

She did not sleep. Neither did Kenji. They worked by lamplight with the shutters drawn, page after page, and when her hand cramped he took the pen from her fingers without a word and wrote while she dictated, and when his eyes blurred she read the cipher aloud and he answered in plain English, and the ledger crossed its halfway mark sometime after midnight with no ceremony at all — just the scratch of the pen, and the rain easing, and the far-off creak of the harbor.

Near dawn, footsteps on the stair. Sato-baasan first, soaked through and unbowed, then Ishida, pale and moving fast, shaking rain from his sleeves.

"You need to hear this," he said. "The harbormaster's office issued new papers tonight. The Kaiyo-maru's departure has been moved forward." He swallowed. "Five days. Maybe less. And the workshop demolition notice goes up tomorrow morning."

Rosalind looked down at the ledger, at the hundred and eleven pages still uncopied, at the mountain of proof still sleeping under the floorboards — and at the tide, which was no longer waiting for them.

Outside, the sky was turning the color of ash, and somewhere on the water, the work-lanterns of the Kaiyo-maru burned on.
