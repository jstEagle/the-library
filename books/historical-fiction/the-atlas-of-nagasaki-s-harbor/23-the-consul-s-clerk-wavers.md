## 23. The Consul's Clerk Wavers

Ishida had dressed carefully for the visit, which was itself a kind of confession.

He stood before the cracked mirror in his rented room on the slope above Oura, knotting the collar of his good jacket — the one he wore to funerals and to interviews with superiors, and there was little difference between the two occasions now — and told himself that a man who dressed like a petitioner would be received as one. The British consular compound sat at the foot of the foreign quarter, all white walls and louvred shutters, its flag hanging limp in the wet morning air like something drowned. Ishida had walked past it perhaps two hundred times in his years with the census bureau, and had never once imagined himself walking through its gate as anything other than an instrument of the state. Now he was about to enter it carrying papers that could hang him.

Rosalind was already waiting at the corner where Higashi-machi met the bund, standing beneath the camphor tree with her hands folded — busy hands, closed mouth; he knew her habits now well enough to read them like weather. She wore her English half this morning: a grey dress cut plainly, her hair pinned high, her posture arranged so that no observer would look twice at her face and see Kume the diver's daughter looking out of it. Only her eyes betrayed her, and only if you knew what to search for. Ishida did. He had spent three weeks learning to read this woman, and what he had learned was that she was made entirely of things other people had failed to notice.

"You're early," she said.

"So are you."

"One of us should be honest about it." She glanced up the hill toward the consulate, then back at him. "The clerk. Mr. Fenwick. You're certain he'll see us?"

"I'm certain he'll see *me*. A census official presenting a routine inquiry about foreign registration records is tedious but unremarkable. A census official arriving with a half-English bookkeeper's assistant is remarkable." He paused, choosing honesty because lying to her had become more effortful than telling truth. "I've written ahead that I have questions regarding household registrations among the foreign firms' employees. It is even true. It simply isn't the whole of the truth, and Mr. Fenwick will understand within four minutes that it isn't, and then we will learn what sort of man he is."

"And if he's Meridian's sort of man?"

Ishida looked at the flag on its dead pole, at the gulls wheeling over the masts below, at the smoke rising from the Kaiyo-maru's funnel where she lay at her moorings like a patient beast being fattened for a journey. Eleven divers aboard that ship by now, or soon. He kept the number written inside his cuff like a prayer.

"Then we leave through the front door," he said, "and we find another door. But I don't believe he is. I watched him last month when the Meridian men came to register their new hires. He checked every name against his rolls twice, and when Pell signed for the lot of them, Fenwick looked at the signature the way a man looks at meat he suspects has turned. There is doubt in that office. Doubt is a door."

They crossed the bund together, not touching, not speaking, maintaining between them the careful distance of two people who had agreed to be conspirators and were still negotiating the terms. The harbor was at its mid-morning business: sampans threading between the hulls, coolies chanting loads up the stone slips, the smell of pitch and brine and charcoal from the noodle stalls along the seawall. Somewhere beneath all that industry lay the workshop with its master ledger, six days from demolition now — five, if the company moved faster than its own notices. Ishida felt each day like a stone added to a sack he carried against his chest.

The sentry at the consular gate examined Ishida's bureau credentials with the leisurely contempt of empire, waved Rosalind through with slightly less, and handed them off to a Chinese house-boy who led them across a courtyard of raked gravel into a corridor that smelled of beeswax and cold tea. Everything in the building was designed, Ishida thought, to make a visitor feel the weight of the floor he stood on: the high ceiling, the portraits of unsmiling officials, the clock ticking with the confidence of a machine that had never once been wrong about anything.

Mr. Fenwick received them in a side office barely larger than a ship's cabin. He was younger than Ishida had expected — thirty at most, sandy-haired, with ink stains on his right cuff and the reddened eyes of a man who worked past lamplight — and he rose when they entered, which consular clerks were not obliged to do. That small courtesy told Ishida more than the interview's first ten minutes would.

"Mr. Ishida. Census Bureau." Fenwick shook his hand once, briskly. "And your colleague?"

"Miss Amberdale," Rosalind said, before Ishida could arrange the introduction. Her English came out polished, faintly clipped, the accent of someone taught by a careful man. "I keep accounts for the Meridian Trading Company. I'm here in a private capacity."

Fenwick's eyebrows performed a small, controlled ascent. He gestured them into chairs, closed the door himself, and returned to his desk without sitting behind it — instead perching on its edge, arms folded, which put them all awkwardly at the same height and was, Ishida suspected, deliberate. Clerks who wished to be underestimated sat behind desks. This one wished to see clearly.

"Now," Fenwick said. "Household registration inquiries. For foreign firm employees. At nine o'clock on a Tuesday, requiring a personal appointment, attended by a Meridian employee in a private capacity." He let the sentence lie there between them like a card played face-up. "I've been at this desk four years, Mr. Ishida. In four years, no census official has ever requested an appointment. They send notes. So either your bureau has undergone a religious conversion toward thoroughness, or you've come about something else entirely. Which is it?"

Ishida had prepared a speech. He abandoned it. Some rooms rewarded preparation; this one would punish it.

"The second," he said. "But before I say more, I need to know what happens in this room after we leave it. Because there are two versions of Mr. Fenwick, and I have come too far to discover the wrong one at the end."

Something flickered across the young man's face — not offense; recognition. The look of a man hearing his own suspicion spoken aloud by a stranger.

"And those two versions are?"

"The version who writes to the governor's office this afternoon and finds himself promoted by spring," Ishida said. "And the version who listens."

The clock ticked. Out in the harbor a ship's bell struck the half hour, the sound coming flat across the water.

Fenwick unfolded his arms slowly. He glanced at Rosalind, then back at Ishida, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped into a different register — quieter, flatter, the voice of a man stepping off solid ground to test whether ice holds.

"Three months ago," he said, "the Meridian Trading Company registered forty-two new laborers for coastal transport work. Deckhands, stokers, cargo crew. I processed the papers myself. Twenty-nine of those men had never held a seaman's certificate in their lives. Several were listed with ages between fifty-five and sixty-one — remarkable vitality in that trade, don't you think? One of them, a Mr. Tanaka, age fifty-eight, I later learned was seventeen." He rubbed the bridge of his nose. "I flagged the irregularities. I wrote a memorandum. My superior thanked me for my diligence, forwarded the matter to the Japanese prefectural authorities, and informed me that labor contracts between Japanese subjects and Japanese companies fell outside consular jurisdiction. Six weeks later, the same forty-two names reappeared on a supplementary manifest, properly certified this time, bearing prefectural stamps I have since reason to believe were purchased rather than issued." He looked up. "So. You wanted to know which version of me sits in this chair. Ask your question."

Ishida took out the copied pages.

He had wrapped them in oilcloth inside a folio marked with census insignia, and he laid them on the desk between them now with the care of a surgeon laying out instruments. Three sheets, transcribed in Rosalind's immaculate hand from the fragments they had smuggled out of the harbor workshop: shipping manifests for 'ceramic ware' whose declared weights exceeded any kiln's output in Kyushu; payment entries routed to a Shanghai syndicate under the name of a respectable Nagasaki merchant; and — the page that had made even Kenji Watanabe go silent — a schedule of transfers listing eleven individuals by tally-number rather than name, valued, in the margin, in yen.

Fenwick read. Ishida watched him read, and watched the color change in the young man's face in stages: first the professional frown of a clerk finding errors, then the stillness of a man understanding that these were not errors, then — when he reached the valuation column — the particular gray pallor that Ishida had seen once before, on the face of a customs officer in Moji who had opened a crate he should not have opened.

"They're selling people," Fenwick said. Not a question.

"Divers," Rosalind said quietly. "Ama women and girls, mostly. Bought out of debt bondage along this coast and contracted to pearl operations outside Shanghai. The contracts run seven years. Most of the women won't survive five at the depths they'll be worked." Her voice stayed level, but her hands, folded in her lap, had gone white at the knuckles. "Eleven of them are aboard a ship in your harbor right now. The Kaiyo-maru. She sails on the morning tide within the fortnight."

Fenwick set the pages down. He stood, walked to the shuttered window, opened one slat, and looked out at the harbor as though expecting to see the crime itself floating there among the junks and lighters. Perhaps, Ishida thought, he did. The water hid everything and revealed everything, depending on how hard a man was willing to look.

"Do you know," Fenwick said, without turning, "what my position actually is? Not what the plaque in the hall says — what it is. I am a junior clerk in a consulate that employs eleven people, in a treaty port where the trade of this entire coast passes through maybe fifteen merchant houses, of which Meridian is the largest. The consul's social calendar is managed by the same merchants. His club membership is subsidized by them. When a British sailor dies ashore, it is Meridian's launch that carries the body out to the burial ground, and Meridian's chaplain who says the words. And when I wrote my memorandum about forty-two phantom sailors—" he turned from the window, and his mouth twisted, "—my superior didn't even read it. I know because I saw it later, still sealed, filed under 'miscellaneous correspondence.' Unopened. My diligence went into a drawer unopened, Mr. Ishida."

"Yet you're listening now," Ishida said.

"I'm listening because I can't stop listening. There's a difference." Fenwick came back around the desk and sat down heavily, and for the first time he looked his age, and younger — a young man carrying a weight that had been set on him by people who never intended to feel it themselves. "You want to know what will happen if you walk into the consul's office tomorrow with those pages and a speech about moral outrage. I will tell you exactly what will happen, because I have watched it happen. The consul will receive you gravely. He will express profound concern. He will say that matters of internal Japanese administration must be referred to the competent local authorities, and he will draft a note — a polite, correct, useless note — to the prefectural governor's office. And the governor's office will forward it to the police commissioner, and the police commissioner dines monthly at Meridian's expense table at the International Club, and within the week the note will be back on the consul's desk marked 'no irregularities found,' and the file will close, and the Kaiyo-maru will sail, and everyone will have behaved correctly throughout."

The words landed in the room like stones into still water. Rosalind had gone very still beside him. Ishida felt the floor tilt beneath the conversation — the ground shifting, precisely as he had feared it might, the machinery of law revealing itself as a wheel that turned only in one direction, away from the powerless.

"There must be exceptions," Ishida said. "Cases the consul cannot decline."

"There is exactly one exception." Fenwick tapped the copied pages with one finger. "This. Paper. Not rumor, not testimony, not outrage — paper. Undeniable, redundant, corroborated paper, presented publicly enough that burying it costs more than acting on it. Do you understand the distinction? The consul does not need courage. No one in this system needs courage. What they need is *exposure*. If the evidence exists only in this room, it can die in this room, and everyone involved will sleep soundly. But if the same figures appear in three independent sources that cannot be discredited — if the names of the sold women are known to people who will raise them aloud — if a detention order, once issued, cannot be quietly reversed without the issuing authority explaining himself to his own government—" Fenwick spread his hands. "Then the arithmetic changes. Bureaucracies are cowardly, gentlemen — forgive me, madam — but bureaucracies are also exquisitely sensitive to embarrassment. You cannot appeal to their conscience. You can only make conscience cheaper than concealment."

"And the consul's conscience?" Rosalind asked. "Where does it sit on that scale?"

Fenwick regarded her for a long moment. "You've been in England," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I was raised by an Englishman."

"Then you may already know the answer. The consul is not wicked. He is worse than wicked; he is comfortable. Comfort is the most durable material ever devised by man." He leaned forward. "But comfort has a weakness, and I am going to tell you what it is, because I have decided — apparently in the last ninety seconds, against every instinct of self-preservation I possess — that I am tired of filing memoranda into drawers."

* * *

What Fenwick gave them, over the next hour, was a map of the machine.

He drew it first in words, then — glancing twice at the closed door — in pencil on the back of a blank registration form: boxes and arrows, the consul, the chancery, the prefectural offices, the police, the merchant houses, each connected by lines labeled with the currency that flowed along them, and it was not always money. Favor flowed. Silence flowed. Invitation flowed. A daughter's marriage prospects flowed. Ishida watched the diagram take shape and felt the peculiar vertigo of seeing, drawn plainly in pencil, the architecture that had governed his whole working life without his ever having seen it — the census bureau a single small box near the bottom, its arrows pointing upward only.

"Your proof must be public," Fenwick said, tapping the top of the diagram. "Not leaked — public. Presented in a manner that creates witnesses. A petition delivered with the press aware of it, however thin the foreign press presence here is — and it is thin, but the *Japan Gazette* in Kobe would print this if the documents were sound, and the Shanghai papers reprint everything. Or testimony given before multiple officials simultaneously, so that no single man can bury it alone. Or—" he hesitated, then finished, "—or a situation so far advanced that doing nothing becomes itself a scandal. A ship detained in open harbor. Names read aloud on a public quay. Once a thing has happened in daylight, it cannot unhappen."

"And if we bring you the complete ledger," Rosalind said slowly, "authenticated — every entry verified by a former company interpreter who handled these ciphers thirty years ago — with the names of eleven living women matched to tally-sticks in the possession of the oldest ama diver on this coast—"

"Then you would have the strongest case file I have seen in four years at this desk," Fenwick said. "And I would place it in the consul's hands myself, and stand there while he read it, and refuse to leave the room until he acted." He said it steadily, but Ishida saw the cost of it move through the young man's face like a shadow under water. "I want to be honest about what I'm risking, because you deserve honesty if I'm asking you to risk the same. If this fails — if the consul hands it back, if the prefecture buries it — the men above me will not forget my name. I will be posted to Hakodate. To Vladivostok. To nowhere, permanently. Possibly worse; there are ways of ruining a clerk that don't require charges." He straightened the copied pages on his desk, squaring their edges with unnecessary precision. "But I have processed the papers of two hundred and forty-one indentured laborers in four years, Miss Amberdale, and I have wondered, every night for four years, how many of those numbers were people. I would like, once, before they post me to the end of the earth, to know a number was a person and act accordingly."

The clock struck eleven. Through the window's slat, the light had shifted; the harbor below had turned silver and busy, and somewhere among those masts the Kaiyo-maru waited with her stolen cargo of women, counting down tides.

* * *

They walked back along the bund in silence for the length of two warehouses. Then Rosalind stopped, at the rail above the water, and looked out at the fleet of small boats working the shallows — the ama boats, their flags bright, the divers' songs carrying faintly over the water in the old cadences that predated every treaty and every trading company on the shore.

"He'll do it," she said at last. "Fenwick. He meant it."

"He meant it today," Ishida said. "Meaning is a perishable commodity, Miss Amberdale. Today he is brave because the decision is still abstract. The day we put the actual papers in his hands — the day refusing becomes a choice he makes in front of witnesses — that is the day we learn whether he holds." He gripped the rail. The wood was sun-warm and salt-roughened under his palms. "Which means our task is unchanged, and shorter than it was this morning. We must finish the copy. We must authenticate it. And we must deliver it in such a way that Fenwick's courage, whatever quantity of it survives contact with his superiors, is not the only thing standing between eleven women and the open sea."

Below them, one of the ama boats came about, and a diver surfaced in its lee, black-haired, gasping, laughing at the boatman's shout — alive, free, uncounted. Rosalind watched her the way one watches a fire in a neighboring house.

"Five days," she said. "Maybe less. The workshop comes down, the ledger burns, and the ship sails."

"Then we work tonight," Ishida said. "And every night until the ink runs out or the tide turns."

It was as they turned from the rail, as they began walking back toward the tenement streets where Sato-baasan would be waiting with rice and news and her terrible vigilant patience, that Ishida saw the man across the street — leaning in the shade of the customs house wall, hat brim low, a face he half-knew from somewhere in the company's orbit. The man did not follow them. He did not need to. He merely pushed off from the wall, unhurried, and walked in the direction of the Meridian counting house, and Ishida understood, with the cold settling certainty of a man who has read too many registers not to recognize a name being taken, that their appointment at the consulate had not gone unwitnessed.

The ground had shifted indeed. The only question left was how fast it was sliding, and whether they could outrun it to the workshop door.
