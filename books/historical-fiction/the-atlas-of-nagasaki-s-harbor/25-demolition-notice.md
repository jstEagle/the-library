## 25. Demolition Notice

The notice arrived on a Tuesday, pasted to the counting house door with rice glue so fresh it still shone.

Rosalind saw it before Mr. Pell did. She had come early — she always came early now, her nights given over to the copying and her mornings running on tea and something harder than sleep — and the low sun off the harbor caught the white paper like a sail. She stopped on the steps. The characters were printed, official, stamped twice in red: one seal for the Harbor Works Bureau, one for the Municipal Office.

*By order of the Nagasaki Harbor Improvement Commission, the structure known as the old chandlery workshop, Wharf Three, is designated for demolition. Clearance of contents must be completed within three (3) days of this notice. Demolition commences in six (6) days.*

She read it three times. The words did not change. The harbor did not stop. Behind her, a cartload of coal rattled over the cobbles, and somewhere out on the water a sampan bell rang the hour, indifferent as ever, as though the world had not just folded its timeline in half.

Three days to clear the contents. Six days to demolition.

The master ledger was inside that workshop.

She heard Pell's footsteps behind her on the stairs and made herself move, made herself climb the last two steps and pull the door open as if she had merely been waiting for it to be unlocked, as if her heart were not hammering against her ribs like a fist on a locked door. Pell came up beside her, hat in hand, squinting at the notice.

"About time," he said mildly. "That place has been an eyesore since before I was weaned." He glanced at her, and something in her face must have shown, because his eyes narrowed a fraction. "Miss Amberdale. You look pale."

"The light," she said. "Off the water. It comes up hard at this hour."

"It does," he agreed, and held the door for her, and she went in smelling of ink and lamp oil and the particular dust of ledgers, and sat down at her desk, and opened the daybook, and did not see a single figure on the page.

* * *

She counted the days of work remaining the way Sato-baasan had once taught her to count breaths between dives: precisely, without panic, because panic spent air faster than fear could refill it.

Two-thirds copied. That was the arithmetic she carried home through the evening streets like a stone in her chest. Two-thirds of the master ledger existed now in duplicate, hidden in the false bottom of Kenji's writing chest beneath a layer of old translation exercises no one would ever want to read. One-third remained — the shipping manifests for the Kaiyo-maru's current voyage, the debt-bondage rolls, the final pages where the syndicate's principal had signed his name in his own elegant hand, believing, no doubt, that paper kept in a locked workshop on company wharf was as safe as paper kept in a grave.

Three days for contents to be cleared.

Six days for the walls to come down.

It should have been enough. It would have been enough, if the copying had been simple, if they could have walked into the workshop by daylight and asked to borrow a book. But every visit was made after midnight, through the gap in the palings behind the cooperage, and every page was copied by lamplight in a room that smelled of hemp rope and brine, and every entry passed through Rosalind's hands, then Kenji's for verification, then Ishida's for the cipher work, and the chain could not be shortened without risking everything.

She climbed the tenement stairs and found them all already gathered, which meant Ishida had seen the notice too, which meant the news had traveled ahead of her the way bad news does, quick as water finding a drain.

Kenji stood at the window with his back to the room. He had aged years in the past month; she could see it in the set of his shoulders, which used to be loose and now looked like a man carrying a beam. Sato-baasan sat on the floor mat with her granddaughter's tally-sticks across her knees, turning them over one by one, each stick a notch-cut record of a dive, a debt, a name — the eleven names they had matched to the Kaiyo-maru's route, the eleven living women held somewhere along that ship's line.

"Three days," Ishida said, without preamble. He had the notice itself in his hand, a second copy he'd pulled from the bureau — legally he shouldn't have been able to, and the fact that he had told Rosalind more about how far his own coercion ran than any confession. "Clearance of contents in three. Demolition in six."

"I read it," Rosalind said.

"Then you understand what it means. They are not tearing down a building, Miss Amberdale. They are destroying evidence. Once those walls come down, whatever is found in the rubble will be 'unrecoverable.' Burned debris, wet paper, salvage claims. There will be nothing left to copy because there will be nothing left."

"Then we finish in three days," Rosalind said. "We work faster."

Kenji turned from the window. His face in the lamplight was terrible with something she recognized but had never seen aimed at her before: the specific fear of a man who has already lost everything once to these same people and knows exactly what they do when cornered.

"Faster," he repeated. "Do you know why the entries take as long as they take? Because the cipher changes. Not page to page — *column* to column, sometimes. The old Meridian system uses rotating substitution keys keyed to the date of entry. If we rush, if we misread even one key character, the whole page is wrong, and a wrong page is worse than no page, because when it goes in front of the consul and fails verification, we will never get a second hearing. We have one chance. One. And you want to spend it on speed."

"I want to spend it on *anything*," Rosalind said, and heard her own voice crack, and hated it. "Sensei. In six days there will be a hole on the waterfront where the proof used to be."

"And if we produce a flawed copy in three," Kenji said quietly, "there will be a trial, and the flaw will be found, and the consul will wash his hands, and every one of those eleven women will sail anyway — and this time they will know who tried to stop it. Do you think the syndicate forgives? I have been forgiven nothing for thirty years. I have watched them forgive *nothing*."

The silence after that was long. Sato-baasan's sticks clicked softly, one against another, as she turned them.

"There is a third way," she said.

They all looked at her. She was very old and very small in the lamplight, her hands brown and knotted as driftwood, and when she spoke it was in the unhurried voice of a woman who had held her breath at the bottom of the sea more times than anyone in the room could count.

"You are arguing about the paper. The paper is not the thing. The thing is what the paper proves." She lifted one tally-stick, held it to the light. "This says Umekichi, third daughter of my son, sold north with her debts, spring of last year. Cut by my own hand, witnessed by two divers who are still alive and can swear to it. This—" she took up another, and another, laying them side by side on the mat until eleven lay in a row like fish on ice— "is testimony. Paper burns. Testimony walks ashore."

"The consul wants documents," Ishida said. "I asked him — or rather, I asked his clerk pretending to ask about passports. Documentary proof, verifiable, cross-referenced. Rumor and testimony alone, he'll hand the matter straight back to the prefecture, and half the prefecture eats from Meridian's bowl."

"Then we give him both," Rosalind said.

And there it was — the shape of the plan arriving in her mind the way the answer to a column of figures sometimes arrives, suddenly, all at once, after hours of staring at the parts. She sat down among them, on the floor, because standing felt suddenly absurd.

"We cannot copy faster without breaking the copy. So we don't break it. We split the work differently. Sensei verifies the cipher as each page comes off — that doesn't change. But Ishida-san takes the finished pages directly to the consul's clerk as they're completed, in batches, instead of holding everything for the end. A partial file in the consul's hands is worth more than a perfect file in a burning building. And Sato-baasan's testimony, and the tally-sticks, go with the first batch — tonight, tomorrow morning, whenever the clerk will see him. Let the consul begin reading while we are still writing. Let him be halfway convinced before the deadline arrives instead of being handed everything at midnight on the last night and expected to act by dawn."

Ishida was already nodding, his pen-hand twitching toward his notebook. "Staggered submission. It also protects us — if we're discovered, the evidence survives outside the city, piece by piece."

"And if the consul decides halfway through that he'd rather not be troubled?" Kenji asked. His voice was flat, but Rosalind heard the question underneath it: *if we fail halfway through, we have shown them our hand.*

"Then we will know it sooner," Rosalind said, "with time left to find another door. Better a closed door on Tuesday than on the morning the ship sails."

Sato-baasan began gathering her tally-sticks into their cloth wrap, deliberately, one by one, and Rosalind understood that the argument was over, because the oldest person in the room had decided, and in this family — this strange, forged, found family of liars and clerks and divers — age decided the way the tide decided.

"Three days," Kenji said at last. He said it like a sentence being pronounced, and then, softer, like a prayer being accepted: "Very well. Three days. But we do it properly or not at all. Rosalind — from tonight, you sleep here. Four hours, no more. You will not copy exhausted, because exhausted hands make the errors that kill us."

"Yes, sensei."

"And you eat. All of you. I mean it. I have watched clever people defeat themselves with hunger and pride, and I refuse to watch it happen to this household."

* * *

She slept badly and woke before dawn and lay listening to the rain beginning on the roof tiles, soft as fingers, and thought about Pell.

She had thought about him constantly since the promotion, since the raise, since the strange afternoon when he had stood too close to her desk and said, in a voice pitched below the other clerks' hearing, *"You remind me of someone I failed once. Don't let me fail again"* — words she still couldn't parse, still turned over nightly like a coin whose denomination kept changing. Complicit or captive? Kind jailer or fellow prisoner? Every kindness he showed her now read double, the way a cipher reads double, and she hated that he had done this to her mind — that she could no longer receive a simple human warmth without auditing it.

But today there was a use for the ambiguity, and she made herself walk to his desk at midmorning with the demolition notice in her hand, wearing her best blank clerk's face.

"Mr. Pell. Forgive me — the Harbor Bureau posted this at the door. The old chandlery on Wharf Three is coming down. I only wondered whether the company holds stored goods there that need inventorying. If it's assigned to us, I could start a disposal schedule."

It was a risk. It was possibly a stupid risk. But she needed to know whether the demolition order had come *through* Meridian or merely *over* Meridian, and Pell's face, in the first three seconds, would tell her more than a week of eavesdropping.

He took the notice. Read it. And Rosalind watched — with the cold, clear attention of a bookkeeper who trusts columns over faces — watched his thumb press white against the paper's edge, watched his jaw tighten once and release, watched the flicker of something that was not surprise.

Not surprise. *Confirmation.*

"No," he said, after a moment too long. "Nothing of ours stored there. The workshop's been dark since the chandler retired." He handed the notice back, and his eyes came up to hers, and held, one beat, two — and she saw in them a question shaped exactly like hers, two people asking each other the same unaskable thing across a desk of honest-looking ledgers. "Curious timing, though. The commission moves slowly on everything else. Roads, drainage, the seawall — years. But a derelict shed gets six days."

"Efficiency," Rosalind said.

"Mm," said Pell. "Efficiency."

He returned to his correspondence. She returned to her columns. Neither of them wrote anything down about the other, and both of them knew it.

* * *

That night the rain came down in earnest, a proper autumn rain off the China coast, drumming on the workshop roof while Rosalind sat at the master ledger with her sleeves tied back and her ink warmed over a candle flame so it wouldn't thicken. The copying went strangely well. Fear, she discovered, was a kind of fuel — it burned clean if you fed it to the work instead of letting it feed on you. Page after page came off her pen steady and true: the manifest of the Kaiyo-maru's outbound cargo, weights entered as ceramic ware that reconciled to no kiln in Japan; the bondage rolls with their euphemism of "contract labor, term indefinite"; and then, near midnight, the page she had been dreading and seeking both — the signature block, where the syndicate's principal had countersigned the route in his own hand, the respected merchant, the temple donor, the man whose name appeared on charitable subscriptions in the *Nagasaki Press*.

Kenji verified the cipher column by column beside her, murmuring the key-changes under his breath like a litany. Ishida worked at the little table by the door, preparing the first submission batch: the opening manifest pages, cross-indexed to Sato-baasan's tally-sticks, wrapped in oilcloth against the rain.

At two in the morning, Sato-baasan appeared in the doorway with rice balls and tea, and stood looking at the ledger for a long moment, her face unreadable.

"How much remains?" she asked.

Rosalind counted the unturned pages with her fingertips. "Thirty-one sheets. At tonight's pace — two nights."

"Demolition is in five days now," the old woman said. "Contents clearance in two." She set down the tray. "You will finish. I have decided it, and I am older than the commission."

Despite everything — despite the deadline collapsing around them, despite the rain, despite the signature of a powerful man drying on the page before her — Rosalind laughed, a small startled sound, and Kenji smiled into his verification, and for a moment the room held something that was almost warmth.

It lasted until Ishida, returning from covering the lantern against a draft, paused at the window facing the wharf.

"Lights," he said quietly. "On Wharf Three. Someone is at the workshop."

They killed the lamp and came to the glass. Through the rain-haze, far off across the black water, two lanterns moved inside the workshop's high windows — slow, methodical, the pace of men taking inventory. Then a third light, at the loading door, and the faint orange bloom of a pipe bowl being lit.

"Not thieves," Kenji breathed. "Thieves don't inspect."

"Measuring," Ishida whispered. "For the demolition crews. Or—" He didn't finish it. He didn't have to.

Or deciding how much needed to burn before the official fire.

Rosalind stood in the dark with thirty-one sheets left to copy and two days of clearance remaining, and felt the deadline contract around her like a closing hand — and then, beneath the fear, something colder and steadier settled into place, the diver's arithmetic, the arithmetic of breath: *you do not fight the water; you count it.*

"Tomorrow," she said, "we start submitting at dawn."
