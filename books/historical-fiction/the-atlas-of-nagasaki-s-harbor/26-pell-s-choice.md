## 26. Pell's Choice

The order came, as all his orders did, on a Tuesday — the day Mr. Pell had long ago learned to dread.

Tuesdays were when the Meridian Trading Company balanced its books, and when the men from the syndicate came down from the hill houses to look over the harbor the way a farmer looks over cattle. On this particular Tuesday, the man who came was not the usual one. This man wore no merchant's broadcloth but a plain grey suit, and he carried no ledger of his own. He sat in Pell's office without being invited, folded his hands upon his knees, and waited while Pell finished totting a column he no longer understood.

"Mr. Pell." The visitor spoke English with an accent flattened by Shanghai docks and Singapore warehouses. "There has been a change of schedule."

Pell laid down his pen. He had learned, across eleven years in Nagasaki and three before that in Foochow, that the first rule of survival was to let such men finish their sentences. The second rule was never to appear relieved, curious, or afraid. He arranged his face into the mild blankness of a man whose whole attention is given to arithmetic.

"A change," Pell repeated.

"The workshop on the east quay. The old boatshed with the loft above it." The grey man consulted nothing; he carried the company's geography in his head the way other men carried prayers. "It is scheduled for demolition. That date has been moved forward."

"How far moved?"

"All of it. Contents are to be disposed of within the week. Burned, if the weather permits. Burned regardless, if it doesn't." The man's eyes were pale and patient, like a fish's. "You will prepare the inventory yourself. You will certify that everything of value has been removed and catalogued. And you will see that nothing — *nothing* — remains in that building that any clerk, consul, or census official might later find instructive."

*Instructive.* Pell turned the word over. In the vocabulary of these men, instructive meant damning. It meant pages that weighed like people. It meant columns of ceramic ware that had never held a single cup.

"I'll need two days to do the inventory properly," Pell said. "The insurers will want—"

"The insurers have been paid off." A thin smile crossed the fish-pale face. "You misunderstand me, Mr. Pell. There is to be no *proper* inventory. There is to be a short inventory. One page. Your signature at the bottom. The building comes down, the ashes go into the harbor, and the matter ends."

Pell looked out the window past the man's shoulder, down toward the water. From the counting house he could see the east quay, and the workshop squatting at its end like an old dog chained too long in one place. Smoke curled from its chimney even now. Boatwrights worked there, and rope-makers, and once — years ago, before his own ruin had been arranged so neatly — a certain kind of bookkeeping had been done there too, away from the counting house, away from the eyes of clerks who might still possess something resembling a conscience.

He thought of the loft above the workshop floor. He thought of the iron strongbox under the floorboards, and what it contained. He had put much of it there himself, in the years when obedience seemed simpler than courage.

"When?" he asked.

"Burn it early," the grey man said, rising. "Before your clever little assistant can find whatever she imagines she is looking for. You've noticed her looking, Mr. Pell. Don't insult us both by denying it."

His heart performed a small, cold somersault. "Miss Amberdale balances my columns better than I could myself," he said carefully. "If she has questions about freight classifications—"

"We know her mother's name," the man said simply, and walked out.

Pell sat for a long time afterward with his hands flat on the desk, as though pressing down papers that wanted to fly. Through the window, the harbor glittered with its usual terrible beauty — junks and steam launches, drying nets, the white hulls of foreign ships at anchor, and beneath all of it, somewhere below the green water, the drowned and the sold and the silent.

He had been in this room, in this posture, eleven years ago, when they had first shown him what his debts would cost. He remembered the exact figure: four thousand eight hundred yen, which might as well have been four million, which might as well have been his pulse. They had not threatened him. Threatening was crude. They had simply explained, with the patience of men describing tide tables, how a disgraced clerk with debts in three ports could be ruined in all of them, and how his sister in Manchester — widowed, consumptive, dependent on his remittances — could be made to learn of it by letter rather than gently, by degrees.

And then they had promoted him. That was the elegance of it. Ruin and promotion, delivered together, like a coin with two faces. He had signed. God help him, he had signed, and every year since, the signature had grown easier and the nights harder, until now the nights were simply the days continued in a different light, and he could no longer remember which version of himself was the forgery.

*Burn it early.*

He rose and went to the window. Below, crossing the yard with her ledgers hugged to her chest, went Miss Amberdale — small, quick, careful, her ink-stained fingers splayed across the bindings the way a diver's fingers spread against the water's pressure. She bowed to the yard keeper. She nodded to the rickshaw men. She kept her eyes down and her mouth shut and her hands busy, and Pell had watched her do it for two years with a growing, unnameable unease, because he recognized the discipline. It was his own. Busy hands, closed mouth. The liturgy of the hidden.

They knew her mother's name.

He went back to his desk and drew a fresh sheet toward him, and began — because the order had been given and orders were the only weather he had known for eleven years — to draft the short inventory. One page. His signature at the bottom. *Contents disposed of; premises cleared.*

His pen stopped after the third word.

* * *

That evening he walked out along the east quay, telling himself it was merely to inspect the premises, as any diligent factor would. The workshop stood open in the last light, the boatwrights gone home, the smell of oakum and pitch and salt hanging in the rafters. Upstairs, in the loft, the dust lay undisturbed except where his own feet had made paths over the years, small pilgrimages of guilt.

He knelt at the floorboard. The key to the strongbox hung, as always, on the nail behind the water cask, and his hand went to it automatically, and stopped.

What was he doing? What, in the name of everything he had signed away, was he actually doing?

He sat back on his heels in the dusty light. Below him, through the gaps in the boards, he could hear the water slapping the pilings, patient as arithmetic. He was fifty-one years old. He had a bad chest, a worse conscience, and a sister in Manchester who wrote twice a year and called him *her good brother*, which was the cruelest thing anyone had ever said to him. If he burned this building with the strongbox intact, the proof would go into the harbor with the ashes, and eleven divers — he had counted them, he could not stop counting them — would sail out on the morning tide inside the Kaiyo-maru, and Rosalind Amberdale would learn, sooner or later, that her supervisor had been the man who closed the account.

Or.

Or he could falsify the inventory — properly, thoroughly, artfully — listing items removed and catalogued that had never existed, certifying with his own signature that the loft was empty, and thereby buying the building three days of being officially worthless. No one guards an empty room. No one searches ash for what the inventory swears was never there.

But the strongbox itself — the original ledger, the shipping manifests, the cipher keys — those could not stay. When the fire came, firemen and idlers would pick through the ruins; the syndicate's own men would sift the ashes with sieves if necessary. The originals must vanish or burn completely, and he could not make them burn completely, because iron does not burn, and someone would eventually pry open whatever the flames left behind.

Which left only one honest dishonesty available to him: the originals must be found by someone else first. Someone who could copy what mattered and scatter what didn't. Someone quick, and careful, and already looking.

Someone with busy hands.

Pell took the key from behind the water cask. It was heavy in his palm, brass gone dark with salt air, and it seemed to weigh exactly as much as four thousand eight hundred yen, exactly as much as a sister in Manchester, exactly as much as thirty years of a dead girl's silence — for he had heard Kenji Watanabe's story once, drunk, at a farewell party nobody attended, and had never forgotten it, though he had pretended to.

He could not give her the key openly. Openly meant both their ruin by Thursday.

He considered the yard, the routes she walked, the habits he had catalogued without meaning to, the way a man in debt catalogs everything. She came in each morning by the quay gate. She kept her accounts in the second drawer of the desk by the window — he knew because he had once searched it, shamefully, on their instruction, and found nothing but pencils, blotting paper, and a pressed sprig of some flower he couldn't name. She ate her midday rice on the loading step when the weather was fine, alone, reading.

Reading.

Pell almost smiled. Almost. His face had forgotten how.

* * *

Three days remained before the demolition crews arrived. Two before the grey man returned to collect the certified inventory. Pell spent them in a fever of small, precise treacheries.

First, the inventory itself. He spent an entire evening composing it, striking out and rewriting until it read as a tired man's honest work: *One lot, assorted boatbuilding tools, worn. One lot, tarred cordage, unsound. One lot, timber offcuts, value nil.* Item by item he shrank the workshop's contents into worthlessness, and at the bottom, where the certification demanded a statement of removed valuables, he wrote: *Company property of record previously transferred to main counting house, per standing practice. Nothing further of value remaining on premises.* Which was even, in its way, true — the counting house *was* where such records ought to have been transferred, years ago, by a competent man, and their failure to be there was a failure he was now formally declaring not to exist.

He signed it. His hand barely shook. Eleven years of practice, it turned out, had taught him something useful after all: how to sign his own condemnation in a steady line.

Second, the timing. The order said *early*. Early, in the mouths of such men, meant *before anyone can act*, and it also meant *tonight, if convenient*. But fires required arrangement — oil men, a plausible origin, the harbor police bought to look slowly the other way. Those arrangements took a day to make, and Pell discovered that a man who has decided to betray his masters becomes wonderfully inventive about obstacles. He mislaid the oil contractor's address. He wrote the wrong date on the requisition to the harbor office — an honest error, a tired man's transposition of digits, *the 14th instead of the 11th* — and then apologized so abjectly that no one suspected design. He bought the arsonists, when they finally came, a round of drinks at the Anchor and talked to them, at tedious length, about insurance schedules, until one of them checked his watch and left muttering, and the other followed, and the evening burned down to nothing without anything else catching.

A day bought. Perhaps two. It was not salvation. It was a crack in the door, and cracks were all he had ever been permitted.

Third, the key.

He thought about it for a night and most of another day, ruling figures with half his mind and solving the problem with the rest, until the solution arrived so plainly that he laughed aloud at his desk, startling a junior clerk into apologizing to *him*.

She reads at midday. On the loading step. Alone.

On the fourth morning, Pell came to the counting house an hour before anyone else, as he always did, and performed the small rituals of his captivity: filled the inkwells, squared the ledgers, wound the clock that had run four minutes fast for six years because correcting it would require admitting the error. Then he took a piece of scrap wrapping-paper, coarse and brown, the kind that came around shipments of nails, and wrapped the brass key in it, folding the corners with a bookkeeper's precision. Around the outside, in his own cramped hand, he wrote nothing at all. A note would be evidence. Evidence was the one commodity he had spent eleven years learning to destroy.

Then he carried it out to the loading step, where the morning sun had not yet reached, and looked around — the empty yard, the sleeping harbor, a cat washing itself atop a coil of rope with obscene indifference to the affairs of men — and set the little brown package on the third board from the top step, flat against the riser, where a woman sitting down with a book and her rice tin would feel it under her before she ever saw it. Where no passing eye would find it, because no one else ever sat there. Where she would understand, because she was clever, and because the shape of a key through brown paper announces itself to anyone who has spent her life among locks and ledgers.

No note. No explanation. Let her decide whether it was a trap. He could not tell her. Telling her would be a confession, and confessions, in his experience, were simply inventories taken too late.

He walked back to his desk and opened the day's correspondence, and there it was, on cream-colored paper smelling faintly of some expensive Shanghai tobacco: a reminder. *The workshop. The schedule stands. Do not disappoint us again, Mr. Pell.* Again. As if he were a schoolboy who had once failed to hand in his sums.

He sat down. He ruled a column. He waited for the sound of the quay gate, and Miss Amberdale's quick steps, and the pause she would make at the loading step, and the small startled silence that would follow.

For eleven years, Edmund Pell had done exactly as he was told, and told himself it was survival, and called the tally even. But this morning, watching the gate through the window with his pen poised above figures he could not read, he understood that the account had never balanced. There was a column running down the left side of his life — a drowned interpreter's career, thirty years of sold women, a dead diver named Kume, a clever girl with forged papers doing sums for men who owned him — and against it, on the right, his signature, entered again and again, year after year, and the two sides had never once come level.

This morning, for the first time, he had entered something on the other side. A key, wrapped in brown paper, on the third board of a loading step. It was nothing. It was treason. It was perhaps four days' worth of delay and a single chance in ten that she would understand it, use it, and survive using it.

It was, he thought, watching her pass the gate with her ledgers hugged to her chest — the smallest entry in the largest ledger he had ever kept — and it would have to do.

Behind her, at the far end of the quay, the workshop chimney sent up its thin thread of smoke into a windless sky, and Pell picked up his pen, and began to wait.
