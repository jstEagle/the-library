## 27. Night of the Long Entries

The lamp in the copying room had been trimmed three times that night, and each time Rosalind Amberdale had done it herself, because she did not trust the flame to anyone else's hands. It was a small oil lamp with a chipped porcelain base, the kind sold for two sen to fishermen's wives, and it threw its light in a narrow gold circle across the table where the master ledger lay open — Meridian Trading Company, Inward and Outward Cargo, 1896 through 1899 — like a patient opened on a table of its own.

Two hundred and eleven pages remained when they began. The workshop around them smelled of tar, hemp rope, cold iron, and the ghost of thirty years of cargo: camphor, coal dust, dried fish, the sweet rot of hides. Beyond the plank walls, Nagasaki Harbor breathed. Water slapped against pilings. Somewhere a night watchman coughed. The Kaiyo-maru lay at her moorings two hundred yards out, her riding lamps doubled tonight as though the ship herself were keeping vigil over what they stole from her masters, page by page, figure by figure, until dawn.

Rosalind dipped her pen and wrote.

The characters came out of her steady and small, English figures in one column and Japanese numerals in another, because Kenji had insisted on both — *the consul reads English; the courts read Japanese; give them no excuse to say the proof belongs to only one world.* Her wrist ached from the elbow down. Her eyes burned in a way that had passed beyond pain into something flat and mineral, like looking at the sea too long. She had been writing since sundown. She would write until the ink ran out or the tide turned, whichever came first, and if the ink ran out first she would grind more.

She did not think about the burning smell that had come off the harbor once, faintly, around midnight, and been gone again before anyone could name it.

*Entry 1,842. Ceramic ware, 40 crates, gross weight 2,960 kan.*

Forty crates of ceramic ware that weighed like forty human beings, because they were not ceramic ware at all, and had never been, and every clerk who had ever signed his hanko beside those figures had known it or refused to know it, and now she was making them all know it forever.

Her hand did not shake. She had learned steadiness at seven years old, crouched on the ama divers' beach with her mother's tally-sticks in her fist, learning that a number written carelessly could mean a diver unpaid, a family unfed, a girl sold. Numbers were not paper things. Numbers were people wearing their thinnest clothes.

Behind her, Sato-baasan sat by the door.

* * *

Sato-baasan had not planned to work. She was seventy-one years old and her hands had not held a pen since the Meiji Restoration made pens fashionable among people who had never held their breath for two minutes under the cold green weight of Iojima channel. But there were things a pen could not do, and so she had appointed herself to them.

She kept the tea hot. She kept the door listened-to. She kept the tally-sticks sorted in their bamboo bundle, sixty-one of them, each notched by a diver's own hand — a notch for a day's diving, a deep notch for a shell taken, a crossed notch for wages owed — and whenever one of the young people faltered over an entry, some column that reconciled wrong, she would take up the matching stick and hold it against the lamplight and say:

"Count again. Kana dived nine days that month. Your ledger says six."

And Rosalind would count again, and find the three missing days folded into a payment marked *miscellaneous*, which meant stolen, and write *stolen* in the margin in her small fierce hand.

It was good work. It was the best work Sato-baasan had done since the year they took her granddaughter.

She thought about Chiyo as the night deepened, the way she always did when her hands were busy — thought about the girl's wrists, thin as the tally-sticks, thought about the morning three years ago when a company man in a clean grey kimono had come to her doorway and set down an envelope heavy with yen and said, *Your silence is worth more than your granddaughter's freedom, grandmother. Choose.* And she had chosen, God forgive her, she had chosen the envelope, because the man had shown her a photograph of a place in Shanghai with barred windows and told her that silence bought rice for the girl and noise bought nothing but her disappearance into it.

Three years of silence. Three years of eating with money that tasted of salt water and shame.

But the envelope was spent now. The silence was spent. And tonight the old woman sat by the door of a condemned workshop with a bamboo bundle of sixty-one sticks across her knees like a sword across a samurai's lap, and every hour that passed without boots on the planks outside was an hour Chiyo came closer to home.

She heard the footsteps first, a little after two o'clock. Two men. Unhurried. The unhurriedness was the frightening part — hurried men might be drunks; unhurried men at two in the morning near a warehouse slated for demolition were men on an errand.

She did not wake the others. She rose, joints cracking like ship's rigging, and stepped out into the dark, and stood in the middle of the narrow lane between the workshop wall and the stacked cordwood, an old woman in a patched indigo jacket, hair white as foam, arms at her sides.

The men stopped. Lantern light found her face.

"Sato-obasan," said the taller one, and she knew him — a harbor policeman seconded to Meridian's pay, she'd seen him take envelopes at the fish market — "you shouldn't be out. Cold night."

"It is," she agreed.

"There's nothing in that building worth guarding."

"No," she said. "There is something worth freeing. Go home."

For a long moment nobody moved. The harbor slapped its slow applause against the pilings. And Sato-baasan stood the way divers stand on a rocking deck, rooted below the waist, immovable above it, and looked at the two men with the same expression she had worn at seventeen when an overseer told her the day's catch was short and she knew to the shell that it was not.

Divers command respect on this coast. Everyone knows why. They go down alone into the dark where the rest of us cannot breathe, and they come back, mostly, and they do not lie about what they saw.

The men went home.

When she came back inside, Rosalind looked up, read her face in one glance, said nothing, and poured her the last of the tea. That, too, was right. Some things are reported; other things are simply poured.

* * *

Kenji Watanabe worked with his eyes closed half the time.

This unnerved Ishida until he understood it. The old interpreter was not reading the ledger pages — he was remembering them. Thirty years ago he had sat in a counting house very like this one, a young man with excellent English and a fatal honesty, transcribing cargo manifests for a syndicate that was then called something else and was called something else now but was, he understood better than any of them, the same body of men, grown older, richer, and more careful. He had noticed the pattern then. He had said so then. And the ruin of Kenji Watanabe had been swift, thorough, and instructive to everyone who watched it happen: dismissal, blacklisting, poverty, the slow erosion of a reputation until the man himself became a cautionary tale told to new clerks — *speak up and end up like Watanabe.*

So now he sat with his eyes half closed and his memory open, and when Rosalind copied a page he would recite, softly, the entries he had handled three decades ago, the antecedent shipments, the names of the ships that had carried the same trade under earlier flags, and Ishida wrote these down in a separate notebook headed, in careful characters, *Corroborating Testimony of K. Watanabe, former interpreter, Meridian predecessor firm (name), dates.*

Names. Dates. Figures. A confession thirty years deferred.

At half past two, Kenji opened his eyes fully and looked at the stack of finished copies — nearly a ream of them now, squared and tied with string in bundles of fifty — and something moved across his face that Ishida, watching, recognized as grief arriving late at its own address.

"I translated for them," Kenji said, to no one, to the ledger, to the drowned. "I put their lies into English so the foreign captains would sign. Beautiful English. My teachers at the mission school would have wept." His hand, liver-spotted and steady, rested on the topmost bundle. "A girl named O-Tsune went south on one of my beautifully translated manifests. Fifteen years old. I have wondered for thirty years whether she is alive, and I have never once had the courage to try to find out, because if I found out she was dead, I would have to stop wondering, and wondering is the last thing I still owe her."

"You'll testify," Ishida said. It was not quite a question.

"To the consul? To a court? To God?" Kenji almost smiled. "I will testify wherever there is paper to receive it. That is all I am good for, Ishida-san. I am a man made entirely of translation." He picked up his pen. "But I intend to be very good at it, one last time."

They worked. The lamp burned. Outside, the tide turned and began, imperceptibly, to run out toward the open sea, carrying the night away with it hour by hour, and somewhere beyond the harbor mouth, far to the south, the route of the Kaiyo-maru waited like a throat waiting to swallow.

* * *

Ishida Kazuo, census taker, third grade, had spent his professional life asking other people questions, and it struck him sometime after three in the morning, as his pen slowed and his vision doubled and healed and doubled again, that he had never once been asked one back.

He was a small precise man with a small precise betrayal of his own: the census bureau answered upward, and upward, and upward, and at the top of the answering lived men whose names appeared nowhere in any registry he kept, men who had instructed him — politely, in writing, with official seals — to note certain households and overlook others, to record certain ages loosely, and to report, monthly, on the movements of persons connected to the Meridian Trading Company. He had obeyed for eleven years. He told himself it was the ordinary obedience of a small official, the kind that greases every government ever built, and most nights he believed it.

Then he had found a discrepancy in a tenement registry — a father's name that belonged to no living man, a household assembled like a forged banknote — and followed it, and been offered instead the strangest bargain of his career: access to shipping manifests, in exchange for silence about a dead diver's daughter.

Blackmail dressed as kinship, he had called it, privately, in the early weeks. He was less sure now. Sitting at a splintery table at three in the morning, copying evidence of debt bondage beside a woman whose entire existence was itself a forgery committed in self-defense, he found the categories blurring. Who in this room was honest? He wasn't. She wasn't. The old man wasn't, by his own cheerful admission. Only the old diver, perhaps, and she had taken blood money once, and knew it.

Perhaps honesty was not a condition. Perhaps it was a direction. And perhaps everyone at this table had finally turned to face the same way.

His hand cramped. He flexed it, grimacing, and Rosalind — without looking up from her own page, without pausing in her own rhythm — slid the little jar of liniment down the table to him. Divers' liniment. Camphor and something green and sharp. It smelled like the beach at low tide, like his childhood summers in Saga, like everything before ledgers.

"Amberdale-san," he said. Then, because the hour made him brave and the liniment made his fingers feel like someone else's: "May I ask you a question? One question, as you once said to me. Not a question too many."

She glanced up. Lamplight caught the exhaustion in her face and somehow refined it, the way salt refines seawater into something you can taste the sky in.

"When this is finished — when the consul has the papers, when the ship is stopped, when it is all arithmetic and ash — what name will you write in the registry?"

The pen in her hand went still.

He watched her not answer. He watched her look at the ledger, at the columns of stolen lives rendered in neat figures, at the margin where she had written *stolen* forty-one times in her small fierce hand. He watched her look at the old man translating, at the old woman guarding, at the harbor invisible beyond the walls but present in every sound.

"I don't know yet," she said at last, and her voice was quiet and completely steady. "But I know it won't be the name on my forged papers. Whatever else happens, Ishida-san — whoever I turn out to be — I'm done being a copy of myself."

She bent back over the page. Ishida picked up the liniment jar and rubbed it into his cramped hand and thought: *there is the answer, then,* and understood that she had given it hours ago, in every entry, in every margin, and he had simply been the last to read it.

* * *

They counted the remaining pages at four o'clock, because Kenji insisted that a job without checkpoints was a prayer without an amen.

"Sixty-eight," Rosalind said, squaring the untouched leaves with cracked, ink-blackened fingers. "Sixty-eight pages. Dawn is at five-twelve. The tide turns at five-forty."

"Then we need forty pages an hour," Ishida calculated, and heard how impossible it sounded, and saw that none of the faces around the table changed, and understood that impossibility had left this room several days ago and had not been invited back.

"We need faster hands," Rosalind said, "and shorter margins. Kenji-sensei, dictate the corroborations directly to Ishida-san — stop writing them out yourself. Sato-baasan—" she hesitated, because the old woman had earned her rest twice over, but Sato-baasan was already holding out her hand.

"Give me the tally-sticks that match the next section. I'll sort them in order. When you reach a diver's name, you won't need to hunt for her stick." A pause, and then, with terrible gentleness: "My eyes are old, child, but my fingers remember sorting. I used to sort shells by lamplight while the boats came in. Sixty years ago. The fingers keep what the head loses."

So it went. The final hour of the night became a machine made of four imperfect people: Kenji dictating testimony in his beautiful ruined English and its equally beautiful Japanese shadow; Ishida transcribing both, his small precise hand flying; Sato-baasan laying out tally-sticks like an apothecary laying out doses, each notched sliver of bamboo finding its page, its name, its stolen wage; and Rosalind at the center, copying, copying, copying, the entries flowing out of her pen now almost faster than thought, because she had stopped reading them as figures and begun reading them as what they were — eleven living women, breathing somewhere on the southern route of the Kaiyo-maru, each one a column that reconciled perfectly the moment you wrote her name beside it.

Kana. O-Yoshi. Chiyo.

When she wrote *Chiyo* — the name appearing in the 1897 manifest, *one female diver, age 19, listed as household servant, destination Shanghai* — Sato-baasan's hands stopped moving. The old woman looked at the page for a long moment. She did not weep. She reached out and touched the wet ink once, very lightly, the way you touch the cheek of a sleeping child you have been forbidden to wake, and then she picked up the matching tally-stick and laid it beside the entry, notch to numeral, so that the girl's own hand, cut into bamboo three years before she was taken, lay witness beside the lie that had sold her.

"That one goes to the consul first," Sato-baasan said. "Before anything. Before my testimony, before yours. That page goes first."

"It will," Rosalind promised. "I swear it on my mother's name."

The sky beyond the high dusty windows began, very slowly, to change its mind about the dark.

* * *

At twenty minutes past five, Rosalind copied the final line.

*End of register. Certified in duplicate. Entries herein verified against tally-records of the Ama Divers' Association, testimony of K. Watanabe (interpreter, retired), and household records of the undersigned.*

Below it, four signatures. Herself. Kenji Watanabe. Ishida Kazuo. And — pressed deep into the paper, the strokes uneven, the pen held like a boat hook, the proudest mark Rosalind Amberdale had ever witnessed — Sato-baasan, who had signed her name for the first time in sixty years, having made Kenji teach her the strokes at midnight, having practiced them eleven times on scrap paper, because a woman who testifies must be able to write her own name, and she was done letting other people write for her.

They squared the copies. They tied them in oilcloth — three full bundles, wrapped against rain and worse. Rosalind divided them in her mind even as her hands bound them: one set to the British consulate, one hidden apart from the workshop in case fire or theft found the first, one to travel with Sato-baasan to the divers' association, where sixty women who could hold their breath for two minutes would hold this paper the way they held pearls — down deep, past reach, until it was safe to surface.

Outside, dawn came up the color of oyster shell. The harbor emerged gray and gentle, treacherously gentle, the Kaiyo-maru riding calm at her moorings with her cargo of silence stowed beneath her decks, sailing on the morning tide, four days from now.

Rosalind stood at the loading door with the oilcloth bundles in her arms and let herself feel it — just for one breath — the enormity of what four tired people had made between sundown and sunrise out of ink and bamboo and stubbornness.

Then she smelled it.

Smoke. Not hearth smoke, not the morning's first cooking fires drifting off Dejima — this was thick, chemical, deliberate, and rising from somewhere below the warehouse row, where the wind off the water should have been pushing it out to sea but instead was pushing it here, along the waterfront, toward the wooden walls of a condemned workshop filled to its eaves with dry rope and thirty years of paper.

Behind her, Sato-baasan was already moving. Kenji was already shouting. And down the lane, running hard, came a figure in a Meridian clerk's coat waving his hat overhead — Mr. Pell, of all the men in the world, Mr. Pell, screaming one word across the water and the morning:

"*Fire!*"
