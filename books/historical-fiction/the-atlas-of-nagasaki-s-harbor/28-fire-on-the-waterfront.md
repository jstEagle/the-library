## 28. Fire on the Waterfront

The night smelled wrong before anything else did.

Rosalind noticed it on the walk down from the tenement, Sato-baasan's lantern swinging ahead of her like a slow yellow heart. The harbor air should have been brine and tar and fish rot and the sweetish stink of the coal barges — the smell she had been breathing since before she could speak, the smell that meant *home* whether she willed it or not. But under all of it tonight lay something sharper, chemical, almost elegant: lamp oil. Not the cheap kind the fishermen burned in their skiffs. The good kind, the kind the Meridian Company imported by the drum for its machinery.

Someone had been generous with it.

"Smell that?" she said.

Sato-baasan did not break stride. The old diver's feet knew these stones better than her own teeth knew her gums, and she walked with the unhurried certainty of a woman who had spent forty years reading water. "I smell a man who is afraid," she said. "Fear smells like oil. It always has."

Rosalind's stomach tightened around nothing. They were three nights finished — three nights since the last page of the master ledger had been copied in Kenji's small, exact hand and verified against his thirty-year-old memory, three nights since she had sewn the pages into an oilcloth satchel and begun sleeping with it strapped beneath her clothes like a second, heavier heartbeat. Three nights since Pell's key had appeared where she would find it, and the workshop had stood open to them like a wound that refused to close.

The demolition was six days out. The contents were supposed to be disposed of in three. She had counted every hour twice.

She had not counted on men who could not wait even for their own schedule.

* * *

The workshop sat at the elbow of the waterfront where the old loading slip met the stone quay, a long low building of timber and corrugated iron that had been swallowing cargo and secrets for thirty years. From the outside it looked asleep. Its windows were dark. Its great sliding door stood shut.

But there were four men near it, and none of them were dockworkers.

Rosalind knew this because she knew every man who worked the night shift by his walk — the rolling gait of men who spent their lives on heaving decks. These four walked like clerks pretending to be laborers, shoulders hunched around their collars, moving with the self-consciousness of guilt. Two carried drums. One carried a coil of rope. The last one held a lantern low against his chest so its light spilled nowhere, and he kept glancing up and down the quay the way a man checks a mirror for someone following him.

"Go back," Sato-baasan murmured.

"The copies are inside—"

"No." The old woman's hand closed on Rosalind's wrist with a grip like wet rope. "The copies are *here*." She touched Rosalind's ribs, over the satchel. "You are wearing them. What is inside is only paper they have already read. Paper they have already memorized. Let them burn their own confession if they want to. That is what it is now — a confession, addressed to whoever finds the ashes."

"But Kenji's annotations—" Her voice cracked low and furious. "Thirty years of his memory written in the margins. If it burns—"

"Then it burns in him too. He will not forget it again because a building fell down." Sato-baasan's face in the lantern light was a landscape of wrinkles, and none of them moved. "Girl. You are the ledger now. Act like it."

Rosalind wanted to argue. Instead she did what Kenji had taught her to do when figures refused to reconcile: stop, breathe, and look again at what was actually in front of you rather than what you expected to be there.

What was actually in front of her: four men, two drums, one lantern. And behind the workshop, at the far end of the loading slip, a fifth figure standing very still beside the moored company lighter — a tall figure whose posture she would have known anywhere, the way one knows a familiar sum by its shape before reading its digits.

Pell.

He was not moving. He was not helping. He was simply standing witness, hands clasped behind his back like a man at a funeral, and when the lantern-carrier glanced toward him, Pell gave a single nod — the smallest possible motion, a nod that said *proceed* and also said *I hate this* in the same syllable.

"Come," Sato-baasan said. "Away from the light. There is a thing I must show you, and we have perhaps ten minutes."

"I'm not leaving until I know what's happening—"

"You are leaving until I know *you* are safe, which is the same thing done properly." And then, softer, with the terrible gentleness the old diver reserved for the drowned and the doomed: "Child. Your mother once hid from men like these in a crevice under Iojima's north point while they searched the water above her for three hours. She learned patience in the dark. So will you."

* * *

They crouched in the lee of a stack of empty kerosene cases forty yards off, among the smell of old wood shavings and cat, and Rosalind watched the workshop begin to die.

It was methodical. That was the worst of it — how methodical. The men did not smash windows or shout or behave like arsonists in any story she'd ever heard. They worked like stevedores, which of course they were. Oil went into the seams of the floor first, poured along the grain of the boards so it would travel. Oil went along the outer wall where the iron siding met the timber sill. One man climbed to the loading loft and disappeared; when he reappeared at the loft window, he tossed down the empty drum, and Rosalind understood that the master ledger's own storage room — the little iron-bound closet where the true book had slept through everything — had just received the most thorough soaking of all.

"They want no ambiguity," she whispered. "No salvageable corner. Nothing half-burned that a curious clerk might puzzle over."

"Burning a lie takes care," Sato-baasan agreed. "Telling one is easy. Destroying the evidence of one — that is skilled work. See? They respect the trade."

The fourth man had gone to the far end of the building, and now Rosalind saw what he was doing and felt the blood leave her face. He was unfastening the great sliding loading door from its track — not opening it, but *loosening* it, wedging something beneath the bottom rail so the whole ton of iron and timber hung balanced, ready to fall shut and jam. The loading door was the workshop's second mouth. If the front stayed barred and the loading door collapsed into its frame, anyone caught inside would have exactly nowhere to go.

Which meant they expected someone to be inside.

Or they were making certain no one ever could be.

"Pell," she breathed. "Pell left us the key. Pell told us — he *made sure* we'd come back tonight, he stalled the inventory, he—"

"And maybe he meant it as kindness," Sato-baasan said. "And maybe his masters found his kindness and turned it inside out. A trap needs bait, girl. A key is very good bait." The old woman's eyes reflected the distant lantern like a cat's. "Or maybe Pell does not know yet, and the men came early precisely so his kindness would kill us without his hand on it. There are many ways to read a column. Choose the one that keeps you alive to audit it later."

Above the quay, the sky had begun to bruise toward dawn — not light yet, but the promise of it, a thin gray seam over Iojima's black shoulder. The Kaiyo-maru sailed on the morning tide. Tomorrow. Everything they had — the consul, the detention order, the public reckoning — depended on the paper against her ribs surviving this night, and on the hours between now and tide.

A match flared by the workshop's front door. Small. Almost tender.

"No!" The word tore out of her before she could stop it.

Sato-baasan's arm clamped across her chest, iron-hard, and pulled her down flat behind the cases. "Quiet. *Quiet.*"

But the match had already kissed the oil trail, and the flame ran along the base of the wall the way water runs along a gutter — quick, bright, almost cheerful, a line of orange stitching itself across the dark. In seconds it reached the soaked sill, climbed it, and vanished inside, and for three full heartbeats nothing happened at all.

Then the workshop exhaled smoke through every gap in its siding at once, like a living thing taking its last breath, and began, quietly and thoroughly, to burn.

* * *

Rosalind did not remember deciding to move.

She remembered Sato-baasan's grip, remembered wrenching free of it, remembered the old woman's voice cracking after her — "*Rosalind!*" — using her name, her real name, the name only three people alive were permitted to know, which told Rosalind more about the depth of the danger than any amount of reasoning could have. Then there were the stones under her feet, and the heat arriving ahead of the flames like a hand pushed against her face, and the smoke pouring from the eaves in ropes.

The front door was barred from outside now — she saw the bar dropped into its brackets as she came around the corner, saw one of the men still standing beside it with his hand on the wood, listening. Listening for what? For screams? For movement?

For her.

She cut wide, keeping to the shadow of the coal shed, and made for the loading slip. The fire was louder here, eating upward through the loft with a sound like tearing cloth multiplied a hundredfold, and the heat came off the walls in waves she could feel through her clothes. Sparks swarmed upward into the gray pre-dawn like a reversed snowfall. Somewhere inside, glass burst — the little window of the counting room where she had spent three hundred mornings of her life bent over columns. The counting room where Mr. Pell had taught her double-entry, praising her neat hand. The counting room where she had first seen the weight discrepancy in the ceramic-ware shipments and felt dread bloom in her chest like ink in water.

All of it going. All of it going fast.

The loading door hung in its frame, wedged, trembling with the draft the fire was pulling through the building. The wedge beneath it was a length of scantling, driven in tight. Beyond the door's lower edge, where it didn't quite meet the cobbles, a ribbon of orange light pulsed — the fire inside, reflected on the slip stones, breathing in and out.

She put her shoulder to it and shoved.

The door ground up two inches and stopped. The scantling screamed against the rail. Heat rolled out through the gap and took the skin off her forearms, and through those two inches she could see the loading bay floor awash in flame, the whole interior transformed into a furnace throat, and the stair to the loft already a column of fire, and the iron-bound storage closet — the closet where the original master ledger had lived, where she and Ishida and Kenji had copied it page by patient page — glowing at its seams like a coal.

The original was in there. Thirty years of Kenji's memory, annotated. His redemption, bound in board and string, burning.

"*Leave it,*" she said aloud to herself, to the fire, to the dead. "Leave it, leave it, leave it—"

Because the copy was safe against her body. Because the copy was the whole point. Because Sato-baasan was right and she had always known Sato-baasan was right: paper they had already read was only a confession now, and confessions survive in the person who makes them.

But her arms pushed anyway.

Not for the ledger. For the shelf beside the closet — the low shelf where Kenji kept his tea tin, his spare spectacles, his worn copy of Johnson's dictionary with her childish pencil-marks still in the margins. For the peg where Sato-baasan's granddaughter's tally-sticks hung waiting to be returned to her. For every human trace of the found family this building had sheltered while the world called it worthless.

The door rose another inch, then another, the wedge splintering, and the heat drove her back a step, and in that step back her heel caught the coiled rope one of the men had left, and she went down hard on the cobbles, the breath knocked clean out of her.

That was when she heard the coughing.

From inside. High up. Thin, wet, human.

Kenji.

* * *

Later, she would reconstruct it the way she reconstructed any impossible entry: not by trusting memory but by trusting arithmetic. Fact: Kenji had returned to the workshop alone, past midnight, to retrieve the dictionary and his annotated cipher notes — notes he had sworn were redundant, notes he could not bear to lose, because a man who has been ruined once for telling the truth learns to hoard the instruments of truth-telling the way misers hoard coin. Fact: the syndicate's men had come early. Fact: the front bar went on while he was inside. Fact: he had not known. Fact: he was on the loft stair when the fire took it, and now he was above the flame line in the loading loft with the roof beginning to glow overhead, trapped behind a window too narrow for a grown man and too high for a jump onto stone.

Fact: Rosalind Amberdale got up off the cobblestones with her palms bleeding and her ribs screaming and put both hands under the loading door and lifted with a strength she did not know she owned, because grief is a kind of strength and terror is another kind and together they can lift a door that reason cannot.

The wedge snapped. The door shrieked up its track — a foot, eighteen inches, enough — and the heat hit her like a wave striking a seawall, driving her head sideways, scorching the hair at her temples. Through the gap the loading bay was a white-orange roar. She could see nothing of the stairs. She could see the loft floor above, buckling, and against the loft window a shape pressed — hands, a face, Kenji's voice coming down through the roar in pieces:

"—the door—the door, child—"

"I'm here! The loading door! Come DOWN!"

"I can't — the stair is — " A coughing fit swallowed the rest. Through the smoke she watched him turn from the window, watched his silhouette stagger along the loft edge, and understood with a clarity that felt like cold water that he was looking for another way down and there wasn't one, there had never been one, the building had only ever had two mouths and she was holding one of them open against a fire that wanted it closed.

The door trembled in her grip. Her arms were failing. The iron frame was hot enough to cook flesh and she could smell her own sleeves scorching.

"SATO-BAASAN!" she screamed, into the fire, into the gray morning, into whatever gods presided over harbors. "HELP ME HOLD THE DOOR!"

And then hands were on the door beside hers — brown, scarred, forty-years-in-the-water hands, and Sato-baasan was there, planted like a piling, taking half the weight without a word, and the door steadied, and the gap held.

"KENJI!" the old woman roared, in the voice divers use across open water, the voice that carries over wind and wave. "THE FLOOR BY THE SLIP HOOK! THE OLD CARGO RING! JUMP AND ROLL, MAN, OR BURN — CHOOSE!"

Silence except for the fire. Then, through the smoke hole where the loft floor had begun to cave, a shape appeared at the broken edge — coatless, spectacled, his white hair streaming, holding the absurd dictionary clutched to his chest like a child.

"Don't you dare bring that book," Rosalind sobbed.

His mouth moved. She couldn't hear the words over the roar, but she read his lips, because she had been reading lips across noisy counting houses since she was nine years old, and what his lips said was: *"Both of us, or neither."*

Then the loft floor went.

It didn't collapse so much as dissolve — a section of planking dropping away into the blaze below, and Kenji dropped with it, and Rosalind's heart stopped in her chest like a stopped clock, and then the old cargo ring caught him. The great iron mooring ring bolted into the bay floor thirty years ago for hauling heavy crates — he had landed across it, or near it, or God knew how, and he came crawling out of the orange hell along the floorboards with the ring's shadow beside him, coat smoking, one sleeve alight, and Sato-baasan let go of the door just long enough to seize his collar and drag him through the gap while Rosalind held it alone on the last of her strength, and then they were all three of them on the cobbles in the open air, rolling him, beating the fire out of his sleeve with bare hands, and the loading door crashed down behind them into its jammed frame with a sound like a coffin lid.

The dictionary lay on the stones between them, its cover charred, its spine split, one corner burned away entirely.

Kenji was breathing. Coughing, scorched, half-conscious, but breathing.

* * *

They got him behind the coal shed and out of sight as the roof came in.

There was no drama in it. No explosion. The workshop simply settled, the way a tired animal settles, and the roof folded down into the heart of the fire and sent up a column of sparks that turned the whole harbor orange — masts, hulls, the black water itself all lit like dusk at noon. On the quay, the four men were already walking away, unhurried, drums abandoned, their work complete. One of them paused to look at the burning building with the satisfaction of a man inspecting a delivered shipment.

Pell was gone from beside the lighter.

Rosalind knelt in the coal dust with Kenji's head in her lap and the oilcloth satchel pressed between her body and the ground, and she watched thirty years of evidence become ash, and she felt — she was ashamed of it even as she felt it — a wild, filthy relief. Because the copy was safe. Because the copy had never been in danger. Because the men who had done this believed, with absolute certainty, that they had destroyed the only proof in existence.

Which meant that tomorrow morning, when the Kaiyo-maru weighed anchor on the morning tide, they would do it lightly.

Which meant that everything now depended on getting to the consul before that anchor left the mud.

Sato-baasan crouched beside her, soot-striped, her silver hair loose and singed at the ends. She took Rosalind's bleeding hands in hers and examined them the way she might examine shellfish for edibility, then wrapped them in strips torn from her own hem without asking permission.

"Your interpreter will live," she said. "Divers' lungs are tougher, but clerks' hearts are stubborn. Between the two of them he'll manage." She looked up at the burning ruin, at the sparks falling into the sea and dying with tiny hisses, each one a page, a margin note, a memory. "We lost the house," she said. "We did not lose the books."

"No," Rosalind said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "We are the books."

Down the harbor, the first bell of dawn rang from the customhouse, thin and clear over the water. The tide was turning. The Kaiyo-maru rode at her moorings in the middle distance, black against the orange sky, riding high and empty-looking and full of eleven stolen lives, and somewhere aboard her or ashore beside her the syndicate's agent was sleeping soundly, believing his secrets had just gone up in smoke.

Rosalind rose, drew the satchel strap tighter across her chest, and turned toward the consulate hill.

"We have until the tide," she said. "Run."
