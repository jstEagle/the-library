## 29. Ashes and Arithmetic

The smell reached Kenji Watanabe before the sight did — a smell he had hoped never to know again in this life, though his life had taught him better than to hope for anything. Char and brine and something sweeter underneath, the ghost of pitch and tar that had sealed the workshop's old beams, all of it lifted on the morning wind off the harbor and carried inland through Oura like a rumor no one wanted to hear spoken aloud.

He walked slowly. He was sixty-eight years old, and he had learned long ago that an old man who walks slowly is permitted to see everything, while a man who hurries sees only what he is running toward or from.

The workshop stood at the edge of the tide flats where it had stood for forty years, and it did not stand anymore. The fire had taken the roof first, by the look of it — the char fell inward, collapsing toward its own heart, which meant the heat had come from above or from all sides at once, set with intent. The walls of grey timber were black now, and half of them were down, and what remained upright looked like the ribs of some enormous fish that the sea had given up. Smoke still threaded from three places in the ruin, thin and patient, the way smoke threads from a doused fire that has not agreed to be dead.

Kenji stopped at the rope line — someone had already strung a rope between two pilings, and a harbor policeman stood at one end of it looking bored and important in equal measure — and he looked at the ruin the way a physician looks at a body on a table.

*Set,* he thought. *Deliberate. They poured oil.* He could see the dark stain in the ash near the eastern doorway, too broad and too even to be accident. He had seen fires in this city, accidental ones, kitchen fires and lamp fires and once a whole block gone because a cooper's shed had caught in a dry August wind. Accidental fire was chaotic. This fire had been tidy.

"Old gentleman." The policeman shifted. "You can't come closer."

"I am not coming closer," Kenji said mildly. "I am standing here, remembering."

The policeman looked at him a moment longer, decided that remembering was not yet against any law he knew, and turned away.

Kenji remained where he was for a long time. Behind him, the harbor went about its business — sampans slapping their way out to the roads, the gulls arguing over whatever the trawlers had discarded, the great hulls of the foreign steamers sitting at their moorings like fat patient animals. Somewhere among those hulls, riding at anchor with her holds full of sleeping human cargo, lay the *Kaiyo-maru*. He did not look at her. He would need all his strength for what was in front of him, and looking at the ship now, with nothing yet done, would only be a way of spending fear for no return.

He took off his hat — the good felt hat Rosalind had pressed and reblocked for him two winters ago — and held it against his chest, briefly, the way one does at a grave. Then he put it back on and went around the rope line at its far end, where it sagged loose between a piling and a stack of salvaged planking, and let himself into the ashes.

* * *

The floor was the sea itself here, or nearly — old boards over mud and gravel, and the fire had opened gaps in them so that he stepped from plank to plank like a man crossing a stream, testing each footing before trusting it. Ash rose around his shoes in small grey ghosts. Here and there the fire had done strange mercies: a shelf bracket still hung on the wall holding nothing; an iron kettle sat unharmed amid a circle of destruction as though the flame had gone around it on purpose; a child's sandal — how had a child's sandal come to be here? — rested whole on a beam.

He found the strongroom corner within ten minutes, because he had known where it must be. Thirty years ago this workshop had belonged to a different company under a different name, but warehouses and workshops along this waterfront kept their bones across the changes of ownership, and Kenji had spent enough of his youth inside such bones to know them. The iron chest had been built into the foundation, sunk below floor level behind a false panel — he had helped move such a chest once, as a young interpreter, and his back remembered the weight of it still. The panel was gone, burned or pried, and the cavity gaped open, empty, scorched black inside.

So they had burned the original ledger. Of course they had. Paper burns beautifully; that is the whole tragedy of paper, and the whole hope of it too.

He knelt at the edge of the cavity and lowered his hand into it. The metal was cold now, cold as the mud beneath it, and his fingertips found only grit and a few flakes of char that came away like moth wings. Nothing. Whatever pages had been hidden there were smoke on the wind over Iojima by now, indistinguishable from every other lie the harbor had ever exhaled.

Except.

Except that paper burns, yes. But arithmetic does not. Arithmetic lives somewhere else entirely — in ink copied onto other paper by cramped and stubborn hands, night after night, in a room that smelled of lamp oil and cold tea; and beyond that, in a place no fire has ever reached, in the memory of an old man who was ruined thirty years ago for saying aloud what the columns said.

Kenji sat back on his heels in the ashes, quite deliberately, the way he had once sat seiza through hours of humiliating interrogation, and he closed his eyes, and he began to count.

* * *

It had begun, all those years ago, with a number so small it seemed innocent: eleven.

Eleven bales of ceramic ware shipped on the *Chōsei-maru*, autumn of 1869, manifested at four hundred and twenty kan each. He had been twenty-eight, quick with languages, trusted with documents, flattered to be trusted. Four hundred and twenty kan was heavy for ceramics but not impossible — he had checked, because checking was his nature and his curse — against the freight rates and the known weights of Seto ware in crates of that dimension, and the numbers would not sit down together. They circled each other like dogs. Four hundred and twenty kan of porcelain does not require that much ballast adjustment. Four hundred and twenty kan of porcelain does not require six men to load each crate, and these had required six men, he had seen them, sweating, and the crate had made a sound when it shifted that no crate of cups ever makes — a soft, dense, terrible sound, the sound of a body turning over.

He had written a memorandum. That was the whole of his crime, if crime it was: he wrote a memorandum, respectful, precise, footnoted, and gave it to his superior, who thanked him kindly and destroyed it and reported him instead. Dismissed for "irregularities in conduct." Blacklisted from every foreign firm on the waterfront within the month. A young man with a wife and no children then, and later a wife buried and no children still, and then, at last, a diver's orphan brought to his door in the rain, and God's ledger balanced in the only currency that mattered.

But the numbers themselves — those he had never been permitted to forget. Ruin had burned them into him deeper than any teacher ever had. For thirty years he had carried the pattern of that fraud in his head the way a man carries a stone in his shoe: painfully, constantly, and finally inseparably from the act of walking.

Now, kneeling in the ashes of the Meridian workshop, he opened that old wound on purpose and read it.

The pattern had a grammar. Fraud always does; that is what he had tried to tell them, thirty years ago, and what no one wished to hear — that dishonesty cannot help leaving fingerprints, because a lie must be shaped to fit around a truth, and the shaping shows. The grammar ran thus: goods that weigh wrong. Shipments entered twice under two names, once as cargo and once as stores. Wages paid on paper to men whose names appear nowhere on any crew list — wages drawn in cash against the signature of one agent, always the same hand, always rounded to convenient figures. And debts: entries marked *advances*, *advances*, *advances*, year upon year, to divers whose advances never diminish, because an advance that never shrinks is not an advance at all. It is a price. It is what a human being was bought for, written in the language of bookkeeping so that no one has to say the word *sold*.

Rosalind had found the same grammar independently, in her first months at the counting house, before Ishida ever came to her door. When she had shown him her notes — shyly, the way she showed him anything, watchful for his disapproval as though he were still the schoolmaster and she still the child at his table — he had felt the floor tilt under him. Not surprise. Recognition. She had inherited not only his figures and his English but his terrible habit of noticing, and now the noticing had brought her to this: crouching in a burned building while the men who lit the match slept soundly in houses paid for by the difference between what things weigh and what the ledgers say they weigh.

He got up. His knees protested, and he informed them, silently, that they would protest again before this was finished and would do so without effect.

There was one more thing to find in the ashes, and he found it where she said it would be: the loading dock on the seaward side, the big door with the counterweighted pulley, where the master ledger had lived its last weeks wedged behind the loose board above the third joist. The joist was charred but whole. The board was gone — taken, or burned loose, or carried off by some scavenger — and the space behind it held nothing but ash and a spider already reclaiming the territory, spinning her geometry across the black wood with perfect indifference to the crimes committed around her.

Kenji looked at that empty hiding place for a long moment.

*They think they have burned the evidence,* he thought. *Let them think it. Let them sleep well tonight, all of them, Pell in his respectable lodgings with his respectable debts, the agent with his smooth face, the merchant-principal with his temple donations and his photograph in the newspaper. Sleep is sweetest on the last night.*

Then he turned and picked his way back across the ruined floor, out through the gap where the eastern wall had been, past the rope line, and up the hill toward the tenement where the true ledger — the one that mattered, the one written out line by line in a girl's careful, beautiful, stubborn hand — waited under the floorboards beside the tea chest, wrapped in oilcloth, guarded also by an old woman who sat outside the door of nights and needed no weapon older than herself to turn men away.

It was time to do the work he alone could do. It was time to check the arithmetic against thirty years of memory, figure by figure, line by line, and find out whether the copy was complete — whether, when the hour came, they would stand before the consul with a whole testimony or a wounded one.

* * *

The room was small and low-ceilinged and smelled of yesterday's lamp oil and today's rice, and by the time Kenji arrived, it had already arranged itself into the shape it had held for nine nights: the low table dragged to the center, the oilcloth bundle unwrapped, the copy stacked in its sections with stones on top against the draft, Rosalind seated cross-legged with her ink-stained fingers laced tight in her lap as though restraining them from starting before he arrived, and Sato-baasan by the door with her pipe, unlit, watching the street through the gap in the shutter.

Ishida was there too, folded into the corner with his census books around him like a fortress he had built and then realized was a prison, his spectacles pushed up into his hair. He looked as though he had not slept. Good, Kenji thought, without malice. None of them should sleep well until this was done.

"You saw it," Rosalind said. Not a question. Her voice was steady, but he knew her steadiness the way he knew his own heartbeat, and he knew what it cost her this morning. Her hands were white where they gripped each other.

"I saw it," Kenji said. He removed his hat and his coat, folded the coat precisely — habit, order, the small ceremonies that keep a man from flying apart — and sat down at the table. "The fire was set with oil, from at least two points. The strongroom was emptied first. They took the original out and burned it separately, or burned it in place after taking the copy they feared most. Either way, they are satisfied. That is our advantage: satisfied men are careless men, and careless men sail on schedule."

"And the ledger?" Ishida asked quietly. "Behind the joist?"

"Gone, of course. But we never intended to retrieve it again. We intended to replace it." Kenji drew the copy toward him and untied the cord with fingers that were, he noticed with distant irritation, not entirely steady. "Now we learn whether we have replaced it completely. Rosalind. Ink. You will write corrections in red, in the margin, and you will not argue with me when I make them, and afterward, if you believe me wrong, you will argue with me all you like. Agreed?"

A flicker crossed her face — the ghost of the girl who used to push back against his dictation exercises, insisting that *though* and *through* were conspiracies designed to torment learners — and vanished into seriousness. "Agreed."

Sato-baasan shifted by the door. "I will listen," she said. "Numbers I don't know. Names I know. If a name is missing that should be there, my ears will say so."

"That," said Kenji, "is worth more than you know."

He began.

* * *

The work took the day and most of the evening, and it was the strangest labor of his life, stranger even than the translation of treaties, because he was translating from memory against memory — his own thirty-year-old recollection of the fraud's grammar laid against Rosalind's fresh transcription of the fraud's present tense, hunting for the places where the two failed to rhyme.

Some failures were harmless. The company had changed its codes since 1869 — the cipher Kenji had taught her two weeks ago, hunched over this same table while she copied the key characters again and again until her hand obeyed without consulting her mind, was a descendant of the old one, grown more ornate but keeping the family features. Where the code differed, the structure did not, and he corrected her margins accordingly: *this character group means "advance," not "wages"; this numeral position is the ship, not the date; here, where your copy reads a blank, there was always a blank, and the blank itself is the entry — the absence of a crew list where a crew list must exist is the loudest line in the whole book.*

Some failures were not harmless, and those he hunted with growing coldness in his chest.

"There," he said, deep in the fourth section, tapping a line with his pipe stem. "The *Kaiyo-maru*'s spring manifest. Eleven names entered as deckhands, hired at Moji. Read me the hiring dates."

Rosalind read them. Ten dates clustered within a single week in March.

"And the eleventh?"

She read it. February. Nearly a month earlier.

"Now the wage rate for each."

She read those too, and her breath caught audibly, because she saw it a half-second before he said it: ten identical rates, and one rate higher by exactly the amount of a redemption fee.

"A buy-back," Ishida said from his corner, very softly. "Someone redeemed a contract early."

"No," said Kenji. "Someone's contract was *recorded* as redeemed, so that the syndicate's books would show a debt settled and a free person signed aboard voluntarily — while the actual girl stayed exactly where she was. Which girl? Sato-baasan."

The old woman's pipe had stopped moving. When she spoke, her voice was gravel over deep water. "Say the entry again. The date in February."

Kenji read it. She closed her eyes. "That is the week they told me my granddaughter's debt was forgiven. That is why I kept silent. They showed me the paper." Her eyes opened, and there was a light in them that Kenji had seen perhaps five times in his long life and hoped never to see directed at him. "They showed me a lie and charged me my silence for the privilege of believing it."

"It is in the copy now," Kenji said. "In your granddaughter's true status, in the true wage, in the true date. Red ink, Rosalind. The margin will testify even if we cannot."

She wrote. Her brush moved steadily. Only the line of her jaw betrayed her.

* * *

By midnight the sections lay around the table like the pieces of a dismantled engine, and Kenji sat in the middle of them with his eyes half-closed, running the final verification, the one he had saved for last because it required the whole of him.

He went back to 1869. He took up the memorandum he had written at twenty-eight — the one that had cost him everything, the one that existed now nowhere in the world except inside his skull — and he compared its pattern, line by imagined line, against the pattern on the table before him. Weights against weights. Double-entries against double-entries. The phantom wages, the eternal advances, the ships' names changing and the grammar identical, thirty years apart, like a father and son with the same face.

And then he counted the sections of the copy against the sections of the original as he had reconstructed them across nine nights of comparison, and the tally came out whole.

Every shipment. Every phantom name. Every advance that never shrank. Every coded line, every cipher key, every date, every sum — copied, corrected, cross-checked, complete. Eleven living divers accounted for by name, including a girl recorded as freed who was not. The principal's name appearing where Rosalind's mother's relic said it must appear, in the agent's column, in the settlement lines, in the quiet round sums that had purchased silence at every level from the quay to the counting house.

"The copy is complete," Kenji said.

His voice came out lower than he intended, roughened, and he paused and began again. "The copy is complete and correct. There is nothing in the burned original that is not on this table. Whatever they destroyed tonight, they destroyed nothing. Do you understand me? They have burned a warehouse full of ash. The evidence is in ink, in triplicate intention, and in four heads in this room, and one of those heads has been keeping their accounts for them for thirty years without knowing it."

No one spoke for a moment. Sato-baasan knocked the dead ash from her pipe against the doorframe, a small dry sound, like a period set at the end of a sentence.

Then Rosalind put both hands flat on the completed copy, all of it, the whole weight of it, and Kenji watched her — his student, his daughter in every way that mattered and several ways that mattered more — and saw that she was not celebrating. She was listening. Her head was tilted slightly, the way she tilted it when a sum refused to reconcile, and she was listening to something beyond the room.

"What is it?" he asked.

"Boats," she said. "Oars, muffled. More than one." Her eyes went to the shutter's gap. "Coming up the creek toward the tenements. And Sato-baasan—" she turned, fast now, all business, "—how long has that boy been standing at the end of the lane? The neighbor's son. Is he there now?"

The old woman rose without a word and put her eye to the gap.

Outside, the muffled oars dipped and pulled, closer, closer, and at the far end of the lane a slim silhouette detached itself from the shadow of the wall and began, unhurried, to walk away — walking, Kenji noted with a clarity that felt like ice water, in the direction of the harbor, in the direction of the counting house, in the direction of the men who believed they had burned the last of the evidence tonight, and who were about to learn, at whatever cost, how wrong the arithmetic of arson always turns out to be.
