## 30. The Ship That Must Not Sail

The news came with the smell of smoke still in her hair.

Rosalind stood at the edge of the burned workshop's footprint, her shoes grey with ash, the completed ledger copy wrapped in oilcloth and strapped against her ribs like a second heartbeat. Across the water, moored at the deep-water berth where the Meridian Company's flag hung limp in the windless morning, the *Kaiyo-maru* sat low in the water. Too low. A ship carrying ceramic ware rode high and light. A ship carrying forty tons of ballast and eleven human beings who had not chosen to be cargo — that ship drew water like a drowned woman.

She had known, in the abstract way one knows arithmetic, that the divers were aboard. Chapter after chapter of figures had told her so: provisions entered for passengers who appeared on no manifest, fresh water drawn at three times the ration for a crew of that size, a surgeon's fee paid in advance to a man whose practice was in Shanghai. But arithmetic was a cold country. Standing here, on the scorched timbers of the workshop where she had spent half her nights, looking at the blackened skeleton of the loading door through which she had run with the proof clutched to her chest while the roof came down behind her — standing here, the abstract became a sound. She could almost hear them. Eleven voices below the waterline, in the hold, breathing air that belonged to someone who had purchased it.

"They sail on the morning tide." Ishida stood beside her, his census book — his real book now, the one he kept for himself — open against his forearm though he wrote nothing in it. His voice was flat in the way she had learned meant fear held under discipline. "The harbormaster posted the notice an hour ago. Tide turns at six-forty."

"Then we have—" She did the calculation without wanting to. Her mind did that; Kenji had trained it to, all those years of sums done by lamplight, numbers as a place to hide. "Nineteen hours."

"Nineteen hours," Ishida agreed. "Minus whatever time it takes a British consul to be woken, dressed, convinced, and courageous." He closed the book. "I have known that man's clerk for six years, Amberdale-san. Courage is not the item I would list first among his qualities."

Below them, at the water's edge, Sato-baasan was doing something Rosalind did not at first understand. The old diver had waded in to her knees despite the season, her sleeves bound back, her arms bare and brown and corded with seventy years of muscle, and she was singing. Low, wordless, the diving song the ama women used to steady their breath before going under — the song Rosalind had heard fragments of all her life without knowing until last month what it was for. Sato-baasan sang it toward the *Kaiyo-maru*, across the grey water, as if sound could travel where bodies could not, as if her granddaughter might hear it through steel plating and know: *you are not forgotten, you are counted, your name is written down.*

Rosalind unwrapped the oilcloth just enough to touch the pages. Ink and salt. Ash had gotten into everything; the top sheet bore a small brown scorch at its corner, a bite taken out of the paper by the fire that had eaten the original. The copy was complete. Kenji had verified every figure against his memory of thirty years ago, line by line, until dawn, and when he finished he had laid his hands flat on the pages and said nothing at all, which from Kenji was the loudest declaration Rosalind had ever heard him make.

The proof existed. It was whole. And it was worth precisely nothing unless it reached the consul before six-forty tomorrow morning.

"We go tonight," she said. "Not at dawn. Tonight, while they're asleep. If we wait for office hours, we'll be waiting for a signature on a detention order that arrives after the anchor's up."

"The consul will not thank you for waking him."

"The consul will not have to look at eleven divers in a hold in Shanghai for the rest of his life," Rosalind said. "I can live with his ingratitude."

* * *

They made their preparations in the tenement, in the room where the copying had happened, where the lamp still sat with its chimney smoked black and the floor still bore the ghost-rings of teacups set down by cramped hands at three in the morning. Kenji sat by the window with his bad leg propped on a stool, mending — of all things — a fishing net, because his hands could not be idle any more than hers could, and because, he said, a net was honest work that caught only fish.

"You'll want the pearl," he said, without looking up.

"I have the pearl."

"You'll want it *visible*. Consuls are like merchants, Rosalind-chan. They believe what they can hold." He tied off a knot. "The note as well. The merchant's name written in your mother's hand. You showed me once — show them. Let the old man see ink that has been dry for twenty years naming a man who dines at the consulate's table twice a month. That is the moment his face changes. Everything before that moment is clerks being polite. That moment is the case."

"And if it isn't?" Rosalind asked. "If he reads it all and says this is a matter for the Japanese authorities, and the Japanese authorities are Mr. Goto's friends, and by the time anyone agrees whose jurisdiction it is, the tide has turned?"

Kenji set down the net. He looked at her for a long time, and in the lamplight she saw again the young man he must have been, thirty years ago, the interpreter who had seen this same pattern in these same ledgers and spoken of it and been ruined for speaking — dismissed, blacklisted, reduced to teaching a half-caste orphan her letters in a rented room above a noodle shop. She saw the man who had ordered her to burn the first copied page because he knew exactly what the company did to people who noticed things. She saw him choose, again, the road that had already cost him everything once.

"Then you do not leave the consulate," he said quietly. "You sit down in his hallway and you do not leave. You tell him you will read the names aloud in the street outside until someone listens, and that there are dockworkers and divers and one very persistent census official prepared to stand with you, and that a crowd of foreigners on the Bund asking questions about a British-flagged charter is the last thing a consul wants. Make yourself more trouble awake than asleep. It is the one negotiation the Empire of Great Britain always loses."

Ishida, in the corner, made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled by propriety.

"He speaks from experience," Rosalind said.

"I speak from observation," Kenji corrected. "I was never brave enough for the hallway. That is the entire tragedy of my life, and I am entrusting its correction to a bookkeeper's assistant and a blackmailing census taker, which tells you what I think of my own judgment." He picked up the net again. "Go. Both of you. Sleep if you can. You cannot argue a man out of his bed at midnight on no sleep; you will sound like a madwoman, and madwomen are escorted out by marines."

But sleep would not come. Rosalind lay on her futon listening to the harbor — the slap of water against pilings, the far creak of the *Kaiyo-maru*'s rigging, a sound she had never noticed before and would now never stop hearing — and she thought about Pell.

She had not seen him since the fire. In the chaos of that morning, in the running and the shouting and the bucket lines, he had simply been gone, and no one at the counting house would say where, and the company's men had looked at her with a new expression she could not read. But last night, coming home through the lanes, she had found tucked into the door of the tenement a folded paper with no name on it. Inside, in Pell's small, precise hand — the hand that had drilled her in double-entry until she dreamed in columns — was a single line:

*The agent boards at five. The divers are loaded at four, before light, so the town does not see. There is a side ladder at the stern. I am sorry for all of it except this.*

No signature. No signature needed. She knew his handwriting better than her own.

She turned the paper over and over in the dark. *I am sorry for all of it except this.* What a sentence to live inside. Pell, who had raised her pay and taught her the trade and perhaps genuinely liked her; Pell, whose debts to the syndicate had made him a prisoner in his own counting house; Pell, who had stalled the demolition order and left a key where she would find it and falsified an inventory with hands that must have shaken — Pell had finally chosen, and the thing he had chosen was a side ladder and a warning about tides. It was not absolution. She understood that. Nothing he could do would un-sell the divers who had already died north, or un-burn the workshop, or give Sato-baasan back the years of silence her granddaughter's safety had bought. But it was something. It was a man in chains moving one link, and the link was worth having.

At four in the morning, then. The divers loaded before light. Which meant that every hour between now and four was an hour in which human beings were being marched down a quay in the dark and put into a hold.

She got up and began dressing.

* * *

The quay at three o'clock was a different country from the quay at noon. Fog lay on the water in long grey ropes, and the gas lamps along the waterfront made soft orange islands that the fog kept trying to swallow. Rosalind moved through them with her collar up and the oilcloth bundle under her coat, and beside her, breathing carefully through his nose, walked Ishida, who had insisted on coming as far as the consulate gate and whom she had not had the heart to refuse.

"You should sleep," she said.

"I have spent fifteen years knocking on doors at improper hours," Ishida said. "It is the only part of my profession I have ever loved. Allow me this."

The consulate building rose out of the fog ahead, a solid foreign mass of stone and shuttered windows, the Union Jack hanging motionless over the door like a sleeping thing. One lamp burned in the ground-floor window — the night porter's post. Rosalind stopped in the shadow of the wall opposite and made herself look at the building the way she looked at a ledger: not as a thing but as a system. Doors, dependencies, flows. The porter controlled the door. The door controlled access to stairs. Stairs led to the consul's rooms, and somewhere above, behind glass and empire and a hundred years of certainty, slept the one man in Nagasaki who could stop a ship with a signature.

"You knock," Ishida murmured. "A young Englishwoman in distress at three in the morning opens more doors than a Japanese census official. It is unjust. Use it."

"It is unjust," she agreed, "and I intend to spend it like coin."

She crossed the street. Her knuckles had barely touched the door when it opened — faster than she expected, as if the porter had been awake and waiting for exactly this, or as if the whole stone building had been holding its breath along with her.

The porter was an old Lascar with a face like weathered rope. He looked at her, at the fog behind her, at the bundle under her coat, and something in his expression shifted from suspicion to recognition — not of her, she realized, but of the situation. Men who keep night doors learn to know the people who come through them at three in the morning. They are rarely selling anything.

"Miss," he said. "Early for the passport office."

"I'm not here about a passport." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "I need to wake Consul Whitcombe. Tonight. Now. It concerns a ship called the *Kaiyo-maru*, sailing on the morning tide, and eleven people in her hold who are there against their will."

The porter did not move for a long moment. Somewhere across the harbor, faint through the fog, came the sound of a bell buoy, and beneath it — or perhaps she imagined it, or perhaps Sato-baasan was still down on some dark stretch of shore singing to the water — the ghost of a diving song.

"Wait there," the porter said, and shut the door.

And Rosalind Amberdale stood alone in the fog on the steps of the British consulate with nineteen hours become three, with a burned ledger against her ribs and her mother's pearl in her pocket and a note in a dead diver's hand naming a respectable man, and listened to footsteps climb stairs overhead, slow, unhurried, each one landing like a verdict not yet rendered.

Above her, a window lit. A shadow crossed the glass. And a voice she had heard only twice before, at the passport counter, bored and precise, called down through the opening sash:

"Whoever you are — this had better be worth my career."

"It's worth eleven lives," Rosalind called up. "I've brought the accounts."

There was a silence long enough to hold a whole tide. Then, above her, the window opened wider.
