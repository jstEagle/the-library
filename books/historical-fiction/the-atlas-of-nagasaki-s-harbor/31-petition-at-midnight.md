## 31. Petition at Midnight

The window stayed open. The voice did not come again.

Rosalind stood in the consulate garden with the rain beginning again — a fine, needling drizzle off the harbor that beaded on the oilcloth wrapping her bundle — and counted the seconds as she had once counted the length of her mother's dives. Thirty. Forty. Fifty. Beside her, Ishida held his lantern low so its light would not spill past the wall, and his breath came in small white ghosts that betrayed how fast his heart was going, whatever his face pretended.

"Mr. Hoskins keeps late hours," Ishida murmured. "Or he sleeps badly. Both are useful to us tonight."

"Both are useful," she agreed, "only if he listens."

The side door of the residence opened — not the grand entrance facing the street with its plaque and its Union Jack, but the clerk's door, the servants' door, the door through which a man might admit trouble without admitting it formally. A candle appeared first, then the face above it: Mr. Hoskins, the consul's second clerk, wrapped in a dressing gown the color of wet sand, his hair flattened on one side from his pillow. He looked, Rosalind thought, exactly like what he was — a man whose authority was borrowed and who knew precisely how far the borrowing extended.

"Miss Amberdale." He pronounced it carefully, as he had at the counter, as though the name were an item he had entered in a register and must now reconcile. "You understand what hour this is."

"I understand what tide this is, sir." She lifted the bundle slightly. "The Kaiyo-maru sails at dawn. After dawn, nothing I carry will matter."

Hoskins glanced past them both, down the lane, up at the dark bulk of the consulate behind him, then stepped back from the doorway in a way that was not quite an invitation but could not reasonably be called anything else. "The conservatory," he said. "Not the house. If my chief wakes and asks, you are petitioners regarding a passport irregularity, which is at least adjacent to the truth."

"Everything tonight is adjacent to the truth," Ishida said quietly. "That is rather the point of it."

* * *

Ishida had sat in many rooms where powerful men decided the fates of people they would never learn the names of. He had learned, over nineteen years in the census bureau, to read those rooms the way a pilot reads water — where the channel ran deep, where the sandbars lay hidden, where a hull would tear open without ever striking anything visible. The British consul's conservatory, lit by two candles because Hoskins dared light no more, read shallow and treacherous, full of things just under the surface.

Hoskins himself was not the danger. Hoskins was a sandbar — obstructive, but honest in his obstruction. He wanted the matter to be someone else's. He wanted, more than anything, a reason *not* to act, because acting would require courage and courage was not listed among a second clerk's duties.

The danger was the name Rosalind had found in her mother's note. The respected merchant. The man whose donations adorned the hospital wing and whose signature appeared on the harbor improvement committee. The man who, if Ishida's arithmetic of influence was correct, dined with the consul at least monthly.

Which meant the room itself might already belong to their enemy, the way a ledger belongs to whoever holds the pen.

"We should begin with the tally-sticks," Rosalind said. She had laid everything out on the long rattan table between the potted ferns: the oilcloth unwrapped, the copied pages squared into a stack three fingers thick, Sato-baasan's bundle of notched bamboo beside it, and last — placed deliberately, though her hand trembled doing it — the pearl, resting on a square of faded silk, and beneath the pearl the note in Kume's careful diving-hand.

Hoskins looked at the pearl first. Of course he did. Men looked at pearls.

"That is not evidence," he said. "That is jewelry."

"It is a signature," Rosalind said. "Read the note."

He picked up the paper with two fingers, the way one handles something possibly contaminated. His lips moved as he read. Ishida watched the lips stop moving halfway down, and watched the man's eyes go back to the top and read it again, slower.

Kume's note named no crime. It named only men — dates, initials, a sum in Mexican dollars, and the name of the syndicate's principal written out plainly, because a diver dying slowly of the cold had no reason left for discretion. Ishida had read it four times since Rosalind had shown it to him at the tenement, and each time he felt the same cold settle in his chest: thirty years ago he had seen figures like these, and had spoken of them, and had been ruined for speaking. Now here was the same current, running under thirty years of harbor silt, and a girl who refused to be ruined quietly.

"This alleges," Hoskins said at last, setting the note down as if it were hot, "that Mr. —" and he could not quite make himself say the name aloud, finishing instead with "— that a gentleman of this city is principal in the sale of indentured labor to a Shanghai concern."

"Not alleges," Rosalind said. "Documents. Sir, may I show you the ledgers?"

"You may show me whatever you like. It changes nothing about what I am empowered to do at two o'clock in the morning without my chief's authorization."

"Then wake your chief," Ishida said.

Hoskins turned on him with the first flash of genuine temper Ishida had seen from him. "Do you imagine, Mr. Census Taker, that Consul Graham can simply be woken and handed a stack of copybook pages? That he will issue a detention order against a ship flying under contract with Her Majesty's consular registration on the strength of — of *bamboo sticks*?"

"On the strength of these." Rosalind opened the copied ledger at a page she had marked with a thread. "Entry forty-one, dated the ninth of last month. Eleven names, entered as crew. Wages column: zero. Rations column: zero. But see the provisioning entry for the same date, same ship — eleven additional rations drawn against 'ceramic ware.' And here—" she turned pages quickly, surely; she had lived inside these columns for weeks and knew their geography better than Pell himself— "here is the insurance schedule. Eleven souls insured at forty dollars each, payable at Shanghai, policy holder a trading house whose director signs with the same chop that appears on every Meridian manifest since 1894."

She laid the tally-sticks beside the page, one by one, aligning each notch with its line of entry.

"These are the divers' own tallies. Cut by their own hands, dive by dive, share by share. Old Sato cut hers across forty years; she taught her granddaughter the same marks. This one—" Rosalind touched a shorter stick, newer wood— "is her granddaughter's. Tsuru. Seventeen years old. Her stick matches entry forty-one, third name down. The company recorded her as ceramic ware, Mr. Hoskins. I am asking you to record her as a person."

The conservatory was very quiet. Somewhere beyond the glass, the harbor breathed — hawsers creaking, water slapping stone, and beneath it all, faint as a pulse, the sound of the Kaiyo-maru's engine-room taking on steam in the pre-dawn dark.

Hoskins looked at the sticks for a long moment. Then he did the thing that Ishida had been waiting for, the thing that would tell him whether the night was lost or won.

He picked up a tally-stick and compared it, himself, notch by notch, against the ledger line. He did it without being asked. And when he finished, he set the stick down very gently, the way a man sets down something that has become heavier than it was.

"The consul," he said, "will want to verify these copies against originals."

"The originals burned," Rosalind said. "Two nights ago, in the workshop fire. Along with the workshop. Which is why there is only this." She put her hand flat on the stack of pages. "Every figure verified against the memory of Kenji Watanabe, who kept Meridian's accounts in translation for six years before you were posted here, and who was dismissed for noticing this same pattern in 1869. His attestation is bound in at the front. In English, Japanese, and the old trade cipher, so that no one can claim the translation altered the meaning."

"Watanabe." Hoskins said the name carefully. "The interpreter who — yes. There are still men in this building who remember that affair." He rubbed his jaw. "They remember it as *his* scandal."

"They remember it wrong," Ishida said. "I have spent nineteen years learning how records get remembered wrong, Mr. Hoskins. It is the whole of my profession. The census does not count people; it counts what powerful men agree people should be. And I am telling you, as a servant of the Japanese government who is about to commit a career-ending irregularity by being here at all — the paper in front of you is true. I have checked it against shipping manifests I had lawful access to. I have walked the ama beach at Iojima and counted the houses with no divers in them. Eleven names. Eleven living women, aboard that ship, whose contracts were purchased with money that never passed through any wage column anywhere."

Hoskins closed his eyes briefly. When he opened them, he looked older, and Ishida understood that the man had just finished the arithmetic he himself had finished days ago: that there was no version of the morning in which he remained uninvolved. There was only the question of which side of the ledger his own name would be entered on.

"If I wake the consul," Hoskins said slowly, "and he refuses to act, you understand that everything in this room becomes evidence *against* you. Against all of us. Forged registries, Miss Amberdale — yes, I know about your papers, I process passports, I notice things too — conspiracy, theft of commercial documents, the defamation of a respected subject of the Emperor by a state official. We would hang together, figuratively speaking. Possibly literally, in Mr. Ishida's case."

"I have considered the arithmetic," Ishida said.

"And you, Miss Amberdale? You have the most to lose. Your entire existence is a forgery. One word from me and you have no legal self at all."

Rosalind met his eyes. She thought of her rule — busy hands, closed mouth — the rule that had kept her alive since she was nine years old, and understood that she was about to break it permanently, in front of a witness, in ink that could not be erased.

"My mother dove for pearls until the sea took her," she said. "My father signed an indenture and died owing money to men who sold his debt onward like a bale of goods. I have spent my whole life being an entry in other people's columns. Tonight I am choosing what column my name goes in. Wake the consul."

* * *

Consul Graham came down in a proper coat over his nightshirt, which told Rosalind more than any speech could: he had dressed for battle, even if the battle was only paperwork. He was sixty or near it, gray and heavy-jawed, with the bloodshot patience of a man who had spent thirty years in treaty ports mediating between gunboats and grain prices. He did not look at the documents first. He looked at the people.

"Census man," he said to Ishida. "Clerk's assistant." To Rosalind. "And you brought my second clerk out of bed, which means either desperation or mutiny. Sit. Both of you. Hoskins — tea, if the stove's still warm, and don't hover."

What followed, Rosalind would remember all her life as less like a hearing than like a tide turning — slow, immense, indifferent to anyone's wishes, moving because the moon of evidence had finally pulled hard enough.

Graham read the attestation first. Then the cipher key, and he made Kenji's verification walk him through two sample entries aloud, translating from the old trade notation while the old man's voice, steady as a surveyor's, laid thirty years of memory alongside thirty hours of copying and let the consul check the seams. Then the manifests Ishida had lawfully drawn. Then the tally-sticks, and at the granddaughter's stick the consul stopped and held it a long time under the lamp.

Then, last of all, the pearl and the note.

He read Kume's note twice. He looked at the pearl for longer.

"Your mother wrote this?"

"She was dying of the cold when she wrote it. Divers' lungs, after twenty years. She knew what was being done with the indenture contracts because the wives of the men who did it bought her pearls at half price and never asked where they came from."

Graham set the pearl down on the silk with unexpected care. He steepled his hands. For a full minute nobody spoke, and in that minute Rosalind heard the harbor sounds sharpen — the steam winches waking along the Bund, the first gulls, and underneath, the Kaiyo-maru's engines turning over from banked fires to full pressure.

"The problem," Graham said at last, "is not whether I believe you. The problem is jurisdiction dressed up as doubt. That vessel carries consular registration papers. Her cargo manifest declares general merchandise. If I board her on rumor, I hand Shanghai a grievance and Tokyo an insult, and I hand *him*—" and this time he did say the merchant's name, saying it the way a doctor says the name of a disease— "a lever to have me transferred by Thursday. If I do nothing, eleven women are landed in Shanghai and enter ledgers no one will ever audit."

"Then don't board her on rumor," Rosalind said. Her heart was slamming but her voice, she noticed distantly, had gone calm — the counting-house calm, Pell's drilled cadence, used now for its opposite purpose. "Board her on paper. Article Six of the Anglo-Japanese convention on indentured traffic — you have the circular in your own files, sir, 1895, the one nobody implements. Detention pending verification of crew contracts. Not an accusation. A *clerk's* action. You are not accusing a respected merchant of anything. You are merely observing that eleven persons embarked as crew hold no wage agreements, which is an administrative irregularity requiring the ship be held until the contracts are produced."

Silence.

Ishida watched it land. He watched the consul look at Rosalind — really look, perhaps for the first time, past the forged papers and the borrowed respectability to the mind underneath, the bookkeeper's mind that had found the one door in the fortress that opened without force.

"Hoskins," Graham said. "Pull the 1895 circular."

Hoskins pulled it. It existed. It had existed, unimplemented, for four years, gathering dust exactly as such circulars gather dust — until a girl with ink-stained fingers had gone looking for it the way her mother had gone looking for pearls, knowing the valuable things hide in the places no one bothers to search.

Graham read it standing. Then he took a sheet of consulate paper, dipped his pen, and wrote — in his own hand, no clerk's, because he understood as well as they did that authorship was everything tonight — the order:

*Detention order. SS Kaiyo-maru, and all persons embarked thereon, held pending production and verification of crew articles and contracts of engagement. No departure on the morning tide or any subsequent tide until released by this office.*

He signed it. He blotted it. He looked up, and his bloodshot eyes had gone sharp as an audit.

"There will be a boarding party at first light. Marines from — well. From wherever I can raise marines by then, which will be a smaller number than either of us would like. And there will be resistance, gentlemen and madam, because men do not surrender forty-dollar heads without argument. Whatever happens on that quay this morning, it will happen publicly, in daylight, with witnesses. That is your protection and mine. Sunlight and paper." He slid the order across the table toward Ishida, then paused, hand still upon it. "One question, Miss Amberdale. The registry discrepancy that started all this — your father's name, your household. When this is over, that forgery will surface. You understand that."

"Yes, sir."

"What will you do?"

Rosalind looked at the pearl on its silk, at the tally-sticks, at the three-finger-thick proof that ordinary labor, patiently done, could bend the world an inch toward decency.

"I'm having my registry corrected," she said. "Under my mother's name."

Something moved at the corner of the consul's mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile might someday be possible. He pushed the detention order the rest of the way across the table.

"Then go and deliver it," he said. "The tide won't wait, and neither, I think, will you."

* * *

Outside, the drizzle had stopped. The eastern sky over the harbor was the color of unpolished silver, and the Kaiyo-maru rode her moorings off the Bund with smoke rising fat and black from her funnel, impatient, certain, ready.

Ishida carried the order in its oilcloth inside his coat, against his chest, and felt it with every step like a second heartbeat. At the head of the quay, small in the graying light, a knot of figures waited: Kenji leaning on his stick, Sato-baasan upright as a mooring post despite her seventy years, and behind them, gathering out of the tenement lanes and the ama beach paths, others — dockworkers with capstan bars, divers with knives in their sashes, faces Rosalind knew from the counting house and the fish market and a lifetime of pretending not to know them.

Sato-baasan saw the oilcloth shape under Ishida's coat and understood at once. She did not cheer. Old divers did not cheer before a storm; they sang. Her voice rose thin and cracked over the waking water — the launching song, the one Rosalind's mother had sung — and one by one the others took it up, until the quay itself seemed to hum with it.

Down the harbor, the Kaiyo-maru began to weigh anchor.
