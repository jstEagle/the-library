## 33. Eleven Names Read Aloud

The tide turned at half past five, and with it the whole harbor seemed to exhale.

Rosalind stood on the quay below the customs house, close enough to the water that the morning swell breathed its salt against her ankles through the boards. Behind her, the crowd had gathered in the grey light — dockworkers still smelling of last night's rain, ama divers in their working whites with their hair bound back, fishwives with baskets set down and forgotten, a scattering of clerks who ought to have been at their desks and were not. Word had moved through Nagasaki the way fire moves through dry grass: *the Kaiyo-maru is held. The consul has boarded her. They are bringing them ashore.*

She had not slept. None of them had. Her hands still carried the ghost of the night — ink in the creases of her knuckles that no scrubbing would fully lift, a shallow cut on her left thumb where the final page had slipped against Kenji's penknife. The completed copy lay wrapped in oilcloth inside Ishida's document case, guarded by two marines who had been instructed, in writing, to treat it as they would treat gold bullion. It was done. It was over. And yet her body refused to believe it; her heart went on beating in the old rhythm of vigilance, quick and low, as though any moment Mr. Pell or one of the syndicate's soft-voiced men would step out of the crowd and take it all back.

But no one took anything back. Instead, at ten minutes past six, the ship's boats began to come down.

* * *

They came one after another from the dark flank of the Kaiyo-maru, pulling hard against the last of the ebb, each boat crowded with figures that did not move like sailors. That was the first thing Rosalind noticed, and it went through her like a blade finding an old wound: the way they sat. Sailors sat loose, sprawled, easy in a small boat's motion. These figures sat upright and close together, shoulders touching, hands folded, the particular stillness of people who have learned that drawing attention is dangerous. People who have spent weeks being cargo.

The first boat grounded on the shingle ramp below the customs steps, and a marine helped them out one by one onto the wet stones.

A murmur ran along the quay. Somewhere behind Rosalind a woman began to weep without any sound at all, her whole body shaking, her hand pressed flat over her mouth.

Ishida stood at the foot of the customs house steps with a paper in his hands — the manifest of detained persons, written out in his careful census-taker's hand, corrected three times before dawn. He caught Rosalind's eye across the crowd and gave her the smallest nod. His face was grey with exhaustion and something else, something almost like awe, as though he had spent his life counting households and had only this morning understood what a household was.

He raised his voice. It was not a loud voice; it was a clerk's voice, precise and unhurried, built for rooms rather than harbors. But the crowd quieted for it anyway, the way water quiets around a stone.

"They will be brought ashore and released," he said. "By order of Her Britannic Majesty's consul, acting under treaty authority, the detention of these persons is ended. Their names will be read." A pause. "Their names will be read," he said again, as though the repetition itself were part of the order, part of the remedy. Rosalind understood. Names had been the currency of the crime — names entered as crew who never sailed, names struck from registries, names reduced to weights and measures in columns marked *ceramic ware*. So now the names would be read aloud, slowly, into the open air, where nothing could be done with them but heard.

"Otake Rin," Ishida read. "Of the Iojima diving families."

An old woman pushed forward from the crowd before the girl herself had even cleared the boat — a diver, Rosalind saw, broad-shouldered and sun-blackened, her forearms scarred white with old salt sores. She did not run. Divers do not run; running wastes breath, and breath is money, and breath is life. She walked down the ramp with terrible steadiness and took the girl's face in both hands and looked at her for a long moment, checking, the way you check a pearl against the light. Then she folded her into her arms, and the sound she made was not crying. It was lower than crying, older — the sound Rosalind imagined the sea itself would make if the sea could grieve and be glad in the same breath.

"Umezawa Chiyo. Of the Takashima beaches."

* * *

They came ashore one by one, and Ishida read them home.

Rosalind stood very still and counted with him, because counting was what she knew how to do, because numbers had been her weapon and her shelter for so long that she could not set them down even now. Eleven. There should be eleven living divers named on the Kaiyo-maru's route; she had matched the tally-sticks to the ledger lines herself, night after night, Sato-baasan's knotted sticks laid beside the copied pages until every figure had grown a face. Eleven names. Eleven women and girls sold north under debts that had been manufactured, inflated, inherited — debts owed to men who had never lent them anything but fear.

Some of them walked steadily. One girl of perhaps fifteen came up the ramp leaning on a marine's arm, her legs unsteady after weeks below decks, and when her feet found the stones of the quay she stopped and pressed her bare sole flat against them, deliberately, as though taking an oath. Rosalind felt tears start and did not fight them. There was no one here to hide from. Half of Nagasaki's waterfront was watching, and every face among them wore the same expression: the fierce, unprotected openness of people witnessing something they had not dared to hope for.

"Hamaguchi Tsune. Of Ohseto."

That one made the crowd stir. An elderly man near the fish stalls dropped his basket entirely — mackerel rolling across the wet boards, no one caring — and shouted a name that must have been hers in a childhood voice, and a woman in her forties turned toward the sound and stood frozen for the space of three heartbeats before she crossed the quay and let herself be gathered in.

Rosalind watched, and held her count, and waited.

Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten.

And then there was a gap. The second-to-last boat touched the shingle and its passengers came up the ramp, and Ishida looked down at his paper, and Rosalind saw his hand tremble — just once, just slightly, the hand of a man who had written ten thousand names into ledgers and never once understood until now what it cost to read one out.

"Nozaki Yuki," he said. "Granddaughter of Sato-baasan. Of the Iojima diving families."

* * *

Sato-baasan had taken her place at the front of the crowd an hour before dawn, and no one had thought to help her there and no one had dared to move her. She stood wrapped in her good indigo kimono, the one with the faded pattern of waves at the hem, her white hair bound simply, her tally-sticks bundled in a cloth under one arm as though she might be called upon to give evidence at any moment. Which, Rosalind supposed, she might. Which, today, she already had — her testimony lay in the consul's hands alongside the copy, alongside the pearl, alongside the note in Kume's hand naming the syndicate's principal.

When the name was read, the old woman did not cry out. She bowed her head once, briefly, the way a diver bows to the sea before going down — acknowledgment, permission, contract. Then she walked forward through the crowd, which parted for her the way water parts for a keel, and stood at the foot of the ramp with her hands folded, waiting, exactly as she had stood guard outside the copying room through those last impossible nights, turning away company men with nothing but her presence and the respect divers command.

Yuki came up the ramp slowly.

She was nineteen. Rosalind knew this from the ledger — birth year entered in the column the syndicate had used to calculate her price, a number that had made Kenji put down his pen and walk out into the garden and stand there in the dark for a long time. Nineteen years old, granddaughter of the oldest diver on the Iojima beaches, sold against a debt of forty-seven yen that had begun, three years ago, as a loan of nine.

She was thin. Her wrists showed the marks of irons, healing. Her hair had been cut short by someone in a hurry, unevenly, and her working clothes had been replaced by a deckhand's cast-off jacket far too large for her, sleeves rolled and rolled again. But she walked up that ramp the way her grandmother walked down to the water: upright, unhurried, spending nothing she did not choose to spend.

Halfway up, she saw the old woman waiting.

What happened then happened quickly and completely, the way a wave happens. Yuki made a sound — not a word, something before words — and broke from the marine's guiding hand and came down the last yards of the ramp at a stumbling run, and Sato-baasan opened her arms, and the girl went into them hard enough that the old woman rocked back on her heels and had to plant her feet, and for a long moment the two of them stood locked together at the edge of the harbor while the sea breathed against the stones and the crowd made no sound at all.

Then Sato-baasan drew back, held the girl at arm's length, and did what the first mother had done: took her face in both weathered hands and looked at her, checking her against the light, counting her — Rosalind could see it, the old woman's lips moving faintly, *whole, whole, whole* — before folding her back in.

Rosalind discovered that she had both hands pressed over her own mouth.

* * *

The eleventh name was read. The eleventh woman came ashore. And then it was done, and the arithmetic closed, and Rosalind stood on the quay with her eleven accounted for and felt something in her chest come apart and reassemble in a new shape.

Kenji found her there. He had appeared at her side without her noticing, as he always had since she was six years old — the disgraced interpreter, the man who taught her English and figures and caution, whose caution had nearly cost them everything and whose courage, in the end, had come back to him like a tide returning. He said nothing. He simply stood beside her, and after a moment his hand came to rest on her shoulder, brief and heavy, and lifted away.

"You counted them," he said quietly. It was not a question.

"All eleven," she said. Her voice came out rougher than she expected. "Every line matched. No one left aboard."

"No," Kenji agreed. "No one left aboard." He looked out at the Kaiyo-maru, riding sullen at anchor under the consul's flag, her departure cancelled, her holds sealed, her agent sitting below in the customs house between two marines while the papers that would end him were stacked in a room above his head. "Thirty years ago I stood on a quay very like this one and watched a ship sail, and I told myself the numbers were wrong, and I let them sail anyway because the numbers were all I had and numbers can be argued with." His jaw tightened. "Today nobody will argue. Today the numbers have faces, and the faces are standing on the stones."

"That was your doing too," Rosalind said. "The cipher. The authentication. Every entry you verified—"

"It was your doing," Kenji said. "I only remembered. You are the one who wrote it all down." He paused, and when he spoke again his voice had gone softer, and older. "Your mother dove for pearls, little one. She brought up beautiful things from the dark. You have her trade, do you know that? You went down into the dark and you brought up the truth and you did not drown." He looked away, out at the water, and did not finish, and did not need to.

* * *

The morning opened around them. The sun came up over the Mizunoura hills and laid a long gold road across the harbor, and the crowd on the quay thickened as word spread — more dockworkers coming off the night shift, more divers, children hoisted onto shoulders to see. Someone began bringing rice balls and tea from the stalls, pressing them on the freed women, on the marines, on anyone; the whole waterfront seemed determined to feed the morning whether it was hungry or not.

The freed divers clustered together near the customs steps, uncertain, blinking in the daylight like creatures brought up from depth — and Rosalind knew something of what that adjustment cost, having made her own ascent from deep water in another sense. Some had families waiting. Some had no one; their villages had believed them dead, or worse, had known and kept silent, bought quiet the way Sato-baasan's silence had been bought. Those ones stood at the edge of the group looking out at the crowd with the particular loneliness of the returned.

So Rosalind did the only thing she knew how to do with a discrepancy. She closed it.

She walked over to the cluster, and she bowed — properly, deeply, the bow of kin to kin — and she said, in the island dialect her mother had sung to her before she could remember singing: "My mother was Kume, of the Iojima beaches. She dove until the sea took her. If any of you have nowhere to go tonight, you will come to our house, and there will be rice, and there will be room, and no one will ask you a single question about the ship."

There was a silence. Then the eldest of the freed women, Hamaguchi Tsune, the one reunited with her father amid the scattered mackerel, looked at Rosalind for a long moment — at her foreign eyes, her mixed face, the forged-and-unforged truth of her standing there in plain sight of the entire harbor — and something passed over her features that was not surprise at all.

"We knew Kume's daughter lived," she said simply. "The beach always knows. We wondered when you would come down to us." She took Rosalind's hand in both of hers, diver's grip, firm and salt-rough. "You came down. That is enough."

Behind her, Sato-baasan, one arm still around her granddaughter, caught Rosalind's eye and nodded once — the same small bow she gave the sea. *Acknowledgment. Permission. Contract.*

Rosalind stood among them then, truly among them, and let the morning happen to her. The sun climbed. The tea went round. Yuki, wrapped in her grandmother's arms, laughed at something — actually laughed, a cracked and startled sound, as though testing whether the thing still worked — and the sound went through the crowd like a spark through dry grass, and other people laughed too, wet-eyed and disbelieving, and the harbor filled up with the noise of the living.

It was, Rosalind thought, the strangest arithmetic she had ever done: that grief, divided among enough people, could come out the other side as something like joy. That eleven names read aloud could balance a ledger that had been running red for thirty years. Not fully. Never fully — the drowned stayed drowned, her mother stayed beneath the water off Iojima, and no reading of names would call her up. But the account was no longer hidden. The debt was named. And the creditors, this morning, were the ones being paid.

* * *

It was nearly eight o'clock when the customs house doors opened again, and the crowd hushed a second time.

Two marines came down the steps first. Then, between them, walking with his hands cuffed before him and his head oddly high, came the syndicate's agent — a neat, silver-haired man whom Rosalind had seen twice at a distance, once at the company's annual dinner, once stepping from a rickshaw outside the workshop. He looked neither right nor left. He looked like a man rehearsing dignity for a room he had not yet entered.

And behind the marines, unbound, unbefriended, carrying his coat over his arm like a schoolboy sent home early, came Mr. Pell.

The crowd stirred and darkened. Rosalind saw fists begin to close along the quay, saw Hamaguchi Tsune step forward with her jaw set, saw the mood of the morning tilt toward something uglier. Whatever mercy history might eventually extend to Thomas Pell, the people on this quay had lost husbands to the debt-boats, daughters to the syndicate, years to the lie — and here was the company's own counting house walking free among them.

Pell stopped at the bottom of the steps. He looked out at the crowd — at the divers, at the dockworkers, at Rosalind — and whatever he saw in their faces made him go pale. Then he straightened, and to her lasting astonishment, he bowed. Deeply. To the crowd, to the freed women, to the stones themselves. He held it a long time.

"I have given my statement," he said, in Japanese, his accent clumsy but audible. "I have named every man above me. I will answer for what I did, in whatever court will have me." A pause, and then, quieter, almost lost in the harbor wind: "I only ask that the record show the pages were copied true."

Rosalind met his eyes across the distance. She thought of the key left where she would find it. The falsified inventory. The stalled fires. The promotion and the raise and the terrible, ambiguous kindness of a chained man trying to be decent inside his chains. She did not forgive him. She was not sure forgiveness was hers to give. But she bowed back — a small bow, a bookkeeper's bow, acknowledging an entry received — and she saw him take a breath as though he had been underwater for years and had finally, finally surfaced.

The marines led him away toward the consul's launch. The crowd parted and closed behind him like water.

And Rosalind turned back to her family — to Kenji and Ishida and Sato-baasan and Yuki and the eleven and the hundreds — and stood in the full sun of the morning tide, an orphan of two countries, a keeper of honest books, home.
