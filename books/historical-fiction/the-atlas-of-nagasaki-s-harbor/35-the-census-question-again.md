## 35. The Census Question Again

The morning after Pell's testimony was read aloud in the courtroom, Nagasaki smelled of rain and charcoal, and Rosalind Amberdale woke in her own narrow bed above the tenement with the strange sensation of having no one to hide from.

She lay still for a long while beneath the quilted cotton, listening. The harbor sounds came through the paper window as they always did — the groan of hawsers, a fishwife's voice cracking across the mudflats, somewhere far off the bell of the foreign ships' chapel — but underneath them there was a new silence, an absence she could not at first name. Then she understood. For eleven years she had woken each morning into a small arithmetic of concealment: which papers were where, which lies belonged to which neighbor, which version of herself had been seen by whom. That ledger, at least, was closed. Someone else's men had carried it off in iron boxes, page by damning page, and now it sat in a government office where she would never have to touch it again.

She rose, dressed in her plainest kimono — the grey one, the one Sato-baasan said made her look like a widow of modest means — and went down to the kitchen, where Kenji Watanabe was already seated before the brazier with his tea going cold beside him because he had fallen asleep sitting up again. He did that more often now. Thirty years of guilt had left him the week before, he told her, like a tooth finally pulled; what remained was only an old man's ordinary tiredness, and he wore it the way other men wore a medal.

"You're awake early," he said, without opening his eyes.

"I couldn't sleep."

"Good." He opened them then, and looked at her with the expression she had known since childhood — the one that meant he had been thinking about something difficult on her behalf and had not yet decided whether to say it. "There will be a visitor today."

"There are always visitors. Half the prefecture wants to stare at the woman who burned down Meridian."

"The other half wants to shake her hand," Kenji said dryly. "No. This one is official. A man from the ward office. About your registry."

Rosalind felt the old cold move through her chest before she could stop it — the cold that had lived there since she was seventeen years old and had first inked a false father's name onto borrowed paper. Eleven years of forged entries, of a household register that described a girl who had never existed except as a convenience. Even now, even with the syndicate in irons and Pell awaiting sentencing, the forgery itself remained hers alone. No trial had absolved it. No consul's order had erased it. It sat in the ward office like a splinter working its way toward her heart.

"They know," she said quietly.

"They have known for some time, I think. Since the beginning, perhaps." Kenji turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "Ishida-san sent word through the clerk at the consulate. He asks that you come to the ward office this afternoon. Or—" a thin smile, "—he asks nothing at all. He says only that the corrected registers are being drawn up, and that certain households require new entries, and that entry number forty-one in the Minamiyamate rolls is among them. He leaves the meaning to you."

*Entry forty-one.* Her own line, the line she had copied out falsely with her own steady hand while her heart hammered like a diver's pulse in her ears. She knew every character of it the way she knew her mother's face — imperfectly, from memory, and with a grief attached.

"And what am I to do?" she asked. "Walk into the ward office and confess?"

"You are to walk in," Kenji said, "and see what a man does when he has spent a year being cruel to you for reasons he could not control. I believe such things happen. I have heard rumors."

* * *

She went at two o'clock, when the rain had thinned to a drifting mist and the stone lanes above the harbor shone like the backs of fish. The ward office occupied a former merchant house near the Suwa shrine steps, all dark beams and sliding doors, and inside it smelled of ink, tatami, and the particular staleness of paper that has been handled by too many anxious hands. A junior clerk took her umbrella without meeting her eyes. Word of who she was — or of what she had done, which was not quite the same thing — had clearly traveled even here.

Ishida received her in a back room where a single lamp burned against the grey afternoon. He looked smaller than she remembered, and older. During the months of their uneasy alliance he had been a figure of menace and then of necessity and then, almost, of trust; now, seated behind a low desk stacked with registers bound in blue cloth, he seemed merely a tired civil servant with ink stains halfway up his right cuff. But his eyes, when they lifted to hers, held the same careful gravity she had first seen in her doorway over a year ago, when he had asked about her father's name — one question too many — and written something down.

"Amberdale-san." He rose, bowed — lower than protocol required, she noted — and gestured to the cushion opposite. "Thank you for coming."

"You could have sent for me officially," she said, settling herself. "You've had the power to do that since we met."

"Yes." He did not flinch from it. "I could have. I chose not to, then, because I needed something from you and had no right to ask it cleanly. I find I have developed a distaste for that method." He drew a document folder from beneath the stack and laid it on the desk between them but did not open it. His fingers rested on its cover as if it might bolt. "Before we begin, there is something I must say, and I ask you to let me finish it, because I have rehearsed it badly and may not manage it twice."

Rosalind waited. Outside, the mist ticked softly against the shutters.

"When I came to your tenement last spring," Ishida said, "I was not acting for the census bureau. You learned that eventually, and you hated me for it, and you were right to. What you may not know is how completely I was owned. My superiors answered to men who answered to the syndicate's agent. Every discrepancy I found was reported upward within the day. Every family I questioned whose answers displeased those men suffered for it — quietly, bureaucratically, with delays and refusals and sudden inspections — so quietly that most never knew why their lives had become harder. When I discovered your forgery, I reported it. I want you to hear that plainly. I reported it, and the report was buried, because a half-foreign bookkeeper was useful to them precisely because she could be destroyed at any moment. Your secret was not your weakness, Amberdale-san. It was their asset. And I helped them hold it."

The room was very quiet. Rosalind found her hands were folded in her lap with the stillness she had learned at a counting-house desk: busy hands, closed mouth. She unfolded them deliberately.

"I know," she said.

He blinked. "You—"

"Sato-baasan told me. Months ago, after the storm." She watched him absorb that — the old diver's reach, the tenement's quiet web of knowledge that no bureau had ever managed to map. "She said a census man cried once, in an alley off the ama beach, after taking down a household's names. She didn't know why. She remembered your face anyway." Rosalind tilted her head. "People here remember faces, Mr. Ishida. It's the one register you can't forge."

Something moved through his features — shame, and under it a flicker of the thing that had driven him through a year of double-dealing: not ambition, she saw now, but a terrible scrupulousness, a man who kept accounts of his own soul and found the sums wanting.

"Then let me balance mine," he said. He opened the folder.

Inside lay a household register, freshly ruled, its columns crisp with new ink. He turned it toward her and set a pen beside it — not the brush a clerk would use, but a steel-nibbed pen, Western-style, the kind used for documents of consequence. Beside the pen he placed a small dish of blotting sand, and then, with a formality that made her breath catch, he folded his hands and recited:

"Entry forty-one, Minamiyamate ward. Current entry reads: daughter of Tanaka Gihei, laborer, deceased; occupation, clerk's assistant. All particulars entered in error and struck through by order of the prefectural registrar, following review." He paused. "The correction requires a new entry. Name of head of household. Name. Parentage, if claimed. Occupation. And—" here his voice slowed, became almost gentle, "—the name under which the person wishes to be known to the Empire of Japan, to the British Consulate, and to whatever gods keep the third register, which no bureau administers."

Rosalind stared at the blank line.

She had imagined this moment, in the way one imagines drowning — vividly, unwillingly, in the dark hours. She had assumed that when it came, it would come as punishment: summoned, exposed, compelled to stand while a clerk wrote *half-caste* in the margin and her life contracted around her. She had prepared speeches of defiance. She had also, though she'd have denied it, prepared speeches of begging. What she had never imagined was this: a blank line, a pen, and a question asked as a courtesy rather than a trap.

"What name do you wish entered?" Ishida said again. "Take whatever time you need. The ink will not dry before tomorrow."

* * *

She did take time. She stood, without quite deciding to, and walked to the window, and slid it open a hand's width onto the wet garden and the sound of the harbor below. And because she was her mother's daughter and her father's both, she thought in columns.

*Amberdale.* Her father's name — Thomas Amberdale, indentured clerk of Liverpool, who had signed five years of his life away to a trading company and died owing them three. She had never met him. She knew him from a single letter, water-stained, that Kenji had kept hidden for twenty years and given her only last month, as if handing over a relic he feared she'd find wanting. The handwriting was careful, a clerk's handwriting, proud of its straight lines. *My wife Kume is the bravest person I have known,* it said, in part, *and if our child inherits her courage and my stubbornness with figures, I pity whoever tries to cheat her.* Rosalind had read it four times and then put it away, because grief that arrives twenty years late arrives all at once, and she had ledgers to copy that night.

*Kume.* Her mother — Kume of Iojima, ama diver, who could hold her breath through two minutes of cold green silence and come up laughing. Who had died in a season of bad weather and worse contracts, drowned off her own island while diving for a company that docked her pay for the pearls she failed to bring up. Whose name Rosalind had spent eleven years erasing, syllable by syllable, because a mixed-blood orphan with a diver's name got no positions, no rooms, no credit, no safety. Because the world had taught her that Kume was a weight that sank and Amberdale was a flag that might, if held high enough and straight enough, pass in the wind.

For eleven years she had believed she must choose. That identity was a door with one handle, and opening it onto either parent meant closing it on the other. Sign *Kume* and be marked forever — watched, discounted, barred from the counting houses, subject to every sneer the word *ama* carried in the mouths of merchants. Sign *Amberdale* alone and be clean, English-adjacent, employable — and a liar until her death, carrying her mother like contraband, visiting her own history only in disguise.

She had chosen the lie at seventeen because she had been alone and frightened and it had kept her alive. She would not call that choice shameful. But she saw now, watching the mist drift over the harbor where the divers' songs were rising with the afternoon tide, that she had never actually made the choice. Fear had made it for her, and she had merely obeyed.

The consul's men had the ledger copies now. Pell, awaiting sentencing, had named every accomplice. The Kaiyo-maru sat detained in the outer roads with marines aboard and eleven divers walking free on her deck, blinking at the sky. Sato-baasan's granddaughter had gone home to the ama beach to a grandmother who had not let go of her hand in six days. Everything that hiding had protected her from losing, she had already lost — and everything she loved had survived the loss. There was nothing left for secrecy to guard.

Except, perhaps, herself. And that was the thing worth guarding least, if guarding it meant keeping it locked away.

"Mr. Ishida." She turned from the window. "When you came to my door last spring and asked about my father's name — why that question? Of everything in my register, why press there?"

He considered her honestly, which she had learned was his best quality and had once mistaken for his worst. "Because the father's name was the only entry you wrote too well," he said. "Every forged line trembles somewhere, Amberdale-san. Yours trembled in the brush pressure. Whoever taught you figures taught you to write like a clerk — and clerks' daughters don't dive for loose pearls at low tide." A pause. "Also, I confess, because I had seen your mother's name in an old shipping manifest and wanted to be wrong about what I suspected. I have rarely wanted anything so much."

"And were you wrong?"

"No."

"Then enter it right," Rosalind said.

She returned to the desk, knelt on the cushion, and took up the pen. The nib was good steel; it took the ink cleanly. Below the struck-through falsehood of *Tanaka Gihei, daughter of*, she wrote in her careful, countin­g-house hand:

**Amberdale Kume.**

Her father's name first, as given names go in the Western fashion he would have used — and her mother's surname after it, standing in the position of honor a family name deserves, where no clerk could mistake it for anything less. Both inheritances. Neither hidden. The strokes came out steady, and she noticed that her hands did not shake, and understood that they had been practicing this signature for eleven years in every honest column she had ever kept.

Below it, in the space for parentage, she wrote: *Daughter of Thomas Amberdale of Liverpool and Kume, ama diver of Iojima.* In the space for occupation: *Bookkeeper.* Not clerk's assistant. Bookkeeper. She had earned the larger word the hard way.

Ishida watched every stroke without moving. When she finished he drew the register back, examined the entry with a professional eye, and then — instead of sanding and sealing it at once — turned it toward her again.

"It must be witnessed," he said. "By regulation, any corrected entry of identity requires the mark of the person concerned, made in awareness, freely." He set the pen down before her a second time. "Sign beside the entry, please. And understand what you are signing. Once this enters the rolls, it is public. The wards will talk. The trading companies will know exactly what you are, and some doors that your old papers opened will close. There is no clause for regret. There is no amendment for fear."

"I know." Rosalind smiled — and found, somewhat to her surprise, that it was her mother's smile, wide and unguarded, the kind she had practiced suppressing for a decade. "Mr. Ishida, I spent a year learning that fear keeps very poor books. It lends everything and repays nothing. I'm done borrowing from it."

She signed. *Amberdale Kume*, again, beside the entry, and the two signatures matched perfectly, which gave her a satisfaction entirely disproportionate and entirely her own.

Ishida sanded the ink, blotted it, closed the register, and tied it with the official cord. Then he remained kneeling a moment longer, hands on the closed cover, and when he spoke again the bureaucrat's formality had thinned into something plainer.

"My own register is also being corrected," he said. "Did you know? The bureau above the bureau is dissolved. Six men arrested yesterday. My reports — the ones I filed honestly, the ones they buried — are being unburied one by one. Some of them concern your mother." He looked up at her. "Kume of Iojima filed a complaint with the prefecture in 1884. Against a pearl-buying contract. Against the weighing of her catch. It was the first documented grievance against the system the syndicate built, and it was buried by the same office that buried my reports. Your mother was not only brave, Amberdale-san. She was right. On paper. First."

Rosalind sat very still. The lamp flame leaned in a draft from the window and steadied. Somewhere below, faint through the floorboards, a clerk was stamping documents with the patient rhythm of the tide.

"She dove for two minutes at a time," Rosalind said at last, "and no one listened for thirty years. Perhaps that's the family trade — holding one's breath until the truth comes up."

"Then the family trade is thriving." Ishida bowed, deeply, and this time it was not protocol at all. "The corrected roll will be lodged with the registrar by Friday. Copies to the consulate, per your request through the clerk. You are, formally and permanently, Amberdale Kume of Minamiyamate ward." He hesitated, and something almost boyish crossed the tired civil servant's face. "For what it is worth — and I know it is worth little — the question I should have asked at your door that day was never about your father's name. It was whether you were safe. I have wanted to ask it properly for a long time."

Rosalind rose, and bowed in return, equal to equal.

"Ask it now," she said.

"Are you safe?"

She thought of the tenement kitchen, and Kenji asleep over his tea; of Sato-baasan on the ama beach teaching her granddaughter the breathing songs again; of eleven names read aloud on the quay; of a workshop that would be rebuilt; of a blank line, filled, in her own unshaking hand.

"Not yet," said Amberdale Kume. "But I have honest work waiting, and people worth keeping books for, and my own name on the rolls at last. Ask me again next spring."

* * *

She walked home along the harbor road as the mist lifted and the evening ferries began their crossings, lanterns waking one by one along the quay like a ledger being filled. At the top of the tenement stairs she stopped, because Kenji had pinned something to the door — a scrap of paper, covered in his precise interpreter's hand:

*A letter arrived from the consulate this morning. Not for me. For you. The seal is British, the envelope is thick, and the courier asked twice whether you could be found. Also, Sato-baasan came by. She says the Kaiyo-maru's owners are not finished, that ships have sisters, and that you should come to the beach tomorrow at dawn. She would not say why. She looked, if I may say so, like a woman holding a very large pearl and deciding whether to show anyone.*

Rosalind read it twice. Then she looked out past the rooftops to the harbor, where the detained Kaiyo-maru rode at anchor under guard, and beyond her, invisible in the dusk, the open sea road toward Shanghai — along which, someday soon, someone would surely come asking questions about a sunken cargo, a burned workshop, and a bookkeeper who had refused to stay a fiction.

She unpinned the note, tucked it into her sleeve beside the receipt for her new registry, and went in to light the lamp.

Tomorrow at dawn, then. The tide, as always, would tell her what it carried.
