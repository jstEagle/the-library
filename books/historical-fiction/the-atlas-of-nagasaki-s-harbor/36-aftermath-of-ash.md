## 36. Aftermath of Ash

The ashes were still warm when the money began to move.

Rosalind learned this the way she learned most things now — through paper. On the third morning after the fire, she sat in Kenji's small rooms above the shuttered teahouse on Higashiyamate hill, copying the detention order and the consul's report into her own hand so that duplicates might survive even if the originals did not, and through the open shoji came the sound of the harbor working itself into a new shape. Cranes that had swung all summer for Meridian stood silent at one pier while, two berths down, the flags of a Yokohama firm went up the halyards like men climbing. By noon the rumor had crossed the water to Dejima and back again: Meridian's charter was revoked, its bonded warehouse sealed under customs lock, its principal — Mr. Goro Tachibana, merchant, philanthropist, subscriber to every charity dinner in the foreign quarter — arrested in his own garden while his wife wept behind the camellias.

The company had not died of the fire. The fire had merely been the last dishonest entry in its books.

She felt no triumph. That surprised her more than anything else. She had imagined, during those long nights of cramped fingers and lamplight, that exposure would feel like surfacing — like breaking through green water into air, the whole bright harbor suddenly hers. Instead it felt like standing on the quay with wet stockings and ash in her hair, watching eleven divers walk down a gangplank into a crowd that did not quite know what to do with them. Justice, she was learning, was not a pearl you could hold up to the light. It was arithmetic. It balanced, or it didn't, and afterward everyone still had to eat.

And the arithmetic of Nagasaki was already rearranging itself around the empty space where Meridian had been.

* * *

A week after the standoff, Rosalind walked the length of the wharf simply because she could — because for the first time in four years there was no ledger she was obliged to reach by nine o'clock, no forged registry waiting to catch her name wrong. The morning was soft, the kind of pale gold light that made even the coal dust look gentle, and the harbor had the loose, unsettled energy of a household the day after a funeral: grief and relief sharing the same tea.

At the ama beach below Iojima's shadow, Sato-baasan sat wrapped in her indigo shawl, supervising nothing, which was how she supervised everything. Around her, women who had spent years diving in fear of the tally-sticks were hauling their boats above the tideline without being told twice. One of them — Matsue, broad-shouldered, her voice ruined by salt — broke off from the others when she saw Rosalind and pressed something into her hand: a single small scallop shell, its hinge tied with red thread.

"For your mother," Matsue said. "From the ones who remember her."

Rosalind closed her fist around it until the edges bit her palm. She could not speak. She nodded instead, and Matsue nodded back, and that exchange — wordless, weighted — held more than any document she had carried to the consul at midnight.

Farther along, the burned workshop stood like a blackened ribcage against the sky. She had not meant to go near it; her feet took her anyway. The demolition crew had begun clearing the wreckage, and amid the char they had uncovered things the fire had spared: an iron hook, fused coral, half a shipping crate stamped with characters she no longer needed Kenji to read. *Ceramic ware.* She stood looking at the stamp for a long time, until a workman touched his cap and asked if she'd lost something there.

"Yes," she said. "But I have copies."

He laughed uncertainly. She did not explain. There were things one kept in duplicate and things one kept nowhere at all, and she was beginning to learn which was which.

By the time she reached the customs house steps, the harbor's new masters had announced themselves in full. Notices pasted to the pillars bore the crest of the Kanto & Sumida Line of Yokohama, announcing the purchase of "certain waterfront assets formerly held by the Meridian Trading Company" and promising "a new era of honest commerce." Below the notices, someone — a diver's child, perhaps, or a dockworker with a memory — had written in chalk across the word *honest*: *we will see.*

Rosalind read it twice and found herself smiling for the first time in days.

* * *

The letter came on a Thursday, delivered by a boy in the livery of the Oriental Steam Navigation Company, and it was written on paper thick enough to stand upright on its own.

Miss R. Amberdale,

It is my understanding that you were instrumental in bringing certain irregularities at the Meridian Trading Company to light, and that your services as a clerk were of the highest order before your — let us say — change of circumstances.

I write to offer you a position. Not here. In London.

Our firm maintains offices in Fenchurch Street, and we are presently in want of a junior accountant familiar with Eastern trade, Japanese documentation, and — forgive me — the particular habits of companies such as the one you served. Your testimony before Consul Whitmore has been circulated, discreetly, among men who take an interest in these matters. Your name is known. Your discretion is vouched for. Your parentage, I am given to understand, is not generally discussed, and need never be.

Passage is arranged aboard the *Ardnamurchan*, sailing on the first morning tide ten days hence. Second class, cabin shared, but comfortable. Salary negotiable upon arrival. London is a city where a clever woman with steady hands may remake herself entirely. You would find few questions asked, and fewer answered.

I do not flatter myself that this offer answers some longing of yours. But I am told you are a bookkeeper, and bookkeepers understand that sometimes the soundest investment is the one that takes you furthest from the site of the loss.

I await your reply, or your silence, which I shall take for an answer either way.

Yours faithfully,
Edmund Harrowgate

She read it three times. Then she folded it along its original creases and put it in her apron pocket, where it lay against her hip like a stone all afternoon.

London. The word had weight in Nagasaki; it had gravity. It pulled at everything. Half the clerks in the foreign quarter spoke of it the way sailors spoke of home ports they'd never seen. And what Harrowgate offered beneath the salary was plainer than any figure: *a city where a clever woman may remake herself entirely.* A place where Amberdale could be the whole of her name, where no census taker would ever turn a page and stop, where the forged papers she still carried sewn into her lining could be quietly retired, replaced by real ones, real and clean and English.

She tried to imagine it. That was the trouble — she could. Too easily. She saw herself at a high desk by a rain-streaked window, figures marching in neat columns, a landlady who knew nothing of pearls, streets where no one sang the diving songs her mother had sung, where the smell of low tide was only a smell and never a summons. It would be safe. It would be quiet. It would be a life with all the difficult entries removed.

It sounded, some treacherous part of her admitted, wonderful.

* * *

Kenji said nothing when she showed him the letter. He read it once, set it down on his low table, and looked out at the harbor for so long that she thought he had forgotten her entirely. His hands — the interpreter's hands, ink-stained, precise, once ruined by a scandal thirty years old — rested in his lap like tools put away for the night.

"You could go," he said finally. "You know that I would never stand in the way of it."

"I know."

"There would be nothing here for you but hard work and people who will always, somewhere inside themselves, be counting your bloodlines." He said it flatly, without self-pity, though she heard underneath it the echo of his own dismissal from the very company whose ledgers had just burned. "England would give you a fresh page. No one tears pages in London on account of a mother from Iojima."

"No one in London knows my mother existed at all."

"Yes," Kenji said softly. "That is exactly what they are selling you."

She turned away and busied herself with the kettle, because busy hands and a closed mouth had been her rule since she was eight years old and it did not fail her now. But the words stayed in the room. *That is exactly what they are selling you.* She thought of the Kaiyo-maru, of the eleven names she had matched to eleven lines, of human beings entered in a ledger as cargo and shipped toward a market that promised them a new life too, of a sort. Escape and sale wore, she realized, disconcertingly similar clothing. Both offered clean papers. Both asked only that you never look back.

Except that no one was selling her. That was the difference, and it mattered, and she clung to it. Harrowgate's offer was honestly meant. The salary was real. The door was genuinely open. That was what made it dangerous — not that it was a trap, but that it wasn't. It was only a choice. Choices were far harder to refuse than traps.

"What would you do?" she asked him.

Kenji smiled, the crooked smile she had loved since childhood, the one that preceded all his worst advice and some of his best. "When I was young, a British firm offered me a post in Shanghai. Good pay, good title, distance from every debt I owed here." He poured the tea, slowly, watching the leaves unfurl. "I took it. I told myself it was ambition. What it was, was flight — and the debts followed me, because debts always do. I ran from one false entry and lived inside another for twenty years." He pushed the cup toward her. "So I am the wrong man to ask. Ask the harbor instead. It has better judgment than I do."

* * *

She went down to the water at dusk, as she had done since she was small enough that Kenji had to carry her home asleep. The tide was coming in, filling the channels, lifting the moored boats on their lines. Somewhere out beyond the breakwater the *Kaiyo-maru* lay impounded under naval guard, its masts bare, its holds empty of everything but seawater and evidence. Divers' songs drifted from the ama beach — the old rising-and-falling calls, the ones her mother's lungs had been shaped by — and the sound moved across the water like weather.

Sato-baasan was waiting on the sea wall, which meant she had known Rosalind would come, which meant she knew everything, which was her permanent condition.

"The Englishman's letter," the old woman said. It was not a question.

"How—"

"Matsue saw the boy in livery climb the hill." She patted the stones beside her. Rosalind sat. For a while neither spoke; the tide spoke for them both, folding and unfolding against the pilings.

"When I was a girl," Sato-baasan said at last, "there was a woman from this beach who married a foreigner and went to live in Shanghai. Everyone envied her. Silk dresses. A house with glass windows. She wrote to us twice, then not at all." She turned her face toward the darkening water. "Twenty years later she came back, alone, old before her time, and stood on this same wall and wept. Do you know what she said? She said, *I forgot which parts of me were true.*"

Rosalind's throat tightened. "I'm not her."

"No," Sato-baasan agreed placidly. "You are not her. You are the girl who copied three hundred pages by lamplight so that other women's daughters would not be sold. Such a girl can make her home anywhere. That is precisely why I am not afraid for you." She rose, joints cracking like ship's rope, and looked down at her with eyes gone pale as mother-of-pearl. "Go if you wish. Stay if you wish. But choose with both eyes open, child. A person who runs to escape her own reflection will find the water follows her."

She left Rosalind alone with the tide and the first stars.

Rosalind took out the letter and read it a fourth time by the harbor lights. *A city where a clever woman with steady hands may remake herself entirely.* Every word of it true. And yet — she looked up at the black shapes of the boats drawn up above the tideline, at the workshop's scorched skeleton waiting to be rebuilt by whoever cared enough to rebuild it, at the lit window of Kenji's room on the hill where a lamp still burned for her — and she understood that Harrowgate had misread her after all. He assumed she was fleeing a loss. But she had not lost anything here. Everything taken from her — her mother, her father's name, her own — had been taken *from* this place, and the taking had happened elsewhere, in counting houses and syndicate offices, in forged registries and burning workshops. This harbor was not the site of the loss. It was the site of everything she had won back.

Still. The letter sat in her hand, warm from her body, patient as a tide table. Ten days. First morning tide. Silence would be an answer either way.

She folded it away and did not burn it, and did not answer it, and walked home up the hill with the diving songs following her through the dark like a second heartbeat — knowing, with a bookkeeper's certainty she could not yet bring herself to total, that in ten days' time she would be standing on the quay whether she sailed or stayed, and that the harder of her two balances had not yet been struck.
