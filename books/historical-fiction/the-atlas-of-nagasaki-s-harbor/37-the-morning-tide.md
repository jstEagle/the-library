## 37. The Morning Tide

The ticket weighed less than a pearl.

That was the first thing Rosalind noticed, standing at the head of the gangway with it pinched between her thumb and forefinger — how little it took to buy a whole life elsewhere. A rectangle of cardstock, printed in three languages, stamped by the P&O office on the Bund, promising her a berth aboard the *SS Ballarat*, mail steamer, departing Nagasaki at half past six on the morning tide, first class passage to Southampton via Hong Kong and the Suez Canal. Paid for, the consul's clerk had told her, out of the fund raised from the Meridian receivership — restitution, they were calling it. A clean start for the woman who had broken the syndicate open.

*Miss R. Amberdale*, the ticket said. Not Kume. Not anything true or false about her. Just the name she had chosen, printed in someone's careful clerk-hand, as though a life could be entered into a ledger and carried away like freight.

The harbor was doing what it always did at this hour, which was everything at once. Sampans slid through water the color of pewter, their lanterns still burning yellow against the last of the dark. The Dutch traders' warehouses along Dejima threw long shadows across the quay. Somewhere behind her, fishwives were arguing over the morning catch in voices that carried all the way to the foreign concession, and somewhere ahead of her, the great ship lay against the pier with steam already breathing from her funnels — that low, patient exhalation of a vessel that knows she will leave whether you board her or not.

Rosalind had dressed for departure. That had been the strange part, dressing for it. She had taken out the good wool traveling costume Kenji had insisted on buying her two years ago, back when they still pretended she might one day want to go to England, and she had put it on over her shift while the sky was still black, buttoning it by candlelight, folding her hair up under a hat with a small netted veil because Englishwomen wore veils and she was, according to every document but the ones that mattered, an Englishwoman going home.

Home. The word had sat strangely in her chest all the way down the hill from the tenement, through the streets where she knew which boards creaked on which bridges, past the tea house where Sato-baasan drank her morning rice wine, past the burned footprint of the Meridian workshop, now swept clean and ringed with new scaffolding, rising again already as something else. Her feet had known the way here without her asking them. They had walked it since she was four years old, since the mornings when her mother carried her down to the ama beach on her back, wrapped in an oiled cloth, and set her down among the drying nets while Kume went under the cold green water after shells.

She looked at the ticket again. Then at the ship.

Then at the harbor beyond the ship, where the light was coming up.

---

Dawn came to Nagasaki the way it came to few places in the world: not gradually, but all at once, as though someone had tipped a bowl of gold leaf across the mountains. The hills above the town caught fire first — not with flame, never with flame again, she would not be able to see fire on these hills without remembering the workshop burning — but with color, deep green going bronze, bronze going rose. The water took the light second. And then the whole harbor was awake and shining, the masts and funnels and drying nets and diving boats all gilded together into one enormous, breathing thing.

It was the same light her mother must have dived into. The thought arrived without permission, the way the important ones always did. Kume Amberdale, born Kume of Iojima, had gone into water exactly like this water, in exactly this light, on hundreds of mornings, holding her breath until her lungs burned, coming up with her hands full of the sea's hidden silver. She had drowned in water like this too, off the point at Iojima, when Rosalind was seven. And now her daughter stood on the quay in an Englishwoman's traveling costume with a first-class ticket to England in her hand, and the water below her held the sunrise the way a cupped palm holds pearls.

"You're thinking very loudly," said a voice behind her. "I can hear it from here. It sounds like arithmetic."

Kenji Watanabe came to stand beside her, his hands folded into his sleeves against the morning chill, his grey hair tied back neatly as it always was before he went anywhere official. He did not look at her. He looked at the ship, with the expression of a man reading a difficult document — measuring it, assessing its terms.

"I'm not thinking anything," Rosalind said.

"Then your face is lying, which is worse." He was quiet a moment. "I have watched people make this decision for thirty years, from both sides of the gangway. Do you know what I have learned? Nobody ever stands here this long if they mean to board."

"I'm standing here because the boarding starts in twenty minutes."

"Mm," said Kenji, who had taught her figures and English and caution, and believed none of her evasions, and loved her anyway. "In my experience, the ones who mean to go are already aboard. They are up on deck claiming the good rail positions and complaining about the coffee. It is only the ones who cannot leave who linger on the quay, telling themselves they are waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

"That is the question you came here to answer," he said. "I am merely the old man standing beside you while you answer it."

Below them, at the foot of the gangway, the steamer's purser was checking tickets with the brisk courtesy of a man who had sent a thousand passengers off to new lives and never once wondered about the old ones left behind. A family of missionaries was going up the plank, their trunks borne behind them by coolies, the children already craning at the funnels. A merchant in a silk hat. Two naval officers. Each of them stepping out of one life and into another with nothing more than a rectangle of cardstock.

Rosalind's hand closed around her own rectangle.

"It would work," she said quietly. It was not really addressed to Kenji; it was addressed to the version of herself that had forged her first papers at fifteen, that had learned to make herself legible to the world in whatever ink the world demanded. "You know it would work. In England nobody checks anything. Half the names there are invented anyway. I'd be *Miss Amberdale*, respectable, no history, no registry, no census taker at my door. I could take the figures Kenji taught me and get employment in any counting house in London. I could be—" She stopped.

"Someone else," Kenji finished. "Yes. You have been someone else for eleven years. You are extremely good at it. It has kept you alive, and I do not regret teaching it to you, because a bird needs camouflage more than it needs song when the hawks are hunting." He turned then and looked at her directly, and his eyes were the old interpreter's eyes, patient and tired and absolutely clear. "But the hawks are dead now, little one. Pell is in prison. The syndicate's principals are named in the newspapers of three nations. Ishida has corrected your registry himself, with the bureau's own seal, so that no one can ever again use the falseness against you. There is nothing left to hide from. Which means the only person you would be erasing, on that ship, is yourself."

The ship's whistle blew — a single low note, preliminary, a quarter-hour warning. Steam scattered across the bright water. Somewhere in her chest, Rosalind felt the pull of it, the honest pull, and she made herself look at it squarely the way she had learned to look at a column of figures that refused to reconcile. It was real, that pull. Escape was sweet; she would not pretend otherwise. There were mornings when being half of two things felt like being wholly of neither, when the mirror showed her a face that belonged everywhere and nowhere, and the fantasy of a city where no one knew what she was — where *what she was* would simply stop existing, dissolve into fog like the coastline behind a departing steamer — that fantasy had carried her through some very bad nights.

But fantasies were entries in a ledger too. And she had spent a year learning what happened to ledgers whose columns did not match reality.

"The tide turns in twenty minutes," Kenji said. "And I am told there is a crowd gathering at the other end of the quay that has been told to wait for you, and that Sato-baasan has been threatening them with her walking stick to keep them orderly. So perhaps you should decide soon, before she decides for you."

---

They were at the far end of the pier, past the customs sheds, where the fishing boats tied up — and at first she thought it was just the morning market crowd spilling over, and then she saw that they had arranged themselves, deliberately, facing the water, facing her, and her breath went out of her.

There were forty of them, maybe fifty. Dockworkers in their padded jackets, men she had known all her life by their nicknames and their loads. Fishwives with baskets on their hips. Old Isobe the rigger, who had once paid for her winter sandals out of his own pocket when Kenji's money ran short. And at the front, wrapped in her good indigo kimono despite the fact that she had not worn it since her husband's funeral twenty years ago, stood Sato-baasan, leaning on her stick, her face set in the ferocious scowl she wore instead of tears.

Behind the dockworkers, in a cluster, stood eleven women.

The divers.

They had come ashore from the Kaiyo-maru's detention nine days ago, blinking in daylight like creatures raised from deep water — which is precisely what they were. Eleven names Rosalind had matched, tally-stick to ledger line, in another lifetime of nights: O-Haru, O-Setsu, Chiyo, the girl Tama who was sixteen and had been sold with her mother's debt still unpaid. Some of them had gone straight back to their villages. But these had stayed, these had waited, and now they stood on the quay in their working clothes with the salt still in their hair, and when Rosalind came toward them they did not bow, because divers do not bow to anyone on land either — they simply opened a space in their line, and Sato-baasan beckoned with her stick.

"We heard there was a boat leaving," Sato-baasan said loudly, in the voice she used at the ama fires, the voice that carried over wind and water. "A big foreign boat. Taking people to England."

"There is," Rosalind said.

"Hmph." The old woman examined her from head to foot, taking in the traveling costume, the hat, the veil pushed up, the ticket in her hand. "You look terrible in that. Like a crow wearing a priest's robe."

"It cost eight yen."

"It looks it." But her voice cracked slightly on the last word, and she covered it with a cough, and Rosalind saw that beneath the scowl the old diver's eyes were wet, and that she was furious about being seen to cry, and that she was crying anyway. "Eleven of us we were, sold north like bales of rice. Eleven came back. Do you know why?"

"Because of the tally-sticks—"

"Because a bookkeeper's daughter read our names in a book and refused to close it." Sato-baasan struck the quay stones with her stick, once, hard, a diver's knock against the hull of the world. "My granddaughter stood on that ship. My blood. I traded my silence for her safety like a coward, and a girl with ink on her fingers bought back what I should have fought for. So." She thrust out her jaw. "Now the girl goes to England, yes? Very well. Very fine. We will wave the boat off nicely. And then I will come home to an empty house, and the tea will be cold, and no one will argue with me about anything, ever again, and I will die alone like a proper respectable widow. It is a beautiful arrangement. Everyone at the company always said I was sentimental."

"Sato-baasan—"

"And the children," interrupted a voice from the line of divers — O-Setsu, broad-shouldered and calm, who had spent four months in the hold of the Kaiyo-maru and spoken barely a word since her rescue until this week. "At the school. In the old workshop. The one you are teaching letters." She shrugged, as if stating something obvious to a slow child. "Who teaches them when you sail? The tide does not teach. The trade companies do not teach. We asked. There is no one else."

"And the books," said old Isobe, shifting his weight. "The little office. Who keeps the honest figures for us now? The receiver's men charge double and round against us. My boy signed a contract last month he couldn't read. Who reads it when you're gone, Miss Amberdale?" He used the name deliberately, her chosen name, and it sounded different in his mouth than it ever had in Pell's — it sounded like something that belonged here, salt-cured, local timber.

Rosalind looked down the line of them. The dockworkers and the divers and the fishwives, the whole loose ragged congregation of the harbor that had fed her and sheltered her and stood guard outside a burning building's copying room while an old man's hands cramped over ciphered pages. Fifty faces. Every one of them watching her with the particular patience of people who already know an answer and are giving you the dignity of arriving at it yourself.

The ship's whistle sounded again. Ten minutes. Down the quay, the purser was calling the final boarding, and the missionaries' children were waving from the rail, and the *Ballarat* strained at her mooring lines like a dog hearing its name.

Rosalind took out the ticket. She held it up to the light — the same light that had gilded the water, that had lit her mother's last mornings, that fell equally on first-class berths and drying nets — and she read it one more time, all of it, the whole small printed contract of escape.

*Miss R. Amberdale. One passage. Southampton.*

Then she turned to the purser's clerk, who had come puffing down the quay after her with his ledger of unclaimed tickets, and she handed the cardstock back to him with both hands, formally, the way one returns a borrowed book.

"I'm afraid I won't need this after all," she said. "Please give my apologies to the captain. Tell him the lady decided she was already home."

The clerk blinked, wrote something in his ledger, bowed, and hurried away, and it was done — entered, closed, reconciled. Rosalind stood for a moment feeling the strangest sensation, as though she had set down a weight she had carried so long she had forgotten it was heavy. Eleven years of forged papers, of busy hands and closed mouth, of being a discrepancy in a registry, a question mark walking around in a borrowed name. Over. Not because she had been exposed, but because she had exposed herself, chosen it, signed it: *Amberdale Kume*, both inheritances, no more hiding.

The *Ballarat*'s lines came off the bollards with a wet slap. The great ship gathered herself, shuddered, and began, almost imperceptibly, to move.

They all watched her go — the fifty of them on the quay, and half the waterfront besides, because everyone stops to watch a big ship leave. Steam and spray, the white wake unfurling across the gold water, the funnels' smoke bending east toward the open sea. Rosalind stood with Sato-baasan's stick tapping impatiently against her boot heel and Kenji's hand resting feather-light between her shoulder blades, and she watched England shrink into a rectangle of painted steel and slide out of the harbor, and she found, examining herself honestly, the way she would audit any account, that she felt no grief at all. Only a vast, quiet relief, like surfacing.

"Well," said Sato-baasan, when the ship was a smudge on the horizon. "That's settled. Now take off that ridiculous hat and come and eat. The whole quay saw you refuse a fortune this morning, and refusing a fortune is hungry work." She was already hobbling away, issuing orders. "O-Setsu, fetch rice. Isobe, tell the school the lesson starts late. Girl—" she turned, jabbed the stick at Rosalind, and her voice, for one moment, dropped its armor entirely, "—welcome home."

Rosalind laughed, and the sound went out over the water her mother had dived, and the divers took it up, and the dockworkers, and for a moment the whole quay rang with it, rough and joyful, the sound of a harbor greeting one of its own.

But as they turned to go — as Kenji tucked her hand into his arm and Sato-baasan quarreled cheerfully with Isobe about whose rice was better — Rosalind glanced back once at the empty water, and felt against her ribs the familiar flat shape she had carried there for weeks without quite admitting what it was: her notebook, half-filled, the pages dense with her small precise figures and the beginnings of an account no receiver had ever asked her to keep.

An account of the drowned, and the sold, and the saved.

She had promised herself she would begin it when there was time. Walking up the quay between her people, with the morning tide running gold beneath the pilings, Rosalind understood that the time had just arrived — and that the first entry could not wait, because that very afternoon, a stranger had been seen asking questions at the harbor master's office about a certain bookkeeper's daughter, and Kenji had gone pale, and had said only: *We should talk tonight.*
