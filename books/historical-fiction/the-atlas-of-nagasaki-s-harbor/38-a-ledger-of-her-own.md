## 38. A Ledger of Her Own

The room above the chandlery on Uramachi Street had been a sailmaker's loft once, and it still smelled of canvas and tar and the ghost of salt that no amount of sweeping would ever fully evict. Rosalind stood in the doorway on the first morning of March and looked at what her savings had bought: a desk with one true leg and three mended ones, a shelf of ledgers she had bound herself from scrap paper and paste, an inkstone Kenji had given her when she was nine years old — chipped at the lip, worn smooth where his thumb had rested for thirty years of other men's accounts. A window that faced the harbor.

That was the whole of it. That was everything.

She set down the sign she had painted herself, letter by careful letter, in two languages because the street spoke two languages and she refused to make anyone choose before they crossed her threshold. AMBERDALE ACCOUNTING, said the English. Below it, in characters she had practiced forty times on rice paper before touching the board: 燈明堂帳場 — the Lamp-Room Bookhouse, though she suspected Kenji's translation was kinder than her brushwork deserved.

*Amberdale.* She had signed that name on the corrected registry five months ago, Ishida standing beside her with his census ledger open like a priest's book, and the clerk at the ward office had read it aloud — *Anbādēru Kume* — as if tasting something new. Both inheritances. Her father's name, which she had carried forged and hidden for years; her mother's, which she had never dared write anywhere at all. Now they sat together on official paper, and the sky had not fallen, and the harbor had gone on breathing its long gray breath beneath them.

Now the name hung over a door in Uramachi Street, above a sailmaker's old loft, and Rosalind Amberdale Kume unlocked it with hands that were, for the first time in her memory, entirely unafraid of being seen.

* * *

Her first customer arrived before the ink was dry on anything worth recording.

Old Genzo the rope-walker came up the stairs sideways, the way he always did, favoring the knee the Meridian cranes had ruined eleven years ago, and he stood in the middle of her floor turning his cap in his fists as though the room might be taken away from him if he touched anything.

"I heard," he said. "Down at the landing, they're saying the Amberdale girl keeps books now. Honest ones." He said *honest* the way some men said *holy* — carefully, as if the word might bruise. "They're saying you don't charge what a man hasn't got."

"Sit down, Genzo-san." She pulled out the second chair, the one with the woven seat Sato-baasan had brought over on her back two days ago without asking whether it was wanted. It was wanted. Everything the divers gave her these days was wanted, even when it was only their presence, their faces at her window, their slow nodding assurance that the daughter of Kume was doing right. "Tell me about your wages."

He told her. And it was worse than she had expected, and she had expected little enough. Eleven years of piece-rates calculated by a foreman who rounded always downward; a "lodging deduction" for a bunk in a company house that had burned in '94 and was never rebuilt; a debt entry from 1891 that had been paid twice over according to Genzo's own tally-sticks, knotted cord and notched wood that he produced from inside his shirt like a man producing scripture. The trade companies kept books for themselves. Nobody had ever kept books for Genzo.

Rosalind spread his tally-sticks across her desk beside the company figures Kenji had helped her obtain through channels she did not examine too closely, and she worked until the light went amber in the window, and when she finished she turned the page around so he could see it.

"Here is what they owe you. Here is how we ask for it." She tapped the column of figures translated into clean, formal Japanese that no shipping clerk could pretend to misunderstand. "And here—" she drew a line under the total, twice, the way Pell used to draw lines under totals, though she caught herself and made the second line her own, straighter, prouder— "here is proof that your debt was settled in 1893. You have been free for six years, Genzo-san. You just didn't have anyone to do the arithmetic."

The old rope-walker looked at the numbers for a long time. Then he looked at her, and there was something in his face that she would learn to recognize over the coming years and never quite grow accustomed to: the expression of a man being handed back a piece of himself he had stopped believing existed.

"They'll be angry," he said. "The company."

"Yes," said Rosalind. "I'm counting on it."

* * *

Word traveled the way water travels in a harbor town — through every crack, along every rope, arriving everywhere before it was sent. By the end of the first week she had seven clients. By the end of the month she had thirty-one, and a waiting list scratched on slate, and Kenji installed on a stool by the window three afternoons a week, translating documents, drafting petitions, and pretending with great dignity that he was not enjoying himself more than he had enjoyed anything since his dismissal thirty years ago.

"You understand," he told her one afternoon, adjusting the spectacles he still wore crooked out of old habit, "that I am doing this as a favor. A temporary arrangement. My reputation—"

"Your reputation, Kenji-san, is that you are the only interpreter in Nagasaki who ever told the truth and lost his position for it. It is the finest advertisement this office has."

He sniffed. But he came back the next afternoon, and the next, and he began bringing his own tea things, which in the grammar of Kenji Watanabe amounted to signing a lease.

It was not easy work. She had imagined — insofar as she had let herself imagine anything past the opening of the door — that honest arithmetic would be simple: compare the figures, find the truth, write it down. But the people who came up her stairs came carrying wounds that had calcified into habit. Dockworkers who flinched when she asked for dates, certain that any record kept would eventually be used against them. Divers who could not read their own contracts and had signed with thumbprints pressed into ink by men who explained nothing, deliberately, for years. A widow whose husband's death-benefit had been swallowed by a "processing fee" levied by a company that no longer existed, and who sat in Rosalind's chair with her dead man's pay-book on her lap and wept quietly while Rosalind traced the theft backward through three mergers of paper.

For each of them Rosalind did the thing that no one had done for her mother, or for the eleven divers whose names had been read aloud on the quay last autumn, or for any of the hundreds whose lives had passed through Meridian columns as weight and tonnage and *ceramic ware*. She explained. Every figure, every term, every consequence, in words sized to fit the listener. She wrote plain-language copies of everything in both scripts. She taught as she worked — teaching without meaning to, then teaching deliberately, then, when the winter rains came and business slowed, teaching openly: two evenings a week, the loft filled with dockworkers' children and divers' grandchildren learning their letters and their sums, because a people who could not read their own ledgers would always, always be robbed.

Sato-baasan attended those evenings. She sat in the corner with her granddaughter Chiyoko — home eight months now, her wrists still bearing pale bands where the shackles had been, her voice returning slowly like feeling into a numbed limb — and the old woman watched the children scratch their characters with the fierce attention of a general reviewing troops. Sometimes she corrected their posture. Once she corrected Rosalind's pronunciation of a diver's song-verse that had crept, somehow, into a counting exercise, and the whole room laughed, and Rosalind laughed too, and thought: *this is what it is to belong somewhere. This ordinary noise.*

* * *

There were hard mornings too.

In February, a representative of the Oriental Consolidated Steamship Line — one of the companies circling the carcass of Meridian like gulls — came up her stairs in a coat worth more than all her furniture, smiled with excellent teeth, and offered her a position. Head bookkeeper. Four times her current income. An office with a view of the foreign quarter. He laid it all out graciously, assuming — because everyone assumed — that a half-caste girl with a borrowed surname and a rented loft would leap at rescue.

She listened to the whole offer with her hands folded on the desk, busy hands, closed mouth, the old rule holding steady. Then she said: "Will Oriental Consolidated permit me to audit its own cargo manifests?"

The smile flickered. "That would hardly be—"

"Then no, thank you." She rose and opened the door for him. "But I appreciate your confidence that my arithmetic is good enough to steal."

He left. That evening she told Kenji about it, expecting his caution — *be careful, Rosalind, you cannot fight every ship in the harbor* — but instead he was quiet for a long moment and then said, "Thirty years ago I said yes to a question like yours. I said yes because I was afraid and because they flattered me. You said no because you are not afraid anymore." He took off his spectacles and polished them, which was what he did instead of showing his eyes. "I am glad to have lived long enough to see someone get it right."

* * *

Spring came up the harbor the way it always did — plum blossom first in the temple gardens, then the smell of warm wood and drying nets, then the ama boats going out again with their songs carrying across the water. On a bright morning in April, Rosalind walked down to the divers' beach at low tide, barefoot in the cold shallows, and dove for loose pearls the way she had on the morning this whole account begins — except that now she did it not to survive between wages but because the sea still knew her, still closed over her head like a hand over a candle, still gave up its small cold treasures to the daughter of Kume.

She surfaced with one pearl, small and imperfect, and swam for shore where Sato-baasan waited wrapped in a blanket despite the sunshine, because old divers earned the right to feel cold whenever they pleased.

"You'll ruin your hands," the old woman said. "A bookkeeper's hands."

"A diver's hands," Rosalind said, spreading them, the web of callus and scar and ink-stain that was hers alone. "Both."

Sato-baasan examined them with professional gravity, nodded once — the highest honor she conferred — and delivered her verdict: "Kume's girl. Stubborn in the water and stubborn on the land. Good."

On the walk back up to Uramachi Street, Rosalind counted what she had, because counting was her prayer. One office, solvent by a margin of eleven yen. Forty-six clients, honest books kept for people the great companies had never troubled to count properly. One adopted father translating petitions and drinking tea he pretended not to love. One old diver guarding her door against nothing now, guarding it anyway, out of devotion. One census taker who dropped by on Thursdays with registry questions that were transparently excuses, and whose visits she had begun, carefully, cautiously, to look forward to — a door in herself she was not yet ready to name, but no longer boarded shut.

One name, her own, over her own door.

She climbed the stairs to the loft as evening came on and the lamplighters moved along the waterfront, and she sat at the desk with the one true leg and opened a fresh ledger, blank, bound in gray cloth, and wrote on the first line in her own neat hand:

*Account of Rosalind Amberdale Kume, bookkeeper, Nagasaki. Opened in trust for the divers and dockworkers of this harbor. All entries to be true.*

Below it, the date. Below that, the first entry of a life that was finally, entirely, hers to record.

Outside, the tide was turning. Somewhere across the darkening water, the workshop that had burned was scaffolding and new timber already — the merchants' association had sold the plot cheap, haunted by its history, and there were plans, talk, beginnings. But that was tomorrow's column.

Tonight there was only the lamp, the ink, the page — and the sound, rising faint from the beach below, of the divers singing the boats home.
