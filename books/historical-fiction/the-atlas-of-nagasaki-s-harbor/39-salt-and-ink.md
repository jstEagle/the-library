## 39. Salt and Ink

The lamp on Kenji's old desk had a crack in its glass chimney that threw a thin line of shadow across every page Rosalind wrote, so that each entry seemed to carry a small scar of light with it down the paper. She had stopped minding this. She had even come to like it — the way a flaw in a thing can become its signature, the way her mother's hands had been scarred by rope and salt and were beautiful for it.

It was the third night of October, 1900, and rain was coming off the harbor in slow gray sheets that made the window of the little accounting office sound as though the sea itself had climbed the hill to listen.

She dipped her pen and wrote:

*Entry the first. Kume, diver, of Iojima. Lost in the season of the great fog. Mother of one daughter. Her hands could hold her breath longer than any woman on the ama beach, and she sang when she surfaced, always, before she breathed.*

The ink took slowly on the cheap paper. She blew on it gently, the way one blows on embers, and watched the words settle into permanence.

This was the account no ledger at the Meridian Company had ever kept: not tons of ceramic ware, not bales of silk, not the tidy columns where human beings had been entered as freight. This was the other book — the one she had decided, somewhere between the ashes of the workshop and the morning the *Kaiyo-maru* was boarded, that someone must write, or the drowned and the sold would vanish twice over. Once from the world, and once from memory.

Kenji had said something once, back when she was small enough to sit on his knee and still call him Uncle Kenji without irony: *A number is only a number until you give it a name. Then it is a person, and persons cannot be erased.* She hadn't understood him then. She had spent a year learning what he meant, and now she was spending ink to prove it.

* * *

The office was hers — truly hers, papers signed, rent paid quarterly from honest earnings. It occupied the front room of a house above the divers' landing, three doors down from Sato-baasan's, with a view of the harbor that Rosalind suspected she would never tire of: the fishing boats going out at first light like scattered coins, the great mail steamers shouldering in past the breakwater, the ama boats riding low and patient on their moorings. In the mornings the divers' songs came up the hill, that strange ululating breath-call that had been the first music of her life, and Sato-baasan swore the whole neighborhood woke better for it.

The sign she'd painted herself, in careful English and Japanese both: **AMBERDALE KUME — ACCOUNTING & CORRESPONDENCE**. It had caused a small sensation. A mixed-blood woman keeping books, under her own two names, in a shop with a glass window! The dockworkers had come first, shyly, wanting letters written to brothers in Kobe. Then the divers' widows, needing sums worked out on salvage shares the companies had always shorted them. Then, to her astonishment, a clerk from one of the smaller trading houses, who paid full rate and pretended not to notice who she was.

Pell's ledgers had taught her everything about how numbers could be bent. She intended to spend the rest of her life teaching numbers how to stand straight.

But the honest accounts were finished for the night. The lamp burned late because of the other work — the atlas, she had begun calling it privately. Not an atlas of coastlines and depths, though there would be those too; an atlas of people. Every diver lost. Every diver sold. Every name read aloud on the quay that gray dawn while marines held back the crowd and Pell stood pale as paper giving testimony against men he had feared for twenty years.

Eleven names. Eleven living women walked ashore that day, and Sato-baasan's granddaughter among them, thin and sea-blind and clutching the old woman's neck as though the quay might tilt and drop her back into the water. But the ledger had contained more than eleven. It had contained years. It had contained the ones who never came home, entered as *cargo lost at sea — insurance claimed*, and those names existed nowhere else in the world except in Rosalind's stolen copy and now, line by line, in this new book written in her own hand.

She turned the page and began again.

*Entry the ninth. O-Haru, diver, of Takashima. Sold north in the spring of 1896. Age given in the company ledger as "merchandise, female, sound." She had a mole beneath her left eye. Sato-baasan remembers her laughing.*

There. A mole beneath the left eye. No customs manifest in the empire carried such a detail. That was the whole point of the work — to write down the things no commerce ever bothered to record, because commerce recorded value and memory records people, and the difference between the two was the whole distance between the Meridian Company's counting house and this cracked little lamp.

* * *

Kenji came on Thursday evenings, as he always did now, carrying a paper bag of roasted chestnuts and his old dictionary under one arm like a scripture. He had grown quieter since the fire. Not diminished — Rosalind watched him carefully for that and found no trace of it — but quieter, the way a man is quiet after setting down a weight he has carried so long his spine has grown around it. Thirty years he had known what the companies did and said nothing, ruined for speaking once and silent ever after. Now the silence was broken, and he seemed to be listening to the shape of the space it left behind.

He settled into the chair across the desk, peeled a chestnut with fingers that trembled slightly — age, or the memory of opium dens, he never said which — and looked at what she had written.

"Read me the last one," he said. He liked hearing the entries aloud. He said they needed air, the way bread needs air.

She read. O-Haru, merchandise, female, sound. A mole beneath the left eye. Laughing.

Kenji closed his eyes. When he opened them they were bright. "I interviewed her," he said. "Years ago. For a shipping report I was made to falsify." His jaw worked. "I wrote that she was ceramic ware."

"I know," Rosalind said gently. "That's why we're writing this."

"And you put in the mole."

"Sato-baasan supplied it. She also supplied the laugh. She says O-Haru laughed at everything, even the cold."

Kenji nodded slowly, several times, the way he did when he was agreeing with something larger than the conversation. "Then she is entered," he said. "Somewhere permanent. You understand what you've built here, don't you? The consul's copy stopped the ship. But this—" he touched the page without quite touching the ink, "—this outlives all of us. Companies burn. Governments forget. But a girl's laugh, written down by another girl's hand — that survives."

"That's the idea," Rosalind said, and was pleased at how steady her voice came out, because her chest had gone tight.

They worked together until the chestnuts were gone and the rain eased. Kenji translated the last of the cipher passages from the master ledger — the syndicate's private correspondence, the merchant's initials, the payments routed through respectable houses — and dictated while she wrote. His English was formal and precise; her hand was fast. Between them they produced pages that no one would ever be able to call rumor.

Near midnight he rose to go, then paused at the door with his hat in his hands.

"You know they will ask you to stop eventually," he said. "Not the company — the company is dead. Other people. People who prefer the harbor comfortable. There are always such people."

"Let them ask," Rosalind said.

He smiled — the old crooked smile she had loved since childhood — and went out into the wet street, and she listened to his footsteps fade toward the house where he had raised a diver's orphan into a keeper of accounts, and felt the strangest thing, which was gratitude for a grief she had never expected to be grateful for. If he had never been dismissed, if he had never taken in the orphaned child of a drowned woman, none of the names would have been saved. Fate was a strange bookkeeper. It balanced its columns in ways no one could audit.

* * *

Sato-baasan came on Sunday mornings, ostensibly to inspect the office, actually to sit in the good chair by the window and watch the harbor and talk.

On this particular Sunday she brought the tally-sticks.

Rosalind had returned them after the trial — the old notched bamboo rods, one per diver, each notch a dive, a debt, a payment, a year — and Sato-baasan had kept them wrapped in oilcloth in her roof-beam cache for months, and now here they were again, unwrapped on the desk like relics.

"They belong in your book," the old woman said. "You matched them to the ledger lines. You counted eleven living women out of sticks and ink. Put the sticks themselves somewhere safe. Somewhere permanent." Her eyes crinkled. "And not your roof beam. Your roof beams leak."

Rosalind laughed and ran her thumb along the nearest stick, feeling the notches like knuckles. Each one was a day of some woman's labor. Each one was proof. During the worst weeks — Pell closing in, the demolition notice, the night the workshop burned and she had run through the loading door with the completed copy pressed against her chest while the original curled and blackened behind her — these humble sticks had done more damage to the syndicate than any sword. Paper against paper. Memory against money. And memory had won.

"The granddaughter asked after you," Sato-baasan said, watching the harbor. "Setsu. She wants to learn figures. She says if she had known numbers, she would have seen what was being written about her before it was done." She turned her sharp old face toward Rosalind. "I told her you would teach her."

"I will," Rosalind said. "Gladly."

"She also wants to learn letters. Both kinds."

"Both kinds," Rosalind agreed, and thought of the schoolhouse plans already circulating among the neighbors — the workshop site cleared, the charred ground sold cheap, subscriptions gathered in coins and dried fish, and a plot staked out where a building would rise in the spring with a schoolroom in it. Ishida had drawn up the paperwork himself, in his careful census-taker's hand, and had remarked dryly that registering a school required fewer questions than registering a household, which was the only kind thing he had ever said about his profession.

Ishida. He came Tuesdays now, no longer blackmailing but visiting, bringing government forms she actually needed and, once, a box of good ink because he had noticed hers was watery. Their alliance had survived the trial, the way alliances forged in fire tend to. Sometimes they sat in this office after hours and did not speak, two liars who had trusted each other anyway, watching the ships. He had told her once that he kept a copy of the corrected registry in his desk drawer and looked at her name sometimes — *Amberdale Kume*, entered lawfully, truthfully, both inheritances side by side — just to remind himself that the bureaucracy could, on rare occasions, be made to tell the truth.

* * *

The rain returned on Tuesday, harder, and with the rain a letter.

Rosalind recognized the crest before she broke the seal: a London firm, correspondence agents for the Amberdale estate solicitors — her father's people, or the people who had been her father's people before he indentured himself to a Nagasaki counting house and drowned his name in a diver's family. The letter was courteous, brief, and astonishing. With the Meridian Company dissolved and its principals convicted or fled, certain inherited matters had resolved. There was money. There was a house near Whitby. There was, the letter implied, a place in society waiting for a young woman of education and respectable blood who wished to claim it. Passage could be arranged on the spring steamer. First class.

She read it three times by the cracked lamp.

A year ago — even six months ago — she would have read it as a door out of a burning building. She remembered standing on the quay as the mail steamer cast off, the offer of passage warm in her pocket, Kenji and Sato-baasan and eleven freed divers behind her, and choosing. She had thought the choice was made once and forever. But choices, she was learning, are like entries: they must be renewed, re-inked, confirmed against the running total. The seduction of escape did not die; it merely waited, patient as tide, and arrived again wearing better clothes.

She set the letter beside the open atlas and looked at the two documents together.

On the left: a house near Whitby, a name restored to respectability, a life where no one would ever again ask about her mother's trade or the color of her mother's skin. On the right: O-Haru, merchandise, female, sound, a mole beneath the left eye, laughing. Setsu wanting to learn her letters. The divers' songs coming up the hill every dawn. The workshop-to-be, rising on burnt ground. A sign painted in two languages over a glass window, and her own two names on it, chosen, not assigned.

The arithmetic was not difficult. She had never once in her life failed to reconcile a column.

She took a fresh sheet, wrote a courteous reply declining passage with thanks, and sealed it. Then, because honesty demanded it even of herself, she allowed the ache — the real ache, the image of green hills and a language of her father's and a self unwatched — to pass through her fully, the way a diver lets a wave pass overhead before descending. It hurt. She let it. And then it passed, and she was still here, and here was where her dead were entered, and her freed, and her loved.

She added a postscript to the letter before sealing it properly: *Should any record survive in England concerning R. Amberdale, clerk, indentured to the Meridian Trading Company of Nagasaki, deceased — kindly forward it to me. I am keeping accounts.*

* * *

That night, alone, she wrote the hardest entries yet.

Not the sold. Not the lost. Those had a terrible ease now; grief, once given work, becomes almost restful. The hard ones were the living — the people who had nearly destroyed her and whom she must enter honestly or the whole book would be a lie.

*Mr. Pell,* she wrote. *Bookkeeper. Complicit for nineteen years out of fear and debt. Falsified an inventory to buy us time. Left a key where I would find it. Testified against his masters knowing it would cost him his freedom. Asked for no mercy and received little. Told me I was the best clerk he ever had.* Her pen hovered. *I believe he meant it. I have not yet decided what to do about that. Perhaps the deciding is also an entry, and perhaps it stays unfinished for a long time.*

And then, after a long moment, the census taker who had started it all with one question too many:

*Mr. Ishida. A man who used a forged girl's fear as a lever, and then stood beside her through fire. Both true. Enter both. A book that records only virtues is a advertisement; a book that records only sins is a weapon. This book intends to be neither. It intends to be true.*

She blew out the lamp finally, well past midnight, and stood at the window watching the rain move across the black harbor, where the buoys swung and the ama boats knocked gently at their moorings and the whole sleeping town lay unaware that somewhere above it a woman was writing it down — the drowned, the sold, the saved, the compromised, the brave — so that no tide, no fire, no company, no forgetting century could carry them away.

Tomorrow there would be honest accounts to keep, and Setsu's first lesson, and a letter to post, and the school subscriptions to tally. Tonight there had been salt and ink, which were, she was beginning to understand, the same substance: both of them preserved what the water would otherwise take.

She opened the atlas to a fresh page, and in the margin of the next blank entry she sketched, quickly, before sleep could take her, a small map of the harbor as it looked from this window — the breakwater, the landing, the burnt lot, the tenement, the sea beyond — and beneath it she wrote the title the whole work had been waiting for:

*The Atlas of Nagasaki's Harbor: being a true accounting of its people, begun in the year 1900 by one who was entered wrongly in every book until she wrote her own.*

Outside, far out past the breakwater, the first light found the water. And on the ama boats, faint and rising with the dawn, the divers had begun to sing.
