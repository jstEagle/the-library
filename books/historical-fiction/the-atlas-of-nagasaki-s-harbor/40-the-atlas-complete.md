## 40. The Atlas Complete

The harbor woke the way it always had, before Rosalind was born and long after her mother's bones had gone into its keeping — slowly, in salt and song.

She stood at the open window of the counting house that was no longer a counting house, listening to the divers' voices carry across the water from Iojima's dark shore. It was a work song, older than the trade companies, older than the ledgers she had once kept and burned through, older than any registry that had ever tried to write a name down wrong. The ama sang to keep their breath company on the long way down, and this morning their song came over the strait clear as a struck bell, rising with the tide.

One year. She counted it the way she counted everything now — in columns, in sums, in things that balanced or did not. One year since the Kaiyo-maru sat sullen at anchor while marines swarmed her rails. One year since eleven names were read aloud on a quay crowded with strangers who wept for people they had never met, because grief spoken publicly is a different creature than grief carried alone. One year since Sato-baasan held her granddaughter Chiyo against her small iron chest and made a sound Rosalind would hear until she died — not a cry, nothing so simple as a cry, but something between a keening and a hymn, as though the old woman were returning something to the sea and demanding a receipt.

Below her window, the workshop was no longer a workshop. That was the strangest arithmetic of all.

* * *

Kenji Watanabe rose before dawn because his body no longer permitted him anything else, and because the hour belonged to him now — an old man's hour, quiet and unclaimed. He put on his good coat, the one he had bought thirty years ago for a position he no longer held, and walked down through the lanes where the morning vendors were laying out their fish and their persimmons and their bundles of dried kelp, and every one of them called out to him by name.

That still astonished him. A dismissed interpreter learns early how to move through a city like a ghost — useful, invisible, forgettable. For three decades Kenji had perfected the art of being passed over. Now fishwives argued with him about the price of mackerel. Dockworkers stopped him to ask whether the school would take their youngest. Children shouted *Watanabe-sensei* across the water like it was the most ordinary word in the world.

He paused at the top of the stone steps above the rebuilt building and let himself look at it, as he did every morning, as if checking that it had not dissolved overnight into smoke and ash.

They had raised it on the old footprint, but taller, and with windows on all four sides — Sato-baasan had insisted on the windows. "A room where children sit should see the sea," she said, "or they'll think learning happens somewhere else." The carpenters had grumbled; the widow of the harbor does not get argued with twice. So the new school stood with its face open to the water, and in the first gray light its paper screens glowed like lanterns, and inside them, faintly, he could already hear the sound of small feet arriving too early because children who love a place will always arrive too early.

Kenji descended the steps. His knees complained. He ignored them with the dignity of a man who had survived worse.

Inside, the smell was ink and charcoal and tatami and, beneath it all, the ghost of tar — they had never quite gotten the old workshop's smell out of the timbers, and Rosalind had declared it a feature rather than a flaw. "Let it remember," she said. "Buildings should be allowed their histories."

He found her already there, of course. She was always already there. Her hair was bound back plainly, sleeves tied with cord, slate in hand, writing out the morning's figures on the great board at the front of the room: numbers marching in neat columns, each one legible, each one true. He watched her a moment from the doorway before she saw him.

"You're early," she said without turning around.

"You're earlier."

"One of us has to set the example." She turned then, and smiled, and it was the smile that undid him daily — not the guarded half-smile of the girl who had forged papers and hidden her mother's blood behind an English surname, but the whole, unhurried smile of a woman standing in her own light.

"Any letters?" she asked.

He set the morning's post on the table: two requests from merchant houses wanting her to audit their books — honest men now, or men who wished to be seen as honest, which in Nagasaki amounted nearly to the same thing — and a letter in a consul's hand that made Rosalind's eyebrows lift.

"From Mr. Fairfax himself," she said, turning it over.

"He writes to you monthly now."

"He writes because he still can't believe his own detention order worked. He wants me to confirm, again, that the figures are correct. As if the paper might have changed its mind over the summer." She laughed softly and set it aside. "Tell him yes. They were correct. They will remain correct. Arithmetic is loyal that way."

Kenji took his seat at the low desk by the window, where the oldest students came for English in the afternoons, and began sharpening pencils with a small knife, and thought: *this is what redemption looks like when it arrives. Not a trumpet. A pencil, and a child waiting.*

* * *

Sato-baasan came down to the water at first light, as she had done for sixty years, though these days she no longer dove. The sea had taken enough from her body; she had decided, with the finality of a woman settling accounts, that it would take no more.

She sat on her usual rock — the flat one the younger ama had named *obaachan's chair* years ago and now dared not call anything else — and watched her granddaughter swim out with the others.

Chiyo dove beautifully. Of course she did; the girl had the family lungs, the family patience, the family stubbornness folded into her spine like a second bone. But Sato-baasan noticed what the other divers could not: the way Chiyo surfaced each time and looked toward shore before going under again. Checking. Making sure the land was still there, still hers, still holding her name.

A year ago the girl had come off that ship thin as a bundle of reeds, wrists ringed with marks, eyes that looked through people instead of at them. She had not spoken for nine days. On the tenth day she had walked down to this same rock, sat beside her grandmother, and asked, in a voice rusty from disuse, *"Will you teach me the old songs? All of them. The ones they sing so they don't drown."*

And Sato-baasan had understood that the girl would live.

This morning Chiyo rose from the water with her catch bag heavy, and the other young divers were laughing around her, teasing her about a boy on the fishing boats, and the sound of it rolled across the harbor like something being repaired.

The old woman pressed her palm flat against the warm stone. Somewhere below her, in the green cold, lay the place where Kume Amberdale had gone down one spring morning and not come up, and where so many others had gone down and not come up, and where — for a few terrible seasons — living women had been loaded onto ships like bales and sold north. The sea remembered everything. That was its cruelty and its mercy both.

But the sea also gave back, when it chose. It had given back Chiyo. It had given back, in its strange roundabout way, a half-English bookkeeper's daughter who had walked into the ama beach one afternoon with fear all over her face and asked questions no one else would ask.

*"You have your mother's hands,"* Sato-baasan had told her that first day, taking them in her own rough ones. *"Diver's hands. Strong at the wrist. The water marked you even if the land forgot."*

Now those hands taught children their letters. The old woman found this funny in a deep, private way, and she laughed to herself on her rock, and the laughing divers heard her and laughed louder, and the harbor filled up with noise like a bowl filling with good broth.

* * *

Mr. Edmund Pell had expected prison. He had prepared for prison — packed a bag, in fact, the night before the Kaiyo-maru was detained, along with a confession written out in his careful clerk's hand, dated and signed, ready to be delivered whenever the knock came.

The knock never came. Or rather, it came, but gently: a summons to the consulate, a long interview, a document laid across the desk. In exchange for full testimony — every accomplice, every shipment, every name — and in exchange for surrendering his position and leaving the trade permanently, the consul's office had let the Japanese authorities settle his case as a lesser collaborator. A fine. A probation. A mark on his record that would follow him to whatever port would take him.

He had argued for harsher treatment. He remembered the look on the consul's clerk's face when he said so — a mixture of pity and alarm, as though Pell had asked to be flogged at a garden party.

So instead of a cell, he had a narrow room above a chandlery on the far side of the harbor, a job copying shipping documents for a Dutch firm that paid poorly and suspected everyone equally, and a habit, formed over the past year, of walking at dusk past the rebuilt workshop to look through its glowing windows.

He told himself it was penance. Perhaps it was. But mostly it was simpler than that: he liked watching her teach.

Tonight he stood across the lane as lamplight spilled onto the stones, and inside, a dozen children recited multiplication tables in two languages, and Rosalind Amberdale moved among them with the authority of someone who had once stared down an empire's paperwork and won. Through the window he could see the corner where she kept a shelf of books, and on the topmost shelf, behind glass, a single ledger — the famous copy, the one that had toppled the Meridian Trading Company and shaken a syndicate across two seas. Children asked about it constantly. She always gave the same answer:

"That's not the important book. The important book is the one you haven't written yet. Make sure yours is honest."

Pell pulled his coat tighter. He had been the best clerk in the settlement, once. He knew exactly what a dishonest column cost, down to the last yen and the last human life. He had paid in a currency he was still counting.

He did not go in. He never went in. But as he turned away, one of the children spotted him through the glass — a small boy, the chandler's son — and waved, guileless, the way children wave at anyone they recognize. And after a moment's war within himself, Edmund Pell lifted his hand and waved back.

* * *

Ishida came to the school on Thursdays, officially to inspect its registration papers — a fiction both parties enjoyed maintaining — and unofficially to sit in the back with the oldest pupils and learn, at fifty-one years of age, to write English letters.

"I am a census taker," he liked to tell the amused children. "I count things. Counting is a serious profession, and I intend to be excellent at it in every language available to me."

Rosalind corrected his exercises without mercy. He submitted to it without complaint. There was something restful, he had discovered, in being a student again — in being, for one afternoon a week, merely a man at a desk making mistakes that cost nothing.

His bureau had been cleaned out the year before, root and branch; the men above him, the ones whose names had lived in the margins of the master ledger, had been removed with a discretion that infuriated Ishida deeply (he felt criminals deserved public spectacle), but removed they were. The new census forms were longer and kinder. There was now a line, argued over for months, that read simply: *Mother's name.* And another: *Father's name.* And a third, added after a certain registrar had made a formal petition with a certain bookkeeper's assistance: *Names by which this person is known, all of them, entered without prejudice.*

Ishida had fought for that third line the way some men fight for promotions. He considered it the finest work of his career.

On this Thursday, after his lesson, he lingered while the room emptied, and watched Rosalind erase the board and rewrite tomorrow's date in the corner — a small ritual she performed with the tenderness other people reserved for calligraphy.

"The anniversary notices go up next week," he said. "For the fire. There will be a ceremony at the quay. The consul is coming over from Dejima to speak."

"He can speak," Rosalind said. "I'll listen. That's more charity than the paper ever showed us."

"There's talk of a plaque. Names carved in stone. The eleven, and Kume Amberdale, and the others lost to the trade."

Rosalind set down the cloth. For a moment she was silent, looking at the board as if the date itself might answer her.

"Stone is good," she said finally. "Stone lasts. But my mother isn't in the stone, Ishida-san. She's in the song they sing at low tide. She's in Chiyo's lungs. She's in—" she gestured at the room, at the slates, at the small scuffed shoes lined up by the door— "she's here, in every child who learns to read a contract before signing it. Let the stone have the names. We'll keep the people."

Ishida bowed, deeply, the bow of a man acknowledging a superior officer. Then he gathered his exercise book, now fat and much corrected, and went home through streets he had counted, street by street, for thirty years, and which had only just begun to be worth the counting.

* * *

Summer leaned into autumn. The persimmon trees behind the school fruited, and Rosalind instituted a strict policy that mathematics lessons ending correctly were rewarded with persimmons, which meant the class's accuracy improved with miraculous speed.

The harbor went on with its eternal business — boats out, boats in, cargo up, cargo down — but the quality of the business had changed, subtly, the way weather changes before anyone admits it. Merchants kept cleaner books now, or at least cleaner-looking ones; the fate of the Meridian Company hung over the waterfront like a cautionary painting. Two smaller trading houses had quietly hired auditors. A British firm had offered Rosalind a partnership with terms generous enough to make Kenji whistle; she had declined it with a polite note and kept her little office above the school, where dockworkers and divers and widows brought their accounts and paid in eggs, in mending, in gratitude.

Some evenings, after the children had gone home and Kenji had gone home and the lamps were trimmed low, Rosalind sat alone at the big table with a blank ledger open before her — a new one, fine paper, leather corners, a gift from Sato-baasan who had traded three seasons' pearls for it and refused to discuss the matter further.

In it she was writing a different kind of atlas.

Not charts of coastlines. Not soundings of channels. An atlas of the harbor's people: the ama and their songs, transcribed; the dockworkers' names and the cargoes they'd carried; the story of the tally-sticks; the testimony of the eleven; her mother's life, reconstructed from memory and from the memories of women who had known her, dive by dive, season by season; and at the center of it, copied out in her steadiest hand, the words her mother had left behind on a scrap of silk-wrapped paper beside a single perfect pearl — the words that had once been ammunition and were now inheritance:

*Tell my daughter the sea keeps what is true.*

She wrote until the lamp burned low. Some nights she wrote history. Some nights she wrote only lists — lists of the drowned, lists of the saved, lists so long they frightened her, and then she would close the book, and walk to the window, and look at the water until her breathing steadied.

The sea kept what was true. But the sea, she had learned, required clerks. Someone had to enter it all somewhere permanent, in a fair hand, with the columns reconciled.

She was that someone. She had chosen it, the way she had chosen her name, the way she had chosen the harbor over the mail steamer, the way she had chosen, one ordinary morning a year ago, to stand up in front of a room of children and begin.

* * *

And so the year turned, and turned again, and on a bright morning in the following spring, a visitor arrived in Nagasaki harbor aboard a coastal packet — a young woman, plainly dressed, with a letter of introduction in her pocket addressed, in a stranger's handwriting, to *the teacher at the harbor school, who keeps honest books.*

She climbed the hill from the quay asking directions, and everyone she asked pointed the same way, cheerfully, as though the school were the most famous building in the city — which, in the way of such things, it had quietly become.

She arrived at mid-morning, when the doors stood open to the sea air and the sound of recitation spilled out onto the stones. Inside, at the head of the room, a woman with her sleeves tied back was writing numbers on a great slate board, and the numbers were beautiful, and the children loved her, and outside on the water the divers were singing as they went down.

The visitor stood in the doorway, holding her letter, and waited to be seen.

Rosalind Amberdale looked up from the board — from the columns, from the sum, from the small eternal labor of making things balance — and smiled, and beckoned her in.

"Yes," she said. "Come in. There's room."

There was.
