## 1. The Ink That Outlived Him

The wind came off the harbor in the hour before dawn and found every gap in the workshop's shutters, so that by morning the candles guttered sideways and Lior Thornbury had learned to warm her ink over the brazier rather than trust it to the cold. Cold ink feathered. Cold ink lied. And an atlas, of all things, could not afford a single lie.

She sat at Renwick's old drafting table—her table now, though she still set his magnifier on its leather rest each evening and took it up again each morning, as if he might return mid-stroke and find his place kept—and she drew the last of the soundings for Plate Forty-One: the eastern approach to Port Mahon, where the channel narrowed between Sant Carles and the rock they called the Devil's Tooth. The line she laid down was true to the half-fathom. She knew because she had taken the soundings herself, three times, from a borrowed fishing boat while the garrison's own hydrographer slept off his wine.

*Fourteen feet at low water,* she wrote in her small, exact hand, *clear of the shoal, but for the kelp.*

Outside, Mahon was waking to rain. The masts along the quay knocked together like restless teeth; a fishwife quarreled with a customs man two streets down, her voice rising through the shutters' seams. The British flag above Fort Marlborough snapped and sagged and snapped again. Two years now the Union Jack had flown over Menorca, and two years since the sea had given back nothing of Aldous Renwick but one waterlogged boot, found on the shingle below Fornells, and the Admiralty's tidy verdict: *lost overboard in heavy weather, presumed drowned.*

Presumed. The word had a smoothness to it that Lior had never trusted. Presumed was what men wrote when they did not wish to look further.

She blew a strand of hair from her eyes and steadied her wrist. The nib whispered across the copperplate. This was the discipline her master had taught her first, before perspective, before projection, before any mathematics: *Grief is noise, Lior. Precision is silence. When your heart breaks, make a straight line.* She had thought him cruel when he said it, seventeen and raw from her father's funeral. She understood now that he had been handing her a rope across a chasm, and she had been walking it ever since—one careful footfall at a time, over the drop where he used to be.

The atlas lay open beside her, forty plates finished, this one nearly so. It was beautiful. It was also, she suspected without being able to prove it, dangerous—which was why it lived beneath a loose floorboard beneath the coal bin, wrapped in oilcloth, and why only she knew all of it existed. The garrison believed Renwick's atlas unfinished at his death, a scattered portfolio of coastal sketches. Let them believe it. Major Hesse's clerks had come twice asking after "the late master's papers," and twice she had shown them honest rubbish: tide tables, a half-ruined sketch of Ciutadella, letters about pigment prices. Her heart had beaten like a trapped bird both times. Her hands had not trembled once. *Precision is silence.*

Rain thickened against the glass. She worked until her candle burned to a stub and the light went gray and then gold, and when the final fathom-mark was dried and fixed, she sat back and looked at what they had made—what *he* had begun and she had carried alone these two years like a child in the womb—and allowed herself, briefly, the luxury of finishing something.

Then she heard footsteps on the stair.

Not the clerk's tread, nor the baker boy's light scamper, nor the measured boots of any soldier. These were uneven, dragging, one step sound and one hesitant, and they paused twice before reaching the landing as though the climber were arguing with himself—or with his wounds.

Lior rose, sliding the plate beneath a folio of blank charts. The workshop door had no lock worth the name. She took up the burnishing tool instead, heavy in her fist, and stood very still.

The knock came soft. Twice. Then, after a pause long enough for her heart to change its mind about everything, a third knock—the way Renwick used to knock. Their old signal, from the days when he'd come up from the docks at odd hours and didn't wish to disturb the neighbors: *soft, soft, wait.* No stranger should have known it.

"Who's there?" Her voice came out steadier than she felt.

"A traveler." The voice was rough, foreign to her, pitched low against the wind. "Wet, and sorry for it. I was told—" A cough, deep and wet. "I was told this was the house of Renwick, mapmaker."

"He is dead." She said it flatly, the way one says *the tide is out.*

A silence. Then: "So I've been told. By everyone. For a very long time." Another cough. "I have letters for the house. Letters *from* him. If the house will hear me out."

The burnishing tool grew cold in her grip. Rain ran down the window in silver ropes. She thought of the boot on the shingle. Of the Admiralty's neat line. Of the word *presumed*, and how she had hated it, and how some traitor part of her had hated herself more for hoping.

"If this is a trick," she said, opening the door a hand's width, chain still fastened, "know that I keep a knife sharper than anything in this room."

"I'd expect nothing less of his apprentice." The man on the landing was young—younger than his voice—with a face the storm had been writing on for days: salt-cracked lips, a cut over one brow closed with ugly stitches, eyes gray-green and watchful as a gull's. His coat had been fine once, a gentleman's coat, now stiff with brine and patched at the shoulder with sailcloth. He held a satchel against his chest with both arms, the way a soldier holds a wounded comrade.

"My name is Callum Vey," he said. "And I've crossed rather a lot of sea so that you would not have to read those letters standing in a doorway. May I come in?"

She let him in, because the alternative was reading them on the stair, and some animal instinct told her she would need to be sitting down.

He dripped on her floorboards and apologized for it. He refused tea until she threatened him with the knife again, and then drank it scalding, both hands around the cup, shivering despite the brazier. Up close he smelled of tar and wet wool and something medicinal—a poultice under the sailcloth patch, she guessed. He watched her the whole time with those gull's eyes, cataloguing, she realized, the room itself: the drafting table, the plates under their folio, the magnifier at its rest. Cataloguing her too. Not rudely. Like a man memorizing a coastline before attempting landfall.

"The letters," she said at last, when the cup was empty.

He reached into the satchel slowly, showing her his hands the whole while, and drew out an oilskin packet, tied with cord gone soft from damp. Inside were letters—not one but seven or eight, their paper buckled with seawater at the corners though the ink within had survived. He untied the cord and laid them on the table between them, face down, like cards.

"Before you turn them over," he said, "I should tell you what I am. Not who—who doesn't matter yet. What. I am not sent by the Admiralty. I'm not sent by anyone you'd call a friend of England. And the man who wrote these trusted me with them at great cost to himself, which is the only credential I'll claim."

"You speak in riddles."

"I speak carefully. There's a difference, and in Mahon just now it may be the difference between us both staying alive." He nodded at the packet. "Turn one over. Only one. Read the hand."

She turned the topmost letter.

The address was hers. *To Miss L. Thornbury, at the workshop above the cooperage, Mahon.* And the hand—

Lior had spent eleven years inside that handwriting. She knew its arrogance and its tenderness: the way he crossed his t's with a single impatient slash, the way his capital R kicked backward like a heel, the little hook he gave the numeral 4 that no copyist could counterfeit because no one else made numbers look *impatient*. She had inked a thousand fair copies of that hand. She had mourned it. She had dreamed of it, waking, in the white margins of other people's maps.

It was his hand. Not aged, not disguised. Alive.

The room tilted very slightly, the way a ship tilts. She put one hand flat on the table and breathed.

"That's impossible," she said.

"Yes," Callum Vey agreed quietly. "That's more or less what everyone says."

"He drowned." Her voice sounded far away, belonging to someone else. "They found his boot. There was an inquest. Hesse signed the—there were witnesses, there was a boat recovered, split on the rocks off Fornells—"

"There was a boat," Vey said. "Split on rocks. Very convincing. Boats can be bought and broken, Miss Thornbury. Boots can be planted. Dead men are extremely useful to live ones—he taught me that, among other things." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for the first time the wariness dropped enough for her to see what was underneath it: exhaustion, and something that looked almost like pity. "Read the letter. All of it. Then decide whether I'm a trick."

Her fingers were not steady. She noticed this distantly, the way one notices weather. *Precision is silence.* She broke the seal—it cracked dryly, absurdly loud—and unfolded the page.

*Lior. If you are reading this then Vey has done the hardest part and you have done the bravest, which is to hope against evidence. Do not blame him for what he has not yet told you; I forbade him to tell it all at once. Some truths are like soundings—they must be taken in stages, or the line snaps.*

*I am alive. I have been alive for two years, which I know is precisely the length of time you have been dead in every way that matters, and for that I owe you a debt I cannot pay in this letter. You have finished the atlas. I know you have; I know you as I know my own lines. Do not show it to anyone. Do not finish it, if it is not finished. Above all do not let it reach the Admiralty, whatever charm they wear when they ask.*

*Burn this after reading. Trust Vey exactly as far as—*

No. That was wrong. She read it again, heart hammering. *Trust Vey exactly as far as I would, and I would trust him with my life, having done so several times already.*

*There is a route in my charts that must never be a route in anyone's charts. You'll understand when he explains. Until then, keep the plates hidden, keep yourself dull, and remember, girl: grief is noise. So is joy. Make straight lines until I can give you back your crooked ones.*

*A.R.*

She read it four times. On the fourth reading her vision blurred and she let it, because no one was watching—except that someone was. Callum Vey sat very still, hands folded, giving her the courtesy of pretending not to see her cry, and she loved him a little for it, and hated him a great deal more for bringing the letter, and did not say either thing aloud.

"He's alive," she said finally. It came out as a question.

"He's alive," Vey said. "Though perhaps not for long, and not only because of what the sea wants from him." He glanced toward the shuttered window, toward the harbor beyond, where somewhere a patrol boat's oars creaked through the rain. "Which brings me to the part he forbade me to soften. There's a man in this town—Major Hesse—who has started asking questions at doors. Yours was next on his list even before I arrived. And Miss Thornbury, the answers Hesse is looking for begin with that atlas under your floorboards, and end, I think, at a gallows."

The rain drove harder against the glass. Lior looked down at the letter in her hands, at the impatient 4s and the kicking R, at two years of silence ending in nine paragraphs, and felt the carefully sealed life she had built—straight line by straight line—begin, very quietly, to crack.

"Tell me everything," she said.
