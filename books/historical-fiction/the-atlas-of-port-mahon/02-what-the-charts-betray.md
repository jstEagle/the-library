## 2. What the Charts Betray

The storm had followed Callum Vey from the open water like a debt collector, and it did not intend to leave empty-handed.

He stood in the doorway of Renwick's workshop with seawater still running from the hem of his coat, watching the girl who was not a girl anymore. Two years. He had been told two years, and he had built a picture of Lior Thornbury from that number—a child grown only slightly taller, still trailing ink like a shadow, still repeating her master's maxims to herself in the dark of an empty room. The woman at the drafting table shattered that picture and left him holding the pieces.

She had not screamed when he showed her the letters. She had gone very still, the way water goes still before it freezes, and that frightened him more than any screaming would have. Screaming could be answered. Stillness had to be waited out.

"You'll want to close the shutters," he said now. "All of them."

She didn't move. "Why?"

"Because what I have to tell you is worth more than your life, and windows carry voices on nights like this."

Something in his tone must have reached her, because she rose without argument and moved through the workshop pulling the shutters closed one by one, each bang of wood against frame punctuating the silence between them like a period at the end of an unfinished sentence. When the last one closed, the room went dim and gold, lit by a single lamp turned low over her drafting table, and the storm outside became something distant and theatrical, rain drumming on tiles that were no longer their concern.

"Sit," she said, though it came out more like a question. She gestured to the stool across from her own, the one Aldous Renwick had occupied every working day of his life until the sea supposedly took him. Callum looked at it for a long moment before sitting down. It felt like theft.

"You knew him," Lior said. It wasn't a question either. "You knew him alive."

"I've known him eleven years." Callum peeled off his ruined gloves finger by finger, laying them on the table like evidence. "I was his soundings man, before you were born almost. Before Menorca. We charted the Adriatic together, and the Tagus approaches, and a stretch of the African coast so unhealthy we buried two crewmen and marked the graves as shoals." He looked up. "He taught me that trick. Dead men make honest landmarks—no one argues with a shoal."

"And then he drowned."

"He drowned beautifully. I helped arrange it." He said it flatly, because there was no kind way to say it, and Renwick had always taught him that cruelty delayed was cruelty doubled. "A fishing boat lost off Cape Cavalleria in a squall. Bodies wash up along that coast every winter; no one counts them twice. There was even a boot. His boot. I put it there myself."

Lior's hand had drifted to the letters lying on the table between them—the ones Callum had carried through two storms and three bribes—and her fingers rested on them the way a person rests fingers on a pulse.

"Why," she said. "Why would he do that. Why would he *let* me—" Her voice caught, and he watched her master it, watched her swallow the grief back down into whatever iron place she kept it. "Two years. I inked his plates for two years thinking the sea had him. I finished work he'll never see."

"He sees everything you do. That's why I'm here." Callum leaned forward. "Miss Thornbury. Lior. What I'm about to say, you need to hear all of it before you hate him, because you will hate him, briefly, and then you will understand, and then you will be afraid. In that order. He's seen the sequence enough times to know it by heart."

The lamp guttered. Rain found a gap somewhere and began ticking against the inside of a shutter, patient as a clock.

"Your master discovered something in his soundings," Callum said. "Something the Admiralty paid him to find and paid him again to keep quiet. A channel. Deep water running closer to the northern shore of this harbor than any chart in Europe admits—a passage wide enough for a ship of the line where the Spanish charts show barely twelve feet. He found it because he stopped trusting the old surveys and started dropping his own lines, alone, at night, in a hired fishing boat, for eighteen months."

"The north shore," Lior said slowly. "That's—that's shelf rock. Every chart agrees—"

"Every chart agrees because every chart copies the same Spanish mistake, made eighty years ago by a man who measured in the wrong season and never came back to correct it." Callum tapped the table. "Renwick checked. Four hundred soundings. He checked the way you'd check if you understood what the answer meant: that any navy holding that knowledge could bring a first-rate through the back door of Port Mahon in fog, under guns that would never train far enough north to meet her. Or—" his voice hardened, "—could seal that channel with scuttled hulks and turn the harbor into a tomb for whoever's fleet is inside it."

"So he hid it."

"So he hid it in plain sight, which is cleverer. The atlas shows the harbor exactly as the world believes it to be. Safe. Ordinary. But the true plates—the corrected plates, the ones with the real numbers—he kept apart. And when both the British command here and the French agents who came sniffing realized the atlas existed, when they understood that whoever holds the finished work holds the truth of this harbor—" Callum spread his hands. "He chose the only exit left to a man whose skill had made him valuable and whose honesty had made him dangerous. He died. On paper. And he sent the atlas into hiding in the one place no soldier would think to search."

Lior went white around the mouth. "Here."

"With you. In the hands of his apprentice, in a workshop full of half-finished plates, in a town where everyone knows poor mad Renwick drowned and left his charts orphaned." Callum almost smiled. "It was elegant. It was also cruel, and he knows that. He told me to tell you—he made me memorize the words—*'Tell her I did not stop trusting her. Tell her I stopped trusting empires.'*"

For a long moment there was only the storm and the tick of rain inside the shutter, and Lior Thornbury looking at the letters as if they might burn her hands.

Then, quietly, in a voice scraped nearly clean: "Where is he?"

"That I can't say. Not because I don't trust you—because the fewer people who can answer that question under pressure, the safer the answer stays." He held up a hand before she could speak. "But I can tell you he's close enough to be caught, and far enough to be safe, and that getting the atlas out of this island before the century turns matters more than anything either of us has ever been asked to do. Because Major Hesse has begun asking questions, Miss Thornbury. At this very door."

* * *

She stood then, and crossed to the window, and put her eye to the shutter seam despite everything he'd said about voices and glass, and Callum let her. Some things needed seeing.

"The garrison commander?" she said to the wall. "Hesse? He came once last month. Bought a chart of Sicily. Paid too much and lingered too long. I thought he was merely a man who liked maps."

"He is a man who likes maps the way a spider likes flies." Callum rose and joined her, careful to stay out of her line of sight through the seam. Through the gap he could see the street below, running silver with rain, lamplight drowning in puddles, and at the corner—there, exactly where his informant said he'd be—one figure standing beneath the eaves of the chandler's shop with the patient stillness of a man being paid by the hour. "That's his man. Has been there since Tuesday. Hesse has a fragment of correspondence, something Renwick wrote before he died—or before he didn't die—and the hand matches the letters in your strongbox. He doesn't know yet whether the writer lives or someone is forging him. Either way, the trail ends at this workshop, and he knows it."

"Then he knows my name."

"He knows your name, your hours, and that you've been ordering copper plate from Barcelona in quantities a dead man's apprentice cannot honestly explain." Callum turned to look at her profile, sharp and pale against the shutter light. "You have maybe days. Perhaps less."

She was quiet a long time. Then she laughed—one short, disbelieving breath of a laugh, nothing warm in it.

"Eleven days," she said. "Until the year turns. You're asking me to move an atlas that could decide a war, past three checkpoints, in eleven days, while the garrison commander watches my door."

"I'm not asking anything. Renwick is. I'm only the messenger who got wet." He hesitated, and then gave her the rest of it, because she deserved the whole weight before she decided. "There are boats. There are routes. There are people who owe Renwick favors older than your apprenticeship. But every plan begins with the same impossible step: the plates leave this building without looking like plates, past eyes that already suspect you, and the first checkpoint is the customs house on the quay, where the senior clerk knew your master for twenty years and will look at your face while he lies or doesn't lie to him."

Lior turned from the window. In the low lamplight her eyes had changed—not the sealed stillness from before but something moving behind the ice, something calculating, and Callum recognized it with a jolt of memory: it was Renwick's look, the one the old man wore when a chart refused to agree with itself and he set about making it agree.

"My master faked his death," she said, "to protect a lie drawn in my ink, in my hand, under my roof. So the lie is mine now too." She pulled the letters toward her and squared their edges against the table, a small precise violence. "Tell me about this clerk."

"His name is Ferragut. He drinks. He remembers everything. And he loved Renwick like a brother, which makes him the most dangerous man on this quay."

"Good." She was already moving toward the cabinet where the coffin-sized shipping crate stood waiting under dust sheets, and Callum understood, with the strange calm of a man stepping off a ledge, that the alliance was made—not sworn, not spoken, but forged, right there, in the space between her fear and her audacity. "Then we'll need to give him something worth remembering."

Outside, the storm redoubled against the shutters, and beneath the chandler's eaves, Hesse's man shifted his weight and settled in to wait.
