## 3. The First Checkpoint: The Customs House

The coffin arrived on the tide of a Tuesday, delivered by a carpenter who smelled of sawdust and cheap gin and who would not meet Lior's eye.

"Paid in full," he said, setting it down in the workshop's center with the delicacy of a man handling something that might still complain. "Elm. Good elm. Won't warp even if the sea gets into it."

Lior paid him again anyway—a coin pressed into his palm with instructions to forget her face—and watched him go down the alley steps two at a time, relieved to be rid of both the box and the business.

She had bought a coffin. The absurdity of it sat in the room with her like a third presence, and she found herself laughing once, short and unsteady, before pressing her knuckles to her mouth. Two years she had mourned Aldous Renwick at a distance of six feet of polished nothing—there had been no body, only the Navy's letter and the empty chair by the window where his coat still hung. And now she was shipping his atlas to freedom inside a coffin built for no one, which felt less like deception and more like the first honest thing anyone had done for him since the sea supposedly took him.

*The dead travel unquestioned,* Callum had said, turning his cap in his hands the night before, rain still dripping from his shoulders onto her floorboards. *Grief makes people polite. They lower their voices around a coffin. They don't look too closely.* He had paused, and something had moved behind his eyes that she could not yet read. *You'll have to be the one who speaks for it, though. A woman alone with a dead man's effects draws fewer questions than a stranger.*

*Fewer questions,* she thought now, *but not none.*

The plates were wrapped by lamplight through the small hours. She worked the way Renwick had taught her—each copper plate swaddled first in oiled linen, then in wool scavenged from an old coat of his that still held the ghost of his smell, tobacco and iron gall ink and something like cold stone. Twelve plates, each one a coastline, each one a betrayal or a salvation depending on whose hands closed around it. Beneath them, as ballast and as camouflage, she laid the workshop's legitimate inventory: surveying instruments, a theodolite in its case, sounding leads, boxes of dividers wrapped individually so they would not chime against one another. A coffin full of a dead mapmaker's tools, bound for a buyer in Ciutadella who existed only as a forged bill of lading in Lior's best imitation of Renwick's hand.

It was strange, how easily the forgery came. Strange and not entirely comfortable, as though his hand had been waiting under her skin all this time.

By dawn the coffin was nailed shut, and Lior had slept perhaps an hour, curled in the chair by the window beneath the dead man's coat. She woke with the taste of copper in her mouth that had nothing to do with ink.

* * *

The Customs House stood at the head of the quay like a fat grey toad, its limestone face stained black beneath each window by two centuries of rain. Inside, the air was different from the street—cooler, salt-damp, thick with the smell of paper, sealing wax, pipe smoke, and the particular staleness of rooms where men sit all day deciding what other men may move from one place to another. Queues of sailors and merchants shuffled across flagstones worn smooth as ship's railings. Somewhere behind a partition, a clerk was arguing about tonnage in Catalan, and somewhere above, gulls screamed at each other over the roof gutter as if they too were disputing a tariff.

Lior joined the line with the coffin's documents in a leather wallet under her arm and her heart beating so high in her throat she could taste it.

She had dressed the part, and the dressing had taken longer than the packing. Her good grey dress, plain enough to be respectable, dull enough to be invisible. Her hair pinned severely. Ink stains scrubbed from her fingers until the skin was raw—though she knew from experience that the stains lived in the creases, that no amount of pumice ever fully freed a working hand, and that a Customs clerk's eye would find them the way a chartmaker's eye finds a false bearing: immediately, and without mercy.

*You are Miss Lior Thornbury,* she rehearsed silently, watching the queue advance. *Apprentice to the late Master Renwick, cartographer. You are shipping your deceased master's instruments to a buyer on the far coast. You are grieving, you are tired, you are bored. Grief is your armor. Wear it.*

The line moved. A sailor argued over a bale of tobacco and lost. A widow shipped a crate of household goods and wept quietly while the clerk stamped her papers without looking up, and Lior thought: *that will be me if my hands shake. That is exactly what I will become.*

Then she was at the desk, and the clerk looked up, and the bottom dropped out of her stomach.

He was perhaps fifty, with a face like a dried apple and spectacles that magnified his eyes into something owlish and terrible. She knew him. Not his name—Renwick had never used names for such men, only *the customs people, the paper-men, the little kings of the quay*—but she knew the face itself, because it had appeared at the workshop door twice that she could remember, come to inspect the license pinned by the entrance, come to squint at Aldous Renwick over his spectacles and pronounce the word *irregular* in a voice like dry parchment. Renwick had charmed him, once, with a hand-drawn sketch of the harbor done on the spot, which the man had kept, she remembered, folded in his breast pocket like a love letter.

The clerk's eyes went from her face to the documents and back.

"You are Renwick's girl," he said.

Not a question. Lior felt the words land like a sounding lead dropped into deep water, sinking and sinking, and she made herself stand very straight.

"I am his apprentice, sir. Lior Thornbury." She set the bill of lading on the desk with fingers that did not shake, because she commanded them not to, and command, she discovered, was nine-tenths of a steady hand. "I'm shipping his instruments to a buyer in Ciutadella. Per the attached manifest."

The clerk read. He read slowly, moving his lips, and Lior used the time to study him the way she had been taught to study coastlines—for the features that would not change under any light. The ink-stained middle finger. The way his thumb kept returning to the corner of the bill of lading, rubbing it, testing the paper.

"You write a fair copy of his hand," the clerk said at last.

"He was my master. One learns."

"One learns." He took off his spectacles, breathed on them, polished them with a handkerchief that had seen better decades, and put them back on. Through the lenses his eyes became enormous and unreadable. "I knew Renwick. Irregular fellow. Brilliant, mind you—did a survey of this harbor once, right there on my counter, in the space of an afternoon, that the Hydrographic Office couldn't have matched in six months. Kept it too, for a while." He touched his breast pocket absently, and Lior's blood went cold and then hot, because the sketch was gone from the pocket now, and she did not want to know why. "Drowned, they say."

"The sea doesn't always say true things, sir."

"No," the clerk agreed softly. "It doesn't. Nor do bills of lading."

The silence stretched. Behind Lior, the queue grumbled. Somewhere a stamp came down on paper with a sound like a small execution. She became aware that her palms had gone damp inside her gloves, and that the coffin waited on the quay outside in the pale morning light, twelve copper secrets sleeping under elm and wool, and that everything—the atlas, Callum's mission, Renwick's life if he lived, her own neck if he didn't—now rested on the next thirty seconds of a dried-apple-faced man's attention.

She could lie harder. She could cry. Both occurred to her, and both felt like retreat.

Instead, grief transmuted itself, in the alchemy of terror, into something else entirely.

"Sir," she said, leaning slightly forward, lowering her voice as one does over coffins, "you knew my master, so I will not insult you with theater. Yes—I wrote that document in his style, because his style sells his instruments better than mine does, and because a dead man's name opens doors a living apprentice cannot. If that is a crime, then arrest me, and I will say so at the trial, and every merchant on this island will learn that the Customs House harasses widows and orphans of drowned men over a matter of handwriting." She let a beat pass, and added, quieter, with genuine feeling because it required none: "He is dead, sir. This is the last of his property leaving his workshop. I would have thought even a paper-man would understand what it costs to sign a dead man's name."

The clerk regarded her for a long moment. A gull shrieked overhead. The stamp fell again, somewhere, like a heartbeat.

Then, slowly, he opened a ledger, dipped his pen, and wrote. Lior watched the letters form upside down, her whole body clenched around the expectation of her own name becoming evidence.

*Approved — instruments, deceased estate. Thornbury, L., consignee's agent.* He blotted it, stamped the bill of lading with a thump that made her flinch despite herself, and slid the papers back across the desk.

"Elm coffin on the quay," he said. "Send it through. Gate two." His eyes held hers a moment longer over the spectacles. "Tell your master's ghost the harbor sketch was the finest thing I ever saw. And Miss Thornbury—" he tapped the ledger once, lightly, almost kindly, "—names stay written. That's the nature of ledgers."

* * *

She did not breathe properly again until the coffin was loaded, until the carter cracked his whip and the wheels ground away toward gate two and the waiting brig, until the elm box carrying everything she loved and feared rolled out of her sight forever.

Callum found her afterward on the seawall, where she stood watching the harbor water work itself against the stones. He said nothing at first, just stood beside her with his hands in his pockets, letting the wind do the talking. When he finally spoke, his voice was careful.

"Well?"

"It went through." She heard how flat her own voice sounded, how much it cost to keep it level. "Gate two. Approved. Deceased estate." A laugh escaped her, thin and strange. "I threatened the Crown's Customs House with public scandal, Callum. Me. I have never in my life raised my voice above a whisper in a government building."

"What did it cost?"

And there it was—the question beneath the question, the soundings beneath the surface. She told him truthfully, because lying to him had begun to feel like lying to Renwick: "My name. In his ledger. In his own words—*names stay written.*"

Callum was quiet for a long moment. Out on the water, the brig was making ready, sails rising like pale wings.

"Then we assume he reads it," he said finally. "Assume the name travels upward. There's a Major Hesse at the garrison who collects names the way other men collect debts." He turned to look at her, and in the winter light she saw for the first time how young he was beneath the weathering, and how frightened, and how determined to be neither. "We should move quickly now. Whatever comes next, it comes soon."

Lior looked out at the harbor—at the grey water, the anchored warships riding at their cables like sleeping animals, the fortress walls where the Empire's flags snapped in the wind. Somewhere out there, if the sea didn't always say true things, a drowned man was alive, and his name had just sailed out of a coffin toward him, and hers had just been written down in a book that powerful men read before breakfast.

First blood, she thought. Drawn with a pen instead of a sword, but drawn all the same.

Three days later, when a sergeant in garrison blue appeared at the workshop door with an envelope sealed in wax and bearing the crest of Major Hesse, Lior understood exactly how the ledger had traveled—and how fast.
