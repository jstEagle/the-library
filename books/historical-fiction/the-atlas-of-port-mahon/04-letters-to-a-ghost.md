## 4. Letters to a Ghost

The storm that had brought Callum Vey to her door had passed by morning, but its aftermath clung to the workshop like a second tenant. Rain still dripped from the eaves in slow, irregular counts—*drip, drip-drip, drip*—onto the flagstones outside, and the light through the salt-fogged windows was the pale grey of unsized paper. Lior had lit every lamp she owned and set them around the workbench like votives before an altar, though what she worshipped there she could not have said.

Callum sat opposite her, his bandaged hands cradling a cup of barley tea he had not touched. He had slept three hours on the cot behind the drying racks, and it had not been enough; exhaustion had carved hollows beneath his eyes and made his movements careful, as if his own body were cargo he was afraid of jostling. But when he drew the oilskin packet from inside his coat and laid it on the bench between them, his hands were steady.

"Eleven letters," he said. "One for each month he's been—"

"Dead," Lior finished. The word came out flat as a burnished plate. "You may say it. I've said it myself often enough, these two years. *Drowned off Cap de Cavalleria*, the Admiralty wrote. *Body not recovered.* They gave me his instruments. His spectacles." She touched the case at her throat where the spectacles lived now, against her skin, like a relic. "They did not give me this."

She nodded at the packet but did not reach for it. Her fingers, she knew, would shake. Two years of precision—of ruling lines so fine they seemed less drawn than grown, of grinding ink until the black was absolute, of sealing grief into the discipline of craft—all of it balanced now on the edge of an oilskin wrapper gone soft with seawater.

"You read them," she said. It was not quite a question.

"Some." Callum's gaze went to the window, to the harbour below where the masts of the blockade fleet swayed like a forest deciding which way to fall. "He asked me to read them only if—" He stopped. Started again. "He said: *if the girl will not hear them, burn them unopened. She has a right to her anger.*"

"I am not angry."

"You are furious." There was no cruelty in it. Only observation, the same flat accuracy she brought to a coastline. "I've watched you all morning. You've re-inked the same border twice and ruled it crooked both times. Master Renwick used to do exactly that, the week before a chart went wrong."

Lior looked down. He was right; the border of the Ciutadella plate wandered like a drunkard's walk, and she had been pretending not to see it for an hour.

"Read them," she said.

* * *

He read the first one slowly, the way one sounds a channel—feeling for the depth beneath each sentence.

*My dear Lior,—*

Her breath caught at her name in his hand. Even recited in a stranger's voice, it was unmistakably him: the small pedantry of the dash after *dear*, the habit of beginning letters mid-thought, as if the page were merely a continuation of conversation.

*—by the time you read this you will have been told I am drowned, and you will not believe it, because you never believe anything until you have measured it twice. Good. Distrust is the first instrument of our trade.*

The rain kept its count on the flagstones. A coal shifted in the stove with a sound like a sigh.

*I faked my death the way one fakes a coastline: convincingly enough to be believed, inaccurately enough to serve. The boat was real. The body was ballast. Do not look for me in coves or churchyards; I am nowhere so romantic. I am in the one place no one looks for a dead man, which is to say: at work.*

Lior made herself breathe. Outside, a gull cried once and was silenced by the wind.

*They wanted the atlas, little compass. Not my atlas—England's, and Spain's, and whoever else could hold a knife to a mapmaker's throat. The soundings of Port Mahon's eastern channel are worth more than the island entire, and both Crowns know it, and neither may ever be permitted to know it whole. So I let them mourn me instead. Grief, I have found, is an excellent archive. No one searches it.*

"He called me *little compass*." Her voice came out strange to her own ears, thin as tracing paper. "He hadn't called me that since I was fifteen. Since the day I corrected his bearing on the Illa del Rei and he sulked about it for a fortnight."

"Did he?" Callum said, and there was something underneath the mildness of it, some current she could not chart.

"He pretended to sulk. Then he cut a new pen nib in the shape of a compass rose and left it on my desk without a word." She laughed—a broken, wet sound—and pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "Read the next one."

* * *

By the fourth letter the light had changed, the grey giving way to a bruised gold as the fog burned off over the harbour. By the sixth, Lior had stopped taking notes. She had begun the morning with her surveyor's discipline intact, meaning to record dates, place-names, anything actionable—but Renwick's voice, moving through Callum's careful mouth, dismantled her method letter by letter.

*I saw the aurora last night from the deck of a Danish brig,* ran the sixth. *Green fire over black water, and I thought: Lior would want to chart it, and there is no projection yet invented honest enough to hold it. This is the trouble with teaching someone to see. You spend the rest of your life seeing through their eyes.*

She discovered, somewhere in the seventh letter, that she had memorized the sound of him—not the voice itself, which two years had worn to a whisper in her memory, but the architecture of his sentences: how he circled a feeling the way he'd circle an uncharted shoal, approaching, retreating, taking soundings, never naming it directly until suddenly it was named and undeniable.

*I am a coward in one respect only,* the eighth letter confessed. *I should have told you I was leaving. I composed the telling a hundred times—in the workshop, at supper, watching you ink the Albufera marshes with your tongue between your teeth—and every time, the words sank like lead in shallow water. You deserved better than a ballast-weight corpse and a packet of paper. Forgive me. Or better: don't forgive me. Chart me accurately. That is all I have ever asked of anyone.*

Lior realized she was crying only when a tear fell on the back of her hand and blurred, absurdly, the line she had been holding there in her mind—an imaginary meridian, straight across her knuckles.

Callum had gone quiet. When she looked up, she found him watching her with an expression she could not immediately classify, and then could, and wished she couldn't: it was the expression of a man standing on a shore watching a ship he loved sail toward water he knows is too deep for him. He looked away quickly, down at the letter, and turned it over as if the next page demanded urgent attention.

"Is there more?" she asked.

"There's always more." He cleared his throat. "There are three letters left."

* * *

The ninth and tenth she heard as through water. In the tenth, Renwick described the harbour at night from the sea—the lights of Port Mahon strung along its cliffs like a constellation fallen and too proud to climb back up—and Lior thought: *he is describing the way I see it. He taught me to see it that way. And now I cannot tell which of us the memory belongs to.*

But the eleventh letter, the last, he read differently. Slower. As though the words were heavy and he was carrying them up a long stair.

*My dear Lior, this is the last I shall write, because hope is a supply like any other and mine is nearly spent. If Vey reaches you, trust him further than he trusts himself—he will understand that instruction better than you will, and be uncomfortable with it, which is proper. If he does not reach you, then burn the atlas, little compass. Burn every plate. I know what I am asking. I spent thirty years drawing that coast and I am asking you to unmake it, because a map in the wrong hands is a weapon, and a map in no hands is only memory, and memory cannot be requisitioned by any navy afloat.*

*I loved my work. But I loved it best in the years it had your hand in it. Whatever becomes of the atlas, the atlas was never the masterwork. You were.*

Silence took the room then, total, broken only by the drip of the eaves and the tick of the stove. Lior sat with her hands flat on the workbench, on either side of the finished plates hidden beneath their linen, and felt the shape of her life rearrange itself around the sentence the way a river rearranges itself around a stone dropped from a great height.

"The atlas was never the masterwork," she repeated. "He wrote that. In his own hand."

"In his own hand." Callum folded the letters with exaggerated care, squaring the edges, aligning the corners—a man buying himself time. "He made me copy them all twice, before we parted. Said a cartographer's rule applied: never carry the only copy of anything."

"And where is he?" She leaned forward. "Truly. What port? What name is he wearing?"

Callum shook his head, and the honesty of the gesture was almost worse than refusal. "He didn't tell me. He said the fewer who could answer under questioning, the safer the answer. I know only that he'll come for the atlas himself, or send word by Candlemas, and that if Major Hesse gets the plates first, none of it matters—Renwick alive, Renwick dead, it's all the same gallows."

"Hesse." The name landed in her stomach like a sounding weight striking bottom. "He came to the door yesterday. While you slept. He stood in that doorway—" she pointed at it, at the ordinary oak of it, suddenly terrible— "and complimented my linework. Asked whether Master Renwick had left any unfinished commissions. He smiled the entire time. He has the smile of a man who has already written the ending and is waiting for the characters to catch up."

Callum rose, wincing at the pull of his bandages, and moved to the window. Below, in the street, two red-coated soldiers walked past the workshop door without stopping—walked past, and then, twenty paces on, one of them glanced back.

"They're counting the times they pass," Callum said quietly. "That's the third since dawn."

Lior joined him at the glass. Down in the harbour, the blockade fleet lay grey on grey water, and beyond it, past the Moll, the customs house flew its flag, and beyond that, up the hill road, Fort San Felipe's walls caught the afternoon light like the edge of a blade being slowly drawn. Every road out of Port Mahon passed a checkpoint. Every checkpoint kept a ledger. And somewhere in a ledger, in a neat military hand, her name was already written.

"He wants dinner," she said.

"What?"

"Hesse. Yesterday, at the door. He said—" she closed her eyes, retrieved it exactly, hating how precisely she remembered everything, always— "*Mademoiselle Thornbury, a mind like yours ought not to go unfed. My table Thursday, seven o'clock. We shall discuss the late master's legacy.*" She opened her eyes. "It wasn't an invitation. It was a summons wearing gloves."

Callum turned from the window, and in the lamplight she saw the decision move through his face like weather. "Then we have until Thursday," he said, "to decide what you're going to feed him."
