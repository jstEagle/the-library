## 5. Hesse's Invitation

Major Edmund Hesse believed that a man learned more from a dinner table than from a battlefield, and that most wars were won or lost over the second course.

He had dressed for the evening with particular care. Not the full dress uniform—that would announce the occasion too loudly—but his best civilian coat, a deep bottle green that softened the soldier in him into something nearer to a gentleman scholar. He had even permitted himself a touch of pomade at the temples, though his valet had been dismissed for the night. Some performances were better staged without witnesses.

The dining room of the governor's residence had been placed at his disposal, and Hesse had used it well. Two candles only, so the light fell warm and low. A fire banked rather than blazing, so that the room would feel like a secret. The long table he'd ordered stripped down and pushed against the wall; in its place, a small round one set for two, intimate as a confessional. The silver was old, Spanish, looted honestly enough during the taking of the island, and it caught the candlelight like fish scales.

On the sideboard, beneath a folded napkin as casual as an afterthought, lay the fragment of paper that had cost him three days of clerical labor and one very uncomfortable conversation with a customs clerk named Ferrer, who now understood precisely what discretion purchased and what carelessness cost.

Hesse poured himself a small glass of sherry and stood at the window, watching the harbor darken. Port Mahon at dusk was the finest thing the Crown had given him—a deep stone throat of water between cliffs, big enough to swallow a fleet, and every ship in it riding under British guns. It was also, he knew with the certainty of a man who read other men's maps before they finished drawing them, the reason someone on this island was lying to him.

* * *

She arrived at seven o'clock exactly, which told him something. Miss Lior Thornbury came up the steps in a gray wool dress that had been brushed clean but not replaced—she had made herself presentable without spending a farthing—and her hair was pinned back in that severe, workmanlike fashion he'd observed through the workshop window on three separate occasions. Her hands were ink-stained at the cuticles no matter how she scrubbed them. He found this detail oddly pleasing. Honest hands.

"Major." She gave him a curtsy measured to the inch—correct, neither deep nor shallow, the curtsy of a woman who had calculated its worth.

"Miss Thornbury. You honor me." He drew out her chair himself, noting how she sat: spine straight, hands folded, eyes making one quick circuit of the room—the windows, the sideboard, the doors—before returning to his face. A surveyor's habit. She mapped rooms the way she mapped coastlines. "I confess I've wanted this conversation some weeks. Your master's atlas has become something of a legend in the garrison."

"I'm surprised anyone in the garrison thinks of it at all."

"Oh, they think of little else, when they think of charts at all." He signaled the serving man—a Portuguese who could be trusted the way a knife can be trusted—and the first course came: white soup, then turbot in a sauce of almonds. Hesse talked while she ate, lightly, pleasantly, of the weather, of the new fortifications at La Mola, of the scandalous price of lemons. He watched her eat the way she did everything else—economically, attentively—and waited until the fish plates were cleared before he turned the blade upright.

"You know," he said, refilling her wine, "when Renwick drowned, I lost more than a surveyor. I lost the finest mind ever applied to these waters. The Spaniards charted Mahon for two centuries and got half of it wrong. Renwick got all of it right, down to the fathom." He smiled. "And you, his apprentice—you've been carrying on his work alone for two years. Alone, in that cold workshop, with no one to check your soundings. That is devotion, Miss Thornbury. That is *fidelity*. The Admiralty doesn't grow such plants in English soil anymore."

"He taught me that a map is a promise," she said. "That whoever reads it must be able to trust every line you draw."

"A promise." Hesse let the word sit in the candlelight a moment. "Yes. And promises, like maps, have readers. The question is always—who is entitled to read them?"

He rose, unhurried, and crossed to the sideboard. When he returned, he did not hand her the fragment of paper. He laid it beside her plate, face up, smoothing it flat with two fingers as though it were a stain he was obliging her to look at.

It was a letter. Or part of one—a single page, torn across, the lower half gone. The ink had faded to brown, but the hand was unmistakable: the sharp forward slant, the peculiar way the capital R kicked like a spurred heel. Hesse had stared at that handwriting for hours. He had committed it to memory the way a man memorizes the face of an enemy.

*the soundings must never reach either ministry. I have seen what London intends—*

The rest was torn away.

"Do you know whose hand that is?" Hesse asked softly.

Lior Thornbury did not move. But he saw it—the stillness that comes over a person when the floor tilts beneath them and they choose, consciously, to keep their balance. She looked at the paper for perhaps four seconds. Then she lifted her eyes to his.

"It resembles Master Renwick's writing," she said. "Many educated hands resemble each other."

"Many educated hands," Hesse agreed, "do not write about soundings."

He returned to his chair and sat, folding his hands, giving her the full warmth of his attention. This was the moment. He had rehearsed it in front of the mirror that afternoon and discarded the rehearsal entirely; a threat delivered by rote was a threat half-made.

"Here is my difficulty, Miss Thornbury. Two years ago, Aldous Renwick went out in a boat that came back empty. No body. No wreckage recovered beyond a shattered oar. And within a month, his apprentice begins working nights on an atlas that the dead man supposedly left unfinished. An atlas containing—" he ticked the points off gently, like a man counting coins, "—every sounding, every shoal, every unmarked channel in Port Mahon. Including, if my sources are correct, a passage through the northern shallows that our own hydrographers believe impassable. A passage that would let a squadron enter this harbor without firing a shot past the batteries."

Her breath changed. Only that. He gave her credit for it.

"Now. There are those at headquarters who would say: the apprentice is complicit. The apprentice is hiding state property. The apprentice should be arrested tonight, and the workshop searched, and whatever is found in it taken by force." He spread his own hands, palms open, the soul of reason. "I am not among them. I am a great believer, Miss Thornbury, in the difference between a crime and a mistake. A young woman, grieving, loyal to her dead teacher's name, finishing his life's work because she cannot bear to let it die with him—that is not treason. That is love, badly aimed. And love badly aimed can be re-aimed."

"And if I refuse the aiming?"

The question came out level. Almost bored. He felt a flicker of genuine admiration, quickly filed away.

"Then it stops being a mistake," he said. "Let me be plain, because I respect you too much to wrap this in lace. Give me the atlas—not the drafts, not copies, the finished plates—and you will want for nothing. Protection. Patronage. Your name on the title page, printed in London, presented to the Admiralty under your own hand. They will call you the woman who mapped Menorca. You will never stand in that drafty workshop again. And should any awkward questions arise about your late master's correspondence"—he touched the torn letter, still lying by her plate, and slid it slowly toward her—"should anyone wonder why a drowned man's letters are circulating on this island—they will find the answers already buried. By me. For you."

The candles guttered. Somewhere below, in the town, a church bell rang the hour, and the harbor wind came down through the window gap smelling of salt and tar.

"And if I do nothing at all?" she asked. "If there is no atlas to give? If Master Renwick's work died with him?"

Hesse smiled, and let the smile carry everything he'd been holding back all evening—the charm draining off it like oil off water, leaving the metal beneath.

"Then I will be forced to conclude that you have been concealing it. And concealment of naval intelligence in wartime carries a sentence I needn't describe to a woman of your education. The gallows at San Felipe are quite visible from the harbor road. Sailors point them out to each other." He picked up the wine and topped off her glass, courteous to the last drop. "I don't want that. Truly. I have admired your work since before I knew your face. But Miss Thornbury—the century turns in nine days. On the first of January, my report goes to Palma regardless. What it says depends entirely on what you bring me."

He stood, signaling the end of the audience, and walked her to the door himself. At the threshold he paused, and lowered his voice as though offering a confidence between friends.

"One more thing, and then I release you to the night. The fragment you saw—I obtained it from a courier who was carrying letters out of this island. Letters in Renwick's hand. Which means either that your master writes posthumously, or that he never drowned at all." He watched her pupils, and saw the darkness widen there, and knew he had struck true. "If he lives, Miss Thornbury—if somewhere on this island a dead man is walking—then understand what that makes him. Not a romantic figure. A deserter who falsified his own death to keep intelligence from his King. There is a word for that, and it isn't *master*. Find him, or forget him. But know that whichever choice you make, I will be reading it in your face before you've finished making it."

He bowed. She curtsied again, that same measured inch, though he thought he detected the faintest tremor in it now, and he chose to interpret it as fear rather than fury. Fear was useful. Fury was merely interesting.

"Until the new year, Miss Thornbury."

"Major."

She went down the steps into the dark, and Hesse closed the door, and stood a moment with his hand flat against the wood, listening to her footsteps fade toward the town.

Then he rang for the Portuguese and ordered the rest of the bottle sent up to his study, where a ledger lay open on the desk. He dipped his pen and wrote, in a hand as neat and patient as an executioner's knot:

*Thornbury, L.—summoned, sounded, warned. Will act within ten days. Watch the workshop. Watch the harbor gate. And find me the man who owns that handwriting.*

He blotted the page, and outside, far out on the black water, the first firework of the season went up—a lone gold star, premature, climbing over the fortress walls and dying there, a bright thing briefly mistaken for a signal.
