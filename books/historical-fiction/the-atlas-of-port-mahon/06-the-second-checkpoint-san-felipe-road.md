## 6. The Second Checkpoint: San Felipe Road

*Lior*

The mule cart smelled of fish and tar, and beneath that—only to her—the ghost of linseed oil rising from the false coffin's seams. She had packed the atlas plates between sheets of oiled canvas, wedged them among broken sextants and a dead man's survey instruments, and nailed the lid shut with her own hands. Now she sat beside Callum on the bench, the reins loose in his fingers, and watched San Felipe rise out of the morning haze like something drawn in a hand less sure than Renwick's.

The hill road climbed in switchbacks, each one offering a view of the harbor below: blue, innocent, crowded with fishing boats pretending they had nowhere important to go. The fort's walls caught the early light and threw it back hard.

"Three hundred yards of open road before the gate," Callum said quietly. "Then the checkpoint proper. Two soldiers at the barrier, a corporal who checks manifests, a sergeant who likes to look inside things."

"How do you know the sergeant?"

"Because I was a sergeant once." He said it without pride or apology. "Men who have searched carts for contraband know where contraband hides."

"And where does it hide?"

"In plain sight, if the searcher is tired. In grief, if he is not." He glanced at her. "You'll wear your mourning. You'll say you're carrying instruments to Es Castell for the estate sale of a drowned surveyor."

"I am," Lior said. "That's the beauty of it. Every word true except the ones that matter."

Something moved behind his eyes—not quite a smile. "Renwick would be proud of that sentence."

She looked away, toward the fort. The wind off the sea carried salt and gunpowder smoke from the morning gun, and beneath her ribs her heart kept its own stubborn time. *He may be alive.* For two years she had inked coastlines for a dead man; now every coastline she drew might be a letter to him.

The cart crested the last rise. The gate stood ahead, and Lior saw the queue of wagons waiting, and saw too the thing Callum had not mentioned: a mounted patrol descending the far side of the hill, moving with the unhurried purpose of men following orders rather than roads.

* * *

*Callum*

He had counted the soldiers twice and made himself count them again, because the first count had frightened him and fear made bad arithmetic. Eight in the patrol. A search party, sweeping up from the customs road—Hesse had not waited politely after all.

"Officer coming," he murmured. "Don't look. Talk to me about the instruments."

Lior turned her face to him, and for a moment he forgot the patrol entirely. There was chalk dust still at her temple, and her eyes held the particular steadiness of people who work alone at night over small precise things. He thought: *she has no idea what she looks like when she's terrified and refuses to show it.*

"The theodolite is cracked along the altitude arc," she said clearly, loudly enough for anyone listening. "Master Renwick dropped it the winter before—" Her voice did not even waver on the name. "The winter before he drowned."

"Shame," Callum answered. "Fine instrument."

The patrol reached the cart. The officer reined alongside, a young lieutenant with a cold-weather flush on his cheeks and a paper in his glove.

"Manifest?"

"Survey instruments," Callum said. "Estate sale, Es Castell. The surveyor Aldous Renwick, drowned two years past."

The lieutenant's gaze sharpened like a compass needle finding north. "Renwick." He unfolded the paper. "There's an interest in that name. Major Hesse's interest." He looked down into the cart bed, at the coffin-shaped crate lashed under sacking. "What's in the box?"

"A dead man's tools going to market," Lior said. "Unless the Crown has begun taxing coffins, Lieutenant."

A soldier laughed before he could stop himself. The lieutenant flushed darker. "Open it."

Callum's mind went white and clear. He stepped down from the bench, positioning himself between the officer and the crate, and let his shoulders drop into the posture of a man too tired to fight. "Lieutenant, I've been on this road since dawn. If you want the lid off, help me lift it—but I'll tell you now there's a theodolite in there worth more than your commission, and if your men crack it prying, the widow will want it from your pay."

"There is no widow," the lieutenant said. "There's an apprentice." His eyes went to Lior. "Miss Thornbury."

And Callum understood, in the space of one heartbeat, that the trap had never been about the road at all. Hesse knew her name. The ledger at the customs house had done its quiet work. The lieutenant wasn't searching—he was collecting.

"Run," Callum said, very softly, in Menorquín, the old island tongue, the language soldiers rarely bothered to learn—and then he swung the iron tire lever off the cart bed and brought it across the nearest soldier's wrist, not to break bone but to buy noise, chaos, three seconds.

Three seconds was enough. Lior was already off the cart, skirts gathered, running for the goat track below the road. Callum followed, and behind them the world dissolved into shouting and a whistle shrieking the alarm down the length of the wall.

They ran through gorse and limestone. The track dropped steeply, treacherously, and Lior took it like someone who had walked it a thousand times drawing it. Behind them, hooves on stone.

Then the shot—a musket, fired badly downhill, the ball skipping off rock. Callum heard it strike before he felt it: a hot slap against his upper arm, spinning him half around. He fell, rolled, came up against a boulder, and pushed Lior bodily into the shadow of the rocks above the cave mouth he'd scouted yesterday and prayed he'd never need.

"In," he gasped. "In, in—"

She pulled him in after her by his good arm, and the darkness of the cave closed over them both like water.

* * *

*Lior*

The cave breathed cold and mineral. Beyond its mouth, thin daylight showed scrub and sky, and somewhere above, boots on stone, voices calling to one another in Spanish and English both, growing fainter as the search fanned eastward toward where they were not.

She tore Callum's sleeve open by feel. The graze ran along the outside of his arm, a furrow ploughed through skin, ugly but shallow. Blood soaked the cloth anyway; grazes always bled like mortal wounds, her father the surgeon used to say, as if flesh liked to exaggerate.

"Hold still." She bound it with strips of her petticoat, working fast, her hands steadier than she expected them to be. "You saved my life back there. Or at least my freedom, which is nearly the same."

"You saved mine first," he said. His voice had gone rough. "At the customs house. When you talked past that clerk—I watched you lie to a man who loved Renwick, and you did it kindly. That's a rare skill, Miss Thornbury. It frightens me how well you do it."

"Lior," she said. "If we're bleeding in caves together, we may as well use Christian names."

He almost smiled. Then the fever took the smile away.

It came on slowly, then all at once—shivering, then heat, his skin burning under her fingers while his teeth chattered. Wound fever, from a filthy musket ball and a dirtier sleeve. She found water seeping near the cave's rear wall and made him drink, wet his brow, kept him talking because she had learned long ago that men sliding into fever should not be allowed to slide quietly.

"Tell me something," she said. "Anything. Tell me about the crossing. Tell me how you found him."

His eyes were bright and unfocused. "Found him in Cádiz," he murmured. "Selling charts. His own charts, under another man's name, and hating every line of it." A breath. "He wrote to you. Did you read what he wrote? About the soundings?"

"He didn't write about the soundings."

"No," Callum whispered. "Because the soundings are why he died. Why he had to die. And why—" His head turned toward her, and though fever glazed his eyes, what lay underneath was lucid and terrible. "Why he sent me."

"To bring him home," Lior said. "To bring the atlas to him."

"To burn the atlas, Lior." The words came out level, each one placed like a stone. "Every plate. Every sounding. He sent me to destroy it. That route through the shoals—if the Navy takes it, they can run a fleet into Port Mahon in any weather, any hour. If the French take it, they can hold this island forever. Either way, men die by those numbers. He told me: nothing written should survive that kills so cleanly. Not even in her hands." His voice cracked. "*Especially* in her hands. He said she'd finish it. He said she was the only one alive good enough to finish it, and that was exactly why it must never be finished."

The cave was very silent. Water dripped somewhere in the dark, counting seconds she did not want counted.

"So the letters," she said at last, and her voice sounded like a stranger's, "were ransom for the atlas. Not proof of life. Proof of leverage."

"No." He caught her wrist with his burned arm, wincing, refusing to let go. "God, no. Listen to me. He loves you—that part was never a weapon. But he asked me to do a thing, and I let you believe otherwise, and I let myself believe otherwise, because—" He stopped. Swallowed. Fever burned high on his cheekbones. "Because watching you work over those plates, night after night, I wanted the atlas to live. I wanted *you* to finish it. I told myself destroying it was impossible, that the sea would take the plates first. I chose the lie because it was kinder to me."

She drew her wrist free. Outside, the light was changing, sliding toward afternoon; the searchers' voices had gone entirely.

"So which is it?" she asked quietly. "Do you serve the man who wants it burned? Or the woman who wants it finished?"

Callum looked at her for a long moment, firelight and fever making his face unreadable.

"That," he said, "is the question I've been carrying since Portsmouth. And I don't have the answer. But Hesse has your name in his ledger, and New Year's Eve is four days off—and whatever you decide, Lior Thornbury, you must decide it before the century turns."
