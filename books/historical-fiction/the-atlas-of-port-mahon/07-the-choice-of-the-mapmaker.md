## 7. The Choice of the Mapmaker

The workshop had never felt so much like a held breath.

Lior bolted the door at dusk and drew the shutters against the wind that came off the harbor in long grey shoves, rattling the glass in its leads as though something outside wanted in. Then she stood in the middle of the room with a candle in one hand and listened to her own heart doing its slow, stubborn work, and understood that everything now depended on what she did before dawn.

Callum's fever had broken near midnight, or so he claimed when she'd left him sleeping in the fisherman's loft above the old salt store, his shoulder wrapped in linen gone rust-brown at the fold. The graze was clean enough. The confession had been less clean. *Renwick sent me not to save the atlas but to burn it.* The words were still lodged in her like a splinter worked too deep to draw.

She set the candle on the drafting bench and uncovered the plates.

They lay beneath oilcloth in the order Aldous Renwick had drilled into her during six years of apprenticeship: north first, then east, then the deep-water approaches, then the harbor itself — Port Mahon rendered in ink so fine it seemed less drawn than whispered. Two years of his hand. Two years since they had pulled a drowned man's coat from the rocks below San Felipe and called it grief and closed the book. And all that time he had been alive somewhere across the water, writing letters in that unmistakable script while she learned to stop flinching at his empty chair.

She touched the edge of the harbor plate. The vellum was cool and faintly greasy under her fingertips, the way good skin feels.

"Three futures," she said aloud, because the silence needed breaking and there was no one left to hear her talk to herself. "Three futures and none of them mine."

* *

The first future sat folded inside her bodice: Hesse's letter, delivered by a corporal whose boots rang on the cobbles like a verdict. She took it out now and read it again by candlelight, though she knew every word.

*Miss Thornbury — Your talents deserve better than obscurity. Come to dinner tomorrow evening. Bring what your late master left unfinished, and I will see you honored, protected, and paid. Fail to come, and I fear I cannot answer for what the courts may conclude regarding the disposition of Crown property.*

He wrote charmingly. That was the horror of it. Hesse could make gallows sound like a garden party.

If she surrendered the atlas, she lived. That was the arithmetic of it, plain as a scale bar. She lived, and Callum's wound was treated by a proper surgeon instead of fisherman's needlework, and the plates went into the hands of men who would read them the way wolves read a sheepfold. Every sounding, every channel, every depth of the finest natural harbor in the Mediterranean laid open for the Navy's use. Renwick had spent two years and a faked death keeping those numbers from both Crowns. She would undo it over a dinner and a compliment, and live comfortably ever after with the taste of it in her mouth.

She thought of the clerk at the Customs House, the one who had known Renwick, whose eyes had lingered on her coffin-shaped crate a moment too long. She thought of Hesse's ledger with her name entered in it in his neat, patient hand. He had already begun writing her story. The question was whether she would let him finish it.

"No," she told the letter. It seemed important to say it aloud. "No."

She fed the page to the candle flame and watched it curl, blacken, and die in the dish, smelling briefly of tallow and spite.

That settled nothing, but it felt magnificent.

* *

The second future required fire of a more serious kind.

Renwick's own instruction, relayed through Callum's fever-cracked voice in the cave above the road: *Burn it, Lior. All of it. If I am dead, if we are taken, if there is no other way — burn it. A chart that saves ten ships can sink a hundred, and I will not have my work used as a knife.* He had meant it. She could hear him meaning it, could see him standing at this very bench with his sleeves rolled and ink to his second knuckle, saying it in that dry voice that made conviction sound like bookkeeping.

She gathered the plates anyway, all eleven, and carried them to the great stone hearth where the winter's kindling waited in a basket. It would take minutes. The vellum would catch fast; the ink might even flare blue-green, copper in the flames, a small aurora for an audience of one. Afterward there would be ash, and after the ash there would be freedom of a sort — Hesse furious, perhaps, suspicious certainly, but holding nothing. No atlas. No weapon. No proof of anything except that Lior Thornbury had obeyed her master one final time.

She knelt at the hearth with the harbor plate in her hands and could not do it.

Not from cowardice — she examined herself for cowardice the way she checked a rule for warp, turning the feeling over, testing its edges, and found it absent. What stayed her hand was something else. It was the plate itself. Because she had finished it. Two nights before Callum ever knocked on the door, she had inked the last contour of the last shoal, and in finishing it she had seen what Aldous had perhaps never allowed himself to say plainly: that these charts were beautiful, and beauty argues back. You do not burn a cathedral because soldiers might billet in it. You lock the doors. You change the locks.

And there was something else, lower and truer, that she admitted only grudgingly, kneeling on cold stone with firelight on her face. If she burned the atlas, she burned him too — the two years of his hidden life, the letters, the stubbornness, the whole secret architecture of a man who had chosen to be dead rather than complicit. Ash was honest, ash was safe, but ash forgave nothing and preserved nothing. She was not ready to preside over his second funeral. Once had been enough.

She rose, knees aching, and carried the plates back to the bench.

"Not yet," she told the ghost who haunted the chair by the window. "You taught me better than obedience."

* *

The third future was the one Callum had argued for in the cave, fever notwithstanding: finish it, sail it free, let the truth go out into the world untampered. Publish the atlas as Renwick made it, complete and exact, and let every nation, every merchant house, every navy under heaven read the same truth simultaneously. Truth as tide — no man owns it, no man commands it.

It was noble. She turned it over lovingly, the way she'd once turned over his brass parallel rule. And it was a fantasy dressed in nobility, which was worse than a lie. The atlas finished and freed would not arrive everywhere at once. It would arrive somewhere first — aboard whatever brig Callum chartered, through whichever port, into whichever hands got there soonest — and soonest meant someone, and someone meant advantage, and advantage meant the same wolfish arithmetic wearing a different coat. Renwick had known it. That was why he had chosen death over delivery. The third future was simply the first future with better manners.

So she stood at the bench with the candle guttering and the wind worrying the shutters, three futures laid out before her like discarded drafts — surrender, ash, escape — and felt the peculiar clarity that comes when every offered road is wrong. Her master had taught her drafting, inking, the mathematics of projection. But what he had really taught her, without ever naming it, was this: when the given projections fail, you invent a new one. That was what a mapmaker was. Someone who refused to accept that the world could only be represented the ways it had always been represented.

Lior pulled the harbor plate toward her and looked at it for a long time.

Then she began, very quietly, to smile.

* *

The alteration would have to be perfect, and perfection would have to look like age.

She started with the soundings — the depths, the atlas's beating heart, the numbers any naval officer would seize first. A chart lies best in its truths. So she kept the outer approaches immaculate, every fathom true as scripture, letting Hesse's hydrographers verify and verify and grow confident. The lie would live closer in, in the inner channels where the water narrowed and the rocks waited. There she shifted the deep channel a cable's length east, toward the shoal ground off Illa del Rei, where the true water shallowed treacherously but where her false lines would promise eight fathoms of forgiving dark. A ship trusting her chart would come in confident and touch bottom hard enough to break its own keel — within sight of the harbor, within reach of the fortress guns. Not a reef invented; merely a reef relocated. The sea itself would enforce the error. No forged document ever had so honest an accomplice.

The beacon bearings came next. She recalculated them off the altered channel, each bearing consistent with each false sounding, so that the whole web of numbers confirmed itself. A single lie is caught; a self-consistent lie becomes a world. She worked until her hand cramped and her eyes burned, dipping her pen, wiping it, checking the line weight against Renwick's original strokes until her forgery of his forgery was indistinguishable even to her.

Near dawn she came to the harbor mouth itself and paused.

Here was the deepest question. Here the chart could kill or could keep whole, depending on her nerve. And here Lior Okonkwo — no, Lior Thornbury, apprentice, orphan, keeper of a dead man's secrets — made the decision that separated craft from vengeance. She did not falsify the harbor entrance beyond rescue. She built into it a margin: shallow enough to punish a hostile fleet arriving in arrogance, but passable, barely, to a local pilot with local knowledge and a calm sea. The Menorquins who fished these waters every day of their lives would read her altered chart, shrug, and steer by memory as their fathers had. Only strangers would suffer. The map would protect its own people and betray its invaders, silently, permanently, requiring no garrison and firing no shot.

Grief, she discovered, makes excellent ink. It flows dark and dries fast and holds its line.

She worked through the small hours, and somewhere in the middle of it she stopped working alone. She could feel him at her shoulder — not the ghost of the drowned man they'd buried in rumor, but the living Aldous, the one from the letters, and she spoke to him as she worked, low, unembarrassed, the way she'd spoken to him across this bench for six years.

"You wanted it corrected," she murmured, laying a false fathom-line along the eastern channel. "Well. Corrected. Just not the direction you meant." A pause; the wind filled it. "You'll be furious. Good. Fury means you're alive to be furious."

The candle burned down to a thumb's width. Outside, the sky over the harbor went from black to iron to the pale bruised gold of the year's last morning. New Year's Eve. Somewhere below, Port Mahon was waking to festival — lanterns going up along the Moll, wine being hauled from cellars, the whole town preparing to be loud and drunk and gloriously difficult to police. Fireworks at midnight, ordered by the garrison itself, a display of imperial confidence that would fill the streets with smoke and confusion and ten thousand bodies no soldier could search.

Ten thousand bodies. One of them hers, with an atlas under her cloak.

At the eastern window, as the light strengthened, Lior saw the brig at anchor in the outer roads — the one Callum had hired, riding quiet among the fishing boats, waiting on a tide and a decision. And nearer, at the corner of the quay where the customs officers drank their morning coffee, she saw the red coat that did not move on, the broad shoulders turned toward her street, the patient face lifted to count her lit windows.

Major Hesse, keeping his appointment.

Lior rolled the plates into a cylinder no wider than her forearm, wrapped them in oilskin, bound them with cord, and slid the whole weight of two years' love and labor down inside her bodice, against her heart, where it beat with her. She banked the fire, erased every trace of the night's work, and took down her cloak.

One checkpoint left between her and the century. She meant to walk through it as though she owned the ground.
