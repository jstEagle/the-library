## 8. The Third Checkpoint: The Harbor Gate

The last night of the century came down on Port Mahon like a held breath finally let go.

Lior felt it in her bones as she crossed the Esplanade, the corrected plates strapped flat against her body beneath a borrowed greatcoat, their edges pressing her ribs like a second skeleton. All day the town had been building toward midnight—lanterns strung from balcony to balcony across the Carrer de ses Moreres, wine barrels broached on the quay, fishermen's daughters braiding orange blossom into each other's hair. The Spanish families who had stayed through two years of occupation celebrated the turning of the year with a defiant, glittering grief, and the British garrison, ordered to tolerate it, had turned out anyway in their best coats to drink and be seen.

Above it all, on every wall and gatepost, the notices: *By order of Major Hesse, all persons passing the harbor gate after dusk are subject to search. Reward for information concerning seditious charts.*

She had read them that morning with a strange calm. Fear, she had learned, was like ink—it could be worked until it dried into something else entirely.

Callum walked three paces behind her, his arm still stiff from the graze at San Felipe Road, a wineskin slung at his hip and the loose, laughing walk of a man going nowhere in particular. They did not look at each other. They had not spoken above a whisper since dawn, when they had agreed on the only plan left: there was no cleverness remaining, no disguise good enough, no route around the gate. There was only the crowd, the fireworks, and the truth of walking straight through the middle of everything.

The harbor opened before them, black water pricked with a hundred reflected lights. The *Resolución*, a merchant brig bound for Algiers with the tide, rode at anchor past the mole, her lanterns burning at her mastheads—a ship waiting for cargo that might never reach her.

* * *

Major Hesse stood beneath the arch of the harbor gate with his hands clasped behind his back and the patience of a man who had set a trap and now required only the world to oblige him.

He had dressed plainly for the occasion, almost modestly—the blue coat without its gold braid—as though tonight were not a spectacle but an errand. Four soldiers flanked the gate. Two more lounged against the customs shed, too casually. He had doubled them since noon. He knew the apprentice would come tonight; the atlas would sail or burn before the century turned, and neither he nor anyone else who understood maps would allow that tide to pass unwatched.

"Sir," said Sergeant Doyle, nodding down the Esplanade. "The girl. In the grey coat."

Hesse saw her at once. She moved through the festival crowd the way water moves through rock—not hurrying, not hiding, simply finding the path of least resistance with an unerring instinct. He felt something almost like admiration, and put it away where he kept such things.

"Let her approach," he said. "No one touches her until I give the word."

* * *

The gate rose ahead of Lior, its arch black against the fireworks just beginning to climb from the mole—gold chrysanthemums bursting over the water, raining sparks onto the harbor like a sky deciding to fall.

Twenty yards. Ten. She could see Hesse's face now, lit from below by a lantern, wearing the courteous expression she remembered from dinner—that careful, intelligent smile that had made her skin crawl precisely because there was nothing behind it but arithmetic.

"Miss Thornbury." His voice carried easily under the arch, pitched to be heard by everyone. "What a night for a walk."

"I'm going to watch the ships," she said. "Everyone is."

"Everyone is." He stepped aside, gesturing graciously at the passage beyond—and then, softly, so that only she could hear it: "Or you're going to make me a famous man. Open her coat."

A soldier's hands seized her shoulders. Another reached for the buttons of the greatcoat, and Lior stood very still and thought, with absurd clarity, of the smell of Renwick's workshop—linseed and lamp-black—and how she would carry that smell with her wherever they sent her, even to the gallows, even—

"Major Hesse!"

The voice came out of the crowd like a blade coming out of a sheath.

The soldiers froze. The crowd near the gate went quiet in spreading rings, silence rolling outward through the noise of the festival the way a stone rolls outward through water. And through it, pushing past a fishwife and a drummer boy, came a tall man in a plain seaman's coat, grey at the temples, with a face Lior had spent two years learning from engravings and one impossible week learning from letters.

Master Aldous Renwick walked up to the lantern light and stopped, and took off his hat.

"You are arresting my apprentice," he said, "for a crime I committed. I suggest you arrest me instead."

Hesse did not move. For a long moment the major's composure held—and then Lior watched something extraordinary cross his face: not shock exactly, but recognition, the look of a cartographer seeing a coastline redrawn before his eyes. "Renwick," he said quietly. "Drowned off Fornells. Confirmed dead. There are papers."

"There are papers," Renwick agreed. "I wrote most of them myself." He glanced at Lior—one long look, warm and grave and full of things that could not be said here—and then back to Hesse. "The atlas you want exists. I am the man who drew it. Whatever charge you bring, bring it against me."

"It doesn't matter whose name is on it," Hesse said, recovering himself, his voice hardening into command. "Seize them both. Search her."

But the crowd had other ideas.

It began, Lior thought afterward, with the fireworks—a great rocket battery on the mole loosed all at once, whether by accident or design she never learned, and the night tore open in white and crimson thunder. In that roar Callum Vey moved. She saw him only in flashes: a shoulder driving into the lantern stand, the gate plunged into strobing half-dark; the wineskin arcing over the soldiers' heads and shattering; the crowd, drunk on wine and century's-end and grievance, surging forward not because anyone shouted a word of instruction but because crowds know a moment when they are handed one. Bells began ringing from the church of Santa María—festival bells, or perhaps not festival bells. Soldiers found their arms full of celebrating strangers. A mule cart overturned, deliberately, across the mouth of the Esplanade.

"Lior!" Callum's voice, raw. "The plates! Go!"

She ran. Under the arch, through the smoke and the falling gold fire, past a sergeant grappling with a man dressed as the old year itself, straw horns flying. Behind her she heard Hesse shouting orders that the night swallowed whole, heard Renwick's voice, calm as a reading aloud, saying words she couldn't catch—buying her seconds with his own name, spending his resurrection like coin.

The quay. The water. A boat waiting at the steps with a boy at the oars, and Callum appearing beside her out of the chaos, bleeding again from somewhere new, laughing, lifting the plates' weight from her shoulders as if it were nothing, as if the last month were nothing.

They pulled for the brig with the fireworks dying overhead and the first minutes of the new century gathering in the dark like weather.

And Lior knelt in the stern, watching the gate shrink behind them, searching the crowd along the mole for one grey-templed face—finding it, at last, at the edge of the lantern light, standing very still among the running soldiers.

He raised his hand. Not farewell. She understood it suddenly, the way she understood a contour line at a glance: *come and finish what we started.*

Then the brig's anchor came up, and Port Mahon began to slide away into the dark, carrying Major Hesse, the gallows he'd built, and the old century with it—and Aldous Renwick stepped out of the crowd at the foot of the gangway ladder, breathing hard, alive, and looked down at her in the boat as though she were the chart and he the man finally learning to read her.

"The atlas," he said. "Show me what you've done to my maps."
