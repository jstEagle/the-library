## 9. The Century Turns

The brig *Cormorant* leaned into the ebb like a tired animal leaning into a fence, and Aldous Renwick stood at her rail with the century dying behind him.

Midnight came to Port Mahon the way it always did on New Year's Eve — with gunpowder. From the water he could see the whole amphitheatre of the great harbor lit in bursts of gold and green, fireworks cracking above San Felipe's dark mass on one shore and the English town's crooked roofs on the other, and between them the crowd at the harbor gate had become a single seething animal, all noise and lantern-light and festival chaos. He could not see Lior. The distance was too great, the night too thick with smoke and sparks. But he knew she was there. He felt it the way an old surveyor feels weather coming in his ruined knee: as certainty, as pressure, as pain that meant the world was about to change shape.

He had watched her walk toward the gate with the atlas wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and he had thought, absurdly, of all the mornings in the workshop when she had bent over his copper plates with her lower lip caught between her teeth, inking coastlines by lamplight while the wind worried at the shutters. She had been twenty then, all wrists and stubbornness, correcting his soundings with a boldness that should have offended him and never had. *You've drawn this reef a cable's length too far east,* she'd said once, tapping his own chart with her own finger. And she'd been right. He had known it within the hour and said nothing, because saying something would have required admitting what he was already beginning to fear — that the apprentice would outgrow the master, that the work would one day belong more to her hands than to his.

Two years dead, and here he stood, alive enough to feel the cold bite through his coat.

* * *

Callum Vey found him at the rail before the last of the fireworks died. The young man moved with the careful gait of a man whose side still remembered a soldier's bullet, one arm pressed against his ribs, salt crusted in the stubble on his jaw. He did not speak at first. He simply came to stand beside Renwick, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, and for a long moment they both watched the smoke drift out over the black water where the century was dissolving.

"She made it through," Callum said finally. His voice was rough, scraped raw by fever and by things he had not yet said aloud. "Hesse had the arrest warrant in his hand. I saw it."

"And?"

"And then you stepped out of the crowd like a ghost stepping out of its own grave." A short, disbelieving breath that might have been a laugh. "I have carried those letters across two seas, Mr. Renwick. I have been shot for them. And I still don't entirely believe you're standing here."

"I'm not entirely certain of it myself." Renwick turned his collar up against the wind. Below them, the deck crew worked in silence, casting off the last lines, and the *Cormorant* began to gather way, sliding away from the mole and into the current that would carry her past the forts, past the guns, out through the channel his charts had mapped and his conscience had unmade. "Where is she?"

"On board. Asleep, or pretending to be, in the cabin aft." Callum hesitated. "She doesn't know yet what you're going to tell her. About why."

"No," Renwick said. "She doesn't." He looked down at his own hands, ink-stained even now, even after everything — the stain so deep in the whorls of his fingers that no lye or seawater had ever fully lifted it. "And I find that I am afraid of telling her. At my age, Vey, that is a humiliating discovery."

* * *

They found her awake after all, sitting in the narrow cabin with a single lamp burning low and the oilcloth bundle across her knees, her fingers moving over its wrappings the way a mourner's fingers move over a closed coffin lid. When Renwick ducked through the doorway and straightened himself in the lamplight, she went utterly still — the stillness of a wild thing deciding whether to bolt — and then she rose, slowly, and struck him across the face with an open hand.

The blow rang in the small space like a shot. Callum, in the doorway, wisely said nothing.

"That," Lior said, and her voice shook only on the first word, "is for letting me bury you."

"Yes," Renwick said. His cheek burned. It was, he thought, less than he deserved.

"And this—" But the second blow never came. Her hand hovered in the air between them and then curled shut, and dropped, and pressed flat against her sternum as if holding something in. Her eyes were wet and furious and searching his face for the forgery in it, the flaw in the plate, the error she could correct. He let her look. There was nothing false to find; he had spent two years making certain of that.

"You wrote to me," she whispered. "Through him. You wrote letters in your own hand and let me read them and grieve them, knowing—"

"Knowing that grief is honest, and relief can be manufactured, but only grief would keep your hands steady on the plates." He said it plainly, because she deserved plainness. "I needed the atlas finished, Lior. And I needed it finished by someone who loved it enough to be angry at it."

"The atlas is finished." She knelt and unwrapped the oilcloth with hands that were no longer shaking, and there they lay in the lamplight: the copper plates, the finished proofs, the corrected soundings — *her* corrections, he realized as he leaned closer, and his breath caught. "Finished, and altered. Look."

He looked.

At first he saw only his own work, his own coastline, the harbor he had drawn a hundred times until he could have drawn it blindfolded, drunk, dying. But no — along the eastern approach, where his original soundings had marked the deep channel that a hostile squadron could thread under cover of darkness, the numbers had changed. Not erased. *Changed.* Subtly, plausibly, with the confident hand of a working mapmaker: depths shifted by fathoms, a shoal extended, a false passage drawn where the true one ran. Any enemy captain trusting these charts would ground his ship on sand that did not exist and miss the water that did. And the alteration was beautiful — undetectable, seamless, the work of a master, not an apprentice covering mistakes.

"You didn't burn it," he said quietly.

"He asked me to destroy it." She glanced at Callum, who stood in the doorway with his jaw tight. "Yes. I know what he confessed in the cave. I know what you sent him to do. But I couldn't burn it, Aldous. Burning felt like another kind of lie — destroying the truth instead of correcting it. So I chose a third thing. I made the atlas lie *for* us instead of against everyone. Hesse believes these plates are the true ones. By morning they'll be in his possession, and by spring every admiral in the fleet will be navigating by your drowned man's masterpiece." Her mouth curved, and there was grief in it, and triumph, and something older than both. "Let them run aground on my imagination."

For a long moment Renwick could not speak. He stood over the plates as a man stands over a grave that has opened and given back something living, and he understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, that the thing he had spent two years protecting — hiding, faking his death for, sending a stranger to destroy — had never been his at all. It had only ever passed through his hands.

"I stayed dead," he said at last, "because I believed the atlas was a weapon that had to be kept from falling into any empire's grip, including ours. I believed the only clean act left was destruction." He touched the edge of the nearest plate, the cold copper, the incised line of the coast. "But destruction is just surrender wearing a nobler coat. You understood what took me sixty years and a death to learn. A map is not the truth and never was. A map is an argument. And you—" his voice failed briefly, and he mastered it, "you have made the better argument."

"Aldous—"

"Kneel down," he said.

"What?"

"Kneel, girl, before I lose my nerve entirely." He waited until she sank onto the cabin bench, bewildered, the lamp throwing her shadow huge against the curved bulkhead. Then Aldous Renwick, who had buried a wife, outlived his rivals, survived his own obituary, and faced the gallows without flinching, lowered himself — stiffly, painfully, with the groan of an old man's joints — to one knee on the pitching deck of a brig leaving Port Mahon, and took his apprentice's ink-stained hand in both of his own.

"I named this work the Atlas of Port Mahon," he said. "I signed it. I guarded it. I died for it. And none of that gave me ownership of it, because a map belongs to whoever sees truly through it. These are not my corrections. They are yours. This is not my atlas anymore. It never will be again." He pressed her hand between his palms, and his voice, when it continued, was very quiet. "I acknowledge you as master of this work, Lior Thornbury — not apprentice, not inheritor, not keeper of a dead man's legacy. Master. What you do with it is yours alone, and whatever you do, I will call it right."

Her tears fell onto the copper plates, onto the harbor they had both loved, and she did not wipe them away. "You let me love a ghost," she said. "That was cruel."

"It was cowardice dressed as strategy, and I will answer for it." He rose, joints protesting, and smiled at her — a real smile, unguarded, the first in two years. "But know this also, since we are confessing tonight: I sent Callum to you carrying my letters because I could not bear the thought of the work ending with me, and somewhere in the writing of them, I could not bear the thought of *you* ending with the work. That was selfish. Love usually is. But I ask nothing back from you, Lior. Not gratitude, not forgiveness, not — anything else. A master owes her work nothing but truth, and I intend to pay mine."

Above them, boots crossed the deck; the watch called the hour; the *Cormorant* heeled gently as she took the open sea's long swell, leaving the century and its wars astern.

Lior looked down at the atlas — at the harbor rendered in copper and correction, at her own hand hidden inside his signature — and then lifted her head, and Renwick saw in her face the expression he had seen only once before, years ago, when she'd found his reef drawn wrong: the look of a person who has just discovered the world is larger than its maps.

"There's one thing you haven't explained," she said. "The letters you sent ahead — to your family. Callum says they're already aboard."

Renwick exchanged a glance with Callum, who had gone carefully expressionless in the doorway.

"They are," Renwick said. "Which raises a question I've been avoiding all evening, and which I suspect you, being who you are, have already begun to answer."

Lior's eyes narrowed. "What question?"

"The question of what name goes on the title page," Renwick said, "when the corrected atlas is published — and who, exactly, the world will be told drew it."
