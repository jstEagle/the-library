## 10. A New Projection

The studio above the harbor had been a sailmaker's loft once, and the smell of tar and hemp still lived in its beams no matter how many times I scrubbed them. I liked it that way. A room should remember what it was before you gave it a new name.

One year, nearly to the day. The fireworks of last New Year's Eve were ash and memory now, but every morning when I opened the shutters, the harbor laid itself out below me like a confession—fishing boats first, then the merchantmen riding at anchor with their lazy sway, then, far out past the Mole, the grey silhouettes of His Majesty's ships, patient as creditors. Port Mahon was said to be the finest natural harbour in all the Mediterranean. I had spent two years learning exactly how fine, and how dangerous, and for whom.

On the long table beneath the north window lay the atlas.

Not Renwick's plates—I had sent those to their rest months ago, burned in the courtyard stove on a windless evening, feeding each one in slowly so the ink flared green before it blackened. What lay on my table now was mine: forty-one charts, re-engraved through the winter by lamplight, corrected not merely in soundings but in intention. Where his original had shown the Navy a deep-water channel threading past San Felipe's guns, mine showed the truth as it served the harbor rather than the empire—the shoals restored where he had smoothed them away, the false passage struck through with deliberate, elegant hatching, as though the error had never existed at all. A map is an argument, Aldous had written once, in the letter I kept. I am only giving it your voice now.

Below the title cartouche, where his name would have gone in letters a hand-span tall, mine read: *Surveyed and Engraved by Lior Thornbury, Port Mahon.* No *apprentice*. No *after the manner of the late Master Renwick*. Just the work, and the name, standing together like two people who have decided to trust each other.

I had signed it in April and regretted it by May and understood it by June. That was the usual rhythm of any honest line drawn on paper.

* * *

The printing was done in the old customs press off Ses Voltes, run by a widow named Frau Costa who had lost her husband to the yellow fever and her patience to everyone else, and who nevertheless handled my copperplates like relics. She printed fifty copies. Thirty went to harbormasters along the coast from Barcelona to Livorno; ten were wrapped in oilcloth for the Admiralty, because a lie told openly to power is sometimes safer than one concealed; and the last ten I kept stacked beside my desk, smelling of ink and size, waiting for hands I could not yet imagine.

The letters I posted in September.

There had been eleven of them in the packet Callum carried out of Algiers two years ago—or twelve, counting the one Renwick had marked *for the girl, when she needs it*, which I did not send anywhere, which lived in the drawer of my drafting table beneath the good magnifier, read so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. But the others—the ones to his sister Margaret in Plymouth, to the nephew he'd never met, to the old surveyor Dansey in Gibraltar—I copied the direction onto fresh covers in my best italic hand and walked them down to the post myself, because some errands should be done on foot, unhurried, in daylight.

Margaret wrote back within two months. Her letter crossed the water smelling faintly of lavender, and it began, *You will forgive me if I do not know whether to thank you or grieve, for I have spent three years mourning a man who was apparently too busy being alive to inform me.* Then, further down, after pages I read four times: *He speaks of you in every letter. He says you finished what he could only begin. Lior—he does not say "the atlas." He says "her atlas." Do not let him take that back from you.*

I folded her letter into the drawer beside his, and I did not cry until the lamp was out, and even then only a little, and I counted it a fair trade.

* * *

Callum came back on the third of November, with the sirocco behind him and salt dried white on the shoulders of his coat.

I heard him on the stairs before I saw him—that particular uneven tread, left foot landing heavier than right since the bullet at San Felipe Road—and I made myself keep engraving. The burin moved through the metal in a long clean curve while my heart conducted its own unruly business below my ribs. Some things a mapmaker learns: that anticipation distorts scale, that what you wait for always appears larger than it is, until you fix it in place and measure it properly.

He filled the doorway the way weather fills a horizon. Broader than I remembered, greyer at the temples, a new scar splitting one eyebrow, and smiling before he said anything, which was unlike him, which meant the news was good.

"You've kept the light burning," he said. "I could see it from the road above Es Castell. Thought the whole island must know where you worked."

"The whole island knows," I agreed. "That was rather the point."

He came in slowly, the way men come into churches they half remember. He looked at the atlas open on the table—at my hatching, my corrections, my name—and took off his hat without being asked, which in Callum Vey was a whole speech.

"It's done, then."

"It's done. Fifty copies. Three already seized by French agents in Barcelona, which Frau Costa says is the highest compliment a printer can receive."

"And Hesse?"

"Promoted to Gibraltar, God help the Rock. He wrote to me in June. Four pages. He called my atlas 'a monument to judicious revision.'" I turned the plate toward the window so the light caught the engraved shoals. "I believe that was a threat wearing a compliment's coat. I wore it anyway."

Callum laughed—a short, real sound—and then went quiet, working up to something. I knew his silences the way I knew weather fronts. I set down the burin and gave him my whole attention, because he deserved it, and because I wanted to watch him say whatever it was.

"There are harbors," he said. "Safe ones. I've spent this year running routes for friends of Renwick's—friends of ours, I suppose, now. There's a cove south of Palermo where nobody asks names. A chandler in Trieste who owes more debts than he counts. You could publish there under any sky you liked. No garrison at your door. No ledger with your name in it." He stopped. Started again, rougher. "Lior. I'm asking you to leave with me. I've been asking myself for eight months and I've finally got the words straight."

Outside, a bell buoy clanked against the Mole. Somewhere below us a fishwife was quarreling gloriously about sardines. The ordinary harbor sounds of an ordinary afternoon, and I let them fill the pause, because I wanted my answer to arrive in silence.

"Renwick taught me projections," I said. "Every flat map of a round world is a choice about what to distort. Mercator keeps the compass true and lies about the size of everything near the poles. You can preserve distance or shape or bearing, but never all three." I stood, and came around the table, and stood close enough to see the salt in the seams of his coat. "I spent years thinking belonging was something you found on another chart. A truer projection. Somewhere the distortion hurt less." I put my hand flat against his chest, over the scar I couldn't see but knew was there. "But I've measured it now, Callum. There isn't one. Every life distorts something. So you choose the distortion you can live inside, and you draw your lines there, and you make them beautiful."

"Is that a yes or a no?"

"That is a mapmaker telling you the territory." I smiled, and felt my face do something unguarded, and let it. "Stay. Not because Palermo isn't safe—but because I am not leaving the harbor I spent two years keeping. This is my center of bearing. Everything true radiates from here." My voice caught, and I steadied it against him. "Stay, and let me draw you into it."

For a man who had crossed the Mediterranean twice under false papers, Callum Vey took a long time answering a question with only one hard word in it. But when he did, he bent his head to mine, and the answer needed no ink at all.

* * *

Later, when the lamp was lit and he sat mending a sail needle's worth of his coat by my fire, I opened the drafting-table drawer and took out the twelfth letter—the one marked *for the girl*—and read it again by the small flame, though I knew it entire:

*If you are reading this, then the atlas is finished and you finished it, and I am either dead in earnest or dead in the only way that matters to the Crowns, which amounts to the same freedom. Do not mourn the master, Lior. Grief is a projection too—it flattens what it loves. Keep the living dimensions instead: the harbor you defended, the name you earned, and whoever is sitting across the fire from you the night you read this. If it is Vey, tell him he owes me a bottle of the good Maltese, and that I always saw how he looked at you, and that a man who watches a woman fall in love with someone else's letters and says nothing has more courage than any soldier I ever surveyed.*

I laughed, once, aloud, and Callum looked up from his needle.

"What?"

"He knew." I folded the letter along its softened creases. "He knew everything, a year before we did."

"Of course he knew." Callum returned to his mending, but I heard the smile in it. "He drew you, didn't he? Men draw what they understand."

* * *

The atlas went on sale from the loft on the first of December, five shillings the copy, and by Christmas the harbormasters' replies had begun arriving—gratitude from Mahón's pilots, a furious marginal note from a hydrographer in Cádiz, and one letter with no signature containing only a pressed sprig of rosemary and the words *Well surveyed. Well revised. — A.R.*, which I pinned above my desk where the north light would find it every morning.

On New Year's Eve I climbed to the roof with Callum, and we watched the fireworks go up over the Mole, gold and green and white, and their reflections shattered and reassembled in the black water below, over channels I had sounded and shoals I had restored and a passage I had closed forever.

"One year," Callum said.

"One year." Below us, the harbor held its breath between explosions, then exhaled boats and bells and singing. "Ask me again in another one."

"I'll ask you every one there is."

And far out beyond the breakwater, just visible in the burst of the next rocket, a brig was standing into the roads flying no colors I recognized—new-built, fast-lined, her deck crowded with figures—and she was making directly for Port Mahon, and she was showing no intention of stopping.
