## 2. Last Train South

There is a particular arithmetic to a government in its final week. The ledgers stop balancing, the clerks stop coming, and the only figures anyone still trusts are the ones written on small pieces of paper that can be swallowed, burned, or sewn into a coat lining. I had spent four years measuring my life by what fit into a leather satchel — two changes of shirt, a fountain pen with a cracked nib I refused to replace because it wrote the way a tired man speaks, and whatever the ministry had decided was too important to let fall into the wrong hands. On the morning they put me on the last southbound train, the satchel weighed eleven pounds, and six of those were paper that could kill a division if read correctly.

The instructions had been delivered to me in the basement of the foreign ministry by a deputy undersecretary whose name I will protect here, not out of loyalty but because I have genuinely forgotten it. He had the look of a man who had been crying earlier and had made peace with it the way one makes peace with weather.

"Half you memorize," he said, laying the cipher books open on a crate of diplomatic brandy nobody would ever drink. "Half you burn. You choose which half is which. We've split them so no single courier carries everything."

"And if I'm searched?"

"You're a commercial attaché traveling to visit vineyard interests. Here." He handed me a letter of introduction so bland it was practically an insult. "Vineyards are good. Nobody interrogates a man about grapes."

I wanted to tell him that nobody interrogates anybody anymore; they simply shoot first and file the paperwork later, and even the paperwork has stopped being filed. But he was already moving down the row of crates, distributing satchels to three other couriers, and I understood that we were each of us a small fire waiting for our match. I shook his hand. His palm was damp. So was mine. It was that kind of republic by then.

* * *

The train station smelled of coal smoke, wet wool, and the specific panic of people who have waited too long to leave. There were perhaps four hundred souls pressed onto a train built for two hundred, and every one of them had performed the same miracle of compression: whole households folded into a suitcase, family silver wrapped in underwear, photographs tucked against the body like second hearts. A woman near the steps was trying to board with a birdcage covered in a tablecloth, and when a conductor told her the cage counted as luggage and there was no room for luggage, she looked at him with such bottomless contempt that he stepped aside. Even now I believe she would have surrendered a child before the canary. Priorities clarify wonderfully at the end of things.

My escort — two soldiers, Corporal Bardi and a boy everyone called Sempre because he answered every order with *sempre, sempre*, always, always — got me aboard through the baggage door of a third-class carriage, which is to say they pushed me through a window while Bardi shouted that I was a diplomatic parcel and therefore exempt from physics. The carriage held perhaps forty people in space designed for twenty-four. I wedged myself onto a wooden bench between a priest who was asleep before the wheels moved and a grandmother who spent the entire journey shelling almonds into her apron with the serene industry of a woman who had survived three wars and intended to survive this one through sheer refusal of drama.

We pulled out at 4:17 in the afternoon. I know the time because I checked my watch against the station clock, and checking watches against official clocks was, in those years, a form of prayer.

For the first hour I did nothing but breathe. This sounds like idleness; it was strategy. A courier's first rule is that you do not touch the material until the environment stabilizes, because a man fumbling with papers in a crowded carriage announces himself to every eye in the county. Instead I watched. The priest slept with his breviary open on his chest like a small sleeping animal of its own. Across the aisle, a young man in a railwayman's cap read a newspaper dated four days old and turned the pages with great ceremony, as though the news might improve with attention. And opposite me, wedged in the corner seat with her back to the wall, sat a woman of perhaps thirty with dark hair cut short in a way that suggested either practicality or a recent escape from something, wearing a gray coat that had been good once and was now merely honest.

She was doing what I was doing: watching. Not nervously. The way a lighthouse watches. Her eyes went around the carriage in slow circuits — priest, grandmother, me, the window, the corridor, back again — and each circuit took exactly the same length of time. I have spent enough years among professionals to recognize a professional, and I filed her away under *later*, which is where couriers keep everything.

At the first tunnel I began to memorize.

The method is simple and horrible. You take a page of the cipher book — columns of number groups, the key tables, the authentication blocks — and you read it until the page exists behind your eyelids. Then you turn the page. The trick, if there is one, is rhythm. You recite in groups of five, tapping them out against your thigh under the coat, and you use the noise of the train as a metronome. Fourteen tunnels between the capital and the mountains, Bardi had told me. Fourteen chances to work undisturbed, because in a tunnel nobody can see your lips move.

By the third tunnel I had the primary table entire. By the fifth, I could recite the authentication block for the southern naval district forward and backward, which is a sentence no living person should be able to say, and which I can still say today, which tells you something about what memory does with the unforgivable.

The burning came later, after dark, and it was the most delicate operation of my professional life. You cannot burn paper on a crowded train without smoke, and smoke is a confession. But the little stove at the carriage's end had been lit against the mountain cold, and the grandmother had established squatter's rights over it, feeding it twigs and almond shells with the proprietary calm of a hearth goddess. Almond shells, it turns out, burn hot and fast and smell strongly enough to mask nearly anything. I waited until she opened her apron of shells, and I fed my first pages into her fire a handful at a time, curled tight, dry as autumn, gone in seconds. She never stopped shelling. At some point she looked up at me and said, "They take longer if you don't curl them," and returned to her work. I have thought about that woman many times since. Somewhere in the world there is a grave marked with her name, and I hope whoever tends it knows that she was, for one night, the most competent intelligence officer in the republic.

Page by page, the books went into the stove and the stove sent them up the chimney and over the mountains we were crossing toward, and by midnight half of everything the ministry still trusted was ash somewhere above the valley of the Lenza, drifting. The other half lived behind my eyes. Eleven pounds became five. My shoulders felt lighter and my skull felt heavier, and both sensations were correct.

* * *

It was during the seventh tunnel, or perhaps the eighth — the tunnels ran together after a while, long black throat, gasp of stars, black again — that the woman in the gray coat spoke to me for the first time.

"You count your taps in fours," she said quietly, without looking at me. "Most people do fives."

The train roared out into moonlight. Snowfields lay blue-white along the valley floor, and the river below carried plates of ice southward, all of us going the same direction.

"I don't know what you mean," I said.

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't." She turned her face to the window. In the glass I could see her reflection regarding mine, two ghosts sharing a compartment. "The man across the aisle reads yesterday's paper front to back and back to front and hasn't once reacted to anything in it. Your soldier friend checks the corridor every ninety seconds and always looks left first. And you," she said, "have been reciting something under your breath for six hours, and it isn't prayers, because priests move their lips differently. They ask forgiveness. You're asking permission."

I considered several responses. Denial, which would convince no one including possibly myself. Deflection, which requires an audience willing to be deflected. Or the third option, the one that lives in the chest like a coin in a pocket you keep touching: the truth, or some portion of it, offered to a stranger precisely because she is a stranger and cannot be trusted, and therefore, in some inverted ledger I have never fully reconciled, can be.

"What's in the paper?" I asked instead.

"Grain requisitions. A communiqué about a victory that happened in a different country than the one I read about last week. An obituary for a judge." She finally turned from the window. Up close her face was narrower than I'd guessed, with a small scar through one eyebrow, old and healed crooked. "His name was the same as yours. Marchetti. I wondered if the south of this country produces nothing but judges and men with your surname."

"My condolences to the family," I said. "Whoever they are."

"Mm." She studied me for a moment longer, and I felt the examination go past my face and into my coat, my hands, the shape of my silence. Then, deliberately, her gaze dropped to the satchel strap across my chest — to the buckle — and rested there exactly one second too long. One second is a long time. One second is an entire language.

I knew what she'd seen, or half-seen: the brass fitting stamped with the ministry's device, the crossed quill and tower that I had meant to cover with electrical tape and had not, because the deputy undersecretary's people had assured me the tape would arrive and the tape, like everything else that week, had not arrived. A courier carrying a sealed satchel with a visible ministry seal on a train full of four hundred frightened people was not a courier. He was a target wearing a sign.

"They'll search the train at Vado Terme," she said, conversationally, as though discussing the weather. "Checkpoint on the viaduct. Militia, not army. Army searches are thorough and fair. Militia searches are neither, and they're fond of the seal. It means ransom, or worse, depending on mood and how much wine is in the patrol."

"And you're telling me this why?"

She shrugged, a small economical movement, the shrug of someone who budgets her gestures. "Because you've been feeding pages to that woman's stove for three hours and she thinks you're warming almond cake. Because you tap in fours. Because—" and here she paused, and something moved behind her eyes that I would spend months learning to read, something that was not pity and not calculation, or was both at once, "—because somebody should get off this train with whatever you're carrying. That's the whole of my reasoning. Don't thank me. Thanking makes it complicated."

"I wasn't going to thank you. I was going to ask your name, which is more dangerous."

"Elena," she said. Then, after a pause in which I watched her decide, consciously, to give the rest: "Elena Voss."

"Marchetti. Xanthe Marchetti."

"I know," said Elena Voss. "The obituary was your uncle's colleague. I was joking about the judges." And almost — almost — she smiled, a brief thaw at the corner of her mouth, quickly re-frozen, like ice on a river that remembers spring and refuses it. "Sleep if you can. I'll watch the corridor. You count in fours; I'll wake you at eighty."

I want to be precise about what happened inside me at that moment, because precision is all I have ever had to offer. A stranger had just identified my trade, named my vulnerability, and offered to stand guard over my sleep — and every doctrine I had been taught said that this was either a trap or a test, and that in either case the correct response was polite withdrawal and a sleepless night. I did not withdraw. I did not sleep either, not properly, but I let my head rest against the cold window and I closed my eyes, and for stretches of minutes at a time I permitted myself to exist in a state I had not entered since the war began: the state of being watched over. It is astonishing how heavy a satchel becomes when you set it down. It is equally astonishing how light.

Somewhere past Vado Terme — we passed the checkpoint at a crawling halt while lanterns swung along the platforms and militia shapes moved down the line, and Elena woke me with two fingers on my wrist, exact as a clock, and I kept my eyes shut and breathed like a sleeper while boots passed within arm's reach and paused, paused, moved on — somewhere past that, I understood that I had done something unprecedented in my career. I had trusted. Not completely; trust, for my kind, is a currency you spend in fractions. But I had handed a stranger the vigil, and she had kept it, and the codes behind my eyelids were still mine alone, and yet the night felt less like a corridor with a firing squad at the end of it and more like a road.

The grandmother gave me a handful of almonds at dawn, unshelled, as wages, she indicated, for my assistance with the stove. The priest woke and blessed the carriage generally, catching us all like rain. And Elena Voss sat with her back to the wall, watching the country slide past — the terraced hills going white, the bridges getting smaller and stonier, the world narrowing toward the mountains as though the land itself were packing a bag.

It was full morning when the whistling started — not the train's whistle, but the thinner, falling sound that comes from the sky — and the carriage lurched as the driver opened the engine to everything she had. Elena was on her feet before I had finished understanding. She caught my eye across the rocking bodies, and she did not shout, and she did not pray. She said, very clearly, over the screaming of the wheels:

"The tunnel ahead. When we're inside, follow me."
