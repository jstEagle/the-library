## 3. The Road Through the Pass

The train died at half past nine in the morning, in a valley that had no name on any map I trusted, and it died the way most things were dying that winter — loudly at first, then very quietly.

I had been awake since four, not from fear but from arithmetic. The cipher books were sewn into the lining of my coat, twelve volumes of a republic's entire conversational memory, and I had spent the dark hours doing what I always did with unbearable things: dividing them into portions small enough to carry. A man can bear almost anything, I have found, provided he is allowed to count it first. Counting was my prayer. It had been my prayer since I was nine years old and my father put me on a boat in Trieste harbor and told me to memorize the number of crates going down the gangway so that later, when the lawyers asked, someone in the family would know the truth. There were forty-one crates. I never learned what happened to them. But I have carried forty-one around inside my skull for thirty years like a stone I swallowed on purpose.

So: twelve books, one satchel, two escorts, three days to the coast if the roads held, five if they didn't. I counted. I felt better. This is either a discipline or a disease; I have never been able to tell which, and by 1943 it hardly mattered.

The strafing came without ceremony. One moment the valley was doing its imitation of peace — a frozen river, some black cattle regarding our train with the contempt cattle reserve for all human machinery — and then there was a sound like the sky being unzipped, and the carriage ahead of ours lifted slightly, the way a man lifts his foot off a floor he has just realized is burning.

What followed was not chaos. Chaos implies a system breaking down. What we had was simpler: everyone discovering simultaneously that there had never been a system. The carriage ahead burned with a greenish flame that made the snow look yellow. People poured out of the windows — through them, actually, glass being an afterthought by then — and stood in the field in their good coats, holding hatboxes and children and, in one memorable case, a caged songbird that would not stop singing, which struck me as either courage or stupidity and which I have thought about every year since.

I got out through the corridor side, because I had learned in the ministry that you always leave a building by the wall least likely to be photographed. My satchel went over my shoulder first, my body second. Priority of cargo. If anyone had asked me afterward whether I had helped anyone off the train, I would have had to say that I checked, quickly and thoroughly, that no one needed helping *in my immediate vicinity*, and then moved. Grief, I have decided, is simply efficiency that has not yet found the right words.

* * *

The two soldiers who had been assigned to me at the last station — Corporal Bruni, who was twenty and built like a question mark, and Sergeant Ottonello, who was fifty and built like an answer nobody wanted to hear — found me within twenty minutes, which I considered a credit to both of us.

"You're unhurt," Ottonello said. It was not a question, but I answered it anyway, because a courier learns that confirmation is cheaper than assumption.

"Unhurt."

"The engine's dead. Driver too." He said it the way he said everything, in the flat tone of a man reading out a laundry list that happens to contain corpses. "There's a supply dump eight kilometers up the valley. Trucks go through tomorrow morning, as far as the pass road allows. After that—" He shrugged, a gesture that contained the rest of the war.

"Then we walk to the dump tonight."

Bruni made a noise. Ottonello looked at him and the noise stopped.

"It's eleven kilometers," Bruni said.

"Eleven kilometers," I agreed, "and the alternative is standing in this field explaining ourselves to the next aircraft that comes over the hill. I don't know about you two, but my explanation would take a while."

Ottonello almost smiled. It was the closest thing to warmth I would see from him for three days, and I treasured it the way one treasures a coin one suspects is counterfeit.

We walked. That is the whole of the adventure, really, and I report it faithfully because the faithful version is somehow worse than any heroic one could be. We walked along the frozen river with the burning train behind us getting smaller, and the green flame became a smudge, and the smudge became weather. Bruni counted his steps aloud for the first hour until Ottonello told him that a man who counts aloud is a man advertising how frightened he is, and Bruni switched to counting silently, which I respected enormously, because silent counting was my own religion.

At the second kilometer we passed a farmhouse with its roof burned off and its walls standing, the way a sentence stands when you remove the verb. In the yard, a woman was hanging washing on lines strung between a cart and a dead pear tree, and she did not look up as we passed. Three shirts, two sheets, a pair of enormous men's trousers. She hung them with the deliberation of a woman performing surgery on the sky. I understood her completely. When your country comes apart, you hang the washing. Not because the washing matters. Because hanging the washing is a way of insisting that Tuesday exists, that next week exists, that there is a future tense worth conjugating.

"Shouldn't we ask her about the road?" Bruni whispered.

"She'd tell us," Ottonello said, "and it wouldn't change the road."

I have repeated that sentence to myself more times than I can justify. It is the whole philosophy of that winter compressed into six words, and I stole it shamelessly and have used it as my own ever since, which is, after all, how philosophy works.

* * *

The supply dump turned out to be a farmyard full of lorries under netting, run by a lieutenant who was perhaps twenty-four and had the eyes of a much older man who had been lent a young face by mistake. His name was Ferrarin. He had a stove, a kettle, and a ledger, and I want to say something about that ledger, because I have thought about it for years.

While the soldiers drank coffee that tasted like roasted chestnuts and regret, Lieutenant Ferrarin sat at a trestle table recording, in beautiful copperplate, the disposition of every item in his charge: fuel, flour, boots, mailbags, one consignment of horseshoes for a cavalry unit that no longer had horses. Every line balanced. The republic was dying around him — the trains stopped, the ports contested, the ministries burning their own archives — and this boy sat in a frozen farmyard making sure the horseshoe account reconciled.

I recognized him instantly. He was me. He was every man whose sanity survives catastrophe by pretending catastrophe is a clerical problem. I sat down across from him and asked, because it seemed polite, whether the cavalry unit still existed.

"I update the ledger weekly against confirmed losses," he said. "As of Thursday, yes. As of today, I couldn't say. The ledger says they exist, so provisionally, they exist."

"And if they stop existing?"

"Then I close the account properly. With a date. Everything deserves a date."

He said it without self-pity, and that was the terrible thing. I looked at his copperplate and saw my own future written in it: a life spent assigning dates to endings, keeping accounts for things that deserved better than accounting. I did not sleep well that night in the hay barn, though I pretended to, and I divided the cipher books into new portions, smaller ones, in case the coat and I should become separated. Twelve books. Two hundred and six pages of operational tables. If I lost the coat, I would lose the books, and therefore I began, lying in the straw with my breath freezing on the wool above my face, to memorize.

Not all of it — no one could hold all of it, and the instructions had been explicit: burn what cannot be carried in the head. But the essential traffic, the routes, the recognition signals, the daily changing keys for the southern network — that could live in a skull, if the skull's owner were willing to give up storing anything else there for a while. Poems, faces, my mother's voice saying my name with the second syllable lengthened the Italian way, which Chiara could never master and teased me about for years — all of it would have to be evicted to make room.

You may think I am exaggerating the cost. I am not. Memory is a house of fixed size, and I was about to fill mine with numbers belonging to a state that might, by spring, no longer exist. I lay in the hay and recited key-tables until they ran behind my eyes like film, and somewhere past midnight I realized I could no longer reliably recall the color of the kitchen in the house where I was born, and that the numbers had taken the space where the color used to be, and that I had paid for the codes with a kitchen. That is the exchange rate of that profession, and no one warns you about it, because the people qualified to warn you have already forgotten the kitchen themselves.

* * *

In the morning there were trucks, or rather there was a truck — one lorry, half-fueled, carrying mailbags, flour, and us. Ferrarin shook my hand at the gate and said, "Safe passage, courier," with a formality that moved me more than any blessing from an actual priest would have, because it acknowledged what I was, which is a thing almost no one in that war was willing to acknowledge about anyone: that some people carry, and the carrying is the whole of them.

The pass road climbed through country that broke my heart in installments. I want to describe it accurately, because accuracy is the only elegy I trust. Terraced hillsides gone back to scrub, because the men who had kept the terraces were dead or conscripted or had chosen, sensibly, to be neither. A shrine at every third bend, most of them recent, the plaster still pale, the saints still blank-eyed with newness — a whole roadside liturgy of accidents and ambushes, each saint installed by someone who had survived whatever the previous traveler had not. Villages that appeared and vanished between bends like thoughts one is ashamed of. Smoke columns on the far ridge that might have been woodcutters and might not, and Ottonello watching them with binoculars and pronouncing, after long study, "Woodcutters," in a tone that meant he had decided to believe it and expected us to join him.

The truck's radiator boiled twice, and both times we stopped and Bruni urinated into it, cheerfully, having been assured by Ottonello that this was traditional, and whether it was true or not it worked, and I mention it only because it strikes me now as the period in miniature: everyone contributing bodily fluids to keep a dying machine moving toward a destination no one believed in.

It was during the second radiator halt that I understood the phrase I have used ever since to describe that journey: a republic eating itself. Not being eaten — eaten *by* itself. On the walls of the last village before the high pass, someone had painted, in careful white letters, the slogan of the government I served: THE REPUBLIC LIVES IN ITS SERVANTS. And beneath it, in different paint, fresher, angrier, someone else had added: THEN IT IS ALREADY DEAD. And beneath *that*, in a third hand, almost apologetic: GOD SEES ALL THREE OF US.

Three hands, three truths, one wall. You could read the whole collapse in the stratigraphy of that plaster, layer over layer, each generation of paint claiming to be the surface of reality. I stood looking at it while Bruni attended to the radiator, and I felt something in my chest that I refused, with great professional discipline, to identify as grief. I logged it instead as fatigue. Fatigue was permissible. Fatigue had a cure. Grief, in February 1943, in the mountains, with twelve books of secrets against your ribs, was a luxury that got people killed — mostly the people around you, who needed you functional, who needed the counting version of you, the version that could walk eleven kilometers and divide a burden into portable portions and never once sit down in the snow to think about kitchens.

"Grief disguised as efficiency" — I wrote that phrase much later, in another life, when someone asked me what the retreat had been like. But I invented the practice before I had the phrase. We all did. It was the national costume of that winter.

* * *

Past the high col, the road descended toward the gorge country, and the world changed its mind about being ruined. Here the valleys narrowed and the villages clung to their ledges like arguments that refuse to end, and there were bells — I heard the first of them at dusk, from a town below us that the map called San Rocco, though I did not yet know the name mattered, and would not know until the next day, and oh, how differently I would hear those same bells afterward, once I knew who waited among them.

That evening we slept in a wayside chapel — Ottonello negotiated with a priest who charged us nothing and fed us polenta and asked no questions, which in that country and season was the highest form of hospitality: the gift of not being known. I lay on the stone flags with my coat rolled under my head, twelve books against my ribs, two hundred and six pages filed behind my eyes, and listened to the priest moving in the dark, banking the stove, setting out candles for the morning.

"Courier," Ottonello said quietly, from his bedroll near the door. It was the first time he had addressed me by function rather than by nothing at all.

"Sergeant."

"Tomorrow we reach the bridge. After the bridge, my orders end and yours continue alone. I'll say this once, because I'm old enough to be permitted it." He paused long enough that I thought he had fallen asleep. "Whatever you're counting — stop counting it. Start carrying it. There's a difference, and you'll need to know it before the coast."

I did not answer. I lay in the dark feeling the difference move into my chest like a splinter working its way toward the heart, and I thought: he is wrong, counting is carrying, counting is all the carrying there is. It took me thirty years and one atlas to learn that he was right, and by then he was dust, and Bruni was dust, and the republic was a set of questions on an examination paper.

But that night, in the chapel at the top of the pass, with the bells of San Rocco faint in the frozen air below — I did not yet know whose hands rang them, or whose ledger waited under them, or that the last ship south would sail from a port whose quota was already, at that very hour, being cut by men in warm offices — I slept, briefly, dreamlessly, efficiently.

In the morning the ice fog had come down into the gorge like a held breath, and we walked the last kilometers to the bridge, and I remember thinking, as the towers of it swam up out of the whiteness — iron bones, cables thick with frost, a queue of vehicles and carts and people backed up for a mile on our side — that bridges are honest things: they are either open or closed, and they tell you which.

This one, though I could not see it yet, had already begun to close.
