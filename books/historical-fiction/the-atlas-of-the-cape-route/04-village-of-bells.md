## 4. Village of Bells

The truck let me off two kilometers below San Rocco because the driver claimed the road above was mined, and because he wanted to be home before dark, and I suspect in that order of priority. I paid him in cigarettes I could ill afford and walked the rest with my satchel against my chest like a sleeping child, which, in every way that mattered to me, it was.

San Rocco announced itself before I saw it: bells. Six of them, by the sound, badly out of tune with one another, ringing the hour as if each had its own private opinion about what hour it was. In the low country we'd stopped trusting bells — they meant air raids, or curfew, or processions for the dead — but up here they still meant only the time, and I found the innocence of it almost unbearable.

The village sat in a fold of mountain like a coin dropped in a palm and forgotten there. Grey stone, slate roofs furred with moss, smoke rising from chimneys in thin, apologetic threads. A river ran below it — the Cape water, already wearing its winter armor of ice along the edges — and above the river, half-hidden in cloud, the gorge, and somewhere in that cloud, the bridge. The bridge. The last span between me and the coast, and between the coast and a ship, and between a ship and whatever remained of the world that employed me.

I had been walking since the strafing, since Corporale Brenna folded into the ditch with his hand still open toward something no one would ever give him, and I will confess that my first thought upon seeing San Rocco was not noble. It was: *hot food*. The second thought was: *the bridge schedule*. The third, arriving late and unwelcome like a guest who knows he's intruding, was: *Teodoro*.

My uncle. My mother's brother. The man who had taught me to read maps upside down and to distrust anyone who spoke of duty without mentioning cost. I hadn't seen him in nine years, and the last time we'd parted it had been over a chessboard he'd overturned rather than resign, which tells you most of what you need to know about both of us.

But that was a problem for later. First, food.

The inn stood on the square, across from a fountain that had been turned off for the season and now held a congregation of frozen leaves. Above its door hung a sign depicting a saint I didn't recognize wrestling an animal I did — a mule, naturally; even hagiography in these hills was honest about the local conditions. Inside, warmth, tallow light, and eleven other stranded travelers arranged along benches like passengers who had missed not a train but a decade.

The innkeeper, a woman built like the mountains she lived under, took my cigarette ration without comment and my questions with slightly more resistance.

"The bridge?" She shrugged the shrug of a woman who has watched empires come and go from her kitchen window and found them all equally tedious. "Engineers came through Tuesday. Something about a cracked pier. They closed it."

"Closed it how long?"

"Until the thaw." She said it the way you'd say *until the Second Coming*. "There's no crossing the gorge in winter. Not on foot, not on the ice below — the riverbed looks solid and isn't. Ask the Ferrero boy." Then, catching herself: "Don't ask the Ferrero boy. He's dead. That's the point."

I sat with my soup and did arithmetic. The ministry's instructions assumed a crossing in November. It was now the second week of December, and the thaw in these altitudes was a rumor belonging to April. Five months. The city I'd left would not be the city I'd left in five months. The codes in my satchel were timed charges of a sort, and I was carrying them away from the blast radius while pretending this was a strategy.

Across the room, a man in a railway uniform was telling anyone who'd listen that the southern port was "processing" evacuees, which everyone understood to mean the way a sieve processes flour. Ships were leaving. Lists existed. Whether any given name survived contact with those lists was a matter the man in uniform was careful not to guarantee, possibly because he valued his teeth.

I wrote nothing down. Writing things down had become a vice I practiced only in my head, where the ink was invisible and the paper burned cleanly.

Later, when the innkeeper cleared my bowl, she looked at me a moment too long. People who carry sealed satchels learn to recognize that look: not suspicion exactly, but inventory. She said, "You'll want the parish office in the morning, then. For the lists."

"What lists?"

"Evacuation lists. Berth allocations. Everything goes through the ledger office now." She wiped the table with a cloth that had clearly seen more history than I had. "Signor Marchetti keeps them."

The name went through me like cold water through a boot.

"Marchetti," I repeated, with the flawless neutrality I reserve for moments of internal collapse. "A common name here?"

"Common enough." She frowned. "He's not from here originally, but he's been running the lists since October. Old gentleman. Very precise." Her eyes narrowed slightly, whether at me or at memory, I couldn't tell. "You might even say pedantic."

"That," I said, "sounds exactly like a Marchetti."

She left me to my ignorance, which was considerable, and I climbed to a rented room under a roof where the wind conducted its own evacuation all night, and lay awake doing what I do instead of praying: planning. And beneath the plans, quieter than the wind, a single sentence kept turning over like a stone in a stream:

*Of all the villages on all the roads in all the mountains, I have arrived in his.*

* * *

From the ledger of Teodoro Marchetti, keeper of names, San Rocco — written that same night, in the hand of a man who believed that a well-kept margin was a form of prayer:

I begin, as always, with the count, because the count does not lie and neither, if I can help it, shall I.

Forty-one names remain on the active manifest for the ship *Cristina*, sailing from the southern port when the naval office deigns to send word. Forty-one names. One berth. This is the equation I carry to bed each night and wake with each morning, and it has begun to sit on my chest like the stone angel on my wife's grave in Trieste, which is to say heavily, and coldly, and without any discernible interest in my comfort.

People imagine that administering mercy is gentle work. Let me disabuse them. Mercy, organized, is a table, a lamp, a pen, and a list of human beings reduced to the length of their claims upon your conscience. Today I struck three names and restored one, and the woman whose name I restored wept on my desk, and the man whose name I struck shook my hand, and I do not know which of them I will dream about tonight. Probably both. Conscience is democratic that way.

The schoolteacher says I should keep the list in strict alphabetical order, for transparency. Transparency! As though these people were goods in a warehouse. No. I keep them in order of arrival of application, because that is the only justice left that costs nothing to administer, and because alphabetical order would put the Bertoldis next to the Brandolis, and old Bertoldi has not spoken to Brandoli since 1931 over a boundary wall, and if they must share a lifeboat together I would prefer they not know it in advance.

I am aware of my own vanity in this. At seventy-one, vanity is one of the few possessions I can still lift unassisted.

Today a courier came through from the north — a young officer of the foreign service, they said, carrying papers, walking down from the pass with frost in his eyebrows. I did not see him. Marta the innkeeper mentioned it at the fountain, and I noted it in the margin of my ledger the way I note everything: *transit staff, one, northbound origin.* The bridge is closed; therefore he is stranded; therefore he joins the great ledger of the waiting. Another entry. Another mouth. Another set of eyes that will look at me in February and ask why.

They always ask why, eventually. Why them, why not the others, why the quota fell from sixty to forty-one, why God permits engineers and bureaucrats to collaborate so efficiently. I have answers for some of these questions and silence for the rest, and I have learned — late, expensively — that silence is often the more honest answer.

What I did not write in the margin, because margins are public property in a village and grief is not, is this: the courier's surname, according to Marta, was Marchetti.

Now, Marchetti is not rare. There are Marchettis in Emilia, Marchettis in the Veneto, a whole tribe of them near Genoa who are, I am told, in shipping, God help them. The name alone proves nothing. But my sister Lucia's son — my nephew, the boy I taught to hold a map upside down, the boy who became a man who became a stranger in a government I never trusted — such a man would be exactly the age of the courier described. Such a man would walk with that particular straightness, shoulders squared around whatever he refuses to put down.

It cannot be him. The odds forbid it. And yet I found myself, this evening, holding the pen over the margin for a long time, unable to write the entry, as though writing it might make it true, or as though leaving it blank might keep the past politely outside the door.

In the end I wrote it. *Transit staff, one.* A ledger must be complete even when its keeper would prefer the world to contain fewer coincidences and kinder ones.

Tomorrow I audit the berth calculations again. Tomorrow the priest wants ten minutes to argue for the widow Pasquali, and the doctor wants ten minutes to argue against her — the doctor believes berths should go to the strong, on the principle that the saved should be able to survive being saved, and there is a terrible logic in it that I hate precisely because it is terrible and logical. Tomorrow, also, the commission sends an interpreter up from the valley. A woman, they said. Attached to the occupying commission, translating for the officers who requisition everything from flour to futures.

An interpreter. Gray coat, probably. Polished boots. The kind of position my niece Chiara would have been clever enough to obtain and foolish enough to believe was neutral.

I have not heard Chiara's voice in six years. I have heard *of* it — the family talent for surviving by accommodation, inherited, no doubt, from some ancestor I'd rather not claim. If she comes up that road tomorrow, I will receive her with the courtesy owed to any official and the coldness owed to any Marchetti who mistakes collaboration for protection. We are, all of us in this family, excellent at dressing our choices in other people's interests.

The bell rang midnight just now — all six of them, disagreeing with one another as usual, and for once I sympathized. Even the bells of San Rocco cannot agree on what time it is. Neither can I. Part of me lives in October, when the quotas were generous and my heart was merely tired. Part of me lives in April, when the thaw opens the bridge and this ledger finally closes, mercifully or otherwise. And part of me, the smallest and most stubborn part, is standing in the innkeeper's doorway listening to her describe a young man with frost in his eyebrows, and hoping — against arithmetic, against odds, against six years of silence — that the name means nothing at all.

Tomorrow I will ask her to describe him more carefully.

A ledger, after all, must be complete.

And somewhere above the square, in the inn under the wrestling-saint sign, a young man was falling asleep with his arms around a satchel, and did not know that in the morning he would stand in front of a desk older than he was, behind which sat the one person alive who could look at his face and see, all at once, his mother, his grandfather, and every mistake the family had ever made —

— and that the bells would ring the hour, all six of them, wrong.
