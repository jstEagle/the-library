## 5. A Sister in a Gray Coat

The gray coat was the first thing I bought with commission money, and I have never entirely forgiven myself for how well it fit.

It was cut in Turin by a tailor who had learned his trade fitting officers of the last war and had simply changed which flags he measured shoulders for. He did not ask me questions. Tailors never do; they only ask you to stand still, and there is something obscene about standing still while a man chalks your waistline and artillery thuds somewhere past the window, but I stood still, because that is what I have always done best. Stand still. Hold my posture. Translate.

"Gray," he said, holding up three grays. "Or gray."

"Gray," I said. "The middle one."

He chose correctly. It is a coat that says: *I am not a soldier, I am not a partisan, I am a person who files things.* It says: *I am useful, therefore let me live.* It has deep pockets, good buttons, and a collar that sits high enough to hide the fact that I am, some mornings, unable to swallow.

I requested San Rocco.

You should understand what that sentence cost me, because at the time it looked like nothing at all — a name spoken over a requisition form, a pencil moving through a column of postings like a ferryman poling through reeds. Captain Weiss needed an interpreter for the mountain district; the mountain district included the Cape gorge road, the supply depot at the pass, and a village called San Rocco where the commission intended to establish a transit screening point. Any interpreter in the pool could have taken it. Two wanted it before I raised my hand. I raised mine anyway, and smiled the way one smiles when volunteering for extra work, which is to say apologetically, as though I were doing everyone a small favor and expected no thanks.

Captain Weiss looked at my file. Marchetti. Born in San Rocco. Father deceased, mother deceased, one brother, occupation: foreign ministry courier, whereabouts unknown.

"You'll be going home," he said.

"No," I said. "I'll be going to San Rocco. Home is a different thing."

He didn't understand that, and I didn't explain it, and this is the arrangement I have with nearly everyone now: they do not understand, I do not explain, and we proceed together down the corridor like two people carrying opposite ends of a piano neither of us wants.

* * *

We came up from the coast in two cars and a truck, the convoy crawling the last twenty kilometers behind a fuel tanker whose driver stopped every hour to listen to his engine the way other men listen to their consciences. The mountains had already put on their winter. Snow lay on the terraces above the road like linen laid out for bodies. At the pass checkpoint a sentry examined our papers with the slow ceremonial boredom of a man who has discovered that power is mostly paperwork and intends to enjoy every page.

"Interpreter," the sentry said, reading my card.

"Yes."

"Italian?"

"Half."

He looked up. "Which half?"

"The half that's talking to you."

He laughed. Sentries always laugh at that line; I invented it in Trieste two years ago and have been paying for it since, because a woman who makes soldiers laugh gets remembered, and being remembered is the occupational hazard of collaboration. You want, in this work, to be furniture. Furniture is not asked questions at two in the morning.

But I confess there are worse fates than being remembered. Being forgotten is worse. Ask the villages nobody bothered to garrison.

From the pass we descended toward the gorge, and there it was below us, the bridge — the bridge I had crossed at nine years old on my uncle Teodoro's shoulders, screaming with delight, while my brother Xanthe walked behind with his nose in a book because even then Xanthe trusted paper more than people. The bridge hung in its gorge like a held breath, iron and stone, and beside it, on the near span, engineers had rigged scaffolding and warning lamps, and there were crates of explosive stacked with the neatness men reserve for the things they intend to destroy properly.

"They're going to blow a section?" I asked the driver.

"Not blow it. Drop it." He said it the way farmers talk about weather. "Span's cracked. They'll drop the cracked section, rebuild through winter, reopen at thaw. Till then, nothing crosses. Nothing at all."

Nothing at all. I wrote the phrase down later in the little notebook I keep against my ribs, because phrases like that are how the future telegraphs itself, and I have learned to read dispatches even when they aren't addressed to me.

* * *

San Rocco smelled the same, which was the cruelest thing about it.

Towns change their faces in war — shutters, sandbags, new flags, old fear — but the smell underneath stays loyal. Woodsmoke and chestnut and the cold mineral breath off the gorge, and under all of it the faint animal sweetness of the stable lanes. Six years since I'd left, and my body knew the town the way a dog knows a house: without thought, without approval.

The bells were ringing noon when we rolled into the square. Of course they were. San Rocco keeps time by bells the way the commission keeps time by forms, and both systems believe themselves eternal.

Villagers watched us unload. That is the part no one tells you about coming home in a gray coat: the watching. Not hostile, exactly. Worse than hostile. *Accounting.* Old Signora Bellini counted me twice and then turned away with a face like a closed ledger. A boy on the church steps made a motion — I could not tell if it was a salute or something else — and his mother pulled him indoors by the ear. And at the fountain stood Marta Ferri, who taught me catechism, who once told my mother I had a voice like a bell, and Marta looked at me the whole length of the square without one flicker of recognition, which was her gift to me, and I took it, because gifts in wartime are not refused.

Captain Weiss established the liaison office in the inn's front room. The innkeeper handed over the keys with the smooth grief of a man surrendering a daughter. I translated the inventory: twelve beds, two stoves, cellar stock of wine to be registered, cheese to be registered, radios — *all* radios, private receivers included — to be surrendered for registration within forty-eight hours. I read out the curfew. I read out the rules regarding unauthorized assembly, unauthorized transmission, unauthorized movement after dark. My voice did the work. My voice has gotten very good at doing work while the rest of me stands elsewhere, arms folded, watching from a great cold height, saying: *look at her, look what she does, is that really you?*

Yes. It is really me. Let us dispense with that question early, because I intend to answer it honestly, and honesty begins with admitting that I chose this.

Not the war. Nobody chose the war. But the gray coat, the posting, the pencil moving through the column — those were mine. Here is why, and here is why the why doesn't help:

When the republic began to fall — and it fell slowly, the way a tree falls slowly, groaning for years before anyone admits the angle — I understood two things at once, in the same instant, the way you understand thunder and lightning are the same event seen from different distances. First: our side would lose. Second: someone with our name would need to be on the winning side, or the name would end. Not the people. The name. Because names are what get confiscated, names are what go on lists, and I had already seen what happened to families whose names appeared on the wrong list in Trieste — the apartment sealed, the bank account frozen, the neighbors' eyes learning a new way of sliding past your windows.

Xanthe stayed loyal. Someone had to; the family required its conscience the way a church requires its bell, hanging up high, ringing at disaster. And I — I reasoned, in the clean white arithmetic I am so proud of — that loyalty needs a twin. Every family that survives a collapse has one child inside the burning house and one outside with the keys. I took the keys. I told myself it was sacrifice. Some mornings I almost believe it.

Other mornings I know it was ambition wearing sacrifice's coat, and the coat fits, God help me, the way everything fits me. That is my curse. Things fit. Uniforms, lies, chairs at officers' tables, other women's husbands' conversations. I have never once in my life been too large for a room. Perhaps that is what cowardice looks like on a woman with good posture.

* * *

On the third day I walked up to the church, ostensibly to deliver the commission's notice regarding bell regulations — even the bells must be registered; we are thorough people — and actually because I wanted to stand where my mother used to stand, third pew from the front, left side, and see whether grief had left any marks on the wood.

The church was empty except for dust and cold and a smell of candle wax, and I stood in my mother's pew and let myself, for ninety seconds, be an orphan instead of an interpreter. Ninety seconds is the allowance I give myself. I learned rationing early.

That was when I saw the loft ladder, and the hatch to the chapel loft standing open a hand's width, and heard — very faintly, beneath the wind off the gorge — a sound like a key being tapped. Tap-tap. Pause. Tap.

Morse. In the loft of San Rocco's church, above the register of baptisms where my name is written in my father's careful hand, someone was transmitting.

I stood absolutely still, in the manner of my profession, and did the arithmetic that is also my profession. A radio in the loft. After the order that every receiver be surrendered. Which meant either a fool or a partisan, and fools don't climb ladders in wartime.

And here is the moment I want recorded, whoever eventually reads this, because I will deny it aloud and I need it written down somewhere true: my first instinct was to protect it. To walk back down the aisle, report nothing, lose the knowledge deliberately. My second instinct was colder and arrived within the same heartbeat, the way the second blade arrives in a folding knife: *knowledge is currency, and you are poor.*

I closed the pew gate softly. I walked out through the cold church with my heels sounding like a metronome, and I told the sentry at the inn that the church inspection was complete and unremarkable.

Unremarkable. I filed it under unremarkable. I am keeping it, the way I keep everything, in the drawer that doesn't lock.

Because here is what I have not yet confessed, the real reason I asked for San Rocco, the reason under the reason like a stone under a stone: I did not come home for the name. Or not only for the name. I came because Teodoro is here, old Teodoro with his ledgers, running whatever mercy the republic still manages to administer, and because Xanthe's route south — I knew his route, I have access to routing schedules the way I have access to everything, that is the entire disease of my position — Xanthe's route south ran through this gorge, this village, these weeks. I requested a posting that sat on top of the last road my brother would travel, and I told myself I wanted to see him safe across it, and perhaps that is even true, and perhaps wanting to see him safe across it is merely the sentimental invoice attached to wanting to see him at all.

Six years. We have not spoken in six years, not since the argument in Trieste that ended with him saying the word *collaborator* quietly, the way he says everything, precisely, like placing a stamp, and me saying the word *martyr* loudly, which was unfair, because he wasn't a martyr yet, martyrs require circumstances, and I have spent six years providing them.

He will come through this village. The bridge will open or it won't; either way he will stand somewhere on this stone, and I will be wearing the gray coat, and one of us will have to speak first.

I have decided it will be me. I have rehearsed it. I have several openings prepared, graded by temperature, from warm to glacial, and I know which one I'll use — the glacial one, I always use the glacial one, warmth is a luxury I rationally declined in the autumn of '38 — but knowing has never once stopped me from hoping, and hoping is the one registration the commission hasn't thought to demand.

* * *

That evening Captain Weiss summoned me to the inn's front room, where the maps were out and the officers stood around them drinking the innkeeper's registered wine.

"The span section comes down tomorrow," Weiss said, tapping the gorge. "Engineers' recommendation. Until thaw, the crossing is closed. All traffic reroutes east — four days around, if the passes hold." He looked up at me. "Which means this village becomes a bottleneck. Everyone stranded between here and the coast funnels through our screening point. Refugees, stragglers, officials, smugglers. You'll process the civilian interviews."

"Yes, Captain."

"And the ministry people." He tapped the map again, on the north, on the capital, the small printed star of the shuttered foreign ministry. "They're evacuating everything southward. Ciphers, couriers, the whole apparatus. If any of them wash up here, they go on a special list. Priority handling." He smiled with the bottom half of his face. "You'd know the type better than anyone, Marchetti."

"I'd know the type," I agreed, and my voice did its work, level and helpful, while the rest of me went very quiet, the way a forest goes quiet before snow slides.

A special list. Priority handling. Somewhere south of here, on the wrong side of a bridge that would tomorrow cease to exist, my brother was walking toward this village carrying whatever it is the dying carry out of capitals, and the commission had just asked me to build the net he would fall into.

I copied the orders neatly. I filed them under unremarkable, in the unlocked drawer, next to the tap-tap-tap from the chapel loft, next to the middle shade of gray, next to everything I know and have not yet decided what it costs.

Outside, the bells rang vespers, and far up the gorge road, though I could not possibly have seen it from the inn window, though I have told myself since that I imagined it, a light moved. Small. Determined. Coming down through the pass toward San Rocco, at the exact hour the engineers set their charges.

Tomorrow the bridge closes. Tomorrow the world stops crossing.

And tomorrow, or the day after, or however long it takes a man with a satchel to walk out of the mountains — my brother walks into this village, and finds me waiting in the gray coat, and discovers that I requested the posting.

I have rehearsed what I will say.

God forgive me, I have rehearsed it in Italian, German, and English, and not one version survives contact with his eyes.
