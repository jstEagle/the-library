## 6. The Bridge Closes

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that governments do badly and mountains do perfectly. The ministry had calculated that I would reach the Cape gorge by the fourteenth of December with two days to spare before the last convoy crossed the bridge. The mountain had calculated that the bridge had been carrying forty-ton supply trucks all autumn over a span whose rivets were weeping rust like an old man at a wedding, and it had decided — without consulting anyone — that enough was enough.

I learned this from a mule driver named Pasquale, who was sitting on a stone wall above the road eating bread and watching the gorge with the expression of a man who has already told everyone everything and is now simply enjoying the telling.

"You missed it," he said.

"Missed what?"

"The bang." He gestured with his chin toward the far side of the gorge, where a plume of dust still hung in the cold air like something embarrassed to be there. "This morning. Seven o'clock. The engineers blew the middle section."

My satchel suddenly weighed what it actually contained, which was the foreign policy of a republic that no longer existed in any legal sense. "Blew it," I repeated. "The bridge."

"Half of it. The span over the deep channel. They said the frost got into the iron. They said it would come down on its own eventually, so better it comes down when nobody's on it." He tore his bread with great ceremony. "They're saying spring. When the thaw comes, they'll build the new one."

"And until spring?"

He shrugged the shrug of a man for whom spring is not a deadline but a season, like any other. "Until spring, you're on this side of the water. Like me. Like everyone."

I stood very still and did the calculation again, as though the numbers might have changed if I stared at them. Two days of margin. A bridge with a hole in it the size of my future. Behind me, somewhere down the valley, an army was advancing at a pace that made a liar out of every timetable printed in my capital since September.

"Well," I said, because one must say something, "at least the weather's nice."

Pasquale looked up at the sky, which was the color of dishwater and had been for three days, then back at me, deciding correctly that I was either a fool or a foreigner, which in the mountains amounts to the same thing.

* * *

The road into San Rocco descends through terraces gone fallow, past a shrine where someone had wired a bicycle wheel to the niche as an ex-voto, and then the village appears all at once below you — gray roofs, a campanile, and beyond it the gorge itself: a black gash in the white hillside with the river frozen along its floor like a vein of tin. The bridge crossed it high overhead, or most of it did. From the road I could see the gap plainly. The engineers had dropped the central span into the channel, and the two severed ends of the roadway reached out over the void like arms interrupted mid-embrace. Snow lay along the broken edges in unbroken lines, which somehow made it worse — snow does not know it is standing on a corpse.

Hundreds of people were strung along the near approach road: families with carts, soldiers without carts, a man leading two goats who clearly considered the entire situation beneath him. They had arranged themselves into the shape every stranded crowd takes — a knot at the barricade where the engineers' sentries stood, a fringe of vendors selling coffee at prices that should have been reported to the League of Nations, and a general atmosphere of people rehearsing their grievances aloud so they'd be ready when someone official finally appeared to receive them.

I left my escort — Corporal Bardi and the boy, Serra, who between them constituted the entire remaining security detail assigned to the codes — with the cart, and walked toward the barricade with the loose, unhurried gait I'd been practicing since the train. It is remarkable how much of espionage, or courier work, or whatever we are calling it, consists of looking like you have nowhere important to be.

The engineer at the barricade was young, sunburned across the cheekbones despite December, and wore the expression of a man who had answered the same question nine hundred times and had begun composing answers in advance.

"Before you ask," he said as I approached. "Spring thaw. April, maybe late March if God is feeling generous, which He hasn't lately. No, there is no other crossing. Yes, I know about the ford at Pontevecchio, it froze wrong this year, there's a shelf of ice under the surface that will take your truck down and keep it. No, there are no boats. The river isn't water right now, it's geology moving slowly."

"I wasn't going to ask," I said.

He blinked. "Everyone asks."

"I watched the dust settle from the ridge this morning. I'm asking different questions." I let my accent do a little of the work; half-Italian, half-English, fully ambiguous. "Who gave the demolition order? Was it local command or the commission?"

Something shifted behind his eyes — the wariness of a technical man asked a political question. "It came down the chain. Why?"

"Because whoever ordered it knew the convoy schedules," I said mildly. "And whoever knew the convoy schedules has just stranded several hundred people on the wrong side of a river with an army coming down the valley. I'd like to know whether that was incompetence or policy. It changes how I complain about it."

He studied me for a moment. Around us, the crowd had gone quiet in that way crowds do when someone says something interesting near a sentry — everyone listening while pretending to examine their shoes.

"The order was correct," he said finally. "The span was finished. Frost-jacked rivets, cracked chord plates, the whole lower lateral system shot. If a loaded convoy had hit it at speed—" He shook his head. "You'd have lost forty people and every vehicle on it. We didn't close the bridge to strand anyone. We closed it because it was going to kill people. The timing was bad luck."

"Bad luck," I said.

"Or good luck," he said, tired. "Depending on which side of the explosion you were standing. Look — friend. Go find a room. There's nothing else to be done here by anybody, including me."

* * *

San Rocco, in winter, smells of woodsmoke, dung, and church incense, in proportions that shift by street. I found the inn by following the sound of an argument about rooms, which is the village's principal musical form in wartime. The innkeeper, a broad woman with flour on her forearms and the eyes of a customs official, informed me that she had exactly one room left, and that it was expensive, and that she did not care about my ministry, my government, or my face, which she suggested I stop making at her.

"It's a neutral face," I said.

"It's a hungry face. Room's at the top of the stairs. Breakfast at seven if there's breakfast. Don't burn anything."

Bardi and Serra took the stable loft with the cart and the spare tins of fuel, which left me alone with the satchel in a narrow room under the eaves, a bed, a washstand, and a window overlooking the gorge. I set the satchel on the bed and looked at it for a long moment.

Here is what was in it: three cipher books of the republic's diplomatic service, the current code tables for military liaison, a sealed folio of credentials signed by men who were, at that hour, possibly already dead or already fled, and a letter of instruction that ended, in the undersecretary's cramped hand, with the phrase *deliver these to the naval attaché at the southern port, who will arrange onward transport.* Deliver them across a bridge that no longer existed, to a port that was, according to the radio chatter Serra monitored at night, being requisitioned piecemeal by the commission even as the army came down from the north. The plan had been a rope bridge of assumptions, and the mountain had just burned the middle of it.

I opened the window. The cold came in like a debt collector. Below, the gorge ran black and silent, the river locked under its ice, and the broken bridge held its two arms out over nothing.

Somewhere on the far side — the side I could see but not reach — was the port, the ship, the attaché, the end of the assignment. Somewhere on this side was a village full of stranded people, a winter that intended to be thorough about itself, and an army that was, by every account, perhaps ten days behind me and closing.

I thought about the undersecretary's face when he handed me the satchel. He had shaken my hand and said, *You're the last one out, Marchetti, try to be the first one back,* and he had smiled, and I had smiled, and we had both understood that neither of us believed a word of it, and we had done it anyway, because that is what ceremony is for: it lets two men say goodbye without having to say goodbye.

* * *

That evening the inn filled with the stranded, and I ate polenta at a corner table and listened, because listening is free and everything else in San Rocco cost too much.

They talked, as stranded people always talk, about the bridge. An elderly schoolteacher held forth on the original construction — 1891, his grandfather had carried stones, the engineer had died penniless and honored, etcetera. A wool merchant insisted there was a cable ferry upstream used during the last war, and was shouted down by three separate people who each claimed personal knowledge that the cable had been cut for scrap in '22. A woman with two children and a trunk of linens said, quietly, over and over, mostly to herself, that the ship would wait, the ship always waits, her sister had written that the ship would wait.

And near the fire, a man in a civilian coat that fit him like a borrowed opinion — too well-tailored for a peasant, too worn for a functionary — sat with a ledger open on his knees, making small marks with a pencil stub, and occasionally answering questions from the desperate with a patience that seemed less like kindness than like discipline.

I heard someone call him Teodoro. Uncle Teodoro, one woman said — not his niece, surely, he looked too old — but *zio*, the honorific the whole valley seemed to hang on him. He kept the evacuation list, someone explained to someone else. The berths for the last ship. He had the authority, such as it remained, of the regional commission, and he administered the names with a pencil stub and a ledger, and the whole room orbited him at a distance, the way planets orbit a star they resent needing.

My family name is Marchetti. My mother's brother went south thirty years ago and became a legend in our house — the uncle who ran things, who knew officials, who could fix anything with a signature. I hadn't seen him since I was nineteen. I had, in fact, spent six years constructing careful reasons not to go to San Rocco, reasons that had survived intact precisely because I never tested them against the actual man.

Now fate, which has a bureaucrat's sense of humor, had deposited me in the same room as him, and I found I could not stand up. I sat with my cooling polenta and watched my uncle make his small pencil marks, striking some names, adding others, and I felt the strange vertigo of a man who discovers the map he's been reading includes his own house.

He looked up once, scanning the room the way administrators do — counting, assessing, filing — and his gaze passed over me without stopping. Of course it did. To him I was one more stranded stranger, another mouth for the arithmetic. There was no reason on earth he should recognize his sister's son in a thin man with a neutral face eating polenta in a corner.

No reason except that I had my mother's chin, and her way of holding still.

He looked away. Then he looked back. The pencil stopped moving.

I want to record honestly what happened next, because later accounts will embellish it, and the truth is small and human: we looked at each other across a crowded inn for perhaps five seconds, and neither of us moved, because both of us understood — with the instant, total comprehension available only to people who share blood — that whatever we said next would have to carry thirty years, a war, and a name that had become dangerous in two languages.

He closed the ledger. He rose, slowly, one hand pressed flat on the table, the way old men rise when they've decided the rising matters more than the pain. He started around the fire pit toward my corner, and the crowd parted for him without noticing it was parting, the way it must have parted for him for years.

I stood up. My chair scraped. Someone shushed someone else. The woman with the linens went on whispering that the ship would wait.

And my uncle stopped two steps away, looked up at me — he was shorter than I'd remembered, or I was taller — and said, in the dialect of our mother's kitchen, which I hadn't heard spoken aloud in six years:

"So. Elena's boy walks into my village on the day the world ends." His voice was steady, but his hand, gripping the ledger, was not. "Your mother would say that's God's idea of a joke. Sit down, Nephew. We need to talk about lists."

Behind him, through the inn window, the broken bridge hung over the gorge in the last gray light, its severed ends reaching for each other across sixty meters of frozen air — and I understood that I was looking at a portrait of my own assignment, my own family, and possibly my own life: two halves, once continuous, now separated by a gap that only a thaw could close, if the thaw ever came at all.

Uncle Teodoro set the ledger on the table between us, opened it to a page dense with names, and tapped one column with the pencil stub.

"Now," he said. "Tell me why the ministry sent a boy with my sister's chin to die in my village — or don't, and I'll guess. Either way, the ship leaves in eleven days, and there is one berth left with our name on it, and I am afraid, Xanthe, that we are going to have to decide who it belongs to."
