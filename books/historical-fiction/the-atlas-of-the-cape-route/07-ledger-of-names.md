## 7. Ledger of Names

A ledger, I have always maintained, is the most honest form of prayer. You write down what you want, you strike out what heaven refuses, and in the margin you record your grievances. I have kept ledgers since I was nineteen years old — grain accounts for my father, olive presses, ferry tolls, and now, at sixty-three, human beings. The paper has not changed. Only the weight of each line.

The ledger lived in the schoolhouse, because the schoolhouse had a lockable cabinet and no children to disturb it. The children of San Rocco were, for the moment, evacuated — sent east to cousins in October when the shelling crept close enough that Nonna Bertoli's roof lost its argument with a shell fragment. So I sat at a child's desk built for someone half my size, my knees complaining like an old ship in weather, and I did arithmetic.

Forty-one names.

I wrote them out again that morning, though I had written them twice already. There is a superstition among clerks — and I am a clerk before I am anything else, whatever they call me now, *coordinator*, *commissioner*, *the old man with the book* — that a list improves with copying. It does not. It only grows more familiar, which is worse, because familiarity is how you learn to stop seeing names as people.

Forty-one names for one berth.

Let me be precise, because precision is the last decency available to a man in my position. The ship — the *Adriana*, a coastal freighter conscripted into passenger service, whose engine coughed like a consumptive — would carry twenty souls south from the port of Vado. That was the original allowance. The commission's port office cut it to twelve in December; then, after some conference at which no one thought to invite the people being counted, it was restored to twenty, minus two reserved for "priority transit," meaning officers' luggage with opinions. Eighteen places, then. And of those eighteen, eleven belonged by prior allocation to families already on the harbor master's roll — millers, the doctor, the Bertoli orphans if they came back for the sailing.

That left one.

One berth, forty-one names. I sat with the figures the way you sit with a diagnosis: not doubting them, just refusing, for a few minutes longer, to let them mean anything.

* * *

You should understand something about quotas. A quota is not a number. A quota is a room in which every argument about the value of a life is conducted without anyone having to say the word *value*. When the port office halved our allowance, they did not kill anyone on paper. They simply made the paper smaller, and let us discover who fit.

I have spent six months learning the grammar of this. In November I still believed that if I presented the case correctly — the aged, the ill, the unaccompanied children — the commission would see reason. I drafted memoranda. I cited precedents from the 1915 evacuations, which I remembered personally and which they did not. I was polite in three languages. The responses came back stamped, dated, and empty, like shells washed up after a storm.

By January I understood: mercy, administered through a ministry, arrives pre-rationed. My task was not to argue for more. It was to decide who got less.

There are men who could do this cleanly. I have met them — the young ones with clipboards and good posture who speak of *throughput* and *processing*. I envied them once, briefly, the way you envy a man anesthesia. But I had been keeping San Rocco's accounts too long to forget what a name weighs. Forty-one times over, I knew exactly.

* * *

My nephew arrived on the Tuesday, in the snow, looking like a telegram that had learned to walk.

I want to record that I recognized him instantly, but honesty is the discipline of the ledger and so I will not lie even here, even to myself. He stood in the doorway of the schoolhouse shaking snow off his coat, and for a moment I saw only a stranger — thin, dark, ministry-collared, one of the many functionaries the war produced and would soon stop needing. Then he pushed back his hood and there was my brother's jaw on my sister-in-law's stubborn mouth, and thirty years folded themselves away like linen.

"Zio Teo."

"Xanthe." I said it carefully. It is an absurd name — his mother's doing, some English fancy — and I had teased him with it across two decades of summer visits, back when teasing was the largest danger a family contained. "You're thinner than the last time I saw you."

"The country's on a diet."

"Worse things than that, apparently." I closed the ledger out of habit, then, hearing the click of the clasp, opened it again. There was no point hiding it. Everyone in San Rocco knew what I kept in the schoolhouse; the secret part was never the existence of the list, only the arithmetic underneath.

He looked at the cabinet, then at me, and I watched him decide not to ask. Good. Asking was a luxury we had all stopped affording.

"You'll stay at mine," I said. "The inn is full of commission men, and Elena Voss has taken the loft rooms, and besides—" I stopped. I had been about to say *besides, you are family*, which was true, which was also complicated, given that his other family member in town was Chiara, and given what the town believed about Chiara. "Besides, my cooking is better than the inn's, which is a low bar but mine."

He almost smiled. It was the first currency I would try to earn back from him, over the weeks that followed, and I note now — writing this in whatever time remains to me — how little of it I ever managed to bank.

* * *

That evening he ate my beans and sausage without complaint, which in a Marchetti constitutes high praise, and afterward he said, quietly, "How bad is the list?"

And so it began.

"How bad do you mean?"

"I've heard the *Adriana* takes twenty."

"The *Adriana* takes eighteen after priority transit, of whom eleven are spoken for." I poured the grappa. Two glasses. I had stopped pouring myself a second glass in ordinary weeks; this was not going to be an ordinary week. "One berth, Xanthe. For everything south of the gorge, one berth that isn't already nailed down."

He set down his fork with the care of a man handling evidence. "For whom?"

"For whoever I choose." I let that sit between us. Grappa smoke and lamplight. Outside, the wind worked at the shutters the way it had all winter, patient, professional. "The commission gave me sole authority over the civilian allocation. They think it's efficiency. What it actually is, is a gift of cruelty — they hand the knife to someone local so the blood doesn't get on their stamps."

"And you've chosen?"

"No."

"Why not?"

Because choosing meant the list stopped being a list. Because every morning I woke with a different name rising in me like a fish to the surface — Bertoli's widow, who had buried two sons; little Marco Ferrero, nine years old, who had been sent east and come back alone when his aunt died; old Serafin, who was ninety and wanted nothing except to die in the valley where his wife was buried, and who would certainly die here either way, so did he need the berth more or less than a child? — and every morning I wrote the name down and then struck it out, because the next morning a different one rose.

"Because the choosing is the worst part," I said, "and I am saving it. Like the last of the firewood."

Xanthe looked at me for a long moment. He has his mother's way of looking at you — not judging, exactly. *Measuring.* She used to measure me across the dinner table too, and I never found out what she concluded.

"Show me the list," he said.

* * *

Here is what I did not say to him that night, and what I must say somewhere, even if only to this page.

I showed him the list because I wanted him to see it. Not because I needed a clerk's second opinion — God knows I can subtract — but because Xanthe Marchetti had walked into my kitchen carrying the particular exhaustion of a man who has been carrying something sealed for a very long time, and I thought: *this one understands weight.* Whatever was in his satchel, whatever errand had brought him south through a strafed valley and a closing bridge, it had taught him the lesson I most needed a witness to share: that some cargoes cannot be put down until they are delivered, and that delivery may cost more than the cargo is worth.

Also — I will be honest, the ledger demands it — I showed him the list because I was tired. Sixty-three years old, a heart that the doctor Santoro had begun listening to with a frown he tried to hide behind his stethoscope, and six months of being the man who says no to widows. I wanted, just once, to sit across from someone younger and watch him read the names, so that the names existed outside my own skull for an hour.

He read them slowly. His lips moved slightly — an old habit; he'd done it over schoolbooks at my table twenty summers ago. Forty-one lines. He took perhaps ten minutes, and I watched him the whole time, and I will testify that he did not flinch, did not skim, did not perform sympathy. He read every name as if each one expected an answer from him personally.

When he finished, he said, "Who cut it from twenty?"

"The port office. Then a committee restored it. Then priority transit took two back."

"So the same government that issued these codes—" and here his hand moved, briefly, toward the satchel he had set beside his chair, then withdrew, "—is also cutting the boats."

"The same government," I agreed, "that is losing."

He nodded slowly. There was something almost comforting in his bitterness; it matched my own the way two halves of a torn stamp match.

"And you?" I asked him. "Where do you stand on this manifest? Are you on it?"

The question was a trap and I set it deliberately, because I am not so old that I have forgotten how to be cruel in small domestic ways. If he said *yes, put me down*, I would know him for a man who had come home to save himself. If he said *no*, I would have to ask why not, and I suspected the answer would be one of those answers that makes an old man feel both proud and afraid.

"I'm transit staff," he said. "Neutral passage. My papers don't go through your ledger."

"Everyone goes through someone's ledger eventually."

"Not everyone gets written down." He turned his glass on the table, a slow orbit. "Some of us are just... carried. Like freight."

I let that pass. There was freight in that satchel; I would have bet my olive groves on it, and my olive groves were, by then, mostly a memory with tax obligations. But pressing him would have been the wrong move. You do not open a sealed thing by staring at the wax. You wait for thaw.

* * *

Over the following days I watched him settle into the village the way water settles into cracks — quietly, thoroughly, finding every weakness in the masonry. He walked the gorge road and stared at the broken span for long stretches, hands in pockets, doing whatever arithmetic engineers and couriers do. He took meals at the inn despite my warnings, sitting with the commission men like a chess piece placed on their board. He went to the chapel, often. Always alone. I noted that. I note everything; it is my profession and my disease.

And every evening he came back to my kitchen, and sometimes he asked about the list, and sometimes he didn't, and both silences told me things.

On the fifth night he asked the question I had been waiting for.

"Teodoro. If you had to choose tomorrow. How would you even begin?"

I refilled his glass, which was a stalling tactic, and then answered anyway, because I owed him truth for the beans.

"You begin by lying," I said. "You tell yourself there's a formula. Age, health, dependents, skills. You make a points system, like a school exam. And it works beautifully right up until you reach the tie — the widow and the orphan with equal claims — and then you discover the formula was never real. It was scaffolding. Underneath it there's just you, holding a pen, deciding which grief gets a boat."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one. The rest is theater we perform so we can sleep." I leaned back. My chest did its new thing — a tightening, like a fist closing slowly around something soft — and I waited it out with the practiced blankness of a man who has decided his body's complaints will be dealt with after the war. "But I will tell you what I've noticed, nephew. The ones who sleep best are not the ones with formulas. It's the ones who've stopped counting themselves."

He frowned. "What does that mean?"

"It means," I said, "that I have written this list four times, and you might check my work."

I slid the ledger across the table. He read it again, slower this time, and I watched his eyes travel down the column of forty-one names and arrive — as I had known they would, as I had arranged, in the small dishonest way I permit myself — at the place where a name was missing.

His own.

I had entered every Marchetti in San Rocco. Chiara, with her gray coat and her commission escort and her guilt worn like a regulation uniform — entered, flagged, argued over. Myself — entered, because omitting myself outright would have been noticed, and a coordinator caught falsifying his own rolls loses the power to protect anyone. Entered, and then struck. Struck on every draft, struck with a straight clean line, struck the way you strike a debt you've decided to pay in another currency.

Xanthe looked at my name, crossed out in my own handwriting, and then he looked up at me, and I watched him understand — not everything, not yet, but the shape of it. The arithmetic underneath the arithmetic.

"Zio," he said. "Why is your name—"

"Bad ink," I said. "Happens with cheap pens."

It was a terrible lie and he knew it was a terrible lie and I knew he knew, and for a moment the two of us sat there in the lamplight holding that knowledge between us like something hot.

Then the door opened, letting in a blade of cold air and the sound of bells — the chapel, marking eight — and Xanthe closed the ledger and said, in a voice gone careful and flat, "Someone's coming up the path."

I looked past him through the window. Snow, lamplight, and a figure in a gray coat standing at my gate, hesitating, the way you hesitate before a house you used to live in.

Chiara.

My niece had come to see the ledger — or to see her brother — or to see whether the family she had traded still kept a light burning for her. And Xanthe Marchetti, who had refused absolution to his sister before he'd even reached my kitchen, was reaching for the latch as if the question of forgiveness were a door that could simply be opened.

I stayed seated. Some doors, I have learned, are better witnessed than opened.

"Come in, then," I said to the night, to no one, to the ledger. "Let's see who fits."
