## 8. Elena's Frequency

I want to say I discovered her secret through cunning — that the courier's instincts sharpened over years of sealed satchels finally paid their dividend in San Rocco. But the truth is less flattering. I found out because a woman asked me to fix a chair leg, and I am constitutionally incapable of leaving a broken thing alone.

It was the fourth morning after the bridge closed. The village had settled into its winter of suspension with the grim competence of people who have been stranded before. Snow fell in the unhurried way that suggests it intends to stay until spring and has already unpacked. I had taken to walking each morning along the gorge path as far as the engineers' barricade, standing before the raw stump where the span's middle section had been detonated — they'd dropped it on purpose, I'd learned, the trusses cracked by frost heave and judged beyond repair until thaw — and then walking back again, as if the ritual might wear a hole in the arithmetic. Bridges do not open because a man stares at them. I knew this. I stared anyway.

On the way back I passed the chapel of San Rocco itself, small and gray-walled, hunched at the end of the lane like an old man avoiding conversation. Elena Voss was outside it, wrestling a ladder against the sacristy wall.

"You," she said, without turning around.

"Me," I agreed.

"Hold this."

She thrust the ladder into my hands and climbed it in boots that were too large for her, which was, I would learn, how she wore everything — coats, gloves, authority — all of it a size or two borrowed from some larger life. At the top she pried at a shutter with a flat iron bar, muttering in German, then Italian, then something that sounded like pure frustration in no language at all.

"The hinge has gone," she called down. "The whole loft rots in winter and the priest pretends otherwise. Hold properly. You're holding it like a man who has never held anything."

"I carried diplomatic codes across two provinces," I said. "They weighed considerably less than you."

"They always do." She glanced down at me then, and there was the flicker again — the one I'd first seen on the train, when her eyes had touched my ministry seal and moved on with a discretion so perfect it had the texture of recognition. "You're the one from the ministry. Marchetti."

"Transit staff now. Transit staff of nowhere. The bridge closed behind me."

"So it did." She came down the ladder in one motion, landing close enough that I caught woodsmoke and something sharper underneath — solder, maybe, though I didn't know yet what solder smelled like. "The chair inside is broken. If you're going to stand at my barricade every morning staring at your ruined future, you may as well be useful on the way past."

* * *

The chapel loft smelled of dust and pigeon and, faintly, of hot metal — that sharp mineral tang again, stronger up here, though I filed it away rather than examined it, which tells you something about how badly I wanted not to know things in those days. The chair in question sat beneath the round window: a plain kitchen chair whose front left leg had split along the grain.

"There's glue in the sacristy," Elena said. "And wire. Father Anselmo keeps everything; he throws away nothing but sermons."

I knelt and worked while she stood at the window with her arms folded, watching the snow come down over the rooftops. For a long time neither of us spoke, and it was the good kind of silence — the kind two strangers can hold between them like a plank both are carrying.

"You memorize," she said suddenly. Not a question.

"The codes? That was the instruction. Burn half, carry half in the head." I fitted the split leg together and began winding wire. "A cipher book can be taken from you. A memory has to be tortured out, and even then it comes out wrong. Fear garbles numbers. It's why they choose people who—" I stopped.

"People who don't frighten easily," she said.

"People who are frightened all the time already," I said, "so there's nowhere further down to go."

She made a sound that was almost a laugh. "You'll forgive me if I don't find that comforting."

"It isn't meant to be. It's meant to be accurate. Comfort is for people whose work permits it."

Below us, the door opened — Father Anselmo, ancient and deaf, shuffling in with an armful of candles, nodding vaguely at the ceiling as though we were weather. Elena waited until his footsteps receded into the vestry before she spoke again, and when she did, her voice had changed. The wryness was still there, but underneath it ran something taut, like a wire strung through the sentence.

"How good is your memory, Marchetti? Honestly. Not the interview answer."

I set down the wire and looked at her. "Group-cipher tables, four thousand groups. Call signs, authentication schedules, route codes for three courier lines, most of them dead now. I can recite the republic's entire diplomatic traffic key from Bari to Bern, and I have done it twice in the last week just to keep it warm."

"And if I gave you something else to carry? Something smaller. Denser. Something where a single wrong digit doesn't lose a shipment of olive oil but loses four men in a ravine?"

The tang of hot metal. The shutter that needed prying open on a winter morning. The way she'd looked at the window while speaking, counting sightlines. I put the pieces together slowly, the way one does when part of the mind is still refusing the picture: the chapel loft, the frequency of her absences, the fact that in a village of two hundred souls nobody ever seemed to know where Elena Voss was, and nobody ever seemed to mind.

"You're transmitting," I said quietly.

She didn't confirm it. She didn't need to. She simply turned back to the window, and the silence confirmed it better than any confession could have.

"That's why the shutter," I went on, feeling my way. "Ventilation for the set. The heat builds. And the antenna — the bell rope? You've run a wire along the old bell rope, haven't you, or inside it. That's why the loft stinks of solder."

For the first time since I'd met her, Elena Voss looked genuinely startled. It lasted perhaps a second and a half, and she converted it into irritation with remarkable speed, but I saw it land.

"Don't look so pleased with yourself," she said. "You've just described a way to get us both shot."

"I describe things. It's my profession. Describing is safe; it's carrying that gets you shot."

"And you would know about carrying."

"I know about carrying," I said, "better than anyone alive, at present, because almost everyone else who knows what I know is dead or on the other side of a broken bridge."

We looked at each other across the broken chair between us, and I understood that we had arrived at the real conversation by way of the furniture, which was, I suspect, entirely her method. She did not approach doors directly. She repaired hinges near them.

* * *

She made me wait three days. Later she told me this was deliberate — that trust given instantly is worth nothing, and that she'd spent those three days asking questions about me in the village, quietly, the way snow accumulates. What she learned, apparently, was that I ate little, drank little, walked the gorge path every morning, and had once spent an entire afternoon helping the cobbler's widow sort her late husband's tools without being asked.

"A man who sorts dead men's tools unasked," she reported, "is either sentimental or thorough. I'm gambling you're both."

It was evening, and she came to the inn where I lodged, and instead of sitting with me in the common room she tilted her head toward the door, and we walked out into the blue snow-light together, up the lane, into the chapel. Up the ladder, through the shuttered loft. And there, under a tarpaulin that had covered hay bales for thirty years, was a radio set built out of scavenged parts with a care that bordered on devotion — a military transceiver's guts in a carpenter's box, coils wound by hand, a dial marked not in frequencies but in tiny penciled crosses.

"This is a crime," I said. It seemed important to say it aloud, formally, the way one registers a document.

"This is several crimes," Elena corrected. "Possession. Transmission. Treason, depending on which government you ask, and I'd ask all of them, they change so quickly. Say the rest of it."

"The rest of what?"

"The rest of what you're thinking. You've been thinking it for three days; I can hear it from across a street. Say it, and then I'll tell you what I actually want from you, and then you can decide which crime you'd prefer."

I sat down on the chair I had mended — it held, which felt like an omen in my favor — and looked at the set glowing faintly under its tarpaulin edge, at the pencil crosses on the dial, at this woman who had crossed half of Europe to sit in a freezing loft above a deaf priest and talk to the dark.

"What I'm thinking," I said, "is that I burned half a republic's cipher books on a train full of refugees, and I have spent every night since wondering whether the other half is still worth carrying. I'm thinking that my government may not exist by spring. I'm thinking that I have been faithful to an idea for eleven years and that the idea is currently represented, insofar as it is represented anywhere, by me, alone, in a mountain village, talking to a woman who smells of solder." I paused. "And I'm thinking that whatever you transmit, someone receives, and keeps faith with, and acts on. Which is more than my sealed satchel can claim anymore."

She studied me for a long moment. Outside, the wind moved the bell rope, and somewhere below our feet the old bell shifted in its yoke with a sound like a sigh.

"Here is what I want," she said. "Three nights a week, the network passes traffic too long to risk sending twice. Schedules, movements, names. I encode, but I cannot memorize — I have a head for machines and none whatsoever for lists. If the patrol takes this loft, they take the set, the codebooks, and everything written down. So the network's rule is: nothing written stays longer than a night. Everything long goes into a head. Until now that head was Marta Ferri's, the schoolteacher, and last month the schoolteacher slipped on the ice and forgot an entire supply schedule, and we lost a week, and a man named Bruno spent that week hiding in a culvert." Her voice stayed level, but I heard the cost in it, banked like a fire for the night. "I need a courier. Not for papers. For memory. You'd take the traffic, hold it, walk it down the gorge line to the relay at the mill when the ice allows, and bring back replies. Nothing written. Nothing carried. Just you, and what you keep."

"And if I'm taken—"

"If you're taken, you know nothing. There will be nothing on you to know. That is the entire elegance of you, Marchetti. You are the safest object in this village precisely because you are full of the most dangerous things."

I laughed — unexpectedly, helplessly — and she allowed herself the ghost of a smile.

"There is one more thing," she said, and here she hesitated, which I would later understand she almost never did. "Your sister works for the commission. Everyone knows whose brother you are. If you do this, you aren't a neutral transit man anymore. You're a Marchetti who runs messages against the people who employ Chiara Marchetti. I'm not asking you to betray her. But I won't pretend the two things live in separate houses."

"No," I said. "They live in the same house. They've always lived in the same house. That's rather the family specialty."

"Then think about it."

"I don't need to think about it." I surprised myself with how quickly it came, and more with how true it was. "Elena — listen to me. On the train south, I burned half the state's secrets because I was ordered to, and it felt like nothing, because orders are a kind of anesthesia. This morning I watched a man named Teodoro strike names off a list with a pencil worn down to nothing, and every stroke cost him something visible. That's the difference, I think. Between duty and whatever this is." I gestured — at the set, at the loft, at her. "I've been carrying sealed things for eleven years. Sealed things don't ask anything of you except weight. I'd like, once, before the war finishes eating everything, to carry something open."

She looked at me for a moment without speaking, and in the dim light her face did something complicated — relief and suspicion arriving together, twins who don't get along.

"Open things can cut," she said finally.

"Yes," I said. "That's generally how one knows they're alive."

* * *

We began that same night. She woke me at two by throwing gravel at my window — an intimacy I resented and treasured in equal measure — and I climbed the chapel ladder in the dark with my heart going like a boy's. Under the tarpaulin the set breathed warmth into the cold loft, and she put a single sheet of paper in my hands: tonight's traffic, encoded, forty groups of figures.

"Read it. Twice. Then give it back."

I read it. I read it again. I handed it back, and she fed the paper into the stove pipe and the flames took it in a bright rush, and for a moment we were lit orange in the dark like figures in an altarpiece.

"Now," she said, "recite."

I recited. All forty groups, without a stumble, and when I finished she was quiet for so long I thought something had gone wrong.

"Marta couldn't do twenty," she said at last. "Marta needed the paper for days." She shook her head slowly. "Do you understand what you are? Do you have any idea what you're worth?"

"A man who sorts dead men's tools," I said, "and remembers things."

"A man who remembers things," she agreed softly. Then, briskly, before the softness could become anything either of us would have to account for: "Tomorrow night, the relay. There's a woman at the mill you'll need to meet, and an ice crossing you'll need to survive, and neither of them cares how good your memory is. Sleep today. Eat something. You'll want your legs."

She doused the lamp, and I went down the ladder into the dark of the chapel, and out into the snow, and the village lay silent around me, every chimney smoking its thin thread into the starlight, every window dark except one — the inn's back room, where a candle still burned.

And in that window, framed against the flame, stood a figure in a gray coat, watching the lane.

Watching me come down from the chapel at three in the morning, snow to my knees, from a building where I had no business being twice in one lifetime, let alone twice in one week.

Chiara did not move. She did not wave. She simply stood there, my sister, six years absent and suddenly, terribly present, and the candle threw her shadow long across the snow between us like a sentence waiting to be finished.
