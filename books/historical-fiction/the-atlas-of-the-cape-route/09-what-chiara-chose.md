## 9. What Chiara Chose

I had rehearsed six years of things to say to my sister in perhaps four minutes flat, standing outside the inn where the commission had billeted her, and by the time she actually opened the door every one of them had evaporated like breath on glass.

She was wearing gray. Of course she was. The occupying commission dressed its interpreters in a gray so precise it could not have been chosen — it must have been calculated, somewhere, by a man whose entire job was the psychology of wool. Gray said: I am not one of you and not one of them. Gray said: judge me by neither standard. It was a color that apologized for itself while refusing to apologize.

"Xanthe." She said my name the way you'd set down something fragile on an uncertain table.

"Chiara." I said hers the way you'd pick it back up.

Behind her, the inn's common room glowed amber with lamplight and the smell of the good coffee — the real stuff, requisitioned, which is to say stolen politely — that the officers drank while the village made do with roasted barley and memory. Somewhere upstairs a gramophone played something Viennese and slightly out of tune, as if even the music were collaborating only under duress.

"You'd better come in," she said, "before someone decides you're loitering."

"I am loitering."

"Yes, but you're my brother, so we'll call it family business and pretend it isn't suspicious."

* * *

He looked thinner than the boy who'd left for the ministry, and older than the man who should have arrived in his place. That's what war does to faces — it skips ahead through the boring decades and takes the interest out of your eyes. He stood in the doorway of the inn with snow melting off his shoulders and his satchel held against his chest like a breastbone he was afraid might crack, and I thought: he still carries everything against his chest. Some habits are just love with nowhere to put itself.

"Teodoro told me you were here," he said.

"Teodoro tells everyone everything eventually. It's how he runs the ledger. Information wants to be filed."

"I came to see you."

"I can see that." I stepped aside to let him in. "You came to see me the way a dentist comes to see a tooth."

"That's not fair."

"No," I agreed. "It's worse than a tooth. Teeth can be pulled." I heard myself say it and winced internally; six years of composing the perfect opening and I'd led with cruelty. But there it is. You don't get to choose your first sentence with family. Family chooses it for you, out of the oldest drawer.

We sat at a corner table the lamplight didn't quite trust. An officer two rooms away laughed at something. I poured him coffee because pouring things gives your hands a job when they'd otherwise do something honest.

* * *

The coffee was real. I noticed that before I noticed anything else, because in San Rocco real coffee was a form of currency more stable than the republic's lira, which by now had all the durability of autumn leaves. My sister drank real coffee while the village chewed barley. I filed this away next to the gray coat, next to the escort I'd seen her dismiss at the edge of the square, next to six years of silence.

"So," I said. "You requested this posting."

Her cup paused halfway to her mouth. A millimeter's hesitation — but Chiara and I had been raised in the same house, and in our house a millimeter was a paragraph.

"Who told you that?"

"Nobody. I deduced it. You taught me deduction yourself, if you remember — the winters we spent working out what Father was hiding in his study from the length of his silences."

"I remember." She set the cup down. "Yes. I requested it."

"Why?"

"Because our uncle is dying and our village is on a list, and I thought — I thought someone with access should be here when the arithmetic gets done." She said it cleanly, without flinching, and I will grant her this: she has never once lied badly. "And because I wanted to see you."

"There were other ways to see me. Letters exist. So does not being a collaborator."

"And there it is." She almost smiled. "Four minutes. You lasted longer than I expected."

* * *

What followed was the argument we had already had, in absentia, for six years — the one conducted across silence, each of us supplying both parts, each of us winning. Now it happened out loud, in the amber light, over stolen coffee, and I learned that hearing her voice say her half of it was infinitely worse than imagining it, because imagination lets you make your opponent stupid.

"The name," she said. "That's where you always stop listening, so let's start there. Marchetti. You know what happened to families with our name in the north after the collapse. You know what happened to the Riccis. To the old Ferrante woman. Our name is not a decoration, Xanthe, it's a liability with a history, and someone had to stand between it and the people holding the lists."

"People are holding lists," I said. "I've noticed. I'm carrying one, in a sense. But there's a difference between standing in front of a list and signing it."

"I never signed anyone's death."

"Not yet."

The words left me before I could weigh them, and I watched them land. Her face did something complicated and then chose simplicity instead.

"You think I'm stupid enough to believe you'd have done better?" she asked. "You, with your sealed satchel and your neutral transit staff and your beautiful professional calm? You measure your life in what fits in a bag. I measured mine in what would still be standing afterward. Different arithmetic. Same war."

* * *

He refused absolution the way Teodoro refuses to round numbers up — absolutely, on principle, with that maddening gentleness that makes refusal feel like a kindness being withheld for your own good.

I had known he would. I had requested this posting partly for the ledger and partly — I'll be honest here, since honesty is the one luxury the commission hasn't requisitioned yet — because I wanted to lose this argument in person rather than alone. There is a kind of relief in being judged by someone who knew you at eleven, who saw you cry over a dead sparrow and bury it with full honors behind the woodshed. Strangers' judgment slides off. Family's goes in like a splinter you'll carry to the grave.

But knowing I wanted to lose didn't mean I intended to surrender the field undefended.

"Our father," I said, "collaborated too. Did Teodoro ever tell you? Not with a uniform. With signatures. Small ones. He signed things in 'twenty-two that let men with torches find the print shop on Via Larga. He told himself it was protection. Protection of us." I watched my brother absorb this. "Teodoro burned those papers after the funeral. All except one page, which he keeps in the ledger box, which he shows no one, which he looks at sometimes the way you look at a scar in the bath. Do you know why he kept it?"

"To remember."

"To be unable to forget. There's a difference. Remembering is a choice you make. Being unable to forget is a sentence you serve." I leaned forward. "You're building yourself a very large archive, Chiara. And the men you work for keep excellent records too. One day someone will open yours and read it without our father's excuse of chaos, without the fog, with perfect hindsight, and they will not ask what you were protecting. They'll ask what you traded. And the answer will be names."

"I haven't traded any names."

"Not yet," I said again, softer this time, and hated myself for the echo, and meant it anyway.

* * *

There is a particular fury reserved for brothers — the kind that arrives dressed as disappointment and speaks in a reasonable tone. He sat there with his ministerial posture and his grief filed under efficiency, telling me about our father as though he'd discovered treason in a drawer, and the worst of it was that he was right in every particular and wrong in every conclusion.

"You want to know what I chose?" I said. "Fine. Here is the inventory. In November they came to the house asking about the Ferrante boy who'd gone into the hills. I told them he'd passed through weeks ago heading south. He hadn't — he was in our barn. They believed me because believing interpreters is efficient. He crossed the pass alive. That is one name I did not trade. Shall I continue? Because I can do this all night. Every gray coat has a lining, brother, and mine has more stitching in it than you'll ever bother to read."

"Then leave," I said. "Walk away from them. Come to Teodoro's ledger. Be useful where usefulness doesn't require a uniform."

"And abandon the access? Abandon the warnings I can give? You'd have me be pure and useless, when impure and useful is the only shape salvation comes in this winter."

"Purity wasn't what I proposed."

"No," she said. "You proposed cleanliness. Which is purity's coward cousin." She stood, gathered the cups, and for a moment her hands shook — the first honest tremor of the evening, worth more to me than the whole preceding hour. "You know what I remember about you? The sparrow. You buried it behind the woodshed and you cried, and Father said a boy shouldn't cry over birds, and you said, 'Someone has to account for it.' Account for it. You were nine. You've been keeping accounts ever since, and now you sit there with your sealed satchel and tell me my ledger is dirty. At least mine has living names written in it."

The gramophone upstairs wound down into clicking silence. Neither of us moved to wind it.

* * *

We parted at the inn door without touching, which is the Marchetti way — we are a family that expresses affection through the precise avoidance of sentiment, like diplomats at a funeral.

"One more thing," she said as I stepped into the snow. "Your midnight walks. Through the churchyard, past the chapel. I've seen you."

My spine went cold in a way the weather couldn't claim credit for.

"I cover for you at the checkpoint when the patrol asks," she continued, calmly, terribly. "I tell them you're ministry staff taking night air. So understand what I'm telling you, Xanthe: whatever you're doing in the dark, I already know, and I'm already lying for you. Think about that the next time you hand me a verdict."

She closed the door. The latch clicked with the finality of a cipher wheel finding its setting.

I stood in the falling snow a long moment, doing the thing I do — measuring, weighing, filing. My sister knew. My sister was covering. Which meant either I had a protector or I had a leash, and with Chiara the two had always been the same object.

I walked back toward the chapel loft, where Elena would be waiting with the evening's traffic, and found — this is the strange part, the part I'd never admit aloud — that my fear and my relief had braided themselves together so thoroughly I could no longer tell which was carrying the secrets and which was simply holding on.

Upstairs in the loft, the radio breathed its thin static song. Elena looked up at me, read my face in one glance the way she read frequencies, and said nothing — which was, tonight, exactly the right amount of absolution.

I took the pencil from her and began copying the first group of figures into memory, and beneath my sister's parting words, beneath the static, one thought kept time like a metronome:

Everyone in this family is now hiding something from everyone else, and the snow keeps falling on all of it equally, indifferent, white, and patient.
