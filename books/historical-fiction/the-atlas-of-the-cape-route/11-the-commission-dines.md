## 11. The Commission Dines

There is a particular kind of invitation that is not an invitation. It arrives in the voice of a junior officer who has rehearsed his courtesy in front of a mirror and delivers it with the flat certainty of a train timetable. *You will dine with the Commission tomorrow evening, signore. Neutral transit staff are expected to attend.* I was given no choice in the matter, which at least spared me the indignity of pretending to have one.

I learned of it from Elena first, oddly enough — a scrap of paper passed across the chapel loft's dusty rail while she pretended to teach me the names of saints whose frescoes had long since flaked into anonymity.

*They want you at the inn. Tomorrow. All the "transit people."*

"Transit people," I said under my breath. "We sound like luggage."

"You are luggage," she said, not unkindly. "The kind that walks."

She was gone before I could answer, down the ladder and out into the blue afternoon, and I stood among the saints feeling like something that had been checked through to a destination that no longer existed on any timetable.

* * *

San Rocco's inn — the Albergo della Campana, though the bell it was named for had been requisitioned for munitions two wars ago and its successor hung cracked and dumb in the tower — had not seen this many boots since the last time an army passed through and called itself a guest. The Commission arrived in three grey cars that ground up the switchback road trailing exhaust and importance: six officers, a stenographer with fingers like a concert pianist's, and enough wireless apparatus to make the mules of the village nervous for a week.

Chiara received them at the door in her grey coat, the one she wore the way other women wore armor or apology, depending on the light. I watched from across the square as she greeted the senior officer — a Colonel Vasseur, soft-spoken, with the face of a man who found the war distasteful but profitable — and I noticed something I did not want to notice: she was good at this. Not merely compliant. Good. She laughed at the right intervals. She steered the officers' coats to the right pegs and their egos to the right chairs. Six years ago she had been the girl who wept when our father drowned kittens rather than let them starve slowly; now she poured wine for men who signed lists, and her hand did not tremble.

That, more than anything she had said during our terrible reunion, told me what these years had cost her.

My own summons came by way of Lieutenant Brandt, the junior officer with the rehearsed courtesy, who found me stacking crates in Teodoro's office as though inventory were a form of prayer.

"The Colonel wishes all transit personnel present tomorrow evening," he said. "It is a courtesy. The Commission values its... neutral partners."

"Of course it does," I said. "Partners are so much easier to manage when they're fed."

He blinked at me, uncertain whether he'd been insulted, and decided — wisely, for a man of his rank — that he hadn't.

Teodoro, when I told him, only grunted over his ledger. "Wear the grey suit. Not the brown. The brown makes you look like a priest, and priests get asked questions." He paused, pen hovering. "And Xanthe. Listen more than you drink. A man's tongue loosens at the fourth glass, but so does yours, and yours has secrets in it worth more than anything you'll hear tonight."

"I thought neutrality meant not listening."

"Neutrality," my uncle said, "means listening very carefully and writing nothing down." He tapped his temple. "In there. Where the ink doesn't show."

He had no idea how literally I had already begun to follow that doctrine — half the republic's diplomatic ciphers living behind my eyes alongside, now, the growing braided rope of Elena's resistance traffic. My head had become the most dangerous piece of luggage in the province. I smiled and promised to be a very dull dinner guest.

I lied, of course. Or rather, I intended to keep the promise and failed, because keeping quiet is easy until someone starts talking about the port.

* * *

The dining room of the Bell had been transformed. Candles in wine bottles, a tablecloth that predated the Republic, and the innkeeper's daughter circulating with a tray and an expression suggesting she would happily poison everyone if the compensation were adequate. Chiara sat at the Colonel's right. I was placed at the far end, between a supply officer named Hauptmann Weiss, who smelled of cologne applied over fear, and the stenographer, who ate steadily and said nothing, which made him the second most dangerous man in the room after Chiara.

The talk began where such talk always begins: weather, roads, the price of eggs, the lamentable state of the mountain telephone lines. I performed dullness with genuine artistry. I asked about the road to the coast twice. I praised the soup. I let Weiss explain to me, at length and with diagrams drawn in spilled salt, exactly why the gorge bridge required a full season of repairs, as though he had personally laid every rivet.

But the fourth glass arrived for everyone at roughly the same moment, as glasses do, and Colonel Vasseur set down his own and looked down the length of the table with the mild satisfaction of a landlord surveying property.

"Gentlemen. And our friends." His gaze crossed me without friction, the way a searchlight crosses neutral shipping. "You should know that your patience will be rewarded. The southern port will be formally requisitioned within the fortnight. Military traffic exclusively."

The room did not go silent. That would have been too honest. Instead it went *careful* — the forks slowed, the breathing adjusted, and every civilian at that table performed the same invisible arithmetic at the same instant. I felt it pass through the room like a change in pressure. Forty-one names on a ledger in my uncle's office. Then twenty. And now this.

"Requisitioned," repeated the innkeeper, who had come in with the cheese and could not help himself.

"For the duration," said Vasseur pleasantly. "Which we all hope will be short." He did not say what would happen to the civilians waiting on the quay, because he didn't need to. Men like Vasseur never need to finish sentences; the sentence finishes itself inside you.

Beside me, Weiss refilled his glass with a hand that was not quite steady. Interesting, I thought. Even the occupiers have ranks of dread. He leaned toward me, collegial, conspiratorial, drunk in the specific way of a man who needs to tell someone something before it crushes him.

"They're moving the garrison convoys south ahead of schedule," he murmured. "Everything through the pass by the end of the month. After that—" he gestured vaguely with the bottle, "—the coast belongs to the navy, and the navy answers to no one you'd want to meet."

"The end of the month," I said.

"Sooner, if the thaw comes early." He drank. "You're transit staff. You'll be processed out with the last convoy. Lucky man. There won't be a last convoy after that one."

I thanked him for the information as though it were a compliment on my tie.

Down the table, Chiara was watching me. Not staring — she was too well-trained for staring — but her attention rested on me the way a hand rests on a door it intends to close quietly later. She had heard. Of course she had heard; she sat beside the source. And whatever calculation she was performing behind her composed face, I understood that she and I had just learned the same fact from opposite ends of the same table, and that one of us would decide what to do with it first.

I raised my glass to her slightly, a brother's gesture disguised as a stranger's politeness, and saw the corner of her mouth tighten. We were fluent in each other still, across six years of silence. It was the cruelest inheritance our family had left us.

* * *

Dessert was a dry cake and a speech. Vasseur stood, thanked the village for its hospitality in terms that made hospitality sound like a tax, and announced that the screening of departing passengers would begin "as soon as the bridge permits movement" — a phrase delivered with the offhand cruelty of a man announcing rain. Every passenger would be documented, interviewed, and cleared. *Cleared.* The word went around the table collecting meanings the way a snowball collects stones.

I thought of Elena's transmitter in the chapel loft. I thought of the false bottom in Teodoro's ledger drawer. I thought of forty-one names becoming twenty becoming, perhaps, none at all, and of a grey-coated sister whose papers would not survive a competent interview, and of a dying old man who refused to choose, and I realized I was doing what my uncle had warned against: writing things down. In there, where the ink doesn't show. The page was filling fast.

Afterward, in the square, the candles were being carried out and the cold came up from the gorge like a tide. Brandt, the courteous lieutenant, materialized beside me with my coat — an act of service so precisely calibrated that it functioned as a warning.

"The Colonel found the evening agreeable," he said. "He remarked that you ask excellent questions."

"Did he."

"He asks that you continue to be available. Transit coordination requires liaison. You may be summoned again." A pause, the rehearsal showing through. "It is better to be useful, signore. Useful people travel."

I took the coat. I thanked him. I walked back through the frozen square past the church, past the shuttered chapel, past the window behind which I knew a radio operator was pretending not to wait up for me, and I stood for a moment looking north toward the bridge — the closed bridge, the dead span, the iron spine of the gorge holding nothing across nothing — and I listened.

Under the ice, deep down, the river was already moving. Thaw-water, patient, pushing. The engineers said weeks. Weiss had said sooner. And somewhere south, at a quay I couldn't see, men were painting numbers on a ship's side that had nothing to do with berths.

I went inside, and I did not sleep, and in the morning I intended to find Elena and tell her that the clock we had all been pretending not to hear had just been wound in public, by a colonel, over dessert.

What I did not yet know — what I could not know until the ledger was opened and the ink examined — was how few pages it had left.
