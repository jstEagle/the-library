## 12. Teodoro's Heart

I have kept ledgers for fifty-one years, and I will tell you what I have learned about arithmetic: it is a coward's way of telling the truth. Numbers do not weep, do not flatter, do not look away. They simply stand there in their columns and wait for you to be brave enough to read them.

That January morning I was being a coward. I had been one for three days.

The berth list lay before me on the table of my office above the parish hall — forty-one names in my own careful hand, each one verified twice against the commission's manifest, each one a person I could describe from memory: not just face and age but the sound they made when they were frightened. Forty-one names. One place remaining. The officials in the south had cut our quota with a stroke of a pen in a warm room, and now the difference between forty and forty-one was a woman named Lucia Brandi who sang in the choir and had buried two sons, or a boy of sixteen whose mother was already across the water, or any of thirty-nine others whose faces arrived uninvited at three o'clock in the morning.

I had spent three days not choosing, which is itself a choice, and a dishonest one, because it let me pretend that the pen might simply decline to move forever.

So I did what I always do when the truth becomes unbearable: I checked the arithmetic again. The fourth time. As though the numbers might have changed their minds overnight, as though two plus two might take pity on me.

* * *

The office was cold enough that my breath showed. Wood was rationed by the same logic as everything else, and I had given my extra allocation weeks ago to the widow Ferrero, whose lungs were bad and whose gratitude embarrassed us both. My fingers ached. I remember noting that — noting it, filing it away under *age*, the drawer where I put everything I refused to examine. Sixty-eight years old, and I had been filing things in that drawer since I was sixty, since fifty-five, since the first morning I woke and thought, *huh*, and went downstairs anyway.

The stove had gone out again. I should have relit it. Instead I drew the list toward me and began, name by name, to re-verify the column of priorities: medical cases first, then mothers with children unaccompanied, then — and here the categories dissolved into the mud where all such categories eventually dissolve — judgment.

Aldo Pellegrini, millwright, fifty-one, lungs full of flour dust. Priority justified. His wife, healthy as an ox and twice as stubborn, would refuse to go without him; she had told me so twice, loudly, in front of witnesses, as if volume could bend a quota.

The Santin sisters, seventy-four and seventy-one. Both failing. One berth between them. I had written *one* beside their joint entry and then crossed it out, because what I had actually written, in the secret shorthand of my conscience, was *choose*.

On and on down the page. I knew them all. That was the cruelty of a small village — you cannot reduce human beings to categories when you have watched every one of them fall off a mule or get drunk at a wedding. The ministry men in the capital, who had drawn up these quotas over brandy, had the luxury of abstraction. I had Aldo's cough. I had the Santins' hands, folded identically in identical laps.

Somewhere around the twenty-third name — I no longer recall which; that is its own small confession — the pain began.

* * *

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only dignity left to a man in my position. It was not the theatrical clutching-at-the-chest you see in operas. It began as a weight, high on the left side, as if someone had set a stone there while I wasn't looking — a smooth river stone, the kind you find in the dry bed below the gorge, warmed by the sun. Except it grew. And beneath it, a dragging sensation, like a rope pulled through me, tightening with each beat of the heart, and the heart — I noticed this with the detached interest of a clerk reviewing another man's accounts — the heart was stumbling. Skipping. Running and then halting, like a horse asked to gallop through deep snow.

I sat back. I waited. Men of my generation are experts at waiting out weather.

It did not pass.

I considered, quite calmly, the following facts: that I was alone in the office; that the door was locked; that if I died at this desk, the list would be found under my hand, and someone — Don Anselmo, probably, good man, terrible with numbers — would have to make sense of my crossings-out and marginal notes, which even I could not fully interpret. This seemed less like dying and more like leaving a mess for a colleague. Unforgivable.

So I stood up. The floor tilted politely, like a deck. I took hold of the table. I remember thinking, with a flash of anger that surprised me: *not now*. Not now, of all times, with the thaw coming, with the ship's departure fixed, with forty-one names and one berth and a nephew sleeping under my roof who had arrived carrying secrets the way other men carry tobacco. Not now.

Then the floor stopped being polite.

* * *

What follows I know partly from memory and partly from testimony, because consciousness left me somewhere between the table and the door. Don Anselmo says he found me on the landing, half down the stairs, one hand still hooked absurdly into the banister rail as though the ledger were a drowning man and I would not let go. He says I was gray. He says I said something before I went under — he thinks it was "the list," though it may have been "the stove," because I had, in fact, just remembered the stove, and there is a certain comedy in a man's last coherent word being about firewood.

I woke on the parsonage daybed with the smell of camphor and candle wax and the doctor's fingers on my wrist.

Dr. Sabbatini was seventy and had delivered half the village and buried the other half. He had the bedside manner of a man who has seen too much to waste time on gentleness and too little time to afford cruelty. He counted my pulse for a long while, his lips moving slightly, and then he sat back on the stool — the stool creaked; everything in that house creaked; I remember fixating on that — and looked at me the way one accountant looks at another's ruined books.

"Well," he said.

"Well," I agreed.

"You know what it is."

"I have a suspicion."

"Your suspicion is correct." He rubbed his eyes under his spectacles. "The muscle is failing. The left side. It has been failing for some time — you've been sleeping propped up, yes? Two pillows?"

"Three, lately."

"Mm. And the swelling in your ankles you've been hiding with your socks."

"They're very good socks."

"Teodoro." He said my name the way he'd say it to a patient who was trying to negotiate with a tumor. "Dropsy. Fibrillation. The murmur I've heard for six years has become a roar. You are running a household, a ledger, an evacuation, and your own circulation off a pump that wants to retire." He paused. "Weeks. I'll say weeks and not days because you are too stubborn to die on schedule, but weeks. If you rest. Which you won't."

Outside the window, snow came down in the flat, businesslike way of the mountains, as if the sky too kept ledgers and was settling accounts. I lay there doing the thing I do — the thing I had done my whole life, the thing that had made me useful to ministries and committees and frightened people — I converted the sentence into figures.

*Weeks.* Call it four. Call it five if the pills helped and God was feeling administrative. The ship sailed when the bridge opened, or when the port fell, whichever came first — say February, possibly March. A man could die of arithmetic as easily as of a heart, I thought, and here was mine doing both at once.

"Sabbatini," I said. "How precise is your science? Could you tell me — if I asked — whether I will see the ship leave?"

He was quiet for a moment. Old doctors know when a question is really a request for permission to plan.

"No," he said finally. "And you wouldn't believe me if I guessed. But I'll tell you this: whatever you intend to finish, finish it early. Hearts like yours don't send warnings. They resign without notice, like a good clerk who's found better employment." He stood, gathered his bag, then stopped at the door. "And Teodoro — tell someone. Whatever it is you're carrying in that ledger besides names. Dying men hoard secrets the way misers hoard coin, and it spends them faster."

* * *

They wanted me to stay in bed. I gave them two days, which I regarded as extravagant, and returned to the office on the third with instructions to climb the stairs slowly and sit near the window, and with Don Anselmo stationed downstairs as a kind of human bell should I topple again.

Here is what nobody tells you about a death sentence issued in weeks rather than years: it simplifies things. All my life I had deferred. Deferred the letter to my brother in Genoa, deferred the apology to the Voss family for a slight committed in 1919, deferred the question of what I actually believed about God, politics, my own marriage — everything filed, everything postponed, on the assumption that the ledger of my days had blank pages enough at the back. Now the pages were numbered and few, and the strangest sensation came over me: lightness. Every task became urgent, and therefore possible. A man with unlimited time can avoid anything. A man with five weeks must choose, and choosing, I find, is the only prayer I have ever been any good at.

So I chose to work. Not despite the diagnosis but *because* of it. The list would not wait and neither would I, and there was a fierce comfort in the alignment.

Xanthe found me there that afternoon. My nephew — Elena's word for him, though she didn't use it yet, and neither did I aloud — stood in the doorway with that watchful half-English stillness of his, taking inventory of the room the way he took inventory of everything: the blanket over my knees, the ink on my fingers, the list squared precisely on the table.

"Don Anselmo talks," I said, before he could arrange his concern into questions. Concern, in my experience, is mostly curiosity wearing its Sunday clothes.

"He's worried about you."

"Don Anselmo worries the way rain falls. On everything equally." I tapped the page. "Sit. I need a second pair of eyes. Mine are apparently mortal now, which introduces errors."

He sat. For a while we worked in silence, the two of us, checking names against manifests, and it struck me — with a pain quite unrelated to my heart — how natural it felt. How he counted under his breath exactly as my brother used to, how he held the pen exactly as I do, loose near the tip, the way our grandfather taught us both through him. Blood keeps its own books.

"The doctor gave you a figure," Xanthe said at last. Not a question. He had inherited that too — the inability to leave a number unexamined.

"Weeks."

"And you're working."

"What else would I do — write memoirs? Admire the view?" I turned the list toward him. Forty-one names. One line left blank where the forty-first decision lived, breathing. "Look at this, Xanthe. Tell me what you see."

He looked for a long time. Longer than most would bother. Then: "Forty people and one seat."

"Wrong. Look again."

He frowned. I watched him do it — genuinely do it, turn the page over in his mind the way I had hoped someone in this family someday would.

"Forty-one people," he said slowly, "and one seat. And whoever holds the pen is the forty-second."

I confess my eyes stung. Old men cry easily; it is the body economizing, using up its surplus where it can. But I did not let him see. I merely nodded and said, "Good. You'd be amazed how many people never get past the arithmetic. They count the names and forget to count themselves. The pen-holder. The chooser. He goes on the list too, Xanthe — he goes on some list, somewhere, in some ledger we don't get to audit. And I have been holding that pen for three months, and my arm is tired, and now—" I laid my hand flat on my chest, on the traitorous stone of it, "—now the pen itself is failing."

"You shouldn't be alone with this."

"Everyone dies alone with it. Some of us just get advance notice of the appointment." I closed the ledger. Outside, the bells rang the hour — San Rocco's bells, cracked since the shelling of '17, a semitone flat so that the whole valley lived perpetually slightly out of tune, and no one minded anymore. "There's a thing I have to ask you. Not yet. Soon. Before the thaw. But not today."

"Today I'm only asking you to eat something and sleep."

"How English. Eat, sleep, keep calm and carry on." But I let him pull me to my feet, and I let him take my arm on the stairs, which cost me more pride than I care to record, and that night I slept propped on three pillows listening to my own heart run its irregular accounts — *beat-beat-skip, beat-rest, beat-beat-beat* — like a clerk drunk at his desk, and I thought: *so that's the sound. That's what it sounds like, the last column filling.*

* * *

In the mornings now I wake early — the heart wakes me, thumping its corrections — and I lie in the dark doing what I have decided to think of as the final audit. Not of the ship's list. Of my own.

Fifty-one years of entries. Debits I prefer not to itemize: the man I denounced in '22 to save the family name — that entry sits in the deepest drawer, the one I will open soon, because Sabbatini was right about hoarding and because a testament that omits its worst lines is not a testament but a forgery, and I have spent my life distinguishing those. Credits: the eleven hundred souls passed through my hands toward safety in three wars, of whom I can account for all but a handful. Outstanding liabilities: one blank line on one list, and a nephew who carries government seals in his coat, and a niece in a gray coat whose name I cannot write even in my private ledger without my hand shaking — though now, God help me, everything shakes; the hand has joined the conspiracy.

The strange thing, the thing I would tell Sabbatini if he asked and I felt like answering honestly, is that I am not afraid. I am annoyed. Death at sixty-eight, with work unfinished, is not a tragedy; it is a bureaucratic inconvenience, and I resent it the way I resent all inefficiency. There is a list. There is one seat. There are forty-two claimants including the man with the pen. And now the man with the pen has been given notice that his office closes within the month.

Very well. Then the audit accelerates. Then I must decide — not the village's forty names, those are ranked by rule and mercy and are nearly settled — but the other question, the family question, the question I have been circling for weeks like a moth around a lamp: when the true accounting comes, among the Marchettis themselves, whose name goes in the last line?

Xanthe will say himself. Chiara will say Xanthe. Xanthe will then say Chiara. I know these children; I have known them since they were heights marked on a doorframe. They will spend the whole winter donating the seat back and forth like a plate at a funeral feast until the ship sails empty-hearted.

Unless I settle it first. Unless I do what a ledger-keeper does, which is close the books myself, in my own hand, before someone else's hand does it worse.

My heart stumbled again just now, writing this — a long skip, a full second of silence in which the pen hovered and I thought, with detached professional interest: *ah, there it is, the resignation arriving early.* Then it resumed, grumbling, loyal, unreliable as ever.

Weeks, the doctor said. Then I had better hurry.

Tomorrow — tomorrow I will call the boy upstairs, and I will put the question to him plainly, the question that has no honest answer, the question I have dreaded more than the diagnosis: *when there is one place and three of us, Teodoro, who do you save?*

No. Not that question. That question he can dodge.

The real question, the one that will crack him open like ice on the gorge: *whom do you love enough to lose?*

Because that is what the last berth is, in the end. Not salvation. A loss, assigned. And someone must hold the pen when it is written down.
