## 14. The Informer

There is a particular hour in a mountain village when the bells stop meaning anything. In San Rocco that hour comes around four in the afternoon, after the Angelus has been rung twice by mistake — once by old Don Anselmo, who counts on his fingers now, and once by whoever minds the rope in his absence — and the sound simply lies over the rooftops like snow, weightless and everywhere, signifying nothing except that another day has survived itself.

I was in the chapel loft at that hour, with my earphones on and my pencil moving, copying down a message from Turin that I would rather not have understood. It concerned grain requisitions for our province. The numbers were large. The word used was *raccolta* — collection — as though the wheat were being asked politely to come along.

I had been transmitting for eleven minutes. That mattered later, so let me say it plainly: eleven minutes, beginning at ten past four.

The set was where it had always been since December — under the floorboards beneath the third pew from the back, the one with the loose kneeler that everyone assumed was simply badly made. It was a reasonable hiding place. Reasonable places are what get people killed. A man who searches a chapel searches pews, floors, the sacristy, the confessional, and if he is thorough, the belfry. He does not search the hay loft above the stable behind the presbytery, because why would he? Hay is hay. Unless the hay has been arranged by someone who has spent two months thinking about nothing else.

That morning — this is the part I want written down correctly, because afterward there were versions — that morning I woke at five with a feeling I could only describe as an itch behind the teeth. Not fear exactly. Fear has a shape. This was more like the sensation you get when you have left the stove on, or said a name aloud that you shouldn't have, somewhere, to someone, and cannot remember which door it went out of.

So I moved the set.

It took me until seven. I carried it up through the frozen garden in a flour sack, on the pretext — if anyone asked, which no one did, because at seven in the morning in San Rocco everyone is busy pretending not to see everyone else — of feeding the priest's goat, who does not exist, the goat having been eaten at Christmas, though the shed remains and so does my excuse. Under the hay, wrapped in oilcloth, wedged between the beam and the wall where the mortar has crumbled into a gap exactly the size of a radio transmitter, because God has a sense of humor and it is a dry one.

Then I swept out the loft under the floorboards, scattered fresh hay dust, replaced the loose board, and knelt in the third pew for twenty minutes doing actual praying, which I recommend to anyone who has just committed a suspicious act inside a church. It covers the sound of your own conscience rearranging furniture.

At half past nine, Marta Rinaldi came to me in the lane outside the bakery and told me that the widow Bertani's nephew had been asking questions.

*

You should understand about questions in San Rocco: they travel faster than the mail, faster than the patrols, faster than grief. Marta delivered hers in the manner of all village intelligence — sideways, while discussing something else entirely, in this case the price of salt.

"Fourteen lire," she said. "Can you imagine. And the Bertani boy was in here yesterday asking after you."

"Asking what?"

"Whether you kept regular hours." She weighed salt into a twist of paper and did not look at me. "Whether you went up to the chapel at night. Whether you had visitors."

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him you were courting the Marchetti courier and kept scandalous hours for entirely human reasons." She handed me the paper. "It's the one story nobody wants to examine too closely. Shame is an excellent lock."

Marta is seventy-one years old and has buried a husband and two sons, one of each war, and she has concluded from this that modesty is a luxury of the unburdened. I took my salt and my humiliation and walked home with both, running the arithmetic.

The Bertani boy. Twenty, maybe, soft-handed, lately seen wearing a coat too good for the village and standing too straight for a farm laborer. He had arrived in October. He had taken up, conveniently, with his aunt, whose house sat with a view of the chapel path.

An informer does not look like anything. That is the first lesson and the hardest. He looks like a nephew. He looks bored. He looks like a young man with nowhere to be, leaning on a wall in the late afternoon, watching a woman climb to a church that holds no service worth attending.

I did three things that evening. I wrote out, in my head and then burned in the stove, the contingency plan: if I am taken, the set stays where it is until spring, then goes to the mill relay, then to no one, because by spring there will likely be no one. I ate supper at the inn — deliberately, publicly, sitting across from Xanthe Marchetti and letting him talk about bridge engineering until I could have built the thing myself — so that the village would have something else to discuss besides my habits.

And I slept in my boots.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is the currency this whole account runs on: I was afraid, but it was a clean fear, almost a comfort. Fear means the danger is still abstract. The night I stopped being afraid would be the night I should start digging my own grave.

The knock came at six the next morning.

*

Not on my door. On the presbytery door. Which was worse, and smarter of them, because a raid announces itself and a polite inquiry does not.

I heard it from the yard, where I was breaking ice on the trough — again with the goat-shed in view, again with my hands in plain sight, a habit I had developed the way other women develop prayer. Four soldiers and a sergeant, breath steaming, boots loud on stones that had heard everything San Rocco had ever done wrong and kept most of it.

The sergeant was not local. He had the flat vowels of the north and the patience of a man being paid by the hour.

"Don Anselmo," he was saying as I came near, "we have received information regarding unauthorized transmissions from church property. You'll want to cooperate fully."

"Transmissions." Don Anselmo held the door with the gentle bewilderment he wore like vestments. "Sergeant, the last transmission from this parish was my sermon on the Prodigal Son, and I assure you, nobody received it."

Nobody laughed. Soldiers never do, at first. They laugh afterward, in barracks, retelling the priest who joked at them, and by the third telling they've decided they arrested him for treason instead, because it sits better.

They searched the chapel.

I will describe it, because someone should, and because the details are the only part of any occupation that survives accurately. They came in with their caps off — even these men, even now, some reflex of childhood holding — and then put them back on when the sergeant wasn't looking, and began.

Pews: lifted, turned, tapped. Floorboards: the entire floor, walked heel-first by a corporal with an ear for hollows, which he plainly had, because he paused at the third pew for a long moment and I felt the pause go through me like cold water. He knelt. He pressed the loose board. It moved — I had loosened it further that morning, oiled the nail, made its hollowness read as rot rather than concealment — and beneath it, packed tight, lay forty years of candle stubs and mouse-nest insulation and one hymnal with water damage, and nothing else.

The corporal looked at the space for a long time. Long enough that the sergeant crossed the nave to see what he'd found. Long enough that I, standing by the font with my missal like a prop in a play I hadn't auditioned for, had time to compose my own arrest, my own interrogation, my own last line, which I decided would be something better than *please*.

He replaced the board. "Rotten timber," he said.

"Whole church is rotten," the sergeant said. "Like the country."

They did the sacristy. They did the confessional — Don Anselmo objected on theological grounds, was ignored, and then got his revenge by hearing, unasked, every soldier's sins as they worked within arm's reach of him, muttering absolutions under his breath with such cheerful efficiency that two of them went red and one laughed outright and was barked at. They did the belfry, disturbing three hundred pigeons into a fury that covered, I noted with professional appreciation, any sound a small transmitter might make, had there been one to make it.

And then — this is the moment I keep returning to, the hinge of that whole gray week — the sergeant turned and looked at me.

"You," he said. "Voss. Elena Voss. You're registered as residing at the presbytery."

"I am."

"You use the chapel."

"For prayer, Sergeant. It's generally considered the intended use."

He crossed the nave toward me, and I stood still, because running is a confession and fainting is a cliché, and because I had learned long ago — from whom, I couldn't now tell you; perhaps from watching my father refuse to flinch at things far worse than a sergeant — that stillness is the loudest thing you can say.

"You were seen here at unusual hours."

"The hours I pray are unusual because my work is unusual. I mend vestments. I keep the accounts for the parish. Don Anselmo's eyes—" I gestured at the priest, who immediately, magnificently, squinted like a mole emerging into daylight.

"It's true," Don Anselmo said. "Since Advent I can barely find the collection plate. Signorina Voss does the arithmetic. If her arithmetic involves the Almighty, I'm sure He needs the help."

Something shifted in the sergeant's face — not belief; men like him don't believe, they file — but a decision to spend his suspicion elsewhere. There had been transmissions, yes, triangulated roughly to this valley, yes, but valleys are full of chapels and barns and attics and one very chatty radio operator in the carabinieri post who transmitted his card games home nightly in clear violation of every regulation. A village of stone and snow swallows signals whole. They had come with a report and found a rotten floorboard and a joking priest and a woman with a missal, and reports need evidence the way fires need wood, and there was none.

"We may return," the sergeant said, to no one in particular.

"People often do," Don Anselmo said. "That's rather the point of a church."

They left. Their footsteps faded down the lane. The pigeons resettled. And I discovered that my knees had opinions, and leaned against the font until they finished expressing them.

Don Anselmo waited until the door was shut. Then he looked at me with eyes that, whatever he pretended about the collection plate, missed nothing and had missed nothing for seventy years.

"The third pew," he said, "has been loose since the flood of '29. I've meant to fix it." He smoothed his cassock. "I never seem to find the time."

"Father—"

"I'm an old man, Elena, and old men hear a great deal and repeat very little. It's the arrangement I have with God. He keeps my heart beating; I keep my mouth shut." He turned toward the altar. "But whoever your correspondent is — and I am not saying there is one — you might mention that the flour sack route past the goat shed is visible from the Bertani house on clear mornings. The widow's window faces east."

I had not told him anything. I had not needed to. That is the terrible economy of villages: everyone knows everything, and the price of knowing is silence, and most people pay it gladly, and a few pay it by dying, and the rest light candles.

*

I moved nothing that day. Moving things the day after a search is the second mistake after hiding things in reasonable places. Instead I sat in the hay loft that evening with the set silent beside me, wrapped in oilcloth like a body prepared for burial, and copied out by memory the traffic I had not dared transmit, and thought about the shape of the noose.

Because that's what it was. Not a raid — raids are blunt instruments and this had been a scalpel's first pass. Someone had reported specific information: transmissions, a location, a woman with irregular hours. Someone had watched me long enough to build a pattern. And patterns, unlike sermons, always get received.

The Bertani boy. Almost certainly the Bertani boy, or whoever paid him, which amounted to the same coat. But certainty is a luxury; the report could as easily have come from the inn, from the checkpoint log, from a soldier's letter home intercepted and mined for gossip. Suspicion in an occupied valley is like damp: it gets into everything, and you never locate the source, you only watch the walls stain.

What I could not shake, lying there in the hay with my breath making ghosts in the cold, was the arithmetic of names. Whoever informed had needed something to inform *with*. Hours. Movements. A face at a window. My movements were careful. My hours were constructed. But I did not move alone anymore — and here my mind did the thing I least wanted it to do, which was walk itself, uninvited, up the hill to the inn, to a room where a ministry courier with a satchel full of secrets kept midnight appointments with a frequency only he and I shared.

Xanthe was careful too. Careful as a man counting coins in the dark. But careful is not invisible, and the village had already assigned us a story — Marta's story, the courting story — and stories protect until they don't. A woman climbing to a chapel at night is strange. A woman climbing to a chapel at night to meet a man is merely human. And the day someone decides to look past *merely human* — the day a Bertani boy follows the lantern glow up the path and sees two shadows in the loft where the hay should be undisturbed —

I lay back and stared at the rafters and made myself finish the thought, because unfinished thoughts fester.

If the noose was tightening, it was tightening around more than me now. Every meeting doubled the risk. Every message we memorized together was a rope we braided between us, and I could not decide, staring upward into the dark, whether that made it stronger or merely easier to hang us both with.

Below me, in the village, a lamp moved up the hill from the inn toward the Marchetti house — a slow, deliberate light I knew by its rhythm, because I had watched it too many nights to pretend otherwise, and because I am, whatever else I am, a woman who reads signals.

It stopped at the gate. It hesitated there longer than a man walking home should hesitate.

And then it went out altogether, and the hillside went dark, and somewhere below, in the house by the road, Chiara Marchetti was awake — I would learn this later, how much she saw, how much she counted, how carefully she said nothing — watching the same dark I was watching, from the other side of her brother's door.

The set stayed cold that night. Some frequencies are better left untransmitted until you know who is listening.
