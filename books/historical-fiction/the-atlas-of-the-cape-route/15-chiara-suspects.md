## 15. Chiara Suspects

There is a particular kind of insomnia reserved for people who have done something wrong and gotten away with it. It is not guilt that keeps you awake — guilt, I have found, is a luxury for people who still believe in consequences. It is arithmetic. You lie awake doing sums: who saw you, what they will say, how long the story holds before it frays at the edges like a cheap overcoat.

I had been doing this arithmetic since 1938, so by the winter of '43 I was very good at it, and very tired, and I slept the way a soldier sleeps on a march — lightly, in fragments, one ear always open for the sound that doesn't belong.

The sound that didn't belong, that February, was my brother's boots on the inn stairs at half past midnight.

* * *

You must understand something about Xanthe before you can understand why his footsteps frightened me more than the patrols did. My brother has never in his life been late for anything, nor early, nor anywhere he was not expected to be. As a boy he kept his shoes paired under his bed with military precision no one taught him, laces tucked inside, ready to be found. When our mother died he arranged the funeral the way other men arrange bank loans — carefully, punctually, weeping only after all the paperwork was done. He is a man who measures out his life in what fits into a satchel, and he does not leave the satchel unattended.

So when I heard him come down those stairs at an hour when honest men are horizontal, and when I heard him pause — *pause*, Xanthe, who pauses for nothing — at the third step from the bottom, where the stair creaks, and then step over it, my first thought was not *my brother has a woman*.

My first thought was: *someone has finally given him something worth lying about.*

And my second thought, arriving with the cold certainty of a hand on the back of your neck, was: *God help whoever that someone is.*

* * *

I want to be fair to myself here, which is difficult, because being fair to yourself requires a witness, and I have spent six years ensuring I don't deserve one.

I did not go looking for trouble. Trouble came to San Rocco wearing gray coats and calling itself a Commission, and I had made my peace with it the way you make peace with weather. I translated. That was my work — menus and requisition forms and the occasional interrogation transcript softened at the edges where I could soften them without anyone noticing. I told myself, in the early years, that I was protecting the family name. Later I told myself I was protecting Teodoro. Later than that I stopped telling myself anything, because the stories wear out faster than the coats.

But I knew what midnight movement meant in an occupied village. I had read enough transcripts to know. And I knew my brother had arrived in San Rocco carrying a ministry satchel and a face like a locked drawer, and I knew he had spent two evenings talking to Elena Voss, the quiet one from the chapel, whose hands were stained with printer's ink she claimed came from restoring hymnals.

Hymnals. In February. In a village where the chapel hadn't held a full congregation since the bells were requisitioned.

* * *

The checkpoint went up on the mill road three days after I first heard the stairs.

It was a lazy thing at first — two carabinieri, a lantern, a table borrowed from the inn itself, which struck me as the occupation in miniature: they take your furniture and use it to stop you going home. But laziness in checkpoints curdles fast. By the second week there were four men, a dog, and orders to log every soul moving between eleven at night and five in the morning. The sergeant — Vittorio, from the Commission's security section, a Bolognese with soft hands and hard eyes — had taken to standing the late watch himself, because, he said, nothing interesting ever happened on day shifts, and Sergeant Vittorio was a man who collected interesting things the way other men collect stamps.

I heard the stairs again on the fourth night of the checkpoint. Half past twelve. The creaking step stepped over, always stepped over now — he'd learned its geography in a week, my careful brother, learning the terrain of his own deception with the same thoroughness he'd once learned conjugations.

I lay in the dark and did arithmetic.

If Xanthe was moving at night, he was either meeting someone or delivering something. If he was delivering something, it was either letters, weapons, or words. Weapons left traces — oil, metal, the smell of them. Letters could be swallowed, but no one risks a checkpoint nightly for letters. Which left words. Words meant a radio. A radio meant Elena Voss, and Elena Voss meant the network the Commission's security section had been sniffing after since December, the transmissions they called *the nightingale* because they could hear her singing and couldn't find the tree.

And if my brother was the courier of the nightingale, then every night he walked out that door, he walked past my arithmetic into theirs, and the only variable I controlled was Sergeant Vittorio's curiosity.

* * *

Here is what I decided, lying awake while my brother's boots faded down the frozen lane toward the gorge road: I decided I would not ask him.

This surprised me. It should surprise you. Six years earlier, when our positions had been reversed — when it was Xanthe in the republic's ministry and I signing the receipt for my gray coat — I had gone to him expecting judgment and received it, delivered with that terrible gentle precision of his, like a surgeon apologizing while he cuts. He had refused absolution then. He would refuse it now. Asking him directly would only teach him to hide better, and hiding better, in San Rocco in the winter of '43, was the same as dying later.

No. If I was going to protect him — and understand, I had not yet admitted to myself that this was protection; I dressed it up as risk management, as keeping my own ledger clean, as all the comfortable phrases collaborationists invent at three in the morning — if I was going to protect him, it would have to be done the way I did everything now. Deceived. Softened at the edges. Signed in someone else's name.

* * *

The opportunity came sooner than I expected, which should have warned me.

On the sixth night I did not hear the stairs until nearly two, and when I did, I heard something else underneath them: the dog. Not barking — worse. The low conversational growl a dog makes when it has found something and is deciding whether to announce it.

I was dressed in four minutes. I had practiced this, though I'd have denied practicing — coat over nightdress, the Commission armband in the drawer by the door since the week I arrived, worn twice, both times to make sure wearing it felt like nothing. Vanity of vanities: I had even rehearsed my expression in the black glass of the window. The expression was boredom. Boredom is the uniform under all my other uniforms.

The lane to the checkpoint ran downhill past the churchyard, and the snow squeaked under my heels the whole way, announcing me, and I let it. Surprise is for people with clean hands.

Sergeant Vittorio stood at the lantern with his hands in his pockets, watching the dog strain at its lead toward the shadowed bend where the gorge road dropped toward the riverbed. Two carabinieri had their rifles unslung, which was theater — they were boys, farm boys, the older one yawning — but theater gets people killed when everyone starts believing the script.

"Signora Marchetti." Vittorio didn't turn. "Late for church."

"The inn's stove smokes. I walk until it clears." I came to stand beside him, close enough that the armband caught the lantern light, and I looked exactly where he was looking, at the empty dark bend of the road, and I thought: *please be past it, Xanthe, please be in the riverbed already, please for once in your punctual life be early.*

"You walk," Vittorio said. "At two in the morning. Toward the checkpoint."

"I walk until the smoke clears. The direction is not my affair."

The dog lunged, once, and was hauled back, and in that half-second of noise Vittorio's head turned toward it, and I used the half-second. Not to do anything. To be seen doing nothing — to shift my weight, sigh, glance at my watch, the small choreography of a woman with somewhere warmer to be. People believe choreography. They believe it more readily than evidence.

"Your brother," Vittorio said.

My stomach performed a slow, cold revolution, and my face, thanks be to whatever gods look after liars, performed nothing at all.

"What about him?"

"He came through Tuesday night. Logged it himself, polite as a priest. Says he can't sleep since the bridge closed. Walking." Vittorio smiled at the dark. "A family trait, apparently."

"Insomnia runs in us like bad teeth." I took out a cigarette I didn't want and lit it with hands that were steady, because I had ordered them to be steady and they were mine. "He walks, I walk. Half this village can't sleep. You've requisitioned the bells, Sergeant. You've taken the men's work and the boys' futures and the harbor. What did you imagine was going to happen to the village's nights?"

It was a good speech. Too good — I heard the polish in it as I said it, the little rhetorical flourishes I used in translation work to make officers feel clever, and I watched Vittorio hear it too, the way a card player watches another card player admire the deck.

Then, from the dark beyond the lantern, a voice said: "Everything all right?"

And Xanthe walked into the light.

* * *

I have seen my brother afraid perhaps four times in our lives. Once when our mother's coffin went down. Once during the bombing of the port quarter, when a wall came apart across the street from him and he stood in the falling dust doing, I am certain, arithmetic. Once — and this one I have never told anyone — the night he found me burning my first Commission letterhead in the garden, years ago, home on leave, and he looked at the ash and said nothing and went inside, and his face as the door closed was the face of a man closing a door on a room he has decided never to enter again.

And now. Standing in the lantern light at a checkpoint with snow in his hair and the gorge road behind him, my brother looked at me with an expression of such complete, composed blankness that I understood instantly he had been afraid every single night, and had simply gotten better at it than I had.

"Sergeant." He nodded. "Signora." A pause of exquisite timing. "You're far from the inn."

"My sister walks off the stove smoke," I said. "I walk off the cold. We're a family of insomniacs."

"Ah." He turned to Vittorio. "Trouble?"

"The dog smells something on the gorge road."

"Do dogs need a reason?" Xanthe bent and offered his gloved hand to the animal with the easy confidence of a man who has never feared anything with four legs. The dog — traitor, blessing, miracle — licked his glove and sat. "There. Whatever crime she smelled, it's confessed and forgiven. May we all pass the checkpoint so easily."

Vittorio laughed. It was the wrong laugh, thin at the top, the laugh of a man filing something away, and I watched my brother file Vittorio's laugh away too, each of us collecting the other, siblings after all.

"Go home, both of you," Vittorio said at last. "Before the dog changes his mind about forgiveness."

We walked up the lane together, my brother and I, two paces apart the way strangers stand, and neither of us spoke until the checkpoint light was a smudge behind us. Then, at the churchyard wall, he stopped.

"You didn't have to come down."

"No," I said. "Neither did you have to be out."

The silence between us filled with everything we weren't saying, and it was so crowded, that silence — six years of it, the gray coat, the burned letterhead, the funeral paperwork, the whole museum of our mutual disappointments — that I almost laughed at the sheer logistics of it. Where would we even begin? There wasn't room in one night. There might not be room in one war.

"Chiara." He looked at me across the snow-light, and for a moment his composure slipped and I saw underneath it, and what was underneath was not fear. It was exhaustion, the deep kind, the kind that comes from carrying sealed things. "Whatever you think—"

"I don't think anything," I said. "That's my profession now. Thinking is above my pay grade."

"Chiara."

"I said goodnight, Xanthe." I climbed the churchyard steps. At the top I paused, and I will confess this here because no one else will ever know it: I wanted, desperately, childishly, to say *be careful*. To say *I covered for you*. To say *whatever you're doing for that girl with the ink-stained hands, know that I stood in the freezing dark and lied to a dangerous man with soft hands, and I did it without being asked, and I don't know what that makes me, but it isn't nothing.*

What I actually said was: "The third stair from the bottom creaks. Have it seen to. An innkeeper who lets his stairs betray him deserves the firing squad."

And I heard, behind me, very quietly, my brother laugh — the first time in six years — and go inside.

* * *

Vittorio came to the inn two days later for dinner, uninvited, which was his way of knocking.

He ate slowly and talked pleasantly about the thaw, the roads, the scandalous price of wine, and twice he mentioned — casually, the way you mention weather to a farmer whose crops you intend to confiscate — the nightingale. Transmissions continuing. Direction-finding improving. A new set of ears coming up from the coast within the fortnight, he'd heard; specialists.

"They'll want billeting," he said to the room generally, and then specifically to me, with his soft smile. "Perhaps you could arrange it, Signora Marchetti. You're so good at arranging."

I said of course. Of course, gladly, anything for the Commission's comfort.

And upstairs, I heard the third stair creak under my brother's weight — he'd stopped stepping over it, after that night, which I chose to read as a message — and below the creak, fainter, from the direction of the chapel, the wind changing, or maybe not the wind.

I sat at the cleared table with Vittorio's wine rings drying on the wood and did the only arithmetic left to me: a fortnight. Whatever my brother and the nightingale were building in the dark, they had a fortnight before the specialists came with their new ears.

And I, it appeared, had a fortnight to decide what a sister who had already sold her soul in installments would sell next — and whether, this time, she might manage to buy something back.
