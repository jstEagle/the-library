## 16. The Berth Question

I have spent thirty years keeping ledgers, and in all that time I have learned one truth that no auditor will ever put in a report: a ledger is never about numbers. It is about what people cannot say to each other's faces. The numbers are only the paper walls we build so we don't have to hear the crying on the other side.

On the evening the question finally came out of me — the question I had been carrying in my chest like a stone in a boot for eleven days — I did what I always do with unbearable things. I set the table properly. I lit two lamps instead of one. I opened the good bottle of wine, the one I had been saving for an occasion, and there being no occasions left in San Rocco except departures and deaths, I decided this qualified as both.

Xanthe came to my office above the warehouse at seven, as I had asked him to. He knocked, which amused me. A man who carries his country's secrets in his skull knocking at his own uncle's door as though he might be turned away.

"Uncle," he said. "You wanted me."

"Sit. Drink."

He sat the way young men sit when they suspect a trap — perched, coat still buttoned, eyes already cataloguing the exits. He had his father's shoulders and my sister's mouth, which meant he could lie beautifully and knew it, which is the most dangerous combination a face can carry.

"Is this about the list?" he asked.

"Everything in this village is about the list," I said. "The bread is about the list. The weather is about the list. Old Marta knits scarves for people on the list. You are drinking wine that is on the list." I poured him a glass. "So yes. It is about the list."

* * *

Here is what the ledger said, and I will write it plainly here because I could not say it plainly to his face, which is the whole disease of our family in one sentence.

The ship *Adriana* would sail from the southern port when the bridge reopened — if it reopened before the port fell, which was a wager even God was declining to place money on. Her civilian allowance had been cut twice already. Forty-one names remained on my master manifest. Berths available: twenty-two. And among the Marchetti papers, after the commissions and the quotas and the requisition clerks had done their work like moths through wool, there remained exactly one place.

One. For three of us.

I had known the arithmetic since December. I had carried it through Christmas, such as it was, through the audit, through the nights when my heart stuttered like a bad engine and I lay listening to it and thinking: *you are a machine with a defect notice already stamped on you.* I had watched Xanthe walk the gorge edge in the evenings and wondered if he was doing sums too. I had watched Chiara come down from the inn in her gray coat, straight-backed as a fence post, and wondered what she told herself at night to sleep. I had done nothing, said nothing, because choosing is a kind of killing and I am by trade a man who records other people's killings, not commits his own.

But the thaw was coming. The engineers said days now. And a man who defers a decision until events decide it for him has not avoided the decision — he has merely chosen cowardice, which is also a choice, and the one I least respect because I have caught myself making it so often.

So: the good wine, the two lamps, the nephew in the chair across from me looking like a dispatch waiting to be read.

"Do you know why I took this work?" I asked him.

"Someone had to keep the lists honest."

"Wrong. Someone had to keep the lists *cruel*. Honest is easy. Anyone can be honest. What this job required was a man cruel enough to strike names and decent enough to hate himself for it, every single day, so that the striking never became easy." I turned my glass. "I have struck four hundred and six names from these manifests since September. Do you know how many of them I can see when I close my eyes?"

He didn't answer. Good. An answer would have been worse than silence.

"All of them," I said. "Every one. In order. That is my pension, Xanthe. That is what I retire on."

"Uncle—"

"The berth." I laid the paper on the table between us, face up, the way you'd lay down a card you couldn't win with. One name-line, blank. "One place. Three Marchettis. The commission takes Chiara's papers apart the moment the screening starts — she knows it, I know it, and whether she deserves rescue is a theological question I am too tired to adjudicate. Your heart is fine, your lungs are fine, you are twenty-six years old and the world will want whatever is inside your head for another fifty years. And I—" I smiled, because one should decorate the truth where possible, "—I have a heart that a village doctor described to me last week in terms usually reserved for condemned buildings."

"I know about your heart," Xanthe said quietly. "Elena told me."

Of course she had. That woman moved information through this village the way water moves through limestone — invisibly, and wearing channels as it went.

"So you understand the arithmetic," I said. "You have understood it longer than I've let myself say it aloud. So I am asking you formally, nephew, as the administrator of the evacuation and as the head of what remains of this family: who takes the berth?"

* * *

And here is the thing I want written down somewhere, in whatever archive survives us, because it is the only honest thing I produced that winter:

I asked him hoping he would say *me*. Take it, Uncle. Take the place.

Because if he said that, then the decision was made, and clean, and I could die in San Rocco with my ledger balanced and my guilt at least symmetrical. If he said *Chiara*, I would have argued — I surprised myself by knowing this — I would have fought him over it, because a man does not get to spend his sister's life buying back his own conscience. And if he had said *yourself*, Teodoro, take it yourself — if he had tried to hand it back to me, this dying old bookkeeper — I think I might have struck him. Not from anger. From love, which in our family has always expressed itself as violence anyway.

What he actually did was worse than any of those.

He looked at the paper for a long time. Then he looked at the window, where the ice on the gorge was making its low sounds, the groaning of something enormous changing its mind. Then he said:

"I defer."

"You *defer*."

"I do. To you. You're the administrator. It's your ledger. It should be your line to draw."

I want to be fair to him, and fairness in our family is a currency we mint rarely and spend badly. There are two readings of what he did that night. The generous reading: he refused to play judge because judging would cost him either his uncle or his sister or his soul, and he chose to keep all three intact by handing the knife back to me. The ungenerous reading: he was a courier, and couriers do not open the packages.

Both readings are true. That is the moral trap, and I built it, and I named it aloud to him while the wine went warm in the glasses:

"You understand what you're doing," I said. "By deferring, you make me choose. And whichever way I choose, you will carry the answer like freight for the rest of your life — *my uncle sent her away*, or *my uncle killed himself for nothing*, or *my uncle chose me and I never asked him to*. Deferring isn't innocence, boy. It's just murder by committee of one."

"I know," he said. And his voice didn't shake, which told me exactly how much he'd rehearsed it. "But I'd rather owe you than owe myself."

"There it is," I said. "The Marchetti motto at last. We'd rather owe someone else. Sixty years I've waited for someone to say it out loud."

We drank. The wine was very good and neither of us tasted it.

* * *

After he left — he stood, thanked me for the wine with terrible politeness, and went out into the blue dark — I sat alone with the blank line and did what administrators do when the arithmetic fails. I cheated.

I took out a fresh draft sheet and began writing versions of the manifest, the way I had done forty times before, striking and restoring names like a man rearranging furniture on a sinking ship. Version one: Teodoro Marchetti. Struck. He's dying anyway; the voyage would kill him faster than the heart would; better he dies at home among his files than in a steerage bunk. But then version two: if Teodoro stays, who protects Chiara at the screening? No one. She goes into that inspection hall alone with her commission papers and her interpreter's badge and comes out the far side in handcuffs or worse. Strike Teodoro, condemn Chiara. Version three: Chiara crosses, Teodoro stays, Xanthe — Xanthe what? Xanthe walks into the mountains with his memorized codes like some medieval monk, and dies of it, or doesn't, and either way the family name ends in a snowdrift. And version four, the one my pen kept drifting toward no matter how I pulled it: give it to the boy. Give it to Xanthe, who carries things, whose survival serves something larger than any of us, and let the old man and the collaborator settle their accounts with this village together, which is, if you think about it, the most honest ending available.

My hand shook writing that one. Not from the heart. From something above the heart.

I struck myself off again. Fourth time that week. Elena Voss keeps count; I'm sure of it; that girl counts everything.

* * *

It was near midnight when I climbed down to check the warehouse seals — habit, ritual, the little ceremonies that keep a man upright — and from the loading door I saw a figure going down toward the river path. Slim, quick, collar up. Not going toward the inn. Going toward the gorge, where no sane person goes at midnight, where the ice talks to itself in voices like furniture being broken slowly in another room.

I knew the walk. I'd known it since he was nine years old and used to run that same slanted, eager gait down to the mill stream to see what the water had brought.

Where was my nephew going, at midnight, in the last week of winter, down to the frozen riverbed below a bridge that was about to reopen?

I am an old man with a failing heart and a blank line on a manifest, and I stood in the cold doorway and understood suddenly that I had been keeping accounts of the wrong household. Everyone in this family was hiding a ledger. Chiara had hers at the inn. Xanthe had his under the ice. And mine — mine was only the visible one, the decoy, the public book — while the real entries were being made in the dark, in pencil, in hands much younger and steadier than mine.

The figure reached the shadow of the abutment and was gone. Beside him, small and sure-footed, a second figure — a woman's, moving with the particular confidence of someone who knows exactly which stones hold weight.

I did not call out. I am an administrator. I know contraband when I see it walking.

I went back upstairs, took the draft sheet with its four versions, and fed the worst of them — the one where I survive — into the stove. The paper burned green and quick. Somewhere down in the gorge the ice groaned once, long, like a held note, and I thought: *thaw.*

Then I thought: *what in God's name is that boy crossing the river to do?*
