## 17. Night Crossing of the Gorge

The moon was against us, which Elena called a gift and I called a symptom of her general relationship with danger. No light meant no witnesses, but it also meant no seeing, and there is a school of thought — my school, formerly quite small and now enjoying rapid enrollment — which holds that walking across a frozen river in absolute darkness beneath a broken bridge is not an activity but a diagnosis.

"You're doing the thing," Elena whispered beside me.

"What thing?"

"The thing where your face does arithmetic." We stood at the lip of the gorge behind the chapel, where the path dropped away into blackness and the wind came up out of the dark carrying the smell of ice and stone. "You add up all the ways this can go wrong and then you stand very still, as if stillness were a solution."

"It's cheaper than most solutions."

"Move," she said, and we moved.

I should explain how I arrived at the bottom of that path, since only hours earlier I had been a respectable man with sealed codes in his satchel and a legitimate complaint about his circumstances. The explanation is Elena Voss, who had come to me at dusk with a request so plainly worded it took me a full minute to understand she wasn't joking. There was a relay station on the far side of the gorge — a mill, two partisans, a radio that needed parts she could not obtain from San Rocco because every road east was watched. The riverbed below the bridge was passable now that the water had frozen hard enough to hold a man's weight. Mostly hard enough. She said *mostly* the way other people say *please*.

She had not asked me to come. That was the trap of it. She had described the route, the need, the hour of departure, and then looked at me with those gray eyes that gave away nothing except, perhaps, a bet she'd placed on me without telling me the stakes.

"I could go alone," she'd said.

"Yes."

"But two sets of eyes on the ice are better than one, and if I fall through, someone should know where the set is buried."

"That's a terrible reason to bring anyone."

"It's the truest one I have."

So: dusk, the satchel locked under the loose flagstone in Teodoro's cellar — I would not carry the codes onto the ice; some superstitions are load-bearing — and my pockets filled instead with the radio parts wrapped in wool, and my heart conducting its usual audit of fear versus obligation and finding, for once, that neither column balanced.

* * *

The first hundred meters of descent were the worst, or so I believed at the time, in the way one believes each present moment is the worst until the next one arrives to correct the record. The path had been cut into the gorge wall by goats or geologists; the distinction mattered less than the fact that neither had installed railings. Elena went ahead with a rope tied around my waist — her rope, her knots, her instructions — paying it out hand over hand while I pressed myself against rock that was colder than any idea of cold I'd brought down from the north.

"Left foot," she said. "There's a shelf. Trust the shelf."

"I've known shelves to betray men."

"Not this one. This one's been here ten thousand years."

"Most traitors have seniority."

She laughed — a short, compressed sound, quickly smothered, the laugh of someone who has learned that noise is a currency spent best in small denominations. But it reached me. In the dark, with my palms bleeding slowly against the rock and the void pulling at my coat hem like a curious child, that single note of laughter did more for my morale than the entire Italian army had managed in four years.

We came down onto the riverbed and the world changed character entirely. Above us the bridge stood in silhouette, a broken sentence across the sky — the engineers had blown the middle span in November, and the severed ends hung over the gorge like the two halves of a snapped wishbone, waiting for spring to grant them. Below, the river lay frozen in long white terraces where it had frozen in stages, freezing and dropping, freezing and dropping, so that the surface was a staircase of ice plates tilted at angles that made my ankles file formal complaints.

"Stay off the white that shines," Elena murmured. "Walk the dull ice. Shiny means thin, thin means new, new means water underneath deciding whether to be winter yet."

"And the dull ice?"

"The dull ice has been thinking about it since October."

We crossed the first terrace in silence. My breath came out in flags. Somewhere above, a chunk of the broken span ticked in the wind, metal contracting, a sound like a clock counting something down in a language I didn't speak. I kept my eyes on Elena's boots and my mind on a discipline I'd learned in the ministry: when the body is afraid, give it facts. Facts are ballast. The parts in my left pocket weighed six hundred grams. The parts in my right pocket weighed four hundred. The distance to the far wall was approximately nine hundred meters. The temperature was cold. These were the facts. They helped exactly as much as facts ever help, which is to say they gave the fear something to organize itself around.

Halfway across, the river spoke.

It was a groan — deep, tectonic, rising out of the ice beneath our feet like the voice of something turning over in its sleep. I stopped so abruptly that Elena, roped to me, was jerked half around.

"Don't move," she whispered.

"It spoke."

"It always speaks. It's settling. Ice settles at night when the temperature drops and the river underneath pulls at it." Her hand found my wrist in the dark and gripped it. Not romantically. The way you grip a man standing on a trapdoor. "Breathe."

"I'm breathing."

"You're wheezing with punctuation."

The groan rolled on beneath us, traveling, I realized, along the length of the frozen river like sound along a wire, passing under our boots and away toward the sea, and then it faded, and the night resumed its ordinary business of being enormous and indifferent. Elena's hand stayed on my wrist a moment longer than the physics required. Then she turned and walked on, and I followed, and I noticed that I was smiling, which under the circumstances suggested either courage or brain damage, and I have never been able to rule out the second.

* * *

Two-thirds of the way across, we found the cart.

That is what I decided to call it, though it was really just the skeleton of one — an iron frame, two wheels sunk to their hubs in refrozen slush, a drift of snow banked against it like a shroud. A refugee cart, Elena said. From October, when the last columns came through before the bridge closed. Some of them had tried to cross here rather than wait for the engineers' schedule, because October people always believe winter will negotiate.

"Were they on it?" I asked.

"No. They abandoned it. It went through the ice two days later." She rested her gloved hand briefly on the iron rim, the way you touch a gravestone you don't have time to read. "A family from the coast. The mother carried a birdcage. Empty. She wouldn't say why she'd brought it."

We stepped around the cart with more care than physics demanded. Some things deserve ceremony.

Beyond it the ice changed — the terraces gave way to a broad flat sheet, dull gray, old, confident — and we made better time, if time can be said to be made by two people bent double against the wind, moving at the pace of cautious elderly penguins. Elena navigated by feel, by memory, by some interior compass she never explained. Once she stopped, held up a fist, and we waited a full five minutes in the dark while a searchlight swept the bridge abutment above us — slow, bored, institutional — and passed on. Patrols crossed the gorge top every forty minutes, she'd told me. Their lamps couldn't reach the riverbed. But sound traveled up the gorge walls like rumor travels up a village street, and a shout down here would be everyone's business in thirty seconds.

"Why the mill?" I asked during the walk after that, when the silence had begun to press on me like a debt collector. "Why is the relay on the far side? Everything else is on this side. You. The chapel. The network."

"Because everything else can be burned," she said. "The set can't be. If San Rocco falls, if they sweep the valley, the relay has to survive to tell the port what happened here. Someone has to be the memory that gets out." A pause, wind filling it. "You of all people should understand keeping something alive by carrying it."

I did not answer that. There are remarks that land too close to the bone to be answered honestly and too honest to be answered dishonestly, and the only remaining option is silence, which she accepted with what I suspected was satisfaction.

The far wall rose up out of the dark sooner than I expected — a black mass resolving, as we neared, into scree slopes and the skeletal geometry of a ruined footbridge, its cables hanging in catenaries of frost. Elena found the start of a trail by touch, counting boulders under her breath like a woman saying a rosary, and led me up through a fold in the rock to where a light showed — dimmed to a coal-red ember behind sacking, but unmistakably a light, and unmistakably human.

"Halt." The whisper came from the dark to our left, low and level. "Word."

"Elena Voss. And a friend."

"His name."

She looked at me. In the faint red spill from the mill door I saw her face clearly for the first time since the chapel — flushed with cold, eyes bright, a strand of hair frozen across her cheekbone — and I understood that the question was not procedure. The question was the whole examination.

"Xanthe Marchetti," I said.

The darkness considered this. Somewhere in it, a bolt was drawn back, unhurried, the sound of a decision being made at leisure by men who owned the night.

"Marchetti," the voice repeated. "Teodoro's nephew. The courier." A shape resolved out of the black — bearded, young, with a rifle held the way farm boys hold rifles, which is to say like an extension of their own arms. "Your uncle struck his own name off the list again today. Third draft."

"I know."

"He thinks nobody notices." The man almost smiled. "Everybody notices. Come in before the wind decides to report you both."

* * *

The mill was a single stone room with a broken waterwheel and a stove that had been fed past hunger into a state of sullen orange glow. Two men — the bearded one, whose name was Sandro, and an older silent one called only il Professore, who sat sorting radio components on a blanket as though performing surgery — and a smell of woodsmoke, machine oil, and coffee stretched with chicory until it qualified as metaphor. Elena shed the rope, the rucksack, the outer layers of caution, in that order, and became in the stove-light someone I had only met in fragments: quick, certain, younger-seeming than the dark allowed.

Sandro heated water. Il Professore opened my wool bundles with fingers gone precise from months of this work, examined each component, and grunted — twice with approval, once with the particular grunt of a craftsman betrayed by supply chains.

"This capacitor," he said, holding it to the ember-light, "was manufactured for a sewing machine."

"It was what I could get."

"It will do." He said it the way absolution is pronounced. "Tell Marchetti what we heard tonight, Sandro."

Sandro crouched by the stove. What they had heard was this: the garrison at the southern port had doubled its guard three days ago. Fuel barges were being moved to the outer harbor, out of bombing range, which meant someone expected bombing. And the requisition order for civilian shipping — the order that would decide whether the last ship out carried refugees or ammunition — had been signed and was sitting in the commission's field office, waiting only for the occupying colonel's countersignature.

"The colonel is coming to San Rocco," Sandro said. He glanced at me, and something in the glance told me he knew exactly whose sister interpreted for that colonel's commission, and chose, for reasons of his own, to leave the connection unspoken. "Within the week, they say. To inspect the bridge works personally."

Elena and I looked at each other across the stove. The firelight did its work on her face, and I watched her arrive at the same arithmetic I was doing: the bridge opening in spring, the port falling perhaps before spring, and between those two dates a window the width of a knife blade, through which forty-one names and one ship must somehow pass.

"That's why the dispatches matter now," she said quietly. More to herself than to me. "Everything that moves from here on moves through that window."

Il Professore began fitting the sewing-machine capacitor into the guts of the radio with the tenderness of a man dressing a wound. Sandro poured three tin cups of the chicory and handed them around, and the four of us drank in a silence that was not empty — it was the loaded kind, the kind that comes before things are said that cannot be taken back.

And it was then, warming my hands on a tin cup in a smuggler's mill at the bottom of the world, that Elena turned to me with the ember-light in her eyes and an expression I had not seen on her before — not the courier's neutrality, not the operator's focus, but something more like a person standing at a door she had decided, finally, to open.

"There's something you should hear," she said, "before we cross back. Something about why I asked you to come tonight, and it isn't the capacitors."

The stove ticked. The river groaned somewhere below us in the dark, patient as a creditor.

"Sit," said Elena Voss. "This will take the rest of the night."
