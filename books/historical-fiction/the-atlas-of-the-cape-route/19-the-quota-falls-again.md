## 19. The Quota Falls Again

The radio came for us on a Tuesday, which I have always considered the most bureaucratic of days. Nothing good has ever arrived in San Rocco on a Tuesday. Wednesdays bring the priest's soup to the infirmary, Thursdays bring the post if the roads are open, and Fridays bring the fish cart from the valley, smelling of diesel and herring and other people's better fortunes. But Tuesdays bring only paper.

This particular Tuesday it was Marta Bellini who brought it — a girl of perhaps nineteen with a bicycle too large for her and a message pouch too heavy for her conscience. She pedaled up through the slush to the schoolhouse where I keep the ledger, and she stood there dripping snowmelt onto my floorboards, holding the envelope the way one holds a dead bird found in the garden: respectfully, and at arm's length.

"From the port authority," she said. "Marked urgent."

"Everything is marked urgent," I told her. "Urgency is the cheapest ink in Italy these days."

She did not laugh. The young do not laugh at my jokes anymore, possibly because they have stopped being jokes, or possibly because they were never funny and only the war made me believe they were. She set the envelope on the table beside the ledger and left, and I sat with both documents side by side like two animals that had not yet decided whether they were going to fight.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is apparently all I have left to offer anyone: I knew what the envelope contained before I opened it. You develop a certain instinct after forty years of administering things. The weight of bad news is different from the weight of ordinary paper. Ordinary paper is indifferent; it falls however it falls. Bad news falls flat, evenly, as though it has already been read by everyone else in the world and is tired of being carried.

I opened it anyway. One does. That is the whole profession, really — opening envelopes that fall flat.

*By order of the Maritime Requisition Commission,* the letter began, in the handwriting of a man who had clearly been promoted beyond his talent, *the civilian allowance aboard the vessel* Fortuna *is hereby reduced by fifty percent effective immediately. Manifests exceeding twenty (20) civilian berths will not be honored.*

Twenty. We had forty-one names. I had spent six weeks building that list the way a mason builds a wall, stone by stone, argument by argument, favor by favor, each name mortared into place with someone's pleading or someone's gratitude or, in two cases I am not proud of, someone's fear of me. Forty-one people. A doctor whose hands the south will need. Three children under ten, because even the commission had once pretended children counted double. The widow Ferrero, whose son died at the front and whose daughter went mad quietly, the way some women do, still sweeping her step every morning. And the Marchettis, or what remained of us: myself, my niece Chiara, my nephew Xanthe, three names I had protected through every previous cut with the shameless partiality of an old man who has nothing left to protect but his own blood.

Forty-one names for twenty places. The arithmetic was so simple it felt like violence. Twenty-one people would remain on this side of the gorge when the ice broke, standing on the shore, watching the ship go, and then watching whatever came after the ship go. There was no elegant way to say it. Half the village was going to be subtracted.

I poured myself the small glass of grappa I permit before noon only on days when the alternative is weeping, and I sat down to do the thing I am supposedly good at, which is choosing.

Here is what nobody tells you about mercy: it is mostly bookkeeping. People imagine mercy as some grand gesture, a soldier throwing himself on a grenade, a stranger giving away his coat. But real mercy, daily mercy, the kind that keeps a village alive, is a woman deciding which neighbor gets the last of the oil, a doctor deciding which patient gets the penicillin, an old clerk deciding which name stays on a list. It is arithmetic performed by people who would rather be doing anything else, and it wears you down to the wire the way water wears stone — slowly, and without any single moment you can point to as the moment you were broken.

I had done this arithmetic before. In 'twenty-two, during the famine year, I kept the bread lists. I was younger then and believed that fairness was a system you could build, that if you wrote the rules correctly the rules would carry the weight for you. By spring I had learned that rules are just words, and words bend for whoever holds the pen, and the pen always ends up in the hand of someone who knows the applicants personally, which is to say: someone who must look them in the face afterward. I gave extra bread that winter to the family of a man who had once lent me money, and I told myself it was need-based, and part of me has never forgiven the rest of me for the sentence.

So I understood, sitting there with my grappa and my flat-falling letter, exactly what kind of work Tuesday had brought me. And I discovered something that surprised me: I could not begin.

Not would not — could not. I picked up the pen. I looked at the forty-one names. I thought: alphabetical? Absurd. The alphabet has never saved anyone. By age? Then I am sentencing the old, including myself, and dressing murder as respect. By usefulness? Then I become the commission itself, weighing human beings like cargo, and Chiara's masters could not have drawn up a more efficient form. By lottery? Then I hand my neighbors' lives to chance and spend my remaining weeks listening to them curse God in my presence, which they would be right to do.

Every system was a mask, and beneath every mask was the same old man deciding who lived, and I found — this is the confession of this chapter, write it down — that I preferred to leave the mask off. If the choice was monstrous, let it at least be honestly monstrous. Let no one say Teodoro Marchetti hid behind procedure. Procedure is how cowards kill.

I put the pen down. I did not strike a single name. I sat in the cold schoolhouse until my fingers stiffened, looking at forty-one names that now required a massacre, and I accomplished precisely nothing, and I want the record to show that accomplishing nothing was the hardest day's work of my life.

*

Xanthe found me there at dusk. He has a way of arriving without announcing himself — a habit, I suspect, of his profession, though he claims the ministry taught him nothing useful except how to fold a map. He stood in the doorway with snow in his dark hair, looking younger than his thirty years and older than mine, which is a trick only war can perform.

"You've heard," he said.

"The radio heard for both of us." I slid the letter across the table rather than pretend otherwise. Hiding things from Xanthe has never worked; he reads paper upside down the way other men read faces.

He read it twice. I watched him do the arithmetic — he is his mother's son, quick with numbers, slow to show what they cost him — and then he did something I did not expect. He laughed. Not bitterly, exactly. More the way a man laughs when the blow he braced for finally lands and turns out to be exactly as heavy as feared, and there is a strange comfort in that, in being right about your ruin.

"Twenty," he said. "Of course it's twenty. Round numbers. Someone in an office needed the manifest to fit a column."

"That is precisely the obscenity of it," I said. "It isn't cruelty with a face. It's cruelty with a rubber stamp. Somewhere south of here a man reduced our village by half between lunch and his afternoon coffee, and he will sleep well tonight, because he never saw a single name. To him it was tonnage."

"To us?"

"To us it's the widow Ferrero." I said it before I could stop myself, and watched it land. "It's the Bortolotti children. It's—" I gestured at the ledger, at everything in it. "That's the difference between their arithmetic and ours. Theirs divides evenly. Ours doesn't divide at all."

He pulled out the chair opposite me and sat, and for a while neither of us spoke, and the stove ticked, and outside the ice on the gorge groaned its long note, the sound it makes when the thaw begins thinking about beginning. We had grown used to that sound. It was the village's pulse now.

"You should choose," he said finally. Quietly. Not cruelly — Xanthe is incapable of cruelty, which is either his virtue or his defect, I have never settled the matter. "Someone has to. If you don't, the port authority will cut the list themselves, and they'll cut from the bottom, and the bottom will be whoever filed their papers latest. Is that what you want?"

"No."

"Then—"

"I said no, not yes." I pressed my palms flat against the ledger pages, as if I could hold the names down by force, pin them there like insects that might otherwise fly off to safety. "You're asking me to pick twenty-one people to abandon. I have known every soul on this list since before they had teeth. I buried half their parents. I recorded their births in that same book — look, here, the Ferrero girl, born in the blue April, I wrote *healthy, loud.* And now I'm meant to write *left behind* beside her name in the same hand. The same hand, Xanthe. Do you understand what that does to a man's hand?"

"It does something worse if you refuse and the commission chooses instead."

"And what does it make you, doing it?" I asked. "Answer me that. What does it make the man who chooses?"

He was silent a long moment. Outside, the ice groaned again.

"It makes you the uncle who stayed," he said at last, "and stayed, and stayed, keeping the lists while everyone else left. It makes you the reason anyone got out at all. Forty-one names, Uncle. Before this letter it was going to be thirty-nine of them alive because of you. Now it's nineteen. Nineteen is not nothing. Nineteen is not nothing."

"Nineteen," I repeated. "You see how quickly we've learned to speak this language? Nineteen. As though the number were a mercy because it isn't eighteen." I took off my spectacles and rubbed the bridge of my nose, an old man's gesture, and I felt every one of my years arrive at once like creditors at a door. "When I was a boy my father told me that a man's worth is measured by what he refuses to do. I thought it was peasant wisdom. It turns out it was the only wisdom on the mountain."

"So you'll refuse."

"I'll refuse." I put the spectacles back on and met his eyes. "Let them send their officer. Let him stand in this room and tell me which grandmother to erase, and let him do it looking at me while he says it. I have one act left in me, nephew, and it is not going to be efficiency."

Something moved across his face then — I want to call it relief, though relief is too clean a word for anything in this winter. Perhaps recognition. He reached across the table and touched the ledger, lightly, the way you touch a coffin lid.

"There's another way," he said.

"There is no other way. There is only the way we can live with afterward, and those are rarely the same road."

But he didn't argue further, and that worried me more than argument would have. Xanthe argues the way water works — patiently, around obstacles, over years. When he goes quiet it is because he has found a crack somewhere. He stood, thanked me for nothing in particular, and went out into the blue dusk, and I watched him from the window walking toward the chapel end of the village with the stride of a man carrying something heavier than satchels.

*

I did not sleep. At two in the morning I rose, lit the lamp, and walked back down to the schoolhouse through falling snow, because an old man's bed becomes intolerable when his ledger lies unguarded elsewhere. I told myself I would simply sit with it. Keep vigil. Refuse aloud, in an empty room, so that the refusal existed somewhere outside my own skull.

Instead I found myself doing the unforgivable thing: reading the names. Not striking them — I want that entered plainly in whatever record survives me — only reading them, one by one, the way a priest reads the dying. *Ferrero, Lucia. Bortolotti, Marco; Bortolotti, Elena; Bortolotti, small Pia.* My finger moved down the page and my lips moved with it, and somewhere past the twentieth name I understood that I was not keeping vigil over the list. I was saying goodbye to all of them in advance, the way you rehearse a speech you pray you'll never give.

And then, near the bottom, my own name. *Marchetti, Teodoro.* Written in my own careful hand, in the first draft, weeks ago, when I still believed there would be room for everyone and that I would board the ship last, the shepherd boarding after the flock. My heart did its new trick — the stumble, the catch, the long pause as though considering whether to continue — and I sat very still until it decided.

The doctor says weeks. The doctor says it kindly, in the vocabulary of management: *rest, avoid exertion, avoid distress.* As though distress were a spice one could simply leave out of the dish. I have administered distress to this village for six years. It is in everything now, in the bread and the water and the ledgers.

I looked at my own name for a long time. And I will admit what I admitted to no one, not even the lamp: I did not strike it out. But I did not defend it either. I merely noticed, with a clerk's detached precision, that of all the forty-one names on the page, mine was the one whose removal would cost the list the least. An old man with a failing heart is not cargo anyone fights over. And a list needs an advocate more than it needs its author.

No. That is vanity dressed as sacrifice, and I am too old to lie in my own head. The truth is simpler and worse: I am afraid of the water. Have been since I was nine, when I went under the ferry at Trieste and came up believing I had seen the bottom of the world. I could dress the fear in duty — the sick old man yields his place, noble, arithmetic-friendly — but the ledger of my conscience demands the plain entry: Teodoro Marchetti does not want to drown, and would rather die in his own bed above his own gorge than in a gray sea among strangers.

There. That is what bureaucracy does, in the end. It does not make monsters of us. It makes us honest, brutally, at gunpoint, about the small cowardices we'd have carried comfortably to our graves. Twenty berths, and every one of the forty-one of us suddenly knows exactly what we're worth to ourselves.

I blew out the lamp. Through the window the gorge lay black under the snow, and far below it the river ran on under the ice, patient as paperwork, carrying everything toward the sea whether we chose it or not.

In the morning, I knew, the letter would demand its answer. In the morning, Xanthe would come back with whatever he had found in the night. In the morning, the ice would groan again, closer now, the sound of a door preparing to open onto the last crossing any of us would ever be offered.

I went home through the snow, past the shuttered chapel, past the inn where light still burned in a window that had no business burning at that hour — and I made myself a promise I had no right to make and no intention of breaking:

Whatever happened, whatever orders fell, whatever names fell with them, I would not be the hand that struck them. Let history find another pen.

The light in the inn window watched me pass. And I had the sudden, unclerk-like certainty that my nephew was awake behind some other window in this village tonight, and that whatever he was carrying in his head — codes, ciphers, plans, sins — it was heavier than anything on my list, and lighter than nothing at all.
