## 20. Chiara's Bargain

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that women of my family are taught early, though no one ever names it as a lesson. My mother did it with the household accounts, hiding a deficit in one column inside a surplus in another, and called it *management*. Uncle Teodoro does it with his evacuation ledger, forty-one souls and one berth, and calls it *duty*. And I have learned to do it with my conscience: you take what is rotten on one side of the page and balance it against something clean on the other, and if you keep the columns close enough together, at night, in bad light, they almost look like the same sum.

I had been rehearsing the bargain for three days before I spoke it aloud.

Not that I admitted to myself I was rehearsing. I told myself I was *observing*. That is the first lie every collaborator learns — that she is merely watching, gathering, keeping her ear open. The commission officers dined at the inn twice more that week, and I sat among them translating menus and insults with equal fluency, and all the while some quiet clerk behind my eyes was filing things away: which of them complained about partisan activity on the mill road, which of them had been reprimanded for letting a courier slip through a checkpoint, which of them owed which favors to whom.

Hauptmann Weissler was the one who mattered. He was not the senior officer present — that honor belonged to a colonel whose name I never learned because he never once looked directly at me, as though I were furniture with an accent — but Weissler was the one who did the actual work, and the actual work was what interested me. He had a habit, when he thought no one was attending, of reading the movement orders upside down off the table, the way schoolchildren do across the aisle. A man who reads upside down is a man who believes information is the only currency that never devalues.

I decided he and I could do business.

* * *

You will want to know how I justified it. Everyone wants to know how I justified it; my brother asked me, six years too late and with his jaw set like our father's, whether I ever told myself stories to sleep at night. Yes, Xanthe. Of course I did. But let me tell you the story properly, because you have heard it secondhand from people who were not in the room.

The ship out of the southern port would carry twenty civilians. Teodoro's list held forty-one names, then thirty-eight, then however many it held after each new cruelty arrived by radio. Every person on that list had a face I had known since before I knew my own name. Signora Bellini and her granddaughter. The Ferretti brothers who mended shoes. Old Marta who had taught both me and Elena Voss our catechism, back when Elena still went to catechism, back when any of us believed heaven was administrated fairly.

Three berths. That was the number I fixed on, because three was large enough to matter and small enough to seem modest. If I asked for everything, I would be a profiteer. If I asked for nothing, I would be useless. Three was the honest middle of a dishonest trade — my family, or most of it, carried past the line that was about to swallow everyone else.

And here is the column entry on the clean side of the ledger, the one I wrote in ink so fine I could pretend not to see it: the partisans were going to be found anyway. Not might be. *Were.* The patrols were already combing the gorge roads, already questioning shepherds, already burning the wrong barns on the strength of rumors half as good as the ones I could give them. If I gave Weissler accurate intelligence, he would strike precisely and stop striking blindly. Every barn he didn't burn because he didn't need to was a family that kept its winter stores. Every search he didn't conduct at random was a door left unbroken. Precision, I told myself, is mercy. Targeted information saves the innocent from the dragnet.

Do you hear it? I hope you do. I want you to hear how reasonable it sounds, because it sounded reasonable to me, and I was there, and I am reasonably intelligent.

* * *

I invited Weissler to take coffee in the inn's back room rather than the common hall, on the pretext of discussing translation schedules for the requisition inventories. He came, because men like Weissler always come to back rooms; the back room is where they believe the real country lives.

He was younger than his rank suggested and tired in a way the uniform couldn't quite press flat. He drank his coffee black and held the cup without drinking from it, which I recognized — my father used to hold his glass that way during arguments, warming his hands on the argument itself.

"Signora Marchetti," he said. "Your file says you requested this posting."

"Files say many flattering things."

"It isn't flattery. It's unusual. An interpreter with your credentials volunteering for a mountain village." He set the cup down. "Volunteers want something."

There it was, offered plainly, the way a card player sets the deck on the table to show he has nothing up his sleeve while having, of course, several things up his sleeve. I gave him my best ministry smile — I had inherited the family talent for it, whatever else I had inherited badly.

"I want passage," I said. "For myself and two relatives, on the last civilian ship south. Your commission is cutting the quota. I'm told it can also restore it."

Weissler looked at me for a long moment. I watched him decide how much pretense was necessary between professionals, and watch him decide on none.

"And what," he said, "would we receive?"

"You have lost men on the mill road. You burned a granary at Valgrande that contained flour and no partisans. Your searches are guesses." I kept my voice level, the tone one uses to read a shipping manifest aloud. "I can make them not-guesses."

"You know this because—"

"I grew up here. I know which farms feed extra mouths in winter. I know which lofts have been cleaned out and which hay has been moved recently. I know that the bell at San Rocco rings the Angelus at six, and that lately it has rung at six, and then stopped, and then rung again — and that the second ringing is not for prayer." This much was true, or nearly true; I had noticed the bells, and I had noticed Elena Voss climbing toward the chapel loft at hours when even the devout stay abed, and I had said nothing to anyone, including myself, for weeks.

It is remarkable, the things one can fail to say to oneself. One develops the skill the way one develops a taste for olives.

Weissler turned his cup a quarter-turn on the saucer. "Names," he said. "Locations. Frequencies, if you can get them."

"Frequencies I cannot. Faces and places I can." And then, because a bargain offered without terms is a confession: "Three berths. Restored through official channels, so the list shows quota error, not favoritism. No one is to know where the correction originated."

"No one," he repeated, with the ghost of a smile, "including yourself?"

"Especially myself."

He agreed faster than I expected, which should have frightened me more than it did. Later I understood: a man who agrees quickly either values what you're selling very highly or intends to take it regardless and is merely being polite about the receipt. With Weissler it was, I think, both. My information was good. His patience was short. In the end it wouldn't matter which of us moved first.

* * *

That night I wrote out the intelligence in my own hand, in German, in the neat script the commission had complimented me on during my first week — *so precise, Fräulein Marchetti, almost Prussian*, they said, meaning it as praise, receiving it as one. Three farmsteads. The mill road relay schedule as far as I could infer it. The pattern of the chapel bells.

I want to be exact about what I knew and when I knew it, because history will be written by people who prefer their collaborators either monsters or martyrs, and I am neither, and the truth — that I was a frightened woman doing competent arithmetic in the dark — serves neither side's appetite.

I knew the farms fed partisans. I did not know, that night, that the mill road relay was scheduled to meet within the week; I inferred it from the frequency of the traffic and the state of the mud. I did not know anyone's name. I told myself these distinctions mattered enormously. A farm is not a face. An inference is not an accusation. I was handing Weissler a map of weather, not a list of victims.

The distinction held for exactly as long as it took ink to dry.

Because here is what I did not write in any column: I knew, somewhere below the arithmetic, in the place where I actually lived, that information given to that machine does not arrive as nuance. It arrives as a hammer. I had translated enough interrogation summaries to know that precision is not mercy — precision is efficiency, and efficiency only means the same cruelty arrives sooner and cheaper. The dragnet doesn't shrink because you've made it smarter. It tightens.

But three berths. My uncle with his failing breath — yes, I had seen it, the pauses in the middle of sentences growing longer, the way he leaned on the inn doorframe as if it were a colleague. My brother with his sealed satchel and his infuriating neutrality, who would refuse rescue out of principle and die of it beautifully. And myself, who had spent six years building a life inside the machine and had earned, I told myself, *earned*, the right to step out of it alive.

I sealed the letter. I addressed it to Weissler's office through the channel we'd agreed on — tucked into the requisition folios, invisible among inventory numbers.

Then I sat at the inn table with the candle burning low and did the thing I always did after such transactions: I washed my hands. Not ritually, nothing so theatrical. Simply washed them, thoroughly, in cold water, watching the skin go white, until the act of washing became its own argument — see, clean, see, ordinary, a woman washing her hands before bed like any other woman in any other village in any other winter.

My mother used to say that the difference between a sin and a transaction is whether you can look at yourself while doing it. She meant it as guidance. I received it as instruction.

* * *

Two nights later, at the commission dinner — the colonel present again, still refusing to acknowledge my existence except when passing him the salt — Weissler was expansive in the way of a man whose work is going well. Talk turned, as it always did now, to the port.

"The requisition order comes through within the fortnight," the colonel said, to the room generally, to the wine specifically. "Civilian sailings end. Whatever the local authorities have arranged, they may un-arrange it."

Beside me, Weissler smiled into his glass. I understood the smile. The quota restoration he had promised me existed on paper he controlled, and paper controlled by a man whose superior has just announced the port will close is paper worth exactly its weight in ash. My three berths had a countdown on them now, and the countdown belonged to someone else entirely.

I excused myself early. In the corridor outside the dining room I passed my brother, standing where the transit staff stood, holding a tray of glasses he had no business holding, wearing the expression of a man listening to a wall. We did not speak. We had not spoken, really, since the night of our argument — since he accused me of trading lives and I accused him of worshipping a neutrality that had never fed a single child, and we had both been right, and we had parted the way our family parts, with perfect posture and no forgiveness.

But as I passed him, I felt his eyes on me — on my hands, specifically, and I realized with a coldness in the stomach that I had not fully dried them, or that some residue of the evening showed there, or that guilt simply has a smell that siblings can detect through walls and years and six hundred kilometers of war. He looked at my hands. I kept walking.

Let him wonder. Let him do his own arithmetic for once. He carries his satchel of secrets and calls it duty; I carry mine and call it survival; and if his secrets are cleaner than mine, it is only because no one has yet forced him to price them.

* * *

The letter traveled Tuesday. By Friday I had confirmation — a note in Weissler's hand, bland as a laundry list: *Amendments to the manifest under review. Expect favorable resolution. Grateful for continued cooperation.*

*Continued.* The word sat on the page like a hook sits in water, patient, decorative, lethal. Continued cooperation. There it was, the true shape of my bargain, the shape I had declined to examine when I negotiated it: not a sale but a subscription. Three berths was the introductory offer. What came after was whatever the machine asked for next, and I had already taught it that I answer.

I stood at my window that night looking down at the village — the dark bulk of the chapel, the inn yard, the thread of frozen river visible through the gorge, and above it all the broken bridge with its severed span hanging like a sentence no one dares finish. Somewhere down that river road was a mill where men met by night to pass words along a wire to people who still believed the republic could be saved. I had drawn the commission a map to that belief.

They will say I chose my family over strangers. I did. I will not insult you by pretending otherwise, and I will not insult myself by pretending the choosing was easy, or that the ease I manufactured afterward — the tidy columns, the cold-water hands — ever quite closed the account.

What I will say is this: I looked at that bridge for a long time, and I thought about crossings. Everything in this winter is about crossings — bridges, borders, lists, the line between the person you were raised to be and the person the paperwork says you are. I had crossed mine quietly, in a back room, over coffee. My brother stood on his side of his own line and called it principle. Our uncle crossed his years ago, in some denunciation none of us ever learned the shape of, and built a life of penance on the far bank, striking names from lists as though subtraction could balance addition.

And beneath us all, moving at night through the frozen riverbed, the ones who crossed without papers at all.

On Sunday morning, I heard the Angelus ring at six, stop, and begin again — and this time I did not look away from what I knew. I took out a fresh sheet of paper, because Weissler had said *continued*, and because a debt once opened collects interest, and because I had convinced myself, one final time, one final column-entry, that knowing a thing precisely was better than letting them find it blindly.

I was halfway through the second letter when I remembered my uncle's ledger — the master copy he kept locked in the office of the parish house, the one document in this village that still told the truth about what we owed each other. I would need to see it. If the amendments went through, the manifest would change, and changes leave fingerprints, and fingerprints, in my experience, always lead somewhere I had sworn never to look.

I did not yet know that my uncle had been striking his own name from every draft. I did not yet know that my brother had begun spending his government's seals like a gambler past caring. I knew only that the machinery I had set turning was turning, and that machines of that kind do not stop for the operator's remorse — they stop, when they stop, on something.

The bell rang again. I dipped my pen.
