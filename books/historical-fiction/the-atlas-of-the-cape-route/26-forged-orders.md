## 26. Forged Orders

Treason, I discovered that night, is mostly a matter of stationery.

I had imagined it differently — torchlight, whispered oaths, a pistol on the table between me and my conscience. Instead I sat at the desk in Teodoro's back room with a guttering oil lamp, a blotter, two seals, and a pot of glue that had gone hard at the edges like an old man's knees, and I committed treason against the republic whose codes I had carried through fire and strafed countryside in a satchel strapped to my chest like a second heart.

The republic, for its part, was not looking. That is the thing about governments in their final winter: they are so busy dying that they no longer notice what their servants do in the margins of their paperwork. A state is a great ledger, and when the ledger begins to burn, every entry becomes negotiable.

* * *

Let me be precise about how I arrived at the desk, because precision is all I had left.

The arithmetic had been done for me, twice over. First by the radio order — the one Elena's network intercepted and read aloud to me in the chapel loft, her voice flat with the particular exhaustion of people who deliver bad news professionally: civilian allowance halved, manifest cut to twenty, effective immediately. Then by Teodoro, who lay upstairs with his heart failing at its own pace and refused, with the stubbornness that had made him a legend among clerks, to choose which twenty names would live.

"I administer," he told me from his bed, his hands folded on the blanket as though awaiting a document he knew would never come. "I do not select. The day I select, I become the kind of man who selects."

He did not say *the kind of man who denounces*. He didn't have to. He had spent three nights telling me about 1938, about Aldo Ferrero and the denunciation he had signed to protect the family name, and the confession sat between us now like a third patient in the room. I understood, finally, why he struck his own name from every draft list. It was not humility. It was penance with a pencil.

So the choosing fell to the paper itself, and the paper, I realized, could be made to say something else entirely.

Here is what I knew, and knowing it felt like holding a lit match in a hay barn: I carried the ministry's full cipher books — or rather, half of them, the other half burned on the train south as instructed, but the surviving half contained exactly what mattered. Among them, section eleven, the transit protocol. The republic, in its wisdom, had built a system whereby regional quotas could be overridden by priority orders from the central authority, authenticated by cipher group and seal impression. Priority orders restored quotas. Priority orders moved mountains, ships, men. In peacetime they were used to evacuate archives and ministers' mistresses. In wartime they were used for whatever the holder could justify.

The government I served had fled, scattered, or surrendered. But the system did not know that. The system was a machine of ink and habit, and machines of ink and habit can be spoken to even after their masters have died, provided you speak in the right accent.

I could forge a priority order restoring the twenty berths. The seals were mine — the small wax matrix I kept sewn into my coat lining, and the ministry stamp I had carried since Rome. The ciphers were in my head, where no auditor could reach them. And the port commandant, whoever he was this month, would see a document bearing the correct authentication groups, the correct seal, the correct bureaucratic cadence, and he would obey it, because obedience to paper was the last religion of the collapsing republic.

The treason was not in the forgery. The treason was in the direction. Every order I had ever carried had moved authority downward, from the center to the periphery, from the powerful to the waiting. This one moved upward — from a nephew at a kitchen table to the fiction of a state, asking the dead government to perform one final act of mercy it had never authorized.

Duty inverted. I turned the lamp higher and began.

* * *

You should understand that forgery, done properly, is not dramatic. It is transcription with ambition.

I drafted the order three times before I trusted any version. The first attempt I burned because I had written *urgently* where the house style demanded *with utmost urgency*, and because I had dated it wrong — the ministry always dated forward from the dispatch registry, never backward, a quirk I had absorbed during four years of carrying other men's instructions without once imagining I'd need the grammar of them. The second attempt failed on the cipher groups; my hand shook, or perhaps my memory did, and I transposed two characters in the third group, which any competent signals officer would catch the way a musician catches a wrong note — not consciously, but with a flinch.

The third time, I closed my eyes first. I let the codes rise the way Elena had taught me to let resistance traffic rise, unforced, trusting the architecture of memory. She had spent weeks drilling me — *don't memorize, inhabit* — until the numbers stopped being foreign and became rooms I could walk through in the dark. Now I walked through section eleven and found everything where I had left it.

The pen moved. The order took shape:

BY DIRECTION OF THE CENTRAL AUTHORITY, FOREIGN MINISTRY OF THE REPUBLIC — and there was the first lie, elegant in its way, since the central authority was at that moment either in a cellar in Switzerland or in the bottom of the harbor, depending on which rumor you preferred —

PRIORITY TRANSIT ORDER NINE-ONE-FOUR: CIVILIAN ALLOWANCE FOR VESSEL DEPARTING SOUTHERN PORT IS HEREBY RESTORED TO FORTY-ONE PERSONS. QUOTA REDUCTION OF [date] IS RESCINDED. EVACUATION LEDGER ADMINISTRATOR, SAN ROCCO, IS DIRECTED TO COMPLY WITH ORIGINAL MANIFEST. AUTHENTICATION FOLLOWS.

Authentication followed. Seal pressed into wax softened over the lamp flame, the impression deep and clean because the matrix was good brass and my hand, for once, was steady. Cipher groups appended in the margin, correct to the last character. Registry number invented but plausible — I gave it a low enough number to seem old, filed quietly months ago, the sort of order that surfaces late because bureaucracies misfile everything in wartime and everyone knows it.

Everyone knows it. That was the genius of my crime: I was not asking anyone to believe something improbable. I was asking them to believe something the republic made probable every single day.

I blotted the page. I held it up to the lamp and looked for flaws and found only my own reflection, faint and doubled in the wet ink, which seemed appropriate.

Then I did the thing that made it real. I signed it.

Not my own name. The name belonged to Undersecretary Vittorio Salviati, who had signed my original assignment in Rome with hands that smelled of cigarettes and who was, by every account, dead or departed. His signature I had seen perhaps forty times. I reproduced it the way a bell-tower reproduces a bell — imperfectly, but recognizably, and recognizability is the entire standard by which signatures are judged in a country mid-collapse.

The lamp fluttered. Outside, the gorge groaned — the ice beginning its long argument with spring — and somewhere below, a dog barked at the sound and then thought better of it.

I sat back and looked at what I had done. One page. Forty-one names' worth of consequence. And underneath it, invisible, the whole inverted architecture of my life: the courier who had finally sent an order instead of carrying one, and the order was false, and the falseness was the truest thing I had produced in four years of service.

They would shoot me for this, if they caught me. My own side, I mean — the remnants of it, the officers still wearing the republic's colors in whatever cellars remained. Forgery under the ministry seal was death by firing squad in peacetime. In wartime they would not bother with the squad.

I found, examining myself honestly, that I could not summon fear. What I summoned instead was something closer to relief, the specific relief of a man who has carried a sealed satchel so long that setting it down — even to pick up a pen and commit treason — feels like putting down weight he had forgotten he was bearing.

* * *

There remained the question of distribution, which is to say: how does a forged order travel?

A genuine order arrives with provenance — a courier, a chain of custody, the faint institutional smell of having passed through many hands. Mine had only me, and I was already known at the bridge checkpoint as neutral transit staff, a category the occupying commission had invented for people they wished to ignore. If I presented the order myself, questions would follow. Questions, at checkpoints, are the opening movement of searches.

So I did what the resistance does, and what the ministry does, and what every family in San Rocco has done for centuries: I used the church.

Elena received the plan in the chapel loft at midnight, sitting cross-legged beside the cold hearth where her radio used to live before the patrol came looking. She read the forged order twice, holding it close to the shuttered lantern, and her face did something complicated — pride and alarm wrestling to a draw.

"You understand what this makes you," she said.

"A criminal."

"Worse. It makes you useful." She set the page down. "The network can move it. Father Anselmo carries documents to the port twice a month with the wine shipments. No one searches a priest going toward the sea; they search priests coming away from it, it's a superstition with uniforms." She looked up. "But Xanthe. If this is discovered — if the commandant verifies with what's left of your ministry —"

"What's left of my ministry cannot verify the weather."

"Someone is always left." Her voice went quiet in the way it did when she was being precise about danger. "And someone will remember that the courier from the north arrived in December with ministry papers, and stayed, and taught himself our traffic, and then a priority order appeared out of nowhere restoring forty-one berths. You are not invisible here anymore. You haven't been since the mill raid."

She didn't say *since my sister sold the relay*. She didn't have to. The mill raid lived between us still, two dead partisans and a Marchetti name attached to the intelligence, and Elena had come back to working with me but had not yet come back to anything past working. We shared a loft and secrets and the grammar of survival, and every night I went down the ladder feeling like a man reading letters addressed to someone who used to live at his address.

"The list," I said, to change the subject or because I couldn't help it. "Teodoro won't choose. With the quota restored, he doesn't have to. Everyone on the ledger boards the ship. Chiara included."

"Chiara included." Elena said the name carefully, tasting it for poison and finding some. "Your sister trades partisan lives for berths, and your forgery restores the berths anyway."

"Yes."

"So the trade bought her nothing except the deaths."

"That," I said, "is the part I intend for her to discover."

Elena looked at me for a long moment. In the lantern light her face was all angles and patience. "That's not mercy," she said. "That's arithmetic with a grudge."

"It's justice with a delay," I said. "She'll still board the ship. I'm not handing her to the commission. But she'll board it knowing the berths never needed buying. Knowing what the price purchased. Let her carry that across the water — it weighs less than what the families of the mill carry, but it's honest weight."

"And you? What will you carry?"

I looked at the order lying between us, the seal gleaming dully like a coin at the bottom of a well. "The codes," I said. "The testament. Whatever fits in the satchel. You know my system."

She almost smiled. Almost. It was progress measured in fractions, and I had learned to bank fractions.

* * *

Father Anselmo took delivery two nights later, tucking the order into a hollowed-out wheel of cheese with the serenity of a man who had smuggled worse under better circumstances. Before he sealed the rind with wax he studied the document, squinting.

"This Salviati," he said. "I knew him in seminary. Terrible handwriting, worse theology. Dead, I assume?"

"Departed," I said.

"Same thing, lately." He pressed the wax down with his thumb. "God forgives many things, young man, but He keeps a special ledger for good lies. They weigh more than sins, because sins at least confess themselves." He patted the cheese. "This one had better be good."

It was. On the morning of the fourth day, a launch went from the port up to the bridge checkpoint carrying a signals officer and a very bad mood, and by noon the news had traveled back down the mountain faster than any official channel: the quota reduction was rescinded. Forty-one names. The ledger stood. The ship would take everyone on Teodoro's original manifest.

I heard it in the piazza, in the noise of it — the village erupting around the announcement the way water erupts around a dropped stone. Old women weeping openly. Men who hadn't spoken to each other in years embracing with the desperate fraternity of the reprieved. Someone started ringing the church bells out of sequence, the old alarm pattern repurposed for joy, and nobody corrected them because there was no one left whose job it was.

In the middle of it all, Teodoro stood at the door of the census office with his ledger under his arm, and he looked up the street directly at me — he knew, of course he knew; the man had administered evacuations too long not to recognize the fingerprint of a forged priority order, and besides, I had left the draft burns in his stove — and he did not smile, exactly. He inclined his head. The gesture of one clerk acknowledging another's work. Then he went inside to amend the record, because that was his liturgy and he would perform it to the end.

Chiara was not in the piazza. I learned later she had been at the commission office that morning, and that she had gone pale when the announcement came through on their wire, and that she had asked — carefully, in her flawless commissioned Italian — whether the restoration order indicated its origin.

It did. Priority Transit Order Nine-One-Four, Central Authority, Foreign Ministry of the Republic. Authentication appended.

She would spend the evening with that information turning in her hands like a stone pulled from a river, smooth and cold and heavier than its size. She would look at the manifest with her own name on it — her name, restored among the forty-one, saved by an act she could neither claim nor repay nor report without destroying herself. She would understand, eventually and precisely, that someone had burned the family's last currency of legitimacy to buy her passage home from her own bargains.

She would come looking for me. Sisters do.

I stood in the piazza while the bells rang their wrong, joyful pattern, and I waited for her the way you wait for weather — certain of the arrival, uncertain only of the form it would take.
