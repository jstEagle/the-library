## 27. Chiara Reads the List

There is a particular kind of paper that smells of authority. You learn it in the commission — the heavy stock, the iron-gall ink, the way a document sits on a desk as though it owns the desk and the building and the men standing in the building. I had spent six years learning to make my own papers smell like that: transit permits, interpreter's credentials, the little laminated card that let me walk past soldiers who would have shot the woman I used to be without breaking stride.

So when Teodoro asked me to check the manifest against his ledger — "your eyes are younger than mine, and your German is better than my arithmetic" — I took the last ship's list with the confidence of a woman handling her own instrument.

I read it once for errors. I read it twice for names I knew. And on the third reading, somewhere near the top, where the restored berths sat in their neat block of twenty like guests who had been told they were no longer welcome and had come anyway, I stopped.

*Marchetti, C. — interpreter, civilian status confirmed.*

My own name.

Not at the bottom, where mercy is usually filed. Near the top. With a ministry stamp beside it that I did not recognize and recognized completely — because it was almost right, and only someone who had spent her life among documents could see how almost. The serial number was from a series retired in September. The countersignature belonged to an undersecretary who had, by every report, left the capital on the last train south with his typewriters and his mistress and not much else. The seal impression itself was flawless. That was the thing that made my hands go cold. Forgery is easy; forgery with *flair* is rare, and flair always has a motive.

I set the list down on the inn table with exaggerated care, the way you set down a glass you suspect is poisoned.

Someone had spent something enormous to put my name back on that page. Not money. Money is cheap here now; a chicken costs more than a life insurance policy. Someone had spent *credibility* — the one currency in this valley that couldn't be counterfeited, except apparently by my brother, using the seals of a government currently being folded up like a map by men who were losing.

Because it was Xanthe. Of course it was Xanthe. Who else in San Rocco could produce a ministry stamp that smelled authentic at ten paces? Who else had both the tools and the reason? The uncle wouldn't — Teodoro struck his own name off lists the way other men bit their nails, compulsively, invisibly, and he'd have cut his own throat before restoring mine. And no one else had a motive shaped exactly like guilt.

I sat there in the empty morning room while the inn's kitchen clattered behind me and thought: *he hates me.*

He does hate me — or whatever we call it, this cold formal wound between us that we dress up as civility when there are witnesses. He refused me absolution in November with a face like a locked door. He watched me pour wine for officers whose boots had walked through our father's vineyard and said nothing, which from Xanthe is a scream.

And he had forged treason to save my life.

I want to be honest about what I felt first, because everyone in this story will eventually tell it more nobly than it happened. What I felt first was fear. Pure, animal, self-interested fear. If the forgery was discovered — and commissions discover things, it is their whole profession — then it would not stop at Xanthe. It would climb the family tree branch by branch. It would find the sister who already dined with majors. It would find the uncle with the ledger. Grace, I learned that morning, arrives in the mail looking exactly like a bomb.

Then, underneath the fear, something worse: shame. Because I had earned those three berths myself, hadn't I? Three names for intelligence. I had sat across from Captain Weiss with my hands folded and traded the mill road schedule for passage, and I had lain awake telling myself the arithmetic until the arithmetic agreed with me. Two partisans moved out of harm's reach, three civilians saved, a net positive, a bargain any reasonable person —

And then my brother had simply *made* the berths exist, with ink and nerve, without trading anyone's blood for them, and my careful arithmetic collapsed like wet cardboard. He had done the thing I claimed to be doing. Without the price tag. Without the bodies.

That was the shame. Not that I had sinned — sin I can manage, sin is practically my profession now. The shame was being shown up by someone who thinks I'm beyond redemption.

* * *

I went looking for him the way you go looking for weather: knowing you'll find it and not knowing what shape it will take.

He wasn't at the chapel. He wasn't at the doctor's house, where I lingered long enough to hear through a door that Teodoro had slept badly and eaten nothing, which I filed away in the place where I keep all my failures, a room that by now must resemble the ministry archive. Finally I found him on the gorge path, standing where the ice was starting to talk — that deep, structural groaning it makes in late February, like a giant turning over in bed. He didn't turn around when I came up. He never turns around first anymore. Six years ago he would have.

"You've seen the list," he said.

It wasn't a question, so I didn't answer it. Instead I said, "The serial numbers are dead ones."

"Dead series," he corrected. "Numbers don't die. Though God knows everything else does." A pause. "You noticed the undersecretary."

"I drank wine with his wife in October. She showed me photographs of his villa."

"Then you know my forgery is better than his filing system." Almost a smile. Almost. His face did that thing it does where the humor surfaces and drowns before anyone can rescue it. "Are you going to refuse the berth? On principle?"

The question had teeth. I stood in the snow-mud and felt them. "No," I said. "I'm going to ask you why."

"Why?" He finally turned, and his eyes had that flat brightness they get when he's holding something down. "Because you're my sister."

"That's not a reason. That's a description."

"It's the oldest reason there is." He looked back at the ice. Below us, the river ran black between its white banks, patient as bookkeeping. "Chiara. I'm not going to give you absolution. You haven't asked for it and I wouldn't know how to administer it if you did. But nobody should die for a choice they made at twenty-four trying to keep their family fed. Even a bad choice. Especially a bad one made by someone who—" He stopped himself, which he does, constantly, a man forever closing doors in his own house.

"Who what?"

"Nothing."

"*Who what,* Xanthe."

"Who was protecting people I've since learned needed less protection than she thought." He said it quietly. "Weiss isn't interested in your partisans' roads. He's interested in whether you're useful. And useful people, in his arithmetic, are the ones who get evacuated *last.*"

I felt that go in under the ribs. Because he was right — I had known it for weeks, in the way you know a tooth is dying, refusing the appointment. My commission contacts had been cooling toward me since January. Politeness is a thermometer; you learn to read it or freeze.

"So you burned yourself to warm me up."

"I burned some paper," he said. "Paper recovers. Mostly."

"Does it? What happens when they audit the manifests at screening? What happens when someone matches that stamp to a courier who's supposed to be neutral transit staff?" I stepped closer. "Xanthe. Listen to me, because I know these men better than you ever will. They don't punish forgery. They punish *being caught.* And when they catch you, they won't ask whether you did it for love. Love isn't a category in their files. There's 'enemy,' there's 'asset,' and there's 'inconvenient.'"

"Then I'll be inconvenient."

"Don't be glib. You've never been able to be glib; you do sincerity the way other men do cruelty, thoroughly and at the worst possible moment." My voice cracked on the last word and I hated it, hated the crack, hated that after six years of controlling every syllable in three languages my brother could still knock a sentence out of true.

He looked at me then — really looked, the way he used to look at me over the atlas on Father's desk, when we were children dividing the world into countries we'd invented and neither of us would ever rule. Something moved in his face that I chose not to name, because naming it would have meant answering it, and I had nothing in my inventory that answered to it.

"The thaw's coming," he said instead. "Listen to the ice. A week, maybe less. When the bridge opens, everything opens — convoys, inspections, the port falling or not falling. Whatever happens next happens fast. So take the berth, Chiara. Don't argue with it. Don't repay it. Just take it and be alive somewhere I'll never have to visit."

"And if the screening finds your stamp?"

"Then I'll show them my real credentials and my very sincere face, and they'll believe the face, because I have one of those faces." He almost smiled again, and this time it survived a half-second longer. "Go home, Chiara. Tell Uncle the list is correct."

I went. But at the bend in the path I stopped and looked back, and he was still standing there above the groaning ice like a man waiting for a train that had already left, and I understood — fully, finally, in the way that understanding arrives too late and charges interest — that he had done it not to save me but to save *himself*. To unburden himself of one debt. To cross out one entry in the ledger he keeps behind his eyes, the one where everything is measured in what fits in a satchel, and I had been too heavy to carry and too loved to leave.

Grace I did not earn. That's the definition of it, I suppose. It comes stamped with a dead series number and signed by a ghost, and it asks nothing, and the asking-nothing is the cruelest clause of all.

* * *

That evening I did something I hadn't done since autumn. I took out my commission notebook — the one with the intelligence I'd traded, the schedules, the safe-house timings, all of it written in my tidy interpreter's hand — and I held it over the inn's stove.

I want to say I hesitated nobly. I didn't. I hesitated the way anyone hesitates: selfishly, calculating. Burning it meant burning my value to Weiss, my shield, my ticket. Keeping it meant remaining the woman who bought berths with partisan roads.

In the end what decided me was not courage but a sound — the ice below the gorge giving a long, splintering groan that carried up through the floorboards, through the soles of my shoes, into the bones of my feet. The thaw was beginning. The bridge would open within days. And when it opened, there would be screenings and lists and men with rubber stamps, and the only question left worth asking was which Marchetti would still be standing when the paperwork settled.

I dropped the notebook in. The pages caught, curled, went orange, went gray. My handwriting vanished last — my name, at the top of the first page, glowing briefly like a signature on a contract with the fire.

Then the door opened behind me, and Captain Weiss stepped into the warmth of the inn, bringing the cold in with him, and smiled his small correct smile, and said:

"Fräulein Marchetti. We need to talk about the passenger screening."
