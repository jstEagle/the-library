## 28. Thaw Begins

I have kept ledgers for thirty-one years, and in all that time I have learned one thing about hope: it is the only column that never balances. You can carry it forward all you like, but the sum always comes out wrong on one side or the other. On the morning the ice began to groan, half the village carried hope forward and half of them carried dread, and both columns were correct.

The sound woke me at four. Not a crack — a groan, long and low, like an old man turning over in bed, which I suppose is what it was. The gorge ice had been holding its breath since November, and now the river underneath had decided to exhale. I lay in my narrow bed listening to it with my hand flat against my chest, taking attendance. One knock, two knock. The doctor's arithmetic held: the heart still answered roll call, if sluggishly, like a clerk the week before his pension.

By six the whole village was out of doors in the blue cold, standing at the gorge edge as if the river had sent invitations. And in a sense it had. Old Beppe Ferrarin came hobbling down from the mill road shouting that the engineers' camp had lit their lamps before dawn, and where the engineers light lamps before dawn, something is being measured.

* * *

The engineers measured. They were a sullen pair, a captain from the north and a sergeant who chewed the same toothpick he'd been chewing since Advent, and they walked the span with instruments that looked like musical equipment for people with no ear. A crowd followed them at a respectful distance the way gulls follow a fishing boat — not helping, merely insisting on being present at anything that might produce something.

I knew the captain slightly, in the way one knows any man who has signed your papers. He had stamped my evacuation ledger twice without reading it, which is the highest compliment bureaucracy pays. When he finished his inspection he stood at the broken section — the gap where the detonation had taken out the middle third, the gap we had all been living around like furniture moved to accommodate a corpse — and he said, loudly enough for the gulls behind him:

"Three days. Maybe four, if the thaw runs fast and the mortar won't set."

The crowd made a sound. I cannot describe it better than that. It was not a cheer; it was the noise forty people make when a number is spoken aloud and every one of them hears a different number inside it. Three days meant the bridge would open. Three days also meant three days closer to whatever was coming up the coast road behind us, because everyone in San Rocco could do subtraction, even those of us who preferred addition.

The captain went on, in the tone of a man reciting weather: "And you should know the port situation is fluid. If the commission requisitions the harbor before your ship sails—" He shrugged, which is what officers do instead of finishing sentences. "Apply early. That's my advice. Apply early."

Fluid. I wrote the word in my head under the day's date. In my experience, *fluid* is what officials say when the water is already over the boots.

* * *

Word travels in San Rocco the way water travels in karst — invisibly, and surfacing where you least expect it. By noon, the news had reached every kitchen, and by one o'clock it had reached me in the form of visitors, because I keep the ledger and the ledger is where this village stores its future.

Signora Pellegrini came first, with her daughter-in-law and the two grandchildren, wanting to know whether the restored berths were real berths or paper berths. This was a delicate question, given how those twenty places had been conjured back into existence, and I gave her the answer I have perfected over decades of public service, which is to look grave and say, "All matters will be resolved in order." She left satisfied. Order is a beautiful word. It means nothing and comforts everyone.

Then Marta the schoolteacher, who wanted to know whether teachers counted as essential personnel. Then the Voss girl's landlady, asking nothing directly but lingering by my window with the expression of a woman who has noticed midnight absences and filed them somewhere careful. Then old Beppe again, who had no question at all, only wanted to sit near the ledger, the way some men want to sit near a fire.

"Teodoro," he said, after a while. "When the bridge opens. Will there be enough time?"

"For what?"

He looked out my window at the gorge, where the ice was shining like the lid of a box. "For everything," he said, which was not an answer, and we both knew it, and he drank his wine and went home.

* * *

In the afternoon I did what I do, which is audit. The list stood at twenty names after the radio orders cut it — twenty, from a village that had once filled four pages — and now, with the bridge opening, the list had become a schedule rather than a prayer. Names needed documents. Documents needed stamps. Stamps needed the commission, and the commission needed Chiara.

My niece came to the inn at dusk to collect the papers, gray coat, gray gloves, the uniform of her compromise. She had grown thinner over the winter; guilt does that, it eats from the inside the way frost gets into stone. She spread the documents on the table and checked each one against my ledger with an efficiency that was almost tender, and I watched her, and she felt me watching, and said without looking up:

"You're staring, Zio."

"I'm auditing," I said. "It's different. Staring is free."

Something crossed her face that might have been a smile in a kinder year. "The screening order," she said. "You've heard. Every passenger inspected at the bridge checkpoint. Papers, questions, the full theater."

"I've heard."

"And you're not worried."

"I'm seventy-one years old and my heart is a bad clock," I told her. "Worry is for people with time."

She stacked the papers, squared their edges twice, a third time. Her hands wanted to be busy. "Zio. If they open the port too late—"

"Then we walk into the sea and swim for it," I said. "The Marchettis have survived worse planning."

She almost laughed. Almost. She gathered her coat and went out into the dark, and I sat with the ledger open in front of me, and I will confess here what I confessed nowhere else: I read the twenty names aloud, slowly, like a grace before meat. Elena Voss. The Pellegrinis. Marta. Old Beppe, who had earned his passage by outliving everyone who might have objected. And then the page turned and there were the struck-through lines, the hundreds who had come off the list one quota at a time, and among them, struck through in my own hand, in ink so deliberate it might have been engraved: *Teodoro Marchetti.*

Seven times. Seven drafts, seven deletions. My own name, removed by my own pen, with the steadiness of a man signing a confession he intends to stand behind. Xanthe had found the trick weeks ago — of course he had, the boy auditors everything — and had said nothing, which is his way of arguing: silence, deployed like artillery.

* * *

That night the ice spoke again, louder. The whole village heard it; lights came on window by window along the high street like a string of beads pulled taut. People stood in their doorways in nightclothes, listening to the gorge grind and shift, and I don't suppose a single one of them went back to sleep quickly. There is a sound the earth makes when it is about to change its mind, and once you have heard it you understand that all our paperwork is a kind of politeness we extend to forces that have never read a document in their lives.

Xanthe found me at the window of the inn past midnight. My nephew carries himself like a man whose pockets are heavier than they look, which in his case is literal, though only two of us know what the weight consists of. He poured us both the rough red the inn serves and stood looking out at the black seam of the gorge.

"You heard the engineers," he said.

"Three days."

"Four, if the mortar's stubborn." He drank. "And the port?"

"Fluid," I said, and enjoyed his wince. The boy hates that word more than any profanity, because he knows precisely what it conceals. He had spent his career translating official language into truth; it had made him fluent in both and comfortable in neither.

"The ship sails when the navy says it sails," he said. "Not when the ice melts. Those are two clocks, Zio, and nobody's promised they agree."

"No," I agreed. "Nobody has."

He was quiet a moment. Below us, the ice groaned again — a longer sound this time, almost articulate, a word in a language older than any of our quarrels. "If the bridge opens and the port holds," he said carefully, "the list goes as written. Twenty names. As written, Teodoro. I want to hear you say it."

Ah, Xanthe. Six years a courier and still surprised that other people can read handwriting. "As written," I said, and lied to him with such professional calm that I felt a flicker of pride, and beneath the pride, the old familiar grief, cool and patient, like water under ice.

Because here is the arithmetic no ledger shows. Twenty names, and my nephew counting them on his fingers every night like a rosary, certain that one of them must be his — duty's arithmetic, the courier's arithmetic, codes delivered and debts paid. But a dying man's arithmetic runs differently. A dying man looks at a list of twenty and sees that it contains no name that cannot survive without him, and sees also a sister on the commission's payroll whose papers will not survive inspection, and sees a nephew who has already given away one berth to a woman who refused to be given things, and understands — with the clarity that failing hearts grant, God's last courtesy to the dying — exactly how the final column should read.

The ice groaned again. Four days, perhaps. Perhaps fewer, if the thaw ran fast.

Perhaps less than that for me; my own thaw was well advanced, and I knew the sound my heart made when it shifted, and it was very like the sound the gorge was making now.

"As written," I said again, to seal it, and raised my glass to the dark window, to the ice, to the clocks that would not agree.

Xanthe clinked his glass against mine and drank, believing me. And downstairs, faint through the floorboards, someone began winding the inn's clock — click, click, click — as if the whole house understood that the counting had begun, and none of us yet knew which numbers would be spent, or on whom.
