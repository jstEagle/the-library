## 29. The Last Broadcast

The chapel loft smelled of pigeon droppings, candle wax, and the particular ozone tang of a radio that has been worked too hard for too long. I had come to think of it as my own smell. There is a certain kind of woman who ends a war smelling of pigeons, and I had made my peace with being her.

Outside, San Rocco was holding its breath. You could hear it in the way the village moved — doors opened and closed more quietly than usual, the mill wheel turned as if apologizing for the noise, and old Frau Battista had stopped ringing the noon bells three days early, which in her case was less discretion than superstition: she believed bells attracted soldiers the way sugar attracts wasps. She was not entirely wrong. Everything attracted soldiers. Sugar, silence, snow. Soldiers were drawn to the world itself, lately, like moths who had decided the flame was theirs by right.

I sat cross-legged on the boards beside the set — the second set, the one that had survived the patrol's search because on the morning they came I had carried it down through the crypt in a flour sack and left it among the sarcophagi of dead friars, who struck me as the only men in this valley who could keep a secret without charging anything for it.

The frequency list lay open on my knee. The dispatch lay under a stone on the sill, weighted against the draft from the broken pane. And Xanthe Marchetti lay somewhere below me in the village, asleep or not asleep, and did not know what I was about to do, which was the whole point of doing it this way.

Let me explain the arithmetic of a radio operator's heart, because nobody ever does. People imagine it is all drama — code, danger, the hiss of faraway voices. In truth it is mostly waiting. You wait for the schedule window. You wait for the acknowledgment. You wait for the day when the acknowledgment doesn't come, and then you learn whether you are a courier or a widow. I had been waiting since October. The network I served had grown thin as winter milk — relay after relay gone silent, answered only by static or, worse, by a voice too fluent in our own call signs, asking sweetly for confirmation of coordinates. We had learned to hear the difference between a friend and a trap the way you learn to hear rain coming: not by evidence but by the hair on your arms.

Tonight's traffic had come up from the south at 21:40, weak and wavering, half-drowned in atmospherics. It took me three passes to be sure of it, and then I sat very still for a long time with the pencil in my hand and the message pad untouched beneath it, because there was nothing left to write it down *for*.

It read, in the flat language we used for exactly this:

FINAL DISPOSITION ALL STATIONS. NETWORK DISSOLVED BY ORDER. BURN ALL CRYPTO MATERIAL. NO FURTHER SCHEDULES. INDIVIDUAL SURVIVAL NOW PRIMARY DUTY. ACKNOWLEDGE IF ABLE. GOD KEEP YOU ALL.

*God keep you all.* Someone at the other end — someone I would never see, sitting in a cellar in some city whose name I'd only ever known as a grid reference — had broken protocol to add four words of ordinary human speech. That is how you know a war is ending. Not the communiqués. The moment the professionals start saying goodbye like people instead of numbers.

I acknowledged, because a last order deserves a last obedience. I sent the receipt twice, short and clean, and then I sat in the dark listening to the empty band, the way you stand in a house after the last guest has gone, hearing the rooms settle.

Then I did what I had been trained to do, and which no training ever makes easy.

I burned the codebooks.

* * *

You should understand what a codebook is before you watch one burn. It is not paper. It is every person who ever trusted you with their life compressed into columns of figures. The pad in my left drawer held the keys to eleven months of traffic — the mill attack, the prison roster lifted from Chiara Marchetti's own commission office, the warnings that got two hundred people over the pass ahead of the roundups, and the warnings that failed, and the names attached to those failures. The book in my right drawer held the authentication tables, and folded inside it, where I had hidden it from myself for weeks, was the personal cipher — the private alphabet Marta and I had invented the first summer, two girls playing at spies before the spies came for real. Marta, who had been arrested in March. Marta, whose file I had copied out letter by letter so that someone, someday, might know she existed.

A stove would have been sensible. The chapel had a small iron stove for the priest's morning hours, back when the priest still climbed up here. But I have always believed that certain things should be witnessed, even if the witness is only pigeons and God, and so I did it properly: in the brazier we kept for soldering, over the stone sink, with the window cracked so the smoke wouldn't blacken the fresco of Saint Michael, who had already lost an eye to damp and deserved better.

The pages went slowly. Paper holds heat grudgingly; ink resists; the figures glow brown, then black, then lift off the page as ghosts before they die. I fed the authentication tables in first, page by page, and watched eleven months of trust curl into ash. Then the pads. Then, last, with both hands, the way you carry something breakable, the summer cipher.

Marta's alphabet went into the fire and came out as a smell — bitter, chemical, briefly like violets, though I knew perfectly well that was memory inventing perfume out of grief. I let myself believe it anyway. There is a version of honesty that is just cruelty wearing a clean shirt, and I had worn enough of those for one lifetime.

While the ash cooled I thought about Xanthe Marchetti, which by then was less a decision than a weather condition.

Here is what I had told myself when I cut him off: that a Marchetti was a Marchetti, that his sister had sold the mill raid for berths on a ship, that blood runs thicker than whatever had been growing between us in the loft, and that a woman who has buried enough friends learns to prune early. It had been a tidy piece of reasoning. Tidy reasoning is how survivors console themselves; we mistake cleanliness for wisdom, the way a swept floor looks like virtue.

And here is what I actually knew, underneath the tidiness, and had known since the night he stood at the bottom of my ladder with snow in his collar and told me, in a voice stripped down to the joists, everything — the codes in his head, the mission dying in the mountains, the uncle striking his own name from the lists:

Xanthe Marchetti was the only man in this valley who had never once asked me to be smaller than I was.

He had not asked me to stop working. He had not asked me to be careful, or soft, or grateful. He had memorized resistance traffic alongside his diplomatic ciphers until the two braided together in his skull, carrying our war inside the republic's war like a seed inside a seed, and he had done it without once suggesting that his safety mattered more than mine, or that mine mattered more than his. When his sister traded lives for passage, he had stood in front of her and said so — publicly, unforgivably, at cost to himself — while I had been busy deciding that her sin was portable and could be transferred to him by surname alone.

That was the flaw in my arithmetic, and I had finally found it: I had done to Xanthe exactly what the commission did to everyone. I had judged him by category. Categories are efficient. Categories are how the world kills people wholesale. And I had always promised myself I would be a retail sort of judge — one face at a time, one act at a time — or else what, precisely, was I transmitting for?

So: the broadcast. So: the fire. So: the line I was going to send, which was madness, which was against every rule I had ever kept, and which I was going to send anyway, on the dead band, to a frequency that no longer existed, addressed to a man who could not possibly receive it — except that Xanthe Marchetti was the kind of man who borrowed a radio operator's manual and read it twice, and had asked me once, shyly, what frequency the network used "in case I ever needed to know," and I, fool that I was even then, had told him.

Farewell, it turns out, is just an invitation you send in advance of having the courage to knock.

* * *

The set warmed up with its familiar stutter, the valves glowing orange through the grille like coals remembering the fire I had just made of its cousins. I checked the band out of habit — empty, empty, a burst of Spanish-language music from somewhere across the sea, empty again — and keyed the call sign one last time. Our sign. The one the patrol had hunted in December, the one Frau Battista's bells had almost covered, the one that meant *a friend is speaking* in a grammar now spoken by perhaps nine living people.

Then I sent it, plain-language, in the clear, because encryption requires codebooks and I had just made a small bonfire of ours, and because some things should travel unarmored or not at all:

TO XM FROM EV. TRAFFIC ENDED. BOOKS BURNED. NOTHING HELD BACK EXCEPT ONE ITEM WHICH IS NOT INTELLIGENCE AND NEVER WAS. THE LOFT IS COLD BUT THE LADDER HOLDS. COME UP BEFORE THE BRIDGE OPENS AND I WILL SHOW YOU WHAT I KEPT. IT IS NOT A SECRET ANYMORE. IT IS ONLY TRUE. — E.

I sat back and looked at it, that little string of letters hanging in the air above the village, already halfway to the moon, unrecoverable, unaddressed to any authority, useful to absolutely no one — the first transmission of my entire career that existed purely as a gift.

There is a discipline to signing off forever. You don't linger on the key. You don't send dots trailing away like a heartbeat flattening. You finish your last character cleanly and you take your hand off the switch, because sentiment on the airwaves gets people killed, and because the machine does not care, and learning to be like the machine was the whole trick of surviving this work.

I finished my last character cleanly. I took my hand off the switch.

Then I sat in the cold loft and cried for about four minutes, thoroughly and without elegance, the way women cry who have spent a year rationing it — fast, hot, and over — while the pigeons shifted on their beams and disapproved, and the ash in the brazier settled into grey, and below me the village slept around its one remaining secret, which was no longer the radio.

* * *

Afterward there was work, because there is always work, and work is mercy for the hands.

I dismantled the set. This took longer than building it had; destruction is always more fiddly than creation, ask any army. The valves went into the stove ashes individually, wrapped in cloth so they wouldn't crack audibly. The chassis I broke apart with the soldering iron and a hammer, folding the copper like bread dough until it was no longer a radio but a sculpture of bad intentions, and wrapped that in the flour sack for the crypt, to lie among the friars until someone with better tools or worse luck found it. The antenna wire I kept — good copper is good copper, and I am not sentimental about hardware, only about alphabets.

By two in the morning the loft looked like what it had pretended to be all along: a dusty room above a chapel where someone occasionally stored chairs. I swept. I righted the chairs. I banked the brazier. And then, instead of climbing down the ladder and going home to sleep, I did the thing I had written into the broadcast without quite admitting I was writing it.

I waited.

Because here is the thing about sending an invitation into the dark: you cannot make yourself not listen for footsteps. I told myself I would give it one hour, purely to secure the room, purely for operational completeness, and then I gave it a second hour, and during the second hour the ice in the gorge began to speak.

You could hear it from the loft on quiet nights — a long groan rolling up the valley like a door opening in the earth, followed by a crack, sharp as a rifle, that made your body flinch before your mind caught up. Thaw was coming early this year. The engineers down at the span had been testing planks for a week, and Teodoro Marchetti's ledger had grown fat with departure dates penciled and re-penciled, and everyone in San Rocco had begun to live in the future tense: *when the bridge opens, when the ship sails, when.* The whole village was leaning toward spring like plants toward a window.

And I understood suddenly, sitting there in the swept and emptied loft, that the thaw was a deadline for me too. Once the bridge opened, the world would resume. Convoys, screenings, commissions, ports falling or not falling. Whatever chance existed for Xanthe Marchetti and me to say the true thing to each other would be buried under the machinery of endings — and I had spent one entire year watching machinery bury true things, professionally, with excellent paperwork.

So I listened for footsteps, and the third hour began, and the candles burned low, and I rehearsed what I would say with the fluency of a girl composing a letter she will never post: *I misjudged you by your name. I am not sorry for grieving them, I am sorry for billing you for it. Also I have kept one thing, and it isn't codes.*

At quarter past three, the ladder creaked.

Not the wind-creak, which comes in gusts and rattles the shutter first. A slow, deliberate, weight-bearing creak, one rung at a time, the creak of a man who has climbed this ladder often enough to know its opinions and carefully enough to respect them — and who pauses, halfway up, exactly where the loft floor becomes visible, the way he always did, giving me the choice of looking away.

I did not look away.

His head rose past the floorboards, snowmelt-wet, absurd, familiar. He looked at me across the ruined room, at the swept dust and the cold brazier and the faint violet ghost of burnt ink, and I saw him assemble it — the empty drawers, the dismantled shelf, the smell of endings — and I saw the exact moment he understood what night this had been, and what I had sent into the sky above his sleeping village, and whether he had heard it or only guessed it, because his face did the thing it did instead of speaking, which was worth ten thousand words and had gotten us into all this trouble to begin with.

"You're up late," he said, which was the worst opening line in the history of two wars, and the best thing anyone had ever said to me.

"Come up," I said, "before the bridge opens," and held out my hand into the cold, palm up, the way you signal *receive*, the way you end a transmission, the way you begin everything else.
