## 33. Teodoro Decides

I have spent forty years of my life teaching arithmetic to children who did not want it and thirty of those years learning that the sums that matter most are never done on paper. So when my heart began its slow betrayal in February — Doctor Anselmi called it *insufficienza*, a word that sounds like a bureaucratic reprimand rather than a diagnosis, which is perhaps why I liked it immediately; even my body was being audited — I understood at once that I had become a number on someone else's ledger. The question was only whose.

Mine, as it turned out. Mine again, one last time.

The thaw had come early that year, or late, depending on how you kept your calendar, and by the middle of March the gorge below San Rocco sounded like a cathedral full of impatient congregants — ice groaning, cracking, muttering its intentions all night long. I could hear it from my bed in the room behind the parish office, where I had been confined since the day I collapsed over the berth list with a pencil still in my hand and twenty-one names crossed out in front of me like a confession I hadn't managed to finish.

Xanthe came every evening. He sat in the chair by the stove with his satchel on his knees — the boy measured his life in what fit in a satchel, he always had, even as a child collecting stones and stamps and, once, memorably, the buttons off my best coat — and he read to me from whatever he thought would keep my mind off the numbers: newspaper scraps three weeks old, a novel with half its pages missing, the parish register from 1889, which I will admit held more drama than the novel.

He thought I didn't know what he was doing there every evening instead of packing, instead of arguing with engineers, instead of doing any of the hundred urgent things a man does when a bridge is about to open and a ship is about to sail and the world has scheduled its ending for Thursday.

He thought I didn't know what he was planning to do with the last berth.

I want it recorded — somewhere, anywhere, even if only in this account that no one may ever read — that I knew. Of course I knew. A man who has administered evacuation lists through two wars develops an ear for silence, and Xanthe's silence around the subject of the twentieth name had a very particular shape. It was the silence of a man rehearsing an argument he intends to win by refusing to have it. He was going to simply not board. He was going to let the berth go empty, or slip it to Elena Voss through some forgery so elegant the commission officers would frame it, and he was going to stand on that bridge with his memorized codes and watch the ship go, and he thought that because he hadn't said it aloud, it wasn't a decision.

I taught mathematics for forty years. I know an unsolved equation when I see one sitting quietly by my stove.

* * *

On the evening of March the nineteenth — I remember because it was the feast of Saint Joseph and the bells rang for the saint and not, for once, for anything terrible — I sent for him formally. I had Elena carry the message, which was a small cruelty, since I knew the two of them were circling each other like weather fronts, but I wanted her to see his face when he heard where he was going, and I am old enough to be permitted small cruelties.

He arrived smelling of snowmelt and woodsmoke, and he took his chair, and he said, "You look better today, Zio."

"I look better every day," I said. "It is one of the advantages of dying slowly. Every morning is an improvement on the alternative."

"Teodoro."

"Sit. Don't arrange your face yet. I need you to listen before you start composing."

He sat. He is good at listening when he remembers to be — better than his father was, God rest the stubborn man, and better than Chiara, who listens the way a diplomat listens, which is to say she hears the words and files the meanings under things to be negotiated later.

"The ship takes twenty," I said. "The list has twenty. Nineteen of them are settled — families, the sick, the ones the commission has already stamped. The twentieth is ours."

"Yours," Xanthe said.

"The twentieth is mine," I agreed. "That is what the ledger says. Now let me tell you what the truth says."

"Zio—"

"Xanthe." I put up my hand, the old schoolmaster's hand, the one that silenced classrooms of boys larger than either of us. "You will get your turn. I promise you the whole evening for your arguments, and you will need it, because you argue like your mother, which means you argue beautifully and arrive nowhere. But first you will hear an old man do arithmetic out loud. Humor me. It is the last lesson I will ever teach, and I would like it to be attended."

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, the ice in the gorge spoke its long grinding sentence.

"Consider the candidates," I said. "Candidate one: Teodoro Marchetti, sixty-eight years of age, heart operating at perhaps a third of its original capacity, a man who cannot walk to the church without resting twice, whose doctor has given him weeks and whose own body has given him fewer. Candidate one's survival depends on nothing, because candidate one has no survival ahead of him. Do you follow?"

"I follow," he said, and his jaw was set the way it set when he was twelve and I told him the dog had to be given away.

"Candidate two: Chiara Maria Marchetti, thirty-four years of age, in excellent health, employed by a commission that has just announced it will screen every passenger on that ship. Think about what screening means for a woman who has spent two years eating at their table and laughing at their jokes and trading what she traded. When their officers fall — and they are falling, boy, you can smell it in the way they've stopped being polite — the lists of who helped them will be found, and her name will be on them in three hands' worth of ink. Her survival depends entirely on leaving. Not partly. Entirely. There is no version of the next six months in which Chiara Marchetti lives here."

"She chose them," Xanthe said quietly. "She can live with what she chose."

"That is a moral judgment," I said, "and moral judgments make terrible boarding passes. I raised you better than to confuse the two." I leaned forward, and my chest did its familiar complaint, and I ignored it, because a teacher does not stop mid-sentence for mere mortality. "Candidate three: Xanthe Marchetti, twenty-nine years of age, carrying in his skull the diplomatic ciphers of a dead republic and the radio traffic of a living resistance, a man whom two separate governments would shoot for different reasons if either caught him, a man who has already decided — don't interrupt, I told you I knew — that he will give his place away and stay behind to walk across that bridge with his secrets and deliver them to people who stopped expecting him months ago."

The fire popped. He didn't move. He has his father's stillness, my brother Marco's stillness, the stillness of men who think that if they hold perfectly quiet the world will lose track of them.

"Now," I said, "do the sum. One seat. Three candidates. Two of them die if they stay — one within weeks, one within the hour some sergeant finds her name on the wrong list. One of them dies if he goes, or worse than dies, loses the thing he actually lives for. Tell me, mathematician. What does the equation give you?"

"It gives you a false premise," he said. "You're assuming someone has to take it. The berth can go empty. Or—"

"The berth will not go empty. If our name comes off that list, the commission fills it within the hour with one of their clerks, and I will tell you what I think of commission clerks, I have dined with them. And if you say the word *Elena*, I will remind you that she refused a place weeks ago, loudly, in front of witnesses, and that a woman like that does not refuse a thing in public in order to accept it in private. She'd throw you in the gorge."

"You're feeling better than you're letting on," he said. "Your humor's back."

"My humor never left. Only my legs did. Don't change the subject."

"Zio, listen to me—" and then he was up, pacing, the satchel swinging, and the argument came out of him exactly as I had predicted: beautifully and arriving nowhere. He talked about duty, about the codes, about how a courier who abandons his cargo is nothing, about how the Allies needed testimony, about how Teodoro Marchetti had given his whole life to this village and deserved — *deserved*, he said, and I confess that word stung, because it was flattery wearing my own values as a disguise — to be carried out of it like a king. He talked himself hoarse. He quoted regulations from a government that no longer existed. At one point he invoked the family honor, which nearly made me laugh, since the family honor had spent the war in three separate countries and none of them was safe.

I let him run. Forty minutes, maybe more. The stove burned down. The ice groaned. And when he finally stood there breathing hard in the middle of my little room, hands empty, having arrived precisely nowhere, I said:

"Finished?"

"For now."

"Good. Then hear the rest of the lesson."

* * *

What I told him next, I had been composing for eleven days, since the night Doctor Anselmi sat on the edge of my bed and told me the truth with the gentleness men use when they think the truth is a wound. What I told him was this:

"I do not want to leave."

Not cannot. Do not. He opened his mouth and I raised the hand again, and this time he stayed down.

"You think this is pride, or sacrifice, or some old man's theater. It is none of those things. Sit down and I will explain, because you have earned the explanation, even if you haven't earned the agreement."

He sat.

"Marco and I grew up in this valley," I said. "Our father walked the mule track to the coast forty times a year and hated every step of it, and he used to say that a man from the mountains who dies in the low country dies twice. I laughed at him when I was young. I went to Turin, I taught in the city, I wore good shoes on flat streets. And then I came back — do you remember why? You were seven. Your grandmother was buried, and I stayed for the reading of the will, and I never left again. Everyone assumed it was the inheritance. It was the bells. I stood in that churchyard and heard San Rocco's bells come up the valley, and I understood that everything else had been a detour." I looked at the window, at the blue dark coming down over the gorge. "Forty years I have listened to those bells. They ring for saints and for funerals and lately for convoys, but underneath all of it they ring the hours, and the hours are the only country I have ever properly belonged to. You are asking me to spend my last weeks in a cabin full of strangers, listening to an engine, waiting to die in salt air among people who will bury me in a numbered plot under a foreign sky. And you call that salvation."

"It's more weeks than you'll get here," he said, but softly now, and I knew I had him, or nearly.

"Weeks," I said. "Measured how? In what? Boy, I have watched you measure your whole life in what fits into a satchel, and it has made you efficient and it has made you lonely, and I have loved you too long to let you hand me the same disease at the end. My weeks here are worth more than my months anywhere else. Here, every day, someone needs the ledger kept, the quarrel settled, the letter read aloud to someone whose eyes have gone. Anselmi comes twice a day and pretends it's professional courtesy. Elena brings me the wireless reports and lets me feel useful. Even Chiara — yes, Chiara — sat with me Tuesday and read the parish register and wept, though she thinks I was asleep and I will take that knowledge to my grave with the tenderness it deserves." I reached for the water glass, and he got it for me before I finished reaching, which is the whole of parenthood in a single gesture. "If I board that ship, I am cargo. If I stay, I am Teodoro. I have been many things in my life, most of them badly, but I have been Teodoro for sixty-eight years, and I mean to finish as what I started as."

"You'll die alone in this room."

"No," I said. "I'll die in a village where every window knows my name, with my nephew holding my hand, having spent my final weeks doing the work I was made for. Compare that, honestly, to a bunk and a lifeboat drill. Compare it and tell me who is being saved."

He was silent for a long time. Outside, the bells rang the quarter hour, and I watched him listen to them whether he meant to or not. He has the valley in him deeper than he admits. His mother put it there, and his summers here, and the stones he collected along that wall when he was small.

"And Chiara?" he said at last.

"Is the actual question, and I'm glad you finally found it." I leaned back. "Here is the part that costs me, so pay attention. I am going to ask you to give her the berth. Not trade it. Not negotiate it. Give it. Freely, and — this is the difficult clause — without telling her it was ever yours to give."

"She won't accept it."

"She doesn't have to accept it. She has to board. Those are different verbs, and the second one is the one that matters. She will fight you. She will dress it up as strategy — *the courier should stay, the interpreter must report to London*, something like that; she is fluent in dressing surrender as tactics, she learned it from professionals. Let her have the fiction. People who are drowning do not need to admit they are drowning in order to grab the rope."

"She traded partisan lives for berths, Zio. Two men died at the mill because of information she passed."

"Yes," I said. "And I denounced a man in 1941 to protect this family, and he was taken, and I have carried it for four years, and I am telling you now, at the end, that carrying it is not the punishment. Staying to carry it — choosing to remain inside your guilt, making it your address — that is the punishment, and it is also, sometimes, the vanity. She can carry hers in London or Lisbon or wherever the ship lands, among the living, where guilt has somewhere to go besides the grave. Or she can carry it here, where the widows of the mill know her face. Which do you suppose the dead would prefer? I knew Aldo Ferri forty years. He was a fool and a gossip and he cheated at cards. He would not want his death spent keeping Chiara Marchetti in a valley to be shot in spring. He'd want it spent getting her somewhere she might one day be of use. That is how the dead bargain. They are more generous than we are, because they have finished needing things."

He put his head in his hands. I let the silence do its work; silence is the oldest tool in a teacher's kit, older than the stick.

"Why not tell her yourself?" he said finally. "You're the head of the family. The authority is yours."

"Because authority is exactly what I no longer have," I said, "and what I am giving away tonight, deliberately, as the last thing I own. Listen carefully, because this is the lesson entire." I waited until he looked up. "For four years I have decided who stays and who goes. I wrote the lists. I struck names. I comforted myself that the pen trembled but never lied. And the truth — the truth I will put in my testament, which you will carry across that bridge with your codes — is that every name I wrote was a choice about other people's lives made by a man who would not put his own on the page. I struck my own name from every draft. Did you know? Yes, you found out, I know you did; you said nothing, which was kind, and wrong, which is usually what kindness is. I hid behind the ledger. Authority let me believe I was serving the village when I was protecting myself from arithmetic." I took a breath, and it cost me, and I paid it gladly. "So no, I will not decide this from my bed like a cardinal in conclave. I will tell you what I believe, and what I believe is that the berth belongs to Chiara, and that you already know it, and that the only thing standing between you and knowing it is your fear that giving it will break something in you that staying will break anyway."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means," I said, "that you love that woman in the chapel loft, and that you have arranged your entire exit around the fantasy that love is something you perform by losing. It isn't. Losing is easy. Any fool in this village can lose; half of them are practicing. Love is staying alive in the same world as the person you love, and finding out, afterward, in some rebuilt city, whether the two of you can build a country of two. Elena Voss does not need your martyrdom. She needs your arrival. There is a difference, and it is the width of one berth."

He stared at me. For a moment he looked seven years old again, caught with my coat buttons in his pocket, and I almost laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough cost me a minute of the conversation while he hovered uselessly and I waved him off with both hands.

When I had my breath back, I said, quietly, the way you say the true things:

"Give her the name, Xanthe. Give it the way you'd give bread, not the way you'd give a verdict. And then get yourself and your satchel and your impossible memory onto that ship, and cross the water, and find her, and write it all down someday — the bridge, the bell, the old fool with the ledger — so that none of us is only a crossed-out line in someone's book. That is the whole testament. Everything else is commentary."

* * *

He argued for another hour. Of course he did. He argued about the screening order voiding restored berths, and I showed him the draft credentials I had asked the doctor to help me sketch — Anselmi has excellent handwriting for a man who drinks — identifying one *T. Marchetti, ministry transit staff,* a fiction thin as communion wine but sufficient for a checkpoint clerk who wants his shift to end. He argued that the papers wouldn't survive scrutiny. I said neither would I. He argued that I was manipulating him. I said yes, absolutely, it was the proudest work of my career, and that manipulation in service of keeping one's family alive is called parenting, and he was simply late receiving it.

He lost. I want that stated plainly, because history, if it bothers with us at all, will want it plain: Teodoro Marchetti did not surrender his berth. He gave it, fully conscious, in sound mind, after winning every argument that mattered — and then he handed his nephew the loss anyway, because that is what authority is for at the end. You use it until the last possible moment, and then you spend it all at once, buying someone else's future with it, and you go to sleep poor and satisfied.

Near midnight he rose, put on his coat, and stood a moment with his hand on the door, not looking at me.

"Chiara will never forgive me," he said.

"Chiara will never forgive any of us," I said. "Forgiveness is not the cargo, boy. Survival is. Load the ship."

Something moved through his face then — not a smile, something older and less comfortable, the look of a man setting down a weight he had mistaken for his spine. He touched the doorframe the way his father used to, two fingers, an old habit of leaving a blessing on the wood, and he went out into the snowmelt dark, and I heard his boots take the churchyard path toward the chapel loft, toward her, walking fast, walking like a man who had finally been given permission.

I lay back against the pillows and listened to the bells ring midnight, and beneath them the great ice in the gorge shifting, loosening, beginning its long surrender to spring.

Then I heard the other sound. Boots on the gravel path — two sets, heavy, unhurried, coming up from the road where no one honest walks at this hour. A fist on the parish door. Three knocks, spaced like a signature I had hoped never to learn.

The commission had come for the list.
