## 34. Screening Day

The bridge opened on a Tuesday, the way governments prefer to do their mercies — midweek, unannounced, with a clipboard.

I heard the news from the baker's boy, who came running up through the slush shouting it to every doorway as though it were a football score. *The span is cleared. The convoys form at noon. Papers at the checkpoint.* By eight o'clock the road above San Rocco had begun to fill with carts and families and that particular silence people wear when they are carrying everything they own and pretending it weighs nothing.

By nine o'clock I had learned the rest of it, which was less kind: the occupying commission would screen every passenger personally. Names against lists. Faces against photographs. Stamps against stamps. No berth restored by any document — including mine — would survive unless its holder survived inspection.

I stood in the doorway of my uncle's house with a cup of coffee going cold in my hand and did the arithmetic I had been avoiding since the engineers fired their first charge into the ice.

Chiara could not pass that table.

Her commission papers were genuine, which is the worst thing a paper can be. Genuine papers can be checked. The commission had spent two years issuing them, and the commission knew exactly what a genuine interpreter's credential looked like, and more importantly it knew what happened to interpreters when an army retreats: they become evidence. Somewhere in a filing cabinet in the port there was a photograph of my sister in her gray coat, standing beside men whose names were already being written down by other men with other lists. She would walk up to that checkpoint table, present herself honestly or dishonestly, and either way the honest answer would find her.

She had traded three berths for intelligence. Two of those berths she had intended for us. Whatever else she was, whatever the ledger of her sins came to in the final audit, she was my sister, and our uncle — who could no longer argue with anyone, being past argument — had told me plainly where his arithmetic ended and mine began.

*Give it to Chiara,* he had said, in the voice of a man distributing his last possessions. *You can survive anywhere. You always could. Your sister cannot survive what is coming here.*

He had said it three days ago. He had said it as though it settled something. Uncles believe their words carry weight; they spend a lifetime not noticing how often we nod just to end the conversation.

But this time he had been right, and I had known it even while I was losing the argument, which is the most humiliating way to be right about someone else.

* * *

The problem, then, was not whether Chiara should cross. The problem was the table.

A checkpoint table has three instruments: a list, an eye, and a stamp. Against the list, a name must appear. Against the eye, a face must match its paper. Against the stamp, a seal must be one the examiner recognizes and fears enough not to question.

I had spent eleven years in the foreign ministry learning precisely one lesson: that authority is a costume, and the trick is not to look like you're wearing one. A man who acts important is searched. A man who acts bored is waved through. The whole architecture of the modern state rests on the fact that clerks are tired.

I also possessed, folded into the lining of my satchel beneath a false bottom I had sewn myself during a dull week in '41, a set of ministry seals, two transit forms, and the memorized cipher tables of a republic that had perhaps four days left as a legal entity. Forging documents is treason. I had done it once already, quietly, to restore twenty names to a ship's manifest. The second offense would not be lighter. But treason, like grief, does not get heavier each time. It only stops surprising you.

What I needed was not courage. I needed theater.

* * *

I found Chiara at the inn, packing.

She packed the way she did everything now — efficiently, without looking at what she was handling, as though sentiment were another item that wouldn't fit in the bag. Her gray coat hung on the door. On the bed lay her commission folder, squared to the mattress edge with a precision that made my chest ache, because it was our mother's precision, inherited intact through six years of estrangement.

"You've heard," she said, without turning.

"The bridge is open. The commission screens everyone." I closed the door. "Your papers will hang you."

"They're genuine."

"That's the problem. Genuine can be verified. And what they verify, Chiara, is that you spent two years inside the machine. When the machine falls, everyone who touched it falls with it. You know this. You've watched it happen to better-placed people than you."

Now she turned. In the gray light from the window her face had the stillness she'd learned in conference rooms — the interpreter's face, the one that gives nothing away while other people decide history.

"And your alternative?"

"I have an alternative."

Something moved behind her eyes. Not hope — she had burned her ration of hope years ago, I suspect, and knew better than to shop for more. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman realizing her brother has finally understood the family trade: we Marchetti do not survive by being good. We survive by being useful to whichever clerk is holding the stamp.

"Show me," she said.

* * *

Here is the sleight of hand, laid out flat on the inn table like a card trick performed slowly for a child:

The ministry maintained a small class of courier — diplomatic transit staff, neutral-flagged, exempt from passenger manifests because couriers were never passengers. They were freight. Living freight, but freight: sealed cases, sealed lips, immunity under the old conventions. A courier did not board ships by berth. A courier crossed checkpoints on a separate line, with a separate stamp, and examiners did not stop couriers, because stopping a courier meant opening diplomatic correspondence, and no officer on that bridge wanted to be the man who opened diplomatic correspondence on the last day of anything.

There was one courier scheduled to cross that afternoon.

Me.

Or rather — the name and number and seal-verified identity of a courier, currently walking toward the bridge wearing my face.

I took out my penknife and slit the stitching of the satchel's false bottom while Chiara watched with her arms folded, and I laid the seals on the table between us like surgical instruments. The republic's eagle. The transit office's roundel. The little date punch I had bought in Trieste in 1938 from a stationer who assumed I collected stamps.

"What are you doing?" she asked, though her eyes had already read the answer off the table.

"Amending the manifest."

"You'll hang."

"Almost certainly not. They don't hang couriers. Couriers know things." I inked the roundel. "They detain them, interrogate them politely, and then lose them in the confusion of a collapsing front, because nobody wants to file the paperwork on a dead diplomat. That has been my professional insurance for eleven years, and today it becomes yours."

"And yours? What do you cross as?"

I pressed the seal into the wet ink and held it a moment longer than necessary, the way you hold a hand.

"As nothing. As a man with no papers worth checking. There will be a crush at that bridge, Chiara. Hundreds of people, one table, one tired captain. A man without papers gets shouted at and pushed down the queue, and if the queue moves fast enough, the shouting is the whole of the examination." I slid the amended transit form across to her. My name on it. My photograph — or near enough; photographs forgive more than faces do. "You will be me. Bored, slightly annoyed, in a hurry. Do not act important. Act inconvenienced. That is the whole art."

She picked up the form. Her hands, which had held rifles' worth of consequences steady for two years, trembled now over a single sheet of forged paper — and I understood, watching them, that this was the first genuinely illegal thing she had ever done. All her crimes until tonight had been committed in daylight, on letterhead, with receipts. Collaboration keeps books. Treason doesn't.

"Why?" she said. Just that. Not *thank you*, which would have been a lie between us, and not *forgive me*, which neither of us had earned. Why.

"Because our uncle told me to," I said, "and because he was right, and because I am tired of being the Marchetti who is right about things and does nothing with it." I gathered the seals back into their hiding place. "And because somewhere on the other side of that gorge there is a ship, and on that ship there should be one person named Marchetti who arrives somewhere warm. If it can't be all of us, let it be the one who chose wrong and knows it. That's the only redemption on offer in this war. Nobody's giving out the other kind."

* * *

Noon came grey and loud. The convoy formed below the village in a river of engines and breath, and the whole population of San Rocco walked down beside it, carrying suitcases, carrying children, carrying the elderly in chairs roped to cart beds. Teodoro went in a cart. He had insisted on attending his own evacuation the way a landlord inspects a departing tenant's damage, wrapped in two blankets and running his audit aloud — *the Ferretti boy, yes, the widow Sartori, count her twice, she counts as one and a half* — while the doctor rode alongside pretending not to monitor his pulse.

At the mouth of the gorge, the commission had built its altar: a trestle table, a canopy of canvas, three officers and a stack of lists, and behind it the new span rising out of the frost-smoke, patched and plated and groaning faintly in the wind like an old man climbing stairs. Beyond it, the far side of the world.

We joined the queue at half past twelve.

I had dressed the part carefully. No coat of quality, no luggage beyond the satchel, a week's stubble cultivated for the purpose. Beside me, Chiara wore my role the way she had once worn her gray coat — completely, without a seam showing. She had even adopted my walk, the slight forward lean of a man perpetually late for a train he despises. Watching her, I felt the strange vertigo of seeing yourself from outside and finding the impersonation better than the original.

The queue moved. The table consumed people. An officer flipped pages; a sergeant checked faces against photographs with the enthusiasm of a man sorting mail; somewhere a stamp thudded down at intervals, patient as a metronome, deciding lives at roughly four seconds apiece.

Ahead of us, an old woman was turned away — no name on the list, no appeal entertained — and walked back past us with her bundle held to her chest, and no one looked at her, because looking costs time and the queue must move.

Then it was Chiara.

The sergeant took her transit form. Read it. Looked up. Looked again — and here my heart performed a small treachery, because his second look lasted a beat too long, and I saw his finger rise toward the officers' table—

—and Chiara sighed.

Not dramatically. The sigh of a woman who has been standing in cold queues all day, who has done this in Trieste and Genoa and a dozen mud-flat border posts, who finds the entire apparatus of her country's death mildly tedious. She tapped the form with one gloved finger. "The diplomatic line," she said, in my flat, bored register, "is *that* side, surely. I haven't got all afternoon."

The sergeant's finger descended. Something in him — some clerk-deep instinct older than the war — recognized the tone of a person whose inconvenience might be reported upward, and decided that his pay grade did not extend that high. He waved her left, toward the narrow channel marked with a white flag, where an examiner glanced once at the roundel, once at her, and stamped.

Thud.

Four seconds. Eleven years of my life, transferred in four seconds.

She crossed the shadow of the first pylon without turning around. At the midpoint of the span she stopped — just once, half a heartbeat, her hand resting on the railing above the black water — and her free hand rose briefly, fingers touching thumb, then wrist, then heart.

Our childhood sign. *All safe. All loved. Go on.*

Then she was across, and small, and gone into the trucks on the far bank, and I stood in the melting slush of the queue feeling the oddest sensation: lighter. As though the part of me that had been waiting six years to forgive her had simply boarded without me and left the rest to manage.

* * *

Teodoro was next.

His papers were in order — the doctor's certificate, the medical priority, the berth confirmed on the restored manifest, the one document in Italy that day bearing my forgery and destined to be honored. The officers barely looked at him. Dying men are not security risks. He paused at the table to inform the examining lieutenant, with great courtesy, that his list-keeping was sloppy and the widow Sartori had been entered twice, and the lieutenant, bewildered, thanked him.

From the cart at the top of the far ramp my uncle looked back across the whole width of the gorge — the crowd, the table, the queue in which I stood — and found me, because uncles always find you at the exact moment hiding would be most convenient.

He raised one hand. Not the family sign. Something older, something from the ledger: two fingers lifted, the gesture he used when striking a name from the rolls. *Removed.* Entered elsewhere. Accounted for.

Then the cart rolled on, and I lost him in the engine smoke, and I stood alone in the middle of a crowd of several hundred people, holding a satchel full of a dead republic's secrets, with no papers, no berth, no uncle within shouting distance, and no plan whatsoever beyond the next ten minutes.

It was, I reflected, the freest I had felt in years.

The queue shuffled forward. The stamp thudded. And it was then, wedged between a fishmonger's cart and a weeping child, that I heard the sergeant behind the table say something to the lieutenant that dropped the temperature of the whole afternoon by ten degrees —

"—orders just came down from the port command. They want the radio people. Every transmission site mapped, every operator named. There's a sweep going out tonight, starting with the villages."

*San Rocco* was the third word after that.

Elena.

I looked at the bridge — open, guarded, flowing — and back down the road, south, toward the chapel loft and the frozen gorge and a woman with a transmitter hidden under the floorboards who did not yet know that the noose had finished tightening while we were all watching a boat.

The queue moved. The stamp fell.

I stepped backward out of the line, into the crowd, and began to run.
