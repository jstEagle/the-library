## 39. The Atlas

The bridge, when they finally opened it, made a sound like an old man clearing his throat after a long illness — a groan of rivets and thawed iron that carried all the way up the gorge to San Rocco, where the village heard it and understood, the way villages understand things before their governments do, that everything was about to change again.

I stood at the southern abutment on the first morning of the crossing and listened to that sound, and I confess I found it funny. Not ha-ha funny. Funny the way God is funny — which is to say, funny if you have survived long enough to appreciate the joke. Six months earlier I had arrived at this bridge four hours too late, carrying forty pounds of cipher books and a ministry's dying instructions, and the engineers had blown the middle span while I watched from the truck road, dust rising out of the gorge like a held breath finally exhaled. Now the same span had been patched with new timber and prayer, and I was going to walk across it carrying nothing at all.

Nothing visible, anyway.

In my head, folded into the folds where other men kept prayers or grievances, I carried the diplomatic ciphers of a republic that no longer existed on any map anyone respected — the full book now, every table and permutation, because burning half of them on the train south had turned out to be the wisest thing I ever did, since it forced me to learn the rest by heart. And braided through them, the way ivy grows through a fence until you cannot say which is holding which up, I carried Elena's traffic: the relay schedules, the frequencies, the call signs of every cell between here and the coast, the network's whole anatomy as she had dictated it to me over three weeks of midnight sessions in the chapel loft while snow fell past the bell rope. Some of those cells were dead now. The raid saw to two of them, and the reprisals after San Rocco saw to others. But dead networks leave bones, and bones can be read.

And under my coat, wrapped in oilcloth against my chest like a second heart, I carried Teodoro's testament.

* * *

He had finished dictating it nine days before he died, which by my arithmetic meant he finished it exactly when he meant to, because Teodoro Nakamura-Marchetti — no, that's wrong, that's my mongrel inheritance talking; he was simply Teodoro Marchetti, keeper of lists — was not a man who left things unfinished unless leaving them unfinished was the point.

The last session took place in the doctor's back room, propped against pillows, his hands flat on the blanket like a man showing customs officials that he carried nothing.

"Read it back to me," he said, when I reached the end of the final page.

I read it back. All of it. The war years, the ledger, the evacuation arithmetic. And the confession — the one he had circled for thirty years like a wound he kept touching to check it still hurt. In the autumn of '38 he had denounced a man. A schoolteacher named Ferrero, who had said something careless in the inn about the government, and who would have said nothing worse than careless things his whole life, and whose name Teodoro gave to a visiting official because the official had hinted — only hinted — that the Marchetti shipping interests might face an audit otherwise. Ferrero went north. He did not come back. Teodoro had never told anyone, had instead spent the war running evacuation lists with the ferocity of a man paying down a debt he knew could never be discharged, striking his own name from every draft because he believed — I am quoting the testament now, because I have read it enough times since to quote it accurately, which is one definition of penance — *"that a place saved is worth more than a place occupied, and I have occupied my place dishonestly."*

I closed the book.

"Well?" he said.

"It's true," I said. "All of it."

"That is not what I asked."

"I know." I sat down on the edge of the bed. His hand moved over and covered mine, light as a bird landing. "I don't forgive you, Uncle. I'm not the one owed forgiveness."

"No," he agreed. "But you're the one who carries things. So carry this one too. It weighs less than you think, once it's written down. That's the whole secret of paper, Xanthe. Everything heavy becomes portable."

He died three nights later, in his sleep, with the ledger on the nightstand squared to the edge of the table the way he squared everything, and I have chosen to believe that in his last hour the arithmetic finally came out even — not because it had, but because he needed it to, and a man who spent his life deciding who deserved mercy had earned the right to extend some to himself.

They put him on the ship. That is another story, mostly Elena's, and she tells it better than I do, and besides, this chapter belongs to the bridge.

* * *

The Allied officer who received me on the far side was a major named Halloran, out of some intelligence section whose acronym I have deliberately forgotten, and he had the particular manner of a man who has interviewed forty couriers that month and expects the forty-first to be either broken or lying.

"Name?"

"Marchetti. Xanthe Marchetti. Foreign ministry courier, last active assignment." I set Teodoro's testament on the trestle table between us. "I also have material to deliver verbally. You'll want a signals man and a stenographer, and you'll want them now, because once I start I intend to go straight through."

Halloran looked at the testament, then at me. I was, I grant him, an unimpressive object: six weeks thinner than the man who'd missed the bridge, wearing a dead uncle's overcoat, with frost still in my eyebrows from the morning climb down the goat track to the abutment. He had seen a hundred like me. He thought he knew my type.

"The republic fell in January," he said. "Your codes are—"

"Your people are using captured republican cipher tables to read intercepted traffic," I said. "Some of it. The old tables. The ministry rotated the diplomatic books twice in the final year and your cryptographers are working from incomplete captures, which is why your assessments of the coastal garrisons keep coming back contradictory. I know why they're contradictory. I know both rotations. I can tell you which intercepts are genuine and which are decoys, and more usefully, I can tell you how the current tables were constructed, which means your people can reconstruct what they haven't captured."

That got the signals man summoned. It also got me a chair, a mug of something that claimed to be coffee, and six hours of the most exhausting conversation of my life, in which I emptied myself page by invisible page while a stenographer with cold fingers tried to keep pace and Halloran stopped pretending to be bored somewhere around the second hour and started looking at me the way men look at a safe they've been told is empty and just heard clank.

Elena's material went last. I made them treat it separately — separate room, separate stenographer, her network described without her name attached, though I gave them the names of the dead so the dead could be entered into whatever ledger the Allies kept for such things. When I finished, near midnight, the stenographer closed her notebook and asked, gently, if there was anything else.

I thought about it. Honestly. There was a great deal else. There was Chiara, somewhere north of the lines by now, carrying my forged credentials under my false name, alive because I had made her a fiction and the fiction had held. There was a village with a broken bell tower and a mill with two fresh graves. There was a woman on a ship, scanning a shoreline. There was a satchel, burned months ago on a train, that had contained the whole professional self of Xanthe Marchetti, courier — and I had discovered since that I did not miss it, which was its own kind of intelligence report.

"There's an atlas," I said.

Halloran raised an eyebrow.

"A school atlas," I said. "Of the Cape provinces. My uncle annotated it. Every name that passed through his ledger — saved, lost, traded, struck. It's in a wall cavity behind the stove at the inn in San Rocco, third brick loose, wrapped in wax cloth. If your administration wants to know what occupation costs a place like that, in units small enough to count, it's there. Forty-one names for one berth. Twenty for twenty. It adds up to nothing, arithmetically. It adds up to everything, any other way."

"We'll send someone," Halloran said, and wrote it down, and I understood that he probably wouldn't, or someone would, or the war's next appetite would swallow the errand — and I found I didn't mind. Because the atlas wasn't really for them. I had already decided, standing on the bridge that morning with the thaw-water roaring below, that I would go back for it myself. Someday. When the roads belonged to whoever they were going to belong to.

A man should know which country holds his accounts.

* * *

They debriefed me for eleven days. On the twelfth, a lieutenant handed me a discharge chit, a travel pass, and a look of faint puzzlement, as if the paperwork had failed to specify what category of person I now was. I sympathized. I had been asking myself the same question since the train.

I walked out of the compound into a spring that seemed to have happened overnight, everywhere at once, the way spring does when you've been indoors reciting ciphers. The road south was open. The port was in Allied hands; the ship was a rumor with a hull number; the war was visibly, audibly ending, the way a long argument ends — not with agreement but with exhaustion.

And I stood at the crossroads where the coastal road met the mountain road, and I had nowhere I was required to be for the first time since I was nineteen years old, and I discovered that freedom, unallocated, is surprisingly heavy luggage.

I did what I always do. I inventoried.

What remained of Xanthe Marchetti: one overcoat (inherited). One testament (delivered; copy retained in memory, where it now lived alongside everything else, a library of the dead and the saved shelved in a skull that used to hold only timetables). One sister, alive, estranged, forgiven in the specific unforgiving way that family manages — we had exchanged no words since the bridge, only the sign, the old childhood sign, her hand touching her collarbone twice as the guards waved her across the span, and I have decided, provisionally, permanently, that this counts as a conversation. One village, mostly standing. Two graves at the mill I intended to visit. One atlas behind a stove.

And one frequency.

Because here is the thing I had not told the stenographer, the thing I had kept back with a courier's precision, the last item in the satchel of myself: on her final broadcast, before she burned her codebooks, Elena Voss had signed off the network with protocol, and then — off-protocol, one line, sent in the clear because there was no one left to hide it from — she had transmitted a sentence addressed to no callsign, no station, no name.

*"To the man who walks late: the atlas has blank pages. Bring yours. Trieste. After."*

After what, she hadn't said. After the war, presumably, which was then a matter of months. After the grief, which was not a matter of anything so tractable. She had refused the berth I offered her — refused to be given things, she said, and she was right, giving had been my cowardice dressed as sacrifice — and so she had done what she always did: she had issued orders. Come find me. As an equal. Bring what you carry, and I will bring what I carry, and we will see what fits in a life when two people pack it together.

I stood at the crossroads a long time. Then I turned toward the mountains first — the graves at the mill, the atlas behind the stove, the debts in order, because a courier delivers in sequence — and after that, north. Toward Trieste. Toward *after*, whenever it chose to arrive.

The bridge was behind me. For once, everything I carried fit.
