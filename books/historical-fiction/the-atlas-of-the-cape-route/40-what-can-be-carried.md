## 40. What Can Be Carried

*Xanthe*

Trieste rebuilt itself the way a man rebuilds after a fever: slowly, grudgingly, and with an unreasonable amount of coffee.

I arrived in the spring of 1949, six years and one lifetime after I had last seen the city, and found it wearing its scars like jewelry. Shrapnel pocks filled with flowers in the window boxes of the Via San Nicolò. The customs house where I had once been issued my first ministry satchel was now a fish market, and the smell of it — salt, ice, honest commerce — struck me as more truthful than anything that had ever been stamped or sealed inside it. The republic I had served was a footnote now, argued over by men who had not bled for it. The codes I had carried were museum pieces. The war itself was becoming furniture: something people lived around, dusted occasionally, resented on Sundays.

I had come to Trieste to forget nothing.

That sounds perverse, I know. Everyone I met in those years wanted to forget; forgetting was the great postwar industry, more profitable than shipping. But I had learned — on a frozen riverbed below a broken bridge, in the ledger of a dying uncle, in the silence of a woman who could not look at me without seeing her dead friends — that what you refuse to carry does not disappear. It simply waits for you, patient as snowmelt, at some border further down the road.

So I carried. And because I am, at bottom, a bureaucrat even now, I carried it in an atlas.

It had belonged to Teodoro. A fat 1921 Baedeker of the peninsula, its spine broken from use, its margins wide enough for a careful hand. He had kept it in the office at San Rocco beneath the berth lists, and when he lay dying in March of '44 — the night before the ship left without him — he made me promise to take it. "A man should know," he said, "where everyone ended up. God keeps no such records. Someone must."

The doctor gave him morphine and three days. Teodoro spent them correcting my penmanship.

I began annotating it that same week, sitting on the floor of his sickroom while Elena slept in the chair across from me, having refused — flatly, furiously, magnificently — to board until they loaded him aboard whether he breathed or not. They did load him. He died somewhere off Cape Point, and I have always chosen to believe he knew the sea was under him again.

Page by page, year by year, the atlas filled.

*San Rocco* — here the village bells, here the chapel loft where a radio hid behind a false wall of hymnals, here the inn where my sister poured wine for men she despised and called it survival. Beside it, small entries in pencil:

*Marta Bellini, miller's daughter, relay runner — died at the mill raid, March 1944. Nineteen.*
*Aldo Ferri, blacksmith, relay runner — died same night. His mother's letters are in the cache; deliver if ever possible.*
*Chiara Marchetti — crossed the span under a name not hers. Alive, last known Genoa, 1951. Does not write.*
*Teodoro Marchetti — buried at sea, which he would have found theatrical and correct.*

And so on, through four hundred names, maybe five hundred. Some I verified through Red Cross inquiries that took years and answered in bureaucratic French. Some Elena confirmed from memory, her memory being the finest instrument I have ever encountered — better than any cipher machine, because it never needed winding and never broke down, though it cost her more to run than any radio ever cost her network.

People ask me sometimes — at dinners, at the archive where I eventually took consulting work, at the tedious commemorative ceremonies where veterans' widows press my hand and call me *the courier* as if it were a title — why I keep the atlas. Why not let the dead rest?

Because the dead do rest. It is the living who wander.

Take Aldo Ferri's mother's letters. In 1952, seven years late, I delivered them. She lived outside Udine, in a house with a new roof paid for by a son who no longer existed. She read each letter standing up, by the door, as though expecting to hand them back. Then she fed me rabbit stew and told me about Aldo at age six, stealing cherries, and I wrote none of it down because some things are not for the atlas. Some things are only for the person standing in the doorway.

That is what the atlas taught me, finally: it is not a record. It is an excuse. An excuse to knock on doors. To sit with the living who thought themselves forgotten. Memory is the only country that never falls, yes — but a country is worthless without borders you can cross, and the atlas was mine.

Elena came back into it — into the country of it — in the autumn of 1955.

But I will get to her. She would want it done properly, in her own voice. She always said I narrated too much and testified too little.

* * *

*Elena*

He thinks I don't know about the atlas.

This is typical of Xanthe. He believes secrecy is his native language, when in fact it is merely his second — his first is inventory. The man counts things. He counted the steps to the chapel loft within a day of arriving in San Rocco, I'd have bet money on it, and I later confirmed it: forty-one. He counted the names on Teodoro's ledger before anyone asked him to. He has probably counted the annotations in that atlas, and if he hasn't, he will, and then he'll feel compelled to count them twice, once forward and once backward, to check.

I loved him for this before I admitted I loved him for anything. There is a kind of man who hoards gold and a kind who hoards grievances, and then there is the rarer kind, the kind who hoards other people — their names, their endings, their unfinished business — as though they were currency he intends to spend on someone's behalf. Xanthe Marchetti hoarded people. He still does. I checked.

We lost each other the ordinary way, which is to say completely.

After the war I did not go looking for him. This requires explanation, and the explanation is not flattering to either of us. When the ship cleared the harbor — when I stood at the rail scanning the shore for a figure that wasn't there, knowing it wouldn't be there, hating myself for looking anyway — I made a private vow. Not the romantic kind. The operational kind. I vowed that I would not be a woman defined by what she'd lost, because I had already been that woman once, in the chapel loft, transmitting the names of the dead until my fingers cramped, and I had sworn never again to become a station that broadcast only grief.

So I built a life out of transmission instead. Rome, then Paris, then a decade of work for the refugee agencies — the unglamorous kind, tracing families, matching lists, doing with paperwork what I had once done with Morse. I was good at it. I was good at it because I understood, better than most, that a list is never just a list. Every line is a person waiting to learn whether they are remembered.

And every so often, in those files, a name surfaced that stopped my hand. *Ferri.* *Bellini.* Once, unbelievably, a *Marchetti* — no relation, a shoemaker from Bologna — and I sat with the file unopened for an entire afternoon, which is unlike me, and then opened it and found a stranger and felt foolish and relieved in equal measure.

I heard about the atlas years before I saw it. A mutual acquaintance — a former partisan turned respectable historian, of whom there are many and none trustworthy except this one — mentioned that the republic's last courier had deposited copies of certain testimonies with the Allied archives, and that he traveled with "a Baedeker full of the dead" and used it, of all absurd purposes, to find the families. That the courier had located Aldo Ferri's mother. That he had stood in her doorway while she read her son's letters aloud, standing up, and had not written down a word of what she said.

I knew it was him from the not-writing-down. No one else would have had the discipline.

Even then I didn't go to him. Pride, partly. Also fear — the specific fear of a woman who has already survived one version of loving a Marchetti and is not eager to audit her losses. Chiara had traded partisan lives for berths, and whatever her reasons, whatever her grace afterward, the mill raid had a price tag and the price tag had our friends' names on it. Loving Xanthe meant inheriting that arithmetic. I had done the sums once, at twenty-six, and concluded I could not afford them.

It took me eleven years to discover that love is not a ledger. That was Teodoro's mistake too, in the end — the old man thought in balances, debts, fair shares, and it nearly killed him months before his heart managed it. You cannot balance the mill raid against the forged orders. You cannot net out the dead against the saved. The atlas taught me that, and I learned it secondhand, through a historian, through rumor, the way one learns everything important.

In the spring of 1955 I was in Trieste on agency business — tracing a Dalmatian family split between three countries, the usual cartographic cruelty — and I gave in to an impulse I had resisted for a decade. I looked him up.

He was living above a bookbinder near the old customs house. I know because I stood outside the building for forty minutes rehearsing a greeting and discarding each draft as insufficiently casual. This, too, is typical: I can transmit coded traffic under artillery fire, but I cannot compose a sentence beginning *Hello, Xanthe.* The war trained us for everything except peace. Peace was the one operation for which no one had issued manuals.

He opened the door himself, carrying a kettle, and looked at me the way you look at a frequency you haven't heard in years and had begun to believe was jammed forever.

Then he said — and I will report it exactly, because exactness matters — "You're early. I had you down for 1960."

* * *

*Xanthe*

She says I'm an inventory-keeper. She says it affectionately, the way you'd accuse a lighthouse of being bright. But she undersells herself: Elena Voss memorized an entire resistance network's worth of cipher keys and burned the paper originals, which makes her the greater hoarder by far. My atlas holds names. Her head held the machinery of a nation's secrets, carried loose in the skull like ballast, for years, with no margin notes possible.

When she appeared at my door — early, by her own account of my expectations, though I confess I had no entry for her at all; hope is the one cargo I've never managed to catalog — I did the arithmetic instantly, as is my affliction. Eleven years. She was forty-three and looked, to my eye, exactly as she had at thirty-two on the frozen riverbed, except around the eyes, where the years had done their honest work, and I found I preferred the newer edition.

"You had me down for 1960," she repeated, setting down her bag. "Explain."

"The trajectory of your pride suggested a slow decay curve," I said. "I estimated a decade."

"And instead?"

"Instead you decayed faster than projected. Congratulations."

She laughed — the real laugh, the one I had heard perhaps a dozen times, always slightly against her will — and pushed past me into the rooms, because Elena has never in her life waited to be invited anywhere, and began, without ceremony, to inspect my life. She inspected the bookbinding tools. She inspected the kettle. She arrived, inevitably, at the atlas, open on the desk to the map of the eastern passes, and went very quiet.

For a long moment she only touched the margins. The penciled names. Marta Bellini. Aldo Ferri. The little cross I'd marked at the mill, and the other crosses, and — this is the part I watched her find, because I had always wondered how it would land — the small circles. The ones I drew for the saved. Chiara, circled. The twenty whose berths my forged orders restored, circled individually, because I am thorough to the point of pathology. And beside the village of San Rocco, a circle with two words: *Elena Voss.*

"I'm in it," she said. Not a question.

"You were always in it. You're in several chapters."

"This isn't a book."

"It's the only genre I know." I took the risk, then, the one I had rehearsed for eleven years and discarded along with every greeting: I told her the truth plainly. "I started it as a debt. Teodoro's instructions. Somewhere around 1950 it stopped being a ledger and became a prayer. Around 1953 it became a letter. I never sent it because I didn't know the address. And then you were at my door holding a kettle's worth of indignation, and it turned out the address had been this one all along."

She closed the atlas carefully, the way you close a casket or a crib, and said, "You're narrating again."

"I'm testifying."

"Then testify to this." She put both hands flat on the cover. "I didn't come because I forgave the name. I came because I finally understood there was nothing to forgive that wasn't also mine. I stood at that rail and I chose the ship over the shore, and I have redrawn that choice every day since, and every day the redrawing costs more than the choosing did. We are both curators of our own guilt, Xanthe. At least yours has maps."

We married in the following spring. Small ceremony; the bookbinder witnessed; the rings bought from a jeweler who had once, neither of us learned until later, smuggled resistance pamphlets inside clock casings — Trieste being Trieste, half the town had a second résumé. On the wedding morning I gave her the atlas, and she gave me back a single sheet of paper: the final line she had transmitted from the chapel loft in 1944, before burning her codebooks, the personal message addressed to me that I had received across a relay fire in the mountains and carried in my head ever since alongside everything else. *Tell the courier the frequency stays open.* She had copied it out from memory, of course, in her own hand, dated it, and signed it. So that I would stop carrying it. So that it could live on paper, in the world, where paper things can be held by two people at once.

The atlas sits on our table now, between the salt and the bread. Guests find it morbid until they understand it, and then they find it irresistible, and soon they are pointing at villages and asking *and what happened to—*, and Elena answers the ones she knows and I answer the ones I've verified, and between us we conduct, every evening, a small parliament of the dead and the saved, adjourning always with the same motion: more wine.

Last month a letter came from Genoa. Chiara's handwriting, older but unmistakable, the letters upright as soldiers. Inside was no apology — I did not expect one, and Elena counseled me correctly that wanting one was a tax I could stop paying. Instead there was a photograph: Chiara, gray-haired, standing before a school in Genoa where she teaches, surrounded by children born long after the war. On the back, one line: *"You circled me. I found out how. Come see what the name built afterward."*

Elena read it twice and handed it back. "Well, courier," she said. "New territory. Get the atlas."

I opened it to the page for Genoa, which was blank except for the circle around Chiara's name, and beneath it, in fresh ink, I wrote the coordinates of a school, and left the margin wide open — because a country that never falls must still, if it is honest, keep expanding — and Elena leaned over my shoulder, watching the pen move, and said, "There. Now it's a living document."

Memory is the only country that never falls. I believed that at thirty, standing on a broken bridge with a satchel of secrets and no one left to deliver them to. I believe it differently at fifty-eight, at a kitchen table in a rebuilt city, with salt and bread and a woman who decays ahead of schedule and a blank margin waiting. The country never falls. But it does, thank God, keep sending dispatches.
