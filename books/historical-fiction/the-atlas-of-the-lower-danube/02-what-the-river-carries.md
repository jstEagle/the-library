## 2. What the River Carries

The river told you things before the town did.

Mara had learned that in twenty-two years of marriage to a ferryman and two more of being his widow, and the lesson held even now, in the last week of November 1875, when the Danube ran low and grey and mean between its banks like a dog on a chain it resented. The water spoke first in the color — green going to slate going to the color of old pewter — then in the smell, mud and iron and the cold coming off the Wallachian side, and finally in the sound, which was no sound at all but an absence, the river pulling inward, gathering itself, the way a man gathers himself before he says the thing that ends a conversation.

She stood on the landing with her hands jammed in her apron pockets and read all of it, and what she read was: three days, maybe four, before the ice took the crossing for good.

After that the bridge would close. It closed every winter, the pontoon sections hauled up onto the bank or left to ride out the freeze at their moorings, groaning all night like something in pain, and Vidin would seal itself into its own company until March. Garrison patrols thickened along the quay. Papers mattered more. Strangers became events. A town that lived by traffic learned to live by memory instead, and memory, in Mara's experience, was a thing people wanted controlled.

"Last loads," said Bogdan, her late husband's brother's son, who ran the ferry now because someone had to and because she had made sure it was him and not some garrison appointee. He was thirty, broad as a door, with his uncle's hands and none of his uncle's patience. "Grain from the Kalafat side, then done. Governor's office sent word — after tomorrow the bridge crews start pulling planks."

"I heard."

"You heard everything."

"That's why I'm still alive." She said it lightly. It was not entirely a joke. Bogdan grinned and went back to arguing with a carter about how many sacks constituted a load when the river was this low, and Mara turned to watch the town come down to the water the way it did every afternoon, the ritual of it, the ordinary holiness of it: carters, fishwives, a garrison corporal walking his rounds too slowly because the wind was off the water and the wall gave shelter, boys throwing sticks onto the current to see them run the ice floes like little boats.

And there, at the third window of the print shop above the tannery row, the shape that was always there and was never there.

Ivo Delacroix. Though nobody called her that. They called her *the type-cutter's girl*, or *the one at the print shop*, or, when they were being careful, nothing at all, just a nod in her direction if they nodded at all. Five years since old Delacroix died and the shop passed somehow into the keeping of a daughter most people had half-forgotten existed, and in five years Mara had watched her come down to the quay perhaps six times, always for ink or paper, always with money counted exact, always gone again before anyone could attach a sentence to her. But every evening, near dusk, she stood at that window. Mara had noticed years ago because noticing was her trade now, the work that filled the space where ferrying used to be. You could set your supper by the girl's lamp. You could set your unease by her silhouette.

Today the silhouette was wrong. Leaning forward, not back. Both hands on the sill.

Mara filed it away, the way she filed everything, and went to help count sacks.

* * *

The grain came across from Kalafat in twelve trips, and the twelfth was the one where the day stopped being ordinary, though Mara would not understand until later exactly where the seam lay.

It was full dusk by then, the lamps on the landing lit, the first snow not falling but threatening, hanging in the air like a debt. The lighters had been slow all afternoon; the current was treacherous with drift ice, and Bogdan had sworn twice as much as usual, which meant roughly four times as much as any Christian man should. The sacks came aboard wet-slapped and frozen at the corners, stacked in the hold's belly where the grain rode warmest, and the carter from Kalafat — a lean Vlach with a moustache like a raven's wing — supervised the stowage with a nervousness Mara first put down to the cold and the lateness and the corporal on the quay who had begun, in the last hour, to watch the crossings rather than merely walk past them.

That was the second wrongness. The corporal had been there since midday. Ferry landings bored soldiers; soldiers liked gates and taverns and the pastry stall by the mosque wall. This one had not moved from the bollard in four hours except to relieve himself, and he had counted the passengers on every crossing, lips moving.

"Bogdan." She kept her voice at cargo-level, under the wind. "Who is that man?"

"Garrison. New since the autumn. They say the governor wants the crossings logged through December this year." Bogdan spat over the side. "Paper for paper's sake. Man can't log a frozen river."

"No," Mara said. "But he can log who tried."

They loaded the last sacks. Seven of them, heavier than grain ought to be, or so the Vlach insisted, complaining about the damp swelling them, and Bogdan told him damp was the least of what crossed this river and got a laugh from nobody, and the lines were cast off, and the lighter pushed out into the darkening water with its lantern swinging and its cargo of wheat and whatever else the wheat was hiding.

Mara stayed on the landing. She had no reason to make the return trip; her part was done; the ledger needed closing at home. She stayed anyway, hands in her pockets, watching the lantern shrink, and told herself it was habit, a widow's superstition, watching her husband's boat come home.

Halfway across, the lantern dipped. Not the wind — the wind was steady off the north bank, steady enough to lean on. The dip was human. Someone aboard had moved against the swing of it, quickly, the way a body moves when it is trying not to fall.

Then the light steadied, and the river carried on talking its iron talk, and the corporal on the quay yawned, and the world resumed its face.

* * *

The lighter came back in full dark. Mara helped catch the line, boots slipping on frost-slick planking, and Bogdan jumped ashore with the manifest under his arm and a look on his face she had seen once before, the year cholera came up from Ruse and he'd found a man dead in his boat.

"Not here," he said, before she could ask anything. Which was answer enough.

They walked up from the landing together, past the shuttered customs house, past the pastry stall cold and dark, and only at the mouth of Tanners' Lane, where the garrison lamplight couldn't follow without following visibly, did Bogdan stop and let his breath out in a long white flag.

"There's a man in the hold," he said. "Under the sacks. Stowed at Kalafat, God knows how, God knows who let him. He's hurt, Mara. Shot, I think, through the shoulder, and it's gone bad — the smell of him. He wouldn't get out at the far bank. Said —" Bogdan's jaw worked. "Said he had to cross here. Said there was someone in Vidin who had to have what he carried."

"What does he carry?"

"A satchel. Oilcloth-wrapped. He wouldn't open it, wouldn't let me touch it. Held it like a woman holds a baby in a fire." Bogdan rubbed his face with both big hands. "He asked for names. I gave him none. I don't know names. I'm a ferryman, not a — whatever he is. But he's dying, Mara, or near it, and I can't take him home. Ana's got the children, and the fever-look is on him, and if he dies in my house with the garrison logging every soul that crosses —"

"You want me to decide."

"I want you to tell me what Uncle would've done."

Mara looked back toward the river. The black water, the running ice, the lamps of Kalafat strung small and foreign on the far shore. Somewhere behind her, up the lane, a window glowed — the print shop, third window, the girl at her dusk post, though it was past dusk now and the lamp burned later than usual.

"Miloš would have carried him," she said. "And cursed the whole way. And been right."

Bogdan exhaled. "Where?"

Not *who*. She noticed that. He didn't ask her to name the person yet; naming could wait until the thing was done, and maybe forever. That was the family wisdom, and it was why she'd chosen him for the ferry.

"The tannery row has a cellar gate nobody uses since the tanner died," Mara said. "Behind it is the yard of the print shop. And behind that door lives a girl who owes this town nothing and asks nothing of it, which means she's the only one in Vidin who isn't already in somebody's ledger." She heard herself and almost laughed, bitter. "Listen to me. Twenty years ago I'd have said the priest."

"Twenty years ago the priest wasn't reporting sermon attendance to the kaymakam," Bogdan said.

"No. Now help me get him off the boat before that corporal decides to count us."

* * *

They did it badly and quickly, which is the only way such things are ever done. A handbarrow from the customs shed, the man wrapped in a tarpaulin that smelled of tar, two stops in doorways when footsteps passed, Bogdan swearing under his breath in a register Mara hadn't heard since the funeral. The courier weighed less than a sack of wheat, which frightened her more than blood would have. Men who are shot are heavy with life; men who are dying go light, as if the body begins packing early.

In the tannery yard, under a sky the color of unwashed wool, she knocked on the print shop's back door with the pattern Miloš used to use — two, pause, three — a knock she hadn't given anyone in years and had no right to be giving now, and heard inside the small sounds stop: the scrape of a composing stick, the tick of metal. Then footsteps, careful, and the door opened the width of a hand.

Ivo Delacroix looked out at her. Up close, in the spill of lamp-oil light, Mara saw what five years of window-silhouettes had never shown her: a young woman with ink stains worked so deep into the creases of her fingers they looked like age, and eyes that took inventory — Mara's face, Bogdan's bulk, the barrow, the tarpaulin, the smell — in the time it takes to blink, and showed nothing at all about what the inventory added up to.

"Forgive the hour," Mara said. "We need your loft. One night, maybe two. No questions you don't want to answer, and I'll answer any you do."

For a long moment the girl said nothing. Behind her the shop breathed — the smell of lead and oil and paper, the smell, Mara realized, of everything written in this town, because every proclamation, every tax notice, every garrison order for fifty years had passed through these cases and these hands. Then Ivo looked past them both, down at the barrow, and pulled the tarpaulin corner aside with two fingers, the way you check bread in an oven.

Whatever she saw in the courier's face, it decided her faster than any argument would have. She opened the door wide.

"Left side of the loft," she said. Her voice was quiet and roughened, a voice used to being alone. "There's straw from the paper crates. Bring him. Mind the stair — the fourth step gives."

They carried him up. The loft was low-beamed and warm from the press below, stacked with reams of paper wrapped in sacking, and they laid the courier down among the straw and Ivo brought a lamp and, without being asked, a basin, clean linen, and a bottle of plum brandy that had clearly been waiting in that loft for some occasion, though whether this one Mara doubted.

"He needs a doctor," Bogdan said.

"A doctor logs what he sees," Ivo said. "So does a priest. So does a neighbor." She knelt, cut the man's coat open along the seam with a knife she took from her sleeve — a working knife, a compositor's knife, kept sharp — and looked at the wound with the same flat attention she gave everything. "Through and through, front to back. Missed the lung by a finger, I think. The trouble will be the rot." She glanced up at Mara. "You said no questions. Here is mine, and it's the only one I'll ask: does anyone know he's here besides the three of us?"

"The corporal on the quay counted boats today," Mara said. "He didn't count sacks."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the truest one I have."

Something moved in the girl's face then — not softness exactly, but recognition, one keeper of accounts meeting another. She nodded once and bent to the wound, and her hands, Mara saw, did not shake at all.

They worked until the small hours: brandy, boiling water, linen strips torn from an old sheet, the courier surfacing once to muttering, gripping Ivo's wrist with a strength that surprised them all and saying words in Serbian, in Turkish, and once, clearly, in French — *les papiers*, the papers — before sinking again. Ivo cleaned and bound and sat. Bogdan left at midnight by the cellar gate, taking the barrow and the tarpaulin and a promise to bring food at dawn. Mara stayed, because leaving felt like a sentence abandoned mid-word.

Near three in the morning, the courier slept, breathing in the shallow fast way that could go either direction, and Ivo sat with her back against a paper bale, arms around her knees, looking at nothing.

"You knew him?" Mara asked softly.

"No."

"You knew what he carried?"

"No." A pause. The lamp guttered and steadied. "But my father had a hiding place in this loft. Under the floorboards by the north wall. He hid type in it — the sorts that would have cost him his license, maybe his neck, if the customs men had ever found them. I found it the week after he died. Empty. He'd cleared it out before the end, or someone had." Her thumb moved over the ink stains on her fingers, back and forth, a compositor's tic, checking letters that weren't there. "Tonight, carrying that man up the stairs, I thought: the boards are the size of a satchel. Isn't that a foolish thing to think."

"It's not foolish," Mara said.

"I didn't say it aloud for five years, anything like it. And tonight I said it in front of a stranger." Ivo looked at her then, straight on, and Mara understood she was seeing the girl properly for the first time — not a shadow at a window but a person deciding something, the decision visible in her like a crack traveling through ice. "Why my shop? Out of every door in Vidin, why did your brother's boy carry him to mine?"

"Because I chose it," Mara said. "Because everyone else in this town is in somebody's ledger, and I judged you weren't. I judged you owed nobody and nobody owned you." She hesitated. "I may have judged wrong. Forgive me if so."

Ivo was quiet a long time. Downstairs, faintly, the press ticked as the stove metal cooled, the shop settling into itself like an animal.

"You judged right," she said at last. "And I don't know yet whether to thank you."

* * *

Mara walked home through streets gone blue-black with the hour before snow, past the sleeping bakery where Stefan's ovens were already lit somewhere below, breathing warmth into the frost, past the mosque wall, past the garrison gate where a new sentry stamped his feet and looked at nothing. At her own door she stopped and looked back once toward the river.

The ice had reached the middle channel overnight. She could see it from here — a paler seam in the black water, widening, patient. Three days, she'd told Bogdan. Maybe two now.

In two days the bridge would close, and whatever that man had carried across in the belly of a grain boat would be sealed in Vidin with them, with the corporal who counted boats, with a governor whose proclamations were typeset by the very girl whose floorboards now held his secret. The river had delivered something. Rivers always did; that was their nature and their cruelty. They brought what you asked for and what you didn't in the same current, and you found out which was which too late to send either back.

Mara went inside, banked her fire, and did not sleep.

At dawn she baked bread — four loaves instead of her usual two, though she could not have said for whom — and set the fifth loaf apart on the windowsill, cooling, while across the rooftops the smoke rose thin and straight from the chimney of a print shop where a girl who had spent five years being nobody's business was sitting alone in a loft with a dying stranger, an empty hiding place, and whatever the river had carried to her door.

By noon, the snow began. By evening, the garrison drums would sound the bridge-closing call, early this year, earlier than any winter Mara could remember — as if someone in the governor's house had known to count boats too.
