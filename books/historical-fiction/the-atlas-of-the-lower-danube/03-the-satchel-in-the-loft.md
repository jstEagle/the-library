## 3. The Satchel in the Loft

The courier lasted three days in my paper loft, and for two of them I convinced myself he was only sleeping.

That is the arithmetic of denial: you take what is in front of you and divide it by what you can bear, and if the remainder comes out small enough, you call it nothing. A man among the reams, breathing shallow under a horse blanket I'd told myself was for damp. A smell in the room I told myself was the river mud on his boots. Blood on the ladder rungs I told myself was rust, though there was no rust in Vidin that winter that hadn't been bought from the garrison armory.

I had not invited him up there. That much was true, and I held to it like a rail.

Mara had brought him. Or rather, Mara had brought grain on the last crossing before the ice took the river, and the grain had come with a passenger folded into it like a splinter into a thumb, and then Mara had come to me at dusk on the second evening with her shawl pulled low and said, in the flat voice she used for talking about flour prices, "There's a man in your loft. He asked for the print shop. Not for a doctor. For the *print shop*."

She said it as if she were handing me something heavy and didn't want to be seen carrying it. Then she left, because Mara understood better than anyone alive that the safest place for a dangerous thing is inside an ordinary errand.

I climbed the ladder that night with a candle and a knife I use for trimming paper, which is not a weapon but was the sharpest thing I owned.

He was worse than I expected. The blanket had soaked through in a way blankets do when they're lying over a wound, and his face had gone the color of the unbleached stock stacked against the wall, that gray-cream shade we sold to the church for its ledgers. He was young. That was the thing that undid me first, before any thought of danger: he was younger than me, twenty at most, with the kind of beard a boy grows when he wants to be taken for a man and hasn't managed it yet. His eyes found the candle and then my face above it, and he did not look afraid of me. He looked relieved, which frightened me more.

"You're the printer's," he said.

"I'm nobody's." It came out automatic, the way a bell rings when struck. Five years of practice in four words.

"The shop." His hand moved an inch off the blanket, toward the walls, toward the racks of type and the stacks of paper and the press below us, all of it mine now by the strange inheritance of being the one who stayed. "Someone said — a shop where things could be printed. Quietly."

"Who said that?"

He smiled. It cost him visibly, like lifting something. "A dead man," he said. "Mostly."

I should have put out the candle and gone down the ladder and locked the trapdoor and let whatever happened next happen without me. I have replayed that moment many times since, the way you replay a road where you might have turned. But instead I knelt down on the cold boards beside him, because he was dying — I knew it the way you know weather coming through a wall — and there is a kind of arithmetic even silence cannot survive, and it is this: a person is about to stop being a person, in your house, and no one else knows he was ever here at all.

"What do you need printed?" I asked.

He laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing brought blood to his lips, thin and bright, and after that we both stopped pretending there would be printing.

* * *

For a day and a night I did what little could be done, which was mostly water and mostly watching. He drank in sips. He talked in fragments when the fever let him, and the fragments were worse than silence, because they had names in them. Villages upriver whose names I knew from freight manifests — I set those manifests myself, every week, the tonnage of wheat and hides flowing past towns that meant nothing to me except as columns of figures. Now the names came out of him one at a time, each one spoken the way you speak the name of a person at a funeral, and I began, slowly, with a feeling like ice forming, to understand that these were not places anymore. They had been places. Something had happened to them in the autumn, upriver, in the villages along the marshes, and whatever it was had made this boy carry a leather satchel two hundred kilometers through a country full of soldiers, and had put the hole in him somewhere south of Ruse that no needle of mine would ever close.

The satchel never left him. Even feverish, even half-conscious, he kept one hand hooked through its strap like a man holding a boat line in rough water. When I tried once to move it aside so I could get at his coat, his fingers clamped down hard enough to whiten, and his eyes opened all the way and were suddenly, completely clear, and he said:

"Not yet. You'll know when."

"I don't want to know anything," I told him, and it was almost true, and he looked at me with something like pity.

"Yes you do," he said. "You just think not wanting it will keep you safe." He breathed for a while. The loft creaked around us, the old beams settling the way they do in hard frost, and below us the town went about its evening — cart wheels, a dog, the muezzin's call thin and far away across the frozen air, then the garrison drums at change of watch. Ordinary sounds. I had built my whole life on ordinary sounds.

"My father kept this shop," I heard myself say. Why I said it I couldn't tell you. Perhaps because the silence between his breaths was becoming unbearable, and a dying man deserves better company than a ticking clock. "He died five years ago."

"How?"

"He fell ill." The lie came out smooth as fresh-set type. It always did. It had come out to the tax inspector and the priest and the neighbors and finally to myself, until it was simply a fact about the world, like the color of the door.

The boy looked at me for a long moment. Fever had burned him down to something very simple and very direct, the way fire burns varnish off wood and shows the grain.

"No," he said gently. "But that's not my business."

And then he closed his eyes again, and I sat in the dark with my hands shaking, furious at him, furious at myself, because he had seen through a lie I hadn't known was a lie, and some load-bearing part of my interior architecture had quietly cracked.

* * *

He died an hour before dawn on the third day.

It was quick at the end, which I am grateful for, though I didn't know until later that gratitude would be the feeling, and not horror. He surfaced once more from wherever he had gone, and his hand found my wrist — strong, shockingly strong for a body that had almost finished — and he pulled the satchel strap free of his own shoulder and pushed it into my hands and held my hands closed over it with both of his.

"Listen," he said, and his voice had gone quiet and precise, a man spending his last coins carefully. "Inside. Testimonies. People who saw. Written down, signed, sealed — some of them pressed their own thumbs. They gave them to us because they believed they would reach print. Reach people. If they burn with me, then everyone who trusted us —" He stopped. Swallowed. "You understand what I'm saying. It isn't paper. It's them. Their names are in it and their names are the whole point."

"Give it to someone else," I whispered. "Take it to the priests, the consuls, anyone—"

"They'll take it to the governor's desk within a day, and the governor will burn it and then come looking for whoever carried it." His grip tightened. "There's one name. Listen. *Veselin.* Say it back."

"Veselin."

"He'll find you. Or you'll find him. One or the other, before spring, or none of this matters." His eyes were going now, focusing somewhere past the ceiling beams, past the frost on the roof tiles, past everything. "Tell them —" he started, and stopped, and I watched a whole sentence die in him unfinished, some message home, some apology, some love, gone the way smoke goes when a door opens.

Then his hands relaxed, and the weight of him settled into the boards with a finality that no living body has, that particular surrender of all the muscles at once, and the loft was very quiet, and the candle flame stood perfectly still as if it too were listening.

I sat with him while the sky went from black to iron to the pale washed yellow of a snow morning. I am not sure why. It seemed wrong to leave before the light came, as if darkness were his and daylight belonged to the living and I shouldn't cross the border early. Somewhere in that hour I noticed I was praying, silently, in the shapeless wordless way of someone who has not prayed since childhood, and I let it go on, because it cost nothing and hurt no one, and it was the first sound I had made in years that was entirely mine.

* * *

Then came the practical problem, which is that I had a dead man in my loft and a town below me that would hang me for him.

I want to be honest about what I felt, because later, when people told the story — and people did tell the story, versions of it, most of them wrong — they wanted me to have felt noble things. I did not feel noble. I felt terror in its purest industrial form, terror like a machine running in my chest, and beneath it a cold clerk's mind taking inventory. Body: one, approximately sixty kilograms, impossible to bury in frozen ground without digging visible in the yard, impossible to weigh down and sink because the river was sealing itself with ice and the garrison patrolled the bank. Time: unknown, but surely short, because a courier overdue somewhere would eventually be missed, and missing couriers make men search lofts. And the satchel in my hands, heavier than its weight by exactly the mass of every name inside it.

I wrapped him in the horse blanket and then in the tarpaulin we used for paper shipments, and I got him down the ladder in stages, lowering him by rope over the loft rail, catching my breath between inches, certain every creak would bring the watch. Below the shop there is a cellar — every building on that street has one, dug deep against the summers, and ours had a section behind the coal bin where the foundation stone had been cut away generations ago and bricked up badly, leaving a gap wide enough for a man who was not particular. My father knew about that gap. I will say no more than that about my father and cellars.

I will not describe the rest of that day. Some work is work, and describing it makes it spectacle, and he deserved better than to become a scene in someone's telling. Suffice it to say that by nightfall the coal bin stood undisturbed, the floor was scrubbed with lye, and the smell of lime masked everything that needed masking, and I had lied to two customers at the front counter with a steady voice while my arms screamed underneath my sleeves, and neither of them noticed anything, because no one ever looks closely at the quiet woman who sets their type.

That was the thought that broke me. Not the death. Not the hole in the boy's side or the blood under my fingernails that wouldn't scrub out. It was standing at the counter, handing old Petar Vrachevski his receipt for sermon paper, smiling my shopkeeper's smile, and realizing that I could stand in front of another human being with a dead man in my cellar and he would see nothing, because I had spent five years making myself into glass, and glass works both ways: no one sees in, and I see out, alone, forever, and I had done it to myself and called it safety.

Petar left. I bolted the door. I sat down on the floor of the shop among the type cases with my back against the press, and I shook for a long time, and no one saw, which used to be the whole point of everything, and for the first time felt like the loneliest fact in the world.

* * *

The satchel I hid properly, because hiding things properly was the one craft my father had taught me as thoroughly as letters.

Behind the stone shelf in the back room, where the wall meets the chimney, there is a cavity. My father built it himself, I understand now — I understood it fully only months later, but even that first night my hands remembered the trick of it, the way you press the third brick and lift. He had kept forbidden type there. Greek characters, Cyrillic sorts cast outside the garrison's licensed foundry, the matrices for pamphlets that had gotten two printers in Sofia hanged the year before I was born. As a child I had found it once, and he had not scolded me. He had sat me down on his knee in front of the open wall and said, "Ivo. Everything that is true is not permitted. So some truths must live in walls. Remember which bricks remember."

Remember which bricks remember. He said things like that, my father. I thought they were just how he talked.

I opened the wall by candlelight. Inside, the space smelled of dust and old metal and, faintly, of linseed oil and lead — the smell of my father's hands. The forbidden sorts were long gone; I had sold the Greek piecemeal in my lean first year, not knowing, God forgive me, not knowing what I was selling. But the cavity was there, dry and dark and patient, exactly big enough for a leather satchel full of other people's courage.

Before I put it in, I opened it. Just once. Just enough.

Papers. Dozens of them, folded and sewn into oilcloth bundles, each bundle labeled in a careful clerk's hand with a village name and a date from the autumn. I untied one — only one, I promised myself, only one — and read the first lines by candlelight, and the first lines were enough. A woman's testimony, dictated because she could not write, taken down and read back to her and confirmed with her mark, a thumbprint in faded ink like a small dark flower. She described a morning in October. She named soldiers. She named officers. She counted, in the plain exhausted voice of someone counting the uncountable, and her count did not match any figure that would ever appear in any proclamation I would ever set for the garrison, and I knew, sitting on the floor of my father's shop with my father's hidden wall open in front of me, that the difference between her number and theirs was the entire reason this boy had died on my boards.

At the bottom of the satchel, wrapped separately, was a roll of paper that made my breath stop: maps. Hand-drawn, inked, annotated in margins crowded with tiny notes — and the handwriting stopped my heart, because I knew it, I had grown up inside it, I had learned my letters from it. My father's hand. Maps of the river villages, drawn years ago, marked with names and dates going back further than the massacre, further than the courier could have carried.

My father had been collecting testimonies before he died. Before the boy. Before any of it.

The candle guttered. Downstairs in the street, boots passed — garrison boots, two men, unhurried, the patrol that walked our street at nine every night. I sat motionless until they faded, and then I rolled the maps closed, tied the bundles as they had been tied, and slid the satchel into the wall, and pressed the third brick home.

I put out the candle and sat in the dark and listened to my own heart, and somewhere in that listening a sentence assembled itself in me, letter by letter, the way type assembles into a line, and the sentence was this:

*They gave their names believing they would be printed.*

And beneath it, arriving quietly, the way frost arrives, the first crack running through five years of useful silence — not a decision, not yet, decisions come later, decisions are expensive — just a question, small and terrible, glowing in the dark like a coal:

Whose brick remembers now?

In the morning there would be bread to buy and a proclamation to set and a town to fool. In the morning I would be nobody's business again. But that night, lying in the loft where the boy had died, listening to the river groan and shift as it froze itself shut for the winter, I understood that something had crossed into my keeping, and that keeping is not the same as hiding, and that the governor's men were already asking questions in the taverns about a courier who should have reached Ruse a week ago —

— and that sooner or later, someone was going to come looking for the print shop.
