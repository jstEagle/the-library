## 4. Governor's Ink

The order came down at half past nine in the morning, which Emil Vukov took as a good sign. Orders that arrived before noon were administrative. Orders that arrived after the governor's second brandy were something else entirely.

"Vukov." Captain Ristich stood in the doorway of the records room with a folder under his arm and the particular blankness of a man carrying something he'd rather not hold. "His Excellency wants the official account of the November operations ready for the courier by Friday."

"Which operations?" Emil asked, though he knew. Everyone in the garrison knew; it was only that no one said the word *operations* out loud without the quotation marks around it.

"The river villages." Ristich set the folder on Emil's desk and did not sit. "Bandit suppression. You'll have the casualty figures from Draganov's column, the engagement reports, and His Excellency's summary of the security situation. Your job is to make it read properly. Clean prose. Nothing that will embarrass anyone in Sofia or anywhere else."

"Clean prose," Emil repeated.

"You're the best hand in the building." Ristich said it like an accusation. Then he left, and Emil sat looking at the folder as if it might open itself out of spite.

He had been a clerk for eleven years. He knew what folders like this contained before he untied the string: numbers already rounded, dates already adjusted, a vocabulary of *armed resistance* and *necessary measures* and *regrettable excesses* waiting to be arranged like type into a page that would be true to no one and useful to everyone. He had written such pages before. That was the thing about complicity — it never announced itself. It arrived dressed as competence.

Emil untied the string.

* * *

The first report was from Major Draganov himself, dictated in the field and transcribed by a subaltern with bad handwriting and worse nerves. It described three days of action against armed bands in the villages along the south bank. The word *action* did a great deal of work. Reading between its lines, Emil could see where the fighting had stopped being fighting: the village of Koshava, listed as having eleven armed men killed, also listed as having forty-one total dead, of whom nine were noted, in a marginal hand, as *women/children — collateral*. In Bregovo the arithmetic was worse. In Novo Selo there was no arithmetic at all, just a line reading *village cleared*, which was the kind of phrase a man writes when he cannot bring himself to write the other kind.

Forty-one. Nine. *Collateral.*

Emil copied the figure onto his working sheet and his pen hesitated over it for a moment longer than a pen should hesitate. Then he wrote the number again in the margin, because clerks are trained to verify, and verification is a form of prayer.

The second document was the governor's own summary, four pages of imperial prose about the security of the Danubian frontier, the perfidy of the komitadji bands, and the gratitude of the peaceful population. The peaceful population. Emil had been through those villages two summers ago on a census errand. He remembered a woman in Koshava who had given him water from her well and complained, mildly, about the price of salt. He could not make that woman fit into any sentence beginning *bandit suppression*. But sentences do not need to fit people. That is their great advantage over memory.

By midday he had the skeleton of the account assembled. By one o'clock his head ached in the specific way it always ached when he spent hours writing things he would not say aloud. He went out into the yard for air and stood watching the smoke rise from the garrison kitchens, thinking about ink.

Ink was the honest part of his trade. Ink was ground oak gall and iron sulfate and gum arabic, and it behaved according to laws. Type was honest too — every letter cast from a matrix, every matrix cut by some craftsman whose habits lived in the metal forever, the way a voice lives in handwriting. A compositor could no more hide himself in his setting than a singer could hide in a note. Emil had learned to read printers the way other men read faces. It was the one pleasure the job had left him.

That was why, when he thought about the proclamation that would accompany the official account — the public version, broadsheet, to be posted in Vidin and the river towns — his mind went immediately, and unwelcome, to Delacroix's shop.

* * *

The shop stood on Krajna Street, wedged between a cooperage and a house that had been empty so long its windows had gone grey. Old Delacroix had run it for twenty years — a foreigner, French or half-French depending on who told the story, a man who cut his own typefaces because he claimed nothing bought from Vienna held an edge properly. Emil had known him slightly. They had talked twice about paper stock and once, memorably, about whether the Cyrillic *zh* could be made beautiful at eight point. Delacroix had argued it could not. Emil had argued back. It had been, he realized later, the last real conversation he'd had in this town.

Delacroix died five years ago. Fever, they said, though fever was the town's universal explanation for anything it preferred not to examine. And the daughter stayed on — Ivo, though almost nobody called her that. She ran the press the way a ghost runs a haunted house: competently, silently, appearing when needed and vanishing otherwise. The garrison sent her work because she was cheap and exact. The town tolerated her because she was useful. Emil had watched her set type perhaps a dozen times, always from the doorway, always briefly.

And here was the thing that had lodged in him like a splinter: he could identify her father's matrices blindfolded. The old man's lowercase *a* had a spur at the shoulder, small and sharp, a signature no foundry ever produced. The *t* crossed low. The numerals leaned. Five years of proclamations from that shop, and every one of them carried the dead man's hand forward into the world, because the daughter had kept the type and the habits both.

Emil walked back inside and pulled the file of past broadsheets. He laid them side by side on his desk: harvest notices, quarantine orders, the announcement of the bridge closing. Same spur on the *a*. Same low crossbar on the *t*. He had looked at these a hundred times and seen nothing but paperwork. Now he saw a lineage, and the lineage troubled him, and he could not immediately have said why.

It troubled him because of the courier. That was the truth of it, the truth he had been circling all morning.

* * *

Three days ago a man had died in the print shop's loft.

Emil knew this the way he knew most things in Vidin: sideways, incompletely, through the accumulation of details that respectable attention ignored. He knew that Mara the ferryman's widow had taken grain across on the last day before the ice closed, and had come back lighter than she went. He knew that Stefan the baker had baked bread that week in quantities his ledgers could not gracefully explain. He knew that a stranger's body had gone into the ground behind the churchyard wall in a coffin the carpenter charged half price for and asked no questions about. And he knew that Ivo Delacroix had come to the garrison office two days after all this to collect a routine printing commission, and that her hands had smelled faintly of lamp oil, and that she had stood in this room with her eyes down and answered every question correctly, which was itself unusual, because frightened people answer incorrectly.

He had not thought much of it then. He thought of it now, with Draganov's casualty figures open on his desk and the word *cleared* sitting in his gut like a swallowed stone.

Because if a courier had come across the river in the last week of navigation — a courier worth smuggling, worth burying cheaply and quickly — then the question was not *what did he carry* but *who wanted it erased badly enough to close a bridge over it*. And the governor's sudden appetite for an official account, polished and couriered south before spring, suggested that the erasure had already begun, and that Emil's clean prose was to be its instrument.

He looked down at his working sheet. Forty-one. Nine. *Collateral.* Somewhere below those numbers were people: the woman with the well, maybe, or someone's version of her. And somewhere above them was a paragraph he had drafted that morning, in his best administrative hand:

*Order has been fully restored in the affected districts. The peaceful population, long oppressed by armed bands, has welcomed the measures of the authorities.*

He had written *welcomed*. He stared at the word until it stopped looking like a word.

* * *

On Thursday the governor summoned him.

Osman Nuri Pasha — the title was Ottoman, the manner was entirely his own — received Emil in the upstairs office where the maps hung, the river drawn in blue thread and the frontier in red. The pasha was a small, precise man who spoke Bulgarian and Turkish and German and used each language for a different category of lie.

"Vukov. The account. Show me."

Emil handed over the draft. The pasha read fast, the way powerful men read, trusting their own conclusions to be faster than the text. At the third page he stopped.

"This figure." He tapped the margin. "Forty-one in Koshava."

"Yes, Excellency. From Draganov's report."

"It reads badly." Not *it is wrong*. It reads badly. Emil understood the distinction the way a locksmith understands locks. "A number like that invites questions. Questions invite correspondence. Correspondence invites inspectors." The pasha looked up, mild as milk. "You will adjust the composition of the losses. Armed men engaged: state the full figure. Non-combatant deaths: omit the category entirely. Grief is not a statistic, Vukov. It does not belong in an official document."

"No, Excellency."

"And the villages themselves. Draganov burned structures?"

"Some structures, Excellency. In the course of clearing positions."

"Then write *reduced*. Villages reduced. It sounds like weather." The pasha smiled faintly, pleased with his own wit. "You have a talent, Vukov. I have noticed it. Most men write what happened. You write what can be survived. That is rarer than courage, and more useful to me."

Emil bowed. On the walk back down the stairs he counted the steps — fourteen, he had counted them for eleven years — and thought: *I am a man who writes what can be survived.* He tried the phrase on like a coat and found it fit too well and weighed too little.

Back at his desk, he did what he was told. He struck the category. He redistributed the dead among the armed, so that forty-one became twenty-six and nine children became arithmetic that had never existed. He changed *burned* to *reduced*. He watched his own hand perform these operations smoothly, without tremor, and understood that this smoothness was the most frightening thing in the room, and the room contained nothing but himself and a lamp.

* * *

Friday morning he took the final draft to the print shop.

It was procedure — the broadsheet had to be set and proofed before the courier left — and it was also, he admitted to himself on the walk down Krajna Street, something else. He wanted to watch her face while she read the text. He told himself this was professional caution: a printer sees everything that crosses her stone, and a printer with a suspicious mind could cause trouble. But underneath the caution was a plainer motive. He wanted someone in this town to know what the document said, even if she never said a word about it. He wanted the knowledge to exist outside his own skull, where it had begun to feel less like information and more like a weight pressing on the backs of his eyes.

She was at the case when he came in, sleeves rolled, hair pinned up carelessly, sorting type with quick blind fingers. The smell of the shop hit him the way it always did — oil, lead, paper dust — and with it came old Delacroix's voice arguing about the letter *zh*, and Emil had to stand a moment in the doorway before he could speak.

"The garrison broadsheet," he said. "For posting. It needs to be proofed by tonight."

She took the draft without comment. He watched her read it — watched her eyes move down the columns, slow at first, then slower. He watched her reach the paragraph about the peaceful population. Her face did nothing. That was the remarkable thing; he had interrogated enough people to know how hard doing nothing was, and she did it perfectly, the way she seemed to do everything, as though invisibility were a craft she practiced daily the way he practiced penmanship.

But her hands. Her hands had gone still on the type case, one resting flat on the wood as if steadying herself against something, and her thumb had moved once, very slightly, across the grain.

"These figures," she said. Her voice was level. "They'll be questioned. People count their own dead."

"People," Emil said, "count many things."

She looked up at him then, directly, for the first time in five years of commissions and doorways, and her eyes were the color of the river in March — brown running under grey — and in them he saw not fear and not anger but assessment, cold and quick, the look of someone deciding what you were worth. He had the sudden vertiginous certainty that she knew about the loft, about the satchel, about whatever the courier had carried, and that she was measuring, right now, whether he was a danger or merely a tool, and finding him — this was the worst of it — probably finding him indistinguishable from either.

"I'll have it proofed by evening," she said, and looked back down at the case.

He left. Halfway up Krajna Street he stopped, because a thought had finally finished forming, the way a figure emerges slowly from a block of wet ink on damp paper.

Her father's type. Her father's habits, preserved letter by letter for five years in every garrison broadsheet. A woman that careful — that disciplined, that invisible — had kept a dead man's spurs and crossbars alive in official documents, quietly, insistently, the way you keep a grave weeded. Such a woman did not lose her discipline in one bad week. Whatever she was holding, she was holding it the way she held everything: exactly, patiently, in plain sight of everyone too busy to look.

And he had just handed her, to set in that inherited type, the government's account of a massacre — sanitized, reduced, deniable — the single document most likely to make her decide that silence was no longer survivable.

Emil stood in the cold street and understood that he had until Friday night, possibly less, to decide which side of his own prose he stood on.

Behind him, faintly, he could hear the press begin to move.
