## 5. Bread and Questions

The bread told the truth before I did.

I had been baking for thirty years, and in all that time my hands had never once lied to me about anything that mattered. They knew when the dough was right before my eyes did. They knew when a loaf would rise and when it would sit in the oven like a stone, sulking, and they knew — this was the thing I could not explain to anyone who had not stood where I stood — they knew when grief walked into the shop, because on those days the dough came out wrong. Too dense. Too sweet. As if flour could taste the mood of the woman kneading it.

So when I set aside four loaves on the morning after the courier died, and wrapped them in cloth instead of paper, and did not put them out for sale — my hands were telling me what I intended to do long before I let myself think it.

They were for Ivo Delacroix.

* * *

You will want to know why it took me two days. Everyone in Vidin would have wanted to know, if anyone in Vidin had known there was anything to know. Two days is nothing, you might say. A widow brings bread to another lonely soul; women do it every week of their lives. But two days was the length of a whole argument, conducted in my kitchen between the part of me that had rowed a dying man across the river and the part of me that had buried a husband for talking too freely to strangers, and neither part was winning.

Petar had drowned eleven years ago, in October, with the water still warm enough that the coroner's man said he must have gone under quick. Quick. That was the word people chose. He had been a ferryman like his father before him, and ferryman's talk is river talk: everything passes through, nothing stays. He used to say it as a comfort. *Mara, whatever they tell us today will be downstream by Thursday.* And then one autumn the things passing through were men with names the garrison wanted, and Petar passed their names along the way he passed along everything, generously, without weighing what a name weighs when the wrong ear catches it.

So I had learned. I learned to sell bread and say nothing. I learned that a widow who talks is a nuisance but a widow who listens is a danger, and I made myself into the first kind so thoroughly that half the town believed I was simple. Let them. Simplicity is a coat, and I have worn mine so long I sometimes forget I am dressed at all.

And then, two nights ago, a man had come aboard among the grain sacks with a hole in his side, and he had looked at me with the eyes of a man doing arithmetic on how much life he had left, and he had said, *the print shop,* and I had rowed him there because it was the only place in Vidin I could think of where a man might be invisible. Not because Ivo Delacroix owed me anything. Because her father had once printed three hundred leaflets on my kitchen table while I kept watch at the window, and he had paid me not in money but in a sentence I have carried since: *A town that cannot print its own grief belongs to whoever burns it.*

Nedelko Delacroix had been dead five years, and his daughter had spent those five years making herself into a shadow with ink-stained fingers, and I had watched her do it the way you watch ice form on the river — admiring the craft of it, knowing it could not hold a man's weight.

Or a satchel's.

* * *

The garrison had sealed the bridge on Sunday. By Tuesday morning the town had already rearranged itself around the fact, the way a body rearranges itself around an old injury. The market stalls sat closer together, voices lower. The coffeehouses emptied earlier. Soldiers walked the main street in pairs now, and everyone had become suddenly fascinated by shop windows, awnings, the weather.

I went up Knyaz Boris Street with the bread warm under the cloth against my hip, and I will confess what I confessed to no one, not even myself, until much later: I was afraid of her. Not of Ivo — of what asking her questions might cost us both. There are doors you knock on only when you are ready to hear what answers.

The shop smelled the way it always did, oil and paper and cold metal, a smell I had loved since childhood because my mother bought our almanac from Nedelko every January and I would stand inside the doorway breathing it in like other children breathed in honey cakes. Ivo opened the door before I knocked. She always did. A woman who has trained herself to be nobody's business develops ears like a cat's.

"Mara," she said. "Is something wrong?"

"Nothing's wrong," I said. "That's the trouble with this town lately. Everything's either wrong or pretending."

She stepped back to let me in, and I saw her glance past me down the street — left, right, the upstairs windows of the tailor opposite — before she closed the door. Careful girl. Careful as her father had been careless. Between the two of them, I thought, there might be one complete person.

* * *

Her father's press still stood in the middle of the room like a sleeping animal, and I put the loaves on the worktable among the type trays and said, "Four loaves. Don't argue. You've been living on whatever that stove of yours produces, and I've seen your stove."

"I've seen it too." But she took the cloth off, and her face did the small softening that faces do around fresh bread, the one nobody can help. "Thank you. You didn't have to—"

"I know what I didn't have to do." I pulled out the stool across from her and sat, which surprised her; I could tell it surprised her, because Ivo Delacroix had arranged her whole life so that no one ever sat down in her shop uninvited, and here I was, sitting, uninvited, with my hands flat on my knees like a woman settling in for a long winter.

"Mara," she said carefully, "is this about the bridge? Because if it's flour prices—"

"It's about a man who died in your loft two nights ago."

The silence that followed had texture. I have heard silence in churches and silence in courtrooms — well, I have heard silence outside courtrooms, watching men go in — but I had never heard a silence with weight to it until that room. Ivo did not move. Her hands, resting near the type case, went very still, the way small animals go still, and I understood that I was looking at five years of discipline holding itself together by its seams.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"Yes, you do."

"You're mistaken."

"I'm a lot of things, child, but mistaken about dead men in lofts isn't one of them." I leaned forward. "He crossed on my boat. Saturday evening, last crossing before the ice. He came aboard with the grain sacks from the Vrashka side, and he was gray as ash, and he pressed a coin into my hand with blood on it, and he said two words: *print shop.* And I rowed him to your steps because your father once told me this was the one roof in Vidin where the truth could take off its boots."

Ivo's breath came out slowly through her nose. Controlled. Counted, maybe. I recognized the technique; I had invented half of it myself over eleven years of widowhood.

"If the garrison learns of this," she said quietly, "you've just hanged yourself. Why? Why tell me?"

"Because you've had two days to run, and you haven't. Because you fed him, or hid him, or held his hand while he went, and then you got up Monday and set type for the governor's proclamation like a good little ghost, and I watched you do it through your own front window because I couldn't sleep, and I thought: either that girl is the coldest creature on the Danube, or she is carrying something so heavy she can't put it down long enough to be afraid." I tapped the table. "And I don't believe you're cold, Ivo Delacroix. Cold doesn't buy mourning candles for a stranger's husband. Cold doesn't leave bread money in the poor box at St. Petka even though she never sets foot inside. Cold doesn't keep her dead father's shop exactly as he left it, down to his reading glasses on the sill, for five years, because throwing them away would mean admitting he's gone."

"That's enough."

"It isn't nearly enough, but it's all I have, and it cost me everything to say it, so you'll listen to the rest." My voice cracked on that last word and I hated it and let it crack anyway. "I helped him. Me. The simple widow. I put a bleeding courier on my boat within sight of the garrison pier and rowed him upstream in the dark, and I have spent two days waiting for soldiers to kick my door in, and I have decided I would rather hang beside someone than alone. So. Are we alone together, girl, or are you going to send me home?"

* * *

She looked at me for a long time. Longer than politeness. Long enough that I counted the cracks in the ceiling plaster behind her head — nine — and listened to the river running under the ice beyond the wall, and wondered if I had just destroyed the only quiet friendship I had.

Then Ivo Delacroix stood up, crossed to the ladder, and climbed into the paper loft.

She was gone perhaps four minutes. I heard boards creak, heard something heavy dragged, heard a sound I couldn't name — metal sliding against wood, the particular whisper of a hidden thing being uncovered — and then she came down holding a leather satchel against her chest with both arms, the way you hold a child, or a wound.

"This," she said, "was in the loft. Under the floor panel. Where my father hid—" She stopped. Started again. "Where my father hid things."

"And inside it?"

"Paper." Her jaw worked. "Testimonies. Names, dates, villages. What happened at Novo Selo, at Gorni Tsibar — what the governor's proclamation calls bandit suppression and what these pages call—" She stopped again, and I saw that her hands were shaking now, fine tremors she couldn't count away. "There must be twenty accounts. Maybe more. Written by people who were there. Some of them signed."

"Signed." I felt the word in my stomach. "They signed their names. To that."

"Some did. Some gave names of the dead instead of their own. One is written by a woman who says she was fourteen." Ivo sat down abruptly on the stool, still holding the satchel. "He made me promise. Before he died. He said, *it goes to the river, it goes across, someone has to carry it.* And then he said a name I don't know, and then he stopped talking."

We sat with that. The stove ticked. Somewhere above us, on the street, boots passed in step, two pairs, and slowed almost imperceptibly at the door, and moved on, and both of us had stopped breathing until they did.

"Ivo," I said, "why did you show me this?"

She blinked. "Because you asked."

"No. People ask questions all day long and I lie to every one of them. You could have lied. You could have sent me home with bread and a smile and I'd have gone, because I'd have understood." I reached across the table and put my hand over hers, over the leather. "Why show me?"

And she said, in a voice so small it barely survived the trip across the table:

"Because I've been carrying it alone for two days and I thought I would die of it. Not the soldiers. Not the governor. Just — the weight. Alone. And you were the only person in Vidin who might understand what it weighs, because you carried him across the river alone, in the dark, and then got up Sunday morning and sold bread."

* * *

Afterward — I could never reconstruct exactly how we got there, only that we arrived — she untied the satchel and drew out a single folded page and read it to me. I offered to read it myself; she said no, softly, the way you decline to let a guest wash dishes, and I understood: she needed to be the one whose voice carried it, so it wouldn't be only hers anymore.

It was from a schoolteacher in Gorni Tsibar. It named eleven men taken from their houses on the night of the fourth of December. It described the yard of the customs house. It described what the yard looked like afterward. It ended with a sentence I have never managed to forget, though God knows I have tried: *I write this so that when they say it did not happen, there will exist one piece of paper that knows otherwise, and I sign my name — Anton Vasilev — so that the paper may be believed even if I am not permitted to remain.*

The stove ticked. The river ran. Ivo lowered the page.

And then she did the thing I regretted more than anything else that winter, more than the bread, more than the confession, more than every careful step that followed: I looked up and saw that she regretted showing me. It was small — a flicker, a tightening at the corner of her mouth, the look of a woman who has set down a heavy load and realized the ground beneath it is thinner than she thought. She had spent five years being safe by being unknown, and she had just handed me the key to her destruction, and some part of her — the part her father had built and the soldiers had finished — was already calculating what I might cost her.

"Don't," I said.

"Don't what?"

"Don't take it back. Whatever you just decided to take back — don't." I stood, and my knees complained, and I ignored them. "I've been a keeper of secrets for eleven years, Ivo Delacroix, and I'll tell you what I know about keeping them. Kept alone, they rot you. Kept alone, they turn you into what this town thinks I am — a simple woman with a bread stall and nothing behind her eyes. I kept my mouth shut for my husband's killers' sake and called it survival, and survival it was, but I will not call it a life. You showed me that page because some part of you is done being alone with it. Listen to that part. It's the only honest voice you've got left."

She stared at me. Ink on her fingers, her father's glasses on the sill behind her, the satchel in her lap like a sleeping animal that might wake and ruin us both.

"The governor wants this story burned," she said finally. "Emil Radivoevich came himself to order the proclamation — the official account. Bandits suppressed, order restored, casualties regrettable. It's already set. It prints tomorrow." Her thumb moved along the edge of the page, back and forth, back and forth. "And I'm going to stand at the press and print it, and then I'm going to hide these under the floor where my father hid his forbidden type, and wait for spring like a good ghost."

"Are you asking my permission?"

"No." A ghost of something crossed her face — not quite a smile, but related to one, the way smoke is related to fire. "I'm telling you what I said to myself this morning. And then you knocked on my door with bread, and now I can't say it anymore, because now someone else knows."

"That's what being known does."

"It's terrifying."

"It's the only thing worse than being invisible," I agreed. "And the only cure for it."

At the door, wrapping my shawl, I paused with my hand on the latch. Through the frosted glass I could see the street, gray with new snow, and at the far corner, where Knyaz Boris meets the market square, a boy was standing in the lee of the chandler's awning — Jovan, the ferry clerk's orphan, thirteen years old and all elbows — and he was watching my back the way boys watch when someone has taught them to watch.

Ivo saw him too, over my shoulder. She went very still.

"He's been there three days," she said quietly. "Watching the shop."

"Then either the garrison has recruited very short soldiers," I said, pulling the door open onto the cold, "or somebody else in this town is counting watchers — and I intend to find out who taught him to do it. Because I knew that boy's mother, Ivo, and his mother kept secrets for your father."

I stepped out into the snow and did not look back, because looking back is for women who haven't yet decided, and I had decided. Behind me I heard the bolt slide home, slow and deliberate, and then — faintly, unmistakably, through a door built to keep sound out — the sound of floorboards lifting, and a girl deciding what to hide and what, at last, to keep.
