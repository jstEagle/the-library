## 6. The Atlas Her Father Drew

I did not open the satchel for two days.

This was not courage. It was the opposite of courage, performed with great discipline, the way my father used to say you could tell a liar from an honest man by watching how carefully he held his hands still. I set type for the garrison's grain requisitions. I locked the shop at dusk and walked the three streets to Mara's and back. I ate bread that tasted of nothing. And twice a day, morning and night, I stood at the foot of the ladder to the paper loft and looked up into the dark where the satchel sat inside the false panel behind the case of condemned Greek type, and I let it look back down at me.

A courier had died in my loft with his blood drying into the floorboards I had scoured until my knuckles cracked, and he had left me a name — *Vesna* — spoken like a coin pressed into a palm, and I had done what I always do. I had put it away.

On the third day it snowed hard enough to close even the garrison's patrols, and there was no work worth doing, and I told myself I was only going up to check that the damp hadn't reached the paper stock. That is the thing about lies: you learn them so well from the outside, watching other people tell theirs, that you can sell yourself one at a fair price.

The loft smelled of size and cold glue and, faintly, of the tallow candle I had burned the night the courier died. I moved the Greek case aside. The panel came free on its leather hinges. The satchel was heavier than I remembered, or I was lighter than I had been.

I carried it down to the composing stone because I could not do whatever came next standing in a loft, hunched under beams, like a thief. I spread a sheet of clean newsprint on the stone. I unbuckled the strap. My hands, which have not shaken since the week of my father's funeral — grief taught me early that shaking costs time — shook now.

Papers. Perhaps thirty of them, folded in quarters, some sealed with wax gone brittle, some tied with string, one wrapped in oilcloth against the river damp. And beneath them, flat as a fallen bird, a folio bound in grey board with no title on its spine.

I took out the papers first because they were on top, and because taking them out delayed the moment when I would understand why the courier had said *Vesna* and looked at me the way men look at a door they are about to stop knocking on.

The first was written in a clerk's hand, careful, ruled margins, the kind of hand that has been punished for sloppiness. *They came to Novo Selo on the Tuesday after the Feast of the Dormition,* it began, and I stopped reading after four lines and folded it shut, because my body understood before my mind did what kind of document this was, the way a printer's hand knows a font by touch before the eye confirms it.

Testimonies. That was the word my father had used once, late, drunk on plum brandy he kept for cleaning the rollers, sitting where I sit now. *One day someone will want testimonies, Ivka, and no one will have thought to keep any.* I had been fifteen and bored and I had asked him who would want them and he had looked at me with an expression I spent five years deciding had meant nothing.

I opened the second paper. A woman's hand, unsteady, spelling phonetically. The same village. The same Tuesday. Numbers. Names of the dead, listed the way women list things — household by household, mother to child, so the list itself became a map of who had lived next to whom.

I opened the third. Then the fourth. I did not read them whole. I read enough of each to know, and then I laid them out on the stone in rows, and I stood over them the way I stand over a forme before locking it up, checking that everything is where it must be.

Thirty-one accounts. One event. Five villages along the south bank.

And every account named the same unit of garrison irregulars, and the same order signed — though none of the writers knew it was an order; they described it as men arriving, men asking questions, men burning — by a name I had set in metal eleven days ago for the governor's New Year proclamation. I had inked that name myself. I had distributed the copies myself, wrapped in brown paper, to the khan and the priest and the schoolmaster, and I had felt nothing, because feeling nothing is my craft, and I had never once wondered whose hands that name came out of, only how many em-quads it needed to fit the line.

I sat down on the stool and put my face in my hands and stayed that way for a long time. The stove ticked. Snow worked at the window. Somewhere upstairs a soldier of the quartered patrol turned over in his sleep, and the ceiling joists spoke.

Then I untied the grey folio.

* * *

My father's maps were famous in our house and nowhere else. He drew them the way other men whittle — compulsively, beautifully, without obvious purpose. As a child I had thought they were for a book he would someday write, an atlas of the Lower Danube, every ford and ferry and fish weir between Vidin and the marshes, every village drawn in his small precise hand with its church and well and the number of households noted in the margin like a compositor counting lines.

There were nine maps in the folio. I knew eight of them. I had grown up with them pinned above the stone, had learned to read by tracing the river's blue thread from island to island.

The ninth map I had never seen.

It was Novo Selo. Drawn in his hand beyond doubt — the little hook he made on every *h*, the way his wells came out slightly oval because he drew them fast — but annotated differently. Not church and well and household count. Names. Dozens of names, in pencil first and then gone over in his good iron-gall ink, each one attached to a house, a field, a stretch of bank. And beside the names, dates, running backward through 1870 and 1869, and beside the dates, single words in his cramped private shorthand, which I should not have been able to read except that as a girl I had copied his job tickets for practice and so knew that *†k* meant *killed* and *dsp* meant *disappeared* and *sold.* meant the soldiers had come and taken what they wanted and gone.

Novo Selo was covered in them. So were the margins of the eighth map, Kozloduy, which I had thought was simply his most loving rendering of the river bend. Names and dates and *†k*, going back years. A census of the dead, kept house by house, in the hand of a typeface cutter in a town that believed he made his living printing proclamations and prayer cards and the ferry schedule.

I turned pages until my fingers went stupid with cold. There was a letter tucked in the back cover, unfinished, addressed to no one, dated six weeks before he died.

*—and if anything happens to me, the folio goes to Vesna K., who will know what it is for, and the papers as I collect them go wherever she says. Tell her it is not finished. Tell her the man in the capital who buys our paper stock is not what he seems and the governor's office knows more than it prints. Tell her Ivka is not to be told until she asks, because a question asked too soon is a bell rung in an empty church — everyone hears where it came from.*

He had died falling through the ice at the ferry landing, so the coroner wrote. Drunk, so the town agreed. Everyone had been very kind to me about it. Emil had carried the coffin's head end. Stefan had sent bread for a month. Mara had sat with me all night and said nothing, which was the kindest thing anyone did.

He had been collecting testimony at the ferry landing in February. Of course he had. That was where the crossings were. That was where people came from the south bank with their bundles and their mouths full of what they'd seen.

I put the letter down. I picked it up again. I read the sentence about me four times, because the mind does that with sentences that rearrange the furniture of a life. *Ivka is not to be told until she asks.*

Five years I had thought I was keeping his shop. Keeping his memory, keeping his type clean, keeping myself small inside the shape his death had left. Useful and quiet. And the whole time I had been the thing being kept — kept ignorant, kept safe, kept as a reserve, like type locked in a case against a job the compositor hopes never comes. He had known. He had known what the governor's office was, had known what the south bank was becoming, had died — had been killed, I could no longer make myself write otherwise, even alone, even in the privacy of my own skull — for the crime of writing down what happened to people whose names no one else would keep.

And he had left me the case, and the key, and instructions not to wake me until I asked.

I had asked. Three nights ago a dying man had asked for me instead, or for her — *Vesna* — and I still didn't know who Vesna K. was, whether she was alive, whether she was the woman who had written the third testimony in that unsteady phonetic hand, whether she had been the one my father trusted and lost track of. But the courier had come to my shop. Out of every roof in Vidin, bleeding, half-frozen, he had climbed to my loft. Someone had told him the address. Which meant the network my father built had survived him, quietly, the way a fire survives in a banked peat, and had handed its last parcel to his daughter without telling her what she was inheriting.

* * *

I worked until dark without deciding anything, which is the only kind of decision-making I trust.

That is the trade secret of compositors, though we rarely say it aloud: you do not decide what a page will say. You take the copy you are given, and you give every word its due measure, and the page decides itself. So I sorted. Testimonies by village, then by date, then by hand — seven distinct hands among the thirty-one, maybe eight. I interleaved them with the relevant map, because a testimony without its geography is a scream in a cellar, and my father had understood that better than anyone: put the name on the house, the house on the street, the street on the bend of the river, and suddenly the dead are not a number but a neighborhood. An atlas of what happened. He had even begun to plan it as print — there were marginal notes in his shorthand about signatures and column widths, about printing it small, pocket-sized, *so it can be sewn into a coat.*

So it can be sewn into a coat. He had planned for flight. He had planned for exactly the winter I was standing in.

By lamplight I made a fair copy list of what the satchel held: thirty-one testimonies, nine maps, one unfinished letter, one item I had saved for last because my hands had refused it.

The last item was a single printed sheet, folded around something stiff. On the outside, in the official style I knew better than my own heartbeat: *Report of the Commission Appointed to Inquire into the Disturbances of the South Bank, November 1869.* I unfolded it. Inside, pasted to the page, was the true casualty list — not the commission's eleven bandits killed in a justified action, but the other list, in another clerk's hand, ninety-three names with ages. A child of four months. A ferryman. Two schoolteachers.

And in the corner, in pencil, in a hand I knew — smaller than his maps, hurried, the *h*s hooked even here — my father had written: *Source: E. Confirm before use.*

E.

Emil, who had carried my father's coffin. Emil, who comes to the shop on Thursdays to inspect the press registration and stays to talk about fonts. Emil, who loved my father, everyone knew that, the way clerks love the craftsmen who make their paperwork beautiful. Emil, who for five years had watched me with those patient gray eyes whenever I handed him a proof, and whom I had catalogued long ago as harmless.

*Confirm before use.* My father had trusted him provisionally, the way you trust a font you haven't proofed. And the courier had carried this sheet — had carried my father's own caution about Emil — into my loft.

Above me, boots crossed the floor. The evening patrol, changing watch, their tread familiar now as weather. I blew out the lamp and sat in the dark with the satchel open on the stone, thirty-one voices and nine maps and one penciled name, and I understood at last what my silence had been all these years. Not safety. Storage. I had been the loft where my father kept the truth, and I had never once been asked my consent, and I found — sitting in the cold, with the snow erasing the town outside — that I was not angry.

I was ready. That was the terrible, quiet fact of it. Five years of useful invisibility had been apprenticeship. Every proclamation I set for the governor had taught me his language; every lie I had typeset had shown me its seams. He had raised a printer the way other men raise sons — deliberately, patiently — and the first job of my mastership was waiting on the stone.

Tomorrow was Thursday. Emil would come at ten, as he had come for sixty-two consecutive Thursdays, to check the registration and talk about fonts.

I would ask him about November 1869. And I would watch his hands.
