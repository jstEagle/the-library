## 7. A Census of the Living

The order came down from the governor's office on a Tuesday, in a hand so fine it looked embarrassed by its own contents.

*By directive of His Excellency the Vali: a complete census of households within the walls of Vidin and the river quarters, to be concluded before the thaw. Every soul to be entered by name, age, trade, and paper of residence. Inspection of shops and storehouses to follow. Delinquencies to be reported under seal.*

Emil Vassilev read it twice at his desk in the records room, where the light came through one window barred like a confession booth, and then he read it a third time because he was hoping he had misunderstood. He had not misunderstood. He never misunderstood. That was his whole value to men like the kaimakam and the governor above him — he understood everything the first time, wrote it out cleanly, and asked nothing.

A census before spring. Which meant a census while the river still held ice, while no barge could leave and no traveler could arrive without explanation. A census taken when every bird was caged.

He took a fresh sheet and began drafting the schedule of wards — which streets, which inspectors, which days. His pen moved with the smoothness of long habit. Somewhere in the middle of the third column he noticed that his hand was steady, and that steadiness frightened him more than trembling would have.

* * *

There were eleven hundred and forty households in Vidin proper, if you counted the tanneries' huts as households, which the last census had not. Emil knew the number because numbers were the only country he had ever been granted citizenship in. Names blurred for him; faces blurred; but columns held their shape. In five years at the garrison's records desk he had built a private architecture of the town out of tax rolls, grain levies, and death registers, and he could walk through it with his eyes closed — the widow Popova at number 14 Knyaz Street, two looms, one son gone to Bucharest; the ferryman Keremedchiev drowned two autumns past, his boat licensed to his widow now at a reduced fee the clerk himself had arranged, though he doubted she knew it.

He did not think of her as a person he had helped. He thought of it as a line drawn correctly. It was important, in his work, to keep such distinctions. Otherwise a man began to feel things about lines, and a man who felt things about lines was of no use to anyone, least of all himself.

The print shop stood at the end of Tanners' Lane, registered to *Delacroix*, first name left blank in every document since the father's death — an omission Emil had permitted five years ago with a shrug he still remembered, because the dead man's daughter had signed the inheritance papers herself, in a small upright hand, and the Ottoman clerk of that season had not cared whether a woman's name went in the column or not, and Emil, who had been translating, had let the carelessness stand. *Delacroix, heir.* No given name. A household of one.

One soul, one trade: printer. Papers in order. The easiest line in eleven hundred and forty.

He would write it himself, he decided. Not because it needed him — any inspector could copy a name off a door — but because he wanted to see the shop again, and he had learned to disguise want as diligence. This too was a skill, and like all his skills it made him smaller the better he got at it.

* * *

Stefan the baker's premises were the second stop on Tanners' Lane, and Emil reached them an hour after dawn on the first inspection day, with a soldier named Karadzha yawning behind him and the cold coming up through his boot soles like water into a sinking hull.

The bakery smelled of woodsmoke and hot wheat, and the smell did something to Emil that he resented — it reminded him that he had eaten nothing but garrison bread for three days, and garrison bread was a punishment invented by someone who hated flour.

"Stefan Angelov," Emil said. "Household census. Open your ledgers."

The baker was a mountain who moved like a man trying to take up less room than he occupied. "Ledgers," he said, as if tasting the word. "For bread?"

"For grain. You draw from the state mill. You owe entries against each sack."

"I know what I owe." But something crossed Stefan's face — quick, there and gone, like a fish under ice — and Emil saw it, because seeing was the other thing he did instead of feeling.

The ledger came across the counter wrapped in a cloth still warm from near the ovens. Emil opened it on a floury barrelhead and began, column by column, to do the arithmetic of a man's survival.

It was good forgery. That was the first thing he understood, and understanding it gave him a strange pride, as if he were appraising craftsmanship rather than crime. The entries matched the mill's issue tallies almost perfectly — sacks in, loaves sold at the controlled price, wastage recorded with a modesty that would disarm suspicion. Almost perfectly. But the mill's February tally, which Emil carried in his head along with everything else, showed forty-two sacks issued, and the ledger accounted for thirty-nine, and the missing three were spread across January in half-sack adjustments so small that only a man who did arithmetic the way other men prayed would ever find them.

Three sacks of grain, unrecorded. Enough flour to feed perhaps fifteen people through the worst of the winter. Or enough to feed one houseful of hidden mouths, or to barter for a forged travel pass, or to pay a courier's fee.

Emil thought of the courier. Everyone at the garrison had thought of the courier since December — the man who had come over the ice with papers in his coat and died somewhere in the town before the garrison could take them back, and whose route the governor's men had been hunting ever since with a fury that suggested the papers mattered more than the man. Three sacks of grain moving through unofficial hands was exactly the shape of that hunt.

"You're short three sacks against the February issue," Emil said.

Stefan did not flinch. That was the remarkable thing. A guilty man flinches; this one simply stood there like a wall being asked about the weather. "Mice."

"Mice ate three sacks of grain and left the sacks."

"Hungry mice."

Behind him, Karadzha snorted. Emil kept his eyes on the ledger. Under the counter, he could hear — faintly, from the room beyond the ovens — a sound he was fairly sure was a child being shushed. The baker's sister had arrived from Lom in November, Emil recalled, with two children; the entry for them existed in the household roll, legal and correct. So why hide children who were legal?

Unless the children in the back room were not the ones on the roll.

He closed the ledger. He heard himself say, in the flat voice he used for officialdom: "Adjust your wastage figures. Mice are an accepted loss at four percent; you've claimed less than one, which looks like falsification rather than frugality. Bring the corrected book to the records office by Friday." And he watched the sentence land on Stefan — watched the big man understand, in one slow blink, that the clerk had found the gap and then chosen to describe it as arithmetic instead of treason — and something passed between the two of them that Emil refused to name even inside his own skull.

"Friday," Stefan said. "Corrected figures. Thank you, effendi."

"Don't thank me. Thank the mice."

Outside, the cold hit like a slap from a stranger. Karadzha said, "That one's hiding something."

"Everyone is hiding something. It's winter." Emil walked on toward the next door and hated how easily the words came, how much truth they carried, and how little they cost him.

* * *

The print shop smelled the way it had always smelled — ink and oil and cold paper, a smell Emil associated with the only friendship of his adult life. Old Delacroix had let him stand at the composing stick some evenings and set his own name in type, letter by reversed letter, and had laughed — a great rolling laugh, ink-black fingers — and said, *Now you exist in two directions at once, my friend. Forward for reading, backward for making. That's more than most men manage.*

Five years dead. And the shop lived on, quieter, run by the daughter nobody seemed able to name.

She was at the press when he knocked, and she came to the door wiping her hands, and up close she had her father's watchfulness without his warmth — a face like a well-made lock. "Effendi. You're expected?"

"Census." He held up the schedule. "Household of record: Delacroix. One occupant. I'm required to confirm."

"One occupant," she agreed.

"Your papers."

She fetched them without hurry. Inheritance deed, trade license, the tax receipts he himself had stamped each autumn. Everything correct. Everything arranged the way her father had taught her to arrange things — plainly, so that plainness itself became the disguise.

While he copied the entries into his field book, she stood with her arms folded, watching his pen, and Emil felt the strangest sensation: that he was the one being transcribed. Her eyes followed the nib the way a compositor follows type — reading him backwards, reading what he was made of.

"The proclamation you printed in January," he said, not looking up. "The grain levy notice. Your spacing was very clean."

"Thank you."

"Cleaner than the notices from before your father died. He crowded his lines. You don't."

"He liked his letters touching. Like people." A pause. "I like room between them."

Emil wrote the final figure — *one occupant, trade: printer, papers verified* — and stopped, nib resting on the page, in the pause where a man might say something true.

He said instead: "You'll receive a notice for the spring inspection. Keep the loft clear. The inspectors count rafters."

Something flickered in her face then — fast, faster than Stefan's fish-under-ice, so fast he'd have missed it if he hadn't spent a lifetime training himself to catch exactly such fish. Rafters. Why had he said rafters? He had meant to say storerooms. The word had come out of him carrying information he had no business giving, and now it was given, and the girl had caught it, and both of them stood there pretending it was nothing, two liars nodding politely across a threshold.

"Rafters," she repeated. "I'll remember, effendi."

* * *

That evening the records room was cold enough to crack ink, and Emil worked late because the alternative was his room above the guardhouse, where the stove smoked and the walls repeated the day back to him in a voice he disliked.

The field books had to be transferred to the master register. Eleven hundred and forty households, and by Friday he would have them all, and the master register would go under seal to the governor, and the governor would hold in one bound volume the complete inventory of every life in Vidin — who hid whom, who owed whom, who could be arrested in which house without disturbing which neighbor.

He dipped his pen. He began at the top of Tanners' Lane and worked down.

Popova, 14: two occupants, verified.
Angelov, Stefan, 16: four occupants, verified — and here his pen paused, because the sister from Lom and her two children made four, and the child he had heard behind the ovens might have been any of them, and he wrote *verified* in a hand as clean as scripture, and understood that he had just committed his first falsification of the census, and that it had cost him nothing at all. One word. Four percent wastage. Hungry mice.

He sat with that for a while. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the sentry called the hour, and the sound went out over the frozen town like a stone dropped down a well.

Then he reached the end of the lane, and the last line waited for him, and it was a line he could have written in his sleep:

*Delacroix — one occupant.*

And here is what Emil Vassilev discovered about himself that night, working alone in the cold with the governor's blank register open before him: that a man can practice honesty for decades the way a monk practices silence, and it will avail him nothing, because the discipline holds only until the moment it must bear weight — and then it gives way all at once, like river ice, not gradually but catastrophically, with a sound like a shot.

Because he did not write *one occupant*.

He hesitated — he would remember the hesitation later, the way you remember the last flat stretch of ice before it goes — and then he wrote, in the same clean hand, in the space where the count belonged:

*Delacroix — two occupants. Second: lodger, seasonal laborer, papers pending.*

A phantom. A fictional man, sleeping in a fictional corner of the print shop, occupying a rafter-space of the register, invisible to every inspector because the inspectors trusted the register more than their own eyes. If soldiers were quartered there in spring, the books would already explain them. If the girl sheltered someone — a fugitive, wounded, hunted, anyone — the books would already forgive her. He had built, with one stroke of the pen, a room the state could not search because the state believed it was already full.

It was elegant. It was the best piece of clerical work he had done in years. And he sat looking at it with a nausea he recognized from childhood — the specific sickness of having gotten away with something — and he understood that the nausea was not fear of punishment. There would be no punishment. That was the horror. The line was perfect. No one would ever know.

No one except the girl, who didn't know yet. No one except God, if God kept registers, which Emil devoutly hoped He did not, because God's register would show what Emil's showed: a collaborator of five years' standing, a man who had written death orders in beautiful handwriting, discovering treason at forty-one years old and finding it *easy*. Finding it as easy as breathing. As easy as leaving a word unsaid, a mouse unfed, a name unwritten.

He finished the page. He blotted it. He locked the register in the cabinet with the governor's seal on the key, and he stood in the dark of the records room listening to the river groan under its ice — a long slow sound, like a giant turning over in sleep — and he told himself the lie he suspected he would tell himself for the rest of his life, whenever he needed it:

*I only adjusted a line. It means nothing. It commits me to nothing.*

But he had seen the girl's face at the door of the shop, reading him backward, letter by reversed letter, the way her father had taught them both — and he knew that she had caught the word *rafters*, and he knew that she was wondering, tonight, in her cold loft among the forbidden spaces, what a garrison clerk had meant by it.

And he knew, with the terrible clarity of a man doing arithmetic in the dark, that sooner or later she was going to give him an answer to a question he had not yet dared to ask — and that whatever she handed him across that counter, satchel or secret or plea, he would have to decide, finally and irreversibly, which side of his own ledger he was living on.

The river groaned again, longer this time. Spring was coming. The ice was thinner than anyone admitted.

So, it turned out, was he.
