## 8. Learning to Read a Town

My father used to say that setting type taught you to read everything. A page of galleys told you who the printer was — whether he was hurried, whether he was afraid, whether he loved his work or merely endured it. *Every hand leaves its mark*, he said, sliding a letter back into its case with two fingers, never looking at it. *Even a silence is set in someone's type.*

I had spent five years proving him right in the narrowest possible way: I could read a town the way I read a forme, letter by letter, and I had learned to leave no mark at all. But a satchel full of dead men's words does not let you keep that kind of discipline. For three days after I found his maps folded inside the courier's testimonies — his handwriting in the margins, his little river-hatched borders, names written beside villages in an ink gone brown as old blood — I did nothing but print proclamations and sweep the shop and wind the clock, all while something in my chest hammered like a loose platen.

He had known. He had been doing what I was about to do, years before the massacre, before the courier, before me. And he was dead for it, or partly dead for it, and nobody had ever told me because telling me would have made me part of it.

Well. The satchel had made me part of it anyway.

So on the fourth morning I took out a sheet of good rag paper, the kind we saved for fine jobs, and ruled it lightly into columns, and at the top I wrote nothing at all — no title, no date — because even my private papers now had to be legible to no one but me. Down the left side I wrote the town: street by street, name by name, the way my father's maps named villages. And in the second column, in pencil, so it could be erased, I began to record what I knew.

It was not much. That was the problem. Five years of useful silence had left me fluent in everyone's surface and ignorant of everyone's depths.

*Stefan the baker* — I wrote, and then sat looking at the pencil for a long time before adding: *breads the garrison. Ledger irregular?* Because that was the thing, wasn't it — I didn't know if it was irregular. I only knew that twice this winter he had charged the quartermaster for forty loaves when I'd seen thirty come out of his oven, and that he had once, a year ago, without explanation, left a basket on my step during the week my rent was due. I had assumed pity. Pity and charity were the only reasons anyone did anything for Ivo Delacroix, daughter of the dead typeface cutter. It had not occurred to me until now that it might have been wages.

That was the humiliation underneath the whole enterprise: I had spent five years being invisible, and invisibility meant I had mistaken every kindness for accident.

*The priest's widow,* I wrote next. *Mme Bogdan. Comes Thursdays for hymnals.* True, and useless. But under it my pencil hovered, and finally added: *Her husband preached against the corvée in '69. They stopped him. She still pays for paper she doesn't take.*

I had noticed that. I had noticed it three years ago and filed it nowhere, because noticing things was dangerous and I had trained myself to notice like a woman half-asleep. Now I made myself remember properly: every month, the same sum, left with me for paper "for the parish," and the parish's own paper came from elsewhere. A standing order for nothing. My father had never commented on it. I had thought it senility, or grief. Standing orders for nothing were neither. They were how you paid for something you couldn't name aloud.

And at the bottom of the column, last, almost against my will: *The boy. Jovan. Watches the bridge.*

* * *

The boy was the test I chose first, and I chose him because failing with a child felt survivable.

Jovan Kolaric was fourteen, maybe fifteen, the ferryman's sister's son, and he hung around the bridge approach in all weather doing nothing anyone could call work. Everyone said he was simple. Everyone said it the way people say such things — comfortably, having decided — and I had repeated it myself in my head for years. But I had watched him from the shop window through two winters, and simple boys don't count patrol rotations under their breath. Simple boys don't learn which sentry drinks on duty and which one counts the minutes between crossings and writes them down.

I tested him the way you'd test a suspect typeface — with something small, something that would break visibly if it was wrong.

On a grey Tuesday I carried a parcel of blank paper down toward the bridge, the ordinary weekly delivery to the toll clerk, and I let Jovan see me fumble it, saw the string loosen, saw a single sheet slide free onto the frozen mud. Then I walked on as if I hadn't noticed.

He picked it up. He looked at it — I heard his steps stop behind me, heard the particular silence of a boy deciding something — and instead of shouting after me, or pocketing it, he ran, caught me at the bridge foot, and held out the sheet with both hands, formal as a pageboy.

"You dropped this, miss."

"Did I?" I took it. Our eyes met for perhaps a second. His were watchful and entirely un-simple.

"It's good paper," he said. "Better than the toll clerk deserves." And then, before I could decide whether that was insolence or an opening, he added, very quietly, watching the bridge and not me: "There's a new patrol schedule since Sunday. Four men at night, not three. Thought the widow should know."

The widow. Not *my aunt*. The widow.

I kept my face doing nothing, which was the hardest work I had done all winter. "I'll mention it to her," I said, "if I happen to see her."

"You see her Thursdays," Jovan said. "For the hymnals." And he touched his cap and wandered off toward the bridge, a simple boy again, hands in pockets, whistling something tuneless.

I stood there holding a sheet of blank paper and feeling the ground rearrange itself under me. He had counted my Thursdays. The whole time I had believed myself a ghost walking through other people's lives, I had been *observed* — noted, scheduled, placed in someone else's census. It should have frightened me. It did frighten me. But underneath the fear was another thing, hot and unfamiliar, and it took me until I was back behind the shop counter to name it.

I mattered to somebody's map. I had been in somebody's column all along.

* * *

Mme Bogdan came Thursday, as the boy had said she would, as she had come for three years, and this time I did not simply wrap her paper and take her coin.

"I've been meaning to ask," I said, keeping my eyes on the parcel, my voice level as a locked case, "what the parish prints. On the paper you order here."

The pause lasted exactly long enough to be honest. "Nothing yet," she said.

"That's a great deal of nothing. Three years' worth."

She was a small woman, dry as a wafer, with hands that had done parish work and burial work and probably worse kinds of work besides, and she looked at me now with an attention I had never once received in this town. People looked *through* me; they looked *past* me; they looked at the counter. She looked at me the way my father used to look at a misregistered proof — seeing both the error and the intention behind it.

"Your father asked me that question once," she said. "Almost word for word."

The shop went very quiet around us. Outside, a patrol passed, boots crunching, and we both stood silent until the sound faded, and the fact that we both stood silent — that we both knew why — was already more confession than I had managed in five years.

"And what did you tell him?" I asked.

"I told him the truth, which was that I didn't know what it was for. He smiled and said that was the best kind of order — the kind where even the person paying doesn't know, so she can't tell what she doesn't know." Mme Bogdan set her coin on the counter precisely, edge to edge. "He was building something, that year. He used to say a printer is a man who owns the only weapon that gets stronger the more copies you make." Her eyes went briefly to the press, to the cases, to me. "I assumed his daughter would have his habits."

"My habits are dull ones."

"Yes," she agreed pleasantly. "Mine too."

She took her parcel and left, and I stood with my heart going like a treadle, and understood that I had just exchanged confidences with a stranger across a counter, in broad daylight, using no word more dangerous than *habits* — and that this was how it was done. This was the grammar of it. Not the courier's desperate satchel, not the governor's edicts, but two careful women talking about paper. My father hadn't built a network out of heroes. He'd built it out of Thursdays.

* * *

Stefan was harder.

The baker was a big man, loud in the market, generous with his seconds and crusts, the sort of man whose goodwill was public currency — and public goodwill is worth nothing in a sealed town, because everyone can see it and therefore no one can trust it. If I tested him and he was the governor's creature, warm-hearted and informer-handed both, I would not survive the mistake. So I watched him for six days first, the way my father's maps had taught me to watch: not at his face but at his rhythms.

What I found unsettled me. Stefan baked for the garrison on a contract, and his ledger — I glimpsed it once when I delivered a printed invoice — ran in a fair round hand that was not his. Someone else kept his books. And on the mornings the quartermaster's men collected bread, they collected from the front; but twice, before dawn, I saw a cart at his yard door, loaded from the back, driven by the ferryman's cousin, going down toward the ice cellars and the river path. Grain moved out of Vidin in winter the way blood moves from a wound: quietly, steadily, under pressure.

I didn't need a map to read that arithmetic. Bread was leaving the town that fed no soldier, and the man who baked it laughed loudest at the garrison table.

But laughing loudest is also the best cover, and I couldn't tell whether Stefan was smuggling flour to the villages that had been burned, or smuggling names to the men who burned them. So I gave him the smallest test I owned.

I brought him a job. A legitimate one: the garrison wanted fifty placards posted announcing the spring grain levy, and the clerk Emil had sent over the copy, and I printed them — I always printed whatever they sent, that was the price of the shop — and I carried a proof copy to Stefan myself, because bakers post placards and it gave me a reason to stand in his warm kitchen and be nobody in particular.

"Fine work," he boomed, holding the proof up to the window light, though he was reading it upside down. "You've your father's eye."

"Do I?"

"Oh, aye." He set it down and sliced me a heel of bread without asking, buttered it, pushed it over. "He did our wedding announcement, twenty-two years back. Set our names in letters with the little flourishes on them. Cost him nothing, he said — said every couple should start life with their names looking important." His voice stayed big and easy. His eyes, for one moment, were not. "Strange thing, ink. Stays put. You can burn a barn, flood a field, arrest a man — but what's printed stays printed somewhere."

"Ink stays put," I agreed, and ate the bread, and left.

Only at the door did I understand what had happened. He had said *arrest a man* to a woman whose father had died suddenly, conveniently, of no cause anyone would name. And he had watched my face while he said it. The whole conversation had been a test run in both directions — mine of his ledgers, his of my nerve — and we had each passed, and neither of us could say so, and I walked home through the snow grinning like an idiot at the sheer elaborate courtesy of conspirators.

* * *

That night I sat up late with my ruled sheet and added to the second column until the candle drowned.

*Stefan: smuggles from the yard door. Books kept by another hand. Knows what ink can do. Unproven.*
*Mme Bogdan: pays for nothing, has paid for three years. Was asked my father's question and answered it correctly. Likely.*
*Jovan: counts patrols. Reports to "the widow." Watched me longer than I knew. Likely.*
*Mara:* — and here the pencil stopped, because Mara was the one I could not test, and the one I most needed not to lose. Mara had carried the courier across. Mara had stood in my loft while he died. Whatever Mara was, she was already entangled with the satchel, and you do not probe a load-bearing wall.

Instead of writing, I got up and went to the loft, to the hiding place behind the loose board where my father had kept his forbidden sorts, and I took out the satchel, and I read one testimony again — the schoolteacher's wife from Oreshets, her account steady and terrible — and then I got out my father's maps and laid them beside it, village by village, and for the first time I let myself see what I had been refusing to see.

The names on the margins weren't random. They repeated. The same handful of names, village after village — the baker, in three towns; the widow, in four; a ferryman here, a schoolteacher there. My father hadn't been collecting testimonies alone. He'd been collecting the *collectors*. Somewhere along this river he had found, in every town, the same quiet people I was finding now in mine: the ones who pay for nothing, the ones whose ledgers lie kindly, the ones who watch bridges.

He had drawn me an atlas of a country that had no borders, only Thursdays. And he had died before he could finish printing it, and now the courier had died carrying it, and the pen had come to me the way a forme comes off the press — heavy, inked, and impossible to set down.

I blew out the candle and lay in the dark above the shop, listening to the soldiers snore in the room below — they'd been quartered on us since the thaw warnings started, and their breathing had become as familiar as the river's — and I did something I hadn't done in five years.

I made a plan out loud, whispering it into the dark like a prayer in a language only printers speak. Pages. Names standing as written. My father's maps, redrawn clean. An atlas of the Lower Danube that told the truth the governor's historians were already grinding smooth.

Sunday, I remembered suddenly. There was a service Sunday, and the garrison chaplain had come up from the fortress to preach, and the proclamation on the church door announced the subject: *Order Restored in the River Villages. Thanksgiving for Peace.*

Everyone in Vidin would be there. Everyone I had just begun to read.

I turned my face to the wall and tried to sleep, and failed, and was glad of it.
