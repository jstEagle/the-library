## 10. Type, Melted

They came on a Tuesday, four of them, with bedrolls and a paper signed by the governor's own hand, and they did not ask.

The order was read aloud at the door by a sergeant whose voice had the flat patience of a man reciting scripture he didn't believe: the print premises of the late Delacroix were hereby requisitioned for the quartering of His Imperial Majesty's soldiers, the press to remain sealed and untouched, the printer — *the son of the late typeface cutter*, the paper said, and Ivo let the error stand as she had let every error about herself stand for five years — to retain access for the maintenance of garrison documents. Emil's hand, she thought. The phrasing had his carefulness in it. He had written her into the order as furniture so that no one would look too closely at what furniture might do.

Four soldiers. Two slept in the loft where the courier had died, which Ivo chose not to mention. One, a heavy man from somewhere east, took the composing room floor and snored like a mill wheel. The fourth was young — seventeen at most, a face still soft around the jaw, hands that hadn't learned yet what they'd be used for. His name was Petar. He told her this on the first evening without being asked, the way boys announce themselves when they are frightened and pretending not to be.

"You're the printer," he said.

"I keep the shop."

"My uncle was a printer. In Negotin." He said it the way other men said *my uncle was hanged* — an inheritance of trouble. "He had letters that sang when you set them fast enough."

Ivo said nothing. Silence was the oldest tool in her case, older than any piece of metal in it. She banked the stove and went up to her room above the shop and lay awake listening to strangers breathe in the rooms below, and understood, in the dark, that everything she had planned now had a deadline with boots on it.

* * *

Her father's most distinctive type was called Vidin Grotesk, because he had cut it himself and named it honestly. It was not beautiful. It was blunt, thick-shouldered, made for proclamations and price lists, but it had a signature in it that no other fount in the province carried: the lowercase *a* with its flattened bowl, the *g* with a tail that hooked left like a finger pointing back at something behind you. Her father used to say a typeface was a voice, and if you set enough pages in one voice, anyone who knew how to listen could pick it out of a crowd. He meant it as a boast.

Emil had already proved him right. In Chapter Four of her life — she thought of her life in chapters now, the way her father had taught her to think of books — Emil had recognized the shop's habits in a printed page, and grown suspicious. Suspicion was a door that opened only one way. If the atlas of testimonies came off this press in Vidin Grotesk and one sheet of it ever reached the governor's desk, the evidence would walk itself home to this room, to these sleeping men, to her.

So the voice had to die before it spoke.

She began on the third night, after the soldiers' lamp in the loft guttered out and their breathing settled into the slow rhythm of the truly exhausted. She worked in the cellar, where the crucible stood — a cast-iron pot on iron legs, built for casting new sorts, its lip stained silver by decades of her father's pourings. Lead and antimony and tin, the old recipe, the alloy her father mixed by feel and never wrote down because writing down recipes was how printers got arrested.

The first sort she fed to the fire was an *a*. It seemed right to start there. The flattened bowl caught the lamplight once, and then it was a bright bead sliding into the grey melt, and then it wasn't anything at all.

She had expected grief. What she felt instead was stranger: a kind of fury so quiet it registered as calm. Every letter she destroyed was a letter that could testify against her. That was the arithmetic of the occupation — even your alphabet became ammunition, and you had to choose which weapons to surrender. She fed in the *g*s next, all of them, the hooking tails dissolving one by one. Then the distinctive numerals, the 4 with its open top that her father had cut stubbornly against convention. Then the swash capitals he'd kept for title pages, the ones he'd used on the atlas maps he drew in the last year of his life, the maps folded now in oilcloth under the floorboards with the testimonies stacked on them like leaves on stone.

By dawn the crucible held the corpse of a voice. She poured the melt into ingot molds — dull grey bars, anonymous as river stones — wrapped them in sacking, and buried them beneath the coal pile, where metal among metal would pass unexamined. Then she sat on the cellar stairs with her hands blackened to the wrist and shook, not from cold, and not entirely from fear.

This was the cost no ledger recorded. Not the danger — she had made peace with danger the night a dying man pressed a satchel into her arms. It was this: that to save the words, she had to kill the voice that would have spoken them. The atlas would come out in borrowed letters, some neutral fount bought from Ruse or sourced from Emil's own stores, and her father's hand — his actual hand, cut in steel, in brass, in the patient geometry of a man who believed towns deserved their own alphabets — would never again set a line of truth in this world. She was erasing him to preserve what he'd died gathering. There was a word for that kind of bargain and she refused to learn it.

* * *

She kept exactly enough.

One case of Vidin Grotesk, hidden in the false bottom her father built beneath the jobbing case — the same hiding place that had held forbidden type during the last crackdown, when Ivo was twelve and thought the loose board was just a loose board. From it she kept a working assortment: enough *a*s and *g*s to set the colophon page only. One page. The last page of the atlas, where the printer's name would stand, if she lived to put it there. Everything else — the maps, the testimonies, the casualty list she didn't yet have — would be set in the plain Dutch fount, the one that looked like every other shop's letters, the voice of nobody.

She was becoming, she realized, a museum of one artifact. A keeper of a single surviving voice in a dead language, to be spoken once, at the end, if there was an end.

* * *

Petar found her on the sixth day.

Not in the act — she was more careful than that, melting only in the deep hours, burying the evidence before first light. But careful is a word for people whose enemies sleep soundly, and seventeen-year-olds do not. He came down to the cellar on some errand — firewood, he claimed, though the wood bin was upstairs — and stopped on the bottom stair, and looked at the crucible, and at the sacking beside it, and at her hands, still grey at the creases no matter how she scrubbed.

He looked for a long time. Long enough for her to count the ways this could end: the shout upstairs, the sergeant, the sealed press broken open, the floorboards pried, the oilcloth bundle found, Mara taken, Jovan taken, Stefan's cell gaining company. She calculated distances. The iron poker was within reach of both of them. She hated herself for calculating it and reached anyway.

"Your fire's low," Petar said.

"Yes."

"Melt goes easier hot."

He crossed the cellar, crouched at the crucible, and fed two sticks into it with the ease of someone who had tended fires his whole short life. Then he straightened, wiped his palms on his trousers, and looked at the sacking over the coal pile for exactly one second longer than accident required.

"In Negotin," he said, "when the tax men came, my uncle melted his ornaments. The fancy initials, the borders. Set the whole shop in plain type for a year, like a beggar wearing grey. The tax men found nothing." He shrugged, a boy's shrug, doing the work of hiding a man's knowledge. "Uncle said a printer's got three possessions worth stealing. His type, his customers, and his neck. You can lose two."

"Which two?" Ivo asked, though her voice barely made it out.

"Whoever's asking." He picked up his armload of nothing, climbed three stairs, and paused. "I don't sleep well in new places," he said, not turning around. "Light bothers me. If there was noise down here, some nights — hammering, or a fire — I'd want to know it was nothing. So I could go back to sleep."

"It's nothing," Ivo said. "Some nights, it will be nothing."

He nodded, once, and went up, and his boots crossed the shop floor overhead, unhurried, and joined the rhythm of the others.

She stood in the cellar a long while after, listening to the crucible tick as it cooled. She should have felt terror. She felt something adjacent to gratitude, and distrusted it thoroughly, because a soldier who covers for you is a debt walking around on two legs, and debts in occupied towns are collected in blood as often as coin. But underneath the distrust, quieter, was the thing she had begun to feel with Mara and Jovan and even Stefan across his bakery counter: the astonishing, destabilizing discovery that she was not invisible. That people saw her. That a boy she had never spoken to had watched her for six days and decided, without being asked, what side of her silence he wanted to stand on.

Her father used to say the Danube doesn't choose its banks; it just keeps moving, and the banks are whatever holds. She was learning that people worked the same way.

* * *

The melt took nine nights. On the tenth, she poured the final ingots, and Vidin Grotesk ceased to exist anywhere in the world except one hidden case and one future page. She washed the crucible, scoured the cellar, scattered fresh coal over the burial spot, and went upstairs past the sleeping loft where the courier had died and two strangers now dreamed whatever soldiers dream.

At the window of her room, the town lay under a hard white moon. The bridge was a black seam on the frozen river. Somewhere beyond it were villages full of names she had read in the dark until she knew them like kin — names the governor was already feeding to his official history, names that would come off her press in borrowed letters because their own town's voice was now a grey bar under a coal pile.

Let him have the voice, she thought. Let him search every fount in Bulgaria and find nothing that points back here. The words were what mattered. Words survived their alphabets. Her father had believed the opposite — that the letter itself was sacred, the vessel worth dying for — and maybe he'd been right, and maybe she was committing a smaller blasphemy than she feared, saving the water by breaking the cup. She wouldn't know until spring. Until the ice cracked and the atlas moved and the question answered itself somewhere on the far bank.

She was still standing at the window when she saw the light: a single candle moving along the river road toward the bridge gate, carried by a figure walking fast through the snow, and behind it, faintly, the sound of the garrison drum waking the guard. Something had happened on the ice. Someone was crossing where no one was supposed to cross.

Below her, Petar's cot creaked as he sat up. In the loft, the sergeant swore and struck a match.

Ivo pulled on her coat and started down the stairs toward whatever the winter had brought in this time — knowing, with the certainty of a woman who has learned to read a town, that by morning, nothing in Vidin would be quite as hidden as it had been the night before.
