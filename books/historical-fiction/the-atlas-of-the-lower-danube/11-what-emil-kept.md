## 11. What Emil Kept

The list came up from the capital on a Thursday, folded inside the governor's dispatch case like a knife wrapped in linen.

Emil Vranov had been copying documents for eleven years — first for his father, who kept accounts for a timber merchant, then for the garrison, where neatness was mistaken for loyalty and rewarded with a desk by the window. He knew paper. He knew how a lie looked when it was set down carefully, with margins even and dates in order, and he knew that the most dangerous documents were never the ones written in anger. They were the ones written in ink so calm it could pass for truth.

This one was calm as still water.

*Persons executed in suppression of bandit activity, November–December.* Forty-one names. Villages along the river he could walk to in an afternoon. Ages, trades, causes of death rendered in the administrative shorthand that turned men into entries: *shot resisting arrest*, *shot attempting flight*, *shot*. The last category had no explanation at all, as though death had simply found them, the way weather finds a field.

He copied it because he was ordered to copy it. Three copies: one for the governor's archive, one for the commandant, one for the capital. His pen did not shake. That was the thing he would remember later, in the cell, in the years after — not any moment of drama, but this: the steadiness of his own hand while it reproduced forty-one deaths in a fair round script.

He was halfway through the third column when the name stopped him.

*Kerim Osmanov, courier.*

Not a village name. Not a peasant with a scythe and bad luck. A courier — which meant the man had carried something, which meant someone had wanted whatever he carried badly enough to put him on a horse in December, which meant the governor's men had chased him across the ice toward Vidin before the bridge closed.

And the courier had died here. Emil had signed the burial entry himself, back in January, one line among thirty: *unidentified male, dead of wounds, interred common ground.* He had not thought about it since. Unidentified men died in river towns all winter; the Danube collected its own.

But he was not unidentified anymore. He was Kerim Osmanov, courier, executed — the past tense doing its quiet work, the paperwork of a living man converted retroactively into the paperwork of a corpse. Someone in the capital had decided the story in advance and sent it downstream for the local offices to make true.

Emil set down his pen.

Through the window he could see the print shop's roof, the chimney standing up against the grey sky, and below it the street where Ivo Delacroix had passed that morning with her collar up, carrying nothing visible at all.

* * *

He told himself he was wrong. He was good at telling himself things; it was most of what the job required.

A courier dies on the road, and his satchel goes into the river, or into a soldier's pocket, or into the ground with him. It does not go into a print shop. Print shops are inspected. Print shops are watched — he watched it himself, in the way you watch anything you've been assigned to watch, which is to say lazily, out of the corner of an eye, until watching becomes a habit indistinguishable from ignoring.

Except he hadn't been lazy about her. That was the trouble. For five years he had known exactly what she was: the typeface cutter's daughter, quiet as a comma, competent enough that the garrison kept sending its proclamations to the shop rather than hauling type all the way to Lom. She set their words in metal and asked no questions, and he had filed her under *harmless* the way he filed everything, because filing things harmless was how a man survived being useful to men who killed forty-one people and called it suppression.

But he had also spent five years reading her father's work without knowing it. Old Delacroix had cut faces with a signature — a particular deep-welled *a*, a descender on the *g* that hooked left like a finger beckoning. Emil had handled thousands of pages out of that shop. When the old man died, the daughter had kept the punches, kept the habits. Her setting bore his hand the way a daughter's voice bears her mother's.

And three weeks ago, checking the proclamation proofs against the official text, Emil had found a correction. Small. A date in the margin adjusted — not an error she'd made, but an error *he'd* transcribed from the governor's draft, and she had caught it, silently, and fixed it, and said nothing. She knew what the true date was. Which meant she read everything they gave her. Which meant she kept, somewhere behind that useful silence, a private account of what the garrison said versus what was so.

A woman like that, given a dying courier's satchel, would not throw it in the river.

Emil sat in his office after the candles burned low and performed the arithmetic he had been avoiding since autumn. The courier had come from the villages. The villages were where the massacre was. The satchel, if it existed, if it had reached her, contained testimony — names, dates, accounts in the victims' own words or close to it. And the governor had just received orders to produce a definitive account of events before the thaw, before spring, before the world outside the frozen town could form its own opinion.

Two versions of the same winter were now sitting in the same small town, and only one of them was allowed to survive.

If he reported his suspicion — a note to the commandant, a search of the shop, soldiers going through the loft where the old man had kept his stock — the matter would be settled in an afternoon. She would be arrested. The papers would be seized. The official account would stand unchallenged, and Emil Vranov would continue to be the kind of man whose hand didn't shake.

He tried to imagine writing the note. He got as far as the salutation.

* * *

What undid him was not conscience, exactly. Conscience was a word for priests and pamphleteers. What undid him was a memory with no importance whatsoever, from four years ago.

He had come to the shop with a rush order — a tariff notice, needed by morning — and found the old man already dead a month and the girl alone at the stone, setting type by lamplight with her sleeves pinned back, her hands black to the wrist. She had checked his copy the way she always did, found his error, corrected it, and then — this was the part — she had shown him the error. Pointed to it with a brass rule, patient, not triumphant. *Here. So you know for next time.*

Nobody had ever done that for him. Eleven years of making copies for powerful men, and not one of them had ever cared whether Emil understood the difference between what was written and what was true. They paid him to not care. And this half-starved girl with ink to the elbows had treated him, for thirty seconds, as a craftsman — as a person whose accuracy mattered.

He had thought about that thirty seconds more often than he admitted, and he had never once let himself think about why.

Now, sitting over the execution list with the lamp guttering, he finally let himself think it. He loved her father's craft, and he had transferred something of that love to the daughter without ever saying a word to either of them, and love that goes unspoken for years doesn't die — it curdles, or it turns into debt, and debt wants paying.

There was also, underneath, plainer than conscience: he had copied the lie too many times. The sanitized account of the villages was due to the historian from the capital within the month, and Emil was to prepare the supporting records, and every day the task grew heavier in his hands, the way wet rope grows heavy. He had begun, without deciding to, keeping things. A draft page here. A casualty tally there. Papers that should have gone into the stove went instead into the false bottom of his document case, and he told himself he was merely disorganized, the way he told himself everything.

Forty-one names. Somewhere in this town was the other version of those names, and it was sitting in a loft above a press, and the governor meant to burn it.

Emil blew out the lamp and sat in the dark and understood that he had already chosen. The choosing had happened months ago, in small treasons, and tonight was only the moment he stopped lying about it.

* * *

He could not simply warn her. A warning is a confession; it can be traced; it puts words between two people that a third party can read. But he was a clerk, and clerks think in documents, and somewhere in the third hour of the sleepless night a solution arrived that was so obviously right he laughed aloud, alone, at his desk, a sound he hadn't made in years.

She was a printer. Speak to her in print.

The official account was in his hands — the draft narrative, *Suppression of Bandit Activity in the River Settlements*, the version with heroic skirmishes and regrettable but lawful measures and a casualty figure of nine, all of them armed. Emil had been ordered to verify it against the garrison records. Verification was his whole life. So he verified it.

Line by line. Date of the Kozloduy action: the record said the 14th; the account said the 17th, to place it after a ceasefire that had never existed. Number of houses burned at Gorni Tsibar: the requisition ledger said sixty-two; the account said twelve. The nine dead: the burial detail's own tally, in Emil's fair round hand, said fifty-three, and he had written it himself in November and never once looked back at it.

He took a clean proof sheet — the good rag paper, the kind the shop used for anything fine — and set it in his own careful script, not as an accusation but as errata. A clerk's corrections. Marginal notes in the dry idiom of his trade:

*Item: date contradicts ceasefire record, see folio 31.*
*Item: dwellings destroyed, per requisition ledger, 62 (not 12).*
*Item: interments, Nov. 20–Dec. 4, per burial detail, 53 (not 9).*

No name. No plea. Just the numbers, the true ones, laid out in the grammar of his profession — the grammar she had once honored with a brass rule and thirty seconds of patience. If she understood, she understood. If a soldier searched his office and found a discarded proof sheet, it was waste paper, an abandoned draft, nothing.

At dawn, before the garrison stirred, he walked the long way through the market square with the sheet folded inside his coat, past Stefan's bakery breathing warm air onto the cold street, past the shuttered ferry house where the widow Mara was already up, her lamp burning behind the ice-furred window. The town was holding its breath under the snow, and everyone in it seemed to know something was coming except the snow itself.

The shop was dark. He did not knock — knocking was a question, and questions were dangerous. Instead he lifted the latch of the type case that stood outside the compositor's door where she left it for cleaning each week, the old wooden cabinet with the sliding drawers, and slid the folded proof sheet beneath the bottom tray, beneath the furniture and the quoins, into the dark where the metal lived. Her country. Her language.

Then he walked home, and the strange thing — the thing he examined later like a specimen — was how light he felt. Eleven years of carrying the governor's words, and the first sentence he had ever placed in the world on his own account weighed less than a feather.

He did not see her take it. He imagined it, though, all that day, while he copied and countersigned and lied in a fair round hand: her fingers finding the paper among the type, unfolding it, her eyes moving down the errata the way they had moved over his tariff notice four years ago — and then lifting, and looking across the frozen street toward the garrison offices, toward his window, and knowing.

Knowing what? That the clerk was hers. That one of the enemy's hands had turned. That the count was fifty-three.

That evening a light burned late in the print shop, longer than usual, and Emil stood at his own dark window watching it and understood that the answer to his message was being composed right now, in metal, by lamplight, in her father's deep-welled letters — and that when it came, it would not be a note slipped into a type case. It would be a book.

He went to bed and, for the first time since November, slept.

In the morning, the governor announced that the official historian would arrive within the fortnight, and that all town records were to be gathered and sealed for his review — including, the order specified, *all private presses and their output.*

Emil read the order twice, and felt the feather in his chest turn to lead.

She had days.
