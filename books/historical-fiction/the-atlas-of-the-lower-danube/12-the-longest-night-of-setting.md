## 12. The Longest Night of Setting

The soldiers upstairs went quiet a little after ten. Not all at once — first the card game broke up with laughter and a chair scraping, then boots on the boards overhead, one pair crossing to the window twice before settling, and finally the long, ragged breathing of men who had drunk enough plum brandy to sleep through a wedding and most of a funeral.

Ivo sat in the dark below them with her hand on the press and listened until she could count their breaths.

Five years she had worked this room in silence, and silence had been a skill like any other, learned the way her father learned letterforms: by repetition, by failure, by doing it again until the hands knew it without the mind. She could move through the shop without a board creaking. She could set type by touch. She had believed those skills were armor.

Tonight they were weapons, and the difference made her sick.

"You're stalling," Mara said from the doorway. She had come in an hour past midnight through the cellar door with a shawl over her hair and flour still on her wrists, and she had not asked a single question since. "Or you're praying. With your family it was always hard to tell."

"My father prayed with his hands too." Ivo struck a match and cupped it, and the candle took, and the shop leapt out of the dark — the cases, the stone floor, the press her grandfather had bought secondhand from a bankrupt printer in Ruse, the paper loft above where a man had died holding a satchel of the dead. "That doesn't mean he expected answers."

"What do you expect?"

"That we'll be caught." She said it plainly because anything else would have been a lie between them, and whatever she and Mara were now — conspirators, or something without a name yet — they had agreed three nights ago that lies were done. "Not tonight, maybe. But eventually. And when we are caught, everyone whose name is in these pages will pay for mine."

Mara set down the basket she'd brought. Bread, wrapped in cloth. A jug. Two more candles, stubs saved from the church, which meant someone else knew and had chosen to know. "Then we print fast," she said.

Jovan came last, at half past twelve, the way he'd promised — through the alley, whistling nothing, walking like a boy going nowhere. He was fourteen and thin as a fishbone and he had been watching the bridge for two weeks without anyone asking him to, and when he slipped inside he looked around the shop with the expression of a boy entering a church of a denomination he'd only heard described.

"This is where the proclamation gets printed," he whispered. "The governor's one. The one that says everything is fine."

"It is," Ivo said.

"Can I see how?"

She almost said no. Then she thought: five years of being nobody's business, of being useful and invisible, and what had it kept alive except the invisibility? Her father had taught apprentices. She could teach a boy to pull a lever.

"Come here," she said. "Hold the candle. If the flame leans toward the press, hold it further back."

* * *

They began with page one, because there was no sense beginning anywhere else.

The forme waited on the stone where she'd locked it up at dusk — eight columns of testimony from the village of Novo Selo, set in her father's smaller pica because the larger type would have made the atlas too many pages to hide. Names. Dates. What was seen, what was heard, what was carried out of the burning houses. She had read each line so many times setting it that the words had stopped being words and become weights, and lifting the forme onto the bed of the press felt like lifting a coffin lid.

"Ink," she said, and Jovan handed her the ball.

The leather was stiff; she'd dressed it herself with goose skin and wool. She spread ink across the slab in strokes she could have made blind — crosswise, lengthwise, crosswise again — and pulled the ink balls over the forme, listening over the sound of her own work for any change in the breathing upstairs. Nothing. A man coughed. Someone turned over. The house held its ordinary noises close, like a mother pretending to sleep.

"First pull," she said. "Watch."

She laid the dampened sheet on the tympan, folded the frisket over it, wound the bar. The press spoke its old language — the rumble of the carriage, the bite of the platen — and then the silence after, which was always the same silence, the pause where the world decided whether the impression was true.

She unwound the bar and lifted the sheet.

Eight columns of black on white. *Testimony of Petar Vlašić, farmer, regarding the events of the twelfth of October.* Below it, in the same small pica, the sentence she had argued with herself about for two nights and finally set exactly as written: *This account is printed in Vidin, on the Danube, in the year 1875.*

"Is that wise?" Mara asked quietly. "Putting where."

"If it has no place, the Governor's historians will say it was printed in Belgrade, by agitators, by foreigners." Ivo hung the sheet on the drying line strung between the cases, where it turned slowly in the candle draft like something alive. "It has to be born here. It has to have a mother. Otherwise they'll take even its birth away."

Jovan was staring at the page. At the names. She watched him find the one he was looking for — his uncle's village, his uncle's neighbor, some name from the winter stories — and watched his face do what faces did: nothing, and then everything.

"They printed it wrong," he said. "In the gazette. They said bandits."

"I know."

"My mother cried anyway. Even believing them." He looked up. "If they read this —"

"Then they'll cry for the right reason," Mara said, "and they'll know why."

* * *

By two o'clock they had a rhythm, and the rhythm was a kind of grace.

Mara dampened and stacked paper. Jovan hung sheets, forty to a line, moving along the cord with the concentration of a boy defusing something. Ivo pulled the press. Two hundred impressions to the forme, she'd decided — enough to seed every market town on both banks, not so many that the type would wear or the paper run short before the last pages. Two hundred of page one. Then break the forme, distribute the type back into the case, letter by letter, and set page two.

It was the distributing that nearly broke her.

Composing was creation, even of this kind — you built the sentence, you locked it up, it stood. Distributing was unmaking. Each letter had to go back to its own compartment in its own case, and her fingers knew the way the way they knew prayer, b in its row, e in its row, and somewhere around the fourth line of the casualty column her hands were sorting letters that spelled the names of people whose houses had burned, putting each dead man's name neatly away into its little wooden box, and she had to stop and stand with her palms flat on the cold stone and breathe.

Mara came and stood beside her. She didn't touch her. That was the right instinct; Ivo would have shattered under a hand just then.

"When my Milan died," Mara said, "the priest wanted to talk about God's plan. You know what helped? Folding laundry. His shirts. Because the shirts were his, and folding them was a thing I could do correctly." She nodded at the case. "Put the letters away, Ivo. It counts."

So Ivo put the letters away, and it counted, and when the case was clean she set the first line of page two with steady hands: *Testimony of Anka Marić, ferryman's daughter, regarding the river road.*

* * *

The trouble came at four.

She heard it before she understood it — a change in the breathing above, a bed frame, boots. One of the soldiers was awake. She killed the nearest candle with a pinch and froze, one hand full of type, and Jovan blew out his without being told, and the three of them stood in the dark like figures in a photograph, waiting.

Boots crossed the ceiling. Paused. Crossed back. The privy door at the end of the hall opened and closed, and the boots went away, and the house exhaled.

"He'll hear the press," Jovan whispered. "When you start again."

"He slept through the first hundred pulls," Ivo said. But her heart was going like a trip-hammer, and she knew the boy was right. Drunk sleep was deep but shallow-edged; the press's rumble was a strange animal in a strange stable. "We muffle it. Straw under the feet of the press. Blankets over the carriage. It will cost us speed."

It cost them nearly everything. Muffled, the press ran slow and hot, and the pulls came harder, and the candles burned down, and by the time page three was distributed and page four locked up, Ivo's arms shook and her shoulders had seized into a knot she could feel in her teeth. Mara cut bread and they ate standing, silently, in the smell of ink and oil, passing the loaf like a communion neither of them named.

"Tell me something," Mara said, halfway through. "Your father. Did he know this would be yours?"

Ivo considered lying, found she couldn't remember how. "He used to say the press was a loom. That towns wove themselves, and someone had to keep the pattern." She looked at the drying lines — sheets one, two, three, turning in the draft, two hundred of each, six hundred testimonies hanging in her shop like laundry, like shirts worth folding. "I thought it was just talk. Printer's talk. He talked to avoid things."

"And now?"

"Now I think he was leaving me instructions the only way he could. In plain sight." She swallowed the last of the bread. "He hid the forbidden type where I found the satchel. He drew the maps. He collected what he collected knowing what it might cost. He wasn't avoiding anything. He was preparing me, and I was too busy being invisible to notice."

Jovan, who had been dozing sitting up against the paper stack, opened his eyes. "My mother says you're the one who fixed the church notices. When the letters fell off. Everyone thought the sacristan did it."

"The sacristan can't tell a pica from a cicero."

"So it was you."

"It was me." Saying it aloud felt like setting her own name in type — dangerous, irreversible, hers. "It was me for five years. Everything in this town that got printed correctly, I printed. And nobody knew."

"We knew," Mara said. "We just didn't know we knew. There's a difference, and I think it mattered. People trusted the notices because they felt honest. That was you, even unnamed." She brushed flour from her skirt. "Names are only the last coat of paint, child. The wall was yours long ago."

* * *

Page five was the maps.

Her father's maps, redrawn by her own hand into blocks she could print — the villages along the south bank, the fords, the roads the soldiers had taken in October, each annotated in his cramped upright script with dates and numbers. Reproducing his hand had taken her three nights of cutting transfer paper and tracing by candlelight, and seeing the first impression come off the press she had to look away, because for one whole second it was his hand on the page, his ink, his certainty that these places deserved to exist precisely.

"Pull another," Mara said gently. "He'd want to see it run."

Ivo pulled another. And another. Somewhere around the fortieth her grief and her purpose fused into a single metal, and she stopped being able to tell them apart, and that was easier.

* * *

Dawn came gray through the shutters at twenty past six, and with it the arithmetic.

Nine formes set, printed, distributed. Eighteen hundred sheets drying on lines strung across every wall, doubled up now, close enough to offset if the air went warm. The soldiers upstairs would wake within the hour; the sergeant demanded coffee by seven and roll call by eight, and roll call happened in the yard, which meant boots on the stairs, which meant the shop must look like a shop.

"We fold the lines," Ivo said. "Stack the sheets flat in the paper loft, under the blank stock. Jovan — the straw, take it out with the ashes. Mara —"

"I know what a shop looks like," Mara said. "I've kept one thirty years. Go wash your face; you've ink on your chin and guilt on your forehead and both are visible."

Ivo laughed — actually laughed, a cracked, exhausted sound she didn't recognize as hers — and went to the basin, and while the cold water ran she heard them working behind her, Mara stacking with a warehousewoman's economy, Jovan whispering the alphabet to himself as he put away the last of the type, *a, b, c*, each letter home in its box.

Her father had died alone in this room. She understood now that he hadn't intended it to stay that way.

* * *

The knock came at seven sharp, and it was not the sergeant's knock.

Two knocks, a pause, one more — the rhythm of a clerk, precise, apologetic, deliberate. Emil's rhythm. Through the shutter crack Ivo watched him in the yard below, papers under his arm, waiting while the soldiers formed their crooked morning line, and he did not look up at the shop, not once, which was itself a kind of message.

She let him wait a full minute. Then she opened the door.

"Census follow-up," he said loudly, for the benefit of the yard. "Household verification. The governor requires signatures." And then, quieter, stepping inside, taking in the clean cases, the empty lines, the faint sweet reek of fresh ink that no amount of airing would hide by eight o'clock: "You printed last night."

It was not a question, and she was too tired to make it one.

"Sixteen pages are dry," she said. "Six remain."

Emil stood very still. She watched him weigh it — report, warn, help — the same scales she had watched him carry since the day he'd first noticed her father's kerning habits in an official proclamation and frowned like a man finding his own handwriting in a stranger's letter. Whatever moved behind his face, it moved slowly and arrived somewhere final.

"The historian from the capital arrives Thursday," he said. "He'll want the shop's records. Every job book since 'sixty-nine." He set a folded paper on the stone counter, squared its edges with two fingers, and left it there. "You should know what he's bringing with him. And you should burn your job books tonight."

"Emil."

"Don't thank me." His voice didn't rise, but something in it bent. "Just finish faster than they can burn you."

At the door he paused, looking back at the press — at her father's press — with the expression of a man visiting a grave he'd never been permitted to mourn at. Then he straightened his collar, raised his voice to its official register, announced to the yard that the household was in order, and walked back into the morning.

Ivo unfolded the paper he'd left.

It was a list. Names, ages, villages. Written in the clerk's neat administrative hand, numbered in a column, sixty-one entries — and at the top, in the same hand, a single heading that the official account would never contain:

*Confirmed dead, October actions, as counted by those who buried them.*

She read it once. Then she carried it to the composing stone, took down the largest body type in the case — her father's display roman, the letters he'd hidden and she had kept — and began to set the first name.

Sixteen pages had been enough for the living. This would be the page for the others, and it would go last, and it would go in the biggest type she owned.
