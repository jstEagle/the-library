## 13. Snow on the Bridge

The river froze on a Thursday, and by Sunday men were walking on it.

Mara stood at her kitchen window with a cup of chicory going cold in her hands and watched them cross — first a boy sliding on his heels, then two soldiers from the garrison, then a whole procession of townsfolk testing the ice like it was a new floor laid over the water. The Danube, which had taken her husband's boat and eleven years of her life, lay flat and white as an unmarked page. She hated it for that. A frozen river was a river that had agreed to be read.

She thought: this is bad. She thought: they can walk to us now.

Behind her, under the loose flagstone where the cold cellar met the old well shaft, forty printed pages of Ivo's atlas lay wrapped in oilcloth inside a flour barrel. Mara had moved them there herself, three nights ago, carrying them two at a time through her own house as though each sheet might cry out. Her back still ached from kneeling on stone. She was fifty-one years old and she had spent the winter becoming a smuggler of paper, which was not what she'd imagined for the widow of Anton the ferryman, who had died dry and respectable with his boots on.

The knock came mid-morning. Not the garrison's knock — theirs was a fist, repeated, indifferent. This was knuckles twice and a pause, which meant Stefan's boy or the priest's widow or someone else from the quiet country of people who knew.

It was Stefan himself, flour to his elbows despite the hour, his face wearing the particular gray of a man who has done arithmetic he doesn't like.

"You've heard," he said.

"About the ice?"

"About the census." He stepped inside without being asked, which was unlike him, and stood in her kitchen dripping snow off his boots onto her clean floor. "They're doing houses again. Not the count they did in autumn — that was heads and names. This one is stores. Grain, salt, oil, paper." He said *paper* the way you'd say a word that had been sitting in your mouth too long. "A clerk came into the bakery at dawn. Wrote down my sacks. Asked why a town of four hundred souls needed nine barrels more flour than last year."

"What did you tell him?"

"That bread rises. That flour goes light in the damp." He almost smiled; it didn't take. "He wrote it down. They write everything now, Mara. That's what frightens me. Not the questions — the writing."

Mara set her cup down. Through the window she could see the patrol on the ice again, four of them this time, walking the middle of the river with the unhurried gait of men inspecting a road they intended to keep. One stopped, stamped his boot, listened to the ice. She found herself holding her breath across all that distance, as if sound carried over frozen water better than over open air.

"They'll find the pages," she said. It wasn't a question. It was the thing she had been saying to herself since Thursday.

"Not in your cellar."

"In anyone's cellar, given time."

Stefan looked at her then, fully, and she saw that he had come here not to trade warnings but to make an offer, and that the offer was already made in his own mind, and that her part was only to refuse it properly.

"No," she said.

"You don't know what I—"

"I know exactly what you're going to say, and no. We didn't build this so one man could stand in front of it like a wall. Walls get knocked down, Stefan. Networks bend."

"Networks break," he said. "I've watched dough break. It's the same principle. You work it past its strength and it tears along whichever line is weakest." He wiped his hands on his apron, leaving two pale ghosts of flour. "I'm the weakest line, Mara. I'm the man whose ledgers already have lies in them. Forged seals, shifted weights, five years of small kindnesses written up as honest trade. If they come for anyone, let them come for a man who's guilty of something anyway."

"That's not—"

"It's exactly how guilt works here. You think the governor cares whether we're innocent? He cares whether the story closes neatly. Give him one baker with false seals and he'll close the book and go home to his supper." He said it lightly, but his hands were flat on her table and very still. "Ivo would never allow it. So I'm telling you instead, because you're the one who carries things between us. Tell her the flour barrel moves tonight. And tell her—" He stopped. Started again. "Tell her I don't regret a crumb of it."

* * *

The barrel moved that night, but only as far as the ice cellar behind the church, because there was nowhere farther. Jovan hauled it on a child's sled while Mara walked ten paces ahead with a lantern held high and ostentatiously visible, a widow going to visit her husband's grave, nothing in the world simpler than that. The boy was fourteen and had learned to move like smoke; Mara had watched him all winter and still couldn't describe how he did it. He simply never seemed to be where a soldier would look, the way a fish is never where the heron strikes.

The church ice cellar was a trench dug into the north bank, roofed with planks and drifted snow, where the parish stored ice through summer for the fevered and the dead. Nobody visited in February. The pages went beneath the ice blocks themselves, in a waxed crate, wedged where only a hand that knew would reach.

"There," Jovan said, patting the refrozen crust with his mitt. "Even God would have to dig."

"Don't joke."

"I'm not joking. It's a compliment to the engineering." He grinned at her, cheeks red as apples, and for one moment he was just a boy playing at danger in the dark, and Mara's chest hurt with tenderness and terror in equal parts. Then his grin faded and he became fourteen going on forty again, which was worse. "Teta Mara. If they take Stefan — what do we do? Do we run?"

"We don't run. Running is a confession."

"And staying?"

"Staying is a choice," she said. "There's a difference. Come on. Your mother will have my head."

* * *

They came for the seal on the ninth day of the freeze.

Mara heard it before she understood it — boots in the street, many, moving not at patrol pace but at purpose pace — and was out her door with everyone else in the lane, drawn the way the whole town was always drawn now, standing in doorways with shawls pulled close, watching the garrison do whatever the garrison had come to do. In Vidin that winter, watching was a civic duty. You watched so the ones being watched knew they weren't alone, even when looking away was safer. Especially then.

Two soldiers stood outside the bakery. A third was inside; she could hear him through the open door, the clatter of tins, the flat voice reading numbers aloud. Stefan stood in the street beside his wife, who was holding their youngest daughter against her hip with the fierce casualness of a woman determined to make an arrest look boring. The clerk — not Emil, a younger one, a man with a copybook face — held up a sack of flour and turned it in the light.

"This seal," he said, "is the governor's provisioning mark. This seal is used on stores bound for the garrison kitchen. Explain its presence in private stock."

"The garrison sells back its surplus," Stefan said. "Openly. Ask your quartermaster."

"The quartermaster's ledger shows no such sales."

"Then your quartermaster should be standing where I am."

Someone in the crowd made a sound — half laugh, strangled at birth. The young clerk's ears went pink. Mara felt the whole street lean, invisible, collective, the way wheat leans before wind.

"You are Stefan Kostadinov, baker, of this parish?"

"I am."

"You will accompany us to the citadel for questioning regarding irregularities of provision."

Stefan nodded slowly, and then — and Mara would replay this moment for the rest of her life — he turned, deliberately, and looked down the lane. Not at his wife. Not at his daughters. Down the lane, toward the print shop, where a curtain twitched once and went still. Toward the direction of the flour barrel, the ice cellar, the whole buried architecture of the winter. He looked there for exactly one second, the way a ferryman checks the current before pushing off, and then he faced forward again and put his hands out for the rope.

They didn't use a rope. They walked him. But Mara saw the gesture, and she understood it, and she knew — with the sick certainty of a woman who has read rivers her whole life — that Stefan had chosen his crossing. He had seen the patrol coming toward the cellar route days ago, or suspected the seal was discovered, or simply done the arithmetic every man in the network had been quietly doing since December, and he had positioned himself along the weakest line on purpose.

His wife did not cry. His youngest daughter did. Stefan kissed the top of the girl's head and said, loudly, in the voice he used for customers: "Mind the ovens, little one. Bread waits for no magistrate."

Then the lane swallowed him, boots and gray coats and all, and Vidin stood in its doorways and watched him go, and nobody said a word, and the silence was the loudest thing Mara had ever heard.

* * *

Ivo came to her house after dark. She came the back way, through the gap between the tannery fences that only children and the desperate used, and when Mara opened the door the printer's face was the color of the ice — white and bearing weight.

"He did it for me."

"He did it for all of us."

"For me." Ivo stepped inside and stood with her back to the closed door, hat still on, snow melting in her collar, a woman forgetting to be invisible for the first time in five years and not knowing what to do with the exposure. "The seal on those sacks — Mara, I know those sacks. I printed the requisition forms that bore that mark, two winters ago, for Emil. Stefan kept them because I asked him to keep anything with our typeface on it, anything traceable, anything that could lead back to the shop. Those seals were mine. My carelessness. And he—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, Mara could see her hating it. "He stood in the street and took it."

"Yes."

"You said *yes* to me just now like it's simple."

"It isn't simple. It's true." Mara took the wet hat off Ivo's head and hung it by the stove, because somebody had to do the ordinary thing or the room would fill up with catastrophe. "Sit down. Drink this. Then listen to me, because I'm older than you and I've buried a husband and I get to say hard things."

Ivo sat.

"Stefan chose this the way a ferryman chooses the crossing point. Not because drowning is fine. Because some cargo must reach the far bank, and he judged himself worth less than the cargo." Mara set the cup in her hands and pressed the girl's fingers around it until she gripped. "You can honor that or waste it. Those are the options. Grief that stops the press wastes it. Grief that prints faster honors it. Do you understand me?"

For a long moment Ivo stared into the chicory like it might show her something. Outside, the wind came down the river valley and pressed at the shutters, and somewhere under all that ice the Danube went on moving, because it always did, because frozen was only ever a surface condition.

"He'll be questioned," Ivo said finally. "They'll ask him who helped him move the surplus. Who bought it. Where it went."

"Yes."

"He won't give names."

"No. But they don't need names, Ivo. They need a story. And right now their story is *bandits, smugglers, sedition*, and a baker with false seals fits it beautifully. The only thing that doesn't fit—" Mara crouched so their eyes were level, and she said the next words slowly, so there could be no mistaking them. "The only thing that doesn't fit their story is a finished atlas. Forty pages, nearly done, waiting under church ice. If the governor finds that, Stefan's sacrifice buys nothing. If the river opens before they search the cellars, it buys everything. It's a race now. Us against the thaw, and the thaw is winning."

Ivo looked up. Something had changed in her face — not steadiness exactly, but direction, the way a compass needle stops trembling once you stop arguing with it.

"How many days to finish the final signature?" Mara asked.

"With the new type Emil left me — four. Five if the candle supply holds."

"And after that?"

"After that," Ivo said, "we wait for the ice to crack. And when it does, everything we are goes into the current at once." She drank the chicory down cold, all of it, like medicine. "Tell Jovan I need him watching the bridge. Every hour. The day the patrols start crossing heavier than usual — that means the searches are coming. I want to know before they know themselves."

"He's already watching. He hasn't slept properly in a week."

"Neither have any of us." Ivo rose, took her wet hat back down, and paused at the door with her hand on the latch. "Mara. When this is over — whatever *over* looks like — I want to bake bread. Ordinary bread, in his shop, for ordinary mornings. Will that be possible? Will any of that be possible?"

Mara thought of Stefan's hands flat on her table, flour ghosting off them. Thought of the lane full of silent watchers. Thought of the river, patient under its lid of white.

"When the ice goes out," she said, "anything is possible. That's the terrible thing about it."

Ivo nodded once and slipped out through the tannery gap, and Mara stood a long while with her hand still raised in a blessing neither of them had named, listening to the wind work at the river, trying to hear — beneath the silence, beneath the freeze — the first faint sound of water deciding to move.
