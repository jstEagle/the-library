## 14. The Governor's Historian

The historian arrived on a Tuesday, in a carriage with mud to its axles and a driver who looked like he'd been hired that morning and regretted it already. Emil watched from the garrison office window as the man descended — small, round-shouldered, wearing a coat too fine for the road and an expression of a man who had been promised something better than this.

"Osman Ferid Bey," the clerk at the door announced, "historiographer of the province, from the capital."

Emil knew the name before the clerk said it. He had written it himself, three weeks ago, in the governor's correspondence: *the capital requires a definitive account before spring. Send someone reliable.*

Reliable, Emil had learned, meant a man with debts.

* * *

Ferid Bey set up in the best room of the konak, the one with the stove that actually drew, and asked for everything. Registers, correspondence, patrol reports, requisition ledgers, the punishment books going back six years. He said it the way a man asks for bread — without appetite, as routine.

"You understand," he said to Emil, who had been assigned to assist him, "that history is not what happened. History is what can be defended."

Emil stood at parade rest and said, "Yes, Bey."

"You wrote most of these reports yourself." The historian tapped the stack with one finger. "Your hand is all through them. That is convenient."

"Is it?"

"It means you know where the seams are." Ferid Bey looked up then, and his eyes were tired and quick, not stupid. "I am not here to find seams, Clerk. I am here to make sure no one else does. There is a difference, and your career depends on knowing it."

Emil said nothing. It was the safest sentence he owned.

* * *

Three days of sorting. The records came into the good room in armloads and went out again in categories: *to be copied*, *to be summarized*, *to be held*. Emil carried them, catalogued them, and read every page twice — once for the historian, once for himself.

He had always known what was in the punishment books. He had entered half of it himself. But reading it as a body — village by village, date by date, the same week repeated across eleven names along the river like a sentence running downhill — was different from knowing it in pieces. In pieces, each entry was an incident. Together, they were a season.

*Kula. Oreshets. Novo Selo.* The villages his own reports called *infestations cleared*. The word *cleared* appeared forty-one times in the autumn ledger. He counted. He could not stop counting.

On the fourth day, Ferid Bey summoned him at dusk.

"The casualty figures," the historian said. "The ones in the final draft. They are wrong."

Emil kept his face still. "Bey?"

"They are too high." Ferid Bey pushed a page across the table. The governor's draft, in the governor's own imperious hand: *nineteen bandits killed, twelve wounded, two captured.* "Nineteen. I have read your patrol reports. Your quartermaster's ammunition expenditure alone argues for more than nineteen. A man does not burn through four hundred cartridges killing nineteen people, unless your soldiers cannot shoot."

"No, Bey."

"So. Where is the true list?"

And there it was, laid open between them like a wound neither would touch: the fact that there was a true list, and that it existed somewhere in the paper ocean Ferid Bey had spent four days drowning in.

"I compiled the returns as they were given to me," Emil said carefully.

"Yes. And the returns were falsified by officers protecting their commissions, and the falsifications were compounded by clerks—" he glanced up, mild as milk "—who are careful men. So somewhere beneath nineteen is the real number, and my task is to bury it deeper, not dig it up. Do you follow me?"

"I follow you, Bey."

"Good." Ferid Bey rubbed his eyes. He looked, suddenly, very old and very far from home. "In Constantinople I edited chronicles. Nice work. Dates, genealogies, the usual lies at a comfortable distance. Then my brother-in-law's business failed, and now I am here, making the dead agree with the living." He stood, straightened his coat. "Tomorrow we do the registers. Household by household. The governor wants the town's papers clean before spring, whatever that means. Get some sleep."

* * *

Emil did not sleep. He walked back to the office he shared with two other clerks and sat in the dark with the lamp unlit, thinking about the shape of a theft.

Because that was what it was now, wasn't it? He had been circling it for weeks — the corrected page left in Ivo Delacroix's type case, the small treasons, the line in the census he'd falsified with a steady hand and a sick stomach. But those were adjustments, nudges, a man leaning against a door. What Ferid Bey had just described — the true list, buried under nineteen — was a thing with weight and edges. A thing that could be lifted.

If it existed. If it hadn't already gone into the *to be burned* pile, or worse, the *to be copied and the original destroyed* pile, which Ferid Bey maintained with a clerk's own thoroughness and which sat in the locked cabinet behind the historian's chair.

Emil had made that cabinet's inventory himself. He knew the lock was English, cheap, and opened to any key ground down thin enough. He knew the guard changed at nine and the corridor lamp guttered around midnight when the oil ran low, because he was the one who filled the lamps and he'd begun filling them a little less.

He also knew that if he was caught, the governor would not need a trial. Men had vanished from Vidin for less than a misfiled page. And it would not only be him. His mind went, traitorously, to the print shop — to the girl who set type like breathing and spoke maybe forty words a week, to her father's ghost standing over her shoulders, to the satchel he was now nearly certain she had. If Emil fell, the first knock on the shop door would come within the hour.

So don't fall, he told himself. Be exact.

* * *

The registers took two more days. Households, heads of families, trades, livestock, papers inspected — the whole town laid out in columns for the historian's red pen. Emil transcribed while Ferid Bey interrogated. He watched the man work and understood him better than he wanted to: Ferid Bey was not cruel. Cruelty would have been easier. He was a professional doing erasure the way a mason lays brick, competently, without hate, and that competence frightened Emil more than any zealot ever had. Zealots could be shamed. Craftsmen could only be outworked.

On the sixth day, the historian found it.

"Delacroix." He said the name flatly, tapping the register. "Type founder. Dead five years. Daughter keeps the shop." He looked up. "You've been out there. Census duty."

"I have, Bey."

"And?"

"And she is quiet, competent, and pays her fees."

"Hm." Ferid Bey made a note. Emil did not see what it said. That night he lay awake composing contingency after contingency — if the historian visits the shop, if he orders the press inspected, if he connects the courier, if, if, if — until dawn came gray through the shutters and he got up with his decision already made for him, the way decisions do when you've been making them in your sleep for weeks.

The true list had to leave the building before the final draft did. After that, it would be locked in the dispatch case and gone to the capital, and memory would be a matter of what nineteen meant.

* * *

The chance came sooner than he'd planned, which should have warned him.

On the eighth evening Ferid Bey was summoned to dine with the governor — a summons delivered with such relish by the adjutant that everyone understood the governor wanted to boast at him about the new bridge timbers, or the road survey, or possibly his wine. The historian went, resigned, taking his coat and his appetite and leaving instructions: *"Finish the summary pages. Lock the cabinet. I'll sign off in the morning."*

Lock the cabinet. As if Emil needed telling.

He waited until the garrison settled into its evening rhythms — mess noise, boot heels on the stair, the guard changing at nine with the scrape of the yard gate. Then he took the thin key he had ground from a bit of clock spring, wrapped in cloth so it wouldn't sing against metal, and walked down the corridor to the good room with a stack of summary pages under his arm, because a man carrying paper is invisible in a records office; a man carrying nothing is a question.

The lamp guttered right on schedule. The guard's footsteps passed, faded, turned at the far end. Emil knelt at the cabinet, and his hands — which had shaken falsifying one line in a census — were perfectly still now, and he noted that about himself with a kind of horror: fear didn't paralyze him anymore. Fear had become fuel. Somewhere in the last months he had become a different instrument, and he did not know yet whether the new instrument was better or merely sharper.

The lock gave with a click no louder than a type falling into a stick.

The *held* folder was third from the top, exactly where he'd filed it, because he had filed it where he could find it in the dark. Inside: the raw burial reports from Kula and the river villages — the ones that had never matched the patrol tallies, the ones the district officers had submitted honestly before being told what honesty cost. Names. Ages. Some entries were just *male, approx. 40, unidentified* and some were heartbreakingly specific: *Milen Petrov, ferryman, and son, 9.* Forty-one times *cleared*, and underneath it, in the honest ink of men who hadn't yet learned to lie: two hundred and thirty-one.

Two hundred and thirty-one. Against nineteen.

Emil crouched in the lamplight and did not move for a long moment. This was the arithmetic the atlas needed — the number that would make the testimonies undeniable, the number that turned grief into record. Ivo could set it in type smaller than a fingernail and it would still weigh more than the governor's entire final draft.

He couldn't take the originals. Missing originals meant a search, and a search meant the shop, and the shop meant everything ended in fire before spring. Instead he did the only thing his craft allowed: he copied. Fast, exact, in his smallest clerk's hand, compressing two hundred and thirty-one lines into eight crowded pages — names, villages, dates, causes where causes were given, the officers' signatures where signatures existed, because signatures were proof and proof was the whole war. His pen scratched. Sweat ran cold down his spine. Twice the guard passed; both times Emil bent lower over the summary pages stacked on the desk, a clerk working late, dull as furniture.

It took him until past midnight. When the last name was down — *Stoyan, shepherd, 60s, Kula* — he blotted the pages, folded them into a oilskin packet the size of a prayer book, and slid it into the false bottom of his document case, beneath a year's worth of genuinely boring requisitions.

Then he relocked the cabinet, trimmed the lamp, and stood a moment in the empty room looking at the shelves of paper that held a town entire — every debt, every birth, every death, every cleared village — and understood that he was looking at the other side's arsenal. Ferid Bey would return tomorrow and sign the final draft, and nineteen would go to the capital in a sealed case, and it would be cited, taught, believed. Unless the eight pages in Emil's case met the satchel in the loft above a print shop, and the two of them together became something the capital could not recall.

He put out the lamp and walked back through the sleeping garrison with his heart going like a press run.

* * *

He almost went straight to the shop. It was the obvious move and therefore, he told himself, the wrong one — a clerk seen crossing town at one in the morning toward the house of a woman he'd falsified a census line for was a confession with legs. The packet could wait one day. One day, delivered openly, in daylight, with a pretext: proofreading the official proclamation, returning borrowed type, anything a neighbor might believe and a watcher might discount.

But the next day, the historian did not sign off.

"The governor wishes revisions," Ferid Bey announced, gray-faced, dropping the draft on the table. "Again. He has discovered enthusiasm for detail." He looked at Emil with something almost like apology. "We will be here another week. Perhaps two."

A week. Two. Every day the list sat in Emil's document case among the requisitions, it grew heavier; every day the ice on the river thickened, and the window in which paper could cross it narrowed toward spring, and spring, everyone now said, would bring the revolution the way thaw brings flood. He thought of Stefan the baker's fraying forged ledgers, of Jovan watching the bridge, of Mara's steady eyes, of Ivo setting type in a shop that soldiers might quarter in at any order — and he understood that he was no longer choosing whether to betray the garrison. That choice had been made somewhere back in chaptered silence, in a corrected page slipped into a type case. Now he was only choosing speed.

That evening he closed the office early, took the long way through the market square, and turned down the street to the print shop with the oilskin packet against his ribs like a second heartbeat. Smoke rose from the shop chimney. Through the frost-feathered window he could see lamplight moving, the shadow of a woman bending over a frame of type, precise as a needle.

He raised his hand to knock — and stopped.

Because the door was already ajar, and from inside, low and urgent, came Mara's voice: *"—soldiers at the bakery. Three of them. They're reading his ledgers, Ivo. Right now, on his own counter."*

Emil stood in the cold with the true list pressed to his chest, and understood that time had just run out for somebody — and that he was about to find out whether it had run out for all of them.
