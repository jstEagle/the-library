## 15. Stefan's Empty Chair

The bakery on Kozloduy Street had gone quiet the way a struck bell goes quiet — not silent, exactly, but holding its sound somewhere you couldn't reach it. I stood across the street in the gray of late February and looked at the windows, which were papered over from the inside now, and at the door, where a garrison seal hung on a leather strap like a medal awarded to no one.

Three days since they'd taken Stefan. Three days since Mara had come to the shop with flour still on her knuckles and told me in four words what had happened, because four words was all she trusted herself to say: *They took him. Ledgers.*

I crossed the street before I could argue myself out of it.

His wife, Ana, opened the door before I knocked. She'd seen me coming, then. Or she'd been standing at the door for three days waiting for anyone at all.

"You shouldn't be here," she said, but she stepped back to let me in, which is the whole grammar of this town in one gesture.

Inside, the bakery smelled wrong. Not of bread — of cold ash, of a fire raked out and left. The ovens stood dark. Flour dust lay on everything undisturbed, a fine gray snowfall over the tables, the scales, the long wooden peel leaning by the wall. It looked like a room that had died mid-sentence.

Ana sat me at the table where I'd eaten bread with Stefan a hundred times without ever once saying anything that mattered. There were four chairs. One of them was empty, and none of us looked at it, which meant all of us were looking at it.

"His brother came," Ana said. "From Lom. He wants them to go — me and the children, back to Lom, until it's over."

"And you?"

She turned her wedding ring around her finger, a slow rotation, like something being wound. "Stefan's bread fed half the garrison. Do you know what he said when I asked him once why he never shorted their measure, not even in the bad winter? He said, *Because they count.*" A small, bitter laugh. "He counted too. He knew exactly how many loaves went out the back door to whom. He kept it all in his head and let the ledgers lie beautifully, and he always said the day they learned arithmetic would be his last."

"He was careful."

"He was proud." She said it without anger. "There's a difference, and he paid for it."

A child moved upstairs — a floorboard, nothing more. Ana's eyes went up and came back down. "My daughter asks when Papa is baking again. I've told her he went to Ruse to buy sugar. She believes me. She's seven; believing me is what she does." Her hands flattened on the table. "Ivo. Why are you here?"

Not *what do you want*. Why are you here. Ana had known me for years as the print shop's quiet daughter, the one who took payment in bread and spoke in measurements. But the network had a shape now, and everyone in it could feel the shape changing.

"I wanted to know if there was anything—"

"Don't lie to me in my own kitchen." Gentle, though. Almost kind. "You're printing something. Jovan carries your pages under his coat like they're warm from an oven, and Mara has stopped singing at the ferry landing, and Emil Vukov walked past this house twice yesterday looking at the seal on my door like it was a sentence he'd written himself. I'm a baker's wife, not a fool. Something is going into the world with all our names near it, and my husband is sitting in a cell because he was the loudest piece of it."

I said nothing. The empty chair said it for me.

"I'm not asking you to stop," Ana said. "God forgive me, I'm not. If they'd taken him for nothing I'd curse you all into the river. But he'd have hated to die for a thing that failed. So don't fail." She stood, went to the dead oven, and came back with a cloth bundle she pressed into my hands — bread, still faintly warm, baked on some smuggled ember or in a neighbor's kitchen, defiance in its own small alphabet. "For you. And tell Mara—" she stopped, started again. "Tell her the recipe for the walnut rolls is written down now. In case."

*In case.* We passed each other's deaths around this town like condiments.

At the door she caught my sleeve. "One more thing. The officer who took him — the young one, the polite one — he searched the house himself. He found nothing. He stood right there"—she pointed at the spot beside the flour bin—"and he found nothing, and then he apologized to me. Apologized! Like a guest who'd broken a cup." Her voice dropped. "Ivo, he knew there was nothing to find. Someone told him where to look and someone else made sure it was clean. Think about what that means about who your friends are."

I carried the bread home through streets that had begun to smell differently — not of spring, not yet, but of thaw, of wet stone and open drains, the river exhaling after months of holding its breath.

* * *

Jovan was waiting in the paper loft, sitting cross-legged among the stacked reams like he owned them, which in a way he did now; he'd been in and out of the shop so often these past weeks that the cat had accepted him and the cat accepts no one.

"You went to see her." Not a question. He was fourteen and had recently discovered that questions were optional when observation would do.

"Her daughter thinks Stefan is buying sugar in Ruse."

"That's kinder than the truth."

"The truth is kinder than the truth right now," I said. "The truth is he's in the cellar of the konak with a fever, according to Mara's cousin who delivers to the guards. Kindness is a story we're telling a seven-year-old until we can afford the real one." I set the bread down and looked at him. "Why are you here, Jovan? You're supposed to be at the bridge."

"I was at the bridge." He grinned, and there was something in the grin that stopped my breath, because I recognized it. It was my father's grin, the one he wore when type was set and the press was inked and the world was about to change by exactly one sheet of paper. "Come and see."

We went down through the shop — past the sleeping quarters the soldiers had vacated only that morning, thank God, past the press under its canvas shroud, past the type case where a single corrected page still waited in the K slot like a letter never sent — and out the back, along the lane, down the frozen ramp toward the water.

The Danube at Vidin in late February is not a river. It's a road — a white road wide enough to march an army across, and everyone in town knows it, and every year everyone pretends they don't, the way you pretend not to know a secret that keeps you safe precisely by remaining unspoken. This year the garrison had posted a sentry at each end of the ice road and a notice nailed to the customs post: **CROSSING FORBIDDEN BY ORDER OF THE VALI.**

Jovan didn't go anywhere near the notice. He led me downstream instead, along the bank, past the fisherfolk's winter huts with their smoke holes breathing thin gray threads, until the town was a smudge behind us and the ice stretched ahead of us, enormous, indifferent, white going blue at the edges where the current worked underneath like a prisoner digging with a spoon.

"Look there." He pointed.

At first I saw nothing. Ice. More ice. Then I saw what he was pointing at — a line in the surface, dark as a hair drawn with a pen, running from the far bank toward us, curving, branching, the way cracks branch. And beyond it another. And between them, where two lines met, a patch of ice that wasn't white at all but gray, soaked through, translucent, the color of a page held up against a candle so you can read the next sheet through it.

"Those weren't there eight days ago," Jovan said. "I mark them. Here—" he pulled a stub of pencil and a folded square of paper from inside his coat, opened it, and showed me a drawing. My father would have called it a map and corrected half of it. But the cracks were all there, dated, each line labeled in the boy's careful hand: *Feb 9. Feb 13. Feb 17.* The web thickening from date to date like veins filling with ink.

"You've been charting the ice."

"Somebody has to know things." He said it simply, without boast, and I understood that he had learned this the way all of us had learned it, from watching adults survive. "The fishermen say the river will go out early this year. Big snow in the Carpathians, warm rain in the Iron Gates already. They say when it breaks here it'll break all at once, in a night, with a sound like the sky tearing. Old Mirko remembers it from thirty years ago. He says the cakes of ice come down standing on edge, taller than a man, and whatever's on the river when it happens belongs to the river after."

I crouched at the bank's edge and put my palm flat on the ice. Cold bit through my skin straight into the bone. Underneath, faintly, I could feel it — not movement exactly, but *intention*, the deep water shouldering its ceiling, patient as a creditor.

Days. Not weeks. Maybe five. Maybe fewer.

"Here's what I keep thinking about," Jovan said, crouching beside me. His voice had changed — dropped its boyish bounce, gone flat and serious, the voice of someone doing arithmetic he doesn't like. "You've got the atlas finished. Bundled, wrapped in oilcloth, hidden. Everyone says wait for the ice to go out, put it on a barge, done. But think about what the ice going out actually means. It means the garrison can't be supplied from the bridge anymore. It means the governor knows the town is about to slip loose. It means the first boats from the other side will be *theirs*, soldiers, reinforcements — before any of ours come. And it means—" he hesitated.

"Say it."

"It means the burning. Everyone whispers it. Seditious premises, they'll call it. They'll burn the shop and the mosque's back room and Mara's loft and probably Stefan's bakery for good measure, and they'll do it in the first forty-eight hours of the thaw while the river's full of ice and nobody can cross to argue." He looked at me. "So you don't have weeks, Ivo. You have however long it takes those cracks to meet. That's your whole budget."

The wind came down the river flat and hard, smelling of water and distance, and I thought about what I'd been carrying around for two days without letting it take a shape, because shapes are commitments.

Here is what the shape was: the atlas was done. Two hundred copies, wrapped, hidden in three places. Every name set in type — the villages, the dead, the witnesses, my father's maps, the casualty list Emil stole, and at the foot of the last page, in letters I had cast myself, my own name, which I had not spoken aloud in five years. It existed. It was real. And it was *portable*. A satchel. Two satchels. I could walk out of Vidin tonight, upstream along the Bulgarian bank where the patrols thinned, and be across at Rahova within three days, and deliver it, and be alive.

And Vidin would burn behind me, and Stefan would rot in his cell or worse, and Ana would take her children to Lom and grow old telling them a lie about sugar, and Mara would stand at the ferry landing watching the smoke, and Jovan—

Jovan was watching me watch the ice, and I had the sudden sick certainty that he could see the whole calculation running behind my eyes, could read it the way I read a galley proof, line by line.

"My father used to say," I heard myself begin, and stopped. Five years of not talking about my father, and now he came out of my mouth sideways, unbidden, in the cold wind. "He used to say a printed thing isn't finished when it comes off the press. It's finished when it's in someone's hands. Until then it's just paper wearing a costume."

"So we get it into hands."

"On the other side."

"On the other side," he agreed. Then, quietly, with the terrible directness of the young: "But it matters whose hands *here* find out it got there. If you vanish, they'll say you ran. Stefan's in a cell because of us — Ana said so herself, whether she meant to or not. If the atlas crosses the river and the town burns and nobody here knows it arrived, then the governor wins anyway, because he'll have taught everyone the lesson he wanted: that the truth is a thing people abandon you for."

I stared at him. Fourteen years old. Where did they grow this one; what soil.

"Mara says you're the keeper now," he added, embarrassed suddenly, scuffing his boot at the snow. "That's all. I charted the ice. That's my part. I'm going to keep charting it every morning, and when the cracks start meeting faster than days, I'll bring you the map, and you'll know it's time."

He folded his drawing away inside his coat, next to wherever he carried my pages, and we stood a moment longer looking at the river — at the beautiful, murderous, patient white of it, the road that was also a clock.

Walking back up the lane, I passed the customs post, and the notice flapped on its nail: **CROSSING FORBIDDEN.** Below it, someone — a child, maybe, or a drunk, or neither — had scratched a small mark into the wood with a nail. I looked closer.

It was a printer's fist. The little hand with the pointing finger, the sign my father had carved above the shop door, the sign that meant *look here; read this.*

Someone in Vidin knew. Someone was already reading.

That night I did not sleep. I sat in the loft among the paper with the oilcloth bundles around me like sleeping animals, and I took out Jovan's ice map, and beside it I drew another map — of the town, of the barges and the laundry boats and the coffin trade and the fisherfolk's huts, of every route off this bank that Mara and I had ever whispered about — and I began, in my father's manner, to annotate.

If the river would give me five days, I would spend them like a miser spends gold. Nobody was running anywhere. Not me, not the atlas, not one name on one page.

Outside, far off, the ice groaned — one long note, low as a church organ, the sound of something enormous deciding.

It sounded almost like a press starting up.
