## 16. Confessions at the Press

The knock came at the hour when even the soldiers upstairs had stopped dreaming out loud, three soft raps on the lower shutter, spaced like a man who had rehearsed them and was afraid of his own rehearsal.

I knew the hand before I knew the reason. Only one person in Vidin knocked that way — evenly, apologetically, as though the door were doing him a favor by being there.

Emil Vranov stood in the snow with no lantern and no coat buttoned, his collar open to the cold like a man who had left in a hurry and decided not to correct it. Behind him the street lay blue and empty under the moon, the drifts sculpted against the bakery shutters, the river a black seam beyond the rooftops where the ice groaned its slow nightly argument with itself.

"You shouldn't be here," I said through the crack in the door.

"No," he agreed. "Open it anyway."

I did. That was the first decision of the night, and I made it before I understood I was making one — which is how most of my decisions went, in those years. Mara always said I thought too long and acted too fast, and she was right about both.

He came in sideways, hat in hand, dripping, and stood in the middle of the composing room as if the room were a church and he were unsure of his standing in it. The candle I'd been working by threw his shadow up the wall behind the type cases, taller than him, thinner, a man stretched into something he hadn't meant to become.

Upstairs, one of the soldiers turned over in his sleep. We both listened until the floorboards settled.

"The governor's historian leaves Thursday," Emil said quietly. "He has everything now. The ledgers, the parish rolls, my summaries. By summer there will be a printed account — clean, official, with a map showing nothing where eleven villages used to be." He looked at the press the way other men look at an altar. "And you have been setting pages all winter that will say otherwise. I've known since November."

My hand found the nearest type case without deciding to. Not to use. Just to touch the metal, cold and certain under my fingers, the way you touch a railing over a drop.

"Then why am I not already in the cellar of the konak," I said, "with my hands tied?"

"Because I've been asking myself that same question since November," he said, "and tonight I finally got an answer I can live with. Or die with. They feel similar at this hour."

* * *

He sat where Jovan usually sat, on the upturned crate by the stone sink, and he put a leather folder on his knees and kept both hands flat on top of it, the way you hold down a thing that wants to fly away.

"I was nineteen when your father hired me to sweep the shop," he began. "Do you remember?"

"I remember a boy who inked rollers badly."

"I inked rollers *terribly*. He kept me anyway. He said a press room needed one person who would do the boring work forever without ambition, because ambitious men ruin presses trying to make them impressive." A small breath that might have been a laugh. "I swept for two years. I learned to set type with my left hand while holding a candle with my right. Your father taught me that letters are load-bearing. That if one is wrong, everything above it sags." His voice caught on the last word and he let it, deliberately, the way a man lets himself bleed to prove the wound is real. "Then I took the garrison clerkship because it paid four times what sweeping did, and I told myself it was arithmetic. And for nine years I have been doing arithmetic."

"What do you want from me, Emil?"

"I want to give you this." He lifted the folder. "But first you should know what it cost, and who paid, so you can decide what it's worth."

The candle fluttered in a draft from the door I hadn't latched. Neither of us moved to fix it.

"When the courier came through in December," he said, "the governor's order was to intercept him at the ferry and burn whatever he carried. The patrol was late. You know why they were late — Stefan sent his boy to loosen the axle pin on the supply cart, and it cost them half a night at the smithy. So the courier reached Mara's boat instead, and then your loft instead of a ditch." He watched me not react. "Yes. I know about the loft. I know about the loose floorboard your father used for the Greek New Testaments in 'sixty-nine, because I helped him carry them up there. I know everything about this shop except how to stay out of its gravity."

"Go on."

"After the courier died, the governor convened us — me, the major, the historian's advance man — to draft the official account. Eleven villages. I wrote 'bandit suppression, casualties minimal.' I wrote it in a fair hand. I sealed it. It is sitting in the konak right now with the governor's wax on it, and next week it becomes the truth." He opened the folder at last, and inside, folded small and creased hard, was paper I recognized — garrison stock, the good rag paper we sold to no one but the administration. "Three nights ago the historian asked me to reconcile the village death registers against my summary. He wanted tidy numbers for his appendix. So I spent an evening with the registers, copying names into a reconciliation table. And somewhere past midnight I stopped reconciling and started stealing."

He turned the folder around and set it on the press bed between us.

Inside were pages in his clerk's hand — columns, ruled true, the numbers aligned the way only a compositor's apprentice aligns numbers. Names. Ages. Villages. Dates in December of the year before last, and some from further back, further back than the massacre, further back —

I stopped reading at the fourth name from the top of the second column, because it was written in a different ink, added later, in a hand I knew better than my own.

*Nikolai Delacroix — printer — Bistrets road — "would not print the correction."*

The room tilted very slightly and righted itself. I gripped the press bed's iron edge and waited, because I had learned long ago that waiting was the only reliable cure for falling.

"My father," I said. It wasn't a question. It barely qualified as a word.

"He refused to reprint the garrison's account of the Bistrets action with their figures. He told the major that a pressman who prints what he knows is false is not a pressman, he's a weapon someone else is firing. Two weeks later he was crossing the Bistrets road at night and a horse came out of the dark. The report says robbery." Emil's jaw worked. "I read the report. I filed the report. I noticed that robbers don't leave a purse containing forty groschen untouched, and I noticed, and I filed, and I noticed, and I filed, for five years. That is what collaboration looks like from the inside, Ivo. It doesn't look like evil. It looks like noticing and filing."

* * *

We stood a long time with the folder open between us, and the ice spoke once, far off, a sound like a giant door testing its hinges.

"Why bring me his name now?" I asked finally. "You've carried it five years. Why tonight?"

"Because Thursday the historian seals the record and every name in that folder becomes a rumor, and rumors die in one generation. Because Stefan is in a cell for a broken seal he broke on purpose to protect pages you printed. Because tomorrow the governor reviews the final casualty appendix, and if the true list exists anywhere in the world after tomorrow night, it will exist only here." He pressed his palm flat on the folder. "And because I have discovered, late and at great expense, that I would rather be hanged as a man who noticed and *acted* than live as one who noticed and filed."

"And if I refuse it?" I said. "If I tell you to take it back and keep filing?"

"Then I'll set fire to the konak records myself, badly, and be arrested for incompetence, and the true list will still reach you eventually because I'll have hidden it somewhere obvious, because I'm bad at treason. Your father warned me about that too."

Despite everything — despite the name in the second column, despite the soldier overhead shifting in his sleep — something in my chest cracked toward laughter and didn't quite arrive.

"It isn't yours to offer," I said. "The list. Those aren't your dead."

"No," he said. "They're mine to *carry*, though. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference." He stood. "Forgive me or don't. Use the pages or burn them. But decide knowing that I didn't come here to purchase absolution. I came because the record needs a keeper and I am constitutionally unsuited for anything but keeping records."

* * *

Here is what I did not say to him, and what I will say now, because a book of testimonies ought to testify honestly even about its own maker.

I wanted to hate him. Hate was ready, patient, well-fed on five years of rationed grief — hate for the man who filed his friend's murder, hate for the uniform, hate for the fair hand that wrote *casualties minimal* while I learned to sleep in a shop where my father's chair still held his shape. Hate offered itself like a warm coat and I nearly put it on. It would have fit. It always fits.

But forgiveness, I have since learned, is not a feeling that arrives. It is a typesetting decision: you examine the line, you find the wrong letter, and then you choose whether to melt it down or reset it. Some errors deserve melting. And some men are simply mis-set — cast from good metal into the wrong word — and the work of a lifetime is to pull them and reset them by hand, patiently, in the dark, knowing most people will never see the correction.

I looked at Emil Vranov standing in my father's shop, offering me my father's name in his own careful clerk's hand, and I made the deliberate, unfeeling, entirely chosen act of reaching past him and taking the folder.

"The list goes in as the final signature," I said. "Before my colophon. Their names first. Always their names first."

"Yes," he said, and his knees went briefly strange, and he sat back down on the crate like a man whose sentence had been commuted and who distrusted the commutation.

"But understand what you're buying," I went on, pulling the folder onto my lap, "because I'm not finished. If the atlas crosses the river, everyone who touched it answers for it — Mara, Jovan, Stefan in his cell, the widow Petrova, the boy at the bridge, and you. Especially you. The governor won't shoot a clerk; he'll do worse, he'll erase you, and I've seen what erasure does to a family." I met his eyes. "So this isn't forgiveness. Forgiveness is between you and whoever you pray to. This is employment."

Something moved through his face — relief and terror sharing a single doorway.

"What's the work?" he asked.

"Tomorrow night the governor reviews the appendix. You'll be in the room." I was already moving, pulling the case for the small text face, my hands remembering themselves. "Which means tomorrow morning you'll bring me the exact wording he intends to publish — page count, map legend, the villages named and unnamed — so that when our edition appears beside his, every lie in his has a numbered refutation in ours. My father believed a correction is strongest when it quotes the error exactly." I slid the first slug of type into the stick, and the little metal click sounded louder than it was. "Can you get the wording?"

"I *write* the wording," Emil said.

"Then we're employed."

* * *

He stayed until the candle burned to its collar, and I taught him — God help us both — to hold a composing stick again, his left hand gone clumsy after twenty years, and he set the header for the casualty list twice and spoiled it twice and got it right on the third try, and when he locked the line into the chase his hands were shaking and I pretended not to see, because pretending not to see is a mercy I know how to give.

At the door he paused with his hand on the latch.

"He would have liked this," he said. "Your father. The three of us, conspiring over type at three in the morning."

"He would have hated that it was necessary," I said, "and loved that it was possible."

Emil nodded and went out into the blue snow, and I watched from the window until his tracks filled in, and then I sat down alone with the folder and read my father's name until the letters stopped being letters and became, briefly, a man walking a road at night with forty groschen still in his pocket.

Then I blew out the candle, because the work had to wait for dark to end and light to begin, and because upstairs a soldier had begun to stir.

Through the floorboards, faintly, came the sound of boots descending — earlier than any patrol, slower than any officer.

Someone was coming down the stairs into the shop, and it was not yet dawn.
