## 17. The Last Signature

The candle had burned down to a stub the width of my thumb when I set the final line of the final page.

I did it slowly. There is a way of handling type when you are tired that ruins everything — a slip of the thumb, a letter turned backward, an em quad left where an em should be — and I had spent the whole night refusing to be tired. The shop smelled of ink and tallow and the cold breathing of the river through the walls, and Jovan slept curled in the paper loft like a cat who believed himself invisible, which at thirteen he nearly was.

The last page was the last for a reason. My father had taught me that a book ends where the printer decides it ends, not where the writing runs out. So I had saved this.

*Printed by Ivo Delacroix, Vidin, on the press of Lazar Delacroix.*

Five years since I had written my own name anywhere that mattered. Five years of signing nothing, of being *the typeface cutter's girl*, of letting the town call me whatever was convenient — Ivo, Ivka, *that one at the print shop* — because a name is a handle, and a handle lets someone pick you up. I had put the name down like a knife before entering a room where knives were forbidden.

Now I stood with the letters in the composing stick, one at a time, and my hands did not shake until they did.

*Delacroix.* The D was my father's D, cast from his own matrix, with the small nick in the lower serif he'd made the winter of the bad coal, when his hands were too cold to file true. He had never recut it. "A flaw you can find again," he said, "is worth more than a perfection anyone can counterfeit." I used to think he meant type. I had spent five years learning he meant people.

* * *

It had taken eleven nights. Eleven nights of Mara standing watch at the window with a knitting project she could abandon in half a second, of Jovan running messages folded into loaves, of melting candles faster than Stefan's cousin could smuggle them in under flour sacks. Eleven nights of soldiers sleeping twelve feet above our heads in the quartered rooms, their boots hanging by the ladder like sleeping animals, and once — only once — footsteps on the stairs at three in the morning that stopped, hung there, and went back up. We froze so long that the wax pooled and guttered, and after, Mara said quietly, "Next time we don't freeze. Next time we keep printing. A press that stops sounds guiltier than a press that goes on." She was right. Everything she said was right, in that flat ferry-woman's way, as if she were reading the current.

The pages lay stacked now in the drying rack, forty-eight of them, and I will try to describe what they were because I have thought since that no one who wasn't there will ever quite believe it.

My father's maps came first. Fourteen villages along both banks, drawn in his precise, unornamented hand — he had hated decoration the way some men hate lying — each map annotated with names. Not the governor's names, not *rebels*, *bandits*, *the disorderly element*. Names: Petar Vukov, ferryman. The Anka sisters, weavers. The whole Mihailov household, nine souls, with ages. Beside each name, in the margins, in a smaller hand — mine now, added over his — the testimony numbers that corresponded to the accounts in the satchel. Forty-one testimonies. Some were dictated statements taken down in careful clerkly script; some were letters; two were children's drawings, and I had reproduced those as faithfully as woodcut allowed, because a drawing of a burning house made by a child who watched it burn says something no sentence can say without flinching.

And then the casualty list. Emil's list.

He had brought it to me four nights ago, wrapped in oilcloth inside his coat, and laid it on the stone floor of the pressroom rather than the wooden table, the way you set down something that might still be hot. Three hundred and six names. The true count. The official account, the one he had drafted himself with his neat collaborator's hand, said *seventy-one bandits killed in action*. He had counted the dead of fourteen villages in the governor's own requisition ledgers — grain requisitioned from households that no longer existed to eat it — and done the arithmetic no one wanted done.

"I falsified one line to protect you," he had said, not looking at me. "I won't falsify three hundred and six."

I had almost hated him for the phrasing, and then understood it was the most honest sentence anyone had spoken to me in five years. Complicity curdling into something else. I took the list. I set it in type myself, every name, and my hands learned the dead the way they had learned the alphabet — letter by letter, name by name, until I could have composed them blind.

There is a thing that happens when you set three hundred and six names by hand. Each one passes through your fingers twice: once as metal, once as meaning. By the second hundred I stopped being able to separate them. By the third hundred I understood what my father had been doing all those years, riding the ferry, buying drinks in village taverns, sketching. He hadn't been collecting information. He had been setting type in people, waiting for the day it would be pulled from the case and printed.

* * *

Near dawn I woke Jovan, because this part he had earned.

He came down the ladder rubbing his eyes, all elbows, and looked at the drying rack with the expression boys wear when they are pretending not to be moved. "Is it done?"

"The text is done. One line left."

"Which line?"

I held out the composing stick. "Yours to help me decide something. When a book is printed, it carries the printer's name. That name can be arrested, hanged, erased. Or—" and here I heard my own voice go dry and careful, "—it can simply not appear. Plenty of true books have gone out anonymous. No one would blame us. No one would even know there was a name to omit."

Jovan considered this with the terrible literalness of the young. "But then it would be a ghost book. Like you were."

I laughed — actually laughed, at that hour, in that room. "Something like that."

"Mara says a ghost is just a person whose name got lost." He shrugged. "Seems like a lot of work, hiding it again."

From the mouth of a boy whose job for weeks had been watching the bridge and counting patrols, it landed harder than any argument. I sent him back up to sleep, and sat alone with the stick, and the candle, and the flaw in the D.

* * *

Here is what I know about fear, having studied it the way I studied type: it has weights. The fear of dying has a certain heft; the fear of others dying on your account is heavier; but the strangest weight, the one I carried to the stick that morning, is the fear of being seen. Not caught — seen. For five years invisibility had been my craft, my discipline, my armor. To print my name was to stand in the town square naked in a way no soldier could accomplish by tearing off my clothes. Anyone could kill a woman. Only someone who knew me could kill *Ivo Delacroix, printer*.

My father had died for less than this. I had told myself for years that he died for carelessness — a man too visible, too curious, too known. Now, with his maps drying on the rack above me, I knew better. He had died for exactly this, and he had known he might, and he had cut his flawed D anyway and taught his daughter its location.

*A flaw you can find again.*

I set the line. *Printed by Ivo Delacroix.* Then, because it was true and because hiding had cost enough, I added the second line he would have wanted: *On the press of Lazar Delacroix, who gathered these testimonies.*

Two names. Five years of silence ending in two lines of ten-point.

I locked the forme, carried it to the press, and pulled the proof myself. The impression came off clean, dark, slightly cockled at the edge where the dampened paper hadn't settled — imperfect, findable-again, ours. I held it at arm's length and read my own name in reverse in the type and forward in the print, and felt nothing dramatic at all, which surprised me. Just a quiet click, somewhere behind the sternum, like a forme locking tight. Like something that had been loose for five years finally sitting square in its chase.

Then Mara came down the ladder without knocking, which meant something was wrong, because Mara always knocked twice, softly, our signal.

* * *

"They've posted a notice on the customs house," she said. "The governor's historian finished his account. It's going to the capital on the first boat that can move — they're saying the ice will hold another week, maybe less." She set a printed broadsheet on the stone floor between us, face up, and I read it in the candlelight with a cold that had nothing to do with March.

*An Account of the Suppression of Banditry in the River Districts, prepared by order of His Excellency the Governor. With a verified register of losses.*

They were publishing theirs first. Seventy-one bandits, killed in action, God rest them. A tidy little atlas of lies, typeset — I bent closer and recognized the face immediately — in type borrowed from a Sofia foundry, because the governor had never trusted my father's shop to print anything but proclamations, and soon would trust it for nothing at all.

"They'll distribute it everywhere," Mara said. "Every chancellery from here to Belgrade. In ten years it'll be the truth, because it'll be the only copy that exists in numbers."

"No," I said.

She looked at me.

"It won't be the only copy." I nodded upward, at the rack, at the formes stacked against the wall, at the stack of printed signatures hidden in the ice cellar under the pickled cabbage, two hundred copies already, three hundred by tomorrow night. "Ours exists. It's slower than theirs, and it travels in grain barges and laundry baskets instead of government couriers. But it exists, and it has three hundred and six names in it that theirs doesn't, and—" I touched the wet proof, my name, still tacky under my finger, "—a printer's name."

Mara read it. I watched her read it. She had known me as the girl at the shop, useful and quiet, for longer than the soldiers had; she had fed me bread through five winters; and I saw her arrive, in real time, at the fact that the person she had been protecting had a surname and had just chosen to use it. Her eyes went bright and ferocious.

"Well," she said. "About time."

Then, because she was Mara: "The thaw's early this year. Jovan says there are cracks below the ferry landing wide enough to lose a sheep. When the ice goes, it will go fast, and the garrison will do something stupid, because stupid men always do something stupid when the ground moves under them." She picked up the broadsheet of the governor's account, weighed it in her hand like something spoiled. "We should assume we have days. Maybe two. Print what's left, hide what's printed, and get ready to move everything the hour the river opens."

After she climbed back to her post, I stood alone in the pressroom with the first light coming gray through the shutters, and I did the thing I had promised myself I wouldn't do: I let myself imagine the other bank. The free bank. The revolution's printers with their bigger presses and their certainties, men who would want my atlas, and want it clean — no awkward villagers' names, no inconvenient griefs, just fuel for the fire they were already building. I imagined them asking who had compiled it, and reaching for my arm to pull me into their story, and I imagined saying, as I had said nothing for five years:

No. Read the colophon. The name stands as written.

Outside, far off, the river groaned — a long, grinding note under the ice, like type being redistributed in a hurry, every letter let go at once. Spring was coming up the Danube whether we were ready or not.

I blew out the candle, locked the door, and went to wake Jovan. We had two days, and three hundred names left to print.
