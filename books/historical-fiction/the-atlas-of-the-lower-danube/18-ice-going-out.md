## 18. Ice Going Out

The river spoke first.

Mara heard it before dawn, a groan under the ice like a great animal turning in its sleep, and she was out of bed with her shawl half-pinned, standing on the frozen mud of her yard, listening. For three months the Danube had been a road — white, silent, patrolled, a thing the garrison owned as thoroughly as it owned the governor's proclamations. Now the road was dying. She could hear it dying.

By full light the sound had become language. Cracks ran from bank to bank like veins of black glass; the ice heaved and settled and heaved again, and along the shallows the water stood up through the fissures in thin silver spines that grew even as she watched. The ferry landing, which had been useless since December, began to drip.

She had been waiting for this day the way you wait for a birth or a burial: knowing it would come, not knowing what shape it would take.

It took the shape of drums.

The garrison beat them at an hour when no honest drum is beaten, and Mara, carrying two loaves to Ivo's shop because bread was the one errand no patrol questioned anymore, stopped in the market square and counted what the drums were saying. Not alarm. Ceremony. The governor meant the burning of the print shop to be an occasion.

* * *

Ivo met her at the door with ink still wet to the elbow.

"You've heard."

"The whole town's heard. They're calling it seditious premises." Mara set the loaves down inside, where the smell of fresh paper lay over everything like snow. "They'll post the notice by noon. Burning at dusk, they always burn at dusk, they want the whole town watching with their suppers cold."

"Then we have a day." Ivo said it flatly, without drama, and Mara understood suddenly how much of the girl's father lived in her — the same way of taking terrible arithmetic and simply doing it. "The last run came off the press at midnight. Four hundred copies. They're in the loft under the wool."

"Four hundred." Mara looked at the stacks of type cases, the press itself, the beautiful iron of it that Ivo's father had bought secondhand from a bankrupt house in Vienna. "And all this?"

"Burns. It has to look real, Mara. If they burn a clean shop, they'll know something was carried out." Ivo wiped her hands on her apron, leaving grey ghosts. "So we give them a fire worth watching."

* * *

By nine o'clock the network was moving, and Mara, who had ferried people across this river for thirty years and smuggled things across it for twenty of those, directed it from her kitchen like a captain working a crossing in fog.

Stefan's wife Lili came first, hood up, face pale from a winter of visiting a husband behind bars, and Mara put four bundles into her basket under the flatbread. "Grain barges," she said. "Three of them loading at the customs quay for the first convoy south. The bargemen owe me. Tell them Mara's flour has weevils and must go below."

Jovan came at ten, breathless, fourteen years old and suddenly looking older, the way boys do when the world stops pretending to protect them. "The coffins," he said. "Old Petar died in the night. There's a funeral tomorrow morning."

Mara had already thought of it — had thought of it, God forgive her, while the man was still breathing his last upstairs from where Jovan now stood. "Tell his widow we'll pay for the finest boards. Tell her the coffin rides heavy with grief." She caught herself and put a hand on the boy's shoulder. "And tell her — tell her Petar kept every secret ever given him, and he can keep these too."

Laundry was Mara's own task. A woman with washing lines was invisible; a woman with washing lines on the river path, on the very morning soldiers were preparing a spectacle, was merely diligent. By eleven she had sheets strung between the willows below the ferry landing, and behind each sheet, pinned to the line itself, pages of testimony dried in the wind — pages that would come down at dusk folded into a washbasket and walk away on a hip.

* * *

At noon the notice went up on the customs house door, and Mara read it aloud for the benefit of the crowd, her voice pitched to carry exactly as far as it needed to:

*"By order of His Excellency the Governor, the premises formerly licensed as a printing establishment are declared seditious, having been used in the production and distribution of treasonous material. The premises will be destroyed by fire at sunset, that order may be restored to this district, and that the poison of lies may be cleansed by flame."*

A murmur ran through the square. Somewhere in it a woman began to weep — not for the shop, Mara judged, but for whatever the shop had printed that she needed the whole town to pretend she hadn't read.

The poison of lies. Mara almost laughed. The governor had spent a winter manufacturing one lie, large and official, and the town had spent the same winter passing the antidote hand to hand in bundles small enough to hide in bread.

* * *

The barges nearly broke her.

The customs officer at the quay — a new one, transferred in February, young enough to be thorough — was making the bargemen open their holds. Mara watched from the fish stalls with her basket on her arm and her heart going like a paddle wheel.

The first barge: opened, grain turned with a shovel, sealed again. Clean — the bundles had gone deep, under the false floor old Toma had built into his hold fifteen years ago for cargo that never appeared on any manifest.

The second barge: the officer lingered. He made the men pull tarpaulins back, made them climb down with lanterns. Mara bought a fish she did not want and stood turning it over in her hands while her entire winter's work sat thirty feet away under forty sacks of maize.

Then the third barge's crew started an argument. Loud. About wages, about a card debt, about a wife in Ruse — fists, shouting, half the quay gathering to watch, and the customs officer turned toward the noise because noise on a quay is either trouble to stop or entertainment to keep, and either way it moves him off his inspection. When the crowd parted twenty minutes later the third barge was sealed and the officer was writing in his ledger, satisfied, and Mara put the unwanted fish down on the stall and walked home on legs that felt borrowed.

Fierce competence, she thought. That was what her mother had called it, in the old country, when women did what had to be done while the men performed their official duties. Fierce competence. It was holding the whole town together today, thread by thread, and none of it would ever appear in any ledger.

* * *

Dusk came early, the way spring dusks do, long twilight bleeding into a violet dark, and the garrison formed up in the square with torches and a brass band, because the governor did nothing without a band.

Mara stood with the crowd, Jovan pressed against her side, and watched them march Ivo's shop out of the world.

They burned it well. The soldiers had soaked the stair and the loft with oil, and when the flames took the roof the whole street glowed orange, and the brass band played something martial and cheerful, and the crowd watched with the particular silence Mara had learned at the chaplain's sermon — the silence that was itself a document.

Beside her, Jovan's hand found hers and gripped it. His eyes were dry, but his knuckles were white.

"She's not in there," he whispered.

"No."

"How do you know?"

"Because I put her in the ice cellar myself at noon, with the atlas strapped to her body under a fishmonger's coat, and because Ivo Delacroix does not die in other people's fires." Mara said it steadily, and only then let her own hands shake, now that no one was watching anything but the blaze.

* * *

But Stefan — Stefan was the day's unfinished business, and the night delivered it badly.

The jail, when it burned — and it burned, because the fire leapt the lane on a wind nobody had forecast, or perhaps somebody had — emptied its cells into chaos. Guards ran buckets; prisoners spilled into the yard; and in the confusion a broad-shouldered baker with three months of prison on him walked out through a smoke-blackened gate and did not stop walking until he reached the willows by the river path, where Mara stood with a lantern shaded by her shawl.

He was soot to the elbows and thinner than she'd ever seen him, and he looked at her and said, hoarse, "The ledgers finally frayed all the way through. I told them it was mice."

"Mice," Mara said.

"I've been blaming mice for five years." He almost smiled, then didn't. "Where is she? Where's the girl?"

"On the river."

They both turned to look at the Danube, black and groaning, its ice rotting in the dark, plates of it grinding against the landing pilings with a sound like millstones. No boat could cross that. No swimmer could cross that. Whatever plan Ivo had — and Mara knew only its outline, the girl trusting her with pieces rather than the whole — it waited on the river finishing its argument with winter.

"Then we keep watch," Stefan said.

"Then we keep watch," Mara agreed, and the three of them — the baker, the widow, and the boy who knew every crack in the ice better than any patrol — stood among the willows while the garrison's bonfire lit the sky behind them and the river broke apart in the darkness, plate by plate, groaning toward the sea.
