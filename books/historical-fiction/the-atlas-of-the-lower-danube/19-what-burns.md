## 19. What Burns

The last run was always going to be the hardest one.

Not because the type was tired — though it was, my father's letters worn round at the shoulders like old men — and not because my hands shook, though they had shaken since dawn. It was the hardest run because it was the last, and I knew it, and the press knew it too, the way animals know a thing before you say it. The iron frame had a smell in winter, cold and mineral, like a well. That morning it smelled like a grave being dug for something still breathing.

Outside, the Danube was breaking up.

You could hear it from the shop: a long groaning under the ice, then cracks that went off like musket fire, then silence, then more cracks. The whole river talking to itself in its sleep, turning over, deciding to wake. Jovan had come at first light with his cheeks raw from the wind and his eyes too bright, and he'd said only what needed saying: *Three days. Maybe two. The barges leave tomorrow.*

Then he'd gone back to the bridge to watch, because watching was his gift and his burden both, a boy of fourteen who had learned to read ice the way I read type.

Mara had left an hour later with the first bundle of atlases sewn into grain sacks. She'd stood at the door a moment longer than she needed to, looking at me with her ferryman's widow face — the face she wore when she was measuring a current she couldn't see the bottom of.

"You don't have to be the last one out," she said.

"Someone has to pull the lever."

"That's not a reason, Ivo."

"It's the only one I've got."

She'd squeezed my arm once, hard, the way her husband must have gripped a rope, and gone out into the white morning.

* * *

By noon I had two hundred copies stacked in oilcloth under the floor of the paper loft, where my father had hidden his forbidden type five years ago, where the courier had died pressing a satchel into my hands. The circle of the thing pleased some grim part of me. Everything this shop had ever kept secret had been kept in the same few feet of floor.

The press itself I could not save. A handpress weighs as much as a dead ox, and there was no time and no barge space and no way to explain it to a patrol if we were stopped. So we would lose it. I had made my peace with that the way you make peace with weather.

What I had not made peace with was leaving before the last sheet came off.

Because Emil had sent word — a scrap of paper folded into a loaf, the baker's boy carrying bread again now that Stefan was free, the whole town a body with messages running through it like blood — and the word said: *They come tonight. Governor's order. Seditious premises. Burned by daylight so the smoke tells the story.*

Burned by daylight. Even the destruction would be printed, published, distributed. The governor understood what I understood: whoever controlled the image controlled the memory. He meant for Vidin to wake to a column of black smoke over the river quarter and think, *ah, yes, they found the sedition, it is being dealt with.* He wanted the burning to be the last page anyone read about my father's shop.

So I decided the smoke would tell a different story.

I stayed to print the final run — fifty sheets, the last signature of the atlas, the casualty list with its true numbers in Emil's careful copied figures, and beneath it, small and stubborn, the line I had set three nights ago with my hands steady for the first time in five years:

*Printed at Vidin by Ivo Delacroix, daughter of Anton Delacroix, typeface cutter.*

My name. In ink. On paper that was already crossing the river in sacks and laundry baskets and one coffin that Mara swore contained nothing but a very heavy sleeping woman's linens and forty books.

I pulled the lever. The platen came down. My father's letters bit into dampened paper, and another copy of the truth existed in the world.

That was the arithmetic of it, the whole arithmetic, and I clung to it through the afternoon while the ice groaned outside and the candles burned down: every pull of the lever was one more copy than the governor could burn. You cannot arrest a number. You cannot drown a multiplication table. Somewhere on the other bank, in a cellar in Bucharest or a garret in Vienna or a farmhouse anywhere the river ran, someone would open these pages and read the names of the dead from the villages, all of them, spelled correctly, set in type by a girl nobody had bothered to learn the name of.

Fifty sheets. Forty-one. Thirty-three. My arms burned. The soldiers would come after dark, and after dark was coming fast, the light through the frost-furred windows going the color of dirty copper.

At twenty-two sheets, someone knocked.

* * *

It was Emil.

He came in sideways, the way he'd learned to enter the shop over years of loving my father quietly and never saying so, and he was wearing his garrison coat with the buttons done wrong, which from Emil was the equivalent of another man screaming.

"They moved it up," he said. "The burning. They're coming within the hour — the officer wants it done while there's still light for the witnesses." His eyes went to the press, the half-finished stack, the ink still wet on the twenty-second sheet. "Ivo. Stop. Now."

"Eighteen more pulls."

"They'll hang you. They'll do worse than hang you, they'll—"

"You counted them," I said. "In your ledger. Every name on the list. You carried the true count out of the historian's office in your boot, and you gave it to me, and you wrote one false line in a census to keep this shop invisible for a year." I turned back to the press. "You don't get to tell me to stop. Eighteen."

For a moment he said nothing, and I heard behind us the river give a crack so long and deep it seemed to come up through the floorboards, through our boots, through our bones. The thaw was accelerating. Everything was accelerating. Spring and revolution and soldiers, all arriving on the same tide.

"I'll stay," he said finally. "I'll meet them at the door. I'm the clerk — I have the requisition order, I can claim the premises were inspected yesterday, I can buy you twenty minutes arguing about seals and signatures. Twenty minutes is nine sheets, maybe ten, if you don't stop to cry."

"I never cry at the press. It smears the ink."

Something crossed his face that was almost a smile, there and gone like fish under ice. Then he straightened his buttons without looking at them, took the requisition folder from under his arm, and went to stand in the doorway of the shop like a man posted there, which, in every way that mattered, he had been for a year.

Twelve sheets. Ten. Outside, boots on frozen mud, several pairs, unhurried. Men who knew no one would stop them.

Eight.

The door opened and Emil filled it, and I did not look up. I pulled the lever. Seven.

"Gospodin Clerk." An officer's voice, bored and cold. "This premises is on the list."

"It is." Papers rustling. Emil's voice had changed — I had never heard it like this, flat and official and enormous, the voice of the empire itself speaking through one tired man. "Inspected yesterday under my seal. No printing apparatus present. The press was removed in February per requisition 114-C. I signed the removal myself."

"The governor says otherwise."

"The governor says many things." A pause, and I could hear Emil choosing each next word the way I chose each letter, from the case, one at a time. "If you find a press in this building, arrest me with it. If you don't find one, and you burn a licensed premises on a false report, the governor will want a name attached to that error before he answers for it to Constantinople. Whose name shall I write?"

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that has rank inside it.

Six. Five.

"Check the building," the officer said at last. "Quickly."

Boots inside. A soldier's voice from the loft overhead — *nothing but rags here, Gospodin* — and I thought of the two hundred copies under the floor and felt my heart try to climb out through my throat. Another voice from the cellar. The oilcloth bundles were gone; Mara had taken everything but these last sheets. There was nothing to find. There was nothing to find. I pulled the lever. Four.

Then a shout from the street, and everything changed.

* * *

Later — much later, on the water, in the dark — I would piece together what happened, because I only ever saw fragments of it: the corner of a window, a silhouette, a sound.

What I know is this. A second squad came down the lane, the one assigned to the warehouses, and their torchlight spilled through the shop window, and in that light the officer saw the press.

There was no hiding it. There never had been. A handpress fills a room like a confession.

"There's your apparatus, Clerk," the officer said, and I heard the pleasure arrive in his voice like a rat into a granary. "Removed in February? Signed yourself?"

Emil did not hesitate. I will give him that until the day I die; he did not hesitate even a half-second, and hesitation is the difference between treason and stupidity, and Emil had spent his whole life being neither.

"That press is imperial property," he said. "Requisition 114-C ordered it removed to the fortress. It has evidently not been moved. Which means the transport detail falsified their report, or the quartermaster sold the removal on paper and pocketed the fee. Either way, this is now an internal matter of the garrison, and you are standing in the middle of it. Do you want to be the officer who burned evidence?"

Another silence. Longer. I could hear the officer thinking, and I could hear him fail to think of anything better.

"Who's responsible for this premises?" he demanded.

"The printer," Emil said. "Delacroix's daughter. She fled — the neighbors say toward Lom, days ago, when the ice began to go. Check if you like. There's no one here."

Three. Two.

And then the officer gave the order I had known was coming since the governor first dreamed it, the order that was the whole point of the evening: "Fire it anyway. Seditious premises. We'll note the apparatus was destroyed with it."

Torchlight bloomed against the window glass. And Emil — I saw this one clearly, through the little pane above the type cases, his face lit orange and absolutely calm — Emil stepped between the soldiers and the wall where the lamp oil was stored, and said, loudly, in the voice of a man reading from a document:

"Then you'll want my signature confirming the premises were empty. Come to the fortress office. I'll write it now. I write everything; that's my function. Nothing burns in this town without a line in my book saying who ordered it and why. Unless the governor prefers arson without documentation."

It was madness. It was brilliant. It was the single most dangerous thing a collaborator could do — make himself the bottleneck, make his paperwork the price of everything, dare the empire to work around the man who kept its records. And it worked, because bureaucracy is the true religion of every occupying army, and Emil was its most devout priest.

They went with him. All of them. To fetch his signature, to argue about forms, to hate him and need him in the same breath.

Which left me alone in the shop with two finished sheets and one unfinished, and a torch already somewhere in the building, or soon to be, and perhaps four minutes.

* * *

Here is what I took, and I have made my peace with each choice the way you make peace with amputations — completely, and forever, and without looking at the stump too often.

I took the atlas. Both finished sheets went into the oilcloth roll, and the roll went against my body, strapped tight under my coat with the cord Mara had rigged for exactly this purpose, so that the truth rode against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

I took my father's last matrix — the small steel punch he'd cut the year before he died, the letter *Đ*, his joke, his pride, the one character in the world that existed nowhere else because he had invented it for a word in his mother's tongue that had no Latin letters to hold it. It fit in my palm like a seed.

I took nothing else. Not the type — my father's life's work, thirty years of cutting steel, melting into slag by morning along with everything else. Not the ledgers of honest work, the wedding invitations and grain receipts and funeral notices of a quiet town. Not the bed upstairs where I had slept since I was six years old. Not the doorframe where my father had penciled my height every spring, a ladder of small marks climbing toward the year he died.

Everything lost except what matters. I had thought that phrase was a comfort. Standing in the shop with the first thread of smoke finding me through the floorboards, I learned it was not a comfort at all. It was a price tag.

The back wall had a loose stone. My father had shown it to me when I was nine, laughing, calling it the shop's other mouth — it opened onto the tanner's yard, and the tanner owed my father for a hundred posters and never asked questions about anything. I put my shoulder to the stone the way I put my shoulder to the lever, and it gave, and the night air came in smelling of hides and river and smoke that was not yet ours but would be.

Behind me, the press stood in the dark. I put my hand flat on the frame one last time. Cold iron, warm wood, the smell of ink soaked so deep into the grain that it would still be there in the ashes, and the archaeologists of some future century would dig down through the cinders of Vidin and find a press that smelled of stories.

"Thank you," I said, to the press, to the shop, to the ghost who had built both. "I'll take it from here."

Then I went through the stone, into the yard, into the dark, and behind me the first real flames found the oil store and the windows lit up gold, and the smoke began to rise over the river quarter exactly as the governor had ordered — except that the story it told was already crossing the ice in oilcloth, in grain sacks, in a coffin, in a girl's coat, and no fire on earth could call it back.

* * *

The river at night, breaking up, is not a sight. It is a presence. It is a god changing its mind.

I came down to the bank below the tannery where Mara said the skiff would be, and the ice was moving — great plates of it grinding south in the moonless dark, groaning, shuddering, splitting with reports like artillery, the whole surface a war between winter and water. The channel near the bank was still solid enough to walk, if walking on a drumskin counts as solid. Beyond it, black water showed in widening veins.

The skiff was there. Mara was in it, and Jovan, and Stefan — Stefan, freed two days past, thinner, his forearms still bearing the marks of the irons, holding the oars like a man holding a promise.

"You're late," Mara said, in the voice of a woman who had aged ten years in the ninety minutes since the sky over the river quarter turned orange.

"The last sheet took longer than the others."

She looked at me — at the ash in my hair, at the strap across my chest, at whatever my face was doing — and she reached out and pulled me into the boat without another word, the way you pull someone out of a current, and held me against her shoulder for exactly as long as it takes a heart to remember its rhythm.

Across the water, the shop burned. From the river it looked almost beautiful, which I suppose is the cruelest trick fire knows. Flames stood up out of the roof like wheat. Sparks went up and were taken by the wind toward the free bank, toward Bucharest, toward everywhere the atlas was already traveling, and I chose — deliberately, with everything left in me — to believe they were pages.

Stefan pushed off. The bow ground across the shore ice with a sound like tearing linen, and then we were among the floes, and the men of the garrison were shouting on the far bank, tiny against their own fire, and someone fired a musket whose ball went God knew where, and none of it mattered. None of it. Let them have the ashes. Let them write their report: *seditious premises destroyed, apparatus lost, printer fled.* Let the governor read it twice and believe it.

He would believe it because he could not imagine the truth, which was simpler and stranger: that everything he had burned was replaceable, because it had already left, and the only things we had carried out were the things he didn't know how to burn. Names. Numbers. A steel letter no alphabet owned. A girl who had spent five years learning to be nobody and had chosen, on the last night of her town, to sign her work.

Halfway across, the ice closed behind us with a long, sighing crunch, sealing the channel, sealing the view of the fire, sealing Vidin like a letter that would not be opened again for a very long time.

Ahead, the free bank rose out of the dark — low, tree-lined, unguarded, ordinary. Somewhere beyond it were printers who spoke the language of revolution, and they would want my testimony, and they would want it anonymous, dissolved into their broadsheets like salt into water.

I put my hand against the oilcloth roll, against the two hundred names riding over the river pressed to my ribs, and I promised them: not this time. Your names go in exactly as written, or not at all.

The keel touched mud. Hands reached down out of the dark — strangers' hands, revolution's hands — and I climbed up onto the other bank, carrying the fire that wouldn't burn.
