## 20. The Other Bank

The river let me go the way a fist opens — slowly, finger by finger, leaving marks.

I crossed at dawn on the ninth of April, in a leaking skiff Mara's cousin poled through drifting pans of ice, and I remember thinking that the water had never once been on anyone's side. It carried grain and corpses and proclamations with equal patience. It had carried my father's type from Vienna, crated and paid for, and it had carried the governor's soldiers to the villages whose names were now stitched into my shirt, printed on paper pressed against my ribs under a coat that smelled of smoke because I had stood too close when the shop went up.

The other bank was not free yet. That was the first lie they told me there.

* * *

The revolution's printing house occupied a disused tannery above the marsh road, and you smelled it before you saw it — lime and old hides and wet wool, the smell of a town that had learned to make do. A boy with a rifle slung wrong across his back stopped me at the door and asked for papers, and I almost laughed, because I had spent five years being nobody's business precisely so that no one would ever ask me for papers again.

"I don't have papers," I said. "I have pages."

He looked at the bundle strapped against my body like he was deciding whether it was a weapon. In fairness, it was.

Inside, the tannery was dim and crowded and alive in a way Vidin's garrison offices had never been: three presses, two of them missing parts, typesetting done by candlelight even at noon because the windows were papered over. Compositors worked standing up, sleeves rolled, arguing about hyphenation while somewhere upstairs men argued about artillery. A woman with ink to her elbows took one look at me and said, "You're the river one. They said someone might come."

"They said?"

"Committee." She wiped her hands on an apron already black. "There's always a committee. Give it here."

I did not give it here. That was the first thing I understood about myself on the free bank, before I'd eaten, before I'd slept, before I'd learned anyone's name: I held the satchel the way I had learned to hold everything, close and quiet, and I was done learning new ways to hold things.

"It's two hundred and six pages," I said. "Testimonies from eleven villages. Maps my father drew. The casualty list from the governor's own records, corrected. And every name set as it was given to me — spelled the way the speakers spelled them, including the ones who couldn't spell at all."

The woman's eyes changed. Not unkindly. The way a buyer's eyes change when he decides the horse is worth more than he offered.

"I'm Ruzica," she said. "I run this floor. Come and sit down, and we'll talk about how we print it."

* * *

We talked about how they would print it for four hours, and by the end of the four hours I understood that the fight over the massacre had not ended on the burning bank behind me. It had only changed uniforms.

They wanted it, that was clear. They wanted it badly. A committee man named Draganic came down from the artillery arguments smelling of tobacco and certainty, read the first testimony standing, and said, "This is exactly what the cause needs," and I felt something in my chest go cold, because I had heard that sentence before. Emil had told me once — the night he confessed at the press, ink on his cuffs, shame in his mouth — that the governor's historian had used nearly the same words about the official account. *Exactly what the story needs.* The massacre as material. The dead as argument.

"We'll fold it into the broadsheets," Draganic said. "Excerpts. We'll lead with the strongest three or four accounts, attribute the rest collectively — 'witnesses of the Danube villages' — and frame it under the committee's declaration. Unified voice. The people reading it need one clear narrative, not—" he turned a page, frowned— "not all these names. Some of these are peasants' spellings. We'll regularize."

"You'll do no such thing."

I said it quietly. Five years of practice. But the room shifted anyway; compositors looked up; Ruzica set down her rag.

Draganic smiled the smile of a man who has never been contradicted by a woman with river mud on her hem. "It's a question of effectiveness—"

"It's a question of whether the dead are yours to spend." I untied the strap. "My father spent ten years collecting these testimonies. He died for the first batch. A courier named Bogdan Ilievski drowned in my loft getting the second to me. A baker named Stefan Petrov is in a cell right now because he broke a seal so these pages could stay hidden. A ferryman's widow ferried your revolution's evidence across a frozen river with her hands cracked open from the cold. Every single one of those people gave their name, or gave their silence, knowingly. You will not dissolve them into 'witnesses.' You will not regularize a murdered man's spelling so it reads better in Belgrade."

"The committee controls publication—"

"The committee controls nothing," I said. "I control the type. I am the only person on this floor who knows how these pages were composed, what order the signatures run in, and where the last third of the casualty list lives — which is nowhere but in my head, because I memorized it crossing the river in case the paper didn't survive. Shoot me if you like. The list dies with me, and your declaration gets to stand next to a hole."

That was melodrama, and I knew it, and part of me hated myself for using the same currency the committees traded in — threat, leverage, the arithmetic of usefulness. But I had learned arithmetic in Vidin. Useful silence. Useful competence. Useful invisibility. If usefulness was the price of survival, then I would finally pay it toward something instead of merely surviving on it.

Ruzica laughed. Short, hard, real. "She's a compositor," she told Draganic. "Look at her hands. We don't shoot compositors; we can't replace them in a war."

"I'm not asking to be kept," I said. "I'm asking for the names to stand."

* * *

They compromised, which is what institutions do instead of listening, and I took the compromise the way you take a ferry in bad weather — gripping what holds, watching what doesn't.

The atlas would be printed entire, all two hundred and six pages, names intact, maps reproduced from my father's drawings. But it would carry the committee's preface, a page and a half of Draganic's prose wrapping the villagers' voices in the revolution's flag, and my name would appear — this took another hour of fighting — not as printer, but as "compiler, initials only." *Compiled by I.D.*

I stood over the galley while Ruzica's best man locked it into the chase, and I looked at those two letters, and I thought of five years of signing nothing. Of setting garrison proclamations with my father's beautiful type and letting the work walk out anonymous. Of being the ghost in everyone else's book.

"No," I said.

Draganic, passing behind me with his tobacco, sighed like weather. "Compositor—"

"My name. Full. *Printed and published by Ivo Delacroix, Vidin.* Delacroix — yes, French, my grandfather came up the river with the steamboats, spell it however your readers like, but spell all of it." I put my hand flat on the forme, over the locked type, the way you'd put a hand on a child's head. "You want the atlas because it's true, and it's true because ordinary people refused to be anonymous in it. The baker signed his ledger. The widow opened her door on the record. The clerk falsified a census line and put his treason in writing, knowing what writing costs. If they could sign, so can I. If I hide, the whole book is a lie wearing true clothes."

The tannery was very quiet. Upstairs, someone dropped something metal and swore.

Draganic looked at me for a long moment, and whatever he saw made him recalculate — I was no longer a delivery mechanism, I was a problem, and problems get either crushed or co-opted. "The committee will want to meet you," he said finally, which meant neither yes nor no.

"Good," I said. "I've met governors. I know how meetings go."

* * *

They printed the first signature that night, and I set it myself.

Not because they asked. Because my hands needed it. Because the last thing I had set in Vidin was a final run by candlelight with the shop burning around me, and a press needs to finish what it starts or the compositor carries the unfinished forme forever, in the arms, in the sleep. Ruzica watched me lock the chase and pull the first proof and said nothing, which among printers is the deepest respect there is.

The proof came up grey and sharp. My father's maps. Mara's testimony in her own plain words — *I took him aboard because the river takes everyone sometime and I choose which side of that I'm on.* Stefan's account of the ledgers. Jovan, fourteen years old, describing the bridge patrol rotations with a precision the committee men couldn't match. And at the foot of the colophon, small and black and permanent:

*Vidin. Printed by Ivo Delacroix.*

I touched the wet ink and it came away on my finger, and I did not wipe it off.

* * *

Near dawn, a boy came down from the watch post on the marsh road and said there were refugees coming in from the ford — a cart, a few women, a man limping. Routine now; the thaw moved people like it moved ice. Ruzica sent bread out. I went out too, because I always went out, because five years of watching the bridge from a shop window does not leave a person.

The cart came slow through the mist. In it, wrapped in a blanket, grey-faced and filthy, sat a man I knew.

Emil.

His wrists were raw. His coat was gone; someone had given him a peasant's jacket two sizes wrong. He saw me across the mud and did not wave, did not call out — just looked at me with the exhausted steadiness of a man who had bet everything on a page reaching the far bank and needed only to see it to rest.

Behind him walked Mara, carrying Jovan asleep against her shoulder, her hands bandaged, her chin up like a woman arriving rather than escaping.

"How?" I said, when they reached me.

Mara smiled with cracked lips. "Stefan got out in the burning. The clerk here—" she nodded at Emil— "told the tribunal the shop fire was his arrangement, alone. Signed a confession. They were going to hang him; the garrison collapsed faster than the paperwork." She shrugged. "So we came. The river goes both ways, Ivo. You should know that by now. You're the one who taught us."

Emil looked at the ink still on my finger, then at my face, and asked the only question that mattered: "Did it print whole? Names and all?"

"Names and all," I said. "Mine included."

He closed his eyes briefly, like a man setting down a weight he'd carried across a country. "Then arrest me or feed me," he said. "Either way, I'd like to see the forme."

And I understood, standing in the marsh mist with the press running behind me, that the fight hadn't ended. Draganic wanted my book for his flag. The governor's historians would rewrite their version within the year. Somewhere a committee was already deciding which testimonies were *useful*, and the word *useful* would follow me the rest of my life like a debt.

But I had a press now. And a trade. And people who crossed rivers for each other.

"Feed him," I told the boy with the mis-slung rifle, and turned back toward the sound of the machine.
