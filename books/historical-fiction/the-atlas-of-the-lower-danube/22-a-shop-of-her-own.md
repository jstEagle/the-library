## 22. A Shop of Her Own

The sign took three days to paint and one hour to hang.

I had wanted to do it alone. Jovan overruled me with the particular stubbornness of a boy who had spent a year learning that the world could be argued with, and Mara simply appeared on the step with a ladder as though ladders materialized wherever she decided they should be. Stefan held the bottom rung and offered opinions about the angle in the voice of a man who had been imprisoned for six weeks and had sworn, on his release, never again to waste a single opinion.

*Higher,* he said. *To the left. No — my left.*

*I know which left you mean,* I said, and moved it anyway, because after everything, arguing about a sign was a luxury I intended to enjoy.

The letters were mine. Not just painted by me — designed by me, cut by me, cast in type I had poured myself over a borrowed fire through the winter, because the press we had was old and its founts incomplete and because there are some things a person should make rather than inherit. The letters read:

**IVO DELACROIX — PRINTER**

Not *the Delacroix Shop*. Not *formerly of Vidin*. My name, whole, unhidden, at eye level where anyone crossing the square would have to look at it or deliberately look away.

Mara stood beside me while Jovan tested the bracket with both hands, hanging from it like an ape until she cuffed him down.

*Well,* she said. *Now everyone knows where to find you.*

*That's the idea.*

*Mm.* She looked at the name a moment longer than she needed to. In Vidin I had been nobody's business for five years; in this town — Novo Selo, half-burned, half-rebuilt, full enough of newcomers that no one's history was anyone else's — I had been nobody's business for exactly eleven months, and then, last Tuesday, I had nailed my own name above my own door like a confession.

*It doesn't frighten you,* she said. It wasn't quite a question.

I thought about lying to her, out of habit. We had long since stopped lying to each other, but the reflex lived in the hands before it died in the heart.

*It terrifies me,* I said. *Every morning when I unlock the door I think: today is the day someone remembers what printing cost us. And then I go in anyway.*

She nodded slowly, satisfied, the way she was satisfied by bread that had risen properly — not by the absence of difficulty but by the presence of something true underneath it.

*Good,* she said. *A person who isn't frightened has either forgotten or never understood. You've done neither. Come inside. The primer sheets are curling and your apprentice has inked his elbows again.*

***

The shop was smaller than my father's had been, and better.

I say that carefully, because for a long time saying anything good about my own work felt like a theft from him. His shop in Vidin had been a cathedral of sorts — high ceilings, the paper loft where a dying man had pressed a satchel into my arms, the hidden compartment behind the third shelf where forbidden type had slept through two regimes. This place was a converted granary with a crooked floor and a window that looked onto the river, and I had chosen every board of it myself, or rather Jovan and Stefan and I had argued about every board of it, and the result was imperfect and warm and smelled of ink and linseed and, on baking days, of Stefan's bread, because his ovens were two doors down and he refused to let a working day pass without delivering a loaf across my threshold whether I wanted it or not.

The press itself had come from the revolution's print house in the spring — a gift, though I had made them call it a trade, because I had set their manifesto pages for free and corrected their compositor's atrocious kerning, and I do not accept gifts from people whose kerning I have fixed.

They had wanted more than my labor, of course. That fight belonged to the other bank, to the first week after the crossing, when the committee men had spread my atlas on their table and begun, gently, reasonably, to explain how the testimonies would be folded into the greater narrative — how individual names complicated the message, how grief was most useful when it was general. I had stood in that office with river water still drying in the seams of my boots and told them that the names were not decoration. I had watched a man's face go carefully blank, the way faces went blank in Vidin whenever the governor's clerk entered a room, and I had understood that I was not among allies so much as among a different kind of authority, and that memory is fought for everywhere, always, against someone.

So I had taken the names back. All of them. And now, a year later, the second edition lay stacked on my bench in quires of sixteen — same testimonies, same maps in my father's hand reproduced by lithography, same casualty list Emil had stolen out of the governor's own records at a cost he was still paying — but with a new colophon, because a printer earns the right to revise her own work:

*Set and printed by Ivo Delacroix, Novo Selo, in the first year of freedom. The names stand as written.*

Jovan had asked me once why the phrase mattered so much, standing as written, when surely any testimony changed a little between the mouth and the page. He was fourteen now and thought like a proofreader, which I counted among my life's quiet victories.

*Because,* I told him, *every time someone smooths a story, they decide whose rough edges can be discarded. Usually the dead ones'. We don't get to discard them. They already paid.*

He'd chewed on that for a day and then come back with a marginal note for the primer instead, which was his way of saying he'd understood.

***

The primer was the thing I had not planned, the way the best things arrive.

In autumn the town had asked me to print notices — grain prices, council meetings, the schedule of the ferry that ran where the bridge used to be. Then the schoolmistress, a sharp-faced woman named Vukčević who had lost her schoolhouse to the same fires that took the rest of the riverside street, asked if I could produce reading sheets, because the children were learning their letters from torn almanacs and the backs of army proclamations, which she considered, rightly, an education in cynicism rather than literacy.

I set the first sheet myself: the alphabet, in letters large and clean, each character paired with a word. *A — ata, horse. B — brat, brother.* And then, because I could not help myself, because I am my father's daughter and my father hid truth in the shapes of ordinary letters, I chose the words the way he would have.

*R — reka, river. S — sloboda, freedom.*

Vukčević noticed. Of course she noticed; she was a teacher. She read the sheet twice, laid it flat on my bench, and looked at me over the top of it with an expression I recognized from mirrors — the look of a keeper of small dangerous things deciding whether to trust another keeper.

*Freedom,* she said. *For seven-year-olds.*

*It's a word,* I said. *They'll need it.*

*They'll need bread too, and you didn't put bread under B.*

*Bread is under Stefan's,* I said. *Two doors down.*

She laughed — one short bark, quickly mastered — and then she said, quietly, *Print forty more. And don't stop choosing the words.*

So the primer grew. A page of syllables. A page of numbers. A short account of how a town rebuilds, written plainly, with nothing smoothed. And on the last page, in letters a child could trace with a finger, the sentence I had argued with longest and finally kept:

*A name is the beginning of every story. Learn yours. Keep others'.*

Stefan's daughter Lili was the first pupil to read it aloud, standing in my shop on a Saturday with forty children crowded behind her, and when she finished, Jovan — who had helped set that page and knew every letter of it — clapped so hard he knocked a forme off the stone, and I spent ten minutes chasing loose type across the crooked floor while the children laughed, and I laughed too, loudly, undignified, visible.

There was a time when being seen laughing would have frightened me more than soldiers.

***

On the anniversary of the burning — we did not agree on a date; the town remembered it variously, and Mara said the disagreement was itself correct, since erasure begins with tidy chronologies — Emil's letter arrived.

He wrote from the prison outside Sofia, where he had been held since the summer of the crossing, when he had stood in front of the garrison patrol and given them a false description of a woman fleeing toward the water while I crossed the ice-melt with the atlas strapped against my ribs. They had eventually established what he had done. Establishing it had taken them a year, which was its own small testament to how thoroughly a careful man can teach an institution to distrust its own records.

His letters came maybe once a season. They were typed, on a machine he had apparently charmed out of his jailers, in the immaculate habits of a garrison clerk — even margins, no corrections — except that Emil could not entirely leave his old craft behind, and so each letter contained one deliberate error, planted where only a typesetter would find it, and the error spelled, across five letters now, a word I had not yet assembled and suspected I did not want to.

This letter was longer than the others. He wrote that a review commission was sitting on his case. He wrote that he did not expect leniency and had stopped asking for it, but that he had learned, in the cell, to distinguish punishment from consequence, and that consequence — living inside what one had done — was bearable, sometimes even clean. He wrote that a guard had smuggled him four pages, loose sheets from a copy of the atlas moving hand to hand through the prison as such things move, and that he had read his own falsified census line reproduced in it — *the household of the printer: amended* — and had wept, briefly, and without shame.

Then, at the end, the planted error. I found it on the third reading: in the middle of a sentence about the commission, an *i* where an *e* should be. *Prisiner.*

I sat with the loose type tray beside me — my new founts, my own letters — and picked out the characters one by one. P. R. I. S. O. N. E. R. No. I tried again, letting the misspelling lead. P-R-I-S-I-N-E-R gave nothing. But shift one letter back from each position —

R. E. L. E. A. S. E.

*Release.*

I sat very still in the smell of ink and bread-smoke, holding five letters' worth of a word a careful man had risked his neck to spell across a year, asking without asking, because even now he would not ask directly — Emil, who had falsified one line of a census and hated himself, who had turned, who had paid, who had taught me that forgiveness is a deliberate act and not a feeling, and who had never once presumed to request the act performed on him.

I got up, walked to the window, and looked at the river.

***

We gathered that evening in the shop — Mara, Jovan, Stefan, Lili doing homework at the corner table, Vukčević come to collect the week's primer sheets. Lamplight, tea going cold, the press a dark patient shape in the middle of the room.

I laid Emil's letter on the bench and told them what it spelled.

Stefan spoke first, as he always did, bluntly. *He's a collaborator.*

*He was,* I said.

*A collaborator is a collaborator. I sat in a cell because of men like him.*

*No,* Mara said, mildly, in the voice she used for ending arguments before they began. *You sat in a cell because a patrol needed someone to blame, and he took the blame off a girl carrying a hundred dead men's names across a thawing river. Sit down, Stefan.*

Stefan sat. He glowered at the letter, then at his tea, then at the letter again, and I saw the exact moment the glower failed — because Stefan had spent six weeks in that cell learning precisely what Emil had described, the difference between punishment and consequence, and no honest man who has learned that lesson wishes it on another.

Jovan, practical as ever: *Can we even reach him? A release requires papers. Petitions. Money for the commission clerks, probably.*

*Money,* Stefan muttered, *and signatures. Character references from respectable persons.*

*From printers,* Vukčević said, looking straight at me, *and schoolteachers, and bakers, and ferry-women. From a whole town that owes him.*

Silence, of the kind I had learned to read the way my father taught me to read type — not as absence but as composition, every element placed, every weight balanced. It was the silence of people realizing, together, what they had become: not survivors clustered around a press but a constituency, a body capable of signing its name.

*The second edition goes out next week anyway,* I said. *Two hundred copies, distributed the usual way — grain barges, laundry lines, coffins if it comes to that.* Mara snorted; the coffins had been her innovation and she remained proud of it. *Each copy carries the colophon. Set and printed by Ivo Delacroix, Novo Selo. If my name can travel that far, so can a petition.*

*And the primer?* Lili asked, not looking up from her homework, in the tone of a girl who had learned from watching us that the biggest decisions are discussed over ordinary tasks.

*The primer,* I said, *gets a new last page.*

***

I set it that night, alone, after they had gone.

This was not the old solitude. I want to be precise about that, because precision is the whole craft. Five years in Vidin I had worked alone because alone was safe, because invisible was alive, because my father's death had taught me that a visible woman is a woman in someone's ledger. Tonight I worked alone the way a hand works alone at the end of a day when everyone else has gone home — by choice, with the door unlocked, with Mara's shawl still over the chair and Jovan's ink-stained rag on the hook and tomorrow's bread already promised, all the evidence of belonging scattered around me like type in a case, each piece in its right compartment.

The new last page of the primer. Below the sentence about names, below *learn yours, keep others'*, I set a short paragraph in letters a child could sound out:

*Our friend is in prison for telling the truth. We are asking for him to be released. When he comes home, we will show him this page, and he will know that his name is kept too.*

And beneath it, the signature block, because a petition is a document and documents have conventions and I am a printer before I am anything sentimental:

*Signed, the undersigned.*

Then I began pulling the case. M-a-r-a. S-t-e-f-a-n. J-o-v-a-n. V-u-k-č-e-v-i-ć. L-i-l-i. I printed each name in turn on the proof press, a single proof sheet, and carried it around the neighborhood the next morning like a collection plate. Mara signed with the firm diagonal she used for ferry manifests. Stefan signed, grumbled, and signed again larger. Lili signed with a backward *s* and refused correction. By evening the sheet held thirty-one names, and I added my own last, at the bottom, in full: Ivo Delacroix, printer — the name I had hidden for five years and burned a shop to claim, now offered up as surety, publicly, for a man who had once been the enemy's pen.

Emil had taught me that forgiveness is a deliberate act, not a feeling. Very well. I had set the act in type. Type can be distributed.

***

The petition went south with the second edition, sewn into a grain barge's manifest. Whether it will succeed, I cannot tell you — commissions are slow, prisons slower, and freedom, we have all learned, is not an event but a practice, renewed daily or not at all.

But here is what I can tell you, what I know the way I know the weight of a well-set line.

On Sunday morning I opened the shop early, as I do now, and hung the sign straight — it still lists a degree to the left, and I have stopped fixing it, because Stefan says it gives the place character and because some imperfections are load-bearing. I lit the lamp. I inked the press. On the stone lay the forme for the day's work: primer sheets, forty of them, each one carrying the alphabet and the river and the word for freedom and a child's sentence about keeping names.

Through the window the light came up over the water, the same water that had carried a courier and a satchel and a girl strapped to a bundle of paper, the same water that had frozen and broken and frozen again. Somewhere upstream was the burned lot where my father's shop had stood; I had not gone back and did not need to. Everything of his that mattered had crossed with me: the maps, the letters, the habit of hiding truth in plain shapes, and now, at last, the name, which he had given me and which I had finally learned to carry openly, the way you carry fire — carefully, but not fearfully.

Jovan arrived first, as always, smelling of the river, and took up his stick without being asked. Mara came with bread. Through the door, before the bell had finished its ring, came the first voices of the day — parents, children, the schoolmistress, a stranger asking after the atlas, everyone knowing now exactly where to find me, my name above my head in letters I had made myself.

I pulled the lever. The press bit the paper. Another page of the world, set and printed, signed.

Outside, on the road from the south, a figure was walking toward the town — thin, hatless, moving with the careful stride of a man relearning freedom after a year of cells. Too far off to be certain. Too familiar to be doubted.

Jovan saw him first. He looked at me, and I looked at the forme waiting on the stone, and reached for the type.
