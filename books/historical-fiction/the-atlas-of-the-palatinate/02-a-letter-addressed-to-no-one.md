## 2. A Letter Addressed to No One

The letters came to Marta Vogel's flat on Kaiserstraße the way rain came to the Palatinate in autumn: steadily, from many directions, and with a persistence that suggested someone, somewhere, had ordered it.

Felix Dravenmoor collected them on Tuesdays and Fridays from the poste restante window at the Hauptpostamt, and on the other days they simply arrived—slipped under the door by hands he never saw, tucked into the lining of bread baskets left by neighbors who were not neighbors, folded once into the breast pockets of coats that hung too long on the rack by her door. He learned early not to ask. He learned even earlier that asking would have been pointless, because Marta would only have looked at him with those grey eyes of hers, the color of the Rhine in February, and said nothing at all, which was her way of answering everything.

He was, after all, only the driver.

That phrase had become a kind of prayer for him, repeated so often it had worn smooth as a river stone. *Only the driver.* When the SA men lounging outside the Pfalztheater studied him too closely, he thought it. When Frau Habermann at the corner shop asked why a man like him—a grown man, capable, unmarried—spent his days ferrying an old woman about town, he thought it. When he lay awake in his narrow room above the garage on Pariserstraße and listened to the boots passing below, he thought it until sleep took him like a trapdoor opening.

A man who is only the driver is a piece of furniture with an engine. Furniture is not arrested. Furniture is not interrogated against a wall in some cellar off the Eisenbahnstraße while a man with a soft voice asks questions in a soft tone that makes you long to confess things you haven't done. Furniture survives. Felix had built his whole life around the architecture of surviving—thirty-one years old, unremarkable face, unremarkable voice, a talent for being overlooked that he had refined the way other men refined their handwriting.

There was only one crack in the architecture, one place where the wall of his invisibility had been mortared badly, and it was this:

He read the letters.

Not all of them. Not at first. It had begun innocently enough, the way most corruptions begin—with steam. One evening in March, waiting in the kitchen of Marta's flat while she argued on the telephone in the next room, he had set a kettle on for tea and noticed a letter lying on the table, its flap sealed but not glued down properly, curling at one corner like a leaf deciding whether to fall. And the kettle breathed beside it, and the steam rose, and the flap lifted like something waking.

He told himself afterward that he had only smoothed it back down. That was true, in a way. He did smooth it back down. But between the lifting and the smoothing there had been perhaps four seconds, and in those four seconds he had read three lines about a printing press in Neustadt that "no longer required oil," and something in him had shifted the way a foundation shifts—imperceptibly, permanently, and with consequences no one notices until the walls begin to lean.

After that, he could not stop.

He developed a method, because a method made it feel less like theft. On Friday evenings, when Marta went to her sister-in-law's in Otterbach and always stayed past dark, Felix sat alone in her kitchen with the kettle and a stack of correspondence, and he steamed each envelope open with the tenderness of a man bathing a child, read what there was to read, dried the paper over the cooling stove, pressed the flaps shut with a wooden roller he'd bought for the purpose, and returned everything exactly where it had lain. No one ever knew. No one ever suspected. The driver was furniture, and furniture does not read.

What he read was mostly dull, which he counted as a mercy. Inquiries about subscriptions to a literary journal that may or may not have existed. Notes about a cousin in Mannheim whose health improved, then declined, then improved again across the space of two years, which should have struck Felix as medically improbable but which he understood, in the wordless way one understands weather, to mean something other than health. Lists of names that were never called names—*Frau Blau, Herr Kessler, die kleine Anna*—attached to addresses and dates and small sums of money, as if Marta ran a lending library for lives.

And once a month, without fail, a letter in a hand he came to know better than his own: upright, severe, the ink always violet, signed only with the initial **R**.

Those letters frightened him, and he read them anyway. That was the thing about himself Felix could not explain, even to himself in the dark: fear did not turn him away. Fear turned him toward. He had spent his life standing at windows watching things happen to other people—the marches, the bonfires, the Jewish families moving out of their houses with their furniture wrapped in sacking—and he had told himself that watching was a kind of innocence. But the letters taught him otherwise. The letters taught him that watching was simply participation at a distance, and distance, he was beginning to understand, was a currency that could be devalued overnight.

On the first Friday of October 1938, the fog came up out of the Rhine valley and lay over Kaiserslautern like a wet wool blanket, and Marta put on her good coat and her hat with the pheasant feather and announced that she would take supper at Otterbach and not expect the car before nine.

"Drive carefully," she said at the door, which she never said, and then she looked at him a moment longer than she ever looked at him, and the fog moved behind her in the streetlamp light like something alive. Then she was gone, heels striking the cobbles, the feather trembling, and Felix stood in the doorway feeling—for the first time in five years of service—as though he had been dismissed from more than an evening.

* * *

The kitchen smelled of yesterday's coffee and today's coal. He filled the kettle out of habit before he remembered to be ashamed of it, and then, because shame had never once stopped him, he lit the burner and waited for the water to speak.

There were seven envelopes that night. Six were ordinary—violet ink, brown ink, one written in pencil on the back of a calendar page for August 1936, as though paper itself had begun to be rationed along with everything else. He read them, dried them, rolled them, restored them. The sixth concerned the cousin in Mannheim, whose health had taken a decisive turn for the worse; the address given was a school for the deaf, which Felix filed away among the small absurdities he no longer questioned.

The seventh envelope was different.

He knew it was different before he touched it. The paper was heavier than the rest, cream-colored, the kind of stock that came from stationers rather than newsagents, and it bore no stamp, no postmark, no address at all. Only his own name—no. He blinked. He looked again. Not his name. A name very nearly his shape: *F. Dravenmohr*, spelled wrong, delivered by hand to a flat where the driver did not live.

His heart performed a small, treacherous maneuver.

He turned the envelope over. Nothing on the back. He held it up to the lamp, and through the cream paper he could see the shadow of writing inside, dense, close-packed, the script of someone who feared running out of page. There was no seal to break—the flap was merely folded in, the way one folds a note meant to be passed quickly, palm to palm, in a corridor.

He unfolded it.

The handwriting inside was not the violet hand of R. It was smaller, hurried, slanting leftward as though the writer had been leaning away from the words even while writing them. The ink was cheap and grey-brown, iron-gall gone thin, and in two places the pen had torn the paper slightly, leaving tiny wounds in the surface.

*To whoever keeps this house on Fridays—*

Felix felt the room tilt gently, like a tram taking a curve too fast.

*You will forgive the absence of a name. Names are dangerous now, and besides, I do not know yours, though I am told you are reliable, which in this year is the highest compliment anyone can pay a stranger.*

*I write about a child.*

*A girl, seven years old this September, dark-haired, small for her age, quiet the way children are quiet when they have already learned that noise costs something. She speaks German and a little French. She is not ill. She is not marked in any way that shows. Her people cannot keep her—you will understand that I cannot say more than that, and you will understand also that I have already said too much.*

*She must be placed somewhere safe before the month is out. Somewhere ordinary. Somewhere no one looks. I am told there is such a place—where the driver sleeps.*

*Forget her face. This is not cruelty; it is instruction. What is remembered can be demanded. What is remembered can be tortured out of a man in an afternoon. If you never learn her face, you can never give it up, and neither can she be found through you.*

*Burn this letter after reading.*

*There will be no second letter. There will be no confirmation. If arrangements fail, we will know by the silence, and silence, this autumn, is the loudest sound in Germany.*

*—A friend of Frau Vogel, and therefore of no one*

Felix read it twice. Three times. The kettle had long since boiled itself hoarse and clicked off, and the kitchen had gone cold around him except for the small circle of lamplight where the letter lay, and still he stood there, reading, as though the words might rearrange themselves into sense if given enough attempts.

*Where the driver sleeps.*

His room above the garage on Pariserstraße. Four meters by five. A cot, a stove, a washstand, a photograph of his mother that he could no longer quite recall arranging to have taken. He slept there six nights out of seven. Anyone in Marta's orbit—or in R.'s orbit, which was wider and darker than any orbit ought to be—could know that. Could say those four words to a stranger in a letter and mean, precisely, the flat-topped roof of the garage, the stair with the loose fourth step, the door that stuck in damp weather.

Which meant that somewhere in the machinery of Marta's world—machinery he had believed himself to be merely observing, merely adjacent to, merely *near*—someone had discussed him. Had weighed him. Had pronounced him *reliable*. His invisibility, the great achievement of his life, had apparently been assessed, certified, and entered into evidence.

He should have burned the letter. The letter said to burn it, and burning it was the single most sensible act available to him, the act that would restore the architecture, reseal the crack, return him to the warm dark of being nobody.

Instead he sat down at Marta's table, in Marta's chair, in Marta's kitchen, and he did the thing he always did with information he should not possess.

He memorized it.

Word by word, line by line, the slanting hand, the torn places, the phrase *quiet the way children are quiet when they have already learned that noise costs something*—which struck him, absurdly, as the finest sentence he had read in years, and he had read Goethe in the Realschule and been praised for his taste. He memorized it because burning felt like murder and keeping felt like theft, and memory, he reasoned, was at least a crime committed entirely within himself, harming no one, implicating no paper, leaving no ash.

Then he burned the letter anyway.

It caught quickly, cream paper going brown at the edges, then black, then rising off the grate in flakes of grey that drifted toward the ceiling and dissolved. He watched it go with the peculiar grief of a man watching a bridge destroyed behind him. When it was done he stirred the ashes with a poker until they were indistinguishable from the ordinary dust of an ordinary kitchen, and he washed his hands, and he stood at the window looking out into the fog of Kaiserstraße, where the gas lamps made soft orange islands and nothing moved except the fog itself.

*Forget her face,* the letter had said.

But here was the flaw in the instruction, the flaw he discovered only now, standing in the lamplight with cold hands: one cannot forget a face one has never been shown, and yet the letter had described her anyway. Dark hair. Small for her age. Seven years old. Quiet. Already, without meaning to, without wanting to, Felix Dravenmoor had assembled her—had built her out of sentences the way a boy builds a ship out of matchsticks, complete and entire, sailing already in some interior sea he had not known he possessed. He could see her. Standing in a doorway. Waiting. Being handed to someone with instructions not to look.

*Before the month is out.* October had eight days remaining.

He tried to dismiss it. That was the term he used with himself later, *dismissed*, as though his mind were a servant and the thought a beggar at the door. It was code, he told the beggar. Of course it was code. Everything in these letters was code; the cousin in Mannheim, the printing press needing no oil, the school for the deaf—all of it code, a whole country speaking to itself in metaphor because plain speech had become contraband. *A child* need not be a child. *Where the driver sleeps* need not be a room above a garage. Perhaps it was a cache. Perhaps a press. Perhaps a person—an adult, a courier, a typecaster named for convenience *she*. Cities full of underground presses called themselves daughters; men called themselves cousins; why should a child not be a revolver?

The reasoning was sound. He constructed it carefully, brick by brick, on the walk home through the fog, and it held his weight almost the whole way, right up until Pariserstraße, where the garage loomed out of the white murk with its one lit window on the top floor—his window—and beside the door, sheltering from nothing worse than the fog itself, small and dark-haired and still as furniture,

stood

a girl.

Seven years old, perhaps. Small for her age. Her coat was too big for her and her shoes were wet through and she was holding a folded piece of paper in both hands the way one holds something that has been placed there by a larger, disappearing hand. She looked up at him. She did not smile. She had already learned, evidently, whatever the letter had promised she had learned—that noise costs something—and so she said nothing at all, and the fog closed softly around the three of them: the girl, the note, and the man who was only the driver, standing on his own doorstep at the end of the eighth day of October, holding a key he suddenly could not remember how to use.
