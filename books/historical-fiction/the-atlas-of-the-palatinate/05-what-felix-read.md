## 5. What Felix Read

The confession began, as most sins do, in a small and comfortable way.

Felix told himself it was an accident, the first time. A letter had come unstuck in the damp of November, its flap lifting like a patient eyelid while he carried the satchel up the stairs of Marta's building on Pariserstraße, and he had seen — quite without meaning to — three lines of a woman's handwriting describing a meeting at a printing shop in Pirmasens. He had looked away. He had looked away for perhaps two full seconds, which was longer than innocence required.

That was how it started: with two seconds of looking. After that, looking became a habit the way a single drink becomes a bottle, so gradually that the man drinking never once feels himself become a drunkard.

By December he had developed a method. He was not proud of it; he was not even fully aware of it as a method until later, when he could no longer call it anything else. But there it was: a kettle in his rooms above the garage, steam held beneath a flap until the glue remembered it had once been water, a letter opened as softly as a door in a sickroom, read by the light of a lamp turned to face the wall, then sealed again with a thin stick of gum he kept wrapped in wax paper like something medicinal. The whole operation took four minutes. He timed himself. Four minutes was the length of time Marta Vogel spent arguing on the telephone, and during those arguments she never once thought about her driver, which was precisely what made him safe.

He learned things.

He learned that Frau Hildebrandt in Neustadt wrote to Marta every Tuesday in a hand that trembled worse in winter, and that her letters always contained one sentence about her son who had gone to Argentina and never written back, buried in the middle like a coin in dough. He learned that a man signing himself only *K.* wrote in numbers, columns of figures that meant nothing to Felix and everything, clearly, to someone else. He learned that Marta's sister in Zweibrücken begged her every month to stop, *Marta, please, think of your lungs, think of your heart, think of something other than your convictions for one hour of one day* — and that Marta answered these letters with cheerful lies about her health and her quiet life and her harmless interest in local history.

He learned that Marta Vogel was afraid. Not the way ordinary people were afraid — not of hunger or illness or being struck by a tram — but afraid in a way that had been folded into her so many times it had become part of her posture. She sat straighter than other women. She laughed louder than other women. And underneath both, the way a river runs under ice, she was terrified, and she got up every morning anyway and put on her gloves and went out into the city that frightened her and made it more frightened still.

Felix read all of this in the yellow lamplight above his garage, and he felt — this was the shameful part, the part he would carry like a stone in his shoe for years — he felt *included*. For twenty minutes a night, he was not a man who drove a car and opened doors and stood in the rain holding an umbrella at an angle calculated to keep himself dry without appearing to. For twenty minutes he was inside the war, or whatever you called the thing that had not yet been named a war, and the names in the letters knew him without knowing him, trusted him without trusting him, because their secrets passed through his hands like banknotes through a teller's fingers.

He was thirty-four years old and he had never mattered to anyone, and he had found a way to matter to everyone, and none of them would ever know.

It should have been enough. It was not enough, but he did not yet know that either.

* * *

The trouble with reading other people's lives is that eventually you learn the endings before they do.

In February there was a letter from K., the numbers-man, and this one was not numbers. It was prose, hurried prose, the handwriting slanting downhill like something fleeing, and Felix read it twice because the first time his eyes refused to believe what they were seeing. *They have someone inside. Not one of ours. Someone who carries. Watch the drivers.*

Watch the drivers.

Felix sat very still on his bed with the letter open on his knees and the lamp facing the wall, throwing his own shadow across the page, and he understood several things at once, the way one understands all the rooms of a house when the lights go on. He understood that somewhere in the Palatinate there was a Gestapo file growing thicker month by month, fed by a traitor whose name he did not know. He understood that the letters he steamed open in private were the same letters the traitor might be photographing in public. He understood that his four minutes a night were not a hobby anymore. They were intelligence. They were — God forgive him — *duty*, and duty was a word he had watched other men die of in 1916 and had sworn off forever.

And he understood, finally and completely, that Marta Vogel was going to be arrested, possibly soon, possibly because of a driver, possibly because of him.

He sealed the letter. His hands were steady, which surprised him; he had expected them to shake, and their steadiness felt obscene, like applauding at a funeral. Then he sat for a long time listening to the rain work at the gutters, and he asked himself the question he had spent seven months avoiding:

What are you going to do about it?

The honest answer was: nothing. The honest answer was that Felix Dravenmoor, bystander to history, survivor by profession, would drive Marta to her meetings and stand in the rain and keep his bland face pointed forward and let events arrange themselves around him the way weather arranges itself around a stone. That was who he was. That was who he had decided to be in the mud at Verdun when he learned that the men who volunteered died first and the men who hid lived to bury them.

But the letter lay on his knees saying *watch the drivers*, and his hands were the hands of a driver, and somewhere out there was a girl named Lena — no. Not yet. In February there was no Lena. In February there was only the letter, and the lamp against the wall, and the terrible new weight of knowledge, which is heavier than any cargo a car can hold.

He kept driving. He kept reading. And he began, without admitting it to himself, to memorize.

* * *

March came in cold and clean, with a wind off the Pfälzerwald that stripped the last leaves from the plane trees along Kaiserstraße. On the fourth of March, Marta asked him to wait outside the bookshop on Steinstraße for an hour, and Felix, who had waited outside a thousand shops for a thousand hours, waited — and used the hour to decide.

Not to confess. Confession was for churches, and he had not been inside one since his mother died. What he decided was smaller and stranger: he decided to give something back. If he was going to steal their secrets, he would at least return the ones that could kill them. He would become, in effect, a reader working for the people he read.

The opportunity presented itself that same evening, because opportunities, having waited years, tend to lose patience all at once.

Marta returned from the bookshop pale and moving quickly, and in the car she said nothing for the first kilometer, which was unlike her, and then said, "Frau Hildebrandt didn't come. She's missed two Tuesdays." Her voice was level, but Felix heard beneath it the river under the ice. "Her letters have stopped. Either she's ill, or—"

"Or she's been taken," Felix said.

The silence that followed lasted long enough for the car to pass the whole length of the Rummel, past the empty fairground where the carousel stood skeletoned under tarps. When Marta spoke again, her voice had changed. It was quieter and harder, like metal cooled suddenly in water. "You don't know anything about Frau Hildebrandt."

"No," Felix agreed. "I don't know anything about anyone. I just drive."

Another silence. Then Marta turned in her seat and looked at him — really looked, for the first time in two years of Thursdays — and Felix felt the look go through him like a bayonet through wet canvas, finding everything he'd hidden.

"Say that again," she said.

"I just drive, Frau Vogel."

"You just drive." She almost smiled. It was not a kind smile. "You just drive, Felix. And yet you knew Hildebrandt writes on Tuesdays. You knew her name without my ever saying it in the car. And just now, when I said 'either she's ill or,' you finished my sentence with the word I hadn't said, because you already knew it was true." She leaned back. Outside the window, the streetlamps of Kaiserslautern came on one by one, small orange suns rising over a city pretending to be asleep. "So. One of us is going to say it plainly, or we're going to spend the rest of this ride insulting each other with fiction. Which would you prefer?"

Felix drove. He drove well, smoothly, the way he did everything — and understood, watching his own hands on the wheel, that smoothness had been his whole life, and that it was ending at forty kilometers per hour on a March evening.

"There's a method," he said. "With steam. It takes four minutes. I've never told anyone and I've never lost a seal. I can show you on any letter you choose, and if the flap tears, you may dismiss me and report me and I won't argue."

He expected fury. He had rehearsed for fury the way soldiers rehearse for bombardment, braced and grim. What he got instead was laughter — short, sharp, genuinely astonished laughter that seemed to hurt her coming up.

"A reader," she said. "I've been paying a reader. Two years. Every letter I've ever sent has been through your eyes first." She shook her head slowly. "Do you know what I ought to do? Do you know what any sensible woman in my position would do?"

"Yes," Felix said.

"And yet." She was quiet for the length of an entire street, and when she spoke again the fury was still in her voice, but something else had arrived alongside it, something colder and more practical, the voice of a woman who had spent thirty years converting outrage into strategy. "Hildebrandt is in custody. I'm nearly certain. Which means within days, maybe hours, they'll know her handwriting, her contacts, her Tuesdays. I need to warn six people in three towns tonight, and every one of those warnings has to sound like a recipe or a shopping list, and I cannot write them myself because my handwriting is known too." She looked at him sideways. "Can you write a letter that sounds like nothing, Felix? Can you write a letter about weather and rheumatism and the price of potatoes that a stupid man reads and forgets and a clever man reads and understands?"

"I can try."

"Don't try. Do you know why I haven't dismissed you?" She turned fully toward him now, and the lamplight caught the grey in her hair and the fury still banked in her eyes, and beneath both — unmistakably, unmistakably — respect. "Because a spy would have lied better. A spy would have confessed sooner, with tears. You confessed like a man handing over stolen goods he's ashamed of, which is exactly what you are. Shame is verifiable, Felix. I can work with shame." She faced forward again. "From now on, you read everything. Deliberately. Twice. And you tell me anything that mentions drivers, or trains, or the initials F.D."

The car turned onto Pariserstraße. Somewhere ahead a dog was barking at nothing, and the wind had dropped, and the evening had gone strangely still, the stillness of a stage between scenes.

"One condition," Felix said, stopping the car.

"You're hardly in a position—"

"Never again do I learn something about you that you don't know I know." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "I spent two years invisible, Frau Vogel. I'm not asking to be important. I'm asking not to be furniture."

Marta studied him for a moment, and whatever she saw in his face made her nod once, briefly, the way officers nod. "Agreed. Furniture gets burned. Readers get used." She opened the door, then paused, half in and half out of the car, and said without turning around, in a voice much smaller than her public one: "Hildebrandt has a grandson. Nine years old. If she talked — when she talks — they'll come for the family connections first." 

She got out and walked to her door without waiting for him to open it, a thing she had never done before, and Felix sat in the idling car and understood that his invisibility was over, that shame had just been converted into purpose the way coal is converted into fire, and that the fire, once lit, would demand fuel he could not yet imagine.

He did not sleep that night. He sat at his table with a blank sheet of paper, practicing letters that sounded like nothing — weather, rheumatism, potatoes — while the wind worried the eaves, and it was not until dawn greyed the window that he noticed the second sheet beneath the first, the one he had covered with columns of figures in a hand not his own, practicing the numbers of K., the numbers-man whose letters had stopped arriving in February.

Whoever K. was, he was no longer writing to anyone.

Felix folded the practice sheets together and put them in the stove, and watched the flames take the false handwriting, and thought: *This is what it costs. This is the price of mattering.* And then, because the mind is a treacherous instrument even in its penitence, he thought of Frau Hildebrandt's trembling Tuesdays, and of the grandson, nine years old, whom he had never met and whose name he did not know —

and would not learn until October, when the child herself appeared on his step, out of the rain, carrying a note that instructed him to forget her face.
