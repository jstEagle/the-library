## 6. The Network of Small Hands

Lena had three secrets, and she kept them the way she kept her marbles: in a tin, under the bed, counted every night before sleep.

The first secret was that she could read a little. Not properly, not like Fräulein Brenner at school wanted, but enough. Letters were just shapes that had decided what they meant, and some of them were kind about it. An O was always an O. A T stood up straight and didn't apologize for it. Lena could pick words out of a page the way she picked currants out of a cake — one at a time, slowly, saving the best ones.

The second secret was that Herr Dravenmoor smelled like rain even when it hadn't rained. She had checked this on sunny days, standing close to him when he crouched to tie his shoe or hand her a boiled sweet from a paper twist, and it was true. Rain lived somewhere inside him, in his coat collar maybe, or behind his ears where the smell of skin went to hide. She thought this might mean he was sad. Grown-ups who smelled like weather were usually sad; she'd noticed it with her mother too, in the last weeks, when Mama had begun to smell of cold air and candle wax instead of lavender water, though the candles weren't lit yet and the window was shut.

She did not let herself think about Mama very long. Thinking about Mama was like holding ice. You could do it for a moment, and then your hands ached, and you put the ice down somewhere safe until you needed it again.

The third secret was the toys.

* * *

It began with the horse.

The wooden horse had come with her from Mannheim, wrapped in Mama's good scarf, and it was the only thing Lena owned that had never belonged to anyone else first. It was brown as chestnut skin and its mane was carved so fine that if you ran your thumbnail along it, it made a sound like a tiny zipper. Its legs came off. This was not brokenness, exactly — the left front leg unscrewed, and inside there was a hollow, which Mama had once said was where the maker hid his mistakes, and Lena had believed that for years. A hollow full of hidden mistakes. It seemed a very honest way to make a horse.

One grey Tuesday — she knew it was Tuesday because Tuesdays Herr Dravenmoor polished the car and swore at the fenders in a low voice, apologizing to God between swearwords — Lena came home from the widow's flat downstairs, where she was learning to knit badly, and found the horse sitting on Marta's desk with its leg off.

Not on the floor. Not in her room. On *Marta's* desk, next to the inkwell shaped like a little well, which it was, and the stack of papers that were never to be touched, which Lena never touched, except with her eyes.

A woman Lena didn't know was rolling a paper into a tube very thin, thin as a cigarette, thinner. Her fingers moved like she did this all day, every day, like her fingers dreamed about it at night. She licked the edge of the paper and wound it tight and looked at it against the lamp light the way Papa used to look at a drill bit, checking it was true.

"Is my horse sick?" Lena asked, because nobody had answered the door-question yet, which was: why is my horse here.

The woman laughed, short, through her nose. "Sick? No, Liebes. He's going traveling."

"He doesn't travel," Lena said. "He's been to Mannheim and back and he hated the train."

Something happened to the woman's face then, quick, like a cloud over water. She looked at Marta, who was standing by the window doing the still thing she did, arms crossed, listening to the street the way other people listened to music.

"Does she know anything?" the woman asked, and it wasn't unkind, but it wasn't kind either. It was the voice people used at the market when they asked if the fish was fresh.

"She knows how to keep her mouth shut better than half my couriers," Marta said, without turning around. "Give it back."

But they didn't give it back right away. First the woman slid the paper tube into the hollow of the horse, into the place where the maker's mistakes were supposed to live, and screwed the leg back on, and held the horse up and shook it gently beside her ear, like Mama checking whether the sewing tin had anything rattling loose inside.

Then it was in Lena's hands again, warm from the lamp, heavier by almost nothing. By nothing at all. By a secret.

"You'll take him to Frau Habermann's tomorrow," Marta said, crouching down to her level, which she did only when she wanted something and wanted Lena to know she wanted it. Marta's eyes were brown and sharp as the points of scissors. "You'll walk the normal way. You'll stop at the baker's and buy rolls — here's the money — and you will complain about the price, loudly, because you're a child and children complain."

"I don't complain," Lena said.

"Perfect. Complain anyway." Marta pressed the coins into her palm and closed Lena's fingers over them, one by one, which made her feel like a small purse. "Frau Habermann will ask for the horse. You'll give it to her. That's all. Can you remember that?"

"Why does Frau Habermann want my horse?"

Marta's face did something complicated, the way weather does. "Because she loves horses."

That was a lie. Lena knew it was a lie the way she knew rain was coming, by a feeling behind her ears, in the place where smells went to hide. But it was also true that grown-ups' lies were sometimes doors, and behind the doors were rooms you weren't tall enough to see into yet, and the lying itself was a kind of politeness, like saying the soup was delicious when it was mostly water. So Lena said, "All right," and took her horse home to the shelf above her bed, and lay awake a long time listening to it not-rattle, wondering what mistake the maker had hidden inside it now.

* * *

After the horse came the dolls.

There were four dolls, inherited in the general shipment of a childhood that wasn't hers originally — a china-headed one with painted lashes, two rag ones, and a soldier doll with a painted-on uniform and a stiff arm that saluted whether you wanted him to or not. The soldier had come from a flea market and Lena had bought him herself with a found pfennig because she felt sorry for him, saluting forever at nothing.

Herr Dravenmoor was the one who sat on the floor of her room one evening with the soldier across his knees, turning him over, frowning at the seam along his back.

"This stitching is new," he said. Not to Lena, exactly. To the room.

"Is it?"

"Mm." He worked a fingernail into the seam. Lena watched him with the particular patience of children watching adults discover things. Inside the soldier, where sawdust should have been, there was sawdust, and also a small oilcloth packet, flat as a communion wafer, which Herr Dravenmoor extracted and weighed in his palm without opening, and then pushed back in, and restitched with small neat stitches that were much better than the originals.

He did not explain. Lena did not ask. Between them there settled an agreement like a blanket: she would pretend not to have seen, and he would pretend she hadn't, and both pretences would be warm.

But after that, Lena started noticing. Noticing was free, and it was the one thing she was rich in.

She noticed that the rag dolls' bodies were stuffed unevenly now, lumpy in ways they hadn't been, and that when Marta's visitors came — and they came more often, quiet men with quiet shoes and women who laughed too brightly, the way people laugh in rooms where someone has just said something dangerous — one of the laughing women would sometimes hold a rag doll absently on her lap while they talked, squeezing it, and leave it slightly wrong-shaped when she went.

She noticed that her jacks — the little iron stars — were counted out and counted back after certain visits, and that there were more of them afterward than before, or fewer, and that nobody ever explained the arithmetic.

She noticed that Marta sewed. Marta did not seem like a person who would sew. Marta seemed like a person who would argue with the needle and win. But on Sunday evenings, when the light went bronze and the tram bells rang down Kaiserstraße, Marta sat with the curtain fabric and a needle in her teeth and stitched hems into which things disappeared — Lena watched a folded paper go into a hem the width of a finger and become simply hem, simply curtain, simply nothing — and Marta's mouth moved while she worked, counting stitches, or maybe praying, or possibly swearing in French, which Lena had heard her do once and been told never to repeat, and had not, except to the horse.

The toys were becoming a country. Lena was its map.

* * *

The worst day was the day of the tea set.

The tea set had been Mama's, real porcelain with painted violets, packed away since Mannheim, and one afternoon Lena came home and the box was open and Marta's friend — the quiet man with the quiet shoes — was wrapping each cup individually in paper. Not newspaper. Plain brown paper, no printing, the kind of paper that leaves no evidence.

"That's Mama's," Lena said, and her voice came out smaller than she meant it to, and angrier.

The man stopped wrapping. He looked at Marta, who had come to the doorway, and there was a whole conversation between them that took no time at all and said everything, the way grown-up conversations did, fast and silent as birds.

"Lena," Marta said.

"It's Mama's tea set. She got it for her wedding. There are violets on it. You can't wrap violets in brown paper, they'll smudge—"

"Lena." Marta crossed the room and knelt, actually knelt, on the floorboards, in her skirt, which she never did, and took Lena's hands, which were fists now, she discovered. Her own hands were being held by Marta Vogel's hands, which were strong and dry and trembled only slightly, at the very tips, like the surface of a cup of tea carried carefully across a room.

"Listen to me. Your mama's cups are going to take a journey. A little holiday. And they will come back."

"When?"

"Soon."

That was a lie. Lena's ears-hurting, rain-coming feeling told her it was a lie, and something else told her that Marta knew it was a lie, and that Marta was telling it anyway because it was the polite door, the soup-is-delicious door, and behind it was a room where the tea set was never coming back, where the tea set was carrying messages instead of tea, because porcelain traveled freely where paper could not, because nobody searched a child's mother's wedding cups, because —

Because Lena understood suddenly, all at once, the way you understand that the ice is melting, that the games had rules and the rules were these: everything soft and beloved and childish was a hiding place, and everything she loved could be turned into a courier, and the reason her toys were heavy by almost-nothing was that the world outside had gotten dangerous enough that grown men rolled papers thin as cigarettes and hid them inside a seven-year-old's horse.

She did not cry. She surprised herself by not crying. Instead she said, "Wrap them carefully. The violets smudge," and the quiet man nodded solemnly, and wrapped each cup like it was an egg, like it was a relic, like it was exactly what it was, which was Mama's, which was ammunition, which was both.

That night Lena took the horse down from the shelf and unscrewed the leg — she'd learned how by watching, children learn everything by watching, it is their whole education — and looked into the hollow. It was empty now; the paper had gone wherever papers went, into other hands, other houses, other hems. But she breathed into the hollow, into the maker's hiding place for mistakes, and whispered into it, quietly, the way you tell a wish to a well:

"I know."

Then she screwed the leg back on and put the horse on the shelf facing the wall, so he wouldn't have to watch.

* * *

Winter came early that year, or seemed to. Smoke stood up straight from chimneys like grey pencils writing on the sky, and the coal deliveries made the street smell of dust and dark, and Lena learned the names of all of Marta's visitors by their shoes. Quiet-shoes man. The laughing woman whose laughter changed pitch when the bell rang. A young one with bicycle clips who always brought apples and always ate none of them himself.

And she learned the routes. Children learn routes the way water learns downhill. To Frau Habermann's, past the bakery, complaining about prices. To the widow downstairs, carrying the knitting, which was worse every week because the widow taught her a new square each time and the squares multiplied. Once, to the stationer's, carrying a book she was told to read on the bench outside like any child waiting for anyone, and she read it, or pretended to, while the book's binding grew slowly warmer against her chest, or seemed to, though warmth cannot be a message, can it, warmth cannot carry anything except itself.

Except that was the whole point, she understood. That was the entire trick of it. Nothing carried anything except itself. A horse carried a horse. A cup carried a cup. A girl walking to buy rolls carried a girl buying rolls. The danger rode inside the ordinary the way the paper rode inside the horse, and the ordinary was her — her small hands, her braids, her loud complaints about bread — and so she, Lena, seven years old, late of Mannheim, was part of something that had no name that anyone would say aloud, and she kept its secrets in her tin under the bed along with her marbles:

Secret one: I can read.
Secret two: Herr Dravenmoor smells like rain.
Secret three: My toys are soldiers.

She counted them every night. All present. All accounted for.

And on a wet Thursday near the end of October, when the gutters ran loud and the lamps came on early and blurred, Herr Dravenmoor knocked at Marta's door with his hat in his hands and rain actually on him for once, real rain, visible, running off his shoulders onto the mat, and his face was the color of the brown wrapping paper, and he said words to Marta that Lena, curled in the window seat pretending to sleep, caught only in pieces:

"—left at my door. Last night. A note. It says—" his voice dropped below the gutter-sound, and rose again once, cracked, "—she says she never sent her, Marta. She swears it. Then who—"

And the room went very still, stiller than Marta's listening-still, a different still, a still with teeth in it, and Lena pressed her cheek to the cold glass and watched the rain write on Kaiserslautern and thought, with the strange calm of children who have already learned the world's arithmetic:

Someone else has hollow places too. Someone else is hiding something inside themselves that is heavy by almost nothing.

She hugged the horse tighter and did not turn him around to face the room.
