## 8. The Berth

The Quaker's office smelled of coal smoke and dried lavender, an unlikely marriage of smells that Felix would remember for the rest of his life, though he could not have said why on that grey Tuesday in November when he climbed three flights of stairs in a building on Mannheimer Straße and knocked twice, then once more, as the slip of paper had instructed.

The paper had come to him folded inside a delivery of typewriter ribbon — a brand Marta did not use, wrapped in brown paper addressed to a Herr Brenner who did not exist at the flat where Felix slept. He had stood in the entryway for a long moment with the package in his hands, feeling the small hard cylinder of it, understanding that somewhere between the shop and his door, someone had decided he was worth trusting with something heavier than ribbon.

Inside, in pencil, in a hand so faint it seemed apologetic: *Thursday. Third floor. Ask for Brother Albrecht. Say you have children who need passage.*

Children. Plural. He read it four times on the stairs.

* * *

Brother Albrecht was not what Felix expected, though he could not have said what he had expected — perhaps a man made entirely of gravity, all beard and solemnity, a walking sermon. Instead he found a small round man with wire spectacles and ink-stained fingers, wearing a cardigan that had been mended at both elbows with different wool, sitting behind a desk so cluttered it looked less like furniture than like a landscape — hills of correspondence, valleys of manifests, a paper mountain range held down at the peaks with chipped teacups.

"You're the driver," Albrecht said. Not a question. "Vogel's man."

"I'm nobody's man," Felix said, and heard how the words sounded even as they left him — defensive, thin, the reflex of a lifetime. "I drive for her."

"Mm." Albrecht took off his spectacles, breathed on them, polished them with a handkerchief that had once been white. "Sit. There's tea if you don't mind it being terrible. Everything here is terrible except the lists, and the lists are excellent."

Felix sat. The chair groaned under him with the particular complaint of furniture asked to hold secrets as well as weight. Through the window behind Albrecht's head, Kaiserslautern went about its morning — a woman shaking a rug from a balcony, the rhythmic whip-crack of it; two boys chasing a hoop down the cobbles, their laughter arriving a half-second after them, bright and obscene against the season; the gas lamp at the corner still burning at ten o'clock because no one had come to turn it off, its flame a pale ghost in the daylight that only Felix, it seemed, noticed.

"Say your piece," Albrecht said. "I have until noon, and at noon I am expected at a funeral I intend to be late for."

"The last transport out of Ludwigshafen."

"Yes."

"Three places."

Albrecht looked at him over the rims of his glasses for a long time, long enough that Felix counted the things in the room to keep from fidgeting: eleven teacups, a crucifix turned to face the wall — no, not a crucifix, a plain wooden cross, unadorned, Quaker plainness even in its symbols — a calendar from 1935 still hanging because the new one would show what the old one already knew.

"Three children?" Albrecht asked.

"One child." Felix's voice did something strange in his throat. "And two women."

* * *

He had rehearsed this on the walk over. He had rehearsed it driving Marta through the rain the evening before, her profile sharp against the fogged glass while she dictated a leaflet she pretended was a grocery list, and he had thought: *I will say it plainly. One child, two women. Three berths. That is all.* Plain things were safe. Plain things were the whole of his craft — the bland face, the forgettable shoulders, the sentence that carried nothing worth searching.

But sitting across from Albrecht, the arithmetic felt different. Heavier. Because saying it aloud made it real, and making it real meant he had chosen, and choosing was the one thing he had spent forty-one years avoiding.

"The child is seven," he said. "Her name is Lena. She has no papers that matter — which is to say, she has papers, and they are lies, and the lies are good ones but they are still lies. She was left at my door in October with a note telling me to forget her face."

"And have you?"

"No."

Albrecht wrote something. His pen moved quickly, decisively, the way pens move in rooms where decisions are made before committees can slow them down. "The first name is enough for the manifest. Children travel light, administratively speaking. It's the adults who need documents, sponsors, guarantees, signatures in triplicate from men in London who sign slowly because they believe the crisis has time to respect their calendars." He glanced up. "The women. Names?"

"One is Marta Vogel."

The pen stopped.

Felix watched the name land in the little room, watched it settle into the corners like dust disturbed from a sill. Albrecht set down his pen with exaggerated care, aligning it parallel to the desk's edge, and when he spoke his voice had dropped half a register, gone quiet the way rooms go quiet when they understand they may be listened to.

"Felix Dravenmoor," he said — and Felix started, because he had not given his name — "you are asking me to put Marta Vogel onto a Kindertransport manifest. A Kindertransport. For *children*. Do you know what happens to that manifest if it is inspected? Do you know what happens to the ship, the train, the other names on the list, the office, me?"

"Yes," Felix said.

"Then tell me why—"

"Because she'll die otherwise." It came out of him without permission, without polish, raw as a knuckle split on ice. "They have a list. They have her photograph. I've seen—" He stopped himself. Chapter nine of his life was not yet written and he could not speak of letters he should not have opened; some confessions kept their own counsel. "I know what's coming for her. Everyone in this city knows and pretends the knowing is weather. She won't leave on her own. She'd sooner stay and burn than run, and running is the only way she lives, and the only door out is a train full of children leaving from Platform Three in December, and I am asking you to smuggle a grown woman onto it under the nose of every officer between here and the Dutch border."

Silence. The gas lamp hissed faintly at the corner outside, or perhaps Felix imagined it, imagining everything now, hearing the whole city breathe.

Albrecht removed his spectacles again. It seemed to be what he did instead of praying. "There are precedents," he said slowly. "Escorts. Chaperones. A woman accompanying her ward — it has been done, rarely, when the sponsor documentation is strong and the woman's papers permit travel at all. Does hers?"

"They permit travel," Felix said. "What they don't permit is staying."

Something flickered across Albrecht's face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the kind of expression men of God develop when the world forces them to appreciate a bitter joke. He pulled a ledger toward him across the landscape of his desk, opened it to a page dense with columns, and wet his pen.

"Third place," he said. "You said one unassigned."

Felix's mouth went dry. This was the part he had not rehearsed, because he had not allowed himself to think it, because thinking it made the shape of it visible, and the shape of it was a question he could not yet answer.

"There's a third berth," he managed. "If there is one."

"There is one. Held against cancellations — families who say yes in September and no in November, when the father decides England is exile and the mother decides the father is right." Albrecht's pen hovered. "Who is it for? I need a name by departure. The manifest does not accept vacancies kindly; inspectors notice empty lines the way dogs notice fear."

"I don't know yet."

"That is not a name."

"It's the truth."

Albrecht studied him over the glasses a third time, and Felix understood that he was being measured — weighed on some scale the Quaker kept behind his eyes, calibrated by years of desperate visitors, tuned to detect the difference between men who lied and men who simply did not yet know their own minds. Apparently Felix passed, or failed in an interesting way, because Albrecht wrote something brief on a slip of paper and slid it across the desk.

"Departure is the fourteenth of December, Ludwigshafen, Platform Three, eight-forty in the morning. Not the twenty-first. The twenty-first is cancelled. If anyone tells you otherwise — anyone official, anyone unofficial, any voice on any telephone — do not believe it. The fourteenth is the last. After that, the border closes to us the way water closes over a stone." He tapped the slip. "This is your confirmation reference. Memorize it and burn it. Do not carry it. Men carrying paper look like men with reasons to be stopped, and stopped men talk, and talking men bury trains."

Felix took the slip. The numbers swam, then sharpened. He repeated them under his breath, twice, three times, sealing them into memory the way he sealed everything now — silently, secretly, in the vault behind his bland face.

"The third berth," Albrecht said, as Felix rose. "You have until the fourteenth. But I'll tell you what I've learned in this work, since you've come all this way and my tea is undrunk." He pushed a cup toward Felix — chipped, lukewarm, terrible-smelling. "The people who take a berth and wait to decide always imagine they're keeping their options open. They aren't. They're just suffering longer. Decide early, Herr Dravenmoor. Hope is heavy enough without carrying it undecided."

* * *

He walked back across the city without taking the tram, though the tram stop was closer and the wind had teeth in it. He needed the walking. He needed the cold to press against him like a hand checking whether he was still solid.

Three berths. The number sat in him like a swallowed stone, smooth and cold and undeniable. *Lena. Marta. And one.*

Lena's berth was settled — had been settled since October, since the moment he'd opened his door on the rain and found her standing in it with her single suitcase and her note, since the moment her chin came up in that stubborn way she had when she was frightened and refusing to admit it. Some choices were not choices. They were simply the truth arriving early.

Marta's berth was settled too, though she didn't know it yet, and telling her would be a battle. He could already hear her — *a train full of children, Felix, I am not a child, I am a weapon and weapons don't get evacuated, they get aimed* — and he would need every ounce of the blandness he possessed, every trick of logistics and paperwork and reasonable-sounding necessity, to maneuver her aboard. She would forgive him eventually. Or she wouldn't, and she'd be alive in London, furious, writing pamphlets in English with her terrible accent, and that outcome suited him fine.

Which left one.

Himself.

He stopped at the bridge over the Lauter, where the water ran thin and quick beneath the ice-fringed banks, and stood at the rail watching leaves — the last leaves, brown and curled, refugees themselves — ride the current toward wherever rivers went when they left home.

Here was the thing he had never said aloud, not to Marta, not to Lena, not to the mirror: he had built his whole life on the belief that he was replaceable. A driver could be replaced. A courier could be replaced. A man with a forgettable face was designed, by God or by his own long practice, to be swapped out without the machinery noticing. It had been his comfort and his armor — *no one needs me specifically, therefore no one can hurt me specifically* — and it had worked beautifully for forty-one years.

But a berth was not replaceable. A berth was specific. A seat on a train, a stamp on a manifest, a body in a space — these were the most specific things in the world. And there were exactly three, and there were — he counted on the cold rail, fingers touching iron — Lena, Marta, himself. Three and three. Clean arithmetic, clean as a solved equation.

Except that arithmetic assumed the world would stay still until the fourteenth of December, and the world had shown no appetite whatsoever for holding still.

And underneath the clean sum, in the margins where the true problems lived, other numbers scratched at him: the widow Frau Hessler whose letters he'd delivered for six years and whose husband had vanished into Dachau's ledgers; the printer's apprentice on Pariser Straße who'd winked at Lena in the market and hadn't appeared at his press in nine days; Marta's network, fraying strand by strand; the photograph he had seen and could not unsee. A city full of faces, each one certain it deserved the berth.

He pushed off from the rail and walked on. At the corner by the bakery the queue had formed already, silent, patient, breath rising from the line like the steam off some great wounded animal, and Felix joined the end of it because joining queues was what invisible men did, and stood among his neighbors thinking: *I have three berths and four weeks and one decision, and every person in this line would fight me for it if they knew, and none of them know, and that is the loneliest sentence ever constructed in any language.*

That night he cooked dinner for Lena — eggs, hoarded, a small extravagance he did not explain — and watched her eat cross-legged on the kitchen floor because she liked it there, near the warm stove, her doll propped beside her plate with its own imaginary portion. She chattered about school, about a girl named Ilse who could whistle through her teeth, about the cat on the roof across the street that she had named something absurd. And Felix listened, and laughed in the right places, and all the while the slip of paper burned to ash in the stove behind her, and the numbers turned over in him like stones in a riverbed: *three, three, three.*

After she was asleep, he sat at the table with Marta's latest letter — entrusted to him for delivery, as always, as innocently as ever — and held it under the kettle's steam, watching the flap lift like something breathing, and did not open it. For the first time since the habit began, he simply held it, seal loosening, contents waiting, and thought about Albrecht's voice: *Decide early. Hope is heavy enough without carrying it undecided.*

From the bedroom, Lena murmured in her sleep — a word he couldn't catch, maybe a name, maybe his.

Felix set the letter down, unopened, unread, and pressed both palms flat against the table as if steadying it against a tremor only he could feel.

Fourteen days later the train left. Before then, he would need a name for the third line.

He fell asleep in the chair with the lamp still burning, and dreamed of Platform Three, and in the dream the platform stretched away in every direction, endless, and every person in Kaiserslautern was standing on it, and there was only one train, and it was already moving.
