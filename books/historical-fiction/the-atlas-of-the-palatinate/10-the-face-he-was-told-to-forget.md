## 10. The Face He Was Told to Forget

The photograph arrived on a Tuesday, folded inside a letter that had no business being in Marta Vogel's post at all. It came in an envelope of cheap grey stock, the kind sold by the stationery shop on Eisenbahnstraße where the proprietor had begun, over the past year, to greet certain customers with a new softness in his voice—a softness that was not kindness but fear wearing kindness's coat. The letter was addressed to a name Felix did not recognize, and it should have gone straight into the fire unread. Instead it sat on his kitchen table through supper, and he sat across from it like a man sitting across from a doctor who has not yet spoken.

He opened it at half past nine, when the house above him—the widow Frick's house, where he rented the ground-floor room with its view of the courtyard and its smell of coal dust and boiled cabbage—had gone quiet except for the ticking of the stove.

Inside was exactly what he had feared and could not have named: a list. Not the whole list, only a fragment of it, a carbon copy passed hand to hand through channels that grew shorter and more desperate every week. Eleven names. Two addresses. And beneath the names, clipped to the page with a rusted pin, a photograph.

Marta Vogel stood on a street corner he knew—Kaiserstraße, outside the bakery that had closed when Herr Baumann fled to Strasbourg—and she was laughing. That was the terrible thing about it. Whoever had taken the picture had caught her mid-laugh, head thrown back slightly, one gloved hand raised as if hailing a tram that would never come, and the joy in her face was so naked, so unguarded, that looking at it felt like reading a letter she never meant him to see. Below the image someone had written in careful administrative script: *Vogel, Martha — Kaiserslautern — priority.*

*Priority.* The word had a bureaucratic neatness that made his stomach turn over. Not *wanted*. Not even *arrest*. *Priority*, as if she were a delivery, a consignment, something to be routed efficiently to its destination.

Felix sat for a long time with the photograph in his hands. The stove ticked. Somewhere above him the widow Frick shifted in her bed, the old springs sighing under her small weight, and he thought—not for the first time, but with new sharpness—about all the letters he had steamed open over the years, all the lives he had read the way a man reads a newspaper, comfortably, at arm's length. He had believed himself a bystander. He had built a whole philosophy out of it: *I drive; I deliver; I am the road, not the destination.* But here was the truth arriving late and ugly—that the road is not innocent. The road knows where everything goes. The road has carried every passenger to every door.

He fed the letter to the stove but kept the photograph, because keeping it felt like the only act of loyalty left to him. He hid it behind the loose brick where he kept his hoard—the three letters now, after the burning was yet to come, though he did not know that; for now there were dozens, dozens of other people's words pressed flat between the pages of a motoring atlas he never consulted because he already knew every road in the Palatinate by heart.

That night he dreamed of trains. He woke with Lena's name in his mouth, though in the dream she had been faceless, which frightened him more than any dream of Brownshirts ever had.

* * *

The berth papers were hidden in the flour tin, wrapped in oilcloth, and every morning he checked them the way other men checked their watches. Three berths on the Kindertransport leaving Ludwigshafen on the twenty-eighth. One for Lena. One for Marta—he had lied beautifully to the Quaker contact, a gentle grey-haired man named Aldous who asked no questions because questions had become dangerous currency—one unassigned, which Felix told himself was contingency, prudence, good logistics.

Lena had begun to grow used to the apartment above the widow Frick's stable. She had a corner now, a chair dragged from the kitchen with a cushion on it, and a shelf where she kept her few possessions arranged with the fierce precision of a child who has learned that belongings can be lost in a single afternoon: a wooden horse with one ear chipped, a hair ribbon the colour of dried cherries, a postcard of the cathedral in Speyer that she looked at sometimes the way adults look at photographs of the dead.

She was seven going on eight, and she had stopped asking when her mother was coming. That silence was worse than the questions had been.

On the evening the photograph burned itself into his memory, Lena was sitting in her corner drawing on the back of a delivery manifest Felix had brought home by accident—or not entirely by accident; he had begun bringing her paper, telling himself it was wasteful to burn clean paper, which was a lie, since paper was paper and he was simply a man unable to refuse a child anything. She drew houses. She drew a train. She drew a woman with wild hair holding up one arm, and Felix's chest went tight, because he recognized the gesture before he recognized the figure—it was the photograph, or rather it was the moment the photograph had stolen, some ordinary evening on Kaiserstraße when Marta Vogel had laughed at something and a camera, patient as a spider, had caught her.

"Who taught you to draw?" he asked, to have something to say.

"No one." She didn't look up. "You just make the lines go where the thing already is."

He thought about that for a long time afterward. *Make the lines go where the thing already is.* It seemed to him a description of everything he had done wrong and everything he might still do right.

* * *

It happened on a Sunday, in the grey hour between the end of the church bells and the lighting of lamps, when the city seemed to hold its breath. Felix was mending the latch on the stable door because the cold had swollen the wood, and Lena came down the stairs without her usual caution—she had learned caution early, the way children learn to swallow around lumps in their food—but tonight she came down fast, as if the thought in her head might escape her if she walked slowly.

"Felix."

No one had called him by his name in years. Customers said *driver*. Marta said *Dravenmoor* when she was angry and nothing at all when she wasn't. Hearing his own first name in the child's thin voice was like hearing a bell rung in an empty church.

"You're supposed to forget my face," she said. "The note said so. I know what it said. I can read now, mostly."

His hands went still on the latch. "Yes," he said carefully. "Something like that."

"But I changed my mind."

She stood very straight in the doorway, this small person in a borrowed coat two sizes too large, sleeves rolled four times at the wrist, chin lifted in a way that was pure Marta Vogel though neither of them would have said so aloud. Behind her the courtyard filled with blue dusk. A cat moved along the wall like spilled ink.

"It's a stupid instruction," she went on, with the merciless logic of children and philosophers. "If you forget my face, then how will you know me? On the train platform, in England, wherever. Anyone could be anyone. You need my face to find me again." She paused, and her voice dropped, and the ferocity cracked just enough to show what was underneath. "And if nobody remembers my face, then it's like I was never here. My mother says—" She stopped. Started again, smaller. "My mother said everyone gets forgotten twice. Once when they die, and once when the last person who remembers them dies. So you have to remember me. That's your job now. I'm giving it to you."

Felix set down the latch. His eyes were doing something traitorous, blurring, and he blinked hard and knelt so that they were level, so that her face filled his whole field of vision the way a face does only when you decide—consciously, deliberately, the way you memorize a number or a route—to look.

So he looked.

He took inventory the way he took inventory of roads: the gap between her front teeth that whistled faintly when she said her s's. The freckle at the corner of her left eyebrow, alone, like a period at the end of a sentence. The small white scar on her chin from a fall she'd never explained and he'd never asked about. Eyes the brown of river water in November—not dark brown, not light brown, but that moving, changeable brown that seemed to hold weather. The way her hair parted slightly off-centre because no one corrected it. The chipped tooth of the wooden horse echoed somehow in the chipped edge of her left thumbnail, bitten, bitten again.

"There," she said, satisfied, misreading his silence as compliance. "Now you have it."

"Lena." His voice came out rough. "I never forgot it. Not for one day. Not for one hour."

She studied him with those river-water eyes, and something in her face softened into an expression far too old for her, an expression he had seen once on Marta's face in the rearview mirror—the look of someone deciding whether to trust, and choosing yes against all evidence.

"Good," she said. "Because I'm going to be somebody important someday. Somebody has to remember that I started here, in a stable, with a driver." She considered. "A very boring driver."

"I'm not boring."

"You're extremely boring. That's why they let you drive everywhere. Nobody looks at boring people." She said it without cruelty, as simple fact, the way she might report that water was wet, and then she took his hand—her fingers cold, small, certain—and led him upstairs to show him her drawing of the train, which had acquired passengers, all of them smiling, none of them faceless.

* * *

He wrote to Aldous that same night, by lamplight, while the widow Frick's radio murmured upstairs—dance music, then a voice reading announcements in that tone that had grown steadily more liturgical, more funereal, over the autumn. He asked whether the berths could be confirmed, whether the date held, whether there was any word from Ludwigshafen. He sealed the letter and set it under the lamp to dry and sat looking at his own hands, which had delivered thousands of letters and read most of them and betrayed, in the end, only himself.

Because here was the arithmetic he was refusing to do, the sum sitting in the corner of his mind like a creditor: three berths. Lena, who must go—there was no question, no question at all, the child went, the child went, he would put her on that train with his own hands if it was the last thing he did. Marta, who was *priority*, whose photograph lived behind his brick, whose laugh a stranger's camera had already turned into evidence. And himself, Felix Dravenmoor, driver of cars, reader of letters, keeper of other people's secrets, a man so practiced at invisibility that even the Gestapo's lists had overlooked him—which was, he understood now, not safety but a kind of sentence. To be invisible forever. To be forgotten twice, both times while still alive.

Lena had given him a job. *Remember me.* And he had accepted it kneeling in a doorway, and acceptance, he was beginning to understand, was not a small thing. Acceptance was a door that, once opened, admitted everything else: that memory required survival; that survival required a body; that his body occupied a berth.

He got up and paced the narrow room. The stove had gone low. Through the window the courtyard was black, and beyond it, beyond the roofs, he imagined he could hear the freight yards of Ludwigshafen, the shunting of carriages, the future assembling itself rail by rail.

There were three berths and there were—his mind circled the number like a moth around flame, unable to land—three of them. Or were there? Was he a third life to be saved, or merely a third name to be crossed off? Marta would say he was being melodramatic. Marta would say it in that clipped voice that cut through self-pity like a blade through paper. But Marta did not know about the photograph. Marta did not know she was *priority*.

Tomorrow he would tell her. Tomorrow he would lay the carbon copy on her table and watch her read her own name in an enemy's handwriting, and whatever she decided—whether she raged or went silent or began immediately, furiously, to plan—he would stand beside her the way a road stands beside its traveller: useless, faithful, going wherever she went.

He blew out the lamp.

In the dark, behind the loose brick, the photograph waited with its stolen laughter, and upstairs the child slept, trusting her face to a boring man, and the train in Ludwigshafen stood in its siding with three empty seats, patient as fate, waiting to learn their names.
