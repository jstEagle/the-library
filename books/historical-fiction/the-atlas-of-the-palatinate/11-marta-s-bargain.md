## 11. Marta's Bargain

The rain had stopped by the time Marta Vogel reached the corner of Steinstraße, but the pavements still shone with it, black and slick as poured tar, holding the lamplight in long trembling ribbons. She walked without hurrying. A woman who hurries is a woman who is afraid, and a woman who is afraid is a woman being watched. So she strolled, chin up, handbag swinging at her wrist like any respectable widow out for an evening constitutional, while her heart counted doorways and her eyes counted shadows.

She had not been to this street in eleven years.

The house had changed. Of course it had. The chestnut tree in front of it was gone—cut down, she supposed, for firewood or for flags—and where its shade had lain across the second-floor windows there was now nothing but bare brick and glass reflecting back the grey evening. A swastika hung from the balcony rail, stiff as a dead bird in the windless air. But the iron railing around the little forecourt was the same railing; she would have known the cold of it anywhere, that particular chill that bit through glove leather in November and never quite let go. She had gripped that railing once, laughing, on a night when the world was younger and so were they both, and Heinrich Falkner had kissed her frost-bitten knuckles one by one and said, *Marta, you will be the death of my good sense.*

He had been right about that much. She wondered if he remembered saying it.

She pressed the bell.

Footsteps inside—heavy, unhurried, a man who no longer climbed stairs two at a time—and then the door opened and there he stood, filling the frame in his Party uniform, brown as autumn, the armband crisp white against the wool. Older. Grey at the temples now, thickened through the shoulders, the lean student she had loved transmuted into something solid and official. But the eyes had not changed. That was the obscenity of it. The eyes were still warm, still a little amused, still the color of strong tea held up to lamplight, and they went over her face the way a man reads a letter he has waited years to receive.

"Marta," he said. Not *Frau Vogel*. Not *Fräulein*. Just her name, spoken softly, as if the word itself were contraband.

"Herr Sturmführer," she said, and watched the warmth flicker, just once, like a lamp in a draft.

He stepped aside and let her in.

* * *

The parlor was exactly what she had expected and it disgusted her anyway: the leather chairs, the framed photograph of the Führer above the mantel where a mirror ought to hang, the sideboard with its decanters arranged like soldiers on parade. Everything orderly. Everything correct. A room with no dust and no history, a room that had been scoured of the boy who had once sat cross-legged on these very floorboards arguing with her about Ibsen until three in the morning while his mother banged on the ceiling.

He poured brandy without asking. Two glasses. He remembered how she took it—one finger of soda, no more—and set hers down within reach of her hand, and did not sit too near her on the sofa. All of it courteous. All of it deliberate. Marta understood that every courtesy was a move on a board, and she hated him a little for playing it well, and hated herself more for noticing that he still played it well.

"You've kept your figure," he said. "You've kept everything. You look—" He stopped himself, smiled, shook his head. "Eleven years, Marta. You might have written."

"You know why I didn't."

"Because you disapproved." He said it lightly, but the lightness had a wire in it. "Because I chose the wrong side of history, and Marta Vogel has never in her life been able to forgive a man for being wrong. Do you remember Vienna? You didn't speak to me for six weeks because I said Schnitzler was superior to Wedekind. Six weeks. Your poor husband used to beg me to apologize first, just to have peace in the house."

"Otto was wiser than both of us."

"He was," Heinrich agreed quietly, and for a moment neither of them touched their glasses. Otto, dead four winters now, buried from the Lutherkirche on a day of thin rain, and even then—she remembered it with a start—even then Heinrich had sent flowers. White lilies, no card. She had known the handwriting of the florist's bill. Some debts you do not acknowledge because acknowledging them makes them real.

"Why am I here?" she asked at last. "You know why I'm here. Let us not perform the whole opera."

"No," he said. "Let us not." He turned his glass slowly on his knee. "There's a list, Marta. You knew that already, or you wouldn't be sitting in my parlor smelling of rain and camphor. Names go on it every week. Most weeks yours isn't on it. This week it is."

The word *list* landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. She had known—of course she had known; she had felt the net drawing tighter all autumn, the arrests in Mannheim, the synagogue windows in Ludwigshafen, the way her own couriers had begun to flinch at uniformed men on corners—but knowing a thing in general is a different animal from hearing it pronounced in a warm room, over brandy, by a man who once loved you.

"How many names?"

"That isn't yours to ask."

"Then this conversation is shorter than I hoped." She made to rise. His hand caught her wrist—not hard, not cruelly, just enough pressure to say *stay*, and the familiarity of it, the terrible muscle-memory of it, nearly undid her. How many times had he taken her arm crossing a street in Vienna? How many times had she let him?

"Marta." His voice had lost its playfulness. "Listen to me before you walk out of here into a cell. There are perhaps forty people between Kaiserslautern and Ludwigshafen who print, carry, or shelter what you shelter. The list has twenty-two names on it already. It grows when we find evidence, and we are very close to finding evidence. Do you understand what I'm telling you? It is not you specifically. It is arithmetic. And you cannot argue with arithmetic; God knows you've tried often enough."

"I don't intend to argue." She freed her wrist, gently, and folded her hands in her lap like a woman at church. "I intend to bargain."

He laughed—a single short note, genuine, astonished. "Bargain. With me."

"With the office, if you prefer. But the office doesn't drink my brandy and remember my husband's funeral, so I'll bargain with you."

"And what," he said slowly, setting his glass aside, "could you possibly have that I want?"

It was the question she had rehearsed for three days, walking the long way home from the market, standing at her kitchen window watching the dark come down over the rooftops, and it had seemed almost bearable in rehearsal—clinical, transactional, a courier's exchange: this for that, terms stated, receipt signed. Now, sitting in the lamplight with his tea-colored eyes on her face, it curdled in her throat and she had to force it past her teeth like a bone too wide to swallow.

"Myself," she said. "My silence. My disappearance from your list. In exchange for the other twenty-one names staying where they are—for a delay. Weeks, not days. Time for certain... shipments to leave the province unmolested. Certain trains not to be searched at certain stations. You'll know which ones when the times come. I give you those dates, you keep your dogs leashed, and everyone walks away having gotten what they wanted."

"And what do I get besides dates?"

"You get me," she said again, and made herself hold his gaze while she said it, because looking away would make it worse and she refused to let it be worse. "Not a photograph in a file. Not a confession extracted in a cellar on Karolinenstraße—I know what happens in that cellar, Heinrich, don't insult us both. Me. Freely given. Which is worth more than anything your interrogators ever pulled out of anyone, and you know it, because you knew me when I was nineteen and gave myself freely and you have spent thirty years measuring every woman since against that memory. Don't lie to me. I can see it's true. It's written all over this ridiculous room—you built it to forget someone and it hasn't worked."

The silence stretched so long she could hear the clock in the hall, a fat tick-tock like a man walking upstairs very slowly. Heinrich's face had gone carefully blank, the official face, and she understood that behind the blankness a war was being fought that had nothing to do with lists or trains.

"You hate me," he said finally.

"I despise the uniform."

"But me."

Marta looked at him—at the grey temples and the strong hands and the boy still living somewhere behind the Party badge like a fly preserved in amber—and she told herself the truth, because she had always told herself the truth even when it cut: *I pity you, and I grieve for you, and I would burn this whole country down before I let it take one child off one train, and none of that is love.*

"I knew you once," she said. "That has to be enough for both of us."

He stood abruptly and walked to the window, his back to her, and for a long moment he simply breathed, and she watched the broad brown shoulders rise and fall and thought: *he is fifty-three years old and he is deciding whether to betray his oath or his heart, and either way he will despise himself tomorrow, and so will I, and that is the only honest thing left in this city tonight.*

"The trains," he said to the glass. "When?"

"When I know. I'll send word through the usual channel—the bookshop on Eisenbahnstraße, a card, no name. You'll have forty-eight hours' warning each time. That's the price of my discretion; I won't carry dates in writing longer than that, and neither will you."

"And after? When the war ends, whenever it ends, however it ends—if it ends the way your friends hope it does—what then, Marta? What am I, afterward?"

She rose. She took her coat from the chair back and put it on with steady hands, and only her voice betrayed her, coming out lower than she intended, roughened at the edges.

"Afterward," she said, "you will be the man who saved twenty-two lives and sold his soul for the memory of a woman who never really came back from Vienna. And some cold morning in a rebuilt city you will tell yourself it was worth it, and you will almost believe it, and I will be old somewhere far away and I will not believe it for you. That is afterward. That is all the afterward there is."

At the door she paused. She did not turn around.

"One condition, Heinrich. If it ever comes to it—if the list moves faster than our arrangement, if they come for the others before the trains run—you warn me. One card to the bookshop. Whatever it costs you. Promise me that."

Behind her, the clock walked its slow stairs. Then: "You have my word, Marta."

His word. She carried it out into the wet shining street like something breakable cupped in both hands, wondering how such a small, tarnished thing could suddenly weigh so much—wondering, too, as she turned toward home, why the night felt colder than it had an hour ago, and whether the driver would still be awake, and what she would possibly say to him if he asked her where she had been.
