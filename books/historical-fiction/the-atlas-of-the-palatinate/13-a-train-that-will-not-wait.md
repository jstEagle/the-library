## 13. A Train That Will Not Wait

The telephone rang at six minutes past seven in the morning, and Felix knew before he lifted the receiver that the world had changed shape overnight.

It was not the hour that told him—though the hour was wrong, the Quakers keeping office hours like a hymn, orderly and merciful—but the sound of it: three sharp bursts, no pause between them, as though the caller's hand were shaking too badly to respect the intervals. He stood in his narrow kitchen with the kettle just beginning to murmur and listened to the bell tear the morning open.

"Dravenmoor," he said.

"Felix." It was Herr Brandt from the Friends' committee, and his voice had been sanded down to something almost unrecognizable, the warmth scoured out of it. "The train has been moved forward. Saturday instead of Monday. The departure is at 4:40 in the morning from Ludwigshafen, Platform Three. You must have your people there by four o'clock. Not four-fifteen. Four."

Felix set the kettle down because his hands had begun to do something independent of him—a fine tremor, like a wire humming. "Saturday," he repeated. "That's—"

"Tomorrow. Yes." A pause on the line, filled with the sound of a man choosing which truth to say next. "There are soldiers at the border stations now. There were always going to be. But the lists—" Brandt stopped himself. "Come to the office if you can. Bring nothing written. Felix. Do you understand me? Nothing written."

He understood. He said that he understood. He hung up the receiver and stood for a long moment in the small gray light of his kitchen, watching steam rise off the kettle like a soul deciding whether to leave or stay.

Tomorrow. The word would not settle into meaning. Tomorrow was a thing that happened to other people; tomorrow was the day the milk went sour, the day Marta's shoes came back from the cobbler. Tomorrow could not be the day the last train left the Palatinate, the day the arithmetic of his life resolved itself into numbers he could not make come out even.

Because he had done the sums already, hadn't he? He had done them every night since the three tickets arrived in their plain brown envelope, folded around each other like sleeping birds. Three berths. He had counted them against the lives he was responsible for the way a miser counts coins in the dark, hoping the total will change if only he counts honestly enough.

Lena, who slept now in the back room with her shoes lined up beside the bed as neat as punctuation, who asked every evening whether the train would have a dining car, whether England had clouds, whether clouds in England were the same kind of cloud. Lena, who had asked him two weeks ago—God, only two weeks—to remember her face after all, reversing the instruction of that terrible note, and who watched him sometimes with an old soul's patience when she caught him looking at her, as if she understood that remembering was a labor he was still learning how to perform.

Marta, whose name was on a list now, whose photograph lived in a Gestapo file somewhere in this city, folded into a folder the color of dried blood. Marta, who had gone out the night before last and come back at two in the morning smelling faintly of cigar smoke and another man's cologne, and who had looked at Felix across the lamplit kitchen and said, in a voice scraped clean of everything, *Don't ask.* And he hadn't asked, because some questions, once answered, cannot be unheard, and because he loved her in the way one loves a country one can never live in—from the border, from the road, from behind the wheel.

And himself.

Three berths. Four lives. The sum refused to balance. He had turned it over and over in his mind like a stone with no smooth side, and every configuration ended the same way: someone stayed. Someone stood on the platform in the cold and watched the taillights shrink, and then walked back into a city filling up with men whose business was forgetting people permanently.

*You are replaceable,* Felix Dravenmoor told himself, the way he had told himself all his life, the litany worn smooth as a rosary bead. *You are the driver. You are the man who delivered silence. The world will not notice the absence of a man whose whole talent was never being noticed.*

But lately—and this was the new terror, worse than the Gestapo, worse than the lists—the litany had begun to ring false. Because Lena needed someone to remember her face. Because Marta, whatever she would never admit, slept better knowing the car waited below with its engine warm. Because a man who reads other people's letters for years learns, despite himself, that no life is small, that every ordinary door opens onto an interior as vast and furnished as any palace, and that to call oneself replaceable is to lie about everyone else too.

He poured the coffee he didn't want and drank it anyway, scalding, standing up, the way men drink when they are pretending the day is ordinary.

* * *

Marta was already awake when he came to Kaiserstraße, sitting at the kitchen table with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea she had not touched, staring at the wall where the map of the Palatinate used to hang before she burned it. The room felt denuded, scrubbed, a stage after the final performance. Even the smell of the place had changed—no more ink, no more the ghost of duplicating fluid that had clung to these walls for two years like a confession. Now it smelled only of cold ash and yesterday's bread.

"They moved it," Felix said. No preamble; she had trained that out of him months ago.

"How far?"

"Two days. Saturday. Four-forty in the morning."

Her hands tightened around the cup until the knuckles went white, and he saw—not for the first time—that Marta Vogel's body always knew the news before Marta Vogel did, that her fingers and shoulders received the world's blows a half-second before her mind consented to feel them. She had been a boxer once, she'd told him, in the way women weren't supposed to be anything once; maybe that was where she'd learned it.

"Two days." She laughed, one dry syllable with no humor in it anywhere. "They're hurrying us into our own rescue. That should tell you something about what's waiting if we miss it."

"Lena will be ready. She's packed. She's been packed for a week—she sleeps beside her suitcase like it's a stuffed animal."

Something moved through Marta's face then, quick as a fish under ice: tenderness, grief, guilt, all of them at once and none of them staying long enough to name. "And the third berth," she said, not looking at him. "Who holds the third ticket?"

"You know who holds the third ticket."

"I know what I've decided about the third ticket. I don't know what you've decided, and I've known you long enough to distrust your silences, Felix. They're the loudest part of you." She finally looked up, and her eyes were terrible and clear, the eyes of a woman who had spent thirty years making choices that cost more than she could pay and paying anyway. "So say it plainly. Who goes?"

"The child goes. That isn't a question; that's the ground we stand on." His voice surprised him with its firmness. "After that—"

"After that, arithmetic." She pushed the untouched tea away. "You were never a mathematician, Felix. You're a reader. And readers always believe there's one more page, one more letter, one more version of the story where everybody lives. There isn't. There's a train, and it leaves at 4:40, and there are exactly as many places on it as there are, and the war does not round up in anyone's favor." She stood, abruptly, restless as a caged hawk. "I need you to drive me to the north side. There are people who have to be warned tonight—all of them, tonight, before they read about it in the papers or don't read about it at all and simply disappear into the machinery. Two days isn't a reprieve. It's a deadline."

"Marta." He caught her sleeve, gently, the way one touches something that might startle. "The list. Your photograph. If the Gestapo has half of what the letter suggested—"

"Then every hour I spend being careful is an hour someone else spends unwarned." She pulled free, but her hand lingered a moment on his wrist, and for one suspended second the kitchen was very quiet, and Felix thought: *she is going to say it, she is finally going to say the thing that sits under all these years like groundwater.* 

What she said was: "Get your coat. The child stays here with the neighbor until we return. Lock the door behind her."

* * *

The city, driving through it that morning, seemed to Felix to be holding its breath.

Kaiserslautern in late autumn had always had a certain soot-and-stone dignity, the cathedral squatting over the rooftops like a fat old bishop at table, the market square loud with cabbage and argument. But today the streets had a rehearsed quality, as if the whole town were an actor who had been handed new pages overnight and hadn't learned its lines. Soldiers at the corner by the post office—he'd seen soldiers there a hundred times, but today they were checking papers, and the queue bent out into the road like a rope pulled taut. A furniture van outside the Rosenbergs' building, loading. The Rosenbergs had run the stationery shop for forty years; Felix had bought the very paper Marta typed her pamphlets on from old Rosenberg, who always slipped an extra ream into the parcel and never once asked why a woman needed so much paper.

Now the van was loaded, and the shutters were down, and a boy of perhaps sixteen stood on the pavement holding a birdcage with no bird in it, just standing there, holding it, as if the cage were the last thing he'd been permitted to keep.

"Don't look," Marta murmured, without turning her head.

"I'm not looking."

"You're always looking. It's your disease. Look with your peripheral vision, Felix—it's slower and it keeps you alive." Her voice was flat, professional, the courier's voice, but he heard underneath it the tremor she would deny to her grave. "Turn left here. We'll take the river road; there are fewer eyes."

He took the river road. The Rhine lay low and pewter-colored under a sky the texture of unwashed wool, barges crawling downstream like animals migrating ahead of a winter that everyone could feel coming in their teeth. On the far bank, smoke rose from the Ludwigshafen works, straight columns of it, unhurried, indifferent—the industry of a country preparing itself, in its deep industrial heart, for whatever was coming, the way a body prepares for fever without asking permission of the mind.

"Saturday," Marta said, watching the water. "Do you know what I was doing on a Saturday thirty years ago? Chaining myself to the railings in Munich. We thought we were so brave. We thought the century belonged to us." She smiled without joy. "We chained ourselves to things, Felix, and the things got taken away, and nobody ever bothered to unchain us. We just stayed chained to where the things used to be."

"That's not true."

"It's beautifully untrue. You've gotten good at that—untrue things, said kindly." But she reached over then, briefly, and pressed his hand on the gearstick, one hard pressure, there and gone, worth more than all the speeches of her suffragist years. "The Warnkes' house is the next lane. Then the Schmitts. Then we double back before dusk. Six houses, Felix. Six families who think they have until Monday."

* * *

He dropped her at the corner of Rittersstraße as the light was failing—the light failing earlier now, the days contracting like a dying animal—and drove home alone through streets where the lamps came on one by one, each one a small yellow assertion against the enormous dark.

Lena was waiting. Of course she was waiting; she had been waiting all day, and she met him at the door before he had his key out, her face lit with the fierce hope of the seven-year-old who has been promised a train.

"Is it Saturday? Did they say? Frau Neubauer wouldn't tell me, she just made me practice my new name." She recited it, solemn as a prayer: *"Lena Hartmann. Born in Zweibrücken. My parents are deceased and my aunt in London awaits me."* She had the accent almost right. She had practiced until the lie sat in her mouth like a stone she'd learned to carry. "Is it Saturday?"

"Yes." He knelt in the doorway, coat still buttoned, rain dripping off the brim of his hat onto the step between them. "Saturday. Very early. Before the sun. So tonight you sleep well, and tomorrow night you sleep well, and Saturday we drive to the station together in the dark, and you get on a big train, and it takes you to England, where the aunt who isn't really your aunt will meet you at the other end, and she will know your face, because I'm going to describe it to her in a letter so carefully that she couldn't mistake you for anyone else in the world."

Lena considered this with the terrifying thoroughness of children. Then: "Will you be on the train too?"

And there it was. The question, arriving not like a blow but like a key turning quietly in a lock he had been pretending wasn't in the door.

Felix opened his mouth. Closed it. In seven years of reading other people's letters—in seven years of steaming envelopes and learning the intimate cartography of strangers' hearts—he had never once been asked a question he could not answer. Lies were easy in writing; lies were easy on doorsteps to strangers. But this child looked at him with Marta's stubbornness and someone else's softness, and every lie he owned died in his throat.

"There are three tickets," he said at last, which was not an answer, which was arithmetic, which was the coward's version of the truth. "Three tickets and we'll see. Now—inside, wash your hands, dinner's in twenty minutes."

She went, obediently, but at the threshold she turned. "Frau Neubauer says grown-ups lie to children to be kind." Her voice was not accusatory. It was worse than accusatory—it was factual, observational, the tone of a small scientist recording data. "She says it's called mercy. I don't think it feels like mercy."

Then she was gone down the hall, and Felix stood in the doorway with the rain coming down behind him and the whole weight of the arithmetic settling onto his shoulders at last, no longer abstract, no longer a stone to be turned over in the dark but a fact with a face and a practiced false name and shoes lined up beside a bed.

Three berths. Four lives. Lena, who must go—there was no universe in which Lena stayed. Marta, whom the Gestapo wanted, whose photograph was already filed among the condemned, whose survival was the survival of everything she'd built, every pamphlet, every warned family, every chained-and-unchained year. And himself: Felix Dravenmoor, driver, reader, bystander, the man whose whole biography fit inside the pause between two sentences.

*You are replaceable.*

He hung up his wet coat. He lit the stove. He took down the brown envelope from behind the loose brick in the pantry—the three tickets, creased soft now from handling, from counting, from the nightly ritual of reassurance that had become a rite of torment—and laid them on the kitchen table under the lamp.

One ticket for Lena Hartmann, born in Zweibrücken.

One ticket for Anna Weber, the name Marta would travel under, God willing, if God still issued such mercies.

One ticket with a blank where a name should go. He had left it blank for weeks, superstitiously, as if writing his own name would fix fate like setting type. Now, under the lamp, with Lena's question hanging in the house like weather, he picked up the pen.

And the telephone rang again.

Three sharp bursts, no pause between them. Brandt's office number. At nine o'clock at night.

Felix crossed the room slowly, each step a decision, and lifted the receiver, and heard Brandt breathing before Brandt spoke, and the breathing told him everything before the words did.

"Felix," Brandt said. "There's been a development. You need to sit down."

Felix did not sit down.
