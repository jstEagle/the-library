## 16. Platform Three

The morning of the departure came down out of the sky like a verdict.

Felix had woken before the alarm clock, before the birds, before the milk cart's iron-rimmed wheels had begun their slow percussion against the cobblestones of Pariserstraße, and he had lain in the dark with his eyes open and understood that this was what a man felt like in the last hour of his old life: not afraid, exactly, but hollowed out, like a house whose furniture has already been carried away on someone else's cart. The arithmetic he had done at three o'clock was still there when the light came, patient as a debt collector. Three berths. Four lives. One train.

He dressed in the dark. He put on his good coat—the grey one, the one Marta once said made him look like a schoolmaster who had wandered into the wrong century—and in its inner pocket he placed two things: his own ticket, soft-cornered from handling, and the letter she had given him two nights ago with the words *Don't read this one, Felix. Promise me.* The envelope was heavy cream stock, sealed with a drop of red wax such as she hadn't used since the early days, when her letters were still innocent enough to be beautiful. It sat against his chest like a second heartbeat.

In the kitchen, Lena was already awake. She had not slept much; he could tell by the bruised shadows under her eyes, though she had arranged her face into an expression of tremendous practicality, the way children do when they have overheard adults saying things they were never meant to hear. Her one suitcase stood by the door—her real name stencilled nowhere on it, because it did not exist anymore. On the tag, in Felix's careful draftsman's hand, was written: *Lene Brandt. Ludwigshafen.* A girl who had never lived until Tuesday, and who would live forever starting tomorrow.

"Is it raining?" she asked.

"It's always raining," Felix said. "It's Kaiserslautern."

"I packed my hair ribbon." She said it the way other children might announce they had packed a lifeboat. "The blue one. In case."

"In case of what?"

"In case England has weather too."

He knelt and straightened her collar, which did not need straightening, and found that his hands wanted to stay there, resting on the small shoulders that had appeared on his doorstep eleven months ago with a note instructing him to forget her face. He had disobeyed that instruction so thoroughly that he now believed he would recognize those shoulders, that stubborn chin, that particular way of tilting her head when the world said something she intended to argue with, even if he lived to be ninety and lost every other memory he owned.

"Frau Vogel will meet us at the station," he said. "She's bringing the documents."

Lena nodded gravely. Then, in the small voice she used only for the largest questions: "You're coming all the way to the platform?"

"All the way to the platform," Felix said. And because it was nearly true, and because lying to her was the one cruelty he had no talent for, he let the rest stand unspoken between them like a wall neither of them would knock on.

* * *

Kaiserslautern station wore its Sunday best for the occasion: swastika bunting hung from the lamp brackets, stiff and dripping, and the great clock above the entrance had been polished by some conscientious employee until its face shone like a coin. Underneath the decoration the building smelled as it always had—coal smoke, wet stone, the sour sweetness of the buffet's coffee—but today the smell seemed to Felix like something from another country, another decade, a smell belonging to the boy he had been when stations were only places where trains went and came back.

They arrived at half past eight for a nine-fifteen departure. Marta was not yet there.

"She'll come," Lena said, watching the doors. She had taken up a sentry's position beside a pillar, suitcase at her feet like a small loyal dog. "She promised."

Marta Vogel did not make promises lightly, but when she made them she kept them the way rivers keep their course—with violence if necessary. Still, Felix found himself counting the minutes against the possibility that she would not come, that the Gestapo's list had caught up with her overnight, that the bargain she had struck with the man from the Party—a bargain she had described to him only in fragments, her voice flat as slate—had been called due ahead of schedule. Every uniformed figure that crossed the concourse made his stomach perform a slow, cold revolution.

At twenty past eight the doors opened and she came through them.

Even now, even knowing everything, some animal part of him lifted at the sight of her: the quick stride, the coat with the missing button she refused to replace because replacement was a concession to vanity, the grey streak in her dark hair that she had stopped dyeing the year the Republic died, as if the country's grief deserved company. She carried a leather satchel and walked as though the platform existed for her convenience rather than the Reichsbahn's.

"You look terrible," she said to Felix, by way of greeting.

"You look like a woman about to commit a crime."

"One of us should." Her eyes went to Lena, and everything in her face changed—not softened, exactly; Marta's face did not soften, it *concentrated*, the way light concentrates through glass. She crouched to the girl's level, heedless of the wet stone, and took both her hands. "Now you listen to me, Lene Brandt. That is your name. Not on the train, not in England, not ever again anything else. When they ask, you are going to an aunt in Manchester who makes hats. Your parents—" her voice held steady, and Felix knew what that steadiness cost, having heard it break once, only once, in a cellar full of burning paper— "your parents stayed behind to run the shop. You miss them, but you are brave, and English people admire bravery more than they admit."

"Yes, Frau Vogel."

"And you—" Marta looked up at him then, and whatever she saw in his face made her stop. She rose slowly. "Felix. What have you done?"

"Nothing. Everything. Come and I'll show you the carriage."

* * *

Platform three smelled of steam and iron and the particular anxiety of departing crowds. The Kindertransport children had been gathered into a loose herd near the second-class carriages, tagged and labelled like parcels of the heart, their mothers hovering at the painted line that marked how far love was permitted to go. Officials moved among them with clipboards. Somewhere a child was crying with the tireless rhythm of a pump, and somewhere else a mother was pretending not to hear it.

Felix checked the manifest with a guard whose boredom was total and merciful. Three names. *Brandt, Lene. Vogel, Marta. Dravenmoor, Felix.* The third ticket sat in his breast pocket like a coal.

He put Lena aboard first. He lifted her up the high step himself, feeling the whole slight weight of her pass through his hands—one moment of weight, and then weightlessness forever—and settled her by the window, wedging the suitcase onto the luggage rack as though its placement were the most important engineering problem of the age, because for one more minute it was.

"The ribbon," she reminded him.

"England will have weather," he agreed. "You'll want the ribbon."

She pressed her forehead briefly against the glass, leaving a small oval of fog, and inside the fog, for just a moment, the ghost of her breath. Then the officials began moving along the corridor calling names, and Felix stepped back down onto the platform, and the distance between the window and the ground was the longest distance he had ever measured.

Marta stood a few feet away with her satchel, watching the crowd, watching the entrances, watching everything except him. He had known her six years. He knew what it meant when she refused to look at a thing.

"Your turn," he said.

"Mm." She checked her watch. Checked the concourse. "Where is the man checking tickets?"

"Marta." He drew out the tickets. Two of them. He held hers out; she took it without looking. Then he held out his own. "Here."

"What am I supposed to do with that?"

"Board the train."

Something happened in her face then, very fast, the way weather happens in mountains—an entire storm crossing a valley in four seconds. "No."

"The berth is paid. The papers match. They expect a Dravenmoor on that manifest and they don't care which Dravenmoor—"

"Felix." Her voice dropped to the register she used for arguments she intended to win. "I know what you're doing. Stop it."

"I'm doing logistics."

"You are doing *something idiotic and sentimental*, and I have spent forty years being embarrassed by men who confuse the two—"

"They have my photograph," he said quietly. It wasn't entirely true. It also wasn't entirely false, and he watched the sentence do its work, watched her glance flick toward the concourse, toward the two plainclothes men who had been standing too long by the newspaper kiosk. "Not yours. Yours is outdated—they've been using the picture from '33, and you've changed your hair. Mine is current. Mine is *accurate*. If anyone on this platform is going to be stopped, it's me, and I can talk my way past a checkpoint—you've seen me do it. What I can't do is sit in England reading newspapers for six years wondering whether you got out. You can't ask me to."

"I'm not asking you to."

"No," Felix said. "That's the point. You'd never ask. So I'm asking for you."

For a moment she simply looked at him, and he understood that she was searching his face the way she searched confiscated letters—for the seam, the tell, the place where the lie showed through. Six years of steamed envelopes and resealed wax, and here at last was the letter he had written her with nothing hidden inside it, and she read it in perhaps three seconds, and he saw her understand it completely.

Her jaw set. "This isn't how it goes. This isn't the order of things. I'm fifty-three years old, I've buried a husband and a movement and most of my friends' futures, I have *earned*—"

"The train leaves in nine minutes," Felix said. "Argue on it."

"Felix—"

"Lena's watching," he said, which was cruel, and he knew it was cruel, and he did it anyway, because the war had taught him that mercy sometimes had to wear cruelty's coat to get through the door. "She's at the window. She can see us. Whatever we do in the next nine minutes is what she carries to Manchester. Decide which version she carries."

Marta closed her mouth. He watched her swallow whatever speech she had loaded—watched her, for the first time in six years, choose silence over victory. Her hand, when it closed around his sleeve, gripped hard enough to hurt, and he hoped it left a bruise. He hoped he would be able to find it later, in the days after the train, and press his thumb into it like a relic.

"If you follow," she said, low and rapid, "if you get on this train, I will never forgive you. Do you understand? I will spend the rest of my life making your existence inconvenient."

"That's the most romantic thing you've ever said to me."

"Don't." Her voice cracked on the single syllable, one hairline fracture in all that masonry, and then it was whole again, and she was Marta Vogel, and the train whistle blew down the platform like a long exhale. "Take care of—" She stopped. Corrected herself. "Take care of yourself. Read nothing addressed to you. It's safer."

"I promise," said Felix, who had never once kept that promise, and whose coat pocket held her unread letter against his heart like contraband.

She boarded. She did not look back at him—she looked forward, chin up, the way she had walked into every losing battle of her long obstinate life—and then she was at Lena's window, and the two of them were pressed together through the glass, foreheads almost touching, and Felix stood on the wet stones of platform three with his hands empty and his pockets empty and the strangest lightness rising through him, as though someone had finally set down a load he had forgotten he was carrying.

The whistle sounded again. Somewhere down the train a conductor shouted. The couplings took up the slack with a sound like a chain being drawn through the world, and the platform began, imperceptibly, to slide.

Lena's palm flattened against the window. Marta's hand covered it from inside, or perhaps it was the reverse; through the streaming glass he could not tell which hand was sheltering which. The girl's mouth formed words he could not hear, and he chose—freely, deliberately—to believe they were *in case of weather*.

Then the train took them, and platform three was only platform three again: wet stone, coal smoke, bunting hanging in the rain like weeds off a drowned ship.

Felix stood until the last carriage cleared the points and the rails sang themselves quiet. The plainclothes men by the kiosk had gone. The clock above the entrance said 9:17.

In his pocket, Marta's letter waited, unopened, warming slowly against his chest.

He turned toward the exit—and stopped.

Three men in leather coats stood at the barrier. Not moving. Not hurrying. Waiting with the terrible patience of men who had already read the manifest, and who knew that the name *Dravenmoor* had boarded a train to England, and who also knew—as Felix himself knew better than anyone alive—that the man walking toward them now was not the man the photograph described, and that photographs, unlike letters, cannot be steamed open and rewritten.

One of them removed his hat against the rain.

Felix Dravenmoor, keeper of other people's words, carrier of one letter he had promised not to read, put his hands in his empty pockets and went to meet them.
