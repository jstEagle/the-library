## 19. The Years Between

The war arrived in Kaiserslautern as a sound before it arrived as anything else — a voice, tinny and triumphant, leaking up through the floorboards from the widow's kitchen radio. Felix learned to read the war by its broadcasts the way sailors once read weather by the color of the sky. *Victory in Poland.* The floorboards hummed with brass bands. *Victory in France.* Church bells he was not permitted to ring were rung anyway, and the vibration came down through the joists like a fever passing into his bones. *Russia.* That one the widow turned down low, her hand trembling on the knob, though whether from fear of the news or fear of being caught listening, she never said.

He had been in her cellar for eleven days when the war began, and by the time it had been going long enough to have seasons, he had stopped counting days at all.

* * *

Her name was Anneliese Brandt, and she was sixty-three years old, a widow of the last war who had buried her husband at Verdun's edge and her only son to influenza two years after, and she lived alone in a narrow house on Pariserstraße with a cellar full of preserved pears, sauerkraut crocks, and now one fugitive. She had taken him in because — this was the part that undid him, that he returned to night after night like a tongue returning to a broken tooth — she had taken him in because he had once delivered her letters.

Not important letters. Nothing coded, nothing dangerous. Letters from her sister in Zweibrücken, four pages every Sunday, describing the price of butter and the health of hens and the slow collapse of her sister's memory into names and dates misplaced like buttons. Letters from a nephew in Mannheim. Once, years ago, a letter from a man whose handwriting shook so badly Felix had wondered if the writer wept while composing it, addressed to Frau Brandt and signed only *E.*, and which she had read standing in her doorway, then folded small and held against her chest for a long moment before going inside without closing the door behind her.

Felix had never opened those letters. He wanted her to understand this, in their first hours in the cellar, when she brought him a blanket that smelled of camphor and asked nothing about why he needed it. He told her anyway — told her he had been a reader of other people's mail, that he had steamed open envelopes for years, that he knew what he was.

She had looked at him for a long time across the single bulb's yellow light. Her face was all planes and patience, a face like a door left slightly open.

"I know what people are," she said finally. "I've outlived most of them." And then: "The pears are on the second shelf. Take what you need. Take less than you need."

* * *

The cellar was not large. A dirt-floored rectangle under the kitchen, low enough that Felix could touch the ceiling beams with both hands flat, cool in summer and cold past endurance in winter. There was a cot made from a door laid over two fruit crates. There was a bucket. There was a shelf of preserves that dwindled year by year like a calendar measured in glass. There was a ventilation grate near the ceiling through which he could see, at an oblique angle, a strip of Pariserstraße — cobblestones, the base of the linden across the street, and the feet of passersby, hundreds upon hundreds of feet, boots and heels and children's shoes, the whole city reduced to its walking.

He learned the city by its feet. He knew the policeman on the morning beat by the squeak of his left boot. He knew when the school let out by the stampede of small soles. He knew the day the Jewish family from the corner house was taken away because he heard no feet at all from that direction for three mornings running, and then heard different feet — heavier, official, unhurried — and then the sound of a hammer prying boards off windows.

That was the arithmetic of the years between: everything above him became sound, and every sound became knowledge, and every piece of knowledge was a small weight added to a scale he could do nothing but hold.

* * *

Anneliese came down twice a day, morning and evening, carrying a tray. At first they spoke little. She was careful — a woman living alone does not become careless at sixty-three and survive — so their conversations were brief, practical, conducted in the flat tones of neighbors discussing weather. But winter came, the first winter, and the trays stayed longer, and something shifted between them the way ice shifts on a river: slowly, invisibly, until one day the surface is moving.

It began with stories. Not hers — not yet. First she told him the news, whispered up through the grate or delivered down the stairs in fragments, and then, because the news was unbearable and brief, she began to pad it out. The neighbor's dog. The price of coal. The way the butcher had begun weighing with his thumb on the scale. Small treasons of gossip, harmless and human, and Felix drank them like water because in the cellar, the ordinary world above was more precious than gold.

Then, one evening in February — he remembered it because the pears were gone and there was only cabbage, boiled to translucence — she sat down on the crate beside his cot instead of standing, which she never did, and said:

"You'll think me foolish."

"I won't," Felix said. "I'm not in a position to think anyone foolish."

"My memory is going. It started with my sister — you'd know that if you'd truly read her letters, and I believe you did not, but I forgive you the confession. Now it's happening to me. Names slip. I stood in the market on Tuesday and could not remember the word for turnips. *Turnips*, Herr Dravenmoor. I stood there like an idiot with a basket and the word simply — gone, like a bird off a wire."

"So ask me for words," Felix said. "I have nothing here but time. Give me your forgetting, and I'll keep it for you."

She laughed — a dry, startled sound, the laugh of a woman who had not expected to laugh again — and then, because laughter opens doors that dignity keeps shut, she began to tell him things.

* * *

This is what Felix understood, in the way one understands things slowly and then all at once, the way one understands that a fever has broken: Anneliese Brandt was not telling him stories to pass the time. She was telling him stories to survive it. Every name she feared losing, she gave to him. Her husband, Karl, who had whistled Bach while cleaning his rifle and come home from Verdun with half his hearing and all of his gentleness. Her son, Jochen, dead at nineteen, who had kept caterpillars in matchboxes and cried when they emerged as moths because he'd grown fond of the caterpillars and felt the moths were strangers. The letter from *E.* — and here she paused, and Felix, who had spent a decade reading other people's secrets, looked carefully away and let her keep this one unopened even now — the letter from *E.*, whom she had loved in 1919 in a summer that smelled of lilacs and coal smoke, and who had written to her once more, decades later, and received no answer, and why she had not answered she did not say, and Felix did not ask.

"Say it back to me," she would say. "Jochen. Say it back."

"Jochen. Matchboxes. Moths. Nineteen."

"Karl."

"Karl. Bach. Half his hearing. All his gentleness."

And she would nod, satisfied, and climb the stairs, and Felix would lie in the dark repeating the inventory like a rosary — *Karl, Jochen, E., the lilac summer, the word for turnips* — because this too was resistance, this too was a kind of fighting. The regime above had lists and photographs and trains; it erased people with stamps and timetables. And down here in the dark, a man who had once been a bystander to history was keeping history alive one dead gardener's name at a time, and no one could burn it, and no one could confiscate it, and no list contained it.

Memory as the last unrationed goods. He hoarded it accordingly.

* * *

The years compressed themselves. That is the only honest way to describe them: not lived but endured, not passed but survived, like weather. 1940: the feet above grew fewer, the rations thinner; Anneliese began eating less so that he might eat, and he began leaving portions untouched so that she might eat, and each believed the other deceived, and both were right. 1941: the strip of street through his grate showed him uniforms of a new cut, French prisoners marched east in columns that moved with the shuffling patience of cattle. 1942: the radio above spoke of Stalingrad in a voice that had begun, very faintly, to fray at the edges, and Anneliese came down the stairs one evening and, before saying anything else, said "say it back" — and when he had recited Karl and Jochen and E. and the lilacs, she sat down heavily and said, "Good. Still here. We're still here."

1943, the bombs came to the Palatinate's edges, and once — once — to Kaiserslautern itself, and the house above him jumped on its foundations like a struck animal, and plaster sifted down onto his cot like grey snow, and Anneliese's footsteps crossed the ceiling overhead, quick and unhurt, and he lay beneath her listening to her walk from room to room counting her own survival, and he understood that he was praying for the first time since boyhood, though to whom he could not say.

She did not come down until nearly midnight. When she did, her hands were shaking so badly she spilled the water, and she knelt beside his cot — knelt, this proud, door-faced woman — and gripped his wrist with fingers like iron wire.

"Say them," she whispered.

"Karl. Jochen. E. The lilacs. The turnips. The moths."

"All of it. Everything. Say everything you have."

So he did. He lay in the dust and the dark with the city burning somewhere far off and recited her life back to her, all of it, every fragment she had entrusted to him across the years — the whistling husband, the moth-boy, the sister losing herself name by name, the summer that smelled of lilacs and coal smoke, the letter she had pressed to her chest without answering — and when he finished she was quiet a long time, and then she said:

"There. Whatever happens now, someone knows it happened. That's more than my sister got."

* * *

By 1944 he had memorized not only her dead but her living: the niece in Landau with the cleft chin, the lodger from before the war who owed her forty marks and had probably forgotten, the recipe for plum cake she dictated to him in October of every year because writing it down, she said, would make it real that no one would bake it. He had become an atlas of one small life, and he understood — with the strange clarity that the dark gives — that this was the work he had been training for all along. Not driving. Not reading stolen letters in the steam over a kettle. This: the keeping. The holding of what others cannot hold.

Some nights he allowed himself the other inventory — Lena's face, seven years old and rain-wet on his step; Marta's voice saying *a reader is useful*; the platform at Ludwigshafen; the lie he had told to make Marta board the train; the officers waiting beyond the barrier; her letter against his chest, unopened, surrendered. He rationed these memories too, more strictly than the pears. Too much of them and he would go mad with grief or hope, and both were dangerous in a cellar. He took them out like coins counted in the dark, touched each one, put them away.

But some nights the coins burned his fingers. Some nights he whispered *Lena* into the dark just to hear the shape of it, and the shape of it was the only prayer he had.

Above him, the war went on losing. He tracked its decline by the radio's voice, by the quality of Anneliese's silence, by the feet through the grate — fewer, faster, younger ones gone, old men and boys in uniforms too big. By the winter of 1944 the boots on Pariserstraße no longer squeaked in triumph. They hurried.

One evening in March 1945, Anneliese came down the stairs and did not sit on the crate. She stood in the middle of the cellar with the tray in her hands and her face lit strangely from within, and she said:

"The Americans are across the Rhine."

Felix rose from the cot. His legs, unpracticed after five years, nearly failed him.

"They'll be here in days. Perhaps a week." Her voice was steady but her eyes were not. "Herr Dravenmoor. Felix." She had never used his given name before. "Before they come — say it all back to me. One last time. All of it. I want to walk up my stairs whole."

So he began. Karl, and the whistling, and Verdun. Jochen and the matchboxes and the moths, and how he had cried at the metamorphosis. The sister in Zweibrücken, the hens, the butter prices, the names slipping like buttons. E., and the lilacs, and the unanswered letter — and here Anneliese closed her eyes, and Felix, keeper of other people's sealed things, kept hers sealed still. The plum cake, the cleft-chinned niece, the forty marks, the turnips, the moths, the lilacs, the coal-smoke summer.

When he finished, she opened her eyes, and they were wet, and she was smiling, and above them, faint and far off, he heard it for the first time in five years: artillery that was not German artillery. Liberation coming up the Rhine like a tide.

"Herr Dravenmoor," she said. "You will want to write letters now. I can see it in you. You'll want to find your women." She set the tray down and, for the first time, touched his cheek with her palm. "Write to everyone. Write to every address you ever read. Someone must answer."

Three weeks later, American boots — lighter, faster, foreign-sounding — came up Pariserstraße, and Anneliese Brandt climbed the stairs ahead of him, straight-backed, whole, every name intact, and unlocked her front door, and Felix Dravenmoor stepped out of the cellar blinking into a ruined spring, gaunt as a fence post, squinting at a sky he had seen only in strips for five years.

He had lost everything except the contents of his head. It was enough to begin with.

That same evening, sitting at her kitchen table for the first time, he took a sheet of her paper, dipped her pen, and wrote the first line of the first letter — *To whoever survives at this address* — and reached for the next sheet, already composing in his mind the addresses he remembered, dozens of them, hundreds, every envelope he had ever held, beginning with a name he had carried unopened in his chest for six years:

*Marta.*
