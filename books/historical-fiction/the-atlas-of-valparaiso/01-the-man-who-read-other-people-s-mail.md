## 1. The Man Who Read Other People's Mail

The fog came off the Pacific before dawn, the way it always did in July, and Silas Eriksen drove through it like a man wading through cold milk. The motorcar's lamps made two pale coins of light that bought him ten meters of road and no more. Behind him, Valparaíso slept in layers — the flat commercial sprawl along the harbor, then the hills rising in crooked terraces above it, cerros stacked on cerros, each one hung with houses the color of rust, mustard, faded rose, their windows dark, their laundry lines invisible until morning.

He had been driving for Ingrid Halvorsen since the spring of 1913, and in all that time he had never once been late. Punctuality was the only virtue he claimed openly. Everything else he practiced in secret.

The letters rode in a leather satchel on the seat beside him, forty-one envelopes bound in bundles of ten with butcher's twine. He had counted them twice at Mrs. Quintanilla's boarding house, where he rented a room on the third floor with a window that looked out on nothing but another wall — a wall the color of old teeth. Counting was also part of the ritual. Forty-one letters to be delivered today: to the schoolmistress on Cerro Concepción, the widow who ran the pension on Cerro Alegre, the Danish shopkeeper whose store smelled of cardamom and kerosene, the English clerk at the customs office who signed his name with a flourish he must have practiced in front of a mirror. Women, most of them. Women who wrote to Ingrid Halvorsen in handwriting that ranged from copperplate confidence to the cramped, apologetic scrawl of those who had learned to write in hiding.

Silas read them first.

Not all — not the ones sealed with wax or marked *privado* in a hand that meant business — but the ordinary ones, the ones closed with a lick and a press, he opened along the seam with a knife so fine the envelope never showed its wound, read standing at the kitchen table while Mrs. Quintanilla's stove ticked below, then resealed them with a cake of gum arabic he kept wrapped in oilcloth. He had gotten good at it. A year of practice, and his hands no longer shook.

He told himself it was harmless. He told himself it was practically a duty — a driver ought to know what he was carrying; if the police stopped the car, better he should know whether he held petitions or dynamite. But the truth was smaller and stranger than duty. The truth was that these letters were the warmest things in his life.

*Señora Halvorsen,* wrote a woman from Cerro Barón, *my husband says a woman who wants the vote wants the street. I want to tell you what I want is the ledger. For eleven years I have kept his accounts and he has kept my name out of them.*

Silas had copied that into his notebook the night before, by candlelight, in his small careful hand. He copied phrases the way other men collected stamps or cigarette cards — a line here, a paragraph there, anything that struck him with its particular weight. The notebook was black, cheap, its spine already cracking, and it held three years of other people's best sentences. A Chilean fisherman's wife on the death of her son at sea. A German merchant's complaint about the price of rope. An English governess describing the harbor fog as *a grey animal lying down on the city*. He knew the notebook by heart, and no living person knew it existed.

* * *

By seven the fog was lifting off the water in ragged sheets, and the harbor revealed itself: the masts, the cranes, the great hulls of the steamers lying against the wharves like sleeping animals, and beyond them, at anchor in the roads, the warships — two British, one German, riding at a distance from each other as careful as guests avoiding a quarrel at a dinner table. Everyone in Valparaíso watched the warships now. Everyone spoke of Sarajevo in the lowered voice reserved for illness and debt.

Ingrid Halvorsen came down the steps of her house on Cerro Bellavista at five past seven, exactly when she said she would, wearing a grey coat and carrying a leather case full of pamphlets. She was fifty-one years old and built like the mast of a ship, tall and straight and made to bear load. Her hair was going white at the temples in two clean wings. She did not greet Silas; she never did. She simply opened the door, settled herself, arranged the case on her knees, and said, "Cerro Concepción first. Then the pension on Alegre. Señora Vidal has new signatures."

"Señora Vidal," Silas repeated, and put the car into gear.

They climbed through streets too narrow for the automobile in places, streets where the cobblestones had been worn into long shallow waves by a century of hooves, and women were already out washing clothes in stone troughs, beating linen with paddles in a rhythm like slow gunfire. A dog trotted alongside them for two blocks with the solemnity of an official escort. Somewhere above, a piano played a waltz badly, stopping and starting, stopping and starting, the player fighting the same eight bars as though they owed her money.

"You've heard," Ingrid said. It was not a question.

"The banks are pulling their gold," Silas said. "Three ships have been chartered south. The German consul flew his flag at half-mast yesterday — for the Archduke, they say." He paused at a corner where two oxcarts contested the road, waited out the drivers' argument with the patience of a man who had spent his life waiting things out. "They say it will come to war."

"They say many things. What do you say?"

"I say the warships at anchor didn't come here to admire the view."

Ingrid made a sound that might have been agreement or dismissal. She took a letter from her case, unsealed it with her thumb, and read it again for perhaps the tenth time. Silas watched her in the mirror rather than directly — watching people directly was a habit he had trained out of himself years ago, the way one trains out of touching the face or clearing the throat. You could learn a great deal about anyone from a mirror. From mirrors he had learned that Ingrid Halvorsen read bad news twice, that her jaw tightened on the left side first, and that this morning's letter had done both.

"Norway will stay neutral," she said finally, mostly to herself. "Sweden will make noises. And every fool in Europe will march his neighbor's son into a trench." She folded the letter away. "The movement splits over it, you know. Half our women want to stop everything and march for peace. The other half think the vote can't wait for men to finish killing each other. Both halves are right. That's what makes it unbearable."

Silas said nothing. There was nothing in his opinion worth the cost of having one.

That was the arrangement between them, unspoken and absolute: he drove, he carried, he asked nothing and offered nothing, and in exchange she paid him fairly and never once, in eighteen months, asked him a personal question. He had gathered — from mirrors, from overheard telephone calls, from the way she pronounced certain words — that Ingrid Halvorsen understood perfectly well what kind of man preferred not to be asked. Perhaps she thought he was running from something. A wife. A crime. A name. She was wrong on all counts, but not far wrong in kind. He was running from nothing so dramatic as all that. He was running from being known, which is a slower and more patient pursuer than any of them.

* * *

He had come to Valparaíso in 1908 with a trunk, a seaman's discharge papers, and a sum of money that would have embarrassed a beggar. He had meant to stay six months. Norway had been cold in every sense; his father had died owing money to cousins; there had been nothing in Bergen that required his presence, which was, he had decided, the kindest thing anyone could say about a place. So he had followed the shipping routes south the way birds follow warmth, and Valparaíso had caught him the way the city caught everyone — with its hills and its harbor and its noise, thirty languages spoken within earshot of the fish market, whole streets where you could pass an hour without hearing Spanish.

It was a city of the disappeared and reappeared. Every second man had arrived under a name that was not quite his own or left under one. The Greeks ran cafés. The Germans ran import houses and the shooting club on Cerro Alegre. The English ran everything else and apologized for none of it. Italians, Croats, Basques, Peruvians, a colony of Syrians nobody could count properly — Valparaíso swallowed them all and asked no questions, because questions were bad for trade. A man could be invisible here the way he could be invisible nowhere else in the world, and Silas Eriksen had made himself a master of it. Six years, and the boarding-house landlady still called him "the Norwegian" to his face, as if Norwegian were a sufficient description of a human being.

And he had let her. That was the thing he did not look at directly, the thing he handled only in mirrors: he had let her, and everyone, because being called *the Norwegian* meant never being asked to be anything else.

The letters were how he touched the city without touching it. Through them he had attended hundreds of lives — births and debts and deaths, proposals accepted and refused, a woman in Playa Ancha who wrote every week to a sister in Antofagasta about nothing, absolutely nothing, the weather and the price of bread and a neighbor's parrot, and whose letters Silas read with more attention than he had ever given any living person. He knew the sister's parrot's name. He did not know the woman's face.

At the top of Cerro Concepción he parked below the blue house with the iron balcony, and Ingrid went up the steps alone, and he waited with the engine ticking and the fog burning off the bay, and below him the whole city lay spread out like a map of itself — the harbor, the mole, the ships, the smoke beginning to rise from a thousand chimneys in the hollows. It was beautiful. He had always found it beautiful, and he had never once felt that it belonged to him, nor he to it. He was the man behind the wheel, the man at the edge of the photograph, the man whose name the ink skipped over. He had believed for six years that this was safety. Some mornings, in the half-dark before the kettle boiled, he suspected it was something closer to erasure — a slow, voluntary vanishing, conducted one unread signature at a time.

But suspicion was a night animal. By daylight he had his route, his satchel, his notebook, and his ritual, and the day proceeded as days proceeded, one delivery after another, the world arriving to him in envelopes.

* * *

It was on Cerro Alegre, at the fourth stop, that the day broke from its pattern — though Silas did not know it yet, and if someone had told him, there in the bright middle of the morning, with Señora Vidal's new signatures safe in the case and the warships glittering far out in the roads, he would not have believed it.

Ingrid came back down the steps from the pension carrying not only her case but an expression Silas had never seen on her before. He had catalogued her expressions the way he catalogued everything: impatience, calculation, the tight smile she used on rivals, the real smile she almost never spent. This was none of them. She got into the car, sat very straight, and looked at him directly — not through the mirror, directly — for the first time in months.

"Silas," she said. "How long have you lived at Mrs. Quintanilla's?"

"Six years. Why?"

She was quiet for a moment. Down the hill, church bells began — noon, or close to it — and the sound rolled down through the terraces of the city like a warning passed hand to hand.

"Because there's a boy missing," she said. "A German child, seven years old, nephew to the consul himself. His mother hasn't been seen since Tuesday, and the family is searching the boarding houses tonight."
