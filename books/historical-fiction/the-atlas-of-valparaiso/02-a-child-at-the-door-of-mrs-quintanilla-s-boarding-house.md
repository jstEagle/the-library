## 2. A Child at the Door of Mrs. Quintanilla's Boarding House

The rain came up from the south that night the way it always did in June, riding a wind that smelled of kelp and coal smoke, and it turned Cerro Alegre's stairways into waterfalls and its lamplight into smudges of gold floating in the dark. Silas Eriksen walked home through it because he liked to walk, because the tram cost two centavos he preferred to keep, and because there was a particular pleasure — he had never admitted this even to himself, but it lived somewhere behind his ribs like a swallowed coin — in arriving wet and unremarkable at Mrs. Quintanilla's boarding house, one more drowned immigrant among thousands, indistinguishable, invisible, safe.

He had spent the evening driving Ingrid Halvorsen to a meeting of the sewing circle in Playa Ancha, where twelve women had argued about tariffs and the vote while rain hammered the shutters like a creditor at the door. He had carried eleven letters out of that house in his coat pocket, and he had read every one of them on the ride back down, standing under an awning on Calle Condell while Ingrid paid the livery fee, his lantern shielded by his body, his eyes moving over other people's lives the way a man's tongue moves over a chipped tooth.

*My husband says I may as well wish for wings,* wrote a woman in Barón, in pencil, in a hand that pressed hard enough to tear the paper. *I tell him I have already flown. He simply was not watching.*

Silas had copied it into his notebook before the match burned down to his fingers. He did not know yet what he would do with these stolen sentences. He suspected he would do nothing with them, ever. They were not for doing. They were for keeping, the way other men kept stamps or coins or the buttons off dead soldiers' coats.

By the time he climbed the last flight of stairs to the boarding house — forty-one steps, he had counted them once during a bout of fever, and now he could not stop counting them — the rain had worked through his coat and found the shirt beneath, and the city below him had become a smear of harbor lights, the masts of a dozen nations nodding in the swell like a congregation agreeing with something no one had said aloud.

That was when he saw the child.

The boy sat on the top step beneath the little iron porch roof, where the rain could not reach him, though it had clearly reached him earlier and thoroughly. He was perhaps seven years old, small for it, dressed in a wool sailor suit that had been fine once and was now soaked black from collar to knee. His hands were folded in his lap with a formality that made Silas's chest hurt — the hands of a child who had been told to sit still and be good, and who intended to obey even if obedience killed him. Beside him stood a carpetbag nearly as large as he was, and pinned to the bag's leather strap was a note, folded once, weighted against the wind by a flat gray stone.

The boy looked up at Silas without fear. That frightened Silas more than fear would have.

"Are you Mr. Eriksen?" the boy asked. His Spanish was precise and oddly accented, each word set down like cutlery on a table. "The Norwegian?"

"I am."

"She said you'd come after ten." The boy considered the sky, the rain, some private arithmetic. "It is ten past."

"Who said?" But Silas already knew, in the way one knows the shape of a thing before one turns on the light. He crouched, working the stone free, taking the note. The paper was good stock — consul's stationery weight, cream, heavy — and the ink had begun to bloom at the folds where the rain had gotten at it.

*To the man at this address.* No name. A woman's hand, slanted, hurried, the letters of someone educated who had learned to write fast and lie faster. *His name is Heinrich. His father is dead. His uncle cannot help him now. You will understand why when you read the name below.*

And then the name, written larger than the rest, pressed into the page as if she wanted to drive it through the paper into the wood of the door:

**WILHELM BRANDT.**

The harbor tilted. Or Silas tilted; there was no way to know the difference from inside it. The rain went on falling around the little iron roof with its sound like static, like applause very far away, and Silas stayed crouched on the wet boards with a stranger's letter in his fist and five years folding themselves closed behind his eyes like a fan.

Wilhelm Brandt. Who had taught Silas to play skat with a deck missing the queen of clubs, and sworn the game was better that way. Who had stood beside him on the fo'c'sle of the *Svend Foyn* in 1909 with cheap aguardiente going warm in the bottle between them and Valparaíso strung up the hills ahead of them like a fleet of lanterns escaped their ships and decided to stay. Who had grabbed Silas by the collar that night, laughing, breath like a distillery, and said: *You are the only man on this coast who asks for nothing, Eriksen. So I am asking for you. If anything ever comes — war, ruin, my own damned family, whatever comes — you look after what is mine. Swear it.* And Silas, twenty-six years old and homesick and drunk on borrowed stars, had sworn. It had seemed a small price. It had seemed, in truth, like nothing at all — a coin flipped into a well so deep he'd never hear it land.

Whatever comes. He had signed the phrase into himself without reading the contract.

"Uncle Wilhelm is my mother's brother," the boy said, watching Silas read. "But he is in trouble now. Mama said the trouble is too big for her to be seen near." The boy recited it carefully, a lesson learned for recitation, but underneath the lesson his voice wobbled like a candle flame. "She said you were a friend from before, from the ship times. She said you were the only name in all of Chile that Uncle Wilhelm trusted."

"When?" Silas asked. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "When did she leave you here?"

"Quarter past nine," said Heinrich. "She counted the bells with me. She said if you weren't back by midnight I was to knock on the door of Señora Quintanilla and say the word *farol*, and she would take me in until morning." He paused. "I don't want to say it yet."

"No," Silas said. "No, you don't have to say it."

He looked up the street, both ways, though the rain had emptied it of everything but water. She could be anywhere. She could be three hills away by now, or on a boat, or standing in a doorway watching him read her handwriting, waiting to learn whether she had chosen rightly — because that was the other thing about the note, the thing that raised the hair on his arms as he read it a second and third time. It was addressed to the man at this address, but it named him. *The Norwegian.* She had known exactly whose door she was abandoning her son at. Which meant she knew about Wilhelm's friends. Which meant Wilhelm, wherever he was, had spoken of Silas recently, and urgently, and by name.

Which meant the promise had been invoked before tonight. By letter, perhaps. Perhaps by a letter Silas himself had carried and read and copied and filed away in his notebook like a specimen, never dreaming it was addressed to him.

He felt sick. He felt, absurdly, seen — the one sensation he had spent thirty-one years arranging his life to avoid. Some woman he had never met had looked across the whole sprawl of Valparaíso, with its hundred thousand strangers, its fog and its secrets, and put her finger on the one man nobody knew, and said: *him.*

"Mama also said—" Heinrich began, and stopped. He looked down at his folded hands.

"You can tell me tomorrow," Silas said. "Come inside. You're frozen through."

"She said one more thing." The boy lifted his chin, and in the porch light Silas saw the family resemblance for the first time — the same stubborn jaw, the same pale eyes, Wilhelm's face rebuilt at half-scale and handed forward through a sister into a new generation. "She said, *forget my face.* She made me repeat it. She said if anyone ever asked me what she looked like, I wasn't to remember. Not even you." His voice cracked on the last word, and he hated it, Silas could see him hating it, straightening his spine to punish himself. "But I do remember. I remember everything. Is that disobedient?"

"No," Silas said quietly. "That's the most obedient thing you've done all night."

He took the carpetbag. It weighed nothing; that was the worst of it, the lightness of everything the child owned in the world. Inside the door, Mrs. Quintanilla's hall smelled of lamp oil and boiled beef and the lavender bags she hung against moths, and the ordinary sounds of the house came through the walls — Don Félix arguing with his newspaper, water running somewhere upstairs — the whole machinery of ordinary life grinding on, oblivious, and Silas stood dripping on the mat with a German consul's nephew beside him and a dead man's promise in his pocket, and understood that the well had finally given up its coin.

Whatever comes. It had come. It was seven years old and soaked to the skin and looking up at him with Wilhelm Brandt's eyes.

"Mr. Eriksen," the boy said, as Silas steered him toward the kitchen and the stove. "Mama was crying. She thinks I didn't see. But I did." A beat, the rain filling the silence against the windows. "Is Uncle Wilhelm in prison?"

The question landed in Silas like a hook finding flesh. He had heard the talk, everyone had heard the talk — the Archduke dead in Sarajevo, the ultimatums ticking across Europe like a clock in an empty house, the papers in Santiago printing maps of borders that might as well have been drawn in blood. German ships leaving port ahead of the declarations. Enemy alien, the new phrase, not yet common, not yet dangerous, but coming, coming down the wire toward Valparaíso like weather.

"I don't know," Silas said. And then, because the boy deserved one true thing tonight: "But I'm going to find out. First thing tomorrow."

Upstairs in his room, after he had fed the boy bread and milk and installed him in Mrs. Quintanilla's spare bed despite her raised eyebrows and her immediate, merciful, unspoken decision not to ask questions until morning — Silas sat at his desk and opened the notebook to a fresh page. For the first time in six years he did not copy another person's words.

He wrote the name at the top of the page, his own hand shaking slightly:

*Wilhelm Brandt.*

Beneath it: *whatever comes.*

Outside, the rain kept falling on the roofs of Valparaíso, and far away across the water, in cities whose names were beginning to fill the newspapers like sparks drifting toward powder, men were signing other documents, larger ones, and the morning tide was already turning toward a war.
