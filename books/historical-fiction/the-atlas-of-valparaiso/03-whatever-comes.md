## 3. Whatever Comes

The stars over Valparaíso in 1909 had no order to them that Silas could find, and Wilhelm Brandt swore this was the whole point of them.

"Look," he said, sprawled on the foredeck of the *Marta Kristina* with a bottle of pisco between his boots, his shirt open to the warm dark. "In Norway you have your tidy heavens, ja? Your fixed constellations, your farmers' calendars. But here — here the sky is cargo that hasn't been claimed yet." He waved the bottle at the Southern Cross, at the Magellanic Clouds like two smudges of breath on glass. "Nobody owns it. That's why I stay."

Silas sat against the capstan with his notebook on his knee, though he wasn't writing. He was watching Wilhelm the way he watched everything: from slightly outside, cataloguing.

They had met eleven months earlier in the crew mess of a freighter out of Hamburg, two foreigners among forty men who spoke a Spanish neither of them had, and friendship between such men is not chosen so much as recognized — the way one drowning sailor knows another. Silas was twenty-three then, three years off the boat from Bergen, driving carts and carrying letters and saying almost nothing in any language. Wilhelm was five years older, a second mate's son from Lübeck who had jumped ship twice and been dragged back once, a man who talked the way other men breathed, continuously and without much thought about where it came from.

"You write everything down," Wilhelm said now, nodding at the notebook. "Every letter you carry for the passengers, you read it first. Don't lie, Eriksen. I've seen you. You hold the envelope up to the lamp like a doctor reading an X-ray."

"It's rude to say what a man does in private."

"It's rude to do it." Wilhelm grinned and passed the bottle. "But I don't mind. You know what I think? I think you're collecting lives. Like stamps. Someday you'll have enough of other people's words to build yourself one."

Silas drank rather than answer. The pisco was cheap and hot going down and the harbor below them was all lantern-light and rope-creak, the bells of the launches, a woman singing somewhere up on Cerro Concepción with an accompaniment of dogs. The smell of it — tar, salt, frying fish, the green rot of the tide line — had become, without his noticing when, the smell of home. That frightened him a little. Home had been a thing he'd decided he could live without.

"Wilhelm." He said the name carefully, testing its weight. "Why did you jump ship? The first time."

Wilhelm considered this with the gravity of the very drunk. "Because my uncle wanted me back in Lübeck to marry a girl whose father owns three barges. And because—" he stopped, and for once the talk ran dry, and Silas saw something underneath it, brief as a fish turning silver in dark water. "Because my sister died having her first child and nobody wrote to tell me until four months after. Four months, Silas. I found out from a letter addressed to someone else that got put in my trunk by mistake." He laughed, but it was a terrible sound. "So now I read other people's mail too. We are the same, you and I. Except I stopped believing letters would save anyone."

They were quiet then. A launch went by below, its lantern swinging, and the light crossed Wilhelm's face in stripes.

"Whatever comes," Silas said at last, not knowing until he said it that he was going to say it. "If anything ever — if there's ever trouble, or need, or a child standing somewhere it shouldn't be — send word to me. Whatever comes. I'll come."

Wilhelm turned his head slowly. In the starlight his eyes were wet, whether from drink or something else Silas couldn't tell and didn't want to examine too closely.

"You mean that?"

"I don't make promises often enough to make them carelessly."

"Then say it again. Slowly. I want to keep it exactly."

And Silas said it again, slowly, under the unclaimed sky, while the harbor bells rang the half hour and the singing woman on the hill reached the end of her song and did not begin another: *whatever comes.* Wilhelm made him shake on it, hand to hand, gripping hard enough to hurt, and then he laughed his big laugh and threw the last of the pisco into the black water for luck, and the moment sealed itself shut like a letter no one would open for five years.

* * *

Five years later, in August of 1914, Silas stood in Mrs. Quintanilla's kitchen at six in the morning holding a cup of coffee he could not drink, listening to the boy breathe behind the thin wall.

Heinrich slept in the narrow bed under the window, curled around himself like a comma, and every few minutes Silas found an excuse to walk past the door and listen. The child breathed. The world continued. Outside, Valparaíso woke in its usual din — the market cries, the ascensores groaning up their hills, a brass band rehearsing badly somewhere — and none of it knew that the world had ended nine days ago in Sarajevo, or that it had finished ending this week in Belgium, or that in this kitchen, on this ordinary Tuesday, a Norwegian cart-driver was harboring the nephew of the German consul while Europe tore itself apart over precisely whose blood ran in whom.

The morning paper lay on the table where Ingrid Halvorsen had thrown it the night before. Silas read it again anyway, because dread makes a man re-read. BRITAIN DECLARES WAR. Below it, in smaller type, the local news: port security measures, reservists called up, foreign shipping subject to inspection. And below that, an item so small it might have been set by accident: German nationals resident in Chile advised to register with authorities; several detained pending review at the discretion of port officials.

*Detained pending review.* Silas knew what those words weighed. He carried letters for men who used them.

He had gone through the boy's small oilskin satchel twice already, hating himself both times, finding nothing but clothes worn soft at the collars, a tin whistle, a carved horse with one ear missing, and the note — *forget my face* — which he had read perhaps forty times. No address. No name for the woman. Only the name she had left him, written beneath in a hand he had known instantly, the way you know a voice through a wall: *his father is Wilhelm Brandt. His mother is — she cannot be found. You promised.*

You promised. As if the promise had been lying in wait all these years, patient as a debt collector.

Mrs. Quintanilla came in for wood for the stove and paused, looking at him with the flat shrewdness of a woman who had rented rooms to fugitives of one kind or another since before either of her husbands died.

"The boy eats eggs," she said. "All children eat eggs. Stop pacing, Eriksen, you'll wear a groove in my floor and I'll charge you for it." She loaded the stove, then added, without turning: "The consul's people came by yesterday asking after a woman with a child. Doña Mercedes saw them on the corner. They'll be back."

"They're not looking for the boy."

"They're looking for whoever helped the boy. It comes to the same door." She straightened, dusted flour off her hands. "I have taken in deserters, widows, one counterfeiter, and a priest who owed money to everyone in the city. I know how doors work."

When she had gone, Silas finally drank the coffee. It was cold. Everything felt cold lately, as though some northern winter had followed the news across the equator and settled into the bones of the city.

He thought about Wilhelm. Five years since the *Marta Kristina*, and in that time their friendship had survived mostly on paper — Wilhelm posted whenever his ship touched a port with a post office, long rambling pages about the China coast, about a wife named Greta whom he'd married in '11 and adored with the same loud totality he brought to everything, about the boy born in '13, *a small tyrant with Greta's lungs and my appetite*. Silas had kept every letter in the bottom of his trunk, and had copied into his notebook only one sentence in all that time, because it was the only one he couldn't bear to copy and therefore had to: *You are the only man who has ever asked nothing of me, and I find I would give you anything.*

And now the last letter, received in June, had been different. Shorter. Colder water in it. *There is trouble with Greta's family — her brother, politics, the kind of trouble I will explain when I can. If letters stop coming, don't think ill of me. Remember the stars. W.*

Then war. Then silence. Then a rain-soaked woman on a doorstep with a sleeping child and a sentence like a knife laid flat on the palm: *forget my face.*

By noon the rumors had thickened the way they always did, each telling worse than the last. At the customs house a clerk swore three German sailors had been taken off their ship at dawn. In the market, a fishmonger told anyone who would listen that warships had been sighted off Coronel, and a woman spat and said good, let them all drown, her cousin had died at sea and the Germans had done it somehow, she was sure of it. Two boys fought in the street over a stick of sugarcane and the crowd shouted *England! Germany!* at them as a joke, and laughed, and the laughter had teeth in it. Silas walked through all of it with his collar up, invisible as ever, and for the first time in twelve years his invisibility felt less like a coat than like a cell. A German child was asleep above a boarding-house kitchen on Cerro Alegre, and the whole city was learning, hour by hour, to hate the word printed beside that child's name in whatever ledger the police kept.

He climbed the stairs in the late afternoon and found Heinrich awake, sitting up in bed, watching the door with the level, unblinking suspicion Silas remembered from stray cats.

"I'm not going to hurt you," Silas said, in German, his accent clumsy after years of disuse.

The boy considered this. "My mother said a man would come. She said you'd have a face like a closed book." Heinrich tilted his head, examining him with terrible thoroughness. "She was right."

Down in the street, boots on cobblestones — more boots than usual, moving together. Silas went to the window and looked down through the gap in the shutter.

Three men in plain clothes stood across the street, talking quietly, not looking at the boarding house, which was how he knew they were looking at it. And walking toward them up the hill, letters in hand, skirts clearing the gutter, came Ingrid Halvorsen — who had left this house the night before knowing more than she had said, and who now, as she passed the three men, slowed just enough to look directly up at Silas's window.

It was not an accident. Nothing Ingrid Halvorsen did was an accident. And the look she gave him said plainly: *we are going to talk.*
