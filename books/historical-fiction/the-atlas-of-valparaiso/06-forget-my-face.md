## 6. Forget My Face

My mother said it three times, and the third time she held my face in her cold wet hands so I would know she meant it.

"Forget my face, Heinrich. Not because you must forget me. Because remembering is a door, and doors open from both sides."

I did not understand then. I was seven and a half and the rain was coming through my collar and her hands were shaking, which they never did, not even when Papa's letters stopped coming, not even when Uncle's men came to the house on the hill with their gray faces and their questions. Her hands shook only that night, on the steps of a boarding house in a street that smelled like fish and coal smoke, and then she kissed me between the eyes — right where the ache lives when you cry without sound — and she rang the bell, and she stepped back into the dark, and she was gone.

The note she left said *forget my face*. The man who opened the door read it by lamplight while I stood dripping on the mat, and I watched his mouth move around the words like they were stones he had to chew.

* * *

His name was Silas Eriksen. I learned this the way I learn everything now: by listening at doors.

Mrs. Quintanilla called him Señor Eriksen even though he was not Spanish. She called me *el alemancito*, the little German, and pinched my cheek too hard, and fed me soup with corn in it that first night until I thought I would be sick, because I had already been sick once in the courtyard where nobody could see, or so I believed.

Here is what I knew about Silas Eriksen after three days:

He drove a motorcar for a lady with white in her braid who shouted at crowds. He ate standing up, fast, like food might be taken away. He slept in the room at the end of the hall whose floorboard sang a high thin note when he crossed it — the fourth board from the window, always the fourth — and he never sang, never whistled, never made any noise on purpose at all.

And he read other people's letters.

I saw him do it on the second evening. He sat in the kitchen with a letter he was supposed only to carry, and he opened it with a knife so gently the seal stayed whole, and he read it twice, moving his lips, and then he took out a little black notebook and wrote something in it, and closed the letter again so perfectly that when Mrs. Quintanilla's niece came for it later she thanked him and ran off happy, knowing nothing, carrying her own secrets out into the world with a stranger's fingerprints all over them.

I should have been frightened. Mama said trust no one, speak little, be a shadow, shadows are safe. But watching him read that letter, I felt something else instead of fear. It was like looking through a window at night and seeing another lamp lit across the courtyard — someone awake when they shouldn't be, someone keeping watch over the dark the same as you.

* * *

Mama's instructions, in order, the way she gave them in the carriage while the horses' hooves struck sparks off the cobbles:

One. If anyone asks, your name is Heinrich Vogel, and you are the orphaned nephew of a merchant in Santiago, and you have no parents living, and you will say it without crying because crying makes people look closer.

Two. Do not tell anyone your true surname. Not the landlady, not the priest, not the police, especially not the police. A name is a rope, and ropes can be pulled.

Three. There is a man here — a Norwegian, a driver, a friend of your father's from before, from the sea. His name is Silas. If everything goes wrong, if I do not come back for you in one week, you may give him the name Wilhelm Brandt, and only him, and only that name, and see what his face does.

"What will his face do?" I asked.

"I don't know," Mama said. "That is how we will know if he can be trusted."

That was the most Mama thing she ever said. She believed faces told truths mouths wouldn't. She used to study people at church, at market, at Uncle's terrible dinners, and afterward she would tell Papa: *that one lies with his whole forehead; that one is kind but ashamed of it.* Papa laughed at her. Papa trusted paper and signatures and the honor of men in good coats, and look where that got us — detained in a city that stopped being his friend in August, behind a door with soldiers outside it, writing letters that took weeks to travel nowhere.

So I studied Silas Eriksen's face. That was my work, those first days, better than arithmetic, better than prayers. I studied it the way Mama taught me, part by part.

* * *

The forehead: honest, mostly, but it kept things. When Mrs. Quintanilla asked him about me — *whose boy is this, where is the mother, what am I supposed to write in my ledger* — his forehead went smooth as a pond and he said, "A relative. Passing through." Both sentences were lies and both lay flat and still on the surface, and underneath I could feel the truth swimming down deep where the landlady couldn't net it. A lying forehead moves. His went quiet. That is different. That is someone protecting something, not stealing it.

The hands: careful. He carried cups of milk to me without spilling, set them down without noise, and once — this I keep like a coin in my pocket — he put his coat over me in the courtyard when I fell asleep waiting for Mama on the third night, and the coat smelled of engine oil and rain and tobacco, and I woke under it and pretended to still be asleep because I wanted the smell a little longer.

The mouth: the problem. The mouth said *forget my face* aloud that first night, reading Mama's note, and then looked straight at me and said, "Your mother has an interesting sense of humor."

It was not funny. I knew it wasn't funny. But his voice cracked slightly on *humor*, like a wheel hitting a stone, and I understood two things at once: that he knew the note was not a joke, and that he needed it to be one more than I needed it not to be. Grown-ups are like that. They need small wrong stories to stand on so the big right ones don't crush them.

* * *

On the fifth day he caught me reading his mail.

Not meaning to. A letter had come for Ingrid Halvorsen — the shouting lady, the one with the white braid, whom I had seen from the window gesturing at a crowd like Moses parting a smaller, colder sea — and Silas had left it on the kitchen table while he went to crank the motorcar, and it lay there facedown and breathing its secret at me, and I was bored, and bored children are just curious children with nothing to lose.

I steamed it over the kettle, the way I had seen Mama do once in Valdivia, holding it flat with tongs, my heart going like a bird against glass. The glue lifted. I read four lines — something about a meeting broken up by police, something about a woman named Rosa being hurt, something about *we do not stop* — and I heard the fourth floorboard sing and there he was in the doorway, and I froze with the letter half-lifted and steam still curling off it, guilty as a cat with a bird.

We looked at each other for a long time. The kettle ticked. Somewhere below, Mrs. Quintanilla was killing a chicken, loudly.

"You opened it," he said.

"You opened hers too," I said. "On Tuesday. And the one before, from Concepción. You use a knife, but I don't have a knife, so I used steam. It works better."

Something happened in his face then that I had not seen in five days of studying it. The careful surface broke — not into anger, but into something startled and almost young, like ice going out on a river. He sat down across from me. He took off his cap, which he never did indoors, and his hair stood up gray and uncombed and suddenly he was not a driver or a landlord or a stranger but just a tired man at a table with a boy who had caught him.

"Does your mother know you do that?"

"She taught me."

"Why?"

"Because letters are where people tell the truth," I said, and then heard whose sentence that was, and wished I could take it back, because it sounded exactly like the kind of thing Mama would say and Mama had ordered me to be a shadow.

But Silas didn't laugh at it and he didn't report it. He took the letter gently from my hand, pressed the flap down with his warm thumb until it sealed, and said, "Steam does work better. Don't tell Mrs. Quintanilla."

* * *

After that we had an arrangement, though we never called it one. In the evenings he let me sit in the kitchen while he read, and sometimes, if the letter was long, he would read a line aloud — not names, never names, he was careful of that even with me — just a sentence that pleased him, the way a man tosses a dog a scrap from his plate. *The harbor smells of tar and oranges today.* *She says her son has lost two teeth and gained a vocabulary.* Once he read: *Tell him the stars over the Pacific are the same stars, and I count them every night.*

"That's from a sailor," I said. "Sailors count stars. My papa counts stars. Counted. He counted them every night from the deck and wrote the numbers in the margin like sums."

Silas went very still. The kettle ticked. Downstairs the chicken had given up complaining.

"Heinrich," he said, and it was the first time he had used my name, my real one, the one Mama said was a rope — except he said it softly, not pulling, just holding the end of it out to me like a hand. "What was your father's ship called?"

And I remembered Mama's instruction number three, sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone all week: *if everything goes wrong, give him the name Wilhelm Brandt, and see what his face does.*

Everything had gone wrong. Everything had been wrong since August, since the gray men, since the rain and the steps and the shaking hands and *forget my face*. One week had nearly passed and Mama had not come, and I was tired of being a shadow, because shadows get cold and shadows don't get their mother back.

"Brandt," I said. "Wilhelm Brandt. My papa."

And I watched his face do it.

The pond-surface broke all the way through, and what came up from underneath wasn't shock, exactly — it was older than that, like something he'd been carrying so long he'd forgotten the weight until someone set it back on him. His hand went flat on the table. His eyes went somewhere far away, out past the wall, out past the hills, to a deck under stars I'd never seen, where two young men drank something bad and swore something good.

"Whatever comes," Silas said quietly. To me, or to Papa, or to the empty kitchen — I couldn't tell. Then he looked at me, really looked, the way nobody had looked at me since Mama's hands on my face in the rain, and he said:

"She told you to forget her face, didn't she. Your mother."

I nodded.

"Well," he said, and pulled the black notebook toward him, and turned to a clean page, and uncapped his pen. "Then we'd better remember her together. Start with her hands. Were they shaking? Tell me everything. Leave nothing out."

And that is when I began to trust him — not because he promised anything, but because he asked for everything, and wrote it down, and signed it, at the bottom of the page, with his own true name.

Outside, far down toward the harbor, a ship's horn sounded once through the fog — long and low, like something counting down.
