## 10. A Letter of His Own

Spring came to Valparaíso the way forgiveness comes — slowly, and then all at once, so that you could not say which morning the light changed.

Silas felt it first on the esplanade above the customs house, where the wind off the Pacific had spent all winter carrying salt and rumor and now carried only salt. The jacaranda on Cerro Alegre had gone purple overnight, as if the hill had decided, without consulting anyone, that enough mourning was enough. Below him the harbor lay bright and busy, cranes swinging their patient arcs, and among the ships at anchor he could find — though he did not need to look — the berth where the *Andes Star* had stood on a morning in June when he had given away the two people who had made him a family.

That was eight months ago. The war went on in Europe like weather happening to someone else. Wilhelm Brandt was back on the water again, signed to a Chilean coastal run because no belligerent nation would have him, and every few weeks a letter came postmarked Talcahuano or Coronel, written in the round, careful hand of a man who still could not quite believe his luck. *They let me go,* the first one had said. *I keep reading the paper over your shoulder, Silas. I keep seeing your name.*

His name. That was the strange thing about this season. His name was everywhere now, or everywhere that mattered — on the masthead of Ingrid Halvorsen's paper, in the small bold type of the column that ran each Thursday under the title *Letters from the Hills*, and most of all in his own hand, at the bottom of pages, where for forty years he had left nothing but blank space.

*Silas Eriksen.* He wrote it and it looked like a stranger's signature and it looked like his own face in a mirror, both at once.

The Daimler coughed and settled as Ingrid came down the boarding-house steps with her satchel and her unyielding posture, fifty-one years old and built like a decision.

"You're smiling," she said, folding herself into the passenger seat.

"I am not."

"You are. It's very unsettling. Drive."

He drove. Down through the crooked streets where the funiculars groaned up and down their cables like old men climbing stairs, past the market where the fish sellers were shouting the morning's prices, along the mole where the gulls wheeled and screamed their one word endlessly. He knew every stone of it now. That was the difference. For years he had driven these streets the way he read other people's letters — present, invisible, taking everything and signing nothing. Now the city knew him back. The woman who sold empanadas outside the print works called out *don Silas* and held up two fingers, meaning she'd save him two, meaning she knew he would come. Children on Cerro Alegre waved at the motorcar not because it was a marvel — by now it was hardly that — but because they knew the tall Norwegian who drove it, the one whose notebook had been read aloud in a police station until half the city had read it too, the one who had put a boy on a ship and then, when it mattered, had refused to disappear.

He had thought, in those first raw weeks after the pier, that exposure would be the end of him. It had been the opposite, though he was careful never to say so where Ingrid could hear, because she would only look insufferably correct.

* * *

The letters came to Mrs. Quintanilla's boarding house now, addressed to him directly, which was its own quiet astonishment. People who could not write brought their words to him the way they once brought them to priests. A widow from Concepción wanting news of a son in the north. A dockworker composing his first letter ever, to a brother in Genoa, dictating in fragments while Silas shaped it into sentences and then, always, read it back aloud so the man could hear his own voice made legible. Silas kept none of these words in his notebook anymore. The notebook was gone — seized, catalogued, returned to him in an envelope with a government stamp, its pages soft from other men's hands — and he had found he did not miss it. He had spent a lifetime living inside other people's sentences. Now people came and lived inside his, briefly, and went home lighter.

On Thursdays he wrote the column. Ingrid had offered it to him in September with characteristic grace: "You'll do it badly at first and then you'll do it well, and either way I'm not asking." It was called *Letters from the Hills* and it was exactly what the name promised: the words of the city's immigrants, its outsiders, its invisible ones — the German baker who had not been arrested only because his wife was Chilean; the Peruvian stevedore; the Croatian widow; the boy from Talcahuano who wrote that his father said the sea did not care what flag you flew, and Silas had printed it because it was true and because truth needed printing more than ever now. He signed every one. *Silas Eriksen, Cerro Alegre.* Each week the name went out into the city like a hand extended, and each week hands came back.

Ingrid watched him work some evenings, saying nothing, wearing the expression of a woman keeping score. She had won their bargain long ago — he had carried her letters through police lines through the worst of the autumn, through checkpoints where young officers with new armbands looked at his foreign face and saw, for once, exactly what he chose to show them. But she had never treated it as a debt between them. "You weren't working for me," she'd said once, watching him seal an envelope addressed to Talcahuano. "You were practicing."

"Practicing what?"

"Belonging. It takes some people longer than others."

* * *

November. The boy's letters arrived on Sundays, when the coastal boats came in.

*Dear Silas,* Heinrich wrote, in Spanish now mostly, though he kept a few German words the way you keep a stone from a beach you loved. *Mama says I must stop growing out of my shoes so fast because shoes cost money in Talcahuano too. Papa is home four days out of ten and when he is home we walk on the breakwater and he tells me about the freighter and the drunk Norwegian who fell asleep on deck and woke up covered in flour. I tell him that is my favorite one. Mama says I should ask you if you are eating properly. Are you eating properly? Mrs. Quintanilla says you eat standing up like a horse. Write back and tell me something true about Valparaíso. Not from a book. Something you saw yourself.*

Something true, something seen. The boy asked for it every time, and every time Silas sat at the table by the window with Mrs. Quintanilla's cat asleep on the cold stove and searched the week for it. The pelicans stacked three deep on the mooring posts of the fishing wharf, motionless as carven figures until the sardine boats came in. The sound the whole city made at six in the evening when the bakery ovens lit and every flue on the hill exhaled at once. The old man on Paseo Gervasoni who played the accordion only the first eight bars of any song, because, he said, endings were for people in a hurry. These were his now. He had collected other people's words for forty years and discovered, at last, that his own eyes had been recording all along, waiting for someone to address the letters to.

He answered every Sunday. He told the truth about the flour. He ate sitting down, sometimes, just to be able to report it.

* * *

One gray afternoon in early December, Ingrid handed him a newspaper proof instead of a satchel. "Page three," she said. "Read the editorial before I send it."

It was hers, unsigned as always, about the internment camps and the families split by decrees written in languages the decree-writers didn't speak. At the bottom, in the space where her name never went, there was a line she had added:

*This paper has printed many voices this year. One of them belongs to a man who spent his life carrying other people's words and hiding inside them. We are glad to report he has stopped hiding. There are ten thousand more like him in this city. They are your neighbors. Learn their names.*

She watched him read it. "Too much?" she asked, and for the first time since he had known her, Ingrid Halvorsen sounded uncertain.

Silas folded the proof along its crease, carefully, the way he handled anything that mattered. "No," he said. "It's the first thing you've printed that isn't too little."

Outside, the light was going gold over the bay, and somewhere down in the maze of streets a ship's horn sounded, long and low, and he found he could hear it without his chest tightening. The tide would go out tonight, as tides did, and come back tomorrow, and the city would still be here, holding all its strangers together on its steep hills like a hand cupped around water, letting none of it fall.

## A Letter

*Talcahuano, 14 March 1915*

My dear Heinrich,

You asked me for something true, something I saw myself, so here it is: this morning on the mole there was a dog asleep inside an upturned rowboat, and the owner let him stay, and the boat rocked a little whenever the dog dreamed, and everyone walking past pretended not to smile, and every single one of them did.

Your mother will tell you I am a serious man. She is right, and she is wrong. I was serious for forty years because seriousness is a kind of invisibility — nobody interrupts a grave face to ask it how it feels. Then a boy left at my door with instructions to forget his mother's face taught me that being forgotten is not the same as being safe, and that a promise spoken drunk under the stars in 1909 can weigh more than a treaty signed by kings.

I want you to know what happened after the ship pulled out that morning, since I have never said it plainly. I stood on the dock and I was the loneliest man in Chile, and I was also, for the first time in my life, entirely visible — a tall foreigner weeping openly on a public pier, and not one person looked away from him, or looked through him. Your father came off that ship into my arms eleven days later because I stopped hiding, and I would trade nothing for that arithmetic.

Eat properly. Grow slowly. Keep the stone from the beach.

Your friend,

**Silas Eriksen**
Cerro Alegre, Valparaíso
