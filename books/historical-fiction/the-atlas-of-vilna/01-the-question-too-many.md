## 1. The Question Too Many

The knock came at nine minutes past four, which was wrong in two ways.

First, census visits in Vilna came on Tuesdays and Thursdays, and this was a Wednesday. Second, they always came before the noon meal or after the evening shift change, when the corridors of the Third Hospital were thick enough with bodies that a single nurse could dissolve into them like salt into broth. Nine minutes past four was the hour when the ward went quiet — when the dying were left alone with their breathing and the living were left alone with their dread, and there was no crowd anywhere to hide inside.

Lior Lockridge stood at the window of the supply room with a roll of bandages in their hands and counted to thirty before answering. This too was discipline. A person who answered a knock instantly had something to prove; a person who made the visitor wait was merely inconvenienced, mildly annoyed, ordinary. Invisibility was not a gift. It was a craft, practiced daily, and Lior had been practicing since they were old enough to understand why their father inked certain lines of a map and burned others in the stove.

The knock came again. Three raps, evenly spaced, patient. Not a soldier's knock. Soldiers knocked as though the door had insulted them.

Lior set down the bandages, smoothed their apron, and opened the door.

The woman in the corridor was small and neat, wearing a gray coat that had been good five years ago and a hat that had never been good at all. She held a clipboard against her chest the way other people held prayer books, and she had the particular posture of someone who intended to be underestimated and did not mind it. Her eyes, though — her eyes were doing none of the underestimating. They moved over Lior's face once, quickly, the way a clerk's finger moves down a column of figures, and stopped somewhere around the cheekbones before continuing on as if nothing had been noted.

"Frau Vogel," Lior said, reading the identification card before it was offered. "You're late in the day for the count."

"I'm late everywhere." Her voice was dry, unhurried, faintly amused by its own honesty. "Marta Vogel, Office of Population Registry. I'm told your ward houses convalescent officers and civilian admissions."

"Civilian admissions are processed through the main office."

"I know how the paperwork flows, Nurse." She smiled without warmth. "I'm asking what you see with your own eyes."

Behind Lior, down the corridor, a man in bed eleven began to cough — the wet, tearing cough that meant the lung, not the throat. Lior let the silence hold one beat longer than politeness allowed, then stepped aside. "You may walk the ward with me. Touch nothing on the bedsides. Some of these men have earned their few possessions."

"A generous phrase," Frau Vogel said, stepping past. "Earned. As though the war were wages."

They walked the ward together, and Lior performed the oldest role they knew: the competent, colorless nurse. They answered questions about bed counts and discharge dates. They recited the admission numbers from memory because the ledger book was locked in the matron's office and Frau Vogel did not ask twice about locked things, which Lior marked as either courtesy or cunning and could not yet decide between. She wrote in a small, upright hand, each stroke identical in pressure, and she did not look at the men in the beds more than the count required.

That was what unsettled Lior first, though it took the length of the ward to understand it. Everyone looked at the wounded too long — out of pity, out of horror, out of the obscene curiosity the dying provoke even in decent people. Marta Vogel looked at them exactly long enough to confirm they existed, and no longer. Her attention was not on the men at all. It was on the spaces between them: the empty beds stripped for new arrivals, the gaps in the numbering, the places where the official record and the physical world failed to agree.

She paused at bed seven, where a boy of perhaps nineteen slept with his hands curled near his face like a child's. "This one has no admission card."

"Cards are printed faster than men arrive," Lior said. "He'll have his by Friday."

"And his name?"

"On the card."

Frau Vogel looked up at that. For the first time her pen actually lifted from the page. "I asked for his name, Nurse. Not the card's name. Though I suppose in this city those have not been the same thing for some time."

The radiator ticked. Somewhere below, in the surgical theater, an instrument tray rang against tile like a dropped bell.

"Vilnius," Lior said, giving the boy a name out of thin air, smooth as pouring water. "Jonas Vilnius. He came in with the transport from the east."

It was a good lie. Lior had learned lies the way other children learned scales — daily, patiently, until the fingers moved without instruction. Give the interrogator a detail slightly too mundane to be interesting. Let them feel clever catching nothing. Jonas Vilnius was a name no mother had given any child, and by Friday the boy would be dead or transferred and the invented name would dissolve back into the air it came from.

Frau Vogel wrote it down.

Then she closed her clipboard, turned to face Lior fully for the first time, and asked the question.

Not *where were you born*. Not *your parents' names*, not *your religion*, not any of the forty questions the form demanded and Lior had rehearsed answers to the way actors rehearse a role they must never be caught performing. She tilted her head slightly, as if listening to something under the floorboards, and said:

"Whom have you buried?"

* * *

Later, Lior would try to reconstruct the moment and fail. Memory kept only fragments: the green paint peeling above bed nine; the smell of carbolic and snow-damp wool; the boy's breath whistling thin; Marta Vogel's gray eyes waiting with the patience of someone who had all evening and no illusions about receiving the truth.

The question was not on the form. Lior could see the form from where they stood; question fourteen asked place of birth, fifteen asked father's occupation, sixteen asked languages spoken. There was nowhere on that page for the dead.

"Nurses bury no one," Lior said. "We return them to their families. Or to the office that does such things now."

"I didn't ask what nurses do." Frau Vogel's voice stayed level, almost kind. "I asked what you have done."

And here was the terrible thing, the thing Lior would carry into the night shift and the next night's shift and the ledger hidden in the binding of Gray's anatomy like a coal wrapped in cloth: for half a heartbeat, they wanted to answer.

There was a name sitting right behind the teeth. *My father*, it wanted to say. *Isaiah Lockridge, printer, Pylimo Street, dead eleven months and unrecorded, unmourned on paper, nonexistent by every registry in this city — buried by me and my own two hands in the yard behind the workshop while the frost was still soft enough to dig.* No census taker had ever asked for him. No form had a line for him. He had been subtracted so thoroughly that even grief felt sometimes like a private madness, a conversation held with empty air.

"We all bury someone," Lior said instead, in a voice worn smooth as river stone. "In 1941, that is not a question. That is arithmetic."

Something flickered across Marta Vogel's face. Not sympathy — sympathy Lior could have managed, sympathy was a currency they knew how to counterfeit. It was closer to recognition. The look of a woman hearing her own sentence come back to her in another person's mouth.

"Yes," she said quietly. "Arithmetic."

Her pen returned to the page. She wrote the final entries — occupation, languages, address — and her hand was steady through all of it, each letter identical in weight to the last. But at the bottom, where the form required the census taker's certification signature, the nib touched paper and stopped.

One beat. Two. Long enough for Lior to hear the boy in bed seven exhale. Long enough for the radiator to tick twice. A pause of no possible administrative meaning, a hesitation with no form to justify it, and then the pen moved and she signed her name in the same small upright letters and everything was ordinary again, or wore ordinariness the way a coat wears a lining.

"Your papers are in order, Nurse Lockridge." She tucked the clipboard under her arm. "I'll not trouble your ward again this quarter."

"The hospital thanks you for your efficiency."

"The hospital," she said, "thanks everyone for their efficiency. It is the century's favorite virtue." At the door she stopped, half-turned, and for a moment the lamplight caught her profile sharp as a woodcut. "You answer questions well, Nurse. Too well, maybe. Most people take a moment to remember. You never seem to need the moment."

Then she was gone, her footsteps fading down the corridor, even and unhurried, a metronome counting off some measure Lior could not yet read.

* * *

The ward at dusk was Lior's alone. The day nurses had gone; the night orderlies were at their soup; the oil lamps burned low along the walls, turning each bed into a small island of amber in a sea of blue shadow. Lior walked the row out of habit, checking pulses that needed no checking, straightening blankets that were already straight.

At bed seven they stopped.

The boy — nineteen, twenty at most, though the official transport list had aged him to twenty-three because the paperwork preferred its soldiers legal — was awake, watching the ceiling.

"Nurse." His voice was gravel and smoke. "Did I dream it, or was there a woman asking questions?"

"You dreamed it. Go back to sleep."

"She didn't write things down right." He turned his head with effort, and his eyes in the lamplight were too old for his face. "My mother writes like that. Small letters, all the same. Like she's afraid of taking up space on the page." He swallowed. "Is there bread tonight?"

"There's bread."

"Real bread, or the kind that's mostly a rumor?"

"Both kinds. Eat slowly."

He smiled and closed his eyes, and Lior stood over him a moment longer than duty required, memorizing without meaning to — the cracked lips, the bandage at the temple gone pink at the edge, the way his hand lay open on the blanket as if he had set something down and meant to pick it up later. Twenty-two names this month the transport lists had mangled or missed entirely. Twenty-two corrections waiting in the binding of the anatomy text on the shelf in the supply room, written in pencil fine as spider silk, each one a small act of rebellion so quiet that even Lior had nearly stopped thinking of it as rebellion. It was housekeeping. It was prayer. It was the only inheritance left from a father who had believed that a map was a moral document, that a city with its true streets drawn was a city that could not be wholly stolen.

Lior finished the rounds. Locked the medicine cabinet. Signed the shift log with the bland, legible hand that belonged to Nurse L. Lockridge, age twenty-six, born Vilnius, no dependents, no church, no politics — a person so carefully constructed of blanks that the forms practically filled themselves in.

Then, in the empty corridor, the fear arrived.

It came the way cold comes through a wall — not suddenly but completely, seeping into every seam. Lior pressed a palm flat against the green-painted plaster and breathed, and the breathing did not help. Because the fear was not the familiar kind. The familiar kind was the fear of discovery, of the ledger found, of the wall and the pistol and the names erased twice over. That fear had lived in Lior so long it functioned as architecture; rooms were built around it.

This was different. This was the fear of being *known*. Of a stranger with a gray coat and identical handwriting looking at the careful blankness of a life and seeing, not the blankness, but the shape of the thing pressed underneath it — the way a sheet shows the outline of whatever is hidden beneath. *You never seem to need the moment.*

Marta Vogel had asked whom they buried because she already suspected there was someone. And she had paused before signing her name because she was deciding — deciding something, deciding anything — and Lior Lockridge, who had survived three years of occupation by ensuring that no one ever had reason to decide anything about them, had just become a decision.

Down in the surgical theater, water ran in a basin. Outside, the first snow of the season came down over the rooftops of Pylimo Street, falling on the workshop, on the press, on the yard where a grave lay flat and unmarked beneath the frost. Lior thought of the anatomy text waiting on its shelf, and of the blank pages remaining in the ledger's binding — room for perhaps sixty more names — and understood, with the calm of someone reading their own diagnosis, that the winter had just changed sides.

Somewhere in this city, a woman was writing down names in small, identical letters.

And Lior had just given her one that wasn't true.
