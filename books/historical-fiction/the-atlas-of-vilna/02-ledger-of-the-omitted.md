## 2. Ledger of the Omitted

The hospital at night was a different country, and Lior had learned its borders by heart.

After the last round of laudanum and lamp-trimming, after the night orderly's boots had faded down the corridor toward the boiler room, the ward belonged to the dying and to whoever kept them company. Lior moved between the rows of iron beds with a candle shielded in a tin cup, its light cut down to a coin, and listened. That was the work no one assigned and no one paid for: listening.

It was March of 1941, or what passed for it under occupation — the calendars on the walls still showed last year's saints' days because no one had been issued new ones, and time here was measured in other ways. In dressings changed. In fevers broken. In men who had names at dusk and none by morning, because the paperwork that arrived with them listed them as *unidentified, male, approximately* — and the approximately was all that would survive them.

Lior had stopped believing that was an accident somewhere around the fourth week.

* * *

Bed eleven tonight was a sapper from the east, both hands wrapped to the wrists, his face the gray of paper left out in rain. He had drifted for two days at the edge of speech, and now he surfaced suddenly, gripping nothing with his bandaged fists, and said, "Yankel."

"Here," Lior said, which was true enough. Someone was here.

"Yankel Kagan," the man said, as if correcting a clerk. "Of Slonim. My brother. If anyone asks. If anyone ever asks."

"No one has asked me anything," Lior said gently. "Tell me anyway."

And he did — Yankel Kagan, who could fix any clock put in front of him, who had a wife named Chaya and a daughter whose name he couldn't retrieve, only her age, four, and the fact that she slept with her shoes on in case there was fire. The sapper recited this like cargo manifest, like a man handing over valuables before boarding, and his voice grew steadier as he went, as if each fact laid down was one less thing that could be lost in him.

Lior stood very still and let the candle coin rest on the man's blanket, and repeated the names back until the sapper nodded, satisfied, and slid back under.

Then Lior walked to the end of the ward, past the beds where men breathed in the shallow synchronized rhythm of the heavily dosed, to the supply alcove where the reference books lived — a shelf of them, mostly German, mostly outdated, dusted once a month by whoever remembered. *Topographische Anatomie des Menschen.* A fat quarto bound in green buckram, spine cracked, corners worn to cardboard. No one had opened it in years; the plates inside were fifty years old and wrong about everything except the general arrangement of a body.

Lior took it down and carried it to the window ledge, where the moon gave just enough light to write by without inviting attention, and opened it not to the text but to the binding itself.

There was a gap between the spine's backing and the boards, a hollow the size of a prayer book, and into it fit the ledger: forty leaves of thin paper, sewn with thread pulled from a surgical drape, small enough to vanish against a palm. Lior eased it out with the care of a surgeon lifting something from a wound.

The rule was one name per line, and beneath the name whatever the dying had surrendered: a village, a trade, a wife, a child's age, the color of a door. Nothing that could identify Lior. Nothing that could identify the hospital. Just the names, and enough of the lives attached to make them more than sounds.

*Yankel Kagan, Slonim. Clockmaker. Wife Chaya. Daughter, four, sleeps in her shoes.*

The pen scratched softly in the dark. Somewhere down the corridor the boiler ticked and knocked like a slow heart.

* * *

There were thirty-one names in the ledger now.

Lior knew the number the way other people knew their own age, and hated knowing it, and knew it anyway. Thirty-one lines in a hollow book in a hospital full of official registers that held thousands of entries and somehow, between the transport manifests and the census forms and the officers' tidy carbon copies, managed to lose almost everyone.

Because that was the trick of it, Lior thought, capping the pen. It wasn't that the occupiers destroyed the records. Destruction left a hole you could notice, a shape of absence. What they did was worse: they *abbreviated*. They reduced a man to *unidentified, male, approximately thirty*, and an approximation is a kind of erasure so smooth you can build a whole administration on top of it and never feel the bump. The paperwork didn't lie. It simply declined to say.

So the ledger existed because someone had to decline differently.

But here was the part Lior could not say aloud to anyone, the part that came at night after the writing was done, sitting on the cold windowsill with the anatomy text closed over its secret: recording the dead was theft.

Not from the dead. The dead were beyond needing anything, though Lior did not fully believe that either — believed instead that names were a kind of breath, and breath stopped being breath when no mouth carried it. The theft was from the living. Every line written here endangered someone. Yankel Kagan's wife, if she was alive, if the name reached the wrong desk. The daughter who slept in her shoes. Even Tomas, the courier boy who sometimes brought bread and asked too few questions — especially Tomas, who had never seen the ledger and must never see it, because a sixteen-year-old caught holding paper like this wouldn't even get the courtesy of a trial.

One discovered name, and the arithmetic was simple. Not one execution. One *category*. A nurse who keeps lists becomes a list herself, and lists travel upward, and upward lists become orders, and orders come back down as trucks.

And yet.

Lior pressed the ledger flat against their chest through the uniform, feeling its edges like ribs, and yet. Because the alternative was to let the abbreviations stand. To let Yankel Kagan become *approximately thirty* forever, his clocks unwound, his wife a widow of no one in particular. There were worse fates than endangerment. There was being unwritten.

Devotion and theft, braided together so tightly Lior could no longer find the place where one strand ended and the other began. They wrote the names because they loved the men. They endangered the living because they loved the dead. Both things were true at once, and neither canceled the other, and Lior had stopped waiting for the day when one would win.

* * *

Toward three in the morning, the fever ward called.

Not called — breathed. A sound from bed nineteen, a Polish cavalryman named Dombrowski, or possibly Dombranski; he'd given both on different days and no one had corrected him, because officially he was *unidentified, male, approximately twenty-five*, and unofficially he was a chest wound with a heartbeat. Lior found him thrashing, the dressing soaked through, his lips moving around syllables that weren't words yet.

Lior worked fast — fresh gauze, the carbolic sting, the practiced pressure — and talked the way nurses talk, low and constant, nonsense and comfort braided, and gradually the man surfaced enough to focus on Lior's face.

"Where am I," he said. Not a question. A test.

"A hospital."

"Which hospital." His hand found Lior's wrist with surprising strength. "Listen to me. I have to send word. My mother is in Grodno. If I die here and no one writes to her, she'll wait. Do you understand? She will wait by the window until she dies too. She'll wait for the postman for years."

"I understand."

"My name is Stefan Dombrowski. Grodno, Ulica Zamkowa, number nine. The blue house next to the pharmacy. Write it down." His eyes were wet and furious. "They told me not to give my name to anyone. They told me at the station. But you're not — " He squinted at Lior's uniform, at the armband, searching for the category. "You're nobody official. Are you?"

"No," Lior said. It was the truest thing they'd said in weeks. "I'm nobody official."

"Then write it down. Please. You're allowed to be a person if you're nobody official."

Lior laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly swallowed — and promised, and meant it, and hated how much the promise cost, and meant it anyway.

Dombrowski died at ten minutes past five, quietly, in the gray hour when the ward's breathing changes pitch. The orderly on morning shift noted it in the log: *unidentified, male, approx. 25, wounds received in action.* Lior waited until the body was taken, then washed their hands, then sat on the windowsill with the candle coin and the hollow book and wrote:

*Stefan Dombrowski, Grodno, Ulica Zamkowa 9, the blue house beside the pharmacy. Mother waits by the window. Tell her.*

Thirty-two.

* * *

By six the light was coming up the color of dishwater, and the first orderlies were moving down the corridor with basins, and the country of night was closing its borders again. Lior returned the anatomy text to its shelf, spine out, unremarkable among the other dead languages of medicine. Then, because invisibility was a discipline and not a gift, they performed the small liturgy of becoming no one: straightened the collar, fixed the face into its working neutrality, checked the pockets — pen, pencil stub, no loose paper, nothing that could be read as evidence of an inner life.

It was on the way out, passing the administrative office, that Lior saw the notice pinned to the board beside the duty roster. New print, fresh ink, the occupiers' favorite font:

*CIVIC NOTICE. All structures on Pylimo Street between the synagogue wall and the tannery lane are scheduled for clearance upon seasonal thaw. Owners and occupants must present documentation or vacate.*

Pylimo Street. The workshop. Father's press, father's plates, the room where young Lior had learned which lines to ink and which to leave blank — slated to come down whenever the river decided spring had arrived, which could be two weeks, which could be four.

Lior stood in the corridor while the ward woke behind them, men groaning into daylight, basins clanking, the great indifferent machine of the hospital starting its day — and felt the ledger's weight through the uniform, thirty-two names pressing against their ribs like a second skeleton.

The book wasn't finished. It would never be finished; the transports kept coming. But there were names in it still half-captured, entries begun at a bedside and abandoned when the fever broke or the man died mid-sentence, fragments waiting for the one thing Lior no longer had anywhere else in occupied Vilna: a table, a lamp, an hour, a press to set the record properly before the world demolished another piece of the past.

The workshop was the only place the atlas could be completed. And the ice was already beginning to talk.

Down at the river, Lior knew without going to look, the floes had started their nightly groaning — a sound like a giant shifting in sleep, like the city clearing its throat before it spoke.
