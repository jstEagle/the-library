## 3. The Workshop on Pylimo Street

The notice was nailed to the door at eye level, which meant it had been put there by a man who wanted to be sure it was read and did not care who read it. *By order of the Municipal Works Administration, all structures on this parcel are condemned. Demolition to commence upon seasonal thaw. Occupants must vacate immediately.*

Occupants. Lior stood in the gray light of Pylimo Street and almost laughed. The building had been empty for three years — empty of living things, anyway. Empty of presses, of ink, of the smell of turpentine that used to cling to their clothes so thoroughly that schoolmates could name the street they lived on by leaning close. The only occupants now were mice and the ghosts of paper dust, and neither of them would be filing an objection.

Lior glanced up the street, down the street. A woman shook a rug from a third-floor window two doors away. A boy dragged a sled with no snow for it over the cobbles, the runners shrieking. Nobody looked at the condemned building. Condemned buildings were the safest places in Vilna; even the authorities had finished pretending to care about what happened inside them.

The lock had been broken since the first winter after Father was taken. Lior pushed the door with their shoulder and stepped into the dark.

* * *

It smelled the way grief smells: cold ash, damp plaster, iron rusting into something sweet. Lior's eyes adjusted slowly. The great press still stood in the center of the room like a sleeping animal, sheeted in dust, its iron arm raised mid-gesture as if it had been interrupted in the act of speaking. Along the far wall, the flat cabinets where the plates lived. On the worktable by the north window, a stone of lithographic limestone, wiped clean but never washed properly — there was still a ghost of a coastline in the grease, if you knew how to look.

Father had taught them how to look.

*You don't see ink, Liorke. You see what ink is doing. Every line is either saying something or hiding something, and the best lines do both at once.*

Lior was nine years old, standing on a crate so they could reach the press bed, and Father's hands moved over the plate like a priest's over a psalter. It was a map of the lake country east of the city — harmless, beautiful, the kind of thing that hung in schoolrooms. Rivers in blue. Roads in black. And along the southern edge, in the marshland where nothing official existed, a cluster of dots no larger than pinpricks, spaced too evenly to be accidental.

"What are those?" Lior whispered, though whispering was unnecessary; the shop was always loud with the hiss of the gas ring and the groan of the rollers.

"Homesteads," Father said. "Farms."

"There are no farms there."

"There are." He tapped the plate once, gently, as if waking it. "They're just not on anyone else's map. Maps are arguments, Liorke. A man who draws a map is telling you what he believes exists. So we believe very carefully." He took Lior's finger and pressed it to one dot. "Ink here. Blank there. That's the whole trade. Everything else is just keeping your hand steady while you decide."

That winter, men came at night and left with maps sewn into their coats, and Father never asked where they were going, and the dots in the southern marshes grew more numerous, and Lior learned to grind ink fine enough that a line could be mistaken for a shadow, a shadow for nothing at all.

Now Lior stood in the ruin of that education and let the cold settle into their bones.

* * *

They went to the cabinets first, because if anything remained, it would be there. The drawers opened with a sound like teeth grinding. Mothballs someone had scattered long ago, dissolved to crust. Paper gone soft as cloth. Most of the plates were gone — confiscated or melted or sold by neighbors with good survival instincts — but in the bottom drawer, wrapped in oilcloth that Father had oiled himself, three plates survived.

Lior lifted them into the light from the window. The first was the lake country again, the dots multiplied into a constellation. The second was a street plan of Vilna itself, but wrong — deliberately, lovingly wrong — with courtyards marked that appeared on no municipal survey, passages between houses that the occupation administration did not know connected, wells that had been sealed and forgotten by everyone except the people who drank from them secretly. The third plate Lior turned over twice before understanding it, because it was nearly blank: a map with streets and rivers and not one single name upon it. Not a street name. Not a district. Nothing.

"Why is this one empty?" Lior had asked, at fourteen, holding it up.

"Because that's how they draw the world," Father said. He was cleaning the rollers, his sleeves rolled, forearms black to the elbow. "Take away the names and you can move people around like furniture. You can't demolish a home. You can only demolish an address." He dried his hands and took the plate and held it so it caught the window light. "Someday you'll understand why I keep it. It's not a map, Liorke. It's a mirror. I keep it so I remember what we are working against."

Lior set the blank plate down last, carefully, the way you set down something that might still be listening.

* * *

They worked through the morning without deciding to. That was the trick of the body, Lior had learned in the hospital: it decided things ahead of you and let your mind catch up later. Hands swept the worktable. Hands tested the press — the screw still turned, the rollers still had give, the bed was level, God knew how, unless Father had shimmed it against exactly this future, against the day his child would come back needing the machine to be ready. Hands found the tin box under the floorboard behind the coal bin where the good ink lived, and found inside it, incredibly, three cakes of lampblack ink gone hard as soap, and a small bottle of turpentine half full, stoppered with wax.

Enough. Enough for what?

Lior sat down on the crate — the same crate, or its descendant, warped now, one leg shortened by rot — and took out the ledger.

It lived in the binding of Gray's Anatomy, sliced into the spine between the boards, sixty pages of thin stock that Lior stole a few sheets at a time from the hospital's own stores, so that even the paper was complicit. They unwrapped it now in the workshop's cold light and turned to the last written page.

Forty-one names. Forty-one men whose mouths had made sounds the officers' forms declined to hear — *Yankl* reduced to initial *J.*, *Berele Kahan* reduced to *K., B., laborer*, villages struck off, wives unmentioned, ages rounded down or up until the boys became men and the men became numbers. Forty-one entries in a hand Lior had trained to be small and regular and utterly without character, because character could be identified, and identification could be traced, and tracing could end in a courtyard wall.

They had started the ledger as devotion. They knew that now, knew it the way you know a fire has become something else than warmth — when it starts eating the furniture. In the beginning it had been simple: a man dying in Ward Six says *tell my wife in Eisiskes my name is Yitzhak Shmerl Rozen and I forgave her*, and Lior writes it down because the form will say *R., J., died 04:10* and then the man will have never existed, and someone in Eisiskes will wait forever. Writing him whole felt like handing him back his life at the last moment, like pressing his face into wax and getting back a person instead of a statistic.

But somewhere along the winter, the ledger had changed weight. Lior could point to the page where it happened — entry twenty-nine, a boy named Motl whose real age was fifteen and whose papers said nineteen, and Lior writing *fifteen* in the margin and feeling, for the first time, not tenderness but anger, hot and specific, aimed at the form, at the pen that made the form, at the hand that held the pen, at the whole apparatus whose genius was omission. After entry twenty-nine the ledger stopped being only a memorial. It kept a second column now, narrow, in pencil: *what the report omitted.* Villages. Wives. Ages. The truth of a man's mouth against the lie of the paperwork, side by side, so that any future reader — and Lior forced themselves to imagine such a reader, some day when the ice of history also broke — could hold the two documents together and see the gap between them, and understand that the gap was the crime.

That was the word Lior had avoided for months and could avoid no longer. Not memorial. Accusation. Forty-one counts and climbing. And accusations, unlike prayers, got people shot — the accuser, the accused's family, the man who ground the ink. Lior had known this abstractly since the first entry. But sitting in the workshop with the ledger open on their knees, they understood it concretely, physically, the way Father had understood ink: every line saying something, hiding something, both at once. The ledger said *these people existed.* It hid *where the evidence lives.* And it could only keep doing both if it survived, whole, in a form worth reading.

A list in a stolen notebook was a secret. Secrets die with their keepers — Tomas had told Lior that, or Lior had told Tomas; the boundary between what they each knew was dissolving lately. But a printed atlas — forty-one names, ninety, two hundred, set in rows, formatted not as a ledger but as a map of the city itself, each street holding its dead, each courtyard its count, the way Father's plates had held farms that officially did not exist — that was not a secret. That was a document. Documents could be copied. Copied things could not be killed by killing one person. Father had understood this about farms and escape routes. Lior understood it now about the dead.

The ledger could not simply survive. It had to multiply.

* * *

Lior spent the afternoon taking inventory like a quartermaster, because grief was useless without arithmetic.

Press: serviceable. Rollers: cracked but true. Ink: sufficient for perhaps eighty pages, if the plates were cut fine. Type: the case of movable type had been ransacked years ago — Lior counted eleven sorts remaining, a handful of e's and n's and one lonesome ampersand — which meant type-setting was dead, and everything would have to go by plate, drawn by hand, reversed, onto zinc or stone. Lior could do it. Their hand had learned steadiness at age nine on this exact table. Whether it could learn speed was another question.

Paper: none here. The hospital's stores were watched; thefts beyond the few sheets a week already vanished into the anatomy text would be noticed. This was a problem for a different hour.

Light: the north window gave four usable hours, less as the days lengthened muddily toward thaw — and thaw was the deadline under everything, ticking. The demolition notice said *upon seasonal thaw,* and the ice on the river said the same thing every night now, a low groaning from the direction of the water, like an animal turning over in its sleep. Two weeks. Perhaps three. Perhaps less, if February ended mild.

Lior laid the blank plate on the worktable and looked at it a long time.

Streets. No names. Father's mirror.

And then the idea arrived whole, the way ideas arrive when they have been waiting years for their moment: turn the mirror around. Keep the streets. Fill the blanks. Draw the map of Vilna as the officials drew it — bare, deniable, innocent — and then engrave into each street, in letters fine enough to pass at a glance as texture, hatching, the grain of the paper, the names of everyone the reports had erased who had lived or died there. A census taker's eye would see a decorative street plan. A reader who knew how to look — who leaned close, who angled the plate to the light, who had been taught, the way a nine-year-old on a crate had been taught — would see that Pylimo Street held six names, that Subačiaus held nineteen, that the marshland south of the lakes held homesteads that officially did not exist.

An argument. A careful belief.

Lior's hands were shaking. Not from the cold.

* * *

It was nearly dark when they heard the footsteps.

Not soldiers' steps — those had a rhythm, a heel-first authority Lior could identify through two walls. These were lighter, unhurried, stopping once near the doorway as if consulting something. Lior slid the ledger into the oilcloth, the oilcloth into the cabinet, and was standing innocently among the dust with the blank plate in their hands when the door opened.

Marta Vogel stood in the frame, census satchel on her hip, breath fogging.

For a moment neither spoke. Then she looked past Lior at the press, the cabinets, the window, with the professional sweep of a woman measuring a room, and said:

"The records show this parcel belongs to a printer." Her voice was flat, administrative, but her eyes were not on the parcel. "I've come about a correction."

She stepped inside, and the door swung shut behind her, and Lior understood that the question too many had never actually been answered — it had only been waiting for a colder room.
