## 4. Marta's Second Visit

She came back on a Tuesday, which Lior would remember later because Tuesdays were the days the linen was counted, and there was no worse day to have a stranger in the ward watching hands move.

Lior saw her through the frost-ferned window first: a gray coat, a gray hat, a folder held against her chest like something warm she was carrying home. The census taker crossed the hospital courtyard in a straight line, unhurried, stepping around the drifts rather than through them, and Lior understood — with the particular clarity that fear gives, sharpening the world like a blade laid flat against the eye — that this woman had come before and would know exactly where to find them.

There were three possible responses. To be in the ward when she arrived. To be elsewhere and let her leave a card. To be found somewhere in between, mid-task, ordinary, unremarkable.

Lior chose the middle of a corridor, holding a basin of water they did not need, at a moment calculated to look accidental.

"Frau Lockridge." Marta Vogel stopped an arm's length away. Not too close. Never too close. "I hope I'm not disturbing the work."

"Nurse Lockridge," Lior said, and smiled the smile they had practiced for two years in every reflective surface the hospital owned. "We're all nurses here, Fräulein Vogel. It disturbs nothing."

"Census Office." She opened the folder against her forearm, and her gloved finger found the page as though it lived there. "A clerical error. Your household registration lists one dependent more than your declaration supports. I'm required to correct it by hand. You'll forgive the intrusion — paper is very demanding."

"Paper," Lior agreed, "is the most demanding patient I have."

Something moved behind Marta Vogel's eyes. Not warmth. Recognition, perhaps — the flicker of a person hearing their own language spoken by a stranger. Then it closed again, and she was only a functionary with a form.

"The error," she said. "It concerns your father."

The basin grew heavier in Lior's hands. Water has weight; grief has more; the trick is to hold both without spilling either. "My father died in thirty-nine," Lior said. "Before the war came here. Before any of this."

"So the declaration states." Her pen hovered. "And yet the old registry — the municipal one, from before — lists him at Pylimo Street as of November. A printer, I believe. Map plates." Her eyes came up, mild as milk. "Was he still printing in thirty-nine?"

"He was ill in thirty-nine." Each word set down like a stone in a wall, fitted, mortared. "He stopped working in the summer. The shop was already empty when he died. You can ask the neighbors — what's left of them."

"I may do that." She wrote. The nib scratched, a small dry sound, and Lior watched her hand and thought: she writes slowly on purpose. She wants me to watch her write. Everything this woman does is a question shaped like paperwork.

"And you," Marta said. "You keep the household accounts yourself now? Since his death?"

"Accounts?"

"Invoices, receipts. A record of what comes in and goes out." The pen paused above the line. "Most households do. Some keep very careful books."

"We keep what the ration office requires us to keep. Nothing more. There isn't enough bread to keep accounts of."

"No," Marta said softly. "There rarely is." She signed the correction with a small precise loop, blotted it, and slid the form into its folder. "Thank you for your patience, Nurse Lockridge. Corrections are tedious, but accuracy matters. A record that lies," she said, closing the folder, "is worse than no record at all. Don't you find?"

Lior heard the sentence land and could not tell if it was a trap or a confession. "I find," they said, "that records are made by people, and people are tired, and hungry, and afraid. The paper remembers what they can bear to write down. That's all any record is."

Marta Vogel looked at them for a long moment — long enough that somewhere down the corridor a door opened and shut, and the draft moved between them like a third presence. Then she inclined her head, exactly the correct degree, wished the ward good health, and walked out through the courtyard in her straight gray line, leaving prints in the snow that the next snowfall would erase, though Lior stood watching long after they were gone, feeling the way one feels after a needle is drawn from a vein: emptied, and strangely lighter, and aware that something has been taken.

* * *

Marta Vogel walked back across the city with the folder under her arm and the conversation folded inside it like a letter she would reread forty times.

She had lied about the clerical error. There was no error. She had created it herself, three weeks ago, with a pen and a borrowed key to the pre-war municipal files — added the father's name back onto the Pylimo Street registry where some efficient hand had struck it, then waited to see who would flinch. It was a crude instrument. But people always flinched at their dead. The living could lie about themselves all day; it was the names of the dead that pulled the truth up out of them like a hook through soft tissue.

*He was ill in thirty-nine.* Delivered without a breath of hesitation. *The shop was already empty.*

Either true, or rehearsed so thoroughly it had become true.

Marta turned down Vokiečių Street past the shuttered café, and let herself think the thought she permitted only while walking, in motion, where it couldn't settle and grow roots: *the ledger exists.*

She had believed it since autumn. Belief was perhaps too soft a word. She had evidence — thin as ice in March, but evidence. A pharmacist in Snipiskes who processed morphine for the military hospital had mentioned, in the way men mention things when they want to seem important, that one of the nurses kept extraordinary notes. That patients' families, sometimes, received word of a son's last hours — details no official report contained. Details that existed nowhere, unless someone was writing them down.

Nowhere official. Nowhere safe. Somewhere private, careful, hidden — but written.

She had pulled the personnel files. Lior Lockridge: twenty-six years old, qualified as a nurse in 1939, transferred to the military hospital when the occupation reorganized the civilian wards. Father deceased. No siblings listed. No dependents. The file was clean the way swept floors are clean — you could smell that something had been carried out of it recently, or never allowed in at all.

And the father had been a printer. Not a jobbing printer — a mapmaker. Engraved plates. She had gone to the municipal archive on her own time, after hours, and asked to see what remained of the Lockridge workshop inventory, and the archivist, a stooped man with ink-stained cuffs, had shown her a single ledger sheet: copperplates confiscated in the first month of occupation, forty-one items, and one line item marked simply *assorted plates, misc., destroyed.* Destroyed, or not. Archivists wrote *destroyed* the way nurses wrote *died quietly in the night* — sometimes as fact, sometimes as mercy, occasionally as cover.

Someone in that family had spent a lifetime making marks on metal so that places could be found again. And someone in that family, Marta was increasingly certain, was now making marks on paper so that people could be.

Her brother's name had been removed from four separate registries in eleven months. She had traced it the way one traces a leak through a ceiling — stain by stain, room by room — until she stood beneath the source: a deportation transport list, typewritten, where his line ended in a smudged half-character, as if the clerk's hand had hesitated and then simply pressed on. Abram Vogel. Age nineteen. Occupation: apprentice bookbinder. Destination column: blank. Every subsequent list: absent. He had not died, as far as paper was concerned. He had never been anywhere at all.

People said, *he's gone,* and meant it kindly, meaning *dead,* giving her the mercy of an ending. But Marta had come to understand that gone was not the same as dead. Dead entered records. Gone was erasure — and erasure was not an ending but a theft, ongoing, compounding daily. Somewhere her brother might be alive inside a labor column, a camp register, a hospital roll, his name misspelled, misfiled, waiting for someone to read it aloud. Or he was dead and unmourned by any document, which was its own horror: a boy who had loved books reduced to nothing that could be shelved.

So she hunted ledgers. Not the occupier's ledgers — theirs she knew, theirs she served inside like a fly sealed in amber — but the other kind. The kind that appeared, always, wherever the official record showed gaps: the private tallies, the memory-books, the lists sewn into coat linings and buried in garden beds. In Kaunas she had found a rabbi's notebook of the missing; in Daugavpils, a schoolteacher's roll of deported students. These documents were contraband of the deadliest kind, and the people who kept them guarded them like hearts outside their bodies.

She should report the suspicion. That was her duty; that was the machine she sat inside; a nurse keeping unauthorized lists of military personnel was not a romantic figure but a security risk, and the penalty was known to everyone and discussed by no one. Her superior, Reimer, had a nose for irregularities and a filing cabinet for a soul. One memo from her, one carefully worded suspicion, and the matter would pass out of her hands forever — into interrogation rooms she had seen the men enter and never described leaving.

She told herself she was waiting for confirmation. That a good census officer verified before reporting. That she needed to know whether the nurse's ledger was what she believed — a memorial — or something else entirely: resistance correspondence, arms inventories, names of partisans. If it were the latter, hesitation would be treason. If it were the former—

If it were the former, then somewhere in Vilna a stranger was doing, in secret and at mortal risk, precisely what Marta prayed some unknown hand was doing for Abram in some unknown place: writing the names down so they could be found.

She reached her lodging as the light failed, climbed the stairs past the landlady's radio murmuring victory, and locked her door, and took from under a floorboard a slim exercise book, a child's schoolbook really, with a sailboat stamped on the cover. On its pages, in her smallest hand, she had copied every fragment of Abram she possessed: the school reports, the apprenticeship certificate, the last postcard, the description of him from a cousin who'd seen him loaded onto a truck — *tall for his age, wearing father's coat.* An atlas of one person. A map with almost nothing on it.

Tonight she added a new entry, dated, precise:

*Lockridge, L. Nurse, military hospital. Father: engraver, Pylimo St., d. 1939 (unverified). Subject denied keeping records. Denied fluently. Denial fluent = practice. Practice = something practiced upon.*

She looked at the last line for a long time. Then she closed the book and put it back beneath the floor, and lay on her narrow bed listening to the river ice groaning in the distance — a sound the whole city pretended not to hear, vast and structural, like the earth clearing its throat before it speaks.

* * *

That night the ward smelled of carbolic and thaw, because the walls had begun to weep, condensation tracking down the plaster in slow silver veins. Lior made rounds with the lamp turned low, checking pulses that mostly beat steadily, pausing longest at the beds of men whose charts were thinnest — the ones the paperwork had sketched in two lines and a serial number.

*Private, male, approx. 30. Gunshot wound, left thigh. Improving.*

Approximately thirty. As though a man could be approximately alive.

In the third bed by the window, a feverish boy from the new transport slept with his fists curled, and on his chart, in Lior's required handwriting, stood an age Lior did not believe: eighteen, the number every conscript claimed and every clerk accepted because accepting it was easier than asking. His real age — sixteen, he'd mumbled in delirium, sixteen in April, mama makes kugel for my birthday — went into the ledger that night, along with his village, Viekšniai, and his mother's name, which he had said twice, the second time clearly, as though correcting the first attempt.

Lior wrote by feel, the anatomy text open on their knees beneath the blanket, the stub of pencil traveling down the hollow spine of the binding where the pages had been slit and resewn. The names went in small, tight, slanted — disguised as binding notes, as repair jottings, meaningless to anyone who didn't know the code of it. Seventy-three names now. Seventy-three lives compressed into a finger's width of hidden space.

They hated how easily the lying came now. That afternoon with Marta Vogel — *he was ill, the shop was empty, we keep nothing* — each falsehood had left their mouth smooth as a pill swallowed whole, no taste, no catch in the throat. Two years ago, lying had cost them a night of nausea. Now it cost nothing, and that was the thing to hate: not the danger, but the competence. Every smooth lie was another layer grown over whoever they had been at their father's elbow, the child who believed a map was a promise that a place would stay where you drew it.

Outside, the ice boomed — a long tectonic complaint, followed by silence, followed by a crack like a rifle shot that made one of the sleeping men cry out and call for his unit. Lior settled him, and stood a moment at the window, and saw below in the courtyard a single line of footprints leading in from the gate and stopping, hours old now, half-filled with fresh snow.

Stopping. Not crossing the courtyard to the gate again. Just — stopping, at the exact point where a person stands to look up at a lit window and count what they see.

Lior blew out the lamp and stood in the dark with their heart going hard, and understood that the census taker had not come for corrections at all, and that the question about accounts had been the gentlest of whatever questions were coming, and that somewhere across the frozen city Marta Vogel was awake too — hunter and hunted lying sleepless on opposite banks of the same widening water, each convinced the other held the thing they could not live without.

Downriver, faint but distinct, the ice spoke again.
