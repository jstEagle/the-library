## 5. What the Officers Omit

The transport came in at four in the afternoon, while the light was going the color of old bone.

I heard it before I saw it — the particular rhythm of a truck carrying men who cannot hold themselves steady, bodies knocking against the slatted sides like cargo that complains. By the time the gates opened I was already at my station, hands washed, basins laid out in their proper row. This is the discipline of hospital work: you make yourself into an instrument before the instruments are needed. You do not let your face arrive before your hands.

Twenty-three men. The orderlies counted them aloud because the officers would ask, and the officers only asked about numbers. Twenty-three wounded, transported from the rail yard at Paneriai's edge after what the accompanying sergeant called "an engagement." He said it the way a man describes weather. He did not say where the engagement had been. He did not say against whom. He handed over a single sheet of paper with a list of surnames and initial letters, as though the men had been abbreviated by the shelling itself.

*J. Abramavicius. M. Berman. S. Druk.* And so on down the page, twenty-three names reduced to their skeletons, the way officials write the world: first letters instead of first names, villages left off entirely, ages rounded to the nearest useful lie.

I took the paper without letting my eyes move too slowly across it. That is another discipline. A nurse who reads too carefully looks like a nurse who is looking for something.

The first man died while they were still lifting him from the truck bed.

He was very young — younger than me, I thought, though I could not have said how I knew. His coat had been cut away somewhere up the line and replaced with a blanket that smelled of engine oil, and his belly was bound in a field dressing gone brown-black at its center. His lips moved. The orderly beside me said, "He's asking for his mother," and I bent close, and what he was actually saying, in a whisper worn down to almost nothing, was a street name. *Vokieciu.* Vokieciu Street. As if the street were the mother. As if naming the street might carry him home the way a name carries a soul back to its body in the stories my grandmother told.

He died between one syllable of it and the next. I closed his mouth with two fingers, gently, the way you close a book you intend to finish reading later.

They carried him to the corner bay and someone threw a sheet over him, and the work went on. There were twenty-two others, and the evening had already begun, and in occupied Vilna the dead do not interrupt the schedule of the dying.

* * *

By nine o'clock there were three sheets over three faces in the corner bay.

The second was Berman — the initial *M*, the surname alone, nothing else on the officer's list. He lasted four hours. For two of them he was conscious enough to talk, and I sat with him through the worst of it because there was no one else to sit, because his file said *M. Berman* and nothing more, no next of kin, no address, no wife or mother who might be sent the news that would be sent to no one. He talked about a shop. He had been apprenticed to a furrier, he said, on a lane whose name I did not catch the first time and made him repeat twice, and his master had a daughter with a crooked tooth who laughed at everything, even the cold, even the war, especially the cold. He wanted to know if I thought she'd have heard. I said yes. It was the kindest available lie, and I have learned that kindness in this city is mostly a matter of choosing which lies cost least.

He gave me his first name at the end. Men do that — they save it, as if the surname belongs to the state and the first name belongs to whoever is holding your hand when the rest runs out. *Mendel,* he said. *Mendel Berman.* Then he said something else, softer, that I chose not to hear, because some things a person says belong to the person and not to the record.

The third died so fast there was almost nothing to do but fail at preventing it. A chest wound; the surgeons shook their heads before they finished looking. He never woke. He was listed as *S. Druk, age 19.*

He was not nineteen. I knew it from his hands, which were a boy's hands still, knuckles unscarred, nails bitten to the quick. I knew it from the size of him — the orderlies had handled him like furniture because he weighed less than the stretcher's frame suggested. Nineteen was what the transport papers said because nineteen was the age at which a boy stopped being a child the army had no use for and became a man the army could spend. Somewhere behind that number was a real age. Fifteen, perhaps. Fourteen. A mother somewhere had signed something, or someone had signed it for her, and the ink had turned her son into a number that lied.

I stood in the corner bay with the three of them and the lamp burning low above, and I felt the thing come up in me that I have learned to call quiet fury, because calling it anything louder would get people killed.

* * *

Here is what the official report will say about tonight.

It will say that a transport of twenty-three wounded arrived at 16:00. It will say that three subsequently expired of wounds received in action. It will list them as J. Abramavicius, M. Berman, S. Druk, aged respectively 24, 27, and 19 — the ages invented or inherited from other papers, copied forward from documents that were themselves copies. It will note the disposal of remains according to protocol. It will be filed. It will be accurate in every particular that matters to the men who read such reports, and wrong in every particular that matters to God, if there is a God, and to the mothers, who are certain.

What the report omits:

It omits that J. Abramavicius spent his last forty minutes trying to say a street name, and that the street was Vokieciu, which narrows a whole city down to a few hundred doors, behind any one of which there may be a woman who lit a candle tonight for a son whose death notice will never come because the son died under a letter instead of a name.

It omits that Mendel Berman was apprenticed to a furrier, that he loved a girl with a crooked tooth, that he was afraid — he admitted it near the end, in the voice men use when fear has stopped being shameful because there is no time left to be ashamed — and that his fear had a specific shape: not death itself, but dying unwitnessed. Dying as an initial. He gripped my wrist hard enough to leave marks and said, *you'll remember, won't you, that I was Mendel* — and I said yes, and unlike the lie about the girl with the crooked tooth, that one I intend to keep.

It omits that S. Druk was a boy. That his hands had never worked a day honest enough to scar them. That whatever village or cellar or orphaned household produced him, it produced him too young for the uniform they buried him in. That the number nineteen on his card is a small forgery, one of ten thousand small forgeries committed daily by clerks who would be outraged to hear themselves called liars.

And beyond these three, it omits the ordinary arithmetic of the ward: the man in bay six who flinches whenever a door bangs because of what happened to him at a place he will not name; the man in bay nine who has begun each of the last three nights by reciting the names of his platoon, checking them like coins, and who stops each time at a name that isn't there anymore; the smell of the antiseptic failing against the smell underneath it, which is the smell of a city bleeding quietly into its own cellars.

The report records none of this because the report was not written to remember anyone. It was written to account for everyone — to close every ledger the state keeps open, so that no loose person, no unexplained death, no unnumbered grief remains to trouble the columns.

That is the difference, I understand now, between their books and mine. Their paperwork exists so that no one can be missing. Mine exists so that no one can be erased. These sound like the same ambition. They are opposites.

* * *

After midnight, when the ward had settled into its breathing and the night sister was asleep at her desk with her chin on her fist, I did the work I actually came here to do.

I waited until the corridor was empty. I checked the door. Then I drew out the anatomy text — *Gray's*, third edition, its spine cracked white with use — and opened the binding where the boards had been hollowed by my father's own hand, years ago, when he taught me that the safest place for a thing is inside the thing no one wants. No soldier searches an anatomy text. To them it is already full of things worse than secrets: diagrams of the body opened like a map of a conquered country.

I wrote by the light of a shielded candle stub, in the shorthand of letters I invented myself, half Lithuanian, half Hebrew script, half pure invention — a cipher that exists nowhere else in the world, which means that when I die, the key dies with me, unless I finish the atlas and put the key in the right hands first. I am aware of the arrogance of this. I am aware of the risk. I write anyway.

Tonight's entries:

*Abramavicius, J. Died 16 March (I date everything; the officials date nothing). Last words: Vokieciu Street. Age unknown. Believed from Vilna or its outskirts. Mother unknown — find her, if the street survives.*

*Berman, Mendel. Furrier's apprentice. Lane near [illegible — return to the ward, ask the man in bay eleven, who comes from the same quarter]. Loved a girl with a crooked tooth. Afraid. Did not want to die as an initial. He was Mendel. Write it so.*

*Druk, S. Listed 19. Not 19. A boy. Chest wound; never regained speech. Real name, real age, real village: all taken from him before he reached me. Whoever signed him into the army at fourteen or fifteen — write that signature down someday too. Accuse it.*

I sat back and looked at the page and understood that something in me had shifted, the way ice shifts before you hear it.

Until now I had thought of the ledger as a memorial. A candle made of ink. A way of keeping faith with the dead because the living had been forbidden to. That was how it began — names copied from dying men's lips, tucked into the anatomy text like flowers in a missal. Devotion. Grief with a system.

But writing *Druk* tonight, writing *not 19* beneath the official lie, I felt no devotion at all. I felt accusation. Every entry was no longer only a rescue; it was a count. Here is what your report omitted. Here is the boy you aged five years to make him legal ammunition. Here is the street name your clerk didn't think worth the space. Here is the fear, the furrier's shop, the girl with the crooked tooth — all of it evidence. All of it testimony against the machinery that reduces a human life to a surname and an initial and calls the reduction efficiency.

A memorial asks to be visited. An accusation demands to be answered. And an unanswered accusation does not sleep; it waits. It sits in a hollowed-out book on a shelf in an occupied city and it waits for the day the machinery stumbles, the day the officers' paperwork burns or scatters or falls into other hands, and then every name in it becomes a question asked aloud in front of witnesses: *where is the woman from Vokieciu Street, and why did no one tell her?*

Someone will pay for this ledger, eventually. Perhaps me. Perhaps whoever carries it after me. Perhaps the men whose initials stand accused in its pages, if history ever grows teeth again. I used to believe I could keep the ledger pure — grief without consequence, remembrance without risk. That belief is dead now, and I buried it tonight between Berman and the boy, and I did not say kaddish over it.

Because here is the truth I arrived at, standing over three covered faces with a candle burning down to its nail: a record kept safely is a record kept useless. If the atlas stays hidden forever, it is just another grave — a mass grave of names, tidy, undisturbed, serving no one. The names must move. They must reach the women on Vokieciu Street and the furrier's daughter and whatever remains of the boy Druk's family, and reaching them means leaving this room, leaving the hollow spine, leaving safety entirely.

Which means the winter has become a deadline, not a shelter.

I blew out the candle and returned the text to its shelf, spine aligned with its neighbors, innocent as bone.

Then I stood a moment in the dark of the corridor and listened. Under the wind, under the groan of the hospital pipes, I could hear it faintly, far off toward the east of the city: the river, working at its chains. The ice was beginning to talk in its sleep. In a few weeks it would wake.

And I thought: the thaw will break the river, and the river's breaking will bring the demolition crews to Pylimo Street, and when my father's workshop comes down, the last place on earth where those names could be printed properly — set in metal, struck onto paper, made heavy and portable and real — goes down with it.

Three sheets in the corner bay. Twenty names in the hollow book. And outside, the ice counting down.

I went back to the ward and began the night rounds, face arranged, hands steady, invisible as ever. Invisible — I have used the word about myself for years as if it were armor. But walking those corridors tonight I understood that invisibility has a price the officers never itemize: everything you witness while unseen accumulates. It compounds, silently, like debt. And debts, in this city, always come due when the ice breaks.
