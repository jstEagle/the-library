## 6. The Ice Reports

The river began to speak on a Tuesday night, and Lior heard it from three streets away.

They had been walking home along the Vilkmergė road with their collar up and their hands buried in their sleeves, the kind of walk that had become a discipline in itself—eyes forward, pace even, a person so unremarkable that the sentry at the corner no longer bothered to look up. Then the sound came rolling up through the frozen ground like something enormous turning over in its sleep: a groan, long and low, followed by a crack that split the air the way a rifle shot splits it, except deeper, older, as though the earth itself had a bone to break.

Lior stopped. The sentry stopped too. For a moment the two of them stood listening together, two strangers bound by the same animal attention, and then the sentry shrugged and lit a cigarette and Lior walked on, heart hammering not with fear of the man but with fear of the arithmetic now unfolding in their chest.

The ice was breaking up.

Not breaking—not yet. Groaning. Waking. The Neris lay under a meter of gray-white plate, and every night now it flexed and muttered and tested its own seams, the way an old house tests its rafters before the roof comes down. The thaw was weeks away still, maybe less. But the river had begun its counting, and Lior knew how to read a countdown when they heard one, because they had been living inside one since autumn.

At home—the narrow room above the cobbler's, where the ceiling stain had grown over the winter into a map of some country that didn't exist—Lior lit the candle stub down to its last centimeter and pulled the anatomy text from beneath the floorboard.

The ledger came out of its binding the way it always did, reluctantly, pages whispering against each other. Forty-one names now. Forty-one lives reduced to ink in Lior's cramped, careful hand, each entry formatted with a precision that would have made their father proud: full name, village or street, age where known, next of kin where spoken aloud, date of death where death was the fact, date of transport where transport was. Some entries were only fragments—a surname, a wife's first name, the word *mill* where a man had run out of breath before he could say which mill, in which town, beside which river. Those gaps were the worst part. Not the deaths. The gaps. Every blank line was a door closing somewhere in the world, a family that would wait forever for a letter that would never come because the man who should have written it had died between one breath and the next, and the only record of him was a number in an officer's report and a whisper in a nurse's ear.

Lior spread the loose sheets across the table and did what they did every night now: counted backward.

The atlas needed finishing. Not just the names—that work went on as long as the war did—but the printing. The plates were at Pylimo Street, waiting in the dark of the workshop, and the final format lived only in Lior's head: each name set into the map of Vilna itself, street by street, so that the city would hold its dead the way a body holds its scars. It was their father's method inverted. He had printed maps to show people where they were; Lior would print one to show them who they had lost getting there.

And the workshop had perhaps three weeks to live.

* * *

The demolition order went up on Thursday, pasted to the door of the building that housed the district office, in German and Lithuanian, in the bureaucratic font that always reminded Lior of teeth.

STRUCTURES ALONG THE RIVERFRONT SCHEDULED FOR CLEARANCE UPON BREAKING OF ICE. OWNERS AND OCCUPANTS MUST REMOVE PERSONAL PROPERTY PRIOR TO THAT DATE. AFTER WHICH DATE ALL REMAINING MATERIALS WILL BE CONSIDERED ABANDONED AND DESTROYED WITHOUT FURTHER NOTICE.

*Abandoned.* Lior stood in front of the notice for a long time, letting the word settle. Their father had been dead eleven years, but the press on Pylimo Street—his press, his plates, his drawers of type sorted into wooden cases worn smooth by his hands—was not abandoned. It was orphaned. There was a difference, and the difference was Lior, standing in the cold reading a sentence that gave them until the ice broke to empty a life out of a room.

Three weeks. Maybe four. Maybe ten days, if the February sun kept doing what it had been doing lately—lingering past five, warming the south-facing walls until the icicles dripped like slow clocks.

Lior ran the calculation again, because running calculations was the only prayer they had left.

Forty-three names, as of last night. Two more men had given fragments on Wednesday, half-names, dying syllables. To finish the atlas properly meant setting every name in type, proofing each plate, pulling clean copies—at least six sets, enough to send by separate roads—and sealing them against water. Six sets, three hundred-odd impressions, plus the master plates themselves. Alone, working nights after twelve-hour shifts at the hospital, walking both directions through a city that checked papers at three corners: Lior estimated forty nights of work.

They had maybe twenty-five.

The math didn't close. It hadn't closed for days, and Lior had been refusing to look at it directly, the way you refuse to look at a wound. Now, standing before the demolition order with the wind cutting through their coat, they looked at it straight on.

*I cannot do this alone.*

The admission landed like a diagnosis. Because alone was safe. Alone was the entire architecture of survival Lior had built since 1939—one person, invisible, unremarkable, owing nothing to anyone and owed nothing by anyone. Every name in the ledger was a thread tied to another human being, and threads could be pulled, and pulled threads unraveled. If Lior brought someone into the workshop, into the work, into the knowledge—then the ledger was no longer a secret. It was a conspiracy. And conspiracies ended at the same wall, in the same yard, with the same sound.

But the math didn't close.

Lior turned from the notice and walked toward the hospital, and behind them the river groaned again, patient as a creditor.

* * *

Tomas found Lior, which was itself remarkable, because almost no one found Lior anymore. Invisibility was a craft, and Lior practiced it daily: the same route, the same slouched gait, the same face arranged into mild exhausted neutrality. Yet on Friday evening, at the hospital's service entrance, amid the carts of ash and the buckets of ice-melt, a boy detached himself from the shadow of the woodpile as if he'd been nailed there waiting.

"You're the one who takes the night shift," the boy said.

Lior looked at him. Fourteen, maybe fifteen. Thin in the way of boys whose bodies were being spent faster than they could be fed—sharp wrists, a coat two winters too small, cheeks chapped raw. His eyes were quick and dark and entirely unsentimental. He held a canvas satchel against his hip the way soldiers hold rifles.

"I'm whoever you think I am," Lior said, and moved for the door.

"Six grosze a delivery," the boy said to their back. "Documents. Bundles. I don't read what I carry."

That stopped Lior, more than anything else could have. *I don't read what I carry.* Everyone in this city read everything; reading was survival, reading other people's mail, other people's faces, the notices on the walls. A boy who announced his own blindness was either very stupid or very smart.

Lior turned back. "Who told you I need things carried?"

"Nobody told me." The boy shrugged. "I watch. You go to Pylimo Street twice a week, late, you stay two hours, you come out with nothing. Nobody goes to a dead print shop to do nothing." He said it flatly, without triumph, a boy reporting weather. "My name is Tomas. I carried parcels for the bookbinder on Dominikonų before they closed him. I know which checkpoints sleep and which ones count. And I'm hungry in a way that's going to start making me stupid soon."

"And if what I asked you to carry got you shot?"

The boy considered this with the seriousness of someone who had already priced his own death and found it affordable. "Then it gets me shot. Everything gets everybody shot eventually. At least this way I'd be carrying something." His jaw tightened, and for a moment the flat merchant's mask slipped, and underneath was something younger and much more wounded. "The Germans took my family off a list. Not with guns. With paper. A clerk wrote us onto a list in nice handwriting and we stopped existing. So." He lifted one shoulder. "Paper matters. I know what paper does. I want to move the right paper for once."

The wind came down the alley and found every seam in Lior's coat. Somewhere beyond the rooftops the river cracked, a short sharp report like a door slamming, and both of them flinched and then pretended they hadn't.

"I can't pay you money," Lior said slowly. Each word was a plank laid over a chasm. "I can pay bread. Real bread, not sawdust bread. And I can promise you that whatever you carry, if you're caught with it, it's nothing. Laundry lists. Inventory sheets. Nothing that can hang you."

"That's a lie," Tomas said. Not accusing. Just precise.

"It's a necessary lie," Lior said. "The best kind I have."

Something flickered across the boy's face—not a smile, but the place where a smile might someday grow back. "When?"

"Tomorrow night. Eleven o'clock. The alley behind the tannery on Pylimo—you know it?"

"I know every alley on Pylimo."

"Come hungry," Lior said. "Bring your satchel. And Tomas—" They waited until his eyes came up. "You said you don't read what you carry. Keep that. Whatever you see in that shop, whatever you hear, it stays in the room. Not because I don't trust you. Because trust is a debt and I can't afford any more debts."

Tomas looked at them for a long moment with those quick dark eyes that had already seen too much arithmetic of survival for a face that young.

"You're afraid of me," he said, surprised, as if discovering something in a shop window. "You've been alone so long you're afraid of one skinny kid with a satchel."

"Yes," Lior said. Because lying to him about that felt worse than all the other lies.

"Good," Tomas said. "Be afraid. Fear means it matters." He swung the satchel up onto his shoulder and melted back toward the woodpile, and then paused, half-turned. "Eleven o'clock. Don't be late. Hungry boys get impatient."

* * *

That night Lior sat with the ledger open under the candle and tried to feel hope, and found instead something smaller and stranger: a loosening. Like a fist unclenching after months.

For two years the invisibility had held. One nurse, unremarkable, moving through the occupied city like a draft through a keyhole. And it had worked—it had kept them alive, kept the ledger alive, kept the workshop undiscovered. But it had also kept them *small*, and now Lior understood that smallness had a price measured in nights. Twenty-five nights of work and twenty-five available, and a gap in between that only another pair of hands could fill.

The river would break. That was certain. The river always broke, and when it did it would take the ice, and the demolition crews would take the workshop, and the spring flood of paperwork would roll through the district offices erasing another street, another block, another set of names that had never been written down anywhere at all.

Unless they were.

Lior trimmed the wick and looked at the forty-three entries, and for the first time let themselves imagine the finished thing: the atlas spread open, the city drawn in fine lines, and inside every street the names nested like roots under pavement—Rudnickų, Jatkowa, Pylimo—each one holding its dead, each dead one holding a street, the whole city becoming a single act of witness. Their father's craft turned against the erasure. Ink answering ink.

It would need hands. It would need a courier who didn't read what he carried and a census taker who asked one question too many, and it would need luck, and luck in Vilna in 1942 was a currency so debased it hardly circulated anymore.

But the math closed. Barely. Ugly. Closed.

Outside, the Neris groaned through the dark, flexing its ancient shoulders, counting down. Lior blew out the candle and lay in the cold and listened, and thought: *twenty-five nights,* and thought: *one skinny boy with a satchel,* and thought—unwillingly, inevitably, with a feeling like stepping onto ice that has already begun to sing—

*and her.*
