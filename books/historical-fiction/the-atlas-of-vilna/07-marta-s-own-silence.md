## 7. Marta's Own Silence

The room Marta Vogel rented on Subačiaus Street had one window, one chair, one bed with a blanket that smelled of the landlady's soap and someone else's life. It was enough. She had learned to need very little; needing little meant owing little, and owing little meant no one could take anything from her that mattered.

The thing that mattered was in the bottom drawer of the bureau, beneath a false lining of winter stockings, wrapped in oilcloth against damp.

She did not open it tonight. She knew its contents by heart. She could have recited them the way a priest recites a liturgy, and in a sense she did, every night, lying awake while the pipes in the wall ticked and groaned: *Jonas Vogel. Born 14 March 1919, Kaunas. Deported 11 September, destination unrecorded. Status: unknown.*

*Unknown.* The word was a splinter working its way toward her heart for two years now. Not dead — dead would have been terrible but legible, dead would have been a line in some ledger somewhere, a cross beside a name, an end she could grieve at the edge of. Unknown meant erased. Unknown meant that somewhere in the vast machinery of paperwork that had swallowed half of Europe, her brother had slipped between the teeth of it and been gone.

She had written to every office that still answered letters. She had stood in queues in three cities, holding her papers, her proof of relation, her patience, her small respectable voice. She had filled out forms asking after a man as if he were a lost parcel. *Last known address. Distinguishing marks. Purpose of travel.* Once, in Kaunas, a clerk with ink-stained fingers had looked at her over his spectacles and said, gently, the way one speaks to the simple-minded: "Fräulein, if he is not in the files, then officially he does not exist."

She had thanked him. She had walked out into the street and stood in the cold for a long time, watching her breath, thinking: *then I will make him exist.*

That was when she had begun to understand what her work actually was. She had taken the census position because it was shelter — a stamp on her papers, a reason to move through the city unquestioned, a wage. But somewhere in the second month of door-knocking and form-filling she had realized that she was not merely recording the city. She was deciding it. Every name she entered became real to the men who ran things; every name she omitted — or misspelled, or shifted by a single digit of an apartment number — became slightly less real, a little easier to lose. The lists were not mirrors of the world. They were hands, and they could close.

So she had begun, quietly, without ever admitting it to herself as a plan, to hunt ledgers. Other people's records. Hospital registers, parish books, factory rosters, the shipping manifests of the disappeared. She told herself it was method: if Jonas existed anywhere, it would be on paper, because paper was where everyone lived now. But there were nights when she caught herself turning pages with something closer to hunger than method, and she understood that she was not looking for her brother so much as she was looking for the principle of him — proof that a vanished name could be found again, that erasure was not final, that the machinery could be made to give back what it took.

And lately — this was the thought she kept pushing away, the thought that returned each night like a cat scratching at a door — lately her search had narrowed to one person.

*

The nurse. Lior Lockridge.

Marta turned the name over as she sat by the window, watching the last grey light fail over the rooftops toward the river. Lockridge. An odd name for this city, foreign-sounding, the kind of name that had been translated once and then left alone too long to translate back. The kind of name a family adopted to survive an earlier century's troubles, and which might be adopted again.

There was nothing concrete. She knew that, and it maddened her, because she was good at concrete — she dealt in dates, addresses, household numbers all day long. What she had instead was pattern, and patterns were harder to write down.

The nurse answered questions correctly. Too correctly. When Marta had asked about the birthplace, the answer had come out smooth as a coin worn from handling — rehearsed, polished by repetition. People who had nothing to hide answered clumsily; they hesitated, they corrected themselves, they offered extra details because truth is generous and lies are economical. Lior Lockridge gave exactly the required information and not one syllable more, and behind the correct answers there had been a stillness Marta recognized, because she wore it herself every day: the stillness of a person listening to their own heartbeat, waiting for it to betray them.

And there was the pen. Her own pen, pausing on the page a beat too long — she had felt it happen, felt her own hand hesitate as if the paper itself had flinched. Why? Because of the question about the burial? Because something in the nurse's face had moved, some flicker of grief or fear or recognition? She had replayed that visit forty times. In thirty-nine of the replays, Lockridge was simply a careful person in a careless world. In the fortieth —

In the fortieth, Lockridge was keeping something.

It was the hospital that convinced her, though she could not have said how. She had visited twice more since the census — once to verify occupancy numbers, once under a thin pretext about a duplicate entry — and both times she had watched the nurse work. Watched the hands. A nurse's hands told everything: they touched the dying differently when they were only doing their duty versus when they were listening. And Lockridge listened. Marta had seen it from across the ward — the lean-in, the stillness, the way the nurse's lips sometimes moved silently after a patient finished speaking, as if repeating something. Rehearsing something. *Keeping* something.

Dying men talked. Everyone knew that. They talked in fragments, they gave up names and villages and sins, and whatever they said went into the air and was gone, unless —

Unless someone was collecting it.

The thought arrived fully formed one night as she lay in the narrow bed, and it frightened her so much she sat up in the dark, heart hammering. If the nurse was recording the dying — their names, their histories, the details the official reports shaved away — then somewhere in that hospital was a document worth more than gold and more dangerous than dynamite. A ledger of the omitted. Everything the machinery had failed to swallow.

And if such a ledger existed, then her brother might be in it. Or if not him, then the proof that such keeping was possible — that names survived outside the official world, in the hands of strangers, in ink that owed nothing to any office.

She should report it. That was the correct procedure. Suspicion of unauthorized record-keeping was reportable; she knew the form number by heart. She had even drafted the report twice, sitting at the little table by the window, pen moving in her neat clerical hand: *Subject exhibits irregularities... recommend investigation...*

Both drafts sat unsent. Both times she had reached the sentence where she described the suspected ledger, and both times her pen had stopped, and she had looked at what she was about to do — hand a stranger over to the machinery, feed another name into the teeth — and she had thought of Jonas, and of the clerk in Kaunas saying *officially he does not exist*, and she could not.

Because here was the arithmetic no form accounted for: if she reported Lior Lockridge, the ledger would be seized, and seized ledgers did not get read carefully — they got burned, along with their keepers. Every name in it would die a second death, officially this time, permanently. Including, possibly, the only unofficial trace of her brother anywhere in the world.

But if she said nothing, and the ledger was discovered anyway — by the hospital inspectors, by the security officers, by anyone less hesitant than herself — the result was identical, except that she would have done nothing to prevent it, which was its own kind of crime.

And if she approached the nurse directly—

Her heart beat faster at that, the way it always did. She pushed it down. Directly was madness. Directly meant exposing herself, admitting what she wanted, tying her own fate to a stranger's secret. She was a census taker. Census takers did not make bargains with suspects. Census takers counted and departed.

She got up, lit the lamp, and opened the bottom drawer.

*

The oilcloth bundle held everything she had of him, arranged with an archivist's precision that would have broken her heart if she let herself feel it: letters, a photograph of a young man squinting into sun with one hand raised mid-wave, a train ticket he never used, and — folded into a separate envelope — copies. Forty-one documents copied in her own hand over eighteen months: fragments of transport lists, detention registers, labor-roster excerpts, every scrap of official paper on which the name *Vogel* appeared anywhere in the occupied territories. She had spent her evenings in archives and offices, copying by hand when copying was permitted and memorizing when it was not, building a parallel record, a shadow file, a private resurrection.

Forty-one mentions. Not one of them later than September eleventh. As if he had stepped off the edge of the paper world entirely.

She sat with the photograph for a long time. In it he was nineteen and laughing at whoever held the camera, and she remembered the day — the river behind him, summer, the ice long broken and forgotten, everything possible still.

"You'd laugh at me," she said aloud to the empty room. "You'd say, Marta, you've become a bureaucrat of ghosts."

He had been the vivid one. She was the one who liked lists even as a child — who inventoried her books, who kept the household accounts at fourteen after their father died, whose handwriting the schoolmaster used as the model on the board. Jonas had called her the abacus. He had said it with love, mostly. And now she was an abacus counting a sum that refused to resolve, and the vivid one was a gap in a column, and the whole world's paperwork could not make the column balance.

Records were the only resurrection she trusted. She had no talent for faith — prayer had always felt like talking into weather — but paper she believed in. Paper held. Paper could be copied, hidden, carried across borders inside a coat lining. Somewhere in her deepest self lived a stubborn, almost shameful conviction: that if she could just assemble enough of the record, complete the file, find the missing page, he would come back. Not alive — she was not a fool, or not entirely — but *present*. Restored to the world's memory. Undeletable.

Which was why the nurse mattered. Which was why she could not stop.

She put the photograph away and took out instead the two unsent drafts of her report, read them once, and fed them one at a time to the lamp flame, watching the clerical language curl and blacken. It was a childish gesture and she knew it, burning paper in a lodging room like a conspirator in a novel, and she did it anyway, and felt briefly, dangerously free.

Then she took a fresh sheet and began, not a report, but notes. What she knew. Dates of her visits. The exact phrasing of the answers given. The pause of the pen. The direction of the ward windows. The schedule of the night shift, deduced across three visits. It calmed her, the writing of it — knowledge laid out in rows, facts behaving themselves — and only when she reached the bottom of the page did she admit what the notes were for.

Not a report. A map. She was mapping her way *toward* the nurse, not around to the authorities. She was going to go back a fourth time. She was going to ask one more question. And she did not know yet what she would do when the answer came.

*You are becoming an accomplice,* she told herself. *Or a fool. Possibly both.*

Outside, far off, the river made a sound — a deep, shifting groan, like a giant turning in sleep. The thaw was coming early this year. Everyone said so. The demolition notices were already going up on Pylimo Street; she had pasted one of them herself, two weeks ago, on the door of a shuttered print shop, and had noticed even then how her eyes kept returning to the building, though she couldn't have said why. Something about it. Something about the way the nurse had glanced at it, that day their paths crossed nearby — a look of such naked grief, so quickly buried, that Marta had thought about it for days afterward.

A print shop. A nurse who listened to the dying. A city full of vanishing names.

She blew out the lamp and lay down in the dark, and the pipes ticked, and the river groaned again, nearer now, or perhaps only nearer to breaking.

Tomorrow she would visit the hospital again. Tomorrow she would ask her question. And whatever Lior Lockridge was hiding, Marta Vogel intended to find it — not to destroy it, she was almost sure, not to destroy it — but because somewhere inside that silence, she was certain now, was a name. Maybe many names. Maybe, God willing, one of them was Jonas.

She slept badly, as she had slept badly for two years, and dreamed of columns of figures in which one line remained blank no matter how many times she wrote it.
