## 8. A Name Spoken Aloud

The fever came for Captain Reinhardt on a Tuesday, riding in on the smell of carbolic and the groan of the radiators. Lior knew it before the charts confirmed it — knew it by the way his breathing changed in the night, shallow and fast as a man running from something only he could see. By morning he was talking.

Not to anyone. To the ceiling, to the wall, to some audience assembled behind the plaster that no one else could see. His voice had the strange clarity fever gives men, each word polished and distinct, as though he were reading from a document held very close to his eyes.

Lior was changing the dressing on the man in bed eleven when they heard it.

"—Kowalczyk. Janusz Kowalczyk, farmer, near Lida. He had a daughter. She ran for the treeline and we—" The voice caught, not with emotion but with the mechanical hitch of a man reciting. "We did not pursue."

Lior's hands went still over the gauze. In bed eleven, the wounded partisan — a boy really, seventeen at most — watched them with the flat patience of someone who has learned that everything in a hospital is overheard eventually, and most of it is better unheard.

"Say that again," Lior murmured, to the patient, to no one. But it wasn't a request meant for bed eleven.

They moved down the ward on their rounds, and Lior timed it: passing bed nineteen slowly, straightening blankets that didn't need straightening, while the captain's fever-voice offered up another name like a coin from a pocket.

"Mieczysław Brandt. He was a shoemaker before the war. He asked to sit down first."

* * *

The ledger lived where it had always lived, sewn into the hollowed spine of Gray's *Anatomy*, 1913 edition, its boards swollen with names the publishers never intended. That evening, after the ward lamps were dimmed to their legal minimum and the night sister had made her first circuit, Lior took it down from the shelf between the pharmacology texts and carried it to the supply room, where a single bulb burned behind a cage of wire.

The anatomy text opened against their chest like a second body. Lior wrote standing up, the way they always did, because sitting felt too much like settling in, and settling in felt like permission to be found.

*Kowalczyk, Janusz. Farmer, near Lida. Daughter — fled to treeline.*

The pencil shook.

It had never shaken before. Not when they'd written down the dying words of men whose names existed nowhere else on earth, not when they'd copied out villages and wives and the true ages of boys the paperwork called *soldier, age unspecified*. Their hands had learned a kind of professional stillness, the same stillness that let them hold a basin under a man's mouth while he died, that let them say *there, there* in a language they barely spoke to men who couldn't understand it.

But those names had been given freely. Whispered by the dying into the ear of whoever would take them. Gifts, terrible ones, but gifts.

These names were being taken. Pried loose from a sleeping man's sickness by a nurse with a hidden book. And the men they belonged to were dead because this man's unit had killed them.

*Liar,* said a voice in Lior's head that sounded like their father. *You've been taking names since the first page. You take them from wives who will never see the graves. You take them without asking.*

Their father had believed ink was a form of justice. He had printed maps of a Poland that officials insisted didn't exist, borders drawn in defiance of every atlas stamped with an eagle and a swastika, and he had taught Lior which lines to ink and which to leave blank so the censors saw nothing but harmless cartography. He had died believing the blank spaces were a kind of mercy.

Lior looked down at what they had written and understood, with a vertigo that reached all the way down through the soles of their shoes into the frozen ground beneath the hospital, that they had just crossed a line their father never drew.

The ledger had been a memorial. Now it was something else. Now it contained the confession of a killer, recorded without consent, and if it was ever found — when it was ever found — Kowalczyk's name would hang beside the name of the officer who ordered him shot, and no future reader would be able to tell devotion from accusation, witness from accomplice.

Lior kept writing anyway.

*Brandt, Mieczysław. Shoemaker. Asked to sit down first.*

Because erasing them would be a second killing, and Lior Lockridge had not survived two years of invisibility to become a censor now.

* * *

"You're writing them down."

Tomas stood in the doorway of the supply room, a bundle of clean linen against his hip, his breath fogging in the unheated corridor behind him. He was fourteen and had the face of a boy who had stopped asking adults questions because the answers kept costing money or blood, and he stood there now with the particular stillness of someone who has seen something he was not supposed to see.

Lior closed the anatomy text. Too late, and they both knew it.

"How long have you been there?"

"Long enough." He came in without being invited, set the linen down, and looked at the book the way he looked at everything — sideways, assessing, a courier's eyes trained by hunger to value paper above almost everything. "That's the book you sleep next to. On the shelf. I've seen you take it down at night. I thought it was prayers."

"Maybe it is."

"It's not." He nodded at the cover. "Prayers don't make your hands shake."

Lior set the text on the shelf between the others, flush with their spines, invisible unless you knew. Then they turned and leaned against the counter and looked at this boy who carried bread and contraband messages through streets where either could get him shot, and heard themselves ask the question they'd sworn never to ask anyone:

"What did you hear?"

"All of it. The captain talks all day now. Everyone knows. Half the ward pretends to be asleep so they can listen." Tomas picked at a loose thread on his cuff. "The Polish patients cross themselves when he gets to the part about the well. Did you hear about the well?"

"No."

"There's a well." He said it flatly, the way he said everything, but his eyes weren't flat at all; they were burning low and steady, like coals banked for a long night. "Near a village he doesn't name. Thirty-one people, he says. Men, women, children. They put them in a well and then—" He stopped picking at the thread. "He says the water was bad for weeks afterward. He says it like he's complaining about the weather."

The wire-caged bulb hummed. Somewhere down the corridor a man coughed the wet, hopeless cough of the ones who wouldn't see spring.

"So you write them down." Tomas jerked his chin at the shelf. "The dead ones. Kowalczyk. The shoemaker. Do you write the captain too? When he dies — because he's going to die, everyone can hear it in his chest — do you put *Reinhardt* in your book?"

"I haven't decided."

"That's a lie." The boy's voice didn't rise. It dropped instead, quiet and hard, and for a moment he sounded less like fourteen than like sixty, like every generation of Lithuanians who had ever watched an occupying army come and go and come again. "You've already decided. You decide things fast, that's what everyone says about you. Nurse Lockridge, who never forgets a face, who writes down everything the officers leave out. So what side is it on?" He pointed at the shelf, at the invisible book. "The ledger. Whose side is it on? Because right now it's got our dead in it and his dead in it, and the same hand wrote both, and one day somebody opens that thing and reads it straight through and they're going to ask me — or whoever's holding it — the exact question I'm asking you."

No one had dared ask it. Not Marta Vogel with her census pen pausing a beat too long on the page. Not the resistance friend who wanted it burned. Not even Lior, in the privacy of three in the morning, had ever phrased it so plainly, because a question plainly phrased demands a plain answer, and there wasn't one.

Lior thought about lying. They were good at it now; that was the whole architecture of their survival, a building of small fluent lies, and they hated how good they'd become. But Tomas stood there with coal-burning eyes and a bundle of linen nobody needed, waiting, and something in Lior — some stubborn grain their father had milled into them over engraving plates and forbidden maps — refused to hand a fourteen-year-old a counterfeit.

"I don't know," Lior said. "I don't know whose side it's on. Maybe it isn't on a side. Maybe sides are just the shape the shooting makes, and the names are what's left standing after."

"That's not an answer."

"No. It's the truest one I have." Lior pulled the anatomy text back down and opened it to the fresh pages, turning them so Tomas could see. Names in columns. Villages. Trades. A daughter fled to a treeline. "Look. This man was murdered. This one too. This one ordered it. Same ink. Same book." They tapped the page. "If I erase the captain's victims, I'm finishing his work for him — he erased them once already, in his reports, and I'd be doing it again. If I erase the captain, I'm doing what his officers do: editing the record until it says what power wants it to say."

"So you keep both."

"I keep both."

"And someday someone finds the book and hangs you for it."

"Someday someone finds the book and *reads* it," Lior said. "That's all I'm aiming for. Reading. What they do after they read is theirs."

Tomas stared at the pages for a long time. Outside, far off toward the river, came a sound they'd been hearing for a week now and pretending not to: a deep, tectonic groan, ice shifting in its bed, the winter beginning to negotiate its own surrender.

"The well," Tomas said finally. "Thirty-one people. Does the captain know their names?"

"He hasn't said them yet."

"Then stay close to his bed." The boy picked up his linen bundle, then paused at the door, not turning around. "My family went onto a list. Just a list. Some clerk with neat handwriting, and then they were gone, and there isn't one piece of paper anywhere that says they were ever alive." His knuckles whitened on the cloth. "So maybe your book doesn't have a side. But it's the only thing in this city that does what it does. Don't lose it. Don't burn it. And don't let them catch you with it."

Then he was gone, footsteps fading down the corridor, and Lior stood alone with the caged bulb and the book and the sound of the river working loose beneath the city.

* * *

Captain Reinhardt lasted four more days.

On the third day, between the hours when the night sister finished her circuit and the orderlies began theirs, the fever broke open whatever last compartment he'd been keeping shut, and he gave Lior the well.

Lior was ready for it. They had arranged their rounds to pass bed nineteen at intervals, had sharpened two pencils, had cleared a full page. They stood at the foot of the bed with a clipboard of fake charts, head bowed as if studying numbers, while a dying officer of the army that occupied their city recited thirty-one names into the dark — farmers, a schoolteacher, a rabbi, children listed by height because he hadn't known their ages, *the small one, the one in the red scarf, the baby that cried* — and Lior wrote them all, hands shaking so badly the letters leaned like a fence in wind.

When he reached the end he lay silent a moment, and then, in a voice gone suddenly young, almost a child's voice, he said: "I held the rope."

He died before dawn. The official report would list him as *died of wounds, pneumonia complicating recovery.* It would not mention the well, or the rope, or the thirty-one.

At six in the morning, Lior sat in the supply room and added a final entry in a hand that had steadied itself by sheer will:

*Reinhardt, capt., d. [date]. Confessed: village unnamed, well, 31 souls, names herewith recorded pp. 41–43. Said: "I held the rope."*

Then they closed the book, and their hands began shaking again, and they let them.

Through the frosted window the sky was going grey-pink, and somewhere under it the river was groaning louder than it had any night yet that winter — long, splintering complaints, like a door in a house too large for anyone to find. The demolition notice on Pylimo Street had given them until the thaw. The thaw was coming early this year; everyone said so; the icemen had said it first, and the icemen were never wrong.

And now, folded inside the anatomy text between murdered men and their murderer, lay thirty-one more names that had to reach the other shore before the ice did what ice does.

Lior slid the book back onto the shelf, flush with its neighbors, and went to begin the morning rounds — and found Tomas already in the ward, stacking wood by the stove, watching bed nineteen's empty sheets with eyes that asked the question again without words, and beside him, filling out a requisition form at the nurses' station with her careful, patient handwriting, stood the census taker Marta Vogel, who was not supposed to be there, and who looked up at Lior across the ward as if she had been waiting all night for exactly this moment.
