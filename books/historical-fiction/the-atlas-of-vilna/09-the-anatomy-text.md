## 9. The Anatomy Text

The inspection came on a Thursday, the way disasters in Vilna preferred to arrive — without ceremony, without warning, wearing the face of routine.

Lior was changing a dressing when the orderlies began moving through the ward with that particular quickness that meant someone official had entered the building. It was not fear exactly; it was the absence of slowness. In occupied Vilna everything moved at two speeds: the trudge of the rationed and the scurry of the watched, and Lior had long ago learned to read the difference in the way men carried basins of water.

Sister Aldona appeared at the end of the row of beds, her hands folded, her expression arranged into the smooth blankness she wore like an apron for German visitors.

"Hauptmann Weiss," she said, too loudly, for Lior's benefit as much as anyone's. "From the administrative commission. He wishes to see the wards."

Weiss. Lior knew the name from the paperwork that crossed the nurses' station — a small man with a large signature, the kind of officer who had never been within earshot of a gunshot and made up for it with stamps. He inspected things. That was his war: he inspected storerooms and ledgers and latrines, and wherever he found disorder he found guilt, because disorder implied concealment and concealment implied treason. He had once closed a pharmacy on Rudnicku Street because its inventory book used two different shades of ink.

And Lior's ledger lived forty paces away, sewn into the hollowed binding of Gray's *Anatomy* on the shelf beside the nurses' desk, between a water-stained pharmacopeia and a volume of case histories no one had opened since the Russians left.

* * *

There is a discipline to terror, and Lior had learned it the way one learns any craft — badly at first, then through repetition, then so thoroughly it became indistinguishable from instinct. The first rule was that the body must be kept stupid. Hands steady. Breath shallow but even. Eyes appropriately deferential, neither darting nor bold. The second rule was that the mind must be kept busy, because an idle mind did only one thing in moments like this: it pictured the ledger open on a table under electric light, and Marta Vogel's neat handwriting beside it taking dictation, and the line of names becoming a line of people becoming a line against a wall.

So Lior kept the hands moving and the mind counting. Six beds in this row. Fourteen patients. Two fevers worth watching. Dressing trolley, third shelf, forceps missing again — stolen, probably, or traded for bread by some enterprising orderly whose name Lior would not write down, though not for lack of wanting.

Weiss came through the doors at half past ten with two adjutants and a clerk who carried a leather folio the way priests carry reliquaries. He walked the center aisle slowly, gloved fingers trailing along the footboards of the beds as if reading them in Braille.

"The mortality figures for this facility," he said to Sister Aldona, "show a certain... inconsistency."

"War is inconsistent, Hauptmann."

"War is arithmetic, Sister." He smiled with the lower half of his face only. "Everything else is sentiment."

Lior kept folding bandages. Fold, crease, stack. Fold, crease, stack. Across the ward, old Jakubas in bed eleven moaned in his fever and no one shushed him, which was itself a kind of luck, because noise drew attention and attention was currently the most dangerous substance in the room.

The inspection took the shape inspections always took: drawers opened, cupboards inventoried, the medicine cabinet's counts checked against the dispensary log while the clerk's pen scratched like a mouse in the walls. Lior worked. Lior did not look toward the shelf by the nurses' desk. Looking toward a thing was how one told the world where one's treasures were buried; Lior had understood this since childhood, since the days in the workshop on Pylimo Street when their father taught them that a map's first duty was to lie about what mattered most.

*Your eye goes where your heart lives,* Father had said, tapping the temple with a graver. *So keep the heart in your pocket and the eye on your work.*

But Weiss was not a man who needed eyes pointed at things. He was a man who smelled them.

He stopped at the nurses' desk.

* * *

Later — that night, and for many nights after — Lior would replay the next four minutes frame by frame, the way one probes a bruise to learn its borders. The Hauptmann's glove resting flat on the desktop. His gaze traveling, unhurried, along the spines of the books. The anatomy text standing fourth from the left, its false spine holding sixty pages of names written in pencil and ink and once, memorably, in the margin, blood.

"These texts are hospital property?"

"They are," said Sister Aldona.

"And they circulate? Between wards? Are they removed from the building?"

"They do not leave," she said, and there was something in her voice — a flatness, a door closing — that Lior filed away to think about later, when thinking was safe again. Sister Aldona had been in this hospital through three regimes. She knew what questions meant even when they were asked about books.

Weiss pulled the anatomy text from the shelf.

Lior felt it happen in the body before the mind could intervene: a dropping sensation, like a floorboard giving way, like ice shifting beneath a boot. The world narrowed to a single object turning in a gloved hand. The binding. He would open the binding. He would feel the cavity, the wrongness of the weight, the paper whispering inside a book that should have been solid —

— except that Lior had built the hiding place well, because hiding places were the one architecture Lior had ever mastered. The cavity ran along the inner spine, sealed with wheat paste and covered with new marbled endpapers bought from a stationer who asked no questions because Lior paid in cigarettes. From outside, the book was a book. Heavy, yes. But medical texts were heavy. No one weighed a book of anatomy against its conscience.

Weiss did not open it to the spine. He opened it to the plates.

His gloved finger traced the diagram of the heart — the great vessels blooming from it like roots drawn upward, the chambers labeled in Latin, the whole organ rendered in cross-section as if cut from a living man who had consented to nothing. Lior watched the officer study it and understood, with a clarity that arrived cold and complete, that this man had never once considered what lay behind a thing. Only what lay upon it.

"Outdated edition," Weiss said, and put it back.

* * *

The inspection lasted another hour and found nothing, which was not the same as finding everything. Before leaving, Weiss paused at the door and delivered his peroration to the room at large, the way small men do when they want to be remembered larger.

"This facility will be subject to periodic review. Unannounced. I advise all personnel to conduct themselves," — here the pause, the small smile — "*consistently*."

The adjutants followed him out. The clerk's footsteps faded down the stairwell. And the ward exhaled — not all at once, but in pieces, in coughs and shifts and the creak of bedframes, each patient returning by degrees to the business of being ill.

Lior did not exhale. Lior stood at the dressing trolley with both hands flat on its surface and let the terror finish arriving, because terror deferred is terror doubled, and the body keeps its own ledger whether one consents to it or not. The shaking started in the forearms and worked inward. Lior gripped the trolley's edge until the metal bit cold into the palms and waited for the wave to pass, counting — fourteen patients, six beds, two fevers — counting the way other people prayed.

When it passed, what remained was not relief. What remained was arithmetic.

Weiss would come back. Not because he suspected anything — Lior was almost certain of that, almost being a word that now had to be carried everywhere like a stone in the shoe. He would come back because coming back was his method, because periodic review was how a man with stamps waged war, because the anatomy text had been touched by his glove and logged somewhere in that leather folio and would be touched again. Every inspection made the next one more dangerous, because every inspection taught him the terrain.

And the ledger could not move. That was the bitter joke of it. There was nowhere for it to go.

Lior had spent two years learning the geography of concealment in this hospital the way Father had learned the geography of rivers: exhaustively, obsessively, with the devotion of a cartographer who knows his map may someday save a life. The loose brick behind the boiler — compromised in December, when the stoker repaired the flue. The space beneath the third-floor linen cupboard — compromised in January, when a new matron arrived with a talent for finding what was not hers. The false bottom of the records trunk — abandoned after the night Lior dreamed, twice, of the trunk being opened, and woke understanding that dreams were intelligence reports from a part of the mind that knew more than it said aloud.

Each hiding place had a lifespan, Lior realized now, standing in the antiseptic quiet of the ward. They aged like everything else in Vilna. A crack widened. A repair revealed a cavity. A person transferred, died, or simply noticed. The city was full of places to hide things and every one of them was temporary, and the winter — the great white accomplice that had muffled the streets and shortened the days and kept inspectors indoors — was entering its final weeks. When the thaw came, the demolition crews would come to Pylimo Street, and the officers would come out of their warm offices with their lists, and the hiding places would run out faster than the cold did.

That was the sentence Lior formed, standing there, and it would stay with them longer than the fear did:

*The hiding places are running out faster than the winter is.*

* * *

The retrieval had to wait for dark, and dark in March came late and reluctantly, the sky holding its blue like a miser holds coins.

At nine o'clock, when the ward lamps were dimmed and the corridor belonged to the night nurses' rounds, Lior went down to the morgue.

It was the coldest room in the hospital and the quietest, a cellar chamber with a drain in the floor and a table of zinc and shelves along one wall where the dead waited, in their wicker baskets, for whatever came next — burial if their families could manage it, the potter's field if they couldn't, transport if the occupation had opinions. Three baskets tonight. Lior knew their occupants by name, which was more than the official forms did. Antonas B., sixty-one, pneumonia, survived by no one that anyone had recorded. The boy from Eišiškės whose age the paperwork insisted was eighteen and whose face insisted otherwise. And a soldier brought in that afternoon with no papers at all, dead before the ambulance doors closed, known so far only to himself and to God and, briefly, to Lior, who had knelt beside the stretcher and caught the last syllables of a name — *Venclova*, maybe, or something near it — before the breath that carried them stopped.

Lior lit no lamp. The moon through the high window gave enough: gray shapes, gray shelves, the zinc table holding its pale cargo of instruments.

The plan had assembled itself during the evening rounds with the cold logic of things that assemble under pressure. The anatomy text could not stay on the shelf — Weiss's glove had consecrated it, marked it, turned it from furniture into evidence-in-waiting. But it also could not leave the hospital in a nurse's bag, because bags were searched at the doors now, randomly, and randomness was just another word for inevitability spread over time. So the book would go down instead of out. Down to the room where the living did not linger and the dead kept no secrets and no inspector on earth would pull a medical text from among a dead man's effects, because to do so would be to admit the dead had possessions worth coveting, and the occupation's entire machinery rested on the fiction that certain people had never possessed anything at all.

Lior lifted the basket belonging to the nameless soldier and set it on the zinc table. Wicker creaked. Inside: a coat folded into a pillow, boots gone, a canvas satchel containing a spoon, a photograph of a woman outside a wooden house, and a prayer card softened by handling. Lior had inventoried these hours ago, officially, for the records — spoon, photograph, card — writing down everything except what mattered, which was the way the woman in the photograph stood with her hip cocked and her chin up, laughing at whoever held the camera, alive, alive, alive in silver nitrate forever.

The book fit beneath the folded coat with room to spare. Lior adjusted the satchel over it, squared the basket's lid, and set the whole arrangement back on the shelf between the boy from Eišiškės and old Antonas B.

"There," Lior whispered to the dark, and hated the tenderness in it, the apology in it. Using the dead as a wall against the living. Using their silence — the one possession the occupation had never managed to confiscate — as a lock for a book full of names. It was devotion and desecration in the same gesture, and Lior could not separate them, and had stopped believing they could be separated. Everything true in Vilna was braided like this: love wound through with harm, rescue indistinguishable from risk.

On the way out Lior paused at the table, and after a moment took the photograph from the satchel and looked at it properly, by moonlight, the laughing woman and the wooden house and the fields behind her going soft at the edges.

"I'll find out where you're from," Lior told the soldier on the shelf. "I'll write it down."

It was a strange vow to make to a wicker basket in a cellar, but vows in Vilna had always been strange — sworn over printing presses and across frozen rivers, witnessed by no one, kept anyway.

* * *

Upstairs, the hospital settled into its nighttime breathing. Lior made the rounds, changed what needed changing, wrote in the official log the official words: *condition unchanged, condition deteriorated, condition unchanged.* At the nurses' desk the gap on the shelf where the anatomy text had stood showed plainly, a missing tooth in a smile, and Lior slid the pharmacopeia and the case histories together to close it, and the shelf looked innocent again, and innocence, Lior thought, was mostly a matter of arrangement.

Tomas was waiting in the stairwell alcove, sitting on the steps with his knees up, a bundle of firewood across his lap that was clearly a pretext, since the boy had delivered the same bundle three nights running and no one had yet burned any of it.

"You're late," Tomas said.

"I'm careful. It's slower."

"Is it still safe? Where it is?"

"It's safer than anywhere else in this building, which is not the same as safe." Lior sat down beside him on the step. Up close, in the draft from the window, the boy's face showed the new sharpness the winter had carved into it — cheekbones where softness used to be. Fifteen years old and already fluent in the grammar of not-asking. "How many days of bread do we have credit for at the baker on Vokiečių?"

"Four. Maybe five if I carry his flour again."

"And the sledge?"

"Tomas's father's sledge is Tomas's sledge now, if that's what you're asking. Runners need wax but the runners always need wax."

Lior looked at him a long moment. The boy stared back with that patience the young have, the patience of people who do not yet know enough to be afraid in the correct proportions.

"We'll need it soon," Lior said. "Before the ice breaks. For paper."

"What paper?"

"The kind that can't hide anymore."

Tomas nodded slowly, accepting this the way he accepted everything — without the question, without the flinch, carrying it home in the bundle that wasn't firewood. And Lior watched him go down the stairs and out into the blue-black street and thought: this is what it costs, this is what it has always cost, every name in the ledger eventually requires another pair of hands, and every pair of hands is a life laid on the scale beside the ink.

In the morgue below, a soldier waited with a book under his coat and a woman laughing in his pocket, keeping better company among the dead, Lior feared, than any of them currently kept among the living.

Three days later, the notice appeared on the hospital door: *Periodic review rescheduled. All wards and storage areas to be made available for examination. Effective immediately.*

Signed, in a large and confident hand: *Hauptmann Weiss.*
