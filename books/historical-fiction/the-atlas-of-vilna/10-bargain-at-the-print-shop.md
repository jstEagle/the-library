## 10. Bargain at the Print Shop

The snow on Pylimo Street had gone grey with coal smoke, and Lior walked it the way a thief walks a church—knowing the holiness of the place, knowing they have no right to it. Twice they stopped to look in shop windows that held nothing worth seeing. Once they doubled back through the courtyard behind the tinsmith's, listening for footsteps that were not their own. The discipline of invisibility. Twenty years of practice, and still Lior could not shake the feeling, which had been growing all week like a fever, that someone was walking three paces behind.

There was. But Lior did not know it yet.

* * *

Marta Vogel had learned to follow people the way she'd learned everything else: from manuals, from superiors, from the flat gray instruction of the occupation. You do not look at the person. You look at reflections, at the space ahead of them, at the way pigeons lift when someone hurries. She kept a half-block between them and let the morning market crowd do its work, a woman with a census satchel moving through the stalls as anonymously as weather.

She had told herself this was procedure. A discrepancy in the file—a nurse whose answers never varied by a single word across three visits, whose rehearsed fluency was itself a kind of evidence—warranted observation. That was what she wrote in her own mind when the word *suspicion* tried to surface. Procedure.

But procedure did not explain why her hands were cold inside her gloves, or why she had stood outside her lodging for an hour before dawn deciding whether to bring the forged correction forms at all.

Lior turned down Pylimo Street and Marta's breath caught. She knew this street. Every clerk in Vilna knew it now; the demolition order had been posted on the corner post in red ink that bled when it rained. What she had not known—what no file said—was that the nurse would stop at the boarded print shop at number 14, glance up and down the street with a thoroughness that made Marta press herself into a doorway, and slide a key into a lock that should not have worked, because the city had confiscated those keys in October.

*They still have keys to something the state took away.* Marta wrote it in her head the way she wrote everything, in columns. And beneath the column, unbidden, her brother's voice: *You always did keep lists, Marta. Even of your dolls.*

She waited until the door closed. Then she counted to two hundred, crossed the street, and put her hand on the handle.

* * *

Inside, the workshop smelled the way Lior remembered every place from childhood smelling: ink and oil and cold iron, the particular sweetness of linseed, the ghost-memory of paper dust. The light came down through a broken skylight in long dusty blades. Nothing had been touched since the confiscation—the Germans had taken the type cases worth taking, but the great flatbed press still squatted against the far wall like a sleeping animal, and the engraving stones were stacked where his father had left them, wrapped in felt.

His father's stones. Lior knelt beside them without meaning to, the way one kneels at a grave, and unwrapped the top one. A map of the city, half-finished, the river rendered in lines so fine they seemed drawn by patience itself. And there, in the lower margin, in his father's small exact hand: *Pylimo — 1937*. Before. When streets still belonged to themselves.

"You should lock your door," said a voice from the doorway. "Anyone could walk in."

Lior did not turn around. There was no point. The voice had already catalogued everything in the room, and turning would only confirm what the pause in the doorway already knew: that Lior Lockridge had been seen.

"Census taker Vogel." Lior set the stone down gently, as if it might break, though it had survived worse than this morning. "Come to count me? I'm told I'm already in your files."

"I come to count what you're hiding." Marta stepped inside and pulled the door shut behind her—an odd gesture, Lior thought later; a hunter closing the trap with herself inside it. Her eyes moved over the press, the stones, the shelves where reams of blank stock still leaned against the wall like uncut bread. "A print shop. On a demolition list. With a key that shouldn't exist."

"The key is my father's."

"Your father is dead." Flat. Not cruel. The tone of a woman reading a column of figures.

"Yes," Lior said. "I buried him. You asked about that once, didn't you. Whom I buried. You asked the question no census asks." They turned then, finally, and looked at her directly, and saw that she flinched—not at the accusation, but at being known back. "So we're both keeping records, Frau Vogel. Shall we compare them?"

For a long moment neither spoke. The skylight blades shifted as clouds moved; the dust turned in the light like something alive.

"You copy names," Marta said at last. It was not quite a question. "Dying men give you names and you write them down. In the hospital. At night."

Lior's heart performed a slow, enormous labor, pumping fear through the whole body at once. Every rehearsal, every fluent lie, assembled itself—and Lior watched it all stand down, useless, because she had followed them here, because the room itself confessed. There was no lie left that would fit through the space between them.

"If I say no," Lior said quietly, "you'll report the key. The shop. The discrepancies. And if I say yes?"

Marta's hand went into her satchel, and Lior's body braced for paper, for orders, for the arrest that had been walking behind them for weeks—but what she drew out was a photograph, soft-edged from handling, and a folded document gone brown at the creases.

"My brother," she said. "Jakob Vogel. Deported in '41, third transport. He existed. I can prove he existed—I have his birth record, his school certificate, his worker's card." Her voice stayed level the way ice stays level over water. "And in every list since—every registry, every census summary, every deportation manifest I've processed in eleven months of service—he is absent. Not marked dead. Not marked transferred. Absent. As if the ink refused him." She laid the photograph on the engraving stone, next to the half-finished map, and her hand trembled once before it stilled. "A man can be erased by paperwork, Nurse. I know because I do the erasing. Six hours a day. Then I go home and I am the only person left in the world who remembers his face."

The dust turned. Somewhere above, a crow walked across the skylight glass, its shadow dragging over them both.

"So you hunted my ledger," Lior said slowly, "not to destroy it."

"I hunt ledgers because ledgers are the only country where my brother is still alive." Now she did look up, and her eyes were dry in a way that cost her visibly. "I don't want your silence, Lockridge. Silence is what killed him. I want him *entered.* His name, his dates, verified against these documents—" she tapped the brown folder "—written where the officers' ink cannot reach him again. In exchange, I never saw this shop. I never saw you. My reports will be so dull they will put my superiors to sleep."

"And if they order you to find a ledger?" Lior asked. "Because they will. Men who erase people eventually notice the ones who write."

Something moved behind Marta's eyes—the thing she had been carrying since the falsified correction forms, the thing she would carry, in one form or another, for the rest of the war. "Then I will fail to find it," she said. "Badly. And I will pay for failing. That is my side of the bargain. Your side is harder: you must trust a census taker. You must believe that somewhere under this uniform there is still a sister."

Lior looked down at the photograph. A young man with his mother's forehead, grinning at the camera as if the lens owed him money. Jakob Vogel, born 1919. Someone's list had swallowed him whole, and here was the last person on earth who could prove he'd ever been on any list at all, offering her whole life as collateral for a line of ink.

It would be salvation, Lior thought. Or it would be a leash—because now there was a second person who knew, and every additional knower was another throat the occupation could squeeze. Tomas. Marta. The ledger itself, fattening daily toward the weight of a weapon. The arithmetic of survival had never balanced, and now Lior was adding terms to the wrong side of it.

But his father had taught him which lines to ink and which to leave blank, and he had also taught him this: *a map that shows nothing true protects no one.*

"Give me the documents," Lior said. "I'll verify them tonight. If they're genuine, Jakob Vogel goes in the atlas—not at the end, as a favor. Where he belongs. Alphabetically. Like everyone else."

For the first time, Marta Vogel smiled, and it was a terrible, beautiful thing, like sun on frozen ground. "Alphabetically," she repeated, as if tasting the word.

They shook hands over the dead press, and the bargain was sealed the way all such bargains are sealed: quickly, in cold air, by two people who understood perfectly well what they might each become to the other.

Marta gathered her satchel and paused at the door. "Lockridge. The demolition crew comes when the ice breaks. Perhaps sooner—they've been short of men for the eastern detail." She glanced back at the press, the stones, the shelves of paper. "Whatever you intend to finish here—finish it fast."

Then she was gone, and Lior stood alone among the father's stones with a photograph in one hand and the future in the other, and out beyond the broken skylight, faint under the sound of the crow, the river began—very softly—to groan.
