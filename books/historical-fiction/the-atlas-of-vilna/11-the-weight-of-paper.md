## 11. The Weight of Paper

The sledge runners sang against the ice of Pylimo Street, a thin high note that Tomas felt in his teeth more than heard. He had learned to love and hate that sound in the same week. Love, because it meant the runners were clean and the load was moving. Hate, because the street was so quiet at three in the morning that any sound carried, and a German patrol could be around the corner before you finished deciding to be afraid.

He pulled the rope over his shoulder and leaned into it. Behind him, wrapped in burlap and tied with the kind of knots Lior insisted on — double loops, never bows, bows came undone when hands shook — lay forty pounds of paper. Printed pages. Names.

It was absurd, he thought, hauling names through the snow like sacks of flour. It was the most serious thing he had ever done.

* * *

Lior walked beside him without pulling. That was the arrangement: Tomas hauled, Lior guided, and neither spoke unless the silence demanded it. Lior moved through the dark city the way water moves through cracks, always finding the gap in a fence line, the unlit stretch between lampposts, the exact depth of shadow behind the tannery wall where two people could stand and simply not exist. Tomas had asked once how they knew all this. Lior had said, *You learn where the eyes are, and then you learn to live in the places eyes skip.*

Tomas was fifteen. He was supposed to be learning typesetting at his uncle's bench, or nothing at all, or dead like everyone else from his street, and instead he was learning the geography of not being seen. He hadn't asked for it. But nobody had asked him for anything in fourteen months, and there was something — he would not have been able to name it, not then, maybe not ever — something almost like being needed in the weight of the rope against his shoulder.

They crossed Vokiečių Street. Somewhere east, the river groaned. It had been groaning for four nights now, long low complaints under the ice, the sound of a thing too heavy to hold much longer. Tomas hated that sound worse than the runners' song. The river's voice meant the demolition crew would come to Pylimo Street soon, and everything Lior cared about was stacked on pallets inside a print shop that was already condemned.

"Here." Lior's hand touched his elbow, light as a moth. They cut through a yard where laundry still hung frozen on its lines, shirts stiff as armor, ghost-white in the snowlight. On the other side was the alley behind the bakery, and the bakery's coal cellar, and the second hiding place.

Tomas set the rope down and stood breathing hard, watching his own breath climb. Forty pounds of paper. He'd carried sacks of grain twice that weight up three flights of stairs for his mother, back when there were stairs and flights and a mother, and those sacks had never made his arms ache like this. But grain didn't matter what happened to it. Grain could fall in the mud and still be grain.

Paper couldn't.

* * *

The first run had been two weeks ago. Lior had come to him at the bakery — Tomas sold black bread mornings now, standing on a crate so customers could see him over the counter — and said, in the flat voice Tomas had learned meant *important things*, "Can you pull a sledge at night? Quietly? I'll pay in bread."

Everyone paid in bread. Bread was the only currency left with any honesty in it.

"I'm strong," Tomas had said, which was true the way a boy says true things, wanting them larger than they were.

"It's not about strong." Lior had looked at him for a long moment, that measuring look, the one that seemed to weigh whether you were the kind of person who could carry something and never put it down. "It's about whether you can keep a secret that isn't yours."

And Tomas had said yes, because what else does a boy say, and because some traitor part of him wanted the secret the way other boys wanted a knife or a girl's attention — as proof he mattered enough to be trusted with danger.

Now he knew what the secret weighed. It weighed forty pounds per trip, and there had been nine trips, and Lior kept saying there were pages yet unprinted, plates yet to cut, and the ice kept groaning like a debtor at the door.

"What's in it?" he had asked the first night, kneeling over the burlap bundle. "I mean — I know it's paper. But what paper?"

Lior had been quiet so long that Tomas looked up, thinking he wouldn't answer.

"Names," Lior said finally. "People the reports leave out. Men who died in my ward with half a name, or no name, or a number where their birthday should be. I write them down. All of them. Where they were born, who they married, how old they really were when the paperwork decided otherwise." A pause. "It's an atlas."

"An atlas is maps," Tomas said. He knew; his uncle had printed maps once, before the wrong kind of maps got his uncle's cousin arrested.

"This one is." Lior's face did something complicated in the dark. "Every street in Vilna. Every street holding its dead. If you read it right."

* * *

Tonight, in the coal cellar, with the third bundle stowed in the false bottom under the potato crates, Tomas finally asked the question that had been sitting in his chest since the first run.

"My family," he said. His voice came out strange, younger than he was. "They weren't shot. You know that?"

Lior went very still. In the cellar's blackness, stillness was its own kind of answer — Lior had stopped moving the way people stop moving when a wound has been named.

"There was no soldier," Tomas said. "No round-up in the street with dogs. I used to think — before — I used to think if it ever happened it would look like a painting. Guns and shouting. Something you could fight." He laughed, a small ugly sound. "There was a list. A man with good handwriting sat at a desk — I saw him, I saw the desk, I was delivering bread to the administration building, I saw right into the room — and he wrote down our street, our house, our names, and he blotted the ink, and he moved on to the next line. That's all. Three weeks later they came with trucks and the list, and the list was correct. Every name. My mother. My father. My sister Rivke, who was eleven." His throat closed. He made himself finish. "A clerk killed us. Efficient handwriting. He probably had nice manners. He probably went home to his dinner."

The stove ticked. Somewhere above, the bakery's ovens were already warming for the morning shift, and the smell of bread drifted down through the floorboards, obscene and ordinary.

"Tomas." Lior's voice was careful. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're making a list." He turned toward where he judged Lior to be. "That's what I can't stop thinking about, every time I haul your sledge. There was a list, and we died of it. And you're making another one. And I help you. And I don't understand — I've tried, I've laid awake trying — how ink can go both directions. How the same thing that erased them can save anyone." He heard his own voice crack and hated it. "So tell me. Tell me straight, because everyone lies to me now, they think I'm too young for truth. Can names really be saved by ink? Does writing someone down actually *do* anything? Or is your atlas just — " he groped for the word, found it, flinched from it — "just another clerk's ledger, going the other way?"

Silence. Long enough that Tomas began to wish he'd kept his mouth shut, begun to rehearse an apology.

"No," Lior said at last.

Tomas blinked in the dark. "No, what?"

"No, I can't promise it saves anyone. Your mother is gone. Rivke is gone. No page I ever print brings one hour of them back." The words came slow, each one set down like type into a frame, checked, locked. "If I told you ink was salvation, I'd be selling you the same lie the clerks sold themselves — that what happens on paper stays on paper. It doesn't. Paper becomes trucks. Paper becomes lists. And sometimes — " a breath — "sometimes paper becomes proof. That's all mine can be. Proof. That they were here. That they had whole names, and villages, and wives, and ages that were real ages, and that the report that reduced them to numbers was lying. An atlas doesn't stop the demolition crew, Tomas. It makes sure that after the demolition, somebody knows what stood there."

Tomas sat with that. It wasn't the answer he wanted. He had wanted — he realized it now, ashamed — he had wanted magic. Ink as resurrection. Some compensation for the blotting pen, some force that ran backward.

"That's not enough," he said quietly.

"No," Lior agreed. "It isn't. It's what there is."

"And me?" He was surprised to hear himself ask it. "Hauling your sledge. What am I, in this? A courier?"

"You're the next pair of hands." Lior's voice changed, softened by a degree. "My father taught me the trade on Pylimo Street. Which lines to ink and which to leave blank — that was the whole lesson, really. Most of remembering is knowing what to leave blank until the right moment." A pause. "I never asked for it either. Nobody asks. It just arrives, like a debt you inherit from someone who trusted you to pay it."

"A burden," Tomas said.

"An inheritance," Lior said. "Same object. Depends which end you pick it up from."

* * *

They made the last leg of the run before dawn, out along Subačiaus toward the drop point near the gatehouse, where the second cache waited in a dry well. The sky was doing the thing it did lately — turning the color of dirty pewter at four in the morning instead of six, the winter losing its grip day by day. Snow underfoot had gone soft at the edges overnight. Tomas noticed it the way you notice a fever starting in yourself: with dread.

The river groaned again, longer this time, and somewhere in the middle of the sound came a crack, sharp as a rifle shot, that made them both freeze against the wall until the echoes quit.

"It's early," Tomas whispered.

"The thaw is early. Everything this year is early." Lior stared down the empty street toward the black line of the river. "Which means the crew comes early. Which means we print faster than we planned, and move more than the sledge can carry, and sleep less than anyone should."

"How many pages left?"

"Too many." For the first time since Tomas had known them, Lior sounded tired all the way down, tired in the bones, tired like the buildings looked. "There's a section I haven't started. The hardest one. And the plates take time, and time is — "

Lior stopped. Not a dramatic stop; a full stop, mid-word, the way a clock stops. Across the square, at the corner by the shuttered pharmacy, a figure stood in the falling light. Tall. Coat too good for the hour. Watching them.

Tomas's hand found the rope. His mind did the arithmetic of panic: forty pounds of paper, thirty seconds of open ground, one sledge, two of them, and a watcher whose face he couldn't read at this distance but whose stillness said *I have been waiting.*

"Lior," he breathed.

"I see her." A beat. "Walk. Don't run. Runners get followed; walkers get forgotten. Turn at the church and we'll lose her in the lanes."

But even as they moved, unhurried, two figures trudging home from honest work, Tomas felt the watcher's attention on his back like a hand between his shoulder blades, and he understood that whatever came next, the easy nights were over. Someone had seen the sledge. Someone had seen *them*. And in Vilna, in 1941, being seen was the beginning of every ending.

Behind them, faint under the groan of the river, he thought he heard footsteps begin to follow.
