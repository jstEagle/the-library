## 12. What Survival Costs

He came to the hospital on a Tuesday, when the corridors smelled of carbolic and thawing mud tracked in on every boot, and Lior knew him before he said a word — by the way he stood too still in a doorway where everyone else hurried, by the way his eyes went to the exits first and to faces second.

Yankel Fried. Four years since Lior had seen him, and the war had spent him like a careless spendthrift: he'd been broad once, a typesetter with forearms like loaves of dough, and now his coat hung off him as though it had been tailored for the man he used to be. His left hand was gloved. In March. Lior looked at the glove and did not ask.

"Nurse," Yankel said, loud enough for the corridor. "I've come about my brother. Ward six."

Lior had no brother in ward six. But they led him there anyway, past the rows of cots, past the man from Minsk who moaned a woman's name all day like a rosary, past the window where Tomas sometimes waited with his sledge and his hungry patience. Ward six was empty except for one sleeping officer with a bandaged jaw. Lior pulled the curtain.

"You shouldn't be here," they said quietly.

"No," Yankel agreed. "But I'm here." He sat down on the edge of an empty cot as though his legs had been waiting years for permission. "I need to see it."

"See what?"

"Don't." The word came out flat and final, a door closing. "Don't do that. Don't give me the nurse's face. I taught you to set type when your hands were too small for the chase. I know every face you own, Lior Lockridge, and I know which one you're wearing now."

The sleeping officer shifted. Somewhere down the hall a trolley rattled over the stone flags. Lior stood very still and felt the ledger's weight against their ribs, though it was three floors away, sewn into the false spine of Gray's *Anatomy* between the chapters on the bones of the hand — a place no inspector had ever thought to look, because who reads about hands anymore when there are so many missing?

"There is nothing to see," Lior said.

"There's everything to see." Yankel pulled the glove from his left hand. Two fingers were gone — the index and the middle, taken at the knuckle, healed badly, the scars shiny as wax. He held the hand up in the gray window light not like a wound but like a document. "Kovno. Eighteen months ago. They wanted names. I gave them two fingers' worth of nothing and then someone else gave them the rest for free, because that's what people do, Lior. That is *what people do.*"

"I'm sorry," Lior said, and meant it, and watched it land like a coin tossed to a beggar.

"Don't be sorry. Be sensible." He stood, and up close Lior could smell the road on him — train smoke, wet wool, the sourness of a man who slept in places he didn't choose. "There are maybe forty of us left who matter. Forty people in this city who can still move paper, move men, move themselves. And every one of us is holding something. A list. An address. A name written somewhere it shouldn't be. And every week the circle gets smaller because someone panics, or someone loves someone too much, or someone simply talks in his sleep." His eyes were terrible and gentle at once. "You know what I mean. You know exactly what I mean."

* * *

They walked after his shift ended, or rather Yankel walked and Lior accompanied him, taking the long way through the streets where the snow was rotting into black slush and the gutters ran loud with the season's confession. Pylimo Street lay two turns out of their way and Lior did not take it; they had learned to ration the workshop, to visit it the way one visits a grave — often enough to keep faith, rarely enough to avoid being followed.

"The ledger," Yankel said, when they were alone on the embankment with the river below them, still wearing its ice like a man refusing to undress. "I know about it. Before you ask — yes, I'm sure. No, I won't say how. It doesn't matter how."

"It matters."

"It doesn't. What matters is that I know, which means others can know. Which means some already do." He stopped at the railing. Below, the Neris moved under its lid of ice with a sound like slow grinding teeth; it had been making that sound for a week now, day and night, the river turning over in its sleep. "Burn it."

The word went into Lior like a splinter. They had imagined this conversation a hundred times, in the sleepless arithmetic of three a.m., and in every version they had answers ready. Now the answers would not come.

"No," Lior said.

"Lior—"

"No." Louder than intended. A woman passing with a bundle of kindling glanced at them and hurried on; even kindness had learned to look away quickly in this city. Lior lowered their voice. "You haven't seen it. You don't know what's in it."

"I can imagine what's in it. Names. Names of the dead, mostly. Names of the—" He paused, weighing. "Names of everyone. Am I right? Not just our dead. Theirs too. The ones the officers whisper when the fever takes them." He shook his head slowly. "God help us. You've been writing down the executions."

"Somebody has to."

"*Nobody* has to. That's the whole point, Lior. That's the entire lesson of the last two years, and you've failed to learn it. Nobody has to. Nobody should. A piece of paper with four hundred names on it isn't a memorial, it's a sentence of death with a distribution list." He gripped the railing with his maimed hand, the two remaining fingers white-knuckled. "You think the Germans find that book, what happens? Not to you. You're a nurse, you're half-invisible, you might survive it with a beating. They'll shoot the courier. They'll shoot the printer's daughter — son — whatever you're calling yourself this winter. They'll shoot the family in the next building. They'll shoot ten strangers whose only crime was living on your street, because that's the arithmetic they use. Ten for one. A hundred for one. And you want to hand them a *ledger*, Lior. A ready-made arrest schedule. Every name cross-referenced to a street."

"They're already dead," Lior said. "Most of them."

"And the ones who aren't?" Yankel turned to face them fully, and for a moment Lior saw not the ruined typesetter but the man who had once stood over a composing stone and said, *the blank space matters more than the ink, little one; ink tells them where to look, blankness tells them nothing at all.* "The ones still breathing? Your boy with the sledge. Your father's old customers. The widow on Rudnicku who lets you store a crate in her cellar. Are they dead yet?"

Lior said nothing.

"That's what I thought." Yankel released the railing. "Burn it. Burn it tonight, in the hospital boiler if you have to, page by page so nothing's legible. Melt the type back into lead. Let the river have the ash." He almost smiled then, and it was the saddest thing Lior had seen all winter, worse than the ward, worse than the morgue. "You could start again afterward. Smaller. One name at a time, kept in your skull where paper can't betray anyone. Memory's safer than ink. Ink can be read by anyone."

"Not by me," Lior said. "Not anymore. I tried keeping them in my head, Yankel. In the beginning. Forty names, fifty. Then sixty and they started slipping. I'd lie awake testing myself and find holes — a face with no name attached, a name with no face. Do you understand what that means? It means the dead die twice. Once in the body and once in the memory of whoever was too tired, too frightened, too full of other griefs to hold them. Paper doesn't get tired. Paper doesn't get frightened. Paper just waits." They heard their own voice rise and did not care. "My father printed maps of a country that didn't officially exist. Blank where they told him to leave blank, ink where they'd never look. He died with press ink in the cracks of his hands and no one wrote down so much as his middle name. I am not doing that again. I am not becoming another blank space."

Yankel was quiet a long time. The river groaned beneath them, a long structural complaint, like a house settling.

"He'd have understood," Yankel said finally. "That's the worst part. Old Lockridge would have understood exactly what you're doing and why, and he'd have helped you, and he'd have gotten himself shot for it inside a month, because he never met a rule he didn't want to break beautifully." He rubbed his scarred hand with his good one, absently, the way one touches an old ache. "Beautifully. That's the word that frightens me. You're doing this beautifully, Lior. And beautiful things get found."

* * *

They parted at the corner of Subačiaus, where the street climbed toward the hill and the church bells above it counted five o'clock in their cracked, indifferent voices. Yankel held out his whole hand; Lior took it.

"Burn it," he said once more, softly, without hope.

"No."

He nodded, unsurprised, unsurprising. Then, as he turned to go, he stopped. Lior watched the change move across his face like weather — the hesitation, the decision, the grim settling of a man committing himself to something he'd sworn not to do.

"One more thing. I argued with myself the whole way here whether to tell you, and I decided you'd rather know. You're the type who'd rather know." He glanced up the hill, down the street, at the emptying road. "Someone talked. Recently. About paper. About a nurse."

The cold that went through Lior then had nothing to do with March.

"Who?"

"If I knew who, I'd have known what to do about it, and I'd have done it." His mouth twisted. "It came through Vilijampolė two weeks ago, secondhand, thirdhand — the way these things travel, each teller sure of his source. A clerk talked to a clerk. Something about lists leaving the city. Something about a hospital worker who writes too much." He reached out and gripped Lior's shoulder with the good hand, hard, painful, deliberate — the grip of a man pressing a seal into wax. "I'm telling you because you deserve one warning in your life that arrives before the knock. This is it. Move the book. Move yourself. Stop visiting places you love, because places you love are the first place they look and the easiest place to wait."

Then he was gone, climbing the hill with his uneven gait, his ruined hand tucked back into its glove, growing smaller against the whitening sky until the bend of the street took him, and Lior stood alone at the bottom of Subačiaus with the bells finishing their fifth hour overhead and the words *someone talked* ringing louder than any of it.

* * *

That night Lior did not sleep. They sat in their narrow room above the laundry with the lamp turned to its lowest flame and the ledger open on their knees — not reading, counting. Four hundred and eleven names. Four hundred and eleven small acts of resurrection, each one a debt incurred.

Because that was the truth Yankel had put his finger on, the truth Lior had been circling all winter without landing: the ledger was not free. Nothing in occupied Vilna was free. Every name had cost something — a risk taken, a lie told, a trust extended — and the interest compounded daily. Tomas carried bundles through dark streets for bread, and if he were caught the bread would not save him. The widow on Rudnicku guarded her cellar crate and had never asked what was in it, and her not-asking was itself a payment. Marta Vogel — and Lior's thoughts snagged on her the way cloth snags on a nail — Marta had asked her questions and gone away, twice now, and each unanswered question was a coin Lior had spent from an account they couldn't see the balance of.

And Yankel himself. Yankel had crossed a frozen country on two fingers' worth of history to deliver a warning, and warnings, in this city, were paid for. Someone would eventually ask Yankel Fried whom he had visited in March, and Yankel would lie, or he wouldn't, and either answer would cost.

*I've been paying with other people's safety all along,* Lior thought, watching the lamp flame shiver in the draft from the ill-fitted window. *Every name I saved went onto a bill someone else might be handed.*

The river groaned in the distance, longer and lower tonight, a sound like a giant rolling over toward wakefulness. The demolition order was posted on Pylimo Street; the crews would come when the ice broke, and the ice was breaking soon — everyone could hear it coming, nights now, a percussion under the city. Days, perhaps. A week.

And someone had talked.

Lior closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a door on a sleeping child, and sewed it back into the anatomy text with fingers that refused, entirely, to shake. Then they blew out the lamp and sat in the dark and made the only plan that fear allowed: tomorrow, before dawn, before the milk carts, before the clerks unlocked their drawers full of other people's lives — the ledger would leave the hospital. All of it. To the workshop, where it belonged, where the plates waited and the press waited and the last pages waited to be printed while there was still a workshop left to print them in.

And if someone was already watching — if the talk had traveled and the waiting had begun — then they would learn what Lior Lockridge was made of at the same moment Lior did.

Outside, far off toward the river, the ice spoke again. This time it sounded less like groaning and more like an answer.
