## 14. Racing the Thaw

The thaw came like an intruder who did not bother to hide.

On the third of March the icicles along Pylimo Street began to weep in daylight, ticking against the cobbles in a rhythm too fast for winter, and by the fifth the gutters ran brown and loud enough to cover conversation — a mercy, Lior thought, for people who had learned to speak only when water spoke first. They stood at their window with a cup of chicory gone cold and counted the drips the way other people counted shells falling: one meant days, many meant hours.

The demolition notice had been posted on the door of number 17 on Tuesday. *Structure condemned. Clearing scheduled upon break of ice.* Someone had chalked beneath it, in a child's hand, a crooked star. Lior had stood before the paper for a long time, reading it not as a sentence but as a deadline, and deadlines could be raced.

The crew arrived Thursday morning, three days early.

Lior saw them from the hospital's upper corridor — four men and a boy with a handcart, unloading timber and tarps across from the workshop, their breath rising white in air that no longer bit. One man wore a surveyor's armband and squinted up at the roofline with a notebook, writing the building's death warrant in advance. The sight went through Lior like cold water through cloth: not fear exactly, but the sudden certainty of a clock they could hear now, tick under tick under tick.

By evening there was a plan, or what passed for one. Marta arrived at the hospital with her census satchel and her official face, and Tomas came with bread and a rumor that the crew would not begin actual demolition until the yard dried out — mud, he said, was the only ally left on Pylimo Street.

"They'll fire the press," Marta said. It was not a question. She had been to the workshop twice now; she knew what sat in its cellar like a sleeping animal.

"They'll burn everything that burns," Lior said. "Which is everything."

"Then we print faster than they burn."

* * *

They worked in shifts, because none of them could work alone anymore and none of them dared be seen together more than necessary.

Marta took the day. She had a legitimate reason to knock on every door on Pylimo Street — a "supplementary census of sublet dwellings," forged so well that even Lior, reading the authorization over her shoulder, felt a flicker of professional admiration. While she kept the crew's foreman occupied with questions about occupancy records and liability for stored goods, Lior slipped through the alley door into the cellar where the press waited under canvas like something being protected rather than hidden.

The plates were finished. That had been the easy part, weeks of it, etched and re-etched until Lior's fingers knew every line blind. Twenty-one zinc sheets, each a district of Vilna, each street rendered as a channel, each channel filled — not with cartographer's ink but with names. The idea had come to Lior in January, half-dreamed, and had refused to leave: the officials drew maps with streets but no people; Lior would draw a map with nothing but people. Every name in the ledger set into the geography it belonged to. Straszuna Street holding its dead in its gutters. The hospital quarter dense as a forest, names packed root to branch. The river itself left blank, because the river had taken no names yet, and Lior refused to jinx it.

Now came the printing, and printing was arithmetic, and arithmetic was cruel.

Two hundred impressions per plate before the zinc softened and the lines blurred. Nine hundred copies needed if the couriers were to carry enough. Paper enough for perhaps six hundred, if the six hundred were printed close and cut carelessly and bound thin. Ink enough for four hundred. Time enough — Lior looked at the ceiling, through the floorboards, at the men overhead hammering staging into place — time enough for maybe five days, if the mud held, if the ice held, if no one looked down the alley at the wrong hour.

Five days to make nine hundred names live four hundred times each.

It was not enough. It was also not nothing. Lior had learned, in the ledger's long apprenticeship, that not-nothing was the only currency this city paid in anymore.

* * *

Tomas worked nights, and he worked like someone paying off a debt he never discussed.

He had rigged two oil lamps with tin reflectors scavenged from a ruined tram shelter, and he'd built a drying rack from lath and string that sagged under the first hundred sheets like a laundry line in a poor district, which, Lior supposed, it now was. He pulled the lever of the press with his whole slight body, feet off the floor some strokes, and he had stopped asking why after the second night. Or rather, he asked a different question, better:

"Which sheet is mine?"

Lior had paused, ink on both hands, and looked at him. Sixteen years old, wrists like broom handles, eyes that had seen whatever the winter of '41 put in front of children and had decided, somewhere in it, to become useful instead of dead.

"I don't know your family name yet," Lior said quietly.

"Kaminski." He said it carefully, the way you set down something fragile. "Tomas Kaminski. My mother was Rózia. My father, Jankiel. My sister—" he stopped, started again. "Chava. She was twelve."

Lior took the ledger down from its place inside the anatomy binding — here, in the workshop, it could sit open for minutes at a time, a luxury that felt indecent — and wrote the four names in the column for the railway quarter, in the space reserved for the deported, whom the officers recorded only as numbers moved and numbers arrived. Then Lior found plate fourteen, the railway district, and showed him where his mother's name sat inside the curve of Stiklių Street, small as a seed, permanent as a seed.

"She's on a map now," Lior said. "Maps get copied."

Tomas touched the zinc once, with one fingertip, the way you touch a photograph of someone. Then he turned back to the press and pulled the lever, and did not speak again for two hours except to count sheets.

That was the shape of the labor, night after night. Feverish, filthy, almost joyful. There was something obscene about how good it felt — the rhythmic groan of the press, the smell of ink and damp paper, the growing stacks like loaves — and Lior understood the obscenity and chose it anyway. Creation under sentence of death has a taste all its own. It tastes like bread eaten too fast. It tastes like living.

* * *

Marta came on the fourth night, past curfew, which meant she had crossed the city by routes she did not explain, and Lior did not ask. She descended into the cellar carrying her census satchel and, incongruously, a paper sack of boiled sweets that she emptied onto the workbench without comment, as though sugar were simply part of the operation.

She stood before the drying rack a long time. Sheets hung in rows, hundreds of them, faint gray ghosts of streets, and she read them the way you read a wall of photographs at a funeral — moving slowly, lips sometimes moving, looking for one thing among the thousands.

"He'd be on plate nine," she said finally. "If your atlas is honest. The ghetto lists wouldn't have him, and the transport manifests lost him, and the Red Cross has nothing. But if this is what you say it is—"

"It's honest."

"Show me."

Lior took down plate nine, the old town, and held the lamp close, and turned it so the lamplight raked across the etched lines, and there in the channel of Jatkowa Street — the street of the booksellers, the street where Marta had grown up, though she had never told Lior so and had only let it slip sideways, the way grief slips — the name sat among forty others. **VOGEL, ABRAM.** Etched backward, as all the names were, so they would print true.

Marta made a sound that was not quite a word. She put her hand flat over the name as if warming it.

"You cut him into a map," she whispered.

"I cut everyone into a map. It's the only way I know to make the city tell the truth. Your officials draw Vilna with streets and no one in them. I'm drawing Vilna with everyone in it and no room left for lies."

"And when the ice breaks? When the press burns?"

"Then the plates die and the prints live. That was always the plan. The press is my father's body." Lior heard the words come out and did not take them back. "The atlas is what he actually made. Everything else was just how he held the tools."

Marta looked at Lior for a long moment across the lamp, and something passed between them that neither of them would ever name — not trust, not friendship, something harder and more useful than either. An agreement between two people who had each already lost the argument with themselves about safety.

"Then we don't stop," she said. "Not to sleep. Not to eat. Not for the crew."

"The crew starts tearing roofs off Monday. Maybe sooner. The mud won't hold forever."

"Then we have until the mud gives out." She rolled up her sleeves, took the stack of clean paper, and began feeding sheets to the platen with the crisp efficiency of a woman who had spent a year making false documents true. "Feed me. Count aloud. If we're going to outrun a demolition crew, I want to know by how many pages."

* * *

They printed through Saturday night, through Sunday dawn, in shifts that overlapped and blurred. Lior slept three hours on a cot of sacking behind the press and woke to Tomas shaking their shoulder, whispering that the crew's boy had been asking questions at the corner shop — who owned the cellar key, had anyone seen lights, did the nurse from the hospital still visit the condemned house. Sunday afternoon Marta went out in her census-taker's coat and spent forty minutes in the shop buying things nobody needed, and came back with the news that the foreman had filed to move demolition up to Wednesday. The mud, their last ally, was losing.

Sunday night the three of them worked together in the cellar for the first time, and it should have been dangerous and instead felt like a lit room in a dark house. Tomas at the press, arms aching, refusing relief. Marta collating and folding, building the atlases signature by signature — twenty-one leaves, sewn with thread from her own hem, wrapped in gray paper covers Lior had lettered with a title that looked, to any casual eye, like a municipal planning document: *Vilna: Survey of Street Conditions, 1942.* Inside, every condition that mattered was a person.

By four in the morning they had two hundred and six complete copies stacked in oilcloth-lined crates. Lior stood over them, ink to the elbows, hands trembling not from fear but from the strange exhaustion of having made something, and felt the feeling arrive whole, unwelcome, undeniable: joy. Ferocious, guilty, temporary joy.

"Look at them," Tomas said softly. "Two hundred cities. All of them true."

"Two hundred and six," Marta corrected, because accuracy was her form of tenderness.

And then the bell above the alley door rang once — the signal they'd agreed on for trouble — and all three of them went still as pressed flowers.

Through the gap under the door, in the blue hour before Monday's dawn, came the sound of boots. Not one pair. Several, unhurried, spaced like men who expected no resistance because resistance had already been arranged away on paper.

Lior reached for the anatomy text. Marta reached for her satchel of forged authorizations. Tomas reached for the nearest crate, as if he could lift two hundred cities and carry them out through his own small body.

The handle turned.
