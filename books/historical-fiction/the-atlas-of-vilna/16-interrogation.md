## 16. Interrogation

**Tomas**

The room they took him to had once been a schoolroom. He knew this because the chalk rail was still bolted above the slate board, and someone had written on it in a careful administrative hand: *Inventory due Friday.* Below that, older, in a child's letters half-erased by a sleeve: *papa come home soon.*

Tomas fixed his eyes on that second line and did not let go of it.

The chair they gave him was a schoolchair too, built for a twelve-year-old, and he was sixteen and all knees, so sitting in it made him look like an idiot or a criminal, whichever the two men across the desk preferred. They were not the ones from the raid. The ones from the raid had shouted. These two did not shout. The thin one drank tea and never seemed to swallow. The fat one wrote, and his pen made a sound like a beetle walking through flour.

"Name," said the fat one.

"Tomas Reznik."

"Age."

"Sixteen."

The pen paused. "You are fourteen. Your papers say fourteen. Why would you lie about being younger?"

"I'm not lying. I grew."

The thin one smiled at his tea. It was the first thing either of them had done that wasn't procedure, and Tomas understood it was meant as a door left open — walk through it, boy, tell us something human, and we will be human back. He had watched Lior talk to frightened patients. He knew how kindness could be a tool when the hands holding it were steady.

He kept his mouth shut.

"You were carrying paper," the fat one said. "Bundles of it. Oilcloth. At dawn. On Pylimo Street, in a building posted for demolition. Explain this."

"I move things for people. Bread, mostly. Paper sometimes. I don't read what I carry. Reading is extra."

"Extra."

"It costs more if you want me to know things. I charge double for knowing."

The pen scratched. The tea steamed. Somewhere below them a stove ticked, and outside a guard laughed at something, one short bark, and the sound came through the frosted window like weather from another country.

"Who pays you?"

"A man called Jonas." This was true; it was also useless. Jonas had died in November. A dead man made a beautiful employer.

"Where does Jonas live?"

"He has a room over the tannery. Or he did. He travels."

"What is in the oilcloth?"

"Paper."

"What does the paper say?"

Tomas looked at him then, directly, the way Lior had told him never to do with soldiers and always to do with clerks. *Soldiers hit what looks at them,* she'd said. *Clerks are frightened of it.*

"The paper says what's on it," he said. "I can't read German. My mother taught me Yiddish letters and my father taught me numbers, and neither of them taught me to be stupid enough to open other people's bundles."

The thin one set down his cup. The porcelain clicked against the saucer, and in the little silence after it, Tomas heard the ice on the river again, that long groaning note it had been making every night for a week, deep under everything, patient.

"Your family," the fat one said, without looking up. "Reznik. There is a record here. Parents, deceased. Two sisters —"

"Don't," said Tomas.

"— listed on a transport manifest of March —"

"*Don't.*"

His voice cracked on it and he hated himself for the crack, because now they knew where the soft place was, they had found it in under four minutes, and the fat one's pen hovered like a fly over a wound.

"They are on a list," the fat one said gently. "You understand lists. You move them. So you understand: a list is only ink until someone makes it flesh. We can make your sisters flesh again, Tomas Reznik. All it requires is that the oilcloth be found."

The chalk rail hung above their heads. *Papa come home soon.*

"There is no oilcloth," Tomas said. "I lost it. I dropped the bundle when they grabbed me. It went into the river."

"The river is frozen."

"Then it went under the ice, didn't it."

The thin one laughed — one breath through the nose, nothing more — and stood, buttoning his coat as though the interview were a coat he was finished wearing. "Keep him overnight," he said to no one in particular. "Cold rooms loosen children. In the morning, ask him again what floats under ice."

At the door he stopped, and looked back, and his voice changed into something almost kind, which was worse than everything before it.

"For what it is worth, boy — whoever wrote those pages trusted you. That is a rare thing to be given. And rarer still to keep."

The door closed. The guard took Tomas by the arm, not roughly, and walked him down a corridor past a row of doors, and behind one of them, faintly, a woman's voice was saying something calm and reasonable and endless, and Tomas thought: *she sounds like Marta Vogel.*

He did not look toward the voice. Looking was knowing, and knowing was the thing they charged double for, and he had decided, sitting in the child's chair with his family's names held out to him like bait, that tonight he was too poor to buy anything.

**Marta**

They gave her tea but no chair, which she understood immediately as theater: stand, be small, be summoned. She stood. The officer behind the desk — Hauptmann Weiss, whose signature she had forged twice in the last month and whose handwriting she knew better than her own brother's — did not look up for a full minute. When he finally did, he smiled, and Marta felt the smile go through her like cold water finding a seam in a wall.

"Fräulein Vogel. Sit anyway. I am told standing interrogations produce lies. Sitting produces confessions. I prefer confessions."

She sat.

"Your census district reports are exemplary," he said. "That is the problem."

"Sir?"

"Ninety-four percent accuracy rate, month over month. No anomalies. No discrepancies. Do you know what I see when I look at ninety-four percent, Fräulein?"

"Honest work."

"I see a hand smoothing the cloth before anyone checks underneath it." He turned a folder around so she could read it upside down, which she could, which was another mistake he was enjoying. "District Seven. Household of L. Lockridge, nurse, Feldstrasse. First visit: notation, *subject evasive re: kinship records.* Second visit — no notation at all. Third visit: a correction slip filed in your own hand, changing a birthdate. Whose error was it, Fräulein? Yours? Or the subject's?"

"Misread handwriting. Hospital ink is poor."

"Weiss ink is excellent," he said pleasantly. "Mine, I mean. I have been reading hospital forms all morning. Your correction slip is dated four days after your visit. Why does a census taker sit on an error for four days?"

"Because I checked it against parish records first. Diligence takes time."

"Diligence." He said the word the way a man says the name of a woman who has disappointed him. Then, without any change of tone at all: "Where is your brother?"

The room went very still. Marta had prepared for this question the way one prepares for weather — knowing it must come, unable to stop it, arranging only what stands near it. She had rehearsed grief; she had rehearsed blankness. What she had not rehearsed was how his voice would make Emil real again, in this room, for three seconds, before she killed him again to protect him.

"I don't have a brother, sir."

"No?" He slid a single sheet across the desk. An old form, pre-war, soft at the folds. *Vogel, Emil.* Her father's handwriting in the margin. She made herself look at it the length of one breath and then raise her eyes, empty.

"That is not my family. Vogel is a common name."

"Is it." He leaned back. "Here is what I think, Fräulein, and you may correct me. I think there is a ledger in my city. I think it is a good one — thorough, careful, kept by someone who understands paper the way I do. I think a nurse keeps it, or guards it, or is its fool. And I think a census taker who files corrections four days late and reports zero discrepancies in a district where I personally know of three unregistered deaths — I think that census taker has seen it."

"Sir, if there were such a ledger—"

"*If.*" He almost laughed. "Yes. If. Here is what happens next regardless of if. Tomorrow morning you will bring me your district files. All of them. And you will write me a report, in your own hand, stating what you know about the Lockridge household. I will compare it against what I already know. Where the two documents agree, we will have truth. Where they differ—" he folded his hands, "—we will have you."

"And if my report agrees entirely?"

"Then you have been honest, and honesty will have been its own reward." He opened the folder again, unhurried, a man with all the time in the world and everyone else's time besides. "There is also a boy. Sixteen, though he claims fourteen. Caught at dawn with paper on Pylimo Street. He is being questioned downstairs even now. His file shows two sisters on the March manifest — a transport, not a death list. Transports can be amended. Names can come off lists as easily as they go on. It is remarkable, really, how much mercy a name can purchase when it is traded properly."

Marta looked at her hands. They were perfectly still in her lap, and she despised them for it.

"You are offering me a trade," she said.

"I am offering you arithmetic." He stood, signaling the end of it, and walked her to the door himself, which he had not done for any subordinate in her memory. At the threshold he spoke quietly, close to her ear, a conspirator's distance.

"One report, Fräulein. One name in it. Give me the ledger's keeper and the boy walks out of here, and perhaps some Vogel somewhere comes off a list. Refuse, and I take all of you apart in alphabetical order. It is not cruelty. It is filing."

**Lior**

Lior stood in the doorway of the corridor because there was nowhere else in the world to stand.

They had brought her in an hour ago — a soldier's hand at her elbow, not rough, the way one handles something already owned — and Lior had followed at the pace of a woman carrying linens, which was the pace of a ghost, which was the discipline of eleven months of invisibility finally paying its dividend. Now she stood with a stack of folded sheets against her chest and watched through the wired glass of the office window as Marta sat with her spine straight and her hands quiet and lied to Hauptmann Weiss with a fluency that made Lior's stomach turn, because she recognized it. It was her own fluency. It was what eleven months of rehearsed answers sounded like coming out of another mouth.

*Household of L. Lockridge.* She could not hear the words through the glass, but she could read the shape of them in the officer's posture, in the folder he turned, in Marta's small precise nods. Her own name was in that room, being weighed. And Marta was holding it the way one holds a bird — firmly enough that it cannot fly, gently enough that it does not die in the grip.

*Why.* The question beat in her like a pulse. Not why lie — that part she could guess, the bargain, the brother's name, the leash she herself had fastened around both their necks at the print shop. But why *well*. Why risk the falsified report, why sit in that chair with her whole future folding itself smaller around her, for a stranger she had hunted and cornered and bargained with?

Because it was never a hunt, Lior thought. Because from the beginning she wasn't hunting the ledger. She was hunting one line in it, and everything else was the forest she got lost in on the way.

A guard came up the corridor and Lior became furniture — eyes down, sheets clutched, the slow apologetic shuffle of a woman who has learned that being noticed is the same as being numbered. The guard passed. Through the glass, Weiss was leaning close to Marta now, saying something into the space beside her ear, and Marta's face did the thing Lior had seen her do once before, at the print shop, over her brother's empty page: nothing. Absolute nothing. The face of a person pouring everything they have into keeping one muscle from moving.

Then Weiss straightened, satisfied or suspicious, impossible to tell, and Marta rose, and was escorted out through a side door, and was gone.

Lior stayed in the doorway a while longer, holding her linens, watching the empty office, the abandoned tea, the folder left squared neatly on the desk like a trap reset.

*Tomorrow morning*, the shape of the last exchange had said. *Files. Reports. In your own hand.*

Whatever Marta had promised in that room, she had bought Lior one night. Perhaps less. And somewhere below, behind a door Lior could not even find, a sixteen-year-old boy was paying in cold for pages he had never read, carrying a weight she had handed him with both hands and then watched him taken away under.

*I should turn myself in.* The thought arrived fully formed, calm, almost relieved. Walk into that office. Say: the ledger is mine, the boy knows nothing, the census taker saw nothing, write my name down, I'll spell it for you. It would end everything — the calculations, the thaw racing under the ice, the atlas three plates from finished. It would end it the way a river ends a bridge.

But it would not save Tomas. Weiss wanted the ledger, not its keeper, and a keeper delivered voluntarily was still just one keeper, while the plates remained hidden and the couriers remained loose and the arithmetic of terror continued exactly as before. Lior had spent eleven months learning what paperwork did to people. It did not want confession. Confession was inefficient. It wanted *everything*, and then it wanted everyone the everything touched.

So she stood in the doorway, and did not move, and understood that this too was a kind of answer — that love, tonight, had narrowed itself to a single terrible discipline: *stay invisible, stay useless, stay alive until dawn*, and then run faster than the ice could break.

Down the corridor, a door opened. A guard stepped out, rubbing his hands against the cold, and behind him — for one second, no more — Lior saw the schoolroom light fall across a thin boy sitting very straight in a small chair, staring at the wall, his lips moving slightly, silently, as though reciting something.

Names, Lior thought. He's reciting names. Mine, probably, and Marta's, and his sisters', keeping them warm the way I taught him the ledger keeps people warm — by saying them, over and over, so the dark doesn't get to be the only one who remembers.

The door closed.

Lior pressed her forehead briefly against the cold frame of the doorway, then lifted the linens higher against her chest and walked, unhurried, invisible, toward the stairwell and the night and the three plates still waiting on Pylimo Street — while somewhere beneath her feet, under the floorboards, under the foundations, under the whole frozen weight of the city, the river shifted in its sleep and groaned.
