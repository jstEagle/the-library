## 17. The Breaking of the Ice

The river spoke first.

Lior heard it through the walls of the workshop at four in the morning, a sound like a giant clearing its throat, deep and groaning, the ice flexing against its own weight. It had been talking for days — small complaints, cracks ticking through the night like someone counting in another room. But this was different. This was a voice with authority.

Lior sat up from the floor where they'd been sleeping between printing shifts, back against the dead press, and listened. Tomas stirred on his pallet by the door, one arm thrown over his eyes, still smelling of ink and river fog even in sleep. The plates were done. All of them. Forty-one streets, each name set in type no larger than a grain of barley, arranged not alphabetically but geographically — Pylimo Street holding its dead along its length, Rudninkų holding its own, every avenue and alley of Vilna rendered as a map of who had lived there and who had stopped. Lior's father had taught them that a map was an argument about what mattered. This one argued that every name mattered, that the city could not be redrawn without carrying all of it across.

The oilcloth bundle sat where it had sat for three days, wrapped and rewrapped, tied with cord, weighing less than a loaf of bread and more than anything Lior had ever carried.

*Hours left.* Marta's note had come at midnight, passed through the hands of a girl who sold cigarettes outside the garrison: *Demolition crew at dawn. They moved it up. Get out before first light or don't get out.*

And so there was nothing left to do but the thing Lior had dreaded most, the thing that had waited at the end of every calculation since January like a debt collector standing patiently in a doorway: the crossing.

Lior knelt beside Tomas and touched his shoulder. He came awake instantly, the way children in wartime learned to, surfacing from sleep like a diver, already listening.

"It's time," Lior said. "The river."

He didn't argue. He'd stopped arguing two days ago, when he'd understood the route himself — that the far bank meant the woods, the couriers, the long way around to safety, and that the bridge was watched now because someone had talked, because survival always cost someone else's safety. He rolled up his pallet and began stuffing straw into the gaps of his coat without being asked. That was the thing about Tomas. He had lost everything to a clerk's handwriting, and he had decided, somewhere in the nights of moving paper by sledge, that ink could fight back. He believed it the way other boys believed in God.

They packed in silence. The final copies were already divided — three bundles for three couriers, hidden in three places Lior would name only once each. But the master atlas, the oilcloth-wrapped heart of it, went with Lior. It always had to go with Lior.

Before they left, Lior stood for a moment in front of the press. Their father's press. The iron was cold and furred with dust, and the great screw hung slack like a jaw unclenched. Twenty years of maps had come off this machine — maps of coastlines, of mountain passes, of roads that did not officially exist — and young Lior had stood on a stool beside it learning which lines to ink and which to leave blank, learning that a blank space was never empty, that it was a held breath, a thing waiting to be named.

"Say goodbye," Tomas said quietly. Not mocking. He understood.

"I'm not saying goodbye to a machine."

"No," Tomas said. "You're saying goodbye to your father." And then, because he was sixteen and merciless in the way only the young are: "Take it with you instead. You've got room. It's just names anyway."

Lior almost smiled. Almost. Then they pulled the tarp over the press, took up the bundle, and opened the door onto the dark.

* * *

Pylimo Street smelled of smoke that hadn't started yet — the anticipation of it, the ghost of a fire that existed only in an officer's order. The sky was the color of wet slate, and the snow had gone gray and granular overnight, the texture of sugar going bad. Spring wasn't arriving. Spring was being *imposed*, the way everything was imposed now, and the city lay under it like a patient refusing treatment.

They walked fast, heads down, the bundle strapped flat against Lior's chest under their coat, where it rose and fell with each breath as though it were a second heartbeat. At the corner of Rudninkų, two soldiers were lighting a brazier outside the transport depot, and Lior counted the seconds it took to pass them — nine seconds, nine seconds of being a nurse walking to a shift, invisible, borrowed invisibility, never owned —

— and then the street opened and there was the river.

It looked wrong. Lior had crossed it in thought a hundred times, planned the crossing down to the meter, but planning was done in ink and this was water. The ice stretched bank to bank, white-gray, scarred with pressure ridges where the floes had shoved against each other like quarreling animals. Along the middle, where the current ran strongest beneath, the surface had gone dark and translucent, thin as a held breath. And everywhere, everywhere, the sounds — cracks traveling outward in long racing lines, a groaning so low Lior felt it in their teeth, then a report like a rifle shot that made Tomas flinch and grab Lior's sleeve.

"It's breaking up," he said. Stupidly, obviously, because they both knew it, because the whole river was announcing it.

"That's why we cross now," Lior said. "By noon there's no crossing at all. By tonight the demolition crew won't be our problem anymore — the river will have solved their workshop for them." The words came out steady, which surprised Lior, because inside there was nothing steady at all, only the arithmetic of desperation: *the atlas must reach the far bank; you can swim maybe thirty seconds in this water; therefore do not fall in.*

They went down the bank at the old ferry landing, where the ice met the shore in a shelf of dirty white. Lior tested the edge with one boot. Solid enough — solid the way a promise is solid right up until it isn't.

"Spread out," Lior said. "Ten paces apart. If I go through, keep going. Don't stop, don't turn around, don't try to help me. You take the bundle if it floats. Do you understand me?"

Tomas looked at Lior for a long moment, and something passed over his face that was too old for sixteen — the expression of someone who had already buried a family and knew exactly what instructions like these cost.

"You first," he said. "I'll follow."

So Lior stepped onto the river.

* * *

The first twenty meters were easy, and easy was the worst thing that could have happened. The ice held with a dull confidence, and Lior's boots rang on it, and behind them Tomas's footsteps kept their ten-pace distance, and ahead the far bank showed its bare trees against the graying sky like a congregation waiting for something to end.

Then the color changed underfoot.

Lior saw it a half-second before feeling it — the white giving way to a smoky translucence, the river showing through like a face behind frosted glass. The current was visible down there, dark green muscle sliding north, and through the ice Lior could see it moving, *moving*, the way you could see the pulse in a throat. Every lesson of the winter said: distribute your weight, slide your feet, don't stop, don't run. Lior slid, weight low, arms out, the bundle pressed tight against the sternum.

A crack opened six meters to the left with a sound like a door slamming in a cathedral. A ridge of ice buckled upward, sheared white against gray, and the whole sheet shuddered — Lior felt it travel through their boots, up the shin bones, into the teeth. Somewhere downstream, a large floe broke free with a groan and began its grinding journey toward the bridge.

Keep moving. Ten meters of translucent ice. Five. Lior's breath came in ragged flags of steam. Below, the current seemed to quicken, as if the river had noticed them, as if it were rising to meet the soles of their boots.

The crack came from directly underneath.

There was no decision in what happened next, only sequence: the sound, the lurch, the world tilting, and then cold — not cold as Lior had ever known it, but cold as annihilation, cold that closed around the body like a fist and squeezed the breath into a single bright spike of pain. The river took them. Down. The world above became a ceiling of churning silver, and the bundle tore free of the coat strap and shot upward, buoyant, treacherous, and Lior's hand shot after it on instinct older than thought — *the names, the names, the names* — fingers closing on the cord just as the current seized their coat and dragged them sideways, tumbling, the light overhead swinging away like a lantern in a struck room.

This is how it ends, Lior thought, with terrible clarity, in the roaring dark. Not with a bullet. With a river keeping their records.

Their lungs burned. The cold had passed through pain into something beyond sensation, a vast indifference, and some part of Lior — the part that had spent two years making lists of the dead — noted distantly how simple it would be to stop. To let go of the cord. To let the river finish what the paperwork had started.

No.

Lior kicked. The boot found ice — underside ice, slick, sloping — and they drove upward along its angle, dragging the bundle, hauling themselves hand over hand along the frozen ceiling until the silver above split open and there was a hole, a black seam of open water breathing fog, and Lior surfaced through it into air that cut like broken glass.

Gasping. Coughing river. Hands locked on the cord, knuckles fused to it with cold. The oilcloth bundle floated beside them, intact, sealed, *dry* — Lior would check this later eleven hundred times, but in that moment they simply knew it, the way you know your own heartbeat.

The edge of the floe was right there. Lior got one forearm onto it and it held, and then the ice on the far side of the hole shifted — a grinding, a settling, the whole sheet rearranging itself around the wound they'd made in it — and for one suspended second Lior lay spread across the gap like a person lying across a grave, half in the water, half out, the current pulling at their legs with patient enormous hands.

*Tomas.* Lior twisted their head. Across the channel of open water, on the far floe, Tomas stood motionless, ten paces of black water between them, his fists clenched at his sides, obeying the instruction — *don't stop, don't turn around* — with the rigid obedience of someone fighting every instinct he had.

"Go!" Lior shouted, and the word tore out of them in steam. "GO!"

He went. Lior watched him turn and run, low and sliding, toward the far bank, growing smaller, and then Lior turned back to the business of not dying, which required everything: elbow by elbow, knee by knee, rolling away from the hole across ice that flexed and complained beneath them, leaving a trail of soaked cloth that froze stiff within minutes. The last stretch to shore they crawled, because standing was no longer possible, hands and knees on the shelving ice, until gravel scraped their palms and the far bank rose around them — mud, dead leaves, the roots of birches — real ground, unbroken ground, ground that stayed put when you pressed on it.

Lior collapsed among the roots and clutched the bundle to their chest and shook. Not weeping, exactly. Something more mechanical than that, the body emptying itself of terror the way a stove empties of ash. Behind them, across the wide gray river, Vilna lay in its bowl of smoke, and the ice continued to speak in its vast cracking language, carrying its floes north like a fleet of broken white ships.

* * *

Tomas found them twenty minutes later, having run the bank downstream to find a place where the open channel narrowed. His face was bloodless. He dropped to his knees in the mud and grabbed Lior's shoulders, shaking them once, hard, then checking — hands flying over the arms, the ribs, as if cataloguing damage — and finally seizing the bundle and turning it over, looking for holes in the oilcloth, finding none.

"It's dry," he said, and his voice cracked on the second word. "It's dry, it's all dry, look—"

"I know." Lior's teeth were chattering so hard the words came out in pieces. "I know. Help me up."

They got Lior upright, and together, leaning on each other, they climbed the bank to the tree line. From there, from the shadow of the birches, Lior turned and looked back one last time.

Across the river, dawn had finally arrived, gray and indifferent. And on Pylimo Street, visible even at this distance as a smudge of wrong-colored air, smoke was rising. The demolition crew had not bothered with demolition. There had been no wrecking bars, no careful dismantling. Just men with cans of kerosene and a torch, and the workshop — the press, the engraving stones, the drawers of type Lior's father had set letter by letter, the stool young Lior had stood on to learn which lines to ink and which to leave blank — all of it going up in a column of black smoke that bent eastward in the wind.

Tomas made a sound beside them, half grief, half fury, and started forward, and Lior caught his arm.

"There's nothing there," Lior said. "That's the point. There hasn't been anything there for years except what we carried out." The lie came easily, fluently, the way all Lior's lies came now, and they hated it, and it was also true, which was worse.

The fire would be in the official reports by evening. *Structure destroyed due to structural instability, contents lost.* Another omission. Another blank line. Lior's father's life reduced to a clerical footnote, and somewhere in the files of the occupation a pen would move across a page without pausing, the way Marta's pen had paused, that first day, on the question no census should ask.

Marta. Lior's chest tightened around something that was not the river's cold. Where was Marta? What had the dawn cost her? She had promised to stall them — forged documents, a falsified inspection, whatever she could build out of her own position like a wall built out of her own body. And Tomas had been supposed to be past the checkpoint by now, not standing in a birch wood watching smoke rise from everything either of them had left.

"We have to keep moving," Lior said. "The couriers' drops are north. If the thaw comes fully today, the roads will flood and we'll lose a week."

But Tomas wasn't moving. He was staring back across the river, at the smoke, at the burning shell of Pylimo Street, and his jaw was working.

"They'll say it was empty," he said finally. "The building. In the report. They'll write that nobody was inside." He turned to Lior, and his eyes were wet and furious and sixteen years old. "But people *were* inside. Your father was inside. Twenty years of him. And they'll write *contents lost*, and that'll be the end of it, unless—" He put his hand flat against the oilcloth bundle, against the forty-one streets sleeping inside it. "Unless somebody says otherwise."

Lior looked at him — this boy who had lost his family to a list and chosen to answer a list with a list — and felt something settle in their chest that was not hope exactly, but its stubborn cousin.

"Then let's go say otherwise," Lior said.

They turned north, into the trees, two figures soaked and smoking in the cold morning air, carrying forty-one streets between them. Behind them the ice groaned and shifted and broke, the whole winter coming apart at once, and the river ran free and dark toward the spring, taking the old year's silence with it.

Ahead, on the road through the woods, Lior could see, far off, a figure walking toward them — a woman's silhouette against the pale sky, moving with a purposeful stride Lior recognized even at that distance, even through the haze of exhaustion and cold.

Lior stopped walking.

"Marta?" Tomas whispered.

The figure raised one arm — not a wave. A warning.
