## 20. Deliverance

The first courier left on a Tuesday, before dawn, when the frost still held its grip on the cobbles and the city belonged to no one but the dogs and the milk carts.

Lior had chosen him the way they chose everything now — by subtraction. Not a stranger, because a stranger could not be trusted with paper that mattered. Not a friend, because friends were the first names in any interrogation file. Not someone who owed them, and not someone they owed, because debts were their own kind of ledger and ledgers got read.

What remained was a woman named Doba Kagan, sixty-one years old, a widow who sold buttons from a tray she carried strapped to her back like a tortoise shell. She had walked to Kaunas eleven times since the war began, trading buttons for flour, and no soldier had ever opened her tray. Buttons, they decided long ago, were beneath suspicion. Lior had spent two evenings sewing the atlas pages into the false bottom of the tray itself, flattening each sheet between squares of waxed linen, until the whole thing weighed no more than a handful of extra stock. Doba had watched them work without asking a single question, which was how Lior knew she understood exactly what she was carrying.

"You'll go by the river road," Lior told her, the night before. "Not the highway. The highway has a checkpoint at Paneriai."

"I know where the checkpoint is." Her voice was dry as the chalk dust she used to mark prices. "I've been walking past checkpoints since your father was learning his letters."

They sat in the back room of a house on Subačiaus Street that belonged to a cousin of a cousin, a room that smelled of tallow and cold plaster. The atlas lay open between them on the table — or rather, one of its copies did. Three copies now. Three cities. Lior had spent the weeks since the ice broke doing nothing else: copying, pressing, binding, hiding, copying again. Their hands had taken on the smell of ink permanently, the way a priest's hands take on incense. Every night they fell asleep seeing columns of names behind their eyelids, marching like soldiers, patient as water.

"Kaunas first," Lior said. "Then you'll give it to the rabbi's wife — I've written the address inside the cover, but only the street and the number. No name."

"And if the rabbi's wife has been deported?"

"Then leave it under the third floorboard of her kitchen and walk away. Someone will find it. Someone always finds things when they're meant to be found."

Doba looked at them across the candle stub. Her face was a landscape of small rivers and old weather, and in it Lior saw something they had almost forgotten existed: a face that had already survived everything it was going to survive. There was a freedom in that. A terrible, quiet freedom.

"You know," she said, closing the copy and running her thumb along its spine, "my husband kept accounts. Forty years a bookkeeper for the tannery. He used to say that numbers don't lie, but people lie *with* numbers, and the trick is knowing which you're looking at." She tapped the cover once. "This isn't numbers."

"No."

"This is worse. This is better." She stood, joints cracking like green wood, and lifted the button tray onto her back. "I'll have it in Kaunas by Friday if the roads hold. If the roads don't hold, I'll have it there by Sunday anyway."

"Doba." Lior rose too. They wanted to say thank you, and found the words had been confiscated along with so much else. What came out instead was: "If they stop you—"

"They won't."

"But if they do—"

"Then they'll find an old woman with buttons," Doba said, "and a story about her sister in Kaunas who needs a trousseau, God rest her imaginary soul. And they'll be bored, because bored men don't search, they wave you through." She paused at the door, small and bent under her enormous shell. "You gave me something to carry that matters. Do you know how long it's been since anyone asked an old woman to carry anything that matters?"

She left before the sky lightened. Lior stood at the window and watched her figure diminish down Subačiaus Street, the tortoise shell swaying, until the fog took her, and then they went back inside and did not sleep at all.

* * *

The second courier left four days later, bound south for Grodno, and he nearly didn't leave at all.

His name was Yankl Bren, nineteen years old, a shoemaker's apprentice with hands stained black at the seams and a stutter that appeared only when he was frightened, which in 1942 was most of the time. He had come to Lior through Tomas — through the network of boys who moved paper and bread and rumor through the occupied streets the way blood moves through capillaries, invisibly, essentially. Yankl had volunteered. That was the problem.

"I can't send you," Lior told him, on the third evening, after watching him rehearse his cover story for the fourth time and stumble over the name of his fictional aunt.

"Because of my—" His jaw worked. His ears went red. "Because I talk wrong."

"Because you talk *carefully*." Lior set down the page they were copying. In the safehouse's single room, lit by a lamp turned low enough to barely qualify as light, the shadows made everything look provisional. "A liar who stumbles makes men curious. Curious men open bundles."

"Then I won't stumble."

"You can't promise that. Nobody can promise that. That's the whole point of fear."

Yankl stared at the floor. When he spoke again, the stutter was gone entirely, which somehow made it worse. "My brother Avreml went east with the deportations. We got one letter, and then nothing. My mother sits by the window every day like the letter might come back and explain itself. I'm not doing this for Vilna, Lockridge. I'm doing it because somewhere there's a list with his name on it, and lists are the only thing that ever happens to us, so I want to be on the right side of one for once."

Lior had heard versions of this speech before — from Marta, once, in another life, standing over their father's dead press. Everyone in this war was hunting a list, or fleeing one, or trying to write one that would outlive the men who wrote the other kind.

"Your aunt in Grodno," Lior said slowly. "Her name?"

"Basya."

"The street?"

"Ozerna—" He caught it. Closed his eyes. "Ostrovskogo. Number fourteen."

"And why are you traveling?"

"To bring her Mother's shawl. For Passover." He recited it flatly, a catechism. Then he looked up, and there was something in his young face that Lior recognized from the mirror: the specific stubbornness of someone who has decided that being afraid is not the same as being stopped. "I practiced for six days. Ask me again tomorrow. And the day after. Ask me every day until I sound like a boy who doesn't think about anything but shawls."

So Lior did. Four days of drills, of interruptions mid-answer, of sudden questions designed to crack him open — *where does your aunt buy her flour, what color is her door, when did you last see your mother alive*. On the fifth day Yankl answered everything smoothly, boringly, with the magnificent dullness of a boy thinking about shawls, and Lior sewed the second copy of the atlas into the lining of his coat collar, split across both sides so no single cut would reveal it.

At the door, Yankl turned back.

"Tomas says you taught him the trade." The stutter flickered and vanished. "Remembering. Is it a real trade? Like shoemaking? Can you apprentice in it?"

"It's a trade," Lior said. "It's the oldest one."

"Then maybe when I get back—"

"When you get back," Lior said, and heard their own voice do something it hadn't done in months: it believed the sentence.

He went south on a Tuesday. By Friday, Doba should have reached Kaunas. Two copies moving through the world like seeds in the wind. Lior counted them at night the way a miser counts coins, and hated themselves a little for counting, and counted anyway.

One copy remained. The master. It stayed hidden in its oilcloth, in the place only Lior knew, waiting for a third pair of hands that hadn't appeared yet.

* * *

Tomas came back on a Thursday in late March, when the snow was rotting in the gutters and the whole city smelled of wet stone and thaw.

Lior heard about it before they saw him — word traveled through the network faster than official news traveled through its own channels, the way water finds cracks a surveyor never mapped. *The boy from Pylimo Street is out. The Vogel woman bought it. They released him Friday.* Each messenger added a detail, and each detail cost something to hear: that Tomas had endured nine days of questioning; that he had given them nothing but the biography of a fictitious uncle; that Marta Vogel had confessed to forging transport documents, had taken the fall for falsified reports, had traded her own position — perhaps her own life — against his release, and had then simply vanished from the record, which was the only way she would ever have wanted it.

Lior received all of this standing in the doorway of the safehouse, holding a cup of chicory substitute gone cold in their hand, saying nothing, feeling everything arrive at once like passengers crowding onto a tram.

When Tomas finally appeared, on that Thursday evening, Lior almost didn't recognize him.

He had gone in as a boy of sixteen with quick eyes and a smart mouth. He came out looking older than Lior felt, which was a sentence Lior would once have thought impossible. His face had been reorganized around some new center of gravity. He moved carefully, the way people move who have learned precisely what their body can be made to endure and would prefer never to verify it again. But his eyes — his eyes were the same eyes that had once stood in a hospital ward and asked the question no one dared: *whose side is the ledger on?* Only now they had answers, and the answers had cost him, and he carried them without complaint, the way he had carried the bundle they arrested him with.

He stood in the doorway of the safehouse and said, "I need work."

Lior stepped aside to let him in. "You should rest. You should eat. You should—"

"I've been resting for nine days on a plank bench while a man with a rubber hose explained to me the error of my ways." Tomas sat down heavily at the table, in the chair Lior always used, and looked around the room with an appraiser's eye. "I'm done resting. I want to learn the trade."

"The trade."

"Remembering. You said it yourself — the night in the ward, when I asked whose side the ledger was on. You said it wasn't on a side. You said it was a *trade*, and trades get passed on." He leaned forward. Under the lamp his hands were steady, and Lior understood that the steadiness was new, was hard-won, was the one thing the interrogators had failed to shake loose. "I lost everyone to a clerk's handwriting, Lockridge. A list. Efficient penmanship. My family didn't die — they were *filed*. And for nine days I sat in that room listening to men talk about files and categories and processing, and I thought: this is what they think paper is for. This is what they think writing is *for*. And then I thought about your ledger, and about Marta —" his voice caught on her name, briefly, and steadied — "about what she did with hers. Paper isn't the weapon. Paper is just the hand. The question is whose hand holds it."

Lior sat down across from him. Between them, on the scarred table, lay the tools of the last year: nibs, a pot of iron-gall ink gone the brown of dried blood, the anatomy text with its hollowed spine, blank sheets cut to match official dimensions. A trade's worth of instruments. Lior had never thought of them that way before. They had thought of them as survival equipment, as contraband, as a secret. But secrets end when the secret-keeper ends. A trade goes on.

"If you learn this," Lior said quietly, "you can't unlearn it. You'll see lists everywhere for the rest of your life. Census forms. Ration cards. Shipping manifests. You'll read them the way I do — looking for the gaps, the omissions, the names that got erased between the line and the page. And every gap will feel like a debt. Every single one. You'll spend your whole life paying other people's arithmetic."

"Good," Tomas said.

"It isn't good. It's a burden."

"It's an inheritance." He said it without drama, the way he'd say *it's raining*. "Someone passed it to me. First the Germans, with their lists. Then you, with yours. Then Marta, with what she gave up." He pulled a blank sheet toward himself and picked up a pen, testing the nib against his thumbnail the way he'd once tested a sledge rope. "I didn't ask for any of it. Neither did you. Neither did she. That's what makes it an inheritance instead of a choice — you get it whether you asked or not. The only question is whether you throw it away or carry it forward."

Lior looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the thaw was working at the city, dripping from every eave, loosening everything frozen into movement. Somewhere south, Doba Kagan was walking toward Kaunas with a city's dead in her button tray. Somewhere further south, Yankl Bren was rehearsing shawls. And here, in a rented room that smelled of ink and tallow, the trade was passing hands — not because Lior had chosen to pass it, but because it had chosen its moment, the way rivers choose their moment to break.

"Rule one," Lior said, and slid the anatomy text across the table. "Never keep the whole ledger in one place. Not in one book, not in one hiding spot, not in one head. Memory is a country. Countries get invaded. So we scatter the map."

Tomas opened the hollowed binding and looked at the false spine, the shallow cavity, the architecture of concealment his own hands had helped build without ever being told its purpose. "Rule two?"

"We write everything twice. Once for the record, once for the ruin. The record gets delivered. The ruin stays with you, here—" Lior touched their own temple. "If they take the paper, the paper was only ever the copy. Rule three:"

But Tomas was already nodding, already reaching for a fresh sheet, already dipping the pen, and Lior stopped talking and started teaching instead — showing him how to shape a name so it could pass for bureaucratic filler, how to date entries backward, how to make a forged hand look *tired*, because tired hands escape suspicion in ways clever ones never do.

They worked until the lamp burned down to a blue nub and the first grey came into the window. At some point Tomas looked up, pen still moving, and said:

"There's one more thing. The master copy. The one that stayed behind." His eyes came up and held Lior's across the dying flame. "Marta's brother. Abram Vogel. She gave everything for a name that's still sitting in oilcloth in a hole in this city — and she's not here to carry it anywhere. Somebody has to finish delivering it. And I know where you kept it, Lockridge. I've known since the raid. I watched you seal it before they took me."

The lamp guttered. Lior felt the weight of the master copy settle onto their shoulders like a hand — the last undelivered name, the last unpaid entry, sitting in its hiding place on Pylimo Street, in the shadow of the burned workshop, waiting for someone to walk back into the ashes and finish the account.

"Pylimo Street," Lior said.

"After first light," Tomas answered. "Before anyone else remembers what's buried there."
