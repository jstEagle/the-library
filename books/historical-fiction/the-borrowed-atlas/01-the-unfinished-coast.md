## 1. The Unfinished Coast

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when Tamsin Fairweather finally set down the pen and flexed fingers that had gone stiff as winter twigs. Outside the workshop window, Falmouth harbor lay black and still, masts scratching at a sky the color of wet slate. Inside, forty-one plates of *The Coastal Atlas of Cornwall and Adjacent Waters* stood in finished ranks along the shelves, and one — plate forty-two, the Carrick Roads and the Fal estuary — sat half-born under Tamsin's hands.

Half-born. That was the phrase that kept rising up like a bubble through all the careful ink. Half-born, and its father dead three months in the ground.

Tamsin blew on the drying line of the estuary's eastern shore and allowed, just for a moment, the luxury of looking at it not as work but as work *done*. The soundings marched down the channel in their tidy numerals like well-behaved soldiers. The shoal off Turnaware Point sat where Mr. Penhaligon had drawn it, rendered in the fine stipples that made sand seem almost to shift under the eye. Even the little trees along the bank were right — not decorative flourishes but honest trees, each one placed from the master's own field notes, because Nathaniel Penhaligon had held that a map which lied about a tree would lie about anything.

"A map is a promise," he used to say, tapping the table with two fingers, tap-tap, the way he tapped everything. "Every line you draw is you telling some poor captain, *I have been here, I have measured this with my own hands, and I stake my name upon it.*" He'd pause there. He always paused there, because the next part was: "And my name is all I've got."

It hadn't been true, quite. He'd had a workshop smelling of gum arabic and lamp oil, a cat named Bosphorus who considered himself senior staff, an apprentice of no particular surname whom nobody had ever legally claimed, and a reputation — once golden, lately cracked — with the Admiralty. But Tamsin understood what he'd meant. A name was what you signed your promises with.

Nathaniel Penhaligon had died for his. That was the plain ugly truth of it, whatever the coroner had written. *Accidental fall upon the harbor stairs*, the paper said, in the same bored hand that wrote *found drowned* over sailors everyone knew had been drunk. Tamsin had seen the stairs. Had climbed those stairs ten thousand times, in fog, at night, carrying boards and ink and once, memorably, Bosphorus under one arm during a mouse emergency. Those stairs were worn smooth as a spoon. Men did not fall on them. Men were helped.

But saying so out loud got you looked at the way the harbormaster now looked at Tamsin — a long, careful look, the look of a man deciding how much trouble a person was worth. So Tamsin said nothing. Tamsin drew coastlines.

There was a strange mercy in it, Tamsin had discovered. Grief was an ocean, and the hands could be a boat. When the hands were moving — ruling lines, mixing Prussian blue, stippling a beach grain by patient grain until the wrist ached — the ocean stayed outside. It was only when the work stopped that the water came in over the gunwales. So the work did not stop. The work had not stopped in three months, except to sleep, eat standing up, and occasionally argue with Bosphorus about whose chair it was.

The candle guttered. Tamsin trimmed the wick, took up the pen again, and began the lettering for Blackrock Point.

* * *

Morning arrived gray and apologetic, and with it came Mr. Treloar.

Treloar was a creditor, or rather the local representative of several creditors bundled together for efficiency, a narrow man with narrow boots and a ledger of his own — though his ledger recorded only debts, never depths, and so in Tamsin's private taxonomy ranked somewhere below a tide table with pages missing. He arrived every Tuesday and sometimes Fridays too, always at the same hour, always with the air of a man doing something unpleasant merely because unpleasant things required doing by someone, and why not him.

"The atlas," Treloar said, removing his hat as if the room might smell better without it. It did not; the room smelled of the workshop, which was to say of home. "Progress."

"Plate forty-two," Tamsin said. "Nearly done. Then the title page, the dedication, the index of—"

"Forty-two plates." Treloar's pen hovered over his book. "And how many does a buyer pay for?"

"Forty-two," Tamsin said, with the patience of a saint who has explained the same thing to the same donkey many times. "The subscription list stands at eleven gentlemen and the Trinity House. Each copy is complete or it is worthless. An atlas missing its final plate is a jest bound in calfskin."

"Mm." Treloar wrote something anyway. Treloar always wrote something anyway; Tamsin suspected he was paid by the entry. "Mr. Penhaligon owed four hundred and six pounds at his death. Interest accrues."

"I know what it does. I watch it do it."

"There's been interest expressed," Treloar went on, in the tone of a man unveiling a bargain, "in purchasing the plates outright. Copper's worth money even blank. There are parties who'd melt the lot and think it a fair morning's work."

For one hot instant Tamsin saw it — the plates, the master's plates, each one months of a dead man's life, tipped into a crucible and poured out as dull ingots — and had to grip the edge of the drawing table to keep the world steady.

"No," Tamsin said.

"It would settle matters."

"*No.*"

Treloar sighed, put his hat back on, and paused at the door with the air of a man delivering a parting blow he'd been saving. "The widow comes Friday," he said. "Mrs. Fairweather. She wrote to say she has claims upon the estate. Family rights." His eyebrows performed a small dance suggesting that in his opinion family rights were like gout — hereditary, painful, and arriving uninvited. "You'll want to have things... in order."

He left. Tamsin stood a moment listening to his boots diminish down the lane, then crossed to the window and watched him go. Across the way, at the window of the house with the blue door, Mrs. Quill stood behind her curtains watching him too. She caught Tamsin catching her, and neither pretended otherwise. Mrs. Quill never pretended anything; pretending, she maintained, was for people with nothing better to do than maintain illusions, whereas she kept accounts.

* * *

The middle of the day belonged to the trade. Tamsin opened the shutters wide, propped the door, and set out the display: charts for sale, a brass dividers polished to temptation, the good hand-paper stacked in fives. Business had fallen off since the master's death — that was the other wound, the one that bled slower. Before, the workshop's name had meant something in Falmouth. Captains had come in squinting from the light to argue happily about soundings. Now they came in less often, and when they did there was a new hesitation in them, a glance thrown at the corner where the master's chair stood empty, and questions asked carefully around the shape of what everyone suspected and no one said.

A fisherman named Curnow bought a chart of the Helford and lingered, turning his cap in his hands.

"They say the Admiralty's new survey's coming out come spring," he offered.

"Are they." Tamsin kept sorting paper.

"Heard it'll show the Manacles swept clear. Deep water right through." Curnow's eyes flicked up, quick and testing. "Your master always said there was a spit of sand there the official charts missed. Said it moved after every big blow, and no Admiralty vessel had bothered to look since before the war. Said—" He stopped himself, cap twisting harder. "Well. You know what he said."

Tamsin knew what he said. Tamsin had heard it a hundred times, had inked the offending sandbar onto the master's private working charts while he dictated, tap-tap against the table. *They've erased it, boy. Wiped it off the face of the sea because admitting it exists means admitting their first survey was wrong, and the Admiralty would sooner lose a ship than lose face. They'll lose both, mark me.*

Then the *Providence* had gone aground on precisely that sandbar on a night the Admiralty chart promised safe passage, with the loss of nine men, and the inquiry had not blamed the chart. The inquiry had blamed the chartmaker. Nathaniel Penhaligon, whose own working surveys showed the hazard plain as a bruise, had been named in the papers as the careless cartographer whose false information misled the vessel — as though his hand-drawn warnings, never published, suppressed by the very office that falsified the printed plate, were somehow the poison. His commission had dried up. His subscriptions had cancelled. Six weeks later he was dead on the harbor stairs, and his unfinished atlas lay on the table waiting for an apprentice to finish it.

"Charts are like sermons," Tamsin said aloud, to Curnow, to the room, to nobody. "Everyone swears by them, and nobody checks the text."

Curnow grinned sadly, paid his penny, and left.

* * *

Afternoon. Bosphorus supervised from the windowsill with the gravity of an admiral reviewing the fleet, which consisted of one fishing smack beating up the estuary. Tamsin worked on the lettering, and the lettering was going beautifully, which should have been a comfort and instead was becoming a kind of terror.

Because here was the thing Tamsin could not say to Treloar, could not say to anyone, could barely say inside the privacy of the skull: the atlas was nearly finished, and finishing it terrified Tamsin more than failing to.

An unfinished atlas needed an apprentice. A finished atlas needed an author. And when the last plate was signed and delivered to eleven gentlemen and the Trinity House, there would be nothing left between Tamsin Fairweather and the plain fact of Tamsin Fairweather — a person of no documented origin, apprenticed informally, never legally bound, named in no will (the master had died too suddenly to make one, or perhaps had simply had no one to leave things *to*, which amounted to the same), possessed of a skill, a cat, a workshop that belonged to the creditors, and a name borrowed off the master's sister's family as casually as one borrows an umbrella. *Fairweather*. The widow's name. Given to a foundling left on a church step in Fowey with nothing but a blanket and, later, a great deal of speculation.

Twenty years in this house and Tamsin was still, on every paper that mattered, *the apprentice*. No one's daughter, no one's son — even that was uncertain enough that Tamsin had long ago stopped letting strangers' eyes decide the question and dressed as convenience suggested, which mostly meant whichever coat was nearest the ink.

When the atlas was done, what was Tamsin then?

The pen hovered. In the margin of plate forty-two, in the tiny space beside the compass rose where a cartographer's initials traditionally went — *del. N.P.*, *drawn by Nathaniel Penhaligon* — there was room for one more abbreviation. The master had always said the apprentice's hand would finish the last plates, and tradition held that the finishing hand be credited. *Del. N.P., fin. T.F.*

Two letters. T. F. It was nothing. It was everything. It was a signature on a promise, and Tamsin's whole life had been spent learning that signatures were dangerous things, that names invited claims, that claiming got people killed on staircases.

The pen came down. The letters went on, small and steady:

*fin. T.F.*

Bosphorus yawned, which Tamsin chose to interpret as approval.

* * *

Evening brought lamplight, bread and cheese eaten over the working copy of plate forty-three — Mount's Bay, barely begun, the coastline still ghost-pale in construction lines — and the ritual of the master's field notebooks. This was the part of the day Tamsin loved and dreaded equally, because reading the notebooks was like hearing the voice again: the cramped confident script, the marginal grumbles (*Admlty chart WRONG here — off by half a cable — scandalous*), the little sketches in the margins of seabirds and pub signs and once, inexplicably, a pig wearing a hat.

Page 312 of the third notebook. Tamsin knew it by heart now.

*Sounded the Manacles approach myself, third time this year. Spit confirmed, extends further east than any printed chart admits. Copied my correction and sent it to the Hydrographic Office at their request — their words — last March. No acknowledgment. If the *Providence* or any vessel grounds there trusting the printed plate, the blood is on the Office, not on me, whatever they print afterward. God forgive the men who draw safe water where there is none.*

God forgive the men who draw safe water where there is none.

Tamsin closed the notebook gently, the way one closes a sleeping child's door, and sat a long while in the lamplight doing sums that had nothing to do with money. Because there was a thought that had been circling for three months like a moth round a flame, never landing, never quite leaving: *they requested the correction.* The Hydrographic Office had *asked* for the master's true survey. Which meant the Office had known exactly what the master knew. Which meant the falsification wasn't ignorance. It was policy. And the master had known that they knew, and had written it down, and had kept copies—

Had kept copies. Where? Tamsin had turned the workshop over twice looking for the master's original corrected survey of the Manacles approach — the field sheets, the fair copy, any scrap in his hand showing what the official charts concealed. Nothing. The drawer where such things lived held only blank paper and a dead spider. Either the Office had never returned them, or someone had removed them after the fall on the stairs, or—

Or they had never been here at all. Sent away, perhaps. Hidden elsewhere. Lost.

The candle burned low again. Tamsin banked the fire, checked the bolts — a habit begun the week after the funeral and not, if truth be told, entirely irrational — and carried the lamp toward the loft stairs. Bosphorus flowed down from the sill and led the way, tail aloft, senior staff conducting the inspection.

Halfway across the floor, Tamsin stopped.

The wind had come up. It worried the shutters and set the sign above the door creaking — the old sign, the painted quadrant and compass needle that had swung over the lane for thirty years. Beneath it, the lane ran dark toward the harbor, and beyond the harbor the sea worked at the dark, and somewhere out in that dark lay the true shape of the coast, patient as evidence, waiting for someone with the courage and the ink to write it down.

Finish the atlas, Tamsin told the empty room. Keep the head down, the hands busy, the name small. Deliver the forty-two plates. Survive the widow on Friday. Let the century end and a new one begin, and see what a new century makes of an apprentice no one claimed.

The lamp flame bowed as a draft slid under the door.

And it was later — much later, past midnight, past the hour when even the taverns along the quay had given up their arguments — that the knock came.

Three soft raps. Then nothing. Then, fainter, as though whoever stood outside had already begun to turn away: a fourth, hardly more than a touch, like a question asked by someone afraid of the answer.

Bosphorus sat up in the dark, ears forward, staring at the door.

Tamsin stared too, heart ticking, the cold of the floor climbing through bare feet, and reached for the lamp.
