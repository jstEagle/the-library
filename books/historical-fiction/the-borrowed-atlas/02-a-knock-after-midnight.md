## 2. A Knock After Midnight

The knock came at the worst possible hour, which is to say it came at all.

Tamsin Fairweather had spent the better part of that evening hunched over Plate Fourteen — the approaches to Carrick Roads, where the master's hand had grown so fine in his last months that his soundings looked less like measurements than like embroidery — and had reached that peculiar state of exhaustion where the candles seem to breathe and the ink smells almost like bread. It was past eleven by the church bells of St. Mawes, and the wind off the harbor had worked itself into one of its December tempers, rattling the shutters like a creditor's knuckles.

So when the knock came, soft and double, Tamsin assumed at first it was only the wind again, or the elm branch, or the general malice of old houses.

Then it came a second time. Deliberate. Human. Apologetic, even — two quick raps followed by nothing, as though whoever stood on the step had immediately regretted the whole enterprise.

Tamsin sat very still, pen lifted from the paper, and listened the way the master had taught them to listen: not for sounds, but for what sounds were *not*. No footsteps retreating down the lane. No second knock. Only the wind, and beneath it, faintly, something smaller — a sound Tamsin could not name and did not yet want to.

"Who's there?" The question came out steadier than expected. Grief does that sometimes; it hollows a person into an instrument that rings clear when struck.

Silence answered. Then, from the doorstep, a different sound entirely — a small, wet, unmistakable sound that no wind has ever made and no creditor ever will.

A baby's cry? No. Too big for a baby. A child, then. Tamsin set down the pen with exaggerated care, because hands that shake spill ink, and ink spilled on Plate Fourteen would be one more thing this terrible autumn had taken, and crossed the workshop floor past the drying racks, past the great flat case where the atlas slept under its dust sheet like a patient too weak to be moved, and unbarred the door.

The cold hit first. December in Falmouth does not knock politely either; it comes in behind you and starts going through your pockets. Tamsin stood in the doorway with a candle stub cupped against the draft and looked out into a night the color of wet slate, fog rolling up from the water in slow grey tongues, and saw at first nothing but the empty street and the lamplight of the Anchor & Hope guttering across the way.

Then the fog shifted, the way fog does, like a curtain drawn by someone who wants you to see exactly one thing, and there on the step sat a child.

Asleep. Or pretending to be asleep, which amounts to much the same thing in terms of inconvenience. Perhaps seven years old, wrapped in a coat far too large — a man's coat, salt-stiffened at the hem, buttoned to the chin — with a hood pulled low over the face and small boots planted together on the stone as though the child had been instructed to sit still and had obeyed with the whole force of a small and serious soul. Beside one boot lay a folded paper, weighted down with a pebble against the wind.

Tamsin looked left. Looked right. The lane was empty in both directions, if you did not count the cat belonging to Mrs. Trevanion, who sat on her wall regarding the scene with the weary expression of a creature who has seen everything twice.

"Hello," Tamsin said.

The child did not stir. The fog rolled. Somewhere down by the water a ship's bell counted the quarter-hour, and its note hung in the air like a held breath.

Tamsin crouched, retrieved the paper, and held the candle close. The handwriting was hurried but educated, slanting hard to starboard as though written standing up, or walking, or running:

*Keep the child safe. Forget my face.*

And then, lower, pressed so lightly the quill had nearly run dry, as if the writer could not stop themselves:

*You will know what she carries.*

*She.* Tamsin read it three times. *Forget my face.* Well, that was easily managed; Tamsin had already managed it, having seen no face at all, only fog and a departing silence. Whoever had knocked had been gone before the door was fully open — gone without footsteps on frozen mud, which meant they'd walked softly, or known the lane's quiet patches, or been made of the same weather as the night.

"Right," said Tamsin aloud, to the cat, to the fog, to God if He was taking minutes. "Right."

The child, offered the entire vocabulary of the English language as a greeting, declined to answer it. So Tamsin did the only sensible thing, which was to stand there in the doorway growing colder while the situation grew no clearer, until the child shivered — a full-body tremor that traveled through the oversized coat like a wave through rigging — and practicality overcame philosophy.

"You'll freeze," Tamsin told the sleeping child sternly, "and then I shall have a dead child on my doorstep, and I have enough trouble with creditors without adding coroners."

No reply. But one small hand crept out of the coat sleeve and closed around the fabric of Tamsin's apron with the blind certainty of a drowning sailor taking hold of a line, and that, more than any argument, settled the matter.

Getting the child inside was a campaign of three engagements: first through the door (the child, half-waking, went stiff as a surveyor's rule and stared up with eyes too dark to read in candlelight); then past the drying racks (negotiated successfully, though the child's gaze tracked every hanging map with an attention that Tamsin noted and filed away); and finally onto the old daybed in the corner by the stove, where the master had napped through his last year, claiming — with a perfectly straight face — that he was resting his eyes *cartographically*.

The child curled up in the master's blanket like a comma finding its clause, and was properly asleep within a minute. Truly asleep, this time. Tamsin watched the small chest rise and fall and felt something in their own chest perform a maneuver for which cartography offers no term.

"Well," said Tamsin to the workshop at large. "This is above my station."

* * *

Above Tamsin's station was, admittedly, not far from anything else. The workshop occupied the ground floor of a narrow house on Arwenack Street, and Tamsin occupied the room above it, and between those two facts stretched the whole of the estate known as Fairweather. Not Fairweather by any legal record — the records said *apprentice*, when they bothered to say anything, and lately they had begun saying *creditor* instead — but Fairweather in every way that mattered, which is to say in ink, in habit, and in grief.

Master Edmund Fairweather had been dead five weeks. Killed — the coroner wrote *found drowned*, two words doing the work of a hundred — after falling from the quay on a night in November when the sea, according to everyone asked, had been flat as a chart table. Tamsin had stopped asking questions after the third person gave the same answer with the same careful eyes. Questions, the master used to say, are like soundings: take them honestly, or don't take them at all.

Since then Tamsin had done the only thing grief permitted: worked. Rose with the light, worked past it. Finished the plates the master had left rough, corrected the ones he'd left wrong, kept the fire lit and the door locked and the ledger balanced just enough that Mr. Penhale the paper merchant continued, grudgingly, to extend credit to a household consisting of one apprentice of uncertain parentage and no visible prospects. *Your master always paid*, Penhale had said last week, in the tone of a man reading an epitaph he didn't quite believe.

And now there was a child in the master's blanket, and a note instructing Tamsin to forget things, which was rich, because forgetting was precisely what Tamsin was constitutionally unable to do. Forgetting required a certain security about one's place in the world. Tamsin's place in the world had never been secure enough to risk misplacing anything in it.

Tamsin banked the stove, trimmed the candle, and then — because there was no help for it, because the note had said *you will know what she carries*, and because a body cannot simply carry nothing — went back to the child and began, gently, to search the enormous coat.

The pockets yielded a marvel of small archaeology: a knot of string; a biscuit, fossilized beyond saving; a wooden top painted with faded stripes; a hairpin of no particular value; and, in the inner breast pocket, against the lining where a man might keep letters he means to reread, a fold of paper.

It was oilcloth-wrapped. That alone made Tamsin's heart change course, because oilcloth wrapping was mapmaker's craft, harbor craft, *master's* craft — the particular triple-fold he used for charts carried aboard small boats, designed to survive a dunking. Tamsin unwrapped it with fingers that had decided to shake after all, and by the stove-light saw what it was, and sat down rather suddenly on the stool, because the legs had opinions of their own.

A survey page. Hand-ruled, hand-sounded, in a hand Tamsin knew better than their own reflection: the master's. Not the fair copy hand he used for clients, but his working hand — quicker, denser, the letters leaning into each other like conspirators. And across the bottom corner, stamped in wax gone dark with age and handling, his seal: the compass rose with the broken needle, the mark he'd used since before Tamsin's time, the mark that appeared on nothing in the official atlas because the Admiralty had rejected it along with everything else he'd submitted in his final years.

The page showed a stretch of coast Tamsin knew intimately, having inked its official twin not ten days ago. Blackrock Reach, and the waters off Gyllyng Point. And the soundings —

Tamsin laid the page flat on the workbench beside the draft of Plate Eleven and put the candle between them, and the difference looked back like two faces in a mirror where one of them is lying.

The official plate — the one bearing the Admiralty's approval stamp, the one destined for the atlas, the one every captain, pilot, and fishing smack from Falmouth to Fowey would trust with their hulls and their lives — showed clean water off Gyllyng Point. Nine fathoms, easing gently to seven, a nice honest shelf of sea.

The master's page showed a shoal. A long, shallow-tongued sandbar lying directly across the approach, four feet under at low water, marked in the master's tight working notation: *shifted eastward since '93 — confirm — DANGEROUS.* And below that, dated in November, barely six weeks ago, a fresh line of soundings confirming it again, each figure measured and initialed, E.F., as if he had known someone would someday need to check his arithmetic.

Someone had wrecked on that bar. Tamsin remembered it — the whole town remembered it — the collier *Providence*, gone to pieces off Gyllyng Point in October with the loss of nine men, and Master Fairweather's name dragged through it afterward, because his published charts, the ones the Admiralty had approved and printed, had shown safe water where there was none. *His error*, the inquiry had said. *His negligence.* His reputation, forty years of it, drowned with them.

But here was his hand saying otherwise. Here was his hand saying the Admiralty knew. Had known, had erased the shoal from the charts — why, Tamsin's mind refused at first to go there, and then went anyway, cold and clear as a winter sounding — because moving the channel markers served the pilots' guild, or served some revenue interest, or served somebody's pocket, and a falsified chart is cheaper than a lawsuit, and dead sailors file no complaints.

The master hadn't fallen from the quay. Tamsin had known it in the marrow for five weeks without permitting the knowledge upstairs into the thinking rooms. Now here it was, delivered by a sleeping child in a borrowed coat, and it could not be sent back down.

Tamsin became aware, gradually, of being watched.

The child sat up in the master's blanket, dark eyes open and fixed — not on Tamsin's face, but on the page spread on the bench. On the seal. One small hand emerged and pointed at the compass rose with the broken needle, and then the hand turned over and pressed itself flat against the child's own chest, over the heart, and stayed there.

"No," Tamsin said — too fast, too loud for the hour. "No, that's — you're mistaken, little one, that's not—"

The child did not argue. The child simply looked at Tamsin with an expression of such patient, terrible certainty that the denial died somewhere around the tonsils. Then the child pointed at the page again, and at the window, and made a small motion — waves, perhaps, or drowning, or merely *go on, look, look properly* — and lay back down and closed their eyes as though the matter were concluded and bedtime was the natural consequence of truth.

Tamsin stood a long while in the middle of the workshop, among the drying maps and the sleeping coasts, holding a piece of paper that weighed nothing and everything.

Outside, the fog thickened. Somewhere in it, footsteps might have passed; Tamsin strained and heard nothing, and understood that this was the point. *Forget my face.* Someone had chosen this door out of every door in Cornwall, had chosen an apprentice with no family, no standing, no protection worth the name — and no reason, either, no reason except the one that couldn't be helped: Tamsin had been taught by the best mapmaker on this coast to take soundings honestly, or not at all.

The candle burned low. The stove ticked. On the daybed the child breathed in the slow rhythm of the genuinely exhausted, one hand still curled near the collar of the great coat as though keeping watch over its pockets even in sleep.

Tamsin looked at the unfinished atlas under its dust sheet — twenty-two plates complete, four remaining, and a fifth now, apparently, that nobody had ordered — and at the child, and at the page with its dangerous honest figures, and heard the master's voice as clearly as if he stood in the room smelling of pipe smoke and turpentine:

*A chart is a promise, Tamsin. Every line on it. Break your lines and you break people.*

"Oh, hang it all," said Tamsin Fairweather to the empty workshop, in the voice of a person who has just lost an argument with themselves and knows it, and fetched the spare blanket from the chest upstairs, and tucked it around a stranger's child with hands that were no longer shaking at all.

It was nearly two o'clock when Tamsin finally blew out the candle. In the last of the light, the true survey page lay on the bench where the official plate ought to have been, its soundings small and black and patient as seeds.

By morning, Tamsin would need to decide what those seeds were going to grow into.

The child, as if sensing the turn of the tide, slept on.
