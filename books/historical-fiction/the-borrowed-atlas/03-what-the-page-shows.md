## 3. What the Page Shows

I did not sleep.

This was not unusual — grief had long ago rearranged my nights so that they belonged to the workshop and the dead man who had filled it — but this night the sleeplessness had a different texture to it. It was not the hollow ache of missing Master Fairweather. It was something sharper. Something with edges.

The child slept in my own narrow bed upstairs, having surrendered to exhaustion somewhere between the third bowl of pottage and my fourth attempt at a comforting sentence that came out sounding like an inventory of blankets. I had carried them up myself, all elbows and cold feet, and they had curled into a shape so small it seemed impossible a whole person could be folded inside it.

And now the page lay on my workbench under the lamp, folded into quarters, wearing its seal like a locked door.

I will confess something here, since no one is listening but the lamp: I did not open it for nearly two hours. I mended a nib. I sharpened three pencils down to stubs. I swept a floor that had been swept that morning. I checked on the child twice, ostensibly to see if they were warm, actually because looking at their sleeping face made the page feel less like a burden and more like a duty, and duties I could manage.

Then I sat down, broke the seal — carefully, along one edge, so it might be pressed flat again if need be — and unfolded the master's final survey.

* * *

I knew his hand the way you know your own reflection. Every cartographer develops habits as distinctive as a walk or a laugh: the particular angle of a coastline's stroke, the way depth numbers march down a slope like men descending stairs, the little hooks he put on the letter *f* that drove every engraver in Plymouth to despair. Edmund Fairweather had drawn coasts for forty years, and his hand was in every plate of the atlas I had spent four months trying to finish without him.

This was his hand. There was no doubt of it, and I resented the doubt anyway, because wishing it were forged would have been easier than what came next.

It was a survey of Whitsand Bay and the approaches thereto, dated the fourteenth of September — nine days before he died. The soundings were dense, meticulous, taken at close intervals in a pattern I recognized from his teaching: *"Never trust water you haven't touched twice, Tamsin. The sea lies once out of habit."*

He had touched this water many times.

I fetched the official Admiralty chart of the same waters from the plan chest — the printed version, the one distributed to every merchant captain and naval officer on the coast — and laid the two side by side beneath the lamp.

For a long moment I saw nothing. The coastlines matched. The headlands sat where headlands sit; the little church at Rame was drawn in its proper place on both. A forgery done well begins with what everyone can verify.

Then I looked at the water.

On the master's survey, a sandbar curved across the bay's mouth in a long sickle, its shoalest point marked at eleven feet. On the printed chart, that bar had been moved half a mile east and deepened to twenty-one feet — deep enough, by happy coincidence, for a laden East Indiaman to cross with ease.

There was more. A rock, marked on his survey at the mouth of the channel with a warning symbol and the notation *"uncovers at low springs,"* appeared on the printed chart as clean open water. A second shoal inside the bay — twelve feet over it, treacherous in any swell — had been erased entirely, as though the sea itself had agreed to forget.

I sat back. My hands, which had been steady all evening, were not steady now.

Because here is the thing about charts, the thing the master taught me before I could hold a pen straight: sailors do not read the sea. They read the chart. The sea is too large, too changeable, too dark to be trusted at a glance, and so a captain coming into an unfamiliar bay steers by the paper in front of him. A chart is not a picture of the water. It is a set of promises.

Someone had broken those promises. Deliberately. In ink.

* * *

I want to say I understood everything at once, in a great thunderclap of revelation. I did not. Understanding came the way dawn comes in December — slowly, grudgingly, in stages, each one colder than the last.

First stage: error. Charts contain mistakes; I had corrected dozens of them in my apprenticeship, small sins of transcription, a number transposed, a symbol dropped by a hurried engraver. Perhaps the engraver's copy had been mislaid. Perhaps the survey sent to London had been an earlier draft, superseded by later work.

Except the master had dated his survey the fourteenth of September, and the printed chart bore a publication date of the first of October. Later. The falsified version came *after*. If there had been a correction, it ran the wrong direction.

Second stage: coincidence. Sand does move. Bars shift with storms; channels silt and open again. Perhaps the bay had simply changed between September and October, and the chart was accurate and the master's survey was the outdated one.

But no storm moves a bar half a mile eastward in a fortnight, and no tide fills a rock until it vanishes. And there was a detail I almost missed, a detail so small and so damning that when I found it I laughed aloud, one short ugly bark that woke nothing but the candle flame.

In the margin of the master's survey, beside the erased shoal, he had written in his cramped hand: *Confirmed 12th Sept., three sets of soundings, J.M. present.* And beneath that, smaller, angrier, the ink dug deep enough to emboss the paper: *The Board has been shown these figures.*

He had told them. He had told the Admiralty exactly what their chart concealed, with witnesses, in writing — and the chart had gone to print unchanged, with the shoal wiped away and the bar shifted east like furniture rearranged to hide a stain.

Third stage, which was not understanding at all but arithmetic: the *Providence*, the merchantman that had gone ashore in Whitsand Bay on the night of the fifth of October with the loss of nineteen souls. She had come into the bay steering by the published chart, trusting the deepened bar and the vanished rock. Her captain had survived and been blamed for poor seamanship. He had hanged himself in January — no. Not yet January. He would hang himself, I realized with a lurch, in a future I suddenly understood the master had been racing to prevent.

And the master, who had known, who had protested, who had been ignored — the master had kept working. Had kept sounding those waters. Had been preparing, by the look of the density of his figures, a full and irrefutable case. Nine days after dating this sheet, he was dead in the lane outside his own door, and the coroner wrote *struck by a cart in the fog* because carts are convenient and fog is forgiving.

I am not a violent person. My hands are for fine lines and steady rules. But sitting in that lamplight, with the two papers side by side — the truth on the left, the lie on the right, both in ink, both official-looking, only one of them honest — I felt a coldness settle into me that was not fear. Fear would have been sensible. This was something else. This was clarity, arriving like frost, killing everything soft.

They had killed him for knowing. They had let nineteen sailors die for convenience, blamed a dead captain for it, and then killed the one man loud enough and careful enough to prove it. And the proof had walked to my door in a child's coat, with an instruction to forget a face.

Forget the face. As if faces were the danger. As if the child were the secret.

The child was not the secret. The child was the messenger.

* * *

Somewhere in the middle of this — I couldn't tell you when — the stair creaked, and I turned to find the child standing in the doorway wrapped in my spare blanket, watching me with the solemn patience of someone who has waited through worse nights than this.

"You should be asleep," I said, uselessly.

They didn't answer. They never answered. They crossed the room — bare feet, I noticed, and resolved to find stockings by morning — and climbed onto the stool beside me, and looked at the two sheets spread under the lamp.

Then they did something I will remember for the rest of my life, however long the rest of it runs. They put one small finger on the master's survey, on the curve of the sickle-shaped bar, and drew it through the air the way the bar ran. Then they moved the finger to the printed chart, to where the bar had been displaced, and shook their head — slow, deliberate, certain. *Wrong. It goes there. That one is wrong.*

"You've seen these waters," I said quietly.

Another shake of the head. Then the child pointed at the survey, at the marginal note — *J.M. present* — and tapped it twice, and looked at me with an expression I had seen once before, on the face of the master himself, whenever a colleague suggested his figures might be mistaken. It was the expression of someone saying: *the numbers do not care what you believe.*

"Who gave you this?" I asked, as gently as I could. "Who brought you to the door?"

The child's jaw tightened. They looked at the page, and then away, at the dark window, and their hand crept across the bench and closed around my sleeve — not pulling, just holding, the way you hold a rail in rough weather.

I let them hold it.

* * *

Here is what resolve looks like in a mapmaker's workshop at three in the morning: it looks like tools being laid out in order.

I took a fresh sheet of the good rag paper, the heavy stock we reserved for fair copies, and I began to make a duplicate of the master's survey — not a tracing, which anyone could dismiss as a copy of a copy, but a true secondary original, every figure re-inked, every notation rendered, the date and the seal impression noted in full. Because a single page can be lost, stolen, burned, or "misplaced" by a coroner's clerk, and I had already lost one master to people who understood the value of losing things.

The original would stay hidden. The copy — well. A copy could travel. A copy could pass through checkpoints, could survive a search, could be smuggled in the binding of an atlas that was, by every appearance, merely an atlas.

And the atlas itself — the master's life's work, the coastal atlas he died protecting, the plates I had spent four months finishing with hands that shook less each week — I looked at it differently now. I had thought I was completing a monument. I saw that I was completing an argument. Every correct plate in that atlas was evidence of how the master worked: honestly, twice-touched, never trusting water he hadn't measured. Set beside the falsified Admiralty chart, the atlas itself became testimony.

"They thought they buried you in the lane," I said to the empty chair where he used to sit, because talking to chairs is a habit I refuse to apologize for. "But you'd already written everything down. You always wrote everything down. You insufferable, wonderful old man."

The chair said nothing, in the manner of chairs, but the lamp flickered in the draught from the window and I chose to take it as agreement.

By the time the sky went from black to the color of dirty pewter, I had finished the fair copy, hidden the original inside the false back of the plan chest drawer where the master had once hidden his brandy from Mrs. Quill — she had found it within a week, but she was Mrs. Quill, and the customs house would not be — and washed my brushes. The child had fallen asleep again on the settle, mid-watch, blanket up around their ears, one hand still faintly charcoal-stained even after my scrubbing.

I stood over them a moment. Forget the face, the note had said. Well. I had looked at the face. I intended to keep looking at it until I understood why someone had wanted it forgotten, and what else the child had seen that they could not or would not say. Children who do not speak have usually been taught silence by someone, and not kindly.

Resolve is a cold thing, I thought, banked downstairs and blowing out the lamp. But cold keeps things. Cold preserves. The sea keeps drowned things perfectly for years, the fishermen say. And I had a feeling that between now and New Year's Eve, whatever warmth this household managed would have to come from stubbornness alone.

I was halfway up the stairs when the knocker sounded.

Three sharp raps, precise as a ruled line, at an hour when honest callers are asleep and dishonest ones don't knock.

The child's eyes opened in the dark.
