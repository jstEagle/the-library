## 4. The Child Who Won't Speak

The child did not cry. That was the first thing Tamsin learned, and it unsettled them more than weeping would have.

Three days had passed since the knock after midnight, three days of the small figure occupying the corner of the workshop like a piece of furniture that had arrived unannounced — a stool, perhaps, or one of the master's old drafting chairs that no one sat in anymore. The child sat where they were put, ate what was offered, slept when sleep came, and said nothing at all. Not a word. Not a whimper when Mrs. Quill's cat hissed at them through the window, not a sound when the fire spat embers across the hearthstone and caught the hem of their coat, which Tamsin beat out with a flat hand while the child watched the flames with an expression of profound, ancient patience, as though fires were old acquaintances.

"Perhaps they're simple," Mrs. Quill had suggested over the fence on the second morning, her ledger tucked under one arm like a psalter. "Some are born that way. Quiet as stones."

"They're not simple," Tamsin had said, before they knew they intended to say it. "Look at how they watch my hands."

And the child *did* watch Tamsin's hands. Watched them the way a starving man watches bread. When Tamsin trimmed the quill, the grey eyes followed the knife. When Tamsin ruled a line across fresh vellum, the child's whole body leaned toward it like a plant toward light.

On the third evening, Tamsin understood why.

* * *

It began with charcoal. There was a stub of it on the floor by the hearth — Tamsin used it for roughing out coastlines before committing them to ink — and while Tamsin's back was turned, mixing washes at the slop bucket, the child crept from their corner, took up the stub, and a sheet of spoiled proof paper from the scrap pile, and began to draw.

Tamsin heard the scratch of it before they saw it. That particular sound — dry carbon whispering over paper — was as familiar to Tamsin as their own heartbeat, and they turned around with the expectation of finding some ruin, some scribbled catastrophe, some crime against good paper committed by small hands.

What they found instead stopped them with the wash brush dripping forgotten over the bucket.

The child had drawn a coastline.

Not a childish coastline — not the wobbly scalloped edge that children give the sea, all bumps like knitting. This was a *survey*. A careful, patient, disciplined line running the width of the paper, with soundings marked off it in neat little figures, depth measurements taken by someone who knew what soundings were, who knew they went in fathoms, who knew the little convention of writing them upright in the shallows and slanted in the deep. And there, at the river mouth, a sandbar rendered in fine stipples — exactly the way the master stippled sandbars, exactly that density, that taper at the edges, that—

Tamsin set down the brush very slowly.

"Where did you learn that?" Their voice came out strange. Rough.

The child looked up. Did not answer. Of course not. But there was something in the look — not defiance, not blankness. Something closer to an apology, or a plea: *I didn't mean to be caught.*

Tamsin crouched down, joints cracking, until they were at the child's level. Up close there was a smudge of charcoal on the small chin, and another on one cheekbone, and the effect was so absurdly like the master — who had gone about for forty years with ink on his face without once noticing, so that respectable clients had shaken hands with a man wearing half of Falmouth Harbour on his nose — that Tamsin felt something crack open behind the breastbone and had to swallow hard against it.

"That's Gull Rock," Tamsin said quietly, tapping the drawing. "Off the eastern shore. You've drawn Gull Rock."

The child's eyes flicked to the shape. Then back to Tamsin's face. And then — deliberately, unmistakably — the child nodded.

* * *

They drew all evening. Tamsin gave them paper — good paper, which went against every frugal instinct bred into an apprentice who had never owned anything that wasn't secondhand or broken, but which felt suddenly like the least important consideration in the world — and the child filled sheet after sheet, working with a concentration that made the fire seem loud.

The drawings came in a sequence. Tamsin understood that too; sequence was the grammar of maps, the order of things along a coast, and this child thought in that grammar as naturally as other children thought in questions.

First, more coastlines. Headlands Tamsin knew the way they knew their own knuckles. Then a harbour, its mole drawn in section, the anchorage dotted with vessels at moorings. Then —

Tamsin's breath went shallow.

A boat. Burning.

The child had drawn it in heavy strokes, pressing so hard the charcoal snapped twice and had to be retrieved from the floor. Flames rose off the deck in jagged black tongues, and beneath the waterline, rendered in those same confident stippled strokes, lay a reef. A reef that appeared on no chart Tamsin had ever pulled from the master's flat files. Figures stood in the water around the burning boat — small strokes, heads only, some above the surface, some below it, indicated only by a fainter mark, a ghost-stroke, the charcoal barely touching the paper, as though the child could not bear to press down on them.

There were eleven figures above the water. Tamsin counted twice. Below, where the fainter marks were, there were more. Many more.

The child's hand hovered over these. Then the child took Tamsin's hand — the first time they had initiated any touch at all — and pressed Tamsin's forefinger down onto the page, onto the water beside the wreck, and held it there.

"I know," Tamsin said. Their voice cracked in the middle like bad vellum. "I know. I'm looking."

The last drawing of the night was different. The lines went soft. The woman's face emerged out of the charcoal gradually, the way a coastline emerges out of fog — first the suggestion of features, then the certainty of them. Dark hair drawn in long confident sweeps. Eyes that the child worked on longest, shading and re-shading, going back over them again and again until the paper shone with pressure and threatened to wear through, eyes that seemed to look out of the page with an expression Tamsin could only call *waiting*.

When the face was finished the child sat back on their heels and looked at it for a long time. Then they looked up at Tamsin, and their chin trembled — the first break in the terrible composure, the first crack in the stone — and a single tear cut a clean white channel down through the charcoal on their cheek.

Tamsin did not think. Thinking would have come later, thinking was for afterward. What happened was simpler than thought: Tamsin gathered the child up — seven years old and light as a bundle of dry reeds — and held them while the silence broke at last into sobs, great silent heaving sobs that shook the small body without ever once making a sound, as though even grief had been taught to keep quiet.

"It's all right," Tamsin said into the dark hair, and found they meant it, and found too that saying it loosened something in their own chest that had been locked since the night their master died. "Whoever she is — whoever you are — it's all right. You're here now. You're here."

* * *

Later, much later, with the child finally asleep on the cot by the fire, Tamsin sat alone with the drawings spread across the big table under three lamps, and made themselves look properly. Made themselves do the work. Grief wanted them to see only the burning boat and the waiting face; the cartographer in them insisted on seeing everything else.

And everything else was the trouble.

Tamsin took up the master's old field notebook — the one from the summer surveys, four years gone now, the summer before the wreck, the summer before the accusations, the summer before the end of everything. The handwriting inside had always been distinctive: the letters small and upright, the numerals with that peculiar flat-topped 4, the 7 crossed with a long deliberate stroke like a man signing his own work.

Tamsin laid the notebook open beside the child's drawings.

The soundings matched. Not approximately — *matched*. The same figures, the same flat-topped fours, the same crossed sevens. On the harbour sketch, the annotation beside the mole read *12 ft at low w.*, and in the master's notebook, in the master's hand, the same words appeared in the same cramped upright style with the same abbreviation, the same everything.

A seven-year-old had not written these. A seven-year-old had *copied* them — copied them faithfully, lovingly, the way children copy the people they watch, the way apprentices copy their masters. Tamsin knew that copying from the inside; their own hand still carried the master's cadences underneath their own, two scripts braided together like rope.

Which meant the child had learned this hand from someone who wrote it. Lived with someone who wrote it. Sat, day after day, close enough to watch someone draw coastlines in Edmund Fairweather's exact hand, with Fairweather's exact conventions, Fairweather's own peculiar soul visible in every crossed seven.

But Edmund Fairweather was dead. Tamsin had found him. Had closed his eyes. Had spent four months since trying not to see the memory every time they closed their own.

So whose hand had the child been watching? And why did a child who could render a survey like a journeyman go entirely silent, as if speech itself were something that had been taken away along with the burning boat?

Tamsin looked at the sleeping child — the charcoal-smudged fist curled under their cheek, the woman's face lying on the table above them, her charcoal eyes still waiting — and felt the dread arrive cold and certain, settling over the workshop like fog off the harbor.

Outside, boots rang on the cobbles of the lane, unhurried, official, and paused — just briefly — outside the door.
