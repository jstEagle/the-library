## 5. Mrs. Quill's Ledger

Mrs. Prudence Quill kept three ledgers, and only one of them was for money.

The first — the one the tradesmen saw, when they came to her door with their bills and their bows and their hopeful faces — recorded rents received, rents owed, and the small honest profits of a woman who owned four houses on Cutwater Lane and let rooms in three of them. It was a tidy book. She kept it tidy because untidy books invited questions, and Mrs. Quill had built a life, brick by careful brick, upon the principle that no one should ever have cause to look at her twice.

The second ledger was private. In it she entered what every soul on the lane owed one another: who had wept at whose window, who had drunk past their means at the Anchor and Weigh, whose boy had been seen selling what he ought not to have been selling behind the cooper's yard. Debts of gratitude. Debts of shame. She did not collect these debts in coin. She collected them the way a miser counts his hoard — nightly, by candlelight, with a certain deep and unfashionable contentment. Knowledge, Mrs. Quill liked to say to herself (she said most things to herself; conversation, in her experience, was mostly an opportunity for other people to be disappointing), knowledge is the only rent that never comes due.

And then there was the third ledger. The third ledger was not written down at all.

It lived entirely behind her eyes, and into it, on the fourth morning of December, Mrs. Quill entered a new line:

*One child. Approximately seven years. Appeared at Fairweather's door after dark. Has not been seen to leave.*

She made this entry while watching through the gap in her shutters, a gap worn smooth by forty years of watching, as the child stood in the mapmaker's yard in a coat too big for it, looking up at the workshop windows the way sailors look up at weather.

*Has not been seen to leave,* she wrote again, mentally, underlining it.

Mrs. Quill had known Elias Fairweather for nineteen years and had liked him for perhaps eleven of them. He was a proud man, which she could forgive, and a secretive one, which she could respect, and he paid his rent a day late every quarter without fail, which she found almost endearing in its consistency — a man ought to have *some* flaw you could set your watch by. His death had grieved her more than she admitted, which is to say she admitted nothing and grieved considerably. She had watched Tamsin Fairweather — apprentice, ward, whatever the legal papers did or didn't say, and Mrs. Quill knew precisely what they didn't say, because she had read them over the notary's shoulder with the effortless skill of a woman who had once lifted a watch from a customs officer without disturbing his snuff — she had watched that young person close the shutters, draw the curtains, and carry on like a clock whose mainspring has snapped but keeps ticking out of sheer stubbornness.

Creditors circled the workshop like gulls after a fish cart. Mrs. Quill knew all their names, their routes, and the exact hour each of them pretended to pass by. And through it all, Tamsin worked. Mrs. Quill could see the lamp burning past midnight, could see the shadow bent over the great drafting table, and she thought: *there is a person trying to finish something before the world finishes them.* She respected that. She had been doing it herself for decades.

But children were a different entry altogether.

* * *

On the fifth morning, the child appeared at the pump.

This was significant. Children on Cutwater Lane went to the pump in pairs or not at all, and they chattered like sparrows, and they were sent by mothers who wanted five minutes' peace. This child went alone, said nothing, filled a bucket with the grave deliberation of a much older person, and spilled a third of it on the way back across the cobbles because its arms were short and its attention was elsewhere.

Mrs. Quill happened to be sweeping her step. She swept her step most mornings whether it needed it or not, as it gave her a legitimate reason to stand outdoors and take inventory of the lane. She noted the bucket. She noted the coat — good wool once, a man's coat cut down badly, the kind of alteration done quickly by someone with other things on their mind. She noted that the child glanced at her exactly once, with a look she recognized from her own childhood mirror: the look of someone deciding whether you are a danger, and provisionally concluding yes.

"Good morning," said Mrs. Quill pleasantly.

The child looked at the ground and walked faster, slopping water.

"Not a talker," Mrs. Quill murmured, entering it into the third ledger. *Silent. Watchful. Afraid of being spoken to, not of me specifically — of being asked.* She leaned on her broom. That distinction interested her very much. A child afraid of strangers was common as rain. A child afraid of *questions* was a story.

By afternoon she had assembled the rest. The baker's boy reported (for the price of a stale bun and the pleasure of feeling useful) that Tamsin Fairweather had bought bread for two since Sunday, and milk, and — this was the detail that made Mrs. Quill stop and tap her chin — a small pot of honey. Honey. Tamsin Fairweather drank tea so plain it might as well have been hot water and had once, famously, refused a sweetmeat at the Anchor and Weigh on grounds of principle nobody could identify. Honey was not a purchase. Honey was a *surrender*.

Someone in that house was being fed like something precious.

Mrs. Quill sat down at her kitchen table with the first ledger, purely for the comfort of its tidiness, and considered.

She was not a busybody. She wished to be clear about that with herself, because self-deception was the one debt she never allowed on her own books. Busybodies collected gossip for the pleasure of spending it. Mrs. Quill collected facts, and facts were not for spending; facts were for *holding*, the way a bank holds gold — not because gold does anything but sit there, but because everyone else sleeps better knowing someone sober is minding it.

The trouble was, this fact was dangerous. Dangerous facts attracted exactly the sort of people who knocked on doors with polite questions, and polite questions, in Mrs. Quill's long experience, were how trouble always began. Trouble never announced itself with shouting. Trouble wore a good coat, apologized for intruding, and asked after one's health.

Elias Fairweather had died in September. Everyone said fever. Mrs. Quill had seen the body carried out and had seen, beneath the sheet's edge, a man far too well-fed for fever, and she had seen the bruises on the undertaker's face afterward when he'd mentioned, over a consolatory glass, that the master had marks on him no fever ever left. The undertaker had been paid to say fever. Mrs. Quill knew what paid words looked like; she kept several on retainer herself.

So: a dead mapmaker with lies on his death certificate, a grieving apprentice buying honey for a silent child that arrived after dark, and now — as of yesterday evening — a gentleman in a fine blue coat asking at the Anchor and Weigh whether the Fairweather household had "taken in anyone lately."

The gentleman had bought two drinks, tipped well, complimented the landlady's port, and left. Mrs. Quill heard about it within the hour, because the barmaid owed her. Everything on the lane owed her something, which was not power exactly, but was adjacent to it, the way a cat sleeping on a letter is adjacent to owning the house.

* * *

She crossed the lane on the sixth morning carrying a basket.

The basket contained half a pound of butter, a jar of her own damson preserve, and a small loaf still warm enough to fog the cloth around it. None of this was charity. Charity implied the giving expected nothing back, and Mrs. Quill had never in her life given anything that expected nothing back; rather, this was an *investment*, and she wanted the recipient to understand it as such from the first moment, because she had a horror of unclear terms.

Tamsin opened the door looking like a candle burnt at both ends — ink to the wrists, shadows under the eyes, that particular guarded flatness young people develop when they've learned the world asks questions before it offers help.

"Mrs. Quill," Tamsin said. "I— thank you, but I can't—"

"You can and you will, and we shall both pretend it means nothing, which is the traditional way." She stepped inside uninvited, on the principle that hesitation reads as guilt. The workshop smelled of ink and tallow and something else — porridge, faintly, and beneath it the sour-sweet ghost of illness or fear. Her eye went where she meant it to go: a small tin cup on the shelf, child-sized. A stool drawn up beside the drafting table with a cushion on it. A cushion. On a workman's stool.

"Tamsin Fairweather," she said, setting down the basket with a click that sounded final as a closing ledger, "I am sixty-one years old, I have buried a husband, outlived three doctors who predicted otherwise, and I have watched this lane through that shutter since before you were born. Do not insult us both by lying to me. There is a child in this house."

Tamsin went very still. Stillness, Mrs. Quill noted approvingly, of the sort that precedes either honesty or flight.

"It's not—" Tamsin began.

"If you say 'not what it looks like,' I shall leave, and I shall leave the butter, because even liars need feeding, but my respect will stay on the doorstep." She folded her hands. "Here is what I know, and do correct me if I'm wrong, though we both know I shan't be. A child arrived at night. It does not speak. You feed it honey, which I am assured costs fourpence more than your pride can easily spare. And yesterday a gentleman was asking after your household at the Anchor and Weigh — a gentleman with soft hands, good boots, and the manner of a man paid to be pleasant. Shall I continue?"

"How do you—"

"I keep accounts," Mrs. Quill said simply. "Everyone on this lane owes me something. Most of them don't know it yet. It is not spying. It is *bookkeeping*."

Something shifted in Tamsin's face — not trust, nothing so generous, but recognition. The look of one solitary creature identifying another across a crowded field. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I am going to offer you silence," said Mrs. Quill, "and you should know its price before you decide whether you can afford it."

"The child stays hidden. No one hears anything from me about it. Not the baker's boy, not the barmaid, not the gentlemen with soft hands. My price—"

"Name it," Tamsin said, and there was flint there now, and Mrs. Quill felt something dangerously like approval.

"My price is that you tell me nothing whatsoever."

A pause. "...What?"

"You heard me." She drew herself up, and for a moment the pleasant landlady fell away and something older and harder stood in her place, a woman who had survived on wit and arithmetic while prettier women survived on husbands. "You think I want your secrets? Child, I want the *opposite*. I want plausible ignorance. If men come to my door asking questions — and they will come, because dead mapmakers and missing children make ripples, and ripples reach shore — I intend to answer them truthfully. 'I keep to myself. I see a light burning late. The apprentice keeps odd hours. Grief makes people strange.' Every word true, and none of it damning, and I cannot hang for what I genuinely don't know." Her eyes narrowed. "So don't tell me why the child is here. Don't tell me whose it is. Don't tell me what Elias was working on, or why he died, or what you're finishing in there at midnight. Because the day I know it is the day I can be made to give it up, and I have no intention of being made to do anything."

Tamsin stared at her. Then — the ghost of a laugh, tired and real. "That's the strangest bargain I've ever been offered."

"It's the only honest kind. Ignorance is the one thing nobody can torture out of you, because it isn't *in* you." She picked up her empty basket. At the door she paused, and her voice dropped, losing its briskness for something quieter and, though she'd have denied it under oath, kinder. "The child drew something on your doorstep stones, I expect. In charcoal or chalk. They do that, the ones who can't speak. They write on the world instead."

Tamsin went white. "How could you possibly—"

"I couldn't. I guessed. You've just confirmed it, which teaches us both a lesson about talking to old women." She permitted herself one thin smile. "I had a brother who didn't speak. Fever took his hearing at four. He drew ships, constantly, hundreds of them, all sailing away. My mother wept over every one. So." She adjusted her shawl. "Feed it properly. Warm the room at night. And if anyone comes asking — anyone at all — send them across the lane to me, and I will bore them so thoroughly about drainage rates that they'll beg for the customs house."

She stepped out into the cold, then turned, one hand on the doorframe, and delivered the rest lightly, as if it were an afterthought about guttering:

"Oh — and the gentleman from the Anchor and Weigh? He wasn't alone. There's another one, plainer, who watches the lane from the corner by the cooper's yard. Evenings, mostly. He thinks the fishmonger's awning hides him." A sniff. "It doesn't. Nothing hides from this side of the street, Mr. Fairweather. Nothing ever has."

She crossed the lane without hurrying, basket swinging, spine straight as a ruled line, and behind her she heard the door close — softly, carefully, the click of someone newly responsible for more than themselves.

Inside her own kitchen, Mrs. Quill opened the first ledger, took up her pen, and made a small mark against the page. Then she closed it and sat a while in the quiet, thinking about her brother's ships, and about how some debts you enter in books and some you carry, unrecorded, and the unrecorded ones are heavier.

Across the lane, the light in the workshop burned on. Beside it now, she noticed, a second, smaller shadow moved.

And at the corner by the cooper's yard, just as the lamplighter came round, the plain man stepped out of the fishmonger's awning and began — slowly, unhurriedly, a man with nowhere to be and all evening to be nowhere — to walk toward her door.
