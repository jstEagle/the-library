## 7. The Man from the Admiralty

Lieutenant Edmund Harrow had learned early in his career that the most dangerous men in England were not the ones who raised their voices but the ones who lowered them. He had been raised by one — his father, a vicar of the old punishing school, who could dismantle a sinner with a smile and a question about the state of the man's soul. Harrow had inherited the technique without the faith, which made him, in his own private accounting, either a better instrument or a worse man. He had never quite settled the matter.

He arrived at the mapmaker's workshop at half past ten in the morning, which was late enough to seem unhurried and early enough to catch a household before it had composed itself for the day. His coat was brushed, his boots were polished to a shine that could shame a looking-glass, and his hat was held at precisely the angle of a man paying a friendly call. Under his arm he carried a wrapped parcel that contained, as far as anyone in the lane needed to know, a set of Admiralty charts he was returning out of common courtesy.

In fact it contained nothing of consequence. The parcel was a prop. Props, Harrow had found, put people at their ease, and ease was where truth lived.

The workshop sat wedged between a chandler's and a shop that sold secondhand shoes, its sign — *Fairweather & Co., Hydrographers* — faded to the colour of weak tea. The *Co.* had always amused him. As far as the Admiralty's records showed, there had never been a co. There had been Josiah Fairweather, master cartographer, lately deceased under circumstances the coroner had recorded as *a fall upon the stair*; and there had been an apprentice of no surname worth entering into any ledger. And now, if the reports were accurate, there was a child.

Harrow knocked twice, gently, the way a neighbour might.

The door opened after a long enough delay to suggest someone had been deciding whether to answer it at all. The apprentice stood in the gap — Tamsin, the file said, though no one seemed able to say whether that was a Christian name or merely what everyone had agreed to call the creature — with ink to the wrists and the particular expression of a person who has been interrupted mid-thought by something they have been dreading all week.

"Mr. Fairweather's apprentice," Harrow said warmly. "Tamsin, is it? Lieutenant Harrow, of the Admiralty." He offered the parcel. "I've brought back some plates your master lent the office last spring. Terribly remiss of us. Mr. Fairweather will want them, I'm sure."

A small silence followed, of exactly the length Harrow expected. Grief did that — it delayed people. They had to climb over the fact of the death before they could reach the sentence waiting behind it.

"He can't," Tamsin said at last. "Want them. He died in November."

"So he did." Harrow let regret settle over his face like a well-fitted coat. "Forgive me. Habit. One keeps addressing the absent — my mother does it constantly to my father, who has been dead eleven years, and I confess I've begun doing it myself. May I come in? It's bitter standing on your step, and I promise I shan't stay above the time it takes to hand these over."

It was a lie, and both of them knew it was a lie, and both of them also knew that refusing would look like something worth hiding. Tamsin stepped aside.

* * *

The workshop smelled of ink and tallow and cold tea, that particular smell of a place where someone worked too many hours and slept too few. Harrow catalogued it automatically, the way another man might catalogue rigging: two worktables, one cleared and one not; a press in the corner; a rack of finished plates wrapped in linen; a cot behind a curtain that had not been slept in recently, or had been slept in badly. A fire burned low in the grate, more habit than warmth.

And on a stool by the window, feet not reaching the floor, sat a child of perhaps seven, bent over a piece of scrap paper with a stub of charcoal, drawing with the fierce concentration of the very young or the very guilty.

"Good morning," Harrow said to the child.

The child looked up, assessed him with an unnervingly level gaze, and went back to drawing. No greeting. No fear. Children usually performed one or the other for strangers; this one appeared to consider him beneath the expenditure of either.

"Found on the doorstep a fortnight ago," Tamsin said, in the tone of someone getting an unpleasant confession over with. "No note worth reading. I've been minding the little one until — arrangements can be made."

"Admirable of you." Harrow set the parcel down on the clear table and did not open it. "The parish will want to know about the child, of course. They do get tiresome about orphans. Forms in triplicate, and a beadle with a nose like a customs dog." He smiled. "I shan't mention it. I remember what it is to be young and not wanting the parish's attention."

That earned him a flicker of wariness, quickly banked, and Harrow noted it with professional satisfaction. He had not mentioned that the child's existence had reached the Admiralty at all. Now he didn't need to. People told you everything by what they rushed to deny.

He turned, ostensibly admiring the room, and let his eyes do the polite work of ransacking it. The atlas lay open on the uncleared table — the great coastal survey, the one Fairweather had spent nine years on and the Admiralty had spent three politely declining to publish. Harrow had read the correspondence himself. *Plate VII unsatisfactory. Depths inconsistent with existing records. Recommend the author re-examine his soundings.* Re-examine his soundings. As if the sea itself had been mistaken, and not the men who had redrawn it to suit the harbourmasters' fees.

"You're finishing it," Harrow said. "The atlas."

"Somebody has to."

"His sister, I understand, has claims on the estate." This was true; Widow Fairweather had written twice, each letter more ornately aggrieved than the last. "She'll be pleased it's in such steady hands."

"I wouldn't presume to say what pleases her."

"No," Harrow agreed mildly. "Few people do, and fewer are right."

He moved along the wall of plates, trailing one gloved finger an inch above them — touching nothing, which frightened people far more than touching. And then, because patience was a tool and he had carried it sharpened all morning, he turned back toward the window and let his gaze fall, quite naturally, quite accidentally, on the child.

Specifically, on the child's sleeve.

The right cuff was stained black to the elbow. Not charcoal — charcoal dusted grey and smudged. This was iron-gall ink, worked deep into the weave, the kind of staining that came only from hours at a drafting table, dipping a pen again and again, wiping a nib on cloth because paper was dear. A child of seven did not spend hours at a drafting table. A child of seven drew with the stub of charcoal that lay, even now, on the windowsill beside the scrap paper.

Someone in this room had been putting a pen in those small hands. Or someone small had been practising, alone, in secret, with an alphabet of lines that belonged to a dead man's trade.

Harrow kept his face pleasant. Inside, something shifted — not triumph, exactly. Something colder and less comfortable. He thought: *the child draws coastlines.* The report from the harbour watchman had said so, dismissively, among other irrelevancies: the foundling sits all day making pictures of water. Harrow had filed it as sentiment. He was beginning to suspect it was evidence.

"And how does the little one occupy the days?" he asked lightly. "Children need employment, or they turn feral. My brother's youngest once dismantled a clock to see where Tuesday lived."

The child glanced up again. For a moment — a long moment, longer than a child's attention should hold — those eyes measured him the way a surveyor measures a coastline: taking bearings, marking hazards. Then the small mouth opened, and closed, and produced nothing at all. Not refusal. Nothing. The silence of a person who has decided, with great care, that words are a door best left shut.

"Dumb since coming here, or shy?" Harrow asked Tamsin, still pleasantly.

"Neither, I hope. Just quiet." Tamsin crossed the room and stood beside the stool, and the hand that came to rest on the child's shoulder was, Harrow observed, entirely unforced. That was interesting. Sentiment, or strategy? With apprentices one could rarely tell, and with this apprentice one suspected both. "Little ones grieve strangely. Whoever the family was, they're lost now. Sea took the mother, they say."

"Ah." Harrow nodded slowly. "Then it's the sea that's orphaned this one, and the sea that should answer for it. Though the sea," he added, smiling, "is notoriously bad about answering questions. One ends up asking other parties instead."

He let that sit in the room like a dropped anchor.

* * *

He stayed another quarter of an hour, because leaving quickly would have confirmed everything. He admired the press. He asked after the trade, whether copperplate or stone, and listened to Tamsin explain engraving techniques with the guarded fluency of someone reciting facts as a wall. He remarked that the light in the workshop was good for fine work, and watched Tamsin's eyes flick, just once, toward the drawer beneath the main table — the flat one, the wide one, the kind built for keeping large sheets of paper. Then the eyes came back, and the conversation continued, and Harrow laughed at something mild and meant nothing by it, outwardly.

Inside, he was writing the report he would not send.

That was the trouble with him, and he knew it, and had known it since Portsmouth in '96, when he'd been ordered to certify soundings he had personally taken and personally known to be wrong. He had certified them. The chart went out; a collier went onto the Dogger shoal in February fog; eleven men drowned, and the Board expressed regret, and Harrow received a commendation for the efficiency of his survey. He had kept the commendation in a drawer for two years and finally burned it, not out of remorse — remorse was a luxury for men with clean hands — but because looking at it made him sick at breakfast, and a lieutenant could not afford to be sick at breakfast.

He served the Admiralty. He believed in the Navy, in the fleet, in the whole vast creaking apparatus that kept Frenchmen off the Channel and grain moving up the coast. He believed in it the way one believes in weather: it existed, it was immense, and one's opinion of it altered nothing. But somewhere between the collier and today, a question had taken root in him, low and persistent as damp in a hull: *an institution that falsifies its own charts is not protecting England. It is merely drowning Englishmen more conveniently.*

And now Fairweather. Fairweather, who had measured the sandbars honestly, who had submitted plate after corrected plate, who had been refused and humiliated and quietly destroyed — reputation first, then credit, then, in November, a staircase and a broken neck and a coroner with a ready pen. Harrow had read the file. The fall was very neat. Falls upon stairs were always very neat when important people wanted them so.

His orders, when he had requested them, had come back in the Under-Secretary's own hand, which was itself an answer: *Ascertain whether any papers of the late Mr. Fairweather remain unpublished. Recover anything of a hydrographic nature. Report directly.* No mention of the child anywhere. Institutions did not mention children. Children were not evidence until they became inconvenient, at which point they became very much evidence indeed.

"More tea?" Tamsin asked, and Harrow realized he had been silent for the better part of a minute, staring at the fire.

"Thank you, no. I've imposed shamefully." He rose, retrieved his hat, and paused at the door with the expression of a man remembering a kindness. "You'll forgive an old sailor's fussiness, but — do lock up carefully at night. There are thieves in this town with a taste for maps. Copper's dear, and a finished atlas plate fetches a shocking price from certain collectors." He tipped the hat to the child, who did not tip back. "Good day to you both. And Tamsin — when the sister comes, as sisters do, you might count the plates beforehand. Families are wonderful things, but inventories are better."

The door closed behind him with a click as soft as a trap.

* * *

He walked down the lane slowly, past Mrs. Quill's house, whose curtains twitched with the punctuality of a ship's bell, and out toward the harbour, turning it all over.

The ink stains. The drawer. The child who looked at a lieutenant of the Admiralty as though measuring his depth and finding it shallow. And underneath it all, the thing the reports kept circling without naming: why would a stranger leave a child at a dead cartographer's door unless the child was connected to the cartographer? And why leave a child anywhere unless the child was carrying something worth protecting?

He stopped at the harbour rail and watched the grey water worry the pilings.

He ought to write his report tonight. *Papers dispersed; nothing of significance recovered; foundling of no relevance.* Two lines, and the matter would drift, and the child would grow up in some corner of England far from Whitehall's attention, and Edmund Harrow would keep his commission and his breakfast and his neatly falling-apart soul.

Or he could write the truth, and watch what the machine did with it.

He found, examining himself with the same cold thoroughness he applied to others, that he did not yet know which man he was. That frightened him more than either answer.

Behind him, up the lane, a curtain twitched again. Ahead, the tide was turning.

He turned with it, and walked back toward the Admiralty to decide.
