## 8. Apprentice No One

The child slept in the master's old chair again.

Tamsin had given up trying to make the settle by the fire work. The boy — girl? Tamsin still didn't know, and the question seemed suddenly less important than it ought to be — curled into the worn leather of the drafting chair as though the shape of it had been waiting seven years for exactly that small body, and slept the way only children and cats sleep: entirely, without apology, with one sock dangling off one foot as if even in dreams the foot had somewhere better to be.

Tamsin stood at the bench with a graver in hand, working the title cartouche of Plate Eleven, and did not look at the chair. Looking at the chair was dangerous. Looking at the chair meant seeing a small head against leather that had held another head for thirty years, and Tamsin's hands knew what happened to lines when the eyes went wet — they wavered, and wavering lines on a copper plate could not be un-wavered.

*Steady,* said the voice in memory, warm and dry as August paper. *A line is a promise, apprentice. Make it or don't.*

"I'm making it," Tamsin muttered to the empty workshop, which had lately become the habit of an entire conversation conducted with a dead man, and got on with the hatching.

It was while burnishing the third flourish of the F in *Fairweather's New and Correct Atlas of the Southern Coast* that the memory arrived, the way memories do when the hands are busy and the mind has been carefully instructed to think about nothing: not as a wave but as a smell. Coal tar and cold river water.

* * *

Tamsin had been nine years old, and had come up the river on a coal barge because there was nowhere else a barge was going, and had stepped off into the mud at the foot of Rope Walk with two shillings sewn into a coat lining and no name that anyone would swear to.

That last part needs explaining, though nobody at the time had explained it, least of all Tamsin.

There had been a woman before the river — this much survived, in fragments, the way a coastline survives in a bad chart: roughly right, dangerously incomplete. A room with a sloped ceiling. The smell of beeswax. A voice singing something about green grows the laurel, always the same verse, as if the rest had been lost overboard. And hands — quick hands, ink-stained at the edges, the way a person's hands get who writes for a living rather than merely for a living's sake. Tamsin had carried those hands around inside for twenty years like a pressed flower, and had never once shown them to anyone, because what would be the use? You cannot find a woman from her hands. The world does not keep registers of hands.

The two shillings had been sewn into the lining by those same hands, along with a slip of paper that said, in a careful round script, *His name is Tam.* Not Tamsin. Tam. As though whoever wrote it had run out of time or courage halfway through the business of naming a person, and left the rest to be filled in later.

Nobody ever filled it in later.

The parish beadle at Rope Walk took one look at a muddy child with two shillings and no surname and performed the arithmetic of the age without any difficulty at all. Foundling. Charity case. He pronounced the word "Tam" as though tasting something doubtful, added "-sin" to it with the generosity of a man giving away someone else's money, appended "Fairweather" because that was the name of the parish fund that would pay sixpence a week toward the keeping of such children, and wrote the whole construction into his ledger with the satisfaction of a problem solved.

And so Tamsin Fairweather came into existence the way most things come into existence on paper: by administrative convenience.

The workhouse was three years of porridge and silence. It is not necessary to dwell on it; Tamsin never dwelt on it, having concluded early that dwelling was a luxury for people with elsewhere to be. What matters is the fourth winter, when a tall stooped gentleman came through the ward measuring everything with his eyes the way he measured everything, and stopped at the window where a small person was copying, onto a slate, from memory, the coastline visible from the glass.

He had watched for a long time. Long enough that the small person's neck prickled.

"You've drawn the point twice," he said at last. "Once as it is, once as it looks from here. Do you know you've done that?"

The small person, who had not known, shook their head.

"The second one is more useful," said the gentleman, "and less true. Remember that. Most people spend their lives confusing the two." He held out a hand. "I'm Fairweather. Nathaniel Fairweather. I make maps. Come along, apprentice."

Not *child*. Not *boy*. The word *apprentice*, spoken like a door being opened.

"But I haven't got a proper name," the small person had said, because the beadle's ledger had taught them exactly what they were worth on paper.

Mr. Fairweather had looked down, and something in his face had done a complicated thing — anger and tenderness fighting for the same ground, as they would go on doing in that face for fourteen years.

"Well," he said. "Neither have I, properly speaking. My mother called me Nat until the day she died, and the Admiralty calls me Mr. Fairweather, and my sister calls me nothing at all unless she wants something. A name is just the first lie on a chart, child. We'll find yours." And then, because he was a man who believed problems were solved by work: "Mind the step. And mind the cat. She bites apprentices she doesn't respect yet. You'll want to earn that."

The cat's name was Mercator, and she never bit Tamsin at all, which Mr. Fairweather declared unprecedented and slightly insulting.

* * *

Fourteen years. That was how long Tamsin had been *the apprentice*. It sounded, said aloud in the workshop — *"the apprentice will fetch the acid," "the apprentice will take these proofs to the printer," "my apprentice will outdraw me within five years, God help me"* — it sounded like belonging. But paper told the truth paper always tells. There was no indenture filed anywhere. No bond signed. Every year at Michaelmas, Mr. Fairweather would say, "We really ought to make it legal," and every year the law office would cost four guineas, and every year there was a new plate that needed engraving instead, and so the paperwork went undone the way paperwork goes undone in workshops where the work is real and the certificates are not.

"He means to leave it till I die," Tamsin heard him tell Mrs. Quill once, over the fence, in the conspiratorial half-whisper he used when discussing Tamsin within earshot of Tamsin, a habit of tactlessness that Tamsin had long ago decided was actually a kind of love. "Then everyone will discover the atlas belongs to the apprentice and there'll be a fine row, and I shall miss it terribly."

"And what's the child to call themselves meanwhile?" Mrs. Quill had asked. She kept accounts on everyone; she had once informed Tamsin, with the air of a woman reading scripture, that Tamsin bought two candles a week and burned them both past midnight, and that honesty about one's candle consumption was the foundation of character.

"Whatever they like," said Mr. Fairweather. "Names are earned. Ask any ship."

Tamsin had heard this exchange from the workshop window and had gone back to the burin with burning ears, telling themselves it was nothing, that names were nothing, that a person who could draw the Dorset coast from memory needed no more identity than that.

It had been almost true for fourteen years.

* * *

Now the fire ticked, and the child in the dead man's chair turned over, and the memory came ashore in pieces, the way wrecks come ashore.

Because here was the thing Tamsin had been not-thinking about for eleven days, with the discipline of a customs officer ignoring a smuggler's wink:

The note pinned to the child's coat had said *forget the face.*

The slip sewn into Tamsin's own coat, twenty-six years ago, had said *his name is Tam.*

Both written in a hurry. Both by hands that wrote for a living. Both leaving a child on a doorstep of one kind or another with less than they deserved and more than nothing.

And tonight, going through the master's papers for the eleventh time — ostensibly looking for the reference measurements for Plate Eleven, actually performing the same fruitless archaeology performed ten times before — Tamsin had found, tucked inside a folio of tide tables, a letter. Old. Yellowed. Twenty-six years old, if the date was honest.

*My dear Nat,* it began, in a woman's hand — a quick, slanting, confident hand, ink-stained even in the memory of the handwriting, if handwriting could be said to have such a thing —

*My dear Nat, forgive the haste and the disguise. If you are reading this I am gone, and I send you the only cargo worth carrying. His name is Tam. I know what you will say — that I should have come home, that pride is a poor pilot. You were right. But there are men watching this house, and they must not learn whose child he is, nor yours. Burn this. Keep him ignorant and keep him safe, and when he is grown, tell him his mother loved the coast as you do, and drew it too.*

*Burn this,* the letter said.

Mr. Fairweather had not burned it. Mr. Fairweather, who lectured daily on the sanctity of the accurate record, who had ruined his reputation rather than sign charts he knew to be false, had committed one forgery of the heart: he had hidden a letter for twenty-six years and let a child believe themselves no one's.

*Tam.* The name the beadle had found ready-made. The name that had never been administrative convenience at all, but inheritance — handed down the river in a coat lining, arriving at a doorstep, picked up by a tall stooped man who had said *come along, apprentice* and meant *come along, blood of mine, whom I may never claim.*

Tamsin set down the graver very carefully, because the lines were definitely wavering now.

The child was the master's grandchild. Tamsin was — what? The letter said *whose child he is, nor yours.* Whose child *he* was — that was the daughter, the lost daughter, the mother of the sleeping child. But *nor yours* hung in the sentence like a sandbar under dark water. It could mean only that the watchers mustn't connect the child to Fairweather. Or it could mean—

Or it could mean that Tamsin was not the apprentice at all. Had never been the apprentice. Was the other secret the old man kept, the foundling taken in not from charity but from— 

From where?

The fire ticked. Somewhere across the lane, Mrs. Quill's shutters closed with the particular click of a woman concluding her observations for the night. In the chair, the child sighed and flung an arm over the armrest, claiming the territory absolutely, and the gesture was — Tamsin's stomach turned over slowly — the gesture was Mr. Fairweather's exactly, performed nightly over the arms of chairs for thirty years.

"Who am I?" Tamsin asked the workshop quietly. It was not a rhetorical question anymore. It was a survey request, and the coastline in question was one's own self, and every chart of it, Tamsin understood with sudden vertigo, had been drawn by someone else, twice — once as it was, and once as it looked from the outside — and Tamsin had spent a lifetime mistaking the second drawing for the truth.

*A line is a promise, apprentice. Make it or don't.*

Well. Perhaps that was the answer, then. Not the letter. Not the beadle's ledger, not the parish fund, not the woman with the ink-stained hands. Perhaps a name was not found the way a wreck is found — salvaged from wherever it sank. Perhaps it was drawn, line by line, the way a coast is drawn, by walking it yourself and promising every yard of it.

But oh, it would have been nice to be somebody's.

The child woke — children wake at the exact moment they become unbearable to watch, this is a natural law — sat up, blinked at the lamplight, and reached without a word for the charcoal stub and the back of a spoiled proof that lived on the bench now for exactly this purpose. Tamsin waited, heart behaving badly.

The charcoal moved. Stopped. Moved again. The child frowned ferociously, tongue between teeth, an expression Tamsin recognized with a jolt because it appeared in the workshop mirror daily.

Then the child turned the proof around.

Drawn upon it, quick and sure and unmistakable, was the front of the workshop — door, window, the crooked shutter, Mercator's old spot on the sill — and standing in the doorway, rendered in perhaps twelve strokes of charcoal, a tall stooped figure with a beard, holding open the door.

Holding it open. Inviting someone in.

Below it, in a child's fierce scrawl, the child had copied out letters that could only have been copied from somewhere — from the atlas title page, from a plate seen in another life: *FAIRWEATHER.*

The child looked at Tamsin. Tamsin looked at the child. Outside, far off toward the harbor, a watch bell rang the hour, and beneath its ring, faint but quite distinct, came the sound of knuckles on Mrs. Quill's door across the lane — polite, patient, and entirely wrong for midnight.
