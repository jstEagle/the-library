## 9. The Cartographer's Widow

The knock came at half past ten in the morning, which was itself suspicious. Nobody respectable called on a mapmaker's workshop before noon, and nobody disreputable knocked.

Widow Fairweather had been watching the workshop for three days from the window of the Blue Boar, where she had taken a room at considerable expense and considered every shilling of it an investment. She had watched Tamsin Fairweather — and she still thought of the creature as *Tamsin Fairweather*, though she knew perfectly well the name was borrowed, a courtesy her brother had extended like an old coat to a stray — come and go with parcels, seen the child's small face appear at the glass like a moon rising, noted the landlady across the lane whose curtains twitched with a regularity that suggested commerce rather than modesty. She had watched. She had waited. And now she smoothed her grey silk skirts, adjusted her bonnet to the precise angle of bereavement, and knocked.

Grief, she had found over six decades of living, opened more doors than keys did.

* * *

Tamsin opened the door with ink on one hand and a charcoal-sticky child attached to the other leg, and knew at once — the way one knows weather coming off the sea — that this visit would cost something.

"You would be the apprentice." The woman's voice was rich as plum duff and about as nourishing. She was perhaps sixty, handsome in the way of portraits that have been touched up, and she looked past Tamsin entirely to the interior of the workshop as though taking inventory. Which, Tamsin thought later, was exactly what she was doing. "I am Mrs. Fairweather. Edmund's sister."

Edmund. The master's name, spoken by someone who had no doubt used it a thousand times and yet somehow made it sound like a deed of ownership being read aloud.

"Ma'am," Tamsin said, and stood in the doorway, which was a statement in itself.

Mrs. Fairweather's eyes performed a slow circuit: the atlas plates drying on the racks, the master's instruments laid out with the reverence of relics, the child now peering around Tamsin's leg with the frank stare of the very young. Something moved behind those eyes — quick, appraising, gone. "You will invite me in, I hope. I have crossed two counties to see what my brother died leaving behind."

There it was. *My*. Not *our*, not *the late Mr. Fairweather's*. My.

Tamsin stepped aside. It was not hospitality; it was tactics. Better to let the wolf into the kitchen where one could count its teeth.

* * *

Inside, Mrs. Fairweather conducted a tour disguised as mourning. She paused at the drafting table and ran one gloved finger along its edge, lifting it to inspect the dust. She held the master's dividers up to the light as if weighing them for scrap. She lingered longest over the atlas plates themselves, and here Tamsin watched her carefully, because here was where a sister might grieve and something else might calculate.

"Extraordinary," she murmured. "He always was the clever one. Our father wanted him in the wool trade, you know. I stayed home and kept Father's last years comfortable while Edmund chased horizons." A small smile, worn smooth with use. "I do not grudge him. But one does feel that family ought to count for something, in the end."

"In what way, ma'am?" Tamsin asked, though the cold in their stomach already knew.

"Why, in every way." She turned, and her face arranged itself into benevolence so practiced it might have been carved. "This atlas. His life's work. It cannot simply be left to —" a pause, delicate as a knife laid flat — "*circumstances*. He had heirs. I am his heir. The copyright alone, properly managed, could be worth a great deal. Charts commissioned by the Admiralty command respectable fees, and there is talk of a second edition even now. Someone must steward all this. Someone with his blood."

"And the workshop?" Tamsin kept their voice level. "And the debts? Because there are debts, ma'am. The creditors have been circling since October. If you're claiming the estate, you claim those too."

Something flickered — the first honest expression he'd seen on her, and it looked remarkably like a woman tasting something sour. "Debts against assets, surely. Any sensible arrangement —"

"There's barely an asset beyond the plates and the press. The bank holds the lease."

"Well then." She recovered beautifully; Tamsin granted her that. "All the more reason the intellectual property must be secured by family. Ink and paper, dear. That's where Edmund truly lives now."

Ink and paper. As if the master were a sum to be extracted rather than a man to be mourned.

It was then the child detached from Tamsin's leg and walked — deliberately, the way the child did everything, as though each act were a sentence in an argument only they could read — to the rack of plates, and stood looking up at the coastal survey with its unfinished margins, and then turned and looked at Mrs. Fairweather with an expression of such flat, unchildlike assessment that the widow actually took a half-step back.

"The little one doesn't speak?" she asked lightly. Too lightly.

"No, ma'am. Draws, though."

"How convenient." The words were out before she could trim them, and Tamsin saw her hear them, see them hanging in the air, and reach smoothly past them. "Children are such mysteries. Now — the arrangements. I shall want the plates inventoried, of course. A proper legal inventory. I'll engage a solicitor in town; I assume the workshop records are in order?"

"They're in order," Tamsin said. "They're also not yours to inventory until you prove they are. The master never married. No children. But he made a will, ma'am — I witnessed it, along with Mrs. Quill across the lane, and it's lodged with his solicitor in Plymouth. Until it's read, this workshop belongs to his estate, and I belong to it."

"A will." Her fan snapped open. "How very thorough of Edmund. And what did the will say?"

"That will be for the solicitor to say, won't it."

The silence stretched between them, taut as chart linen. Then Mrs. Fairweather smiled again — and Tamsin understood, watching that smile, that he had just told her something valuable without meaning to: that the will existed, that it might say anything, that time mattered. Her eyes did their quick arithmetic.

"Of course. We shall let lawyers be lawyers." She extended one gloved hand, and Tamsin took it; her grip was dry and strong and brief. "You've kept his things beautifully, apprentice. Whatever your name is. Edmund never did make it official, did he? Never claimed you. It seems cruel, really — a whole life under his roof and no paper to show for it."

She said it gently. That was the worst part. She said it the way one slips a blade between ribs — kindly, almost tenderly, with the full knowledge of exactly where it goes.

"No paper," Tamsin agreed.

"But paper can be drawn up," she said pleasantly, gathering her reticule, "and paper can be torn up. Do give my regards to the child. Family should know family, after all — even the borrowed kind."

* * *

From the window of the Blue Boar, Widow Fairweather allowed herself a small, satisfied glass of Madeira.

The boy — girl? it hardly signified — had spine. Good. Spine meant resistance, resistance meant negotiation, negotiation meant terms. And beneath the terms, the true prize sat waiting: not the workshop, not the instruments, which were worth pennies. The atlas. The Admiralty connection. A widow of reduced circumstances with the sole rights to the most talked-about coastal survey of the decade could name her own price to the Board, and the Board, she happened to know through channels she preferred not to examine too closely, was *very* eager indeed to lay hands on Edmund Fairweather's remaining papers. There were men in London who had asked after those papers with a persistence that had less to do with cartography than with burying something.

She was not a wicked woman. She wished that understood, whoever kept the great ledger of such things. She had earned comfort the hard way — years of her father's sickroom, years of a husband who drank the wool profits — and comfort was owed her, and Edmund dead could not begrudge her taking it from what he'd left. Blood was blood. It was the oldest title there was, older than wills, older than apprenticeships, older than whatever sentimental arrangement her brother had made with a foundling he'd never troubled to adopt.

As for the child —

She turned her glass in the lamplight.

The child was a complication. A silent, drawing complication with Edmund's own look about the brow, if she was honest, and she was honest with herself the way a merchant is honest with his ledger: privately, and only when it profited. Where the child had come from, whom it belonged to, why a stranger had left it at that door — none of it was her concern, except insofar as a dependent child strengthened the apprentice's moral claim on sympathy and weakened her own. Sentiment was currency too, and the foundling had cornered the market.

But if the child were *family* — truly family, hers by blood —

Then the claim became unanswerable.

She finished the Madeira and began, in her neat mercantile hand, a letter. She addressed it to London. She wrote the way she did everything: efficiently, without flourish, and without writing down anything she would not care to have read aloud in court.

* * *

Across the lane, behind curtains that twitched with commerce rather than modesty, Mrs. Quill watched the widow's door of the Blue Boar close upon the departing coach, and entered a fresh line in the ledger of her mind: *One vulture, arrived Tuesday, departed Friday, well fed.*

Then she picked up her shawl and went to warn Tamsin that winter had gotten teeth.
