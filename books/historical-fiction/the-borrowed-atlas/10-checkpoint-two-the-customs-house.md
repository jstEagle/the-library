## 10. Checkpoint Two: The Customs House

Mrs. Prudence Quill had kept a ledger of the lane for thirty-one years, and in that time she had learned three truths about her fellow creatures: everyone owed somebody; nobody believed they owed as much as they did; and the most dangerous person on any street was not the one with secrets but the one who kept other people's.

Her own ledger lived behind a false spine in the bookcase, bound in plain calf leather and labeled, for the benefit of casual browsers, *Sermons, Vol. IV*. Nobody had ever opened Volume IV. Nobody ever would. It contained, in Mrs. Quill's small copperplate hand, the debts, vices, weaknesses, and quiet shames of every soul between the harbor wall and the chandler's on Cutpurse Row — which is to say, every soul who mattered.

She did not think of herself as a blackmailer. Blackmail was vulgar, and vulgarity attracted attention. She thought of herself as a banker of a peculiar currency, one that appreciated rather than depreciated, because shame only grew with time, like a good cheese or a bad reputation.

It was this banking instinct, and nothing so soft as affection — she was quite firm on that point, at least in the privacy of her own kitchen — that had drawn her into the affair of Tamsin Fairweather and the silent child.

* * *

She had noticed the extra mouth within a day. That was not skill; that was arithmetic. One pound of bread became two. A half-pint of milk appeared on the step each morning, paid for from a purse that should have been emptying, not filling — someone, evidently, had settled a debt to the Fairweather estate after the master's death, and Mrs. Quill made a note to find out whom, because generosity in a creditor was either guilt or strategy, and both were worth knowing about.

But it was the child itself that told her the real story.

Mrs. Quill watched people the way some women watched weather. The child stood at Tamsin's window each afternoon, charcoal in hand, drawing on scraps of paper, and what the child drew was not the usual nonsense of childhood — no dogs with too many legs, no houses floating in the sky. The child drew coastlines. Precise ones. Headlands with the correct number of notches. And once, when the light was right and Mrs. Quill's window was doing its work as a mirror, she had seen the child draw a boat, and fire above the boat, and then stop, very still, the way children stop when they have drawn something true.

She put down her needlework and thought about it for a long while, the way she thought about all investments.

Then there was Lieutenant Harrow.

He had come up the lane on a Tuesday, all polished buttons and polite menace, asking after papers, and he had smiled at Mrs. Quill in the doorway of her own house as though she were a door he intended to walk through eventually. She had given him nothing but tea and pleasantries, both of which she could produce in unlimited quantity at negligible cost. But she had noted his visit in the ledger, under a new heading she created that evening: *The Admiralty Question.*

And beneath it, after some deliberation: *T.F. has something worth a lieutenant's time. Therefore worth mine.*

* * *

On the first Thursday of December, Tamsin Fairweather knocked on her door looking like a person about to apologize for existing.

"Mrs. Quill," Tamsin said. "I — that is — I've been meaning to—"

"You owe me four shillings for the milk since November," Mrs. Quill said, "and you've been standing on my step working up your courage for the better part of two minutes, during which time my tea has gone lukewarm. Come in before the fog does."

Inside, she poured fresh tea and waited. Patience was another of her currencies. She had learned long ago that if you held your tongue open like a net, most people eventually threw themselves into it.

"I need to move something," Tamsin said at last. "A document. Through the customs house. Officially. With official stamps and official hands touching it, so that afterward no one can say it didn't pass through properly."

"And why," said Mrs. Quill, stirring her tea with the air of a woman discussing the price of candles, "would anyone want a document to pass through the customs house? Documents aren't goods. They don't pay duty."

"It needs provenance." Tamsin's hands were around the cup like it was the last warm thing in England. "If it just appears — if it comes from me, an apprentice, a foundling, a nobody — the Admiralty can call it a forgery and burn it. But if it arrives inside the official dispatch packet, stamped by the customs house, logged in their own books, carried in their own bag — then it has a history. Then it exists the way things exist when the powerful say they do."

Mrs. Quill set down her spoon. Somewhere in the house a clock ticked, and the fog pressed against the windows like a creditor at a door.

"You want," she said slowly, savoring it, "to use their own machinery against them. To make them certify the thing that indicts them."

"I hadn't phrased it that way, but yes."

"Good. I prefer my schemes phrased properly." She folded her hands. "Now. Tell me what it actually is, Tamsin Fairweather, and don't waste my breath lying, because I knew your master longer than you did, and I know what he died of, even if the coroner wrote 'fall' on his certificate. Men don't fall downstairs twice."

Tamsin flinched. Then, in a low voice, with frequent glances at the door as though the walls had been bought by the Admiralty, Tamsin told her. The survey page. The falsified charts. The sandbars moved a mile eastward on the official plates, the shoals erased entirely, the wrecks that followed, the master's ruin, the master's death. The stranger at the door. The sleeping child. *Forget the face.*

Mrs. Quill listened without moving a single muscle of her face, which cost her considerable effort, because inside she was doing sums. Large ones.

When it was done, she said: "And you mean to carry it yourself."

"I have to. If it fails—"

"If you fail, you hang, or drown, or fall downstairs, and the child goes to the parish, and the parish gives children to whoever asks least politely." She rose and went to the bookcase and drew out *Sermons, Vol. IV*, and laid it on the table between them, and enjoyed — she admitted it, she enjoyed it — the way Tamsin went pale. "Before we discuss how this is done, we will discuss what it costs. Not in money. In obligations."

"Mrs. Quill—"

"Sit down, apprentice. You came to a banker. Let me bank."

* * *

Mr. Ebenezer Pellow had been a clerk in the customs house for nineteen years, and in nineteen years he had committed exactly one act of courage, which was marrying a woman his mother disapproved of, and had regretted it weekly ever since. He owed Mrs. Quill eleven pounds, fourteen shillings, and sixpence, incurred over three winters of coal advanced against wages, plus interest calculated at a rate Mrs. Quill described as "neighborly" and her ledger described more accurately.

He also had a weakness that Mrs. Quill had catalogued with particular care: he wished, desperately, pathetically, to be *seen*. Nineteen years of stamping other men's cargo had left him with a hunger for significance that no amount of official sealing wax could satisfy. He wanted to be a man who mattered. He wanted it so badly he could be steered by it, like a cart horse by a wisp of straw.

She called on him at his office on a gray morning when the fog had crawled up from the harbor and turned the customs house into a building adrift at sea, which struck her as fitting.

"Mrs. Quill." He half-rose from his desk, knocking over a pot of sand. "This is — I wasn't expecting — is this about the coal?"

"The coal is a settled matter between friends," she said, which made him go white, because he knew perfectly well it was not settled and they were not friends, and both facts now sat in the room wearing visible clothing. "I've come about a professional matter. I know how seriously you take your position here, Mr. Pellow. A man of your diligence. Nineteen years."

"Nineteen in February," he said faintly.

"A milestone. Which is why it pains me that the senior clerks still treat you as furniture. That young Mr. Cobbett, for instance, who has been here *four* years and already signs the dispatch packets."

Pellow's jaw tightened in a manner most satisfying. "Cobbett signs because his uncle is—"

"Yes," said Mrs. Quill pleasantly. "His uncle is. Whereas you, Mr. Pellow, have only competence, and we both know how far competence carries a man in this building."

She let him sit in the silence for exactly as long as it took him to start sweating, and then she produced, from her reticule, a proposal written out fair in her best copperplate.

The terms were simple. On Friday, the outbound dispatch packet to London would be assembled — routine correspondence, manifests, quarterly reports. Into that packet, among the sealed items, would go one additional sealed folio, bearing the customs house stamp, entered in the log as *Misc. Survey Papers, ref. 44-C.* Mr. Pellow would perform the stamping and the logging himself, with his own hand, and would see it into the mail bag with his own eyes. No questions. No reading. The seal on the inner folio would remain unbroken until it reached its addressee.

In exchange: the debt of eleven pounds, fourteen, and sixpence would be torn up, in his presence. And further, Mrs. Quill would speak — quietly, in the right ear — of the diligent clerk who had managed the December dispatches flawlessly while young Cobbett was, as it happened, indisposed at his uncle's dinner table on the crucial afternoon. A small matter. A mention. A seed of significance, planted in the soil of Mr. Pellow's hunger, where it would grow all by itself.

"And if I refuse?" Pellow asked, in the voice of a man asking whether the tide might consent to come in later.

"Then nothing happens at all," said Mrs. Quill. "That is the beauty of my business, Mr. Pellow. Refusal changes nothing today. It merely leaves everything where it lies. The debt. The coal. The winter." She smiled at him, warmly. "I never threaten, Mr. Pellow. I merely decline to prevent things."

He signed.

* * *

The difficulty — and Mrs. Quill saw it clearly, which was why she charged what she charged — was the seal. The inner folio needed a credible seal, something that looked like it belonged in official correspondence, and neither she nor Tamsin possessed one. Forgeries of seals were hanged-for offenses, and worse, clumsy ones were *noticed*-for offenses, which in Mrs. Quill's experience was considerably more dangerous.

The answer, when it came, arrived in the shape of the child.

Tamsin brought the little one round on Wednesday — partly, Mrs. Quill suspected, because the workshop felt haunted these days, and partly because Tamsin had begun to understand that keeping the child hidden was itself conspicuous. The child sat on Mrs. Quill's kitchen floor with paper and charcoal, drawing coastlines, while the adults bent over the problem of seals, and at some point Mrs. Quill glanced down and stopped talking mid-word.

The child had drawn the customs house. Recognizably. The columns, the pediment, even the crooked drainpipe on the north side that the masons had been promising to fix since the year of the mutiny. And beside it, drawn with the same sure little hand, the customs house *stamp* — the anchor and crown, every line correct, copied from memory off the hundreds of crates and documents the child must have stared at on the journey that had ended at Tamsin's door.

"Tamsin," Mrs. Quill said quietly. "Look at what your foundling can do."

Tamsin looked. Something moved across Tamsin's face — grief and wonder fighting for the same chair. "The master's eye," Tamsin murmured. "It's uncanny. It's almost—"

"Almost useful," said Mrs. Quill briskly, before the moment could become sentiment, which was expensive. "Very well. We shall not forge the stamp. We shall *borrow* the impression of it. The child remembers it; you, apprentice, can draw a facsimile fine enough to pass at a glance, and the wax will do the rest. A seal is read for three seconds by a man who wishes to be eating his lunch. Three seconds is a wide country, and we shall live in it."

They cut a stamp from a piece of boxwood that evening, Tamsin carving with the steady hands of a mapmaker and the child directing with small imperious jabs of a charcoal stick — *no, the crown sits higher, the anchor's arm bends there* — and Mrs. Quill watched them work together at her kitchen table, heads bent close, and felt something in her chest that she refused to identify and firmly escorted back out of the premises.

*Sentiment,* she noted in the ledger that night, under the Admiralty Question. *Watch it. It makes bad arithmetic.*

* * *

Friday came gray and cold, with a wind off the water that smelled of salt and tar and other people's cargoes. Mrs. Quill dressed in her second-best wool — respectable enough to be waved through, forgettable enough to be forgotten — and took the sealed folio up the hill to the customs house in a market basket, beneath a layer of sprats, because there was no inspection so thorough as to make a customs officer reach past fish.

The dispatch room was exactly as warm and exactly as dull as she had predicted. Clerks moved paper like pallbearers. Mr. Pellow stood at his station with the expression of a man performing his own execution and hoping no one would notice it was also his coronation.

The stamping took four minutes. The anchoring and crown pressed into red wax, the entry written in the log in Pellow's careful hand — *Misc. Survey Papers, ref. 44-C, one folio, sealed* — the folio slid into the canvas dispatch bag among forty of its betters, the bag cinched, locked, and carried out by a porter with a face like a closed shutter.

Mrs. Quill watched it go with her hands folded and her heart beating at a pace she found professionally embarrassing.

"Well," she said to Pellow as the door shut. "You are now a man who matters."

He beamed like a lighthouse. It was pitiful. She gave him a nod worth exactly what it cost her — nothing — and swept out past the sprats and the fog, and did not permit herself to exhale until she was three streets downhill.

*Done,* she thought. *Through the gate, into the machine, certified by their own hands. Let the great lords of the Admiralty open their own post and find their own sins staring up at them from their own stamped paper. There is a justice in it that even a sermon couldn't improve.*

*Sermons, Vol. IV*, indeed.

She treated herself to a bun on the way home. A penny. She entered it in the ledger anyway, under *Miscellaneous*, because triumph unrecorded is triumph unaudited.

* * *

It was three days later, on Monday, that the shadow fell across the arithmetic.

She had gone back to the customs house — a woman of her habits returned often to places where she held leverage, the way a gardener returns to a bed he has planted — ostensibly to settle a small matter of duty on a consignment of French ribbon that had, through clerical error, arrived without any French ribbon in it at all. While the desk clerk scratched his head over it, she drifted, all innocence, along the corridor toward the copying room, where junior clerks made fair copies of anything the seniors considered worth duplicating.

The door stood open. The fog-light came through the windows gray and thin.

At the third desk sat a boy of perhaps sixteen, bent low over a sheet of paper, transferring — line for line, with a copyist's blind diligence — the contents of an opened folio. Coastlines. Soundings. A headland with its notches. Figures in a neat hand, and beneath them, faintly, where the original writer had pressed hard: the ghost of a signature Mrs. Quill had seen on thirty years of documents in this lane.

*Nathaniel Fairweather.*

The boy had the original beside him. Stamped in red wax, cracked open carelessly. Anchor and crown. *Ref. 44-C.*

Mrs. Quill did not stop walking. She did not slow. She continued to the end of the corridor, examined a notice about quarantine regulations with the absorption of a woman who could not read, and turned and went back the way she came, past the copying room door, catching in one glance as she passed the topmost corner of the boy's copy — the title block, half-finished:

*Survey of the eastern approaches... [illegible] ...contrary to Chart No. 14, Admiralty...*

And below it, waiting in the boy's next line, the place where a name would go. Where a copy always recorded its provenance. *Copied from original received in dispatch packet, ref. 44-C, per E. Pellow, clerk.*

Someone upstairs had decided the miscellaneous survey papers were worth a look. Someone had broken the seal that was supposed to survive all the way to London. And the contents were being duplicated, in a government office, by a government hand, and would sit in a government drawer — a second copy, outside her control, outside Tamsin's, outside anyone's but theirs.

She walked home through the fog at a pace slightly faster than dignity required.

Triumph, she reflected, was rather like fish. Best consumed the day it was caught, and after that, increasingly likely to poison you. They had gotten the page through — that much stood, the logbook entry, the stamp, the provenance, the machinery turning as designed. Whatever lay in the Admiralty's London postbag would arrive authenticated beyond denial.

But now there existed a second version, loose in the building, in the hand of strangers, describing precisely the crime that certain powerful men had committed — and those men employed the copying room.

*Tamsin must know tonight,* she thought. *And we must decide, quickly, whether that copy in that drawer is our doom or our second weapon. Because mark me, a thing the enemy has copied is a thing the enemy has read — and a man who reads evidence sometimes burns it, and sometimes—*

She stopped at her own door, key in hand, and allowed herself, for one unguarded moment, the smallest of smiles.

*—and sometimes keeps it. Against his masters. For himself. Every clerk dreams of a drawer full of someday.*

The question was whose someday would arrive first.

Inside the house, the clock ticked. Across the lane, lamplight burned in the workshop window where a mapmaker and a silent child bent over their papers, unaware. And somewhere uphill in a warm office, a man she had never met was deciding what to do with a piece of paper that could hang them all — or raise them.

Mrs. Quill went in, lit her lamp, and opened *Sermons, Vol. IV* to a fresh page.

Under *The Admiralty Question*, she wrote: *The copy exists. Unknown hands. Clock running.*

And beneath that, after a moment's thought: *Find out who reads what, and fast. The century ends in nineteen days.*
