## 11. The Copy

The package arrived on a Tuesday morning, which Lieutenant Nathaniel Harrow would later consider the last ordinary day of his life, though at the time it seemed distinguished chiefly by a cold in his left boot.

He was at his desk in the Admiralty annex — not the grand map room where the great men argued over the shape of the world, but the narrow office one floor down and two corridors removed, where the paperwork of empire went to be read by junior officers with good handwriting. The room smelled of tallow, damp wool, and the particular staleness of documents that had been filed by someone who hated filing. Harrow had long ago pinned a small engraving of Portsmouth harbor above his desk, less from sentiment than from a need to look at water while reading about it. A man could go strange otherwise, reading about shoals all day in a room without a window that opened.

The package bore the customs house seal, addressed to him personally, marked *private*. He knew the hand on the wrapper — Mr. Pell, the second clerk, a man whose loyalties were generally understood to be for sale at rates so reasonable they were almost charitable. Harrow had paid him twice before for small kindnesses: a list of outgoing vessels here, a glance at a manifest there. This was larger. This had cost more. He wondered idly what Mrs. Quill had promised, because nothing moved through that customs house without the landlady across the lane knowing about it first, and he had learned to read her fingerprints in other people's transactions the way a geologist reads strata.

He broke the seal.

Inside, folded twice, was a survey page. Hand-drawn. Coastal profile, soundings in fathoms along the margin, tide tables corrected against three separate observations. And in the lower right corner, small and unhurried, a signature he knew very well indeed:

*J. Fairweather.*

Harrow sat quite still for a moment.

Then he did what he always did when something important crossed his desk, which was to make tea. Not because he wanted tea — the annex tea tasted of iron and regret — but because the ritual bought him four minutes in which he was obliged to think about nothing except water temperature, and four minutes was often the difference between a decision and a mistake.

He lit the spirit lamp. He filled the kettle. He watched it with the patience of a man who had once waited eleven days off Ushant for weather that never came.

*So it exists,* he thought. *And now it is on my desk, which means it is either the end of this business or the beginning of a much worse one.*

---

He had been sent to Wexmouth in November with instructions that had sounded, when spoken aloud, perfectly innocent. *Ascertain whether any papers of the late Mr. Fairweather remain in private hands. Recover anything of navigational significance. Be discreet; the family has suffered enough.* The Under-Secretary who gave him these orders had a way of smiling that involved only the lower half of his face, like a puppet operated by someone with a grudge.

Harrow had gone, had asked his questions, had been politely stonewalled by an apprentice mapmaker with ink-stained fingers and eyes that measured you the way a theodolite measures a hillside — without flattery, without fear, and with a strong suggestion that you might be slightly out of true. There had been a child in the workshop, small and silent, watching him from behind a shelf of copper plates. There had been ink stains on the child's sleeve, which Harrow had noted at the time as curious and had thought about, since then, rather more than was comfortable.

He had reported back that no papers of consequence remained. This was, he had believed at the time, mostly true.

Now he unfolded the survey page fully and laid it flat beneath his desk lamp, and understood within ten minutes that he had been lying to himself as well as to the Under-Secretary, and that both lies had just expired.

---

Here is what Harrow knew about the Wexmouth charts, and had known, in the way one knows a thing without letting oneself look directly at it, the way one knows a relative has taken to drink:

In the spring of '97, three colliers had gone aground on the Wexmouth sands in conditions that should have permitted safe passage. Twenty-two men drowned. The official chart, surveyed and approved by the Hydrographic Office, showed clear water where the ships had broken their backs. Captain Josiah Fairweather, the master surveyor whose name was on that chart, had protested — loudly, formally, in writing — that the published plate did not match his field observations, that sandbars had been drawn where none existed in his soundings and, more damningly, that known shoals had been *erased*, smoothed away, as though some clerk had decided the coast was more cooperative than God had made it.

Fairweather had been accused of falsifying his own surveys to conceal errors. The accusation stuck. A man's reputation, Harrow had observed, sinks faster than a collier, and with roughly the same survival rate. Fairweather had spent two years in professional disgrace before someone had put a knife into him in his own workshop, and the general opinion of Wexmouth was that a ruined man attracts ruin, and the matter was closed.

Except.

Harrow took out his own copy of the official Wexmouth plate — he kept one in his desk drawer the way other men kept letters from women they ought not write to — and laid it beside the smuggled survey.

The differences were not subtle. That was the first thing that struck him. He had expected discrepancies, small drifts, the honest fog of imperfect instruments. What he saw instead was forgery of a confident and systematic kind. The inner channel — the deep-water passage every pilot used — appeared on the official plate at a bearing nearly two points east of where Fairweather's survey placed it. Two points. Any ship trusting the printed chart would steer confidently into the outer sands. Three ships had done exactly that.

And the erased shoal — the one marked on the true survey in Fairweather's neat, patient hand, *danger, dries at low water, 9 ft* — simply did not exist on the official plate. It had been removed entirely. Not misplaced. Removed. Someone had taken a coastline that had killed twenty-two men and edited the killing out of it.

Harrow sat back. His tea had gone cold. He drank it anyway, on principle.

*Why,* he asked the lamp, in the manner of a man who has conducted enough investigations to know that the question is rarely *what happened* but *who profited*, *would anyone do that?*

The answer arrived with the unpleasant punctuality of all answers one has been avoiding.

Insurance. Lloyds underwriters wrote policies against the sands; a chart showing safe water where there was none meant premiums collected on voyages that were, statistically speaking, doomed. Or salvage rights — wrecks on a charted hazard were acts of God, but wrecks on "clear" water raised ugly questions, whereas a wrecked ship sitting on an uncharted shoal could be written down as misadventure and picked clean at leisure. Or simpler still: the Hydrographic Office had licensed its plates to private printers, and certain printers had certain partners, and a corrected survey would have exposed a decade of profitable falsehood.

It did not matter precisely which. What mattered was that the falsification had been done *inside* the Office. The corrections were in the official engraver's style, on the official plate stock, bearing the official approval stamp. No outside forger could have touched it. Which meant the order had come from above the engravers. Considerably above.

Which meant that the man who signed Harrow's pay chits had either ordered the erasure of that shoal or had signed off on it, and either way had sent a lieutenant to Wexmouth to make sure the dead surveyor's evidence stayed buried with him.

Harrow looked at the engraving of Portsmouth harbor above his desk. Portsmouth harbor, he reflected, was accurately charted. He found this strangely moving.

---

He spent the afternoon being scrupulously ordinary. He answered correspondence. He initialed a requisition for ink powder. He ate lunch at his desk — a pork pie of heroic staleness — and told himself, in the firm tone one uses with a nervous midshipman, that there was no hurry. The page was not going anywhere. The truth, unlike a ship, could wait in harbor indefinitely.

But the truth, he admitted around three o'clock, had a crew. That was the trouble. Truths came attached to people. There was an apprentice in Wexmouth — Tamsin Fairweather, the late man's sister claimed kinship, though the word *apprentice* had been used like a wall between them — who had protected this page through murder, creditors, and whatever else the winter had thrown at that drafty workshop. There was a silent child with ink on its sleeves who had carried the thing out of harm's way inside a coat lining, seven years old at most, performing courier work that would have daunted half the officers Harrow had served with. There was a landlady with a ledger and a customs clerk with a price list, both of whom now had fingerprints on this affair, and both of whom would hang if it suited anyone to stretch the rope.

And there was, he supposed, himself: a lieutenant of nine years' service, unmarried, unpropertied, and unremarkable, whose entire career fit in a single dispatch box and could be emptied from it as easily.

He thought about what reporting the full findings would mean. The Board would convene. There would be an inquiry — inquiries being the Admiralty's principal product after maps — and the inquiry would be managed, and the management would be handled by the same gentlemen who had ordered the falsification, and the outcome would be a paragraph in the records and a pension for whoever needed silencing. Unless the evidence went wider than the Board. Unless it reached the newspapers, or the opposition, or the underwriters' rivals. In which case heads would roll — but not, he noticed, the right heads, and the apprentice and the child would be trampled in the stampede regardless. Scandals do not check their collateral damage. He had seen what became of witnesses in the '95 affair. Some of them were still technically alive.

And if he suppressed it — if he reported, as instructed, that the recovered page was a fair copy of existing plates, of no independent significance — then he would be complicit. Not passively, not by omission, but actively, deliberately, with a document in his own hands. He would become part of the machinery that had ground up Josiah Fairweather and would grind up whoever came next.

Harrow got up, walked to the window, and watched the rain perform its annual inspection of London. Somewhere out there, past the smoke and the slate roofs, was a coast he had helped keep dishonest.

*You are a cartographer's problem now,* said a dry voice in his head that sounded uncomfortably like his father. *You deal in accurate representations of reality, Lieutenant. You are currently deciding whether to draw yourself falsely.*

His father had been a dissenting minister and had died believing his son had wasted his life on the Navy, which was unfair, because Harrow had wasted it quite thoroughly and on purpose.

---

At five o'clock he wrote his report.

He wrote it three times. The first draft was honest and he burned it in the grate, watching the ink curl and vanish with a sensation he chose not to examine. The second draft was obedient and he burned that too, because reading it made him feel the way the annex tea tasted.

The third draft was a work of art, and he despised it even as he admired the craftsmanship.

*Sir,* it began. *I have recovered a document purporting to be a survey page in the hand of the late J. Fairweather, transmitted through unofficial channels. I have compared it against the published Wexmouth plate. The page appears to represent an earlier field state of the survey, predating final revisions. Discrepancies noted are consistent with normal revision practice. I find no evidence of deliberate alteration in the published chart. The original has been secured pending further instruction.*

Every sentence was defensible. Every sentence was a lie by arrangement, the way a well-drilled line of infantry is a lie about the chaos of battle. *Consistent with normal revision practice* — God, the elegance of it. Two points of bearing error and a deleted shoal, described as tidying up.

But he kept one thing back from the lie, one small splinter lodged under its skin: *the original has been secured pending further instruction.* Not destroyed. Not returned. Secured. By whom, the report did not say. Where, it did not say. It gave him — he told himself — options. Time. A thread of honesty left dangling in the weave, thin as a sounding line, which he might later follow back down to the bottom.

He sealed it. He addressed it to the Under-Secretary. Then he did not send it. He put it in his coat pocket instead, telling himself he would deliver it in the morning, and spent the evening discovering how many hours a man can sit in a tavern with an unsent report pressing against his ribs like a second heart.

---

It was nearly midnight when he admitted the real reason he had hesitated, and it was not career, and it was not conscience in the abstract, and it was not even the twenty-two drowned men, terrible as that arithmetic was.

It was the ink stains on the child's sleeve.

He kept returning to them. A seven-year-old, silent as a locked chartroom, standing behind a shelf of plates in a dead man's workshop — and stained to the elbow with writing ink, not charcoal, not dirt. *Writing* ink. As though the child worked at a desk. As though the child copied things. And the smuggled page, when he held it to the light a second time that night, showed faint pencil marks beneath the inked lines — corrections, trial bearings, a learner's hesitations overlaid on a master's confidence. Fairweather's hand teaching someone. Or someone's hand learning from Fairweather.

A household of the unclaimed, he thought. An apprentice nobody had ever legally adopted, a grandchild nobody official had ever acknowledged, a sister who arrived claiming rights she may or may not possess. People bound together by ink and work instead of blood and law. He had spent his career among men whose belonging was certified by commission and confirmed by purchase, and he had never once felt he belonged to any of it.

He wondered what it would be like to belong to a workshop instead. To be claimed by a coastline.

The fire in his grate had gone down to a sullen orange. On the desk lay the sealed lie, the true survey, and his own copy of the poisoned plate, arranged like a hand of cards he had been dealt by three different players.

He was still looking at them when the knock came — soft, hesitant, entirely wrong for midnight at the annex — and he opened the door on wet fog and a figure he did not expect, holding something wrapped in oilcloth, and the figure said his name in a voice that cracked on the second syllable, and Harrow understood that the century intended to end early for him.
