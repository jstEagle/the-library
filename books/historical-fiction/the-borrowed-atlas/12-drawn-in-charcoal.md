## 12. Drawn in Charcoal

The workshop smells of glue and cold and the tall man's hands.

The tall man is called Tamsin. The child knows this because the landlady woman across the lane shouts it over the fences, and because the tall man says it to himself sometimes, low, like checking a knot holds. *Tamsin. Tamsin.* A name is a thing you say to yourself so you do not lose it. The child had a name once. It was taken away along with everything else, folded up small and put somewhere dark, like the paper in the coat.

Paper. The coat. The tall man keeps looking at the coat.

The child watches him look at it while pretending to sweep. Sweeping is a good pretending. Grown people stop watching you when you have a broom; a broom makes you furniture. The child learned that early. Furniture is safe. Furniture does not get put on boats or asked questions by men with hard voices who smell of tar and mean.

The tall man does not smell of mean. He smells of ink and tallow and the outside of doors. He talks even when nobody answers, which is most of the time, because the child does not answer. Words went into the sea with the boat. Words are heavy. If you carry words, you sink. So the child carries pictures instead. Pictures float.

* * *

There is a box of charcoal stubs on the third shelf, behind the jar of bad quills. The child has known about it for six days. Six days of sweeping past the shelf, of eating the bread the tall man leaves within reach without being asked, of sleeping in the cot that still smells faintly of another person — an old person, pipe-smoke and camphor, the grandfather-smell, and the child sleeps inside that smell like inside a coat.

On the seventh night the tall man works late at his big table, scratching away with a pen, and the lamp throws his shadow huge against the wall, a shadow with wings made of hanging maps. The child takes three charcoal stubs off the shelf. Black, grey, and one that is only half black, half wood, good for water. Water needs a softer mark than land. Everyone knows that. The child knew it before knowing anything else.

There is brown wrapping paper in the corner bin. Good. Big as a door if you open it out flat. The child opens it flat on the floor by the stove, where the warmth lives, and kneels on it, and the charcoal feels like coming home.

First: the sea.

The child draws the sea all around the edge of the paper, big looping waves, wave after wave after wave, because the sea was everywhere and would not stop. Then the boat. The boat is small in the middle of all the waves, too small, much too small — draw it smaller, smaller again — and the boat has a name painted on its side but the child cannot write names, so the child draws a bird above the mast instead, because the boat's name was a bird-name, and Mother said it every morning like a prayer, *the Kestrel, the Kestrel*, patting the hull.

Then Mother.

Mother stands on the deck. The child draws her tall, taller than the mast almost, because she was the biggest thing in the world. Her hair comes down in the wind. She is holding her arm up — not waving. Holding it up toward the sky, toward the lighthouse on the point that did not blink that night. The child draws the lighthouse on the shore, and then, thinking hard, draws a little X through the lamp room. No light. That is the important part. That is the whole secret part. Grandfather drew charts, and the chart said the rocks were elsewhere, and the lighthouse keeper said he was told not to light it, and the men in the fine coats said the wreck was Grandfather's fault, his fault his fault his fault, until his face went grey and folded up like wet paper.

The child draws Grandfather next, small, at the edge of the shore, arms down. Arms down is how he stood in the last days. He used to lift the child onto his shoulders. In the drawing there is no room for shoulders-lifting anymore. There is only arms-down and the X where the light should be.

The child looks at the drawing for a long time.

Then adds one more thing: under the water line, drawn pale and soft with the side of the charcoal, the true shape of the shoal. Long as a sleeping whale, lying right where no chart said it lay. Right under the Kestrel.

* * *

In the morning the tall man finds the drawing.

The child hears him stop moving. Stops breathing, nearly. There is a long silence in which the stove ticks and the kettle thinks about boiling, and then footsteps, quick, and the paper rustles as he lifts it.

"Oh," the tall man says. Just that. Just *oh*, very quietly, like something has been handed to him that weighs more than paper should.

He sits down on the floor. Not at the table — on the floor, cross-legged, right there beside the child, which no grown person has done since Mother. He lays the paper between them on the boards. His finger hovers over the boat.

"Is this —" He stops. Starts again, gentler, the voice people use for frightened animals. "Is this your mama?"

The child nods. Once.

The tall man's breath goes out slow. His finger moves to the lighthouse, to the little X scratched through the lamp room.

"And the light wasn't lit." Not a question now. His voice has gone strange, tight, like wire wound too far. "Who told you to remember that? Did someone tell you?"

Nobody told. The child shakes the head. Then points — at the drawing, at the child-in-the-drawing, small stick figure standing on the cliff beside Grandfather, both of them looking at the dark tower. *I saw,* says the pointing. *I was there.*

"You were there." The tall man says it like the words hurt his mouth. He rubs a hand over his face. When it comes down he is looking at the child differently — not at the child like a burden or a mystery, but at the child like a witness. Like a map. Something in his eyes has caught fire, low and steady, banked-coal fire, the kind that lasts all night.

"The shoal," he says. His finger traces the pale whale-shape under the waves. "You've drawn the shoal. Where it actually lies."

The child nods.

"And it isn't where the Admiralty charts say it is."

Nod.

The tall man gets up suddenly and goes to the big flat drawers against the wall, the ones that hold the master plates, and pulls one out, and carries it back, and kneels down again, and lays it beside the drawing. And the two of them sit on the floor together, the grown man and the child, comparing the truth and the lie, back and forth, back and forth, his finger going from paper to plate to paper.

"They moved it," he murmurs. "God help us. They drew it a full mile east. They erased the second bar entirely." He laughs, one short hard note with no joy in it at all. "He tried to tell them. He stood in that great stone building with this exact evidence and they called him a liar and a drunk and they broke him with it. And it was all here the whole time, carried about in a seven-year-old's coat."

The child looks at him. The tall man's eyes are shining wet. Grown men cry differently from children, quietly, sideways, like leaks they're ashamed of. The child knows about crying sideways. The child does some now too, a little, without meaning to.

And then, because the drawing is not finished — because the truth is not finished — the child takes up the charcoal again and turns the paper over.

* * *

The other side is harder.

This side has no sea in it. This side has the night after the wreck, when the men came to Grandfather's house. Three men. The child draws them tall and straight-backed, all alike, coats with buttons buttoned high, faces left blank on purpose — not because the faces are forgotten but because the faces were hats and collars and hard voices, faces you never properly saw, faces like closed doors. One of them carries a lantern. One carries papers. The third one's hand is drawn as a fist, and the fist is raised, and near it the child draws Grandfather falling, small, arms-down forever.

Mother was already gone by then. Lost with the boat — no, not lost, the child corrects the picture silently, *taken by the sea*, which is different, which is a thing the sea does, which is nobody's fault except maybe the fist-men's fault, a little, because of the unlit light. The child had cried for three days and then stopped crying, and that was when the words went into the sea too, packed in Mother's sea-chest, sunk with everything else.

But there is one more thing to draw. The hardest thing. The thing that must be drawn last because it is the reason the child ran instead of staying, the reason for the coat and the paper sewn in the lining and the long cold walk to the workshop door and the stranger saying *forget my face* in a voice like a cracked bell.

The child draws a house. Grandfather's house, with its crooked chimney. And in the window of the house, the child draws Mother.

Not ghost-Mother. Real Mother, warm, whole, sleeves rolled, flour on her hands — the way she looked on ordinary mornings, the way she will always look inside the child's head no matter what the sea says. Because here is the thing the child has never told anyone, the thing too big and too impossible to carry in words, the thing that could only be charcoal:

After the wreck, after the funeral-that-wasn't, after the fist-men came — Mother came back. Wet to the waist, coughing salt, half-dead, alive. Pulled from the water by a fisherman two coves down and carried home in the night like smuggled goods. Alive. The child had slept four nights in the crook of her arm, listening to her heart go like a hammer on a hull, while she whispered plans: the page from Grandfather's survey hidden in the lining, the journey south, the workshop, *give it to the apprentice, the apprentice will know what to do, forget my face, forget my face, forget my face* — because the fist-men were still looking for anyone who carried the truth, and a mother can be caught, but a faceless child carrying nothing visible might slip through.

She meant to send for the child later. She said so. She kissed the top of the head and said *later, I promise, later* — and then the fist-men found the cove, and there was shouting, and the fisherman shoved the child out the back and down the lane with the coat and the page and no time, no time at all, and the child never saw which way she went, and has not stopped listening for her heartbeat in every silence since.

The child finishes Mother in the window. Adds the flour-hands. Adds, after a moment, a small figure beside her — the child themselves, drawn holding her sleeve. Because that is how it was supposed to be. That is the drawing of how things ought to have gone.

Then the child puts down the charcoal.

The tall man has not moved. He has watched the whole second drawing come into being with his hand pressed flat against his chest, and when the child turns the paper fully toward him, he reads it the way he reads charts — slowly, carefully, letting each mark be true — and the child watches understanding arrive in him like tide filling a harbor, inch by inch by inch.

"Your mother," he says hoarsely. "Your mother survived the wreck."

Nod.

"She hid you. She sent you here with the evidence because they'd kill to destroy it, and she thought — she thought being forgotten was the safest thing she could make you." His voice cracks clean through on the last word. He scrubs his sleeve across his eyes, not bothering to hide it this time, and then he does something extraordinary. He reaches out and very gently turns the child's charcoal-blackened hand over in his own, examining the stained fingers like a craftsman among craftsmen.

"Do you know," he says, and there is a wobble in it, and also, underneath the wobble, something that might almost be laughter, "I have spent six weeks trying to get the truth out of the Admiralty with letters, and petitions, and a great deal of very expensive brandy bought for very useless clerks. And you've done it in one evening with a burnt stick and the back of some wrapping paper."

The child looks at their own hand in his. Black to the wrist. Mother's hands were always floury; these are charcoaly. It is nearly the same thing. Things that come from making.

"Tamsin!" booms a voice from across the lane, Mrs. Quill's voice, sharp as a cleaver through the frosty window glass. "Your chimney's smoking crooked again and if you burn the lane down I'll be putting it in the ledger!"

The tall man closes his eyes briefly. "That woman," he mutters, "could ledger a thunderstorm." But he is smiling, just slightly, and the child catches it, files it away, adds it to the small growing collection of safe things.

Then the smile goes, and he looks back down at the drawings — the wreck, the unlit light, the buried shoal, the fist-men, Mother alive in a window — and the banked coal in his eyes flares up bright and hard.

"They'll come for this," he says quietly. "For you, and for what you carry. Sooner rather than later. Someone tipped the harbor watch, and now this—" he taps the drawing of the fist-men, "—means we know exactly whose errand they're on." He stands, pacing, thinking aloud the way he does, forgetting the child is furniture. "We need the original page. We need to move it tonight, or tomorrow at the latest, before—"

A knock at the street door.

Not Mrs. Quill's knock — hers arrives like weather, continuous and complaining. This is three raps, precise, evenly spaced, the knock of a man who has knocked on many doors in many towns and never once been turned away.

The tall man goes absolutely still. The child goes still too. Two kinds of stillness meeting in the lamplight: his is fear; the child's is older, colder, the stillness of a small animal that knows the shape of hard voices through doors.

Three raps again. Then, smooth as oiled paper, a voice:

"Good evening. Lieutenant Harrow, of the Admiralty. I'm calling after the late Mr. Fairweather's papers — purely a formality, I assure you."

The tall man looks at the drawings on the floor. At the child. At the coat on its peg, with its secret lining. The child sees him do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic Mother must have done in a fisherman's kitchen — and watches him arrive, white-faced, at the sum.

"Under the table," he breathes. "Now. Take the charcoal."

The child gathers the stubs in one black fist and folds the truth up small and slides beneath the table among the shadowed legs, heart going like the Kestrel in a gale — and through the gap under the door, the child watches a tall silhouette in a naval coat step in out of the snow, polite as Sunday, patient as winter, and begin to look around the room.
