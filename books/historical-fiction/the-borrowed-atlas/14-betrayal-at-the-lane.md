## 14. Betrayal at the Lane

Cordelia Fairweather had always believed that grief, properly managed, was an investment.

Her brother Edmund had understood this too, though he'd been disgracefully bad at it. He had invested thirty years in a coastal atlas that would never pay him back — not in coin, not in reputation, not even in the simple satisfaction of a job well done, because the Admiralty had taken even that from him before the end. And what had his devotion earned him? A cold room, a cold grave, and a workshop full of creditors who circled the premises like gulls after a fishing boat's leavings.

Cordelia did not intend to make her brother's mistake. She intended to make his death profitable.

She sat in the best parlor of the Bell and Anchor, which was not saying much — the best parlor of the Bell and Anchor had water stains on its ceiling shaped like a map of nowhere and smelled permanently of tallow and old pipe smoke — but it was private, and private was what she required. She had dressed for the occasion in her second-best mourning (her best being reserved for occasions when she wished to be underestimated, which this was not) and she had arranged her face into the expression that had served her through two funerals and one bankruptcy: composed sorrow, with just enough steel beneath to suggest that composing herself further would cost somebody money.

Lieutenant Harrow arrived precisely on time. Cordelia approved of punctuality; it suggested a man who kept accounts.

"Lady Fairweather." He bowed — not deeply enough to be servile, not shallowly enough to be rude. A measured bow. She liked measurements.

"Mrs. Fairweather," she corrected. "The Fairweathers were never titled. My brother would have found titles absurd." *My brother found nearly everything except his charts absurd,* she did not add. "But you are kind."

"I am thorough," said Harrow, sitting. "I am told the two are often confused."

*Oh,* thought Cordelia, with the small warm click of a lock turning true. *Oh, I am going to enjoy doing business with you.*

She had prepared three openings for this conversation: one dignified, one sentimental, one practical. She discarded the dignified immediately — Harrow had the eyes of a man who filed dignity under *decoration* — and reached past the sentimental without regret. Practical it was.

"My late brother," she began, "was the finest cartographer this coast has ever produced. I say that not as his sister but as a woman who has read every survey he ever drew, including those he believed no one else had seen." She let that settle. "The Admiralty ruined him. You know it. I know it. The men who signed the falsified plates know it, which is why they killed him — or had him killed, I make no distinction between the hand and the order."

Harrow's teacup paused halfway to his mouth. It resumed its journey. But Cordelia had watched men's hands for fifty years, and she noted the pause the way a banker notes a tremor in a signature.

"You make serious accusations, madam."

"I make accurate ones. There is a difference, Lieutenant, and I suspect you trade in it." She folded her hands. "Now. The atlas. The true atlas — the finished plates, my brother's own hand, complete or near enough to complete that a competent engraver could have it ready by spring. It sits in his workshop on Cooper's Lane, guarded — if that is the word — by his apprentice."

"The apprentice." Harrow said the word neutrally, but something moved behind it, like a fish under ice.

"Tamsin." Cordelia pronounced the name with delicate distaste, as though tasting a wine of dubious vintage. "A foundling. Never legally bound, never formally adopted, never given my brother's name despite bearing it in common usage — you will notice the workshop is called *Fairweather*, and the apprentice is called only Tamsin. There is a reason for that, and the reason is that my brother, whatever his virtues, understood property. An unclaimed apprentice is a legal shadow. The work of a legal shadow belongs—" she smiled, thin and bright, "—to the family."

"And you are the family."

"I am the *only* family." She let the words ring. "Which brings me to the other matter, and I will be direct, because I can see you are a man who appreciates directness the way other men appreciate brandy."

Harrow set down his cup. "Please."

"There is a child."

This time she watched him carefully, and this time she caught it — not a pause but a stillness, the particular stillness of a man who has been hunting something and hears, at last, the sound of its breathing.

"Go on," he said quietly.

"A child arrived at my brother's workshop some weeks ago. Left at the door. Perhaps seven years old. Does not speak — or does not choose to, which in my experience amounts to the same thing in children, who are all natural conspirators." Cordelia allowed herself a dry note here, a small human touch, because the best lies — and this part, at least, was no lie — were seasoned with truth. "The apprentice has taken it in. Fed it. Hidden it. Drawn it about the lanes like a nursemaid with a secret."

"Why should that interest me?"

"Because," said Cordelia, "you have been asking after my brother's papers at every dock, every tavern, every chandler's along the waterfront — do not trouble to deny it, Lieutenant, Mrs. Quill across the lane from the workshop knows more than the customs house does and charges less for it — and a man who asks that many questions about papers is a man who believes certain papers are still in circulation. And a child left at a dead cartographer's door in the same month he died is either a coincidence or a courier. I have never once, in fifty-one years, encountered a coincidence worth acting upon."

Silence. Somewhere below them, the Bell and Anchor's ordinary clattered on: a dropped mug, laughter, the landlady's voice calling for more coal. Cordelia waited. She had learned long ago that silence was like tide — if you stood still and let it come, it brought everything to your feet.

"What is it you want?" Harrow asked finally.

Straight to it. Good. Cordelia had feared she might have to endure an hour of fencing first, and her knees were not what they were.

"Three things." She raised one gloved finger. "First: the atlas. Full legal recognition of my inheritance — the plates, the copper engravings, the copyright entire. My brother died intestate and unproven, which means the work is legally adrift, and I mean to have letters patent confirming me as its owner before any court, any Board, any inquiry says otherwise." A second finger. "Second: a formal statement from the Admiralty acknowledging my brother's authorship of the coastal surveys — posthumous credit, printed, official. Not justice. Justice is beyond both of us, I think. But credit costs the Admiralty nothing and costs me everything if I don't secure it now, while the relevant gentlemen are frightened." A third finger. "Third: passage. When this business concludes, I intend to remove myself somewhere warmer than England, with the proceeds of the atlas, and I require safe conduct and no questions at the port."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange," said Cordelia, "you will know where to find the child, the page it carries, and the apprentice who has been hiding them both from His Majesty's government for the better part of a month."

There it was. Laid on the stained tablecloth between them like a card turned face-up.

She watched Harrow take it in. He had a good face for reading, actually — better than most military men, whose faces were drilled blank. What crossed his now was not triumph. It was something wearier, and beneath the weariness, briefly, something that looked almost like distaste. Cordelia had seen that expression before, on the faces of men signing things they'd rather not sign, and she knew it for what it was: not conscience, exactly, but the memory of conscience. Conscience's ghost, rattling its chains politely.

"You believe this child carries my brother's original survey," Harrow said. "The one proving the falsification."

"I believe the child is the reason my brother is dead, or very close kin to that reason. Blood runs in strange channels, Lieutenant. I have spent my life following the channels and letting others worry about the blood."

Something flickered across Harrow's face at that — quickly gone, quickly buried — and Cordelia wondered, with the idle curiosity she reserved for other people's secrets, what channel he was thinking of. But he only nodded, once, slowly.

"You understand," he said, "that if the child has the survey, the child is evidence. Evidence is not harmed. But evidence is *taken*."

"I ask nothing about your methods. I want the atlas, the credit, and the boat. What happens to a foundling child is between you and your superiors and, presumably, whatever God you answer to." She rose, smoothing her skirts. "The workshop is number eleven, Cooper's Lane. The child sleeps in the loft above the drafting room — the apprentice thinks no one knows. Mrs. Quill knows; Mrs. Quill knows everything; but Mrs. Quill can be bought and I have already priced her." This last was a guess, but a confident one. Everyone could be bought. The price merely varied. "Come Thursday. The apprentice takes the atlas plates to the framer's Thursday mornings and locks the child in — habit, not malice; the child wanders. You will have your window."

"Mrs. Fairweather." Harrow rose also, and there was something new in his voice, something almost gentle, which she distrusted profoundly. "Your brother must have loved you very much."

Cordelia paused with her hand on the door.

"He loved his charts," she said. "He loved his coasts and his soundings and his insufferable precision. He loved a daughter he lost to the sea and a granddaughter he never met. As for me—" she smiled, and it was, for once, entirely honest, which made it worse, "—he tolerated me the way one tolerates weather. Which is why I owe him nothing, Lieutenant, and why neither of us need pretend otherwise."

She swept out into the December afternoon, feeling lighter than she had since the funeral. Grief, properly managed, was an investment. And today, at last, it had paid out.

* * *

What Cordelia Fairweather did not know — could not have known, though had she known it she would have been mortified down to her petticoats — was that the best parlor of the Bell and Anchor shared a wall with the establishment's linen closet, and that the linen closet shared a door with the kitchen, and that the kitchen door had been propped open with a brick since Tuesday because the hinge squeaked and the landlady refused to pay for oil until quarter day.

And what she further did not know was that Mrs. Prudence Quill, landlady of number twelve Cooper's Lane, keeper of ledgers, holder of debts, and knower of everything, had come to the Bell and Anchor that very afternoon for the perfectly innocent purpose of collecting six shillings owed to her by the potboy — a debt recorded in her ledger under *Tobias W., sundry, October* — and had been standing in the linen closet, counting shirts while she waited, when the lieutenant and the widow came in.

Mrs. Quill had heard every word.

She stood now among the linens, quite still, a woman built like a well-scrubbed turnip and possessed of a mind like a fisherman's net — broad, strong, and nothing gets through it. Her first thought was professional outrage. *Thursday mornings.* Selling another woman's habits, selling a *child's* sleeping hours, for paper and a boat ticket. In forty years of keeping accounts, Mrs. Quill had entered some ugly transactions in her ledger — she was not a woman to throw stones from a glass house, hers having windows of a particularly cloudy sort — but this sat wrong even by her standards. Debts were debts. Children were not currency.

Her second thought was arithmetic, and it was this: if the lieutenant took the child Thursday morning, then the Admiralty would have the survey page, and if the Admiralty had the survey page, then Tamsin Fairweather would have nothing — no master's name restored, no future secured, nothing but an empty loft and a lifetime of knowing the child had been sold out from under them by the master's own blood.

And Mrs. Quill — who would have denied it under oath, who had in fact denied softer versions of it under actual oath twice — Mrs. Quill had grown fond. Fond of the silent little creature who sat on her step some evenings drawing coastlines in charcoal on slate, fond enough that she'd begun leaving out seed cake without being asked, fond in the specific way of a woman who had buried a husband and a son and had decided, privately, irrevocably, that she was done burying people and equally done watching children go hungry within earshot of her kitchen.

So: Thursday. Two days. That was the shape of it.

Mrs. Quill put down the shirt she had been counting, straightened her cap, and departed the linen closet via the kitchen, nodding to the cook as though she had every right in the world to be walking through, which in her experience was ninety percent of having rights anywhere. Then she went out into the street and walked — did not run; running attracted attention, and attention was the enemy of all her trades — walked as fast as a brisk woman could walk toward Cooper's Lane, her ledger bag bumping against her hip like a second heartbeat.

* * *

She found Tamsin in the workshop, bent over plate fourteen, the lamp already lit against the early dusk. Through the window above the drafting table Mrs. Quill could see the child in the loft, curled on the cot with a stub of charcoal, drawing something on a board balanced against both knees — drawing, always drawing, that child, as if speech had been poured instead into the hand.

Tamsin looked up when the bell over the door rang, and Mrs. Quill saw the wariness arrive before anything else — the quick flick of the eyes to the doorway behind her, checking for shadows. Poor creature. A month of looking over one shoulder wears grooves into a person.

"Mrs. Quill." Tamsin set down the ruling pen. "Is something — is the child all right? They were on your step an hour ago, eating you out of seed cake, I expect."

"Child's fine. Ate four slices and drew a lighthouse on my clean tablecloth, which we'll call rent." Mrs. Quill shut the door behind her, leaned her back against it, and got her breath. "Tamsin. We've no time for pleasantries, so I'll say it plain, the way I'd enter it in a ledger. Your mistress has sold you."

The lamp flame stood very still. So did Tamsin.

"Cordelia Fairweather," Mrs. Quill went on, "sat in the Bell and Anchor not two hours past with a lieutenant of the Admiralty, one Harrow, tall, grey-eyed, polite as a bill collector, and she told him where the child sleeps. Loft above this room. Thursday morning, while you're at the framer's." She held up a hand before Tamsin could speak. "Before you ask how I know — the linen closet, the propped door, and God forgive me the whole parish's sins if I'm lying. I heard every word. She wants the atlas copyright, a printed credit for her brother, and a boat to somewhere warm. He wants the child. She gave him the child like handing over a parcel."

Tamsin had gone the color of old paper. One hand had risen, unconsciously, to press flat against the drafting table — over the place where, Mrs. Quill happened to know, beneath a false bottom in the map drawer, the true survey lay sewn into oilcloth these five days, ever since the harbor failure. She watched Tamsin's fingers find that spot and rest there, and thought: *good, the instinct's alive.*

"She's his sister," Tamsin said at last, and the voice was small and level and terrible. "His blood. Family."

"Family," Mrs. Quill repeated, with a snort that could have cleared a chimney. "I've kept accounts on families for forty years, Tamsin Fairweather, and I'll tell you what's in them. Blood is what people write down. Belonging is what people *do*. Your mistress writes herself down family and sells a seven-year-old to save her pocketbook. Meanwhile a foundling apprentice with no surname worth the ink feeds that child, clothes it, risks the rope for it." She jabbed a thumb toward the loft, where small footsteps could now be heard overhead — the child had noticed voices and come to the edge of the ladder, peering down through the floor hatch with dark, serious eyes. "You tell me which one of you is kin."

Overhead, the child made a small sound — not a word, never a word — and held up the charcoal board so it could be seen through the hatch. On it, drawn quick and sure: three figures in a room. A tall one, a round one, a thin one. And beside them, unmistakably, a little stick figure running.

Even the child knew. Even the child had read the fear coming up through the floorboards.

Tamsin stood very slowly. When Mrs. Quill looked at the apprentice's face, she saw what she had perhaps been waiting all month to see: the grief and the caution burning away together, like fog off the water at sunrise, leaving something hard and bright underneath.

"Thursday," Tamsin said. "Two days."

"Two days. Maybe less — lieutenants have a way of arriving early when they're keen." Mrs. Quill pushed off from the door and began, briskly, practically, to look around the workshop with new eyes: the coat hooks, the back stair to the yard, the cellar hatch. "So. You can't stay, and you can't go by the harbor, and the customs house road will be watched the moment they miss their window. Which leaves one door in this town nobody watches because nobody's fool enough to knock on it." She turned and fixed Tamsin with a look that had quelled better debtors than any lieutenant. "And before you ask — yes, I know which door I mean, and no, I haven't lost my mind, and yes, I'm coming with you, because that child eats my seed cake and I've made arrangements."

Outside, far off toward the river, a watchman's bell rang the hour, and the sound of it seemed to fall into the workshop like a stone into a well. Two days. Perhaps less.

Tamsin reached up toward the loft, and the child came down the ladder into waiting arms without a moment's hesitation — no pause, no weighing, none of the careful negotiation that governed every other relationship in that room. Just trust, handed over whole.

"Pack nothing you'd hate to lose," said Mrs. Quill, already pulling coats from their hooks. "We leave tonight."
