## 15. Flight Through the Fog

Mrs. Quill's knock came at half past ten at night — three quick raps, then two more, which was not her way at all. Mrs. Quill's ordinary knock was a single authoritative thump, as though the door had personally offended her.

Tamsin opened it and knew before a word was spoken.

"They've sold you," she said. It was not a question. The fog had crept into the entryway behind Mrs. Quill, grey and thick as wool unwound from a great spool, and somewhere out in it a lantern swung — too regular, too patient, the walk of a man being paid by the hour.

"The widow," Mrs. Quill said, breathless in a manner most undignified for her. "She met him at the corner of Cutler's Lane not two hours past. I heard every word through my own window, and my window hears everything worth hearing. She gave him the direction of this house and took his promise of the atlas copyright in return." Her mouth went thin. "Family," she added, with the contempt of a woman who had buried three husbands and kept meticulous accounts of all of them.

The child was asleep on the cot behind the drafting table, curled around a stub of charcoal even now, small fingers smudged black. Tamsin looked at that sleeping face and felt something in her chest turn over and lock into place, the way a ratchet clicks.

"Get your shawl," Tamsin said. "Both of you. We're going tonight."

"I'm seventy-one years old, girl."

"You're seventy-one years old and you know every drain, alley, and debt in this quarter. That's why I'm not leaving without you."

That earned her something rare: Mrs. Quill almost smiled.

* * *

They had prepared, in the way that frightened people prepare — quietly, over weeks, telling themselves they were merely being thorough. The coat had been Tamsin's work, three nights of it, sitting by the lamp after the child slept, needle flashing. The true survey page, the master's own hand, the proof that had cost him everything: folded to the size of a prayer book and sewn flat between the lining and the wool of her left breast, so that it lay over her heart like a second heartbeat. She had tested it by wearing the coat through a full day's rain; the page had come through dry as August.

Into the child's coat she had sewn nothing but warmth. Whatever happened, the child would carry only charcoal and drawings.

"We go north first," Mrs. Quill whispered in the doorway, pulling her shawl over her cap. "Not toward the river. They'll watch the river."

"The Admiralty is south."

"Aye, and so is every watchman in London tonight. We go north until we're lost, then we double back through the fog while they're looking for us in Kent." She said it the way she said everything — as though the world were an account that had failed to balance and she intended to correct it personally.

Tamsin woke the child gently, one hand cupped over the small mouth before the first whimper could escape. Wide eyes opened in the dark. Tamsin held up one finger: *quiet*. The child nodded, solemn as a judge, and reached up to be lifted. That trust — given so freely, so completely, by someone who had been abandoned at a doorstep — was heavier than any cargo Tamsin had ever carried.

They went out the back, through the yard where the drying lines hung like rigging, over the wall Mrs. Quill swore no one under sixty could climb (she climbed it herself, swearing magnificently), and into the fog.

London in December fog is a city dissolved. The lamps became smudges of orange, then suggestions, then memories. Sound traveled strangely: a dog barked somewhere to the left and seemed to come from above; their own footsteps arrived late, as if following rather than leading them. Tamsin had spent years drawing coastlines — the whole art of her trade was knowing where land ended — and here was a night that refused all boundaries. She hated it and was grateful for it in equal measure.

Behind them, faint but persistent, came the swing of that lantern.

"He's not hurrying," Tamsin breathed.

"He doesn't need to hurry," Mrs. Quill said grimly. "He thinks he knows where we must go. Every road south funnels past the customs house or the bridge, and both have men on them tonight. Which is why—" she seized Tamsin's sleeve "—we are not going south yet. In here."

*In here* was a coal merchant's yard, black and smelling of dust and old rain. Mrs. Quill led them along the fence to a gap she clearly knew of old, through a passage between warehouses that smelled of tallow and rat, and out onto a street Tamsin did not recognize at all, which alarmed her until she understood that this was the entire point.

The child rode on Tamsin's hip without complaint, head tucked against her neck, small body burning hot with feverish wakefulness. Once, passing beneath a lamp, the child raised their head and stared back down the street they'd come by, and Tamsin felt the small frame go rigid.

"What is it?"

The child didn't answer — never answered — but pressed a charcoal-blackened finger against Tamsin's cheek and then pointed backward, down into the dark. *Someone following.* Then the finger moved: pointed forward, ahead of them too, into the other dark.

Tamsin stopped walking.

Between the two darknesses, in a fog that hid everything, they were already inside the search. Not pursued by it — surrounded by it. Somewhere behind, the lantern. Somewhere ahead, who knew what. And threading invisibly between them all, the widow's bargain, moving faster than any of them because betrayal always traveled lightest.

* * *

They sheltered through the dead hours in the porch of a chapel whose doors were locked, huddled together under Mrs. Quill's enormous shawl, taking turns pretending to sleep. The fog pressed at the porch like a living thing, breathing against them. Around three in the morning the bells began — wrong, at first, Tamsin thought, until she counted and realized some parish across the city was ringing out an alarm or a fire or simply its own enthusiasm, and every bell within earshot took it up, and the sound rolled over the rooftops like a tide coming in.

The child sat bolt upright, eyes enormous.

"Hush, little one," Mrs. Quill murmured. "It's only the city talking in its sleep."

But the child shook their head, and drew — right there on the chapel flagstone, with the nub of charcoal clutched like a talisman — a quick, fierce spiral. Bells. Then, beside it, a tower. A tall square tower with a light at the top. Then the child looked at Tamsin with such urgency that words finally broke loose from Tamsin's own throat:

"The Admiralty has a signal tower. On the roof. For the semaphore line." She stared at the drawing. "You've seen it?"

A nod. Fierce. Then the child pointed at the tower again, and at themselves, and made a shape with both hands — a roof, held overhead, a shelter.

"You know a way in," Tamsin said slowly. "From before. When your mother—"

The child flinched, just slightly, at the word mother, and then nodded once, hard, and put both hands flat on the stone as if to say *enough, remember it, let's go.*

Mrs. Quill looked from the child to Tamsin and back. "Well," she said. "It appears we are expected."

* * *

Dawn came, or something that pretended to be dawn: the fog turned from black to grey to a pale, sour milk color, and still it did not lift. By full light they were moving again, south at last, doubling back through streets where the watchmen stood peering into white nothing and seeing nothing, because Mrs. Quill took them down a lane so narrow that two people could not walk abreast, a lane she claimed ran behind a chandler's and had done since the Fire and was known to precisely four living persons, of whom she was one and God, she suspected, had mislaid the rest.

Twice they were nearly caught.

The first time, a watchman materialized out of the fog not six feet away, so suddenly that Tamsin had time for exactly one thought — *the page, the page is over my heart* — and then Mrs. Quill stepped forward, transformed. Her spine straightened, her chin came up, and she addressed the young man in the voice of a duchess discovering a footman in her linen cupboard.

"You there. Yes, you. My niece has been taken ill — the fog, you understand, the vile fog — and I require you to run to Mr. Pellow, the apothecary on Cordwainer Street, tell him Mrs. Quill sent you and that he owes me for the goose in November. Run, boy. Lives hang upon it."

The boy ran. Whether an apothecary named Pellow existed, whether a goose had ever been owed, Tamsin would never learn, but the lane stayed empty and they walked swiftly on.

"How long have you been able to do that?" Tamsin whispered.

"Since I realized, somewhere around my fortieth year, that everyone in this city is terrified of being caught not knowing something." She sniffed. "It is the great secret of England, dear. We are all of us bluffing, and the ones who admit it are the ones who fall."

The second time was worse. They had come out near the river at last, within sight — a shape, a suggestion — of the great buildings, when boots sounded behind them: not one pair but several, unhurried, closing like a hand.

Tamsin pulled the child into a doorway and pressed them flat against the wood, her own body a shield, and felt the page over her heart and thought with terrible clarity: *I will tear this coat open and feed the page to the river before I let them take it, and I will do worse than that before I let them take the child.*

The boots passed. Three men, uniforms under oilskins, speaking low. One said a name — Harrow — and another answered with something that made the first laugh, and then they were gone into the white, hunting some other doorway, some other shadow.

Only when they were fully gone did Tamsin realize the child was trembling — not with fear, she understood with a lurch, but with fury. Small fists clenched. Jaw set in a way that belonged to no seven-year-old, that belonged to an older face, a face in a portrait miniature sewn inside a dead man's hatband, the daughter lost at sea. Grief and rage handed down like eye color.

"We're nearly there," Tamsin whispered, gathering the child close. "Do you hear me? Nearly there. And I swear to you — I swear it on every map I'll ever draw — no one is taking you anywhere. Not the widow, not the watchmen, not the whole bloody Admiralty. You're mine now. Ours. That's decided, and it's decided forever, and the fog can swallow the rest of London before it swallows that."

The trembling slowed. Stopped. The child looked up at her, and in the grey light Tamsin saw — plainly, unmistakably, for the first time — the master's eyes. His exact eyes, set in a small fierce face.

Blood and ink. Both, after all.

* * *

The last stretch was the strangest: the fog began to thin just as they reached the broad front of the Admiralty, so that the building assembled itself out of the whiteness piece by piece — pillars, windows, the signal tower rising above like the mast of a beached ship. Guards stood at the doors, bored and cold and very awake in the way of men paid to be awake.

And there, descending the steps, hat in hand, coat immaculate despite the hour, was Lieutenant Harrow.

He saw them and stopped. For a long moment neither party moved, the four of them frozen in the thinning fog like figures in a print left out in the weather. Tamsin's hand found the child's shoulder. Harrow's hand, she noticed, was nowhere near his sword.

He looked tired. He looked like a man who had not slept, and who had spent his sleeplessness reading something that had rearranged his insides.

"Miss Fairweather," he said at last, quietly. "You should not be here."

"No," Tamsin agreed. "I shouldn't. But I am, and so are you, and I think we've both run out of places to hide." She lifted her chin toward the great doors. "I need to get inside, Lieutenant. Tonight. Tomorrow at the latest. And I think — I think you already know why."

The fog eddied between them. Behind her, Mrs. Quill muttered something about standing in a draft. Against her hip, the child's small hand slid into hers and gripped tight.

Harrow closed his eyes briefly, as if consulting some ledger of his own.

"Then you had better come with me," he said, "before anyone else sees you. And God help us both, Miss Fairweather — I mean that quite literally."

He turned and walked toward the doors, and Tamsin followed, carrying her heart, her proof, and her family through the enemy's door.
