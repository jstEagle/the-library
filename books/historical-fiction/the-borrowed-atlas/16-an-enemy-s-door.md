## 16. An Enemy's Door

Lieutenant Edmund Harrow had spent the better part of a decade learning that the most dangerous hour of any pursuit was not the chase but the waiting — the long grey interval when the quarry had gone to ground and every instinct insisted on action while every principle of his trade counseled patience. He had waited out smugglers in Cornish cellars and mutineers in Portsmouth alehouses, and in all that time he had learned one further lesson, which he kept folded away like a letter never sent: *the ones who run are rarely the guilty ones. The guilty ones stay put and hire lawyers.*

So when the fog of the twenty-eighth of December swallowed the lane off Cutler Street and his informants reported that the Fairweather workshop stood dark, the apprentice gone, and the child with her, he did not curse or ride out into the white blindness after them. He sat down at the window of the Anchor & Compass, ordered a second cup of coffee he did not want, and thought about ink stains on a small sleeve.

*She has taken the page,* he thought. *And she is going where I would go if I were a frightened mapmaker with one piece of proof and three days left in the century.*

She was going to the Admiralty.

The realization settled over him like wet wool. He turned his coffee cup slowly on its saucer, once, twice, and admitted to himself what he had been refusing to admit since the customs clerk's copy had arrived on his desk wrapped in brown paper and tied with the particular greasy string that clerks favored: that he had read it four times, and that each reading had made the room feel colder.

Sandbars moved by a draftsman's hand. Shoals inked out entire. A channel marked safe that had swallowed eleven men off the Skerries while the official chart promised them depth enough for a man-of-war. And in the corner, in a hand Harrow now knew as well as his own — because he had studied the master's accused survey until he could reproduce it from memory — the initials *J.F.* and the date two years before the wreck.

His superiors had not merely tolerated the falsification. They had commissioned it. The old coastal atlas was inconvenient; certain leases, certain privateers' arrangements, certain gentlemen whose names appeared nowhere but glowed through everything like watermarks, required the coast to be legally, officially wrong.

Harrow had written his partial report anyway. *No evidence recovered implicating the late master. Investigation continues.* It had cost him something to write *no evidence* when the evidence sat in his drawer smelling faintly of lamp oil and charcoal. It would cost him considerably more, he suspected, whatever he did next.

He was still turning the cup when the door opened and let in the fog.

* * *

She came in the way a stray cat comes into a strange kitchen — low, quick, along the wall, with the child bundled against her chest under a fisherman's coat far too big for either of them. Tamsin Fairweather. Twenty-two years old, perhaps, with lamp-black permanently worked into the creases of her knuckles and the look of someone who had not slept in a week and had decided sleep was a luxury item to be sold.

Harrow did not stand. He did not reach for the pistol he carried more from habit than hope. He simply moved his boot off the chair opposite and said, quietly, "You'll want to close the door. The fog gets into everything."

Tamsin closed the door. Then she stood there with her back against it, breathing hard, and looked at him with an expression he had seen on the faces of people standing at the edge of very high places.

"You followed me," she said.

"No one follows anyone in this fog. God himself couldn't do it." He gestured at the chair again. "Sit down, Miss Fairweather. Or Mr. — I confess I've never learned which the Admiralty's records prefer, since they appear to prefer nothing at all."

"Apprentice," Tamsin said. "The records say apprentice."

"Ah." Something moved behind his ribs at that — a small cold hook of recognition, because he too had spent years being a title rather than a man. *Lieutenant*. A thing you could be promoted out of or hanged for, but never simply *Edmund*. "Then let us dispense with records. Sit down. The child may keep the coat on. It's filthy weather for principles, ours or anyone else's."

The child's face emerged from the collar of the coat: a girl of six or seven, solemn as a Sunday, with charcoal smudged along one jawline as though she had been sleeping pressed to her own drawings. She looked at Harrow without fear and without warmth — the appraising stare of someone cataloguing him, and he had the sudden absurd feeling of being surveyed, sounded, and entered onto a chart under some unflattering notation.

"She doesn't speak," Tamsin said. It was not quite an apology and not quite a warning.

"I know. I've seen her drawings." He paused. "The one she left chalked on your workshop door before you fled — the boat with the broken mast and the three figures wading ashore. Very precise work, for a child."

Tamsin went very still. "You've been inside my workshop."

"I've been inside everyone's workshop, Miss Fairweather. It is the whole of my profession and the better part of my shame." He leaned back. "Now. You have walked through December fog carrying stolen government evidence toward a building guarded by Marines who shoot first and file reports afterward. You cannot get past them. There is no disguise clever enough, no pretext thin enough, no — forgive me — no name respectable enough to open that door. You are an unregistered apprentice of a disgraced master, traveling with an undocumented child, carrying the single most dangerous piece of paper on this coast."

"And yet here I am," Tamsin said, "sitting in your inn, drinking — " she looked at the untouched coffee — "nothing, apparently. So either you're very bad at catching people, Lieutenant, or you wanted me caught."

"That," said Harrow, "is the first intelligent question anyone has asked me in a fortnight."

He took the copy from his breast pocket — the clerk's copy, the greasy string, the brown paper gone soft at the corners from four days of handling — and laid it on the table between them, face up, like a card turned over in a game he was losing.

Tamsin stared at it. He watched the exact moment she understood what it was: not the original sewn into her coat lining, but its twin, its echo, its betrayal made paper. Her hand came halfway across the table and stopped.

"The customs clerk copied it," she said flatly. "Mrs. Quill paid him and he copied it anyway."

"He copies everything. It's how he keeps his position and his mistress and his brandy account all simultaneously solvent." Harrow found, to his mild surprise, that he did not much care anymore who knew the machinery. "It reached me four days ago. I have read it. I know what it proves. I know *who* it proves it against."

"And?"

"And I wrote a report saying I'd found nothing."

The silence stretched. Outside, the fog horn spoke twice, patient as a priest. The child, sensing perhaps that the adults had finally arrived somewhere important, climbed onto the bench beside Tamsin and began, with great deliberation, to draw on the table's surface in the condensation of Harrow's neglected coffee cup — a coastline, quick and sure, a headland and a bay.

"Why?" Tamsin asked. Not accusingly. Genuinely, the way one asks a colleague why a calculation has come out wrong.

"Because if I report what that page shows, I end a career I spent nineteen years building, I implicate three men who can ruin me with a signature, and I become the officer who lost the Admiralty its argument in front of its own Board." He turned the cup again. One rotation. "Because also, Miss Fairweather, eleven men drowned believing their government's chart, and I have their names in my notebook, and one of them was a boy of fifteen called Peter Enys who signed aboard to send money home to his mother, and I have not been able to drink coffee properly since I wrote that sentence."

The child stopped drawing. She looked up at Harrow with those cataloguing eyes, and then — deliberately, unmistakably — she reached out one small finger and completed the coastline: a little harbor, a little cluster of houses, and a church with a spire, drawn in spilled coffee on scarred oak, more accurately than Harrow could have managed with instruments.

"That's Porthenow," he said quietly. "That's where they wrecked."

"Yes," Tamsin said. Her voice had gone rough. "She draws it every day. Sometimes twice."

Harrow looked at the coffee harbor for a long time. When he spoke again, the lieutenant's polish had thinned somewhat; underneath it there was a younger man's voice, the one from before the uniform fit.

"When I was fourteen, my father put me aboard a collier out of Shields. My first crossing, we ran aground on a bar that wasn't on the chart. Nobody died — we were lucky, and lucky is the whole difference between an anecdote and a tragedy — but I remember lying in the hold thinking, *the chart lied. The one true thing, the thing that's supposed to be beyond lying, and it lied.*" He met Tamsin's eyes. "I joined the Admiralty hydrographic service because I believed charts were the last honest documents in England. Do you understand? I didn't join to protect the men who made them lie."

"Then help me," Tamsin said. Simply. No theatrics. She had gambled everything on walking through that door, and now that she was through it she did not waste breath on begging. "Get me in front of the Board. That's all. You don't have to testify, you don't have to sign anything, you don't even have to be seen. Walk me past the guards on the thirtieth as a delivery — maps for the map room, nobody questions a cartographer carrying maps — and I'll lay the page down myself and take whatever comes. All I'm asking is the door, Lieutenant. I'll walk through it alone."

"It isn't alone that concerns me," Harrow said. "It's *which* door. If you go through the front entrance, the Board will never see you; the porters will lose you, and the paper will lose itself, and by spring you'll be a story told in taverns. But—" and here he felt the strange vertigo of a man stepping off a cliff and finding, instead of sea, a staircase— "there is another way. The Board convenes on the thirty-first for the year-end review of the coastal surveys. In the map room. The same map room where your master stood two years ago and was called a liar to his face."

Tamsin's breath caught. "You were there."

"I was the junior officer holding his accuser's papers." He said it evenly, but the words tasted of ash even now. "I held them and I did not read them carefully, because I was twenty-four and trusted, and trust, Miss Fairweather, is the cheapest material in that building. It's used for everything."

The child tugged at Tamsin's sleeve and pointed at the coffee-drawn church spire, then at herself, then made a motion — small hands rising and falling, rising and falling — like bells.

"Bells," Tamsin murmured. "New Year's Eve."

"They ring the old century out at midnight on the thirty-first," Harrow said. "By which time, if we succeed, the Board will already have seen the true survey. And if we fail—" He allowed himself the ghost of a smile, the first in days. "If we fail, we shall at least hear very fine bells while they escort us out."

"This is treason you're describing," Tamsin said. "Or something near enough that the word won't save you."

"It is a delivery of maps," said Harrow, "conducted by a serving officer, in the presence of the Board, pursuant to his duty to ascertain the truth of His Majesty's coastal surveys. I have spent four days finding the sentence that makes it legal, Miss Fairweather. Give me credit; I am very good at sentences. It is the truth I've been worse at." He drew a breath. "I'll need the original. Tonight, so I can study it and prepare the presentation. You'll want it back in your coat lining by morning — I don't ask you to travel unarmed, so to speak. And I'll need one thing more."

"Name it."

"A promise. If the Board does what Boards do — buries it, settles it, buys the silence — you will not let this die. Whatever they offer. Pension, retraction, restoration of the master's name in the ledgers. Take it, all of it, take every farthing — and then finish the atlas anyway. Publish the true plates. Let the honest version exist whether or not the powerful permit it to be acknowledged." He pushed the copy across the table toward her. "Charts outlive governments, Miss Fairweather. It's the only comfort our profession offers. I intend to be on the right side of one of them, once, before I'm done."

For a long moment Tamsin Fairweather — apprentice, foundling, fugitive — looked at him across the ruined table, and Harrow had the distinct sensation of being measured against a coastline and found, narrowly, sound.

"You'll do," she said at last. It was not gratitude. It was something better: acceptance. An entry in the ledger. *Reliable.*

"Magnanimous of you," he said dryly, and then, softer, nodding at the child: "What will you tell her? About tomorrow? She understands more than she says. I suspect she understands a great deal more than any of us."

Tamsin considered. The child had returned to her coffee-harbor, adding waves now, patient little arcs, the sea rendered by someone who had clearly watched it do its worst and respected it anyway.

"I'll tell her the truth in pieces," Tamsin said. "It's what my master did with me. Never a whole frightening thing at once — just enough to hold, and more when you'd grown to carry it." She glanced up. "It's how belonging works, I think. You don't inherit it whole. You get it a piece at a time, from people who decide you're worth the telling."

Harrow looked at his own reflection, faintly, in the cold window glass — a lieutenant-shaped smudge in the fog — and thought of nineteen years of belonging to a uniform that had never once decided *he* was worth the telling.

"Then we had best begin," he said, rising, and held out his hand for the page. "We have two days, one fog, and the last honest document in England to carry through an enemy's door."

Outside, the fog horn sounded again — once now, as if the sea itself had given its answer — and somewhere across the dark city, Mrs. Quill was lighting her lamp and opening her ledger, unaware that the entry she was about to make would matter more than any she had ever recorded.
