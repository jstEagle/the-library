## 18. The Century Turns

### I.

The bells began at half past eleven, and Mrs. Quill heard them the way she heard everything: as an entry in a ledger.

*One hundred years,* she thought, setting down her pen. *A whole century closed, and not one of my accounts balanced by accident.*

She had lived through enough New Year's Eves to know that people made promises on such nights and broke them by Epiphany, but the promise she had made to herself on the fog-bound night Tamsin Fairweather had come pounding at her door — *I will keep them safe until this is done* — was not the sort one broke. It was the sort one charged interest on, which was entirely different.

Across the lane, the workshop windows were lit. She could see the silhouettes: Tamsin bent over the drafting table, and beside it, perched on a stool too tall for them, the child, charcoal in hand, drawing what appeared to be — Mrs. Quill squinted — a dog. Or perhaps a horse with ambitions.

"Madhouse," she said fondly, and put on her shawl.

The knock she gave at their door was the knock of a woman who owned the building and intended that everyone remember it. Tamsin opened it with ink on three fingers and terror nowhere visible, which Mrs. Quill noted with approval. Two days since the Admiralty. Two days since the Board had taken the true survey into its great pale hands, and still no soldiers, no writs, no arrest. Only silence, which in Mrs. Quill's long experience was either the sound of nothing or the sound of something very large turning over in its sleep.

"You'll both come across the lane," she announced. "I have a ham, a bottle of port I've been saving for an occasion worth corrupting me, and a clock that strikes the hour whether God wills it or not."

Tamsin blinked. "Mrs. Quill, are you inviting us to—"

"I am informing you," she corrected. "Invitations can be declined. Information cannot. Come along. Bring the child. Bring the charcoal too; if they're going to draw on my walls they may as well do it where I can supervise."

The child looked up at Tamsin — a look Mrs. Quill had catalogued carefully over the past weeks, the look of someone asking permission for a thing they had already decided to want — and then gathered up their papers with the solemnity of a clerk transporting state secrets.

At the threshold, the child paused and tugged Mrs. Quill's sleeve. She looked down. The child held up a drawing: a woman with a severe bun, holding what was unmistakably a ledger, and beside the figure, in careful letters copied from a shop sign, the word *HOME.*

Mrs. Quill examined it for a long moment through her spectacles.

"Hm," she said, in the voice she reserved for debts she did not intend to collect. "Flattery will get you everywhere. In."

### II.

Lieutenant Harrow spent the last hours of 1799 writing the report that would end his career.

He had begun it four times. The first draft had been honest and short. The second had been honest and longer. The third had been cowardly, and he had burned it in the grate with a satisfaction he was slightly ashamed of. The fourth — the one now drying before him, page after page in his careful service hand — was simply true, arranged in the order the truth required, which meant beginning with the falsified surveys of 1794 and ending with his own signature beneath the words:

*I was shown evidence of these alterations on the twenty-seventh of December last and failed to report it at the first opportunity. I record this failure here so that no part of this account may be mistaken for self-exculpation.*

His pen hovered. Somewhere outside, the bells were warming up, practicing their peals like singers before a performance.

He thought about his father, who had served thirty years and retired with a pension and a portrait nobody looked at. He thought about the map room, and the way the Board members had received Tamsin Fairweather — this ink-stained apprentice, this nobody — with the particular courtesy powerful men extend to things they intend to absorb and forget. And he thought, unexpectedly, of the child's sleeve. Of the small ink stain he had noticed weeks ago in the workshop, and how certain he had been, in that moment, that he held a thread worth pulling — and how the thread had turned out to be attached not to a conspiracy against the Crown but to a conspiracy of the Crown, wearing its own uniform.

The bell above his mantel struck midnight.

Harrow signed his name, blotted it, sealed the report, and addressed it to the First Lord. Then he took out a second sheet of paper, much smaller, and wrote upon it five lines. When he had finished, he read it aloud once, alone, to hear how it sounded in the air.

"My resignation," he said. "Effective immediately following the inquiry."

It sounded, he decided, like the first honest sentence he had spoken in eleven years.

He put on his coat, tucked the report under his arm, and stepped out into the street just as every church in the city let go its voice at once — a hundred bells tumbling over one another, the century dying loudly and the new one arriving without any instructions whatsoever.

A boy ran past him scattering sparks from a bonfire, shouting, "Happy New Year, sir!" Harrow found that he meant it when he answered.

### III.

In the workshop, Tamsin Fairweather heard the bells and did not move from the window.

Below, the lane had emptied into the main street, where bonfires threw orange light against the fog, and people were dancing badly and singing worse, and somewhere a fiddler was losing a war with a drum. The child stood on a chair to see out, chin on the sill, watching the sparks climb.

"One hundred years," Tamsin said quietly. "Master used to say the century would end before the atlas did. He'd stand exactly there—" they touched the worn place on the floorboards, "—and say, *Tamsin, mark me, they'll be celebrating a new age while my coastlines are still wet.*"

The memory arrived without pain, which startled them. For months the master's ghost had sat heavily in every corner of the workshop, reproachful, unfinished. But tonight the room felt differently occupied — as if he had moved from accuser to witness, standing quietly behind the drafting table, waiting to see how the last plate came out.

"He was wrong about one thing," Tamsin told the child. "The atlas will be finished. And he was wrong about another." They hesitated, then said it plainly, because the child deserved plainness even if plainness cost something. "They'll restore his name. That's what the Board promised. Quietly, in the records. Not in the papers. The papers won't print a retraction; retractions don't sell."

The child considered this with the gravity of a magistrate, then drew something quick and pushed it across.

Tamsin looked down. A newspaper page, drawn in charcoal — and across it, in the master's unmistakable hand, the words *THEY WERE WRONG,* underlined twice.

Tamsin laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of them, the first in weeks. "No. No, they won't say it that loudly. But listen—" they crouched to the child's height. "Everyone who sails those waters will use our charts. Every captain, every pilot, every sailor's wife tracing his route by lamplight. They'll never know whose hand drew the lines that keep them off the shoals. But the lines will be true. Isn't that better than shouting?"

The child studied Tamsin's face for a long time, the way they studied coastlines — looking for the features that mattered. Then they nodded once, decisively, and went back to the window, and Tamsin understood that they had just been forgiven something they hadn't known needed forgiving: the wish for applause.

Outside, the bells rolled on. Tamsin pressed a hand flat to the cold glass.

"Happy New Year, Master," they whispered.

### IV.

Widow Fairweather heard the bells from a rented room above a chandler's shop, and they sounded to her like a verdict being read.

Her claim had collapsed that morning, and it had collapsed not with a crash but with a sort of administrative sigh. Lieutenant Harrow — *the turncoat*, she thought, though some treacherous part of her supplied *the honest man* — had presented the Board with testimony regarding the provenance of the atlas: the master's own working notes, in his own hand, naming Tamsin Fairweather as sole inheritor of the work "and all plates thereof, for the work is the apprentice's as much as mine, having been made twice over by those two hands." She had heard the phrase read aloud in that cold paneled room and understood, with the clarity of the truly defeated, that her brother had outmaneuvered her from beyond the grave with a sentence written fifteen years ago.

And then there was the other matter. The matter of the letter she had written to Lieutenant Harrow in November, offering to trade the child's whereabouts. He had read it back to her, gently, horribly gently, in front of the Board, and asked whether she wished to amend her testimony.

She had not answered. There had been nothing to answer with.

Now she sat by the fire with her rings — all that remained portable of the Fairweather name — and listened to the city celebrate a year she would enter owing money and dignity in roughly equal measure. She ought, she supposed, to feel shame. What she felt instead was something more instructive and more painful: the sudden, appalled recognition of how little she had actually wanted the atlas, compared to how much she had wanted to matter to a brother who had never once written to her without a postscript about money.

He had left everything to an apprentice of no family and a grandchild he'd never met, because they had stayed. Because they had *stayed*, through creditors and winter and watchmen and fog, while she had sent lawyers.

The bells crashed toward their final peal. Widow Fairweather took off her rings, set them on the table, and looked at them for a long time.

"New century," she informed the empty room. "New habits."

She did not entirely believe herself. But she wrote the first line of a letter anyway — *To Miss Fairweather, cartographer* — and found, to her surprise, that her hand did not shake.

### V.

The inquiry convened at nine o'clock on the second of January, in the same map room where, six years earlier, Josiah Fairweather had stood accused of negligence that killed fourteen sailors.

Tamsin stood — they had not been offered a chair, and had declined the one Harrow kicked toward them on principle — in nearly the same spot. The room smelled of wax and old varnish and the particular dust of maps that had not been unrolled in decades. Along the walls, charts hung in gilded frames: the official plates, the beautiful, confident, murderous plates with the sandbars drawn safely elsewhere.

Admiral Lord Vessey presided, a man built like a ship's figurehead and about as expressive. He cleared his throat with the gravity of a cannon salute.

"The Board has reviewed the survey recovered by— " a pause, a glance down at his notes, as if the title were unfamiliar, "—Miss Fairweather. And the corroborating testimony of Lieutenant Harrow. The findings are as follows."

What followed took eleven minutes. The 1794 coastal revisions had been ordered by the Hydrographic Office under pressure from the Trinity House interests, who had wished the outer channel kept open on the charts to protect pilotage revenues. Captain Fairweather's original survey, marking the true migration of the Gannel sands, had been suppressed; his public censure, manufactured. Fourteen deaths at the wreck of the *Providence* had been, in the language of the findings, "attributable to official charting error rather than navigational incompetence."

Official charting error. Tamsin watched the phrase travel around the room, softening as it went, the way a stone smooths in a river. Nobody said *falsified.* Nobody said *killed.* The century had turned, but the language had merely changed coats.

Still. Still. The master's name was read back into the record — *Captain Josiah Fairweather, Royal Corps of Coastal Surveyors, exonerated, posthumously commended* — and Tamsin felt the words land somewhere below the ribs, in the place where grief keeps its accounts.

Then Admiral Vessey looked directly at Tamsin for the first time.

"The Board further acknowledges the service rendered by Miss Fairweather in recovering the original survey at considerable personal risk. The Atlas of the Southern Coast will be published under the names of Fairweather and Fairweather." A dry pause. "The late captain having specified the arrangement himself. The Board finds itself, unusually, bound by the wishes of the deceased."

Someone at the far end of the table almost smiled.

"And the child?" Tamsin asked. Their voice sounded small in the big room, and they let it. Smallness, they had learned, was sometimes a weapon. "The granddaughter. She'll have the pension?"

"The widow's pension attaches to the daughter's service record," Vessey said. "The daughter is presumed lost, but presumption does not disqualify a dependent. The Board has ruled the pension payable to the guardian." He shuffled his papers. "That would be you, Miss Fairweather. Until such time as the girl claims it herself."

Until such time as the girl claims it herself. Tamsin turned the sentence over and found it good. Not *the ward.* Not *the dependent.* The girl, claiming it herself, someday, when she had words for what had happened to her.

"Thank you, my lord," Tamsin said. "She'll have it. And the atlas will be finished by Lady Day."

Vessey raised an eyebrow. "Ambitious."

"It's only coastlines, my lord," Tamsin said. "We know where they are now."

This time several people at the table did smile, and one of them, Tamsin noticed, was Lieutenant Harrow, standing at parade rest near the door with the expression of a man attending his own funeral and finding it a relief.

### VI.

Afterward, in the corridor, the resignation was delivered.

Harrow handed the folded paper to the Board secretary with the crisp motion of a man returning borrowed property. Tamsin, gathering their portfolio, watched him do it and felt something complicated move in their chest — gratitude tangled with guilt, because his career had gone into the fire alongside theirs.

"You didn't have to," Tamsin said, when the secretary had gone. "The partial report would have kept you. You could have buried the rest."

"Yes," Harrow agreed. "That was rather the point of not doing it." He adjusted his collar. "Eleven years I told myself the Admiralty's errors were errors — oversights, honest miscalculation. Your page ended that comfort. One doesn't resign over an oversight. One resigns over a decision." He glanced at Tamsin, and for a moment the courteous mask slipped entirely, and underneath was something tired and young. "Your master stood in that room and refused to lie about a coastline. I stood in it for eleven years and lied about considerably less than coastlines, which is somehow worse. The arithmetic is humiliating."

"What will you do?"

Harrow considered. "There's a survey office in Plymouth taking on civilian hands. Charting the western approaches. Poor pay, no rank, no one salutes anyone." The corner of his mouth moved. "I'm told the work is honest."

"They'd be lucky to have you," Tamsin said, and meant it, and was surprised by how easily the meaning came.

They stood a moment in the corridor while somewhere above them the great clocks of the Admiralty marked the hour — the first ordinary hour of the new century, no different from the last ordinary hour of the old one, which was, Tamsin thought, rather the point of hours.

"The child drew you something," Tamsin said suddenly. "Before we left. She said—" they caught themselves, smiling at the correction, "—she *wrote* me that you were to have it. She's begun copying words. Watch out; it's catching."

Harrow unfolded the paper. Charcoal, smudged at the edges where small fingers had gripped too hard: a man in a hat that was unmistakably military, standing beside a door — and the door was open, and light came out of it in straight determined rays, and beneath it, in laborious capitals: *HE LET US IN.*

Harrow looked at it for a long moment. His jaw worked once.

"Tell her," he said finally, folding the paper with exaggerated care and putting it inside his coat, over the pocket where a less sentimental man would keep his orders, "that officers are not permitted to accept bribes." He cleared his throat. "I shall keep it anyway. As evidence."

### VII.

That evening, Mrs. Quill's front room held all of them — Tamsin, the child, a ham reduced to ruins, the port opened at last — and Mrs. Quill presided over it with the satisfaction of a woman watching a particularly satisfying column of figures come out exactly right.

"So," she said, refilling glasses with the economy of a siege commander. "The century's turned, the Board's confessed, the widow's gone off to write letters of apology she'll never send, and the lieutenant's become a civilian. Is that everything? Have we overlooked anyone? I keep a list."

"The list," Tamsin said, "is the most frightening document in England."

"It is," Mrs. Quill agreed placidly, "and your name is in it under *assets.*"

The child, cross-legged on the rug, had covered three sheets of paper already. Tamsin leaned over to look and went very still.

It was the harbor. Their harbor — the workshop lane, the customs house, the masts beyond — but drawn as it might appear from the sea, looking in. And in the foreground, filling the bottom of the page, the open water stretched away in confident charcoal strokes to the very edge of the paper, where the child had stopped, leaving a wide blank margin, and looked up at Tamsin with an expression of immense patience.

As if to say: *the rest is yours. Draw it.*

Tamsin looked at that empty margin for a long moment — at all that clean white space waiting at the edge of a seven-year-old's certainty — and outside, faintly, the evening bells rang the quarter hour, unhurried, indifferent, keeping time for whatever came next.

"Well then," said Mrs. Quill, reaching for the port. "Somebody pass me that pencil. If there's charting to be done in this house, there'll be supervision."
