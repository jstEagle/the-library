## 22. The Open Sea Chart

The letter came on a Tuesday, which was fitting, because Tuesdays were the days Mrs. Quill pretended not to watch the workshop door while watching it very closely indeed.

Tamsin found it wedged under the latch sometime after dawn, its paper heavy and cream-colored, sealed with wax that had been pressed with an anchor and a crown. The postmark was London. Tamsin stood in the doorway holding it for a long moment, feeling its weight, letting the morning air move through the shop behind them — the smell of size and lampblack and spring coming off the harbor in slow grey-green tides.

Behind them, a charcoal pencil scratched.

"Careful with the pressure," Tamsin said without turning around. "You'll snap the point again."

"I am *careful*," said Wren, with the enormous dignity of a seven-year-old — nearly eight now, though they counted the months like coins — who had snapped three points already that week.

Tamsin smiled and broke the seal.

The letter was from the Hydrographic Office. Tamsin read it twice, standing in the doorway, then a third time sitting down on the step, because the second reading made their hands unsteady and it seemed wiser to be seated for the third.

*Sir or Madam* — well, that was a start; somebody in London had done their arithmetic about the signature on the finished atlas and chosen their pronouns accordingly, which was more courtesy than Tamsin had ever received from an official document in twenty-three years. *Having examined the recently completed coastal atlas submitted by your late master's workshop, and having further reviewed the corrected surveys therein, this Office wishes to commission the undersigned cartographer to undertake the charting of newly surveyed waters along the northern coast...*

They wanted Tamsin. Not the workshop. Not Fairweather's name, restored though it now was in every ledger and record that mattered. Not the atlas's reputation, considerable as it had become since the inquiry. They wanted the hand that had drawn it.

*It is understood that you are guardian to a young person of remarkable observational gifts.* Tamsin laughed out loud at that — someone at the Admiralty had heard about the child who'd testified to the Board with nothing but a sheaf of charcoal drawings and a silence no grown man could argue with. *Arrangements may be made for both parties to travel.*

Both parties. As if Wren were a piece of equipment to be shipped along with the instruments. And yet — Tamsin read the sentence again — as if Wren were expected. As if the Admiralty, that vast cold building where a small person had once stood in a large room and refused to be small, understood perfectly well that the two of them came as a set.

"Is it bad?" Wren asked, appearing at Tamsin's shoulder with the particular stealth of a child trained by circumstance never to startle anyone from the front.

"It's good," Tamsin said. "It might be the best letter I've ever gotten."

"Better than the one from the widow?"

"The widow's letter only apologized. This one wants to pay me."

Wren considered this. "Then it's better."

"Infinitely better." Tamsin held the page up so the morning light came through it. "They want us to go north. To chart waters nobody's properly mapped. Weeks on a survey vessel. Maybe the whole summer."

Wren's eyes went wide — and then, Tamsin noticed, did something complicated: widened, then flicked toward the workshop, toward the drying racks and the plate press and the corner where the cot still stood, then back to Tamsin's face, checking, always checking, whether the ground was about to shift.

"You'd come," Tamsin said. "Obviously."

"Obviously," Wren repeated, testing the word, and something in the small shoulders let down half an inch.

That was the thing about belonging, Tamsin thought, watching the child go back to the drawing table with a lighter tread. It wasn't a thing you were given once and kept forever. It was a thing you re-gave, daily, in small obvious sentences. *You'd come. Obviously.* Like watering a plant. Like winding a clock. You could never say it too many times.

* * *

Mrs. Quill arrived within the hour, which surprised nobody. She had a sixth sense for mail, the way some people had a sense for weather, and her arrival across the lane was preceded by the sound of her front door closing with what she probably believed was casualness.

She swept into the workshop on the pretext of returning a borrowed ruler — a ruler Tamsin had lent her in February and which had been returned, borrowed again, and re-returned eleven times since, until the transaction had become less a matter of stationery than of ritual.

"So," she said, setting the ruler down precisely parallel to the desk edge. "London writes."

"How—"

"Tamsin. I have lived opposite this door for nine years. I know the weight of your post by the sound it makes on the floorboards." She peered at the letter. "Cream stock. Anchor seal. That's Hydrographic. That's money."

"That's a commission," Tamsin admitted. "North coast. Whole season."

Mrs. Quill went very still in the way she did when she was calculating something important, her lips moving slightly, running figures. Tamsin knew those figures. They were the same ones Tamsin had been running all morning.

"You'd close the shop," Mrs. Quill said.

"For the season. Perhaps longer."

"And the child would go with you."

"That was in the letter. In writing. Both parties."

"Hm." Mrs. Quill removed her spectacles, polished them on her apron, replaced them, and looked at Tamsin over the top of them anyway, which was her method of seeing more clearly than spectacles ever allowed. "And what am I meant to do with myself all summer? Watch your windows for creditors who no longer exist?"

"You could come," Tamsin said — because the thought had been forming since the third reading, half-formed and ridiculous, and it came out now before caution could catch it.

Mrs. Quill stared.

"As cartographer's assistant," Tamsin went on, warming to it, watching the idea take shape on Mrs. Quill's face like a coastline emerging from fog. "Someone has to keep the ledgers. Someone has to negotiate with ship's captains, and I have watched you do that, Mrs. Quill, and frankly no captain alive could stand against you. Someone has to make sure we eat anything besides biscuit and salt pork for four months."

"I am sixty-one years old."

"You're fifty-eight and everyone knows it."

"I have a house."

"Mrs. Hobbs next to you would fight a duelist for the chance to keep your cats."

"I have *cats.*"

"They travel beautifully. Wren will carry the basket. Won't you, Wren?"

From the drawing table, without looking up: "I already asked them. Duchess says yes."

"Duchess cannot speak."

"Duchess communicated," Wren said serenely, and went on shading a headland.

Mrs. Quill opened her mouth. Closed it. Took off her spectacles again and polished them far longer than any lens required, and when she put them back on, her eyes were bright in a way she would have denied under torture.

"Well," she said briskly. "Somebody will have to mind the accounts. Heaven knows you'd sign away your instruments to the first flatterer who called your work elegant." She straightened her shawl with a decisive tug. "I shall want a cabin with a window. A real window, not one of those portholes they give to luggage. And I shall want it understood that I am not a nursemaid, nor a cook, nor a—"

"Cartographer's assistant," Tamsin said. "In writing, if you like. I'll draw up the terms tonight."

"In writing," Mrs. Quill agreed, and then, quieter, almost to herself, looking around the workshop — at the plates, the press, the cot in the corner, the whole improbable household that had assembled itself out of grief and stubbornness and a child left on a doorstep — "Well. Isn't that something."

It was, Tamsin thought. It really was.

* * *

That evening, after supper — Mrs. Quill stayed, ostensibly to discuss terms, actually to eat the pie Tamsin had bought specifically on the assumption that she would stay — Tamsin unlocked the flat drawer beneath the main desk and took out the last unfinished map.

It had hung over the workshop like a held breath for eighteen months. Master Fairweather's final work: a chart of the open approaches north of the Hebrides, begun in his own hand and abandoned at the exact place where the known coast ended and the blank paper began. Tamsin had found it the week after the funeral, rolled in oilcloth behind the plate cabinet, and had never been able to finish it. Every other plate in the atlas had gone out signed — *drawn by M. Fairweather, completed by T. Fairweather, cartographer*, the two names joined by ink and choice rather than blood. But this one Tamsin had left alone, because finishing it felt like closing a book, and Tamsin hadn't been ready for the book to close.

Now, with the letter lying on the desk beside it and the smell of pie still in the room and Mrs. Quill's voice going on comfortably about cabin dimensions in the background, Tamsin spread the chart out under the lamp and looked at the empty horizon for a long time.

The coast stopped. Beyond it, nothing: clean white paper waiting, faintly gridded, patient as snowfall. Somewhere out in that nothing were shoals and soundings and islands that no honest chart had ever recorded — waters the old falsified maps had simply invented, because inventing them had been cheaper than sailing them. That was how the whole ruin had started. That was why Master Fairweather was dead, and why his name had needed restoring, and why a child had once been left on a doorstep with instructions to forget a face.

The blank space wasn't emptiness, Tamsin realized. It never had been. It was a question. And questions were what Tamsin had been apprenticed to answer since the age of ten, standing on this very stool, holding this very pen, belonging to nobody and therefore — eventually, unexpectedly, entirely — to everybody in this room.

"What is it?" Wren asked, climbing onto the neighboring stool. Wren always asked, even though Wren had seen the chart a dozen times. Asking was part of the ritual, the way the ruler's travels were, the way *you'd come, obviously* was. Rituals were how this family prayed.

"The last one he started," Tamsin said. "And the first one I'll finish my own way."

"His lines are better than yours," Wren offered, with a seven-year-old's gift for honesty.

"His coastlines, certainly. His letters were a disgrace. Look at that 'N' — it looks like it's fleeing the page."

Wren leaned in and studied the offending letter gravely. "It does look frightened."

"Exactly. Now watch." Tamsin dipped the pen. "This is how you finish a dead man's map. You don't copy him. You *answer* him. You draw the water the way it truly lies, and you sign it plain, and you trust that somewhere he'd rather have your truth than his ghost."

Tamsin touched the nib to the blank paper, just past the edge of the known coast, and drew the first line of the open sea.

It was a small line. A single confident stroke heading north, toward everything unmapped. But as it went down, Tamsin felt the strange lightness that comes when a long-held breath finally leaves the body — the sense not of leaving but of extending, the way a root extends, carrying the tree's whole history into new soil. The workshop would be shuttered till autumn. Mrs. Quill would complain about the cabin window and secretly love every day of it. Wren would fill sketchbooks with coastlines no adult had thought to look for. And Tamsin would stand at a rail with the wind off honest water, drawing home ahead of them, mile by mile, because home had turned out to be a thing you carried, not a place you kept.

"There," Tamsin said, lifting the pen. "Started."

Wren studied the line. Then picked up a stub of charcoal, leaned over the great careful chart with total disregard for protocol, and added — just past the last graticule, small and sure and unmistakable — a tiny sailing boat, headed the same direction.

"Wren. That is an official working document."

"It's a *dedication*," Wren said. "Like the last one."

And because there was no arguing with that — because there never had been, from the very first charcoal coastline onward — Tamsin laughed, and reached for another sheet, and began to write out the terms of employment for one Mrs. Quill, cartographer's assistant, window guaranteed, cats included.

Outside, the spring tide was coming up the harbor, filling the dark with the sound of water going somewhere it had decided, long ago, to go.
