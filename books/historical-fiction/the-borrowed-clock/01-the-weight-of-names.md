## 1. The Weight of Names

The candle had burned down to a stub the width of a thumb, and Ulyana Iversen decided — as he did every night, and as he would decide again tomorrow night — that this was the last page he could honestly see by.

He wrote anyway.

*Anselm Kroll, private, 4th Reserve. Left thigh. Died at half past two in the morning, asking whether his boots had been found.*

The nib scratched. Outside, the wind coming off the marshes rattled the shutters of what had once been a granary and was now, by military decree, Dressing Station Number Fourteen. The wounded slept — those who could sleep — in three long rows on straw pallets, their breathing a tide that rose and fell against the silence. Somewhere near the stove, Corporal Voss snored with the untroubled conviction of a man who believed himself immortal.

Ulyana blew on the ink, waited for it to dull from black to grey, and turned back one page to check a spelling. *Kroll.* Yes. He had it right the first time.

It was a strange library he kept, and he knew it. The official ledger of Station Fourteen lived on the surgeon's desk: a fat government book with printed columns, filled each morning in Major's-hand handwriting by Captain Ehlers, who had survived four years at the front by mastering the art of writing nothing that could be held against him later. Ehlers's ledger counted men the way a grain merchant counts sacks. Admitted. Treated. Evacuated. Returned to duty. And occasionally, in a small tidy hand that never trembled:

*Died of wounds.*

Ulyana's ledger counted something else entirely.

He had begun it in the spring, though if he were honest with himself — and the candlelight hour was the honest hour — it had begun before that, in fragments, on the backs of ration cards and the margins of bandage wrappers, wherever paper could be found and stolen without anyone mourning its loss. It had begun because of a boy named Piet Aalders, who had come through the station in April with shrapnel in his belly and had died on the second day without ever regaining consciousness, and whom no one could identify because his identification disc had been traded, sometime before, for a tin of sardines. There had been no name to send home. There had been a photograph instead, folded in his breast pocket: a girl on a bicycle, squinting into the sun, laughing at whatever the camera said. On the back, in pencil gone soft with handling: *For my Anke, wait for me.*

There had been no one to write to. No name, no regiment confirmed, nothing. Captain Ehlers had entered him as *Unknown, male, approx. 19.* And then the body had been carried out, and the war had rolled over the space where Piet Aalders had been, like a cart wheel over a puddle.

That night Ulyana had written, in a hand steadier than he felt: *A boy with a girl on a bicycle. He laughed at her once, probably every day. Let someone know he existed.*

Since then there were forty-three pages.

He did not think of it as rebellion. Rebellion required energy, organization, a banner — none of which Ulyana possessed after fourteen hours of tending wounds. What he did was smaller and, he suspected, more durable. When a man died at the station, Ulyana asked questions. Where was your home? Who is waiting? What did you do before all this? Soldiers, even dying ones, mostly liked being asked. It proved they were still people and not merely injuries with numbers. The surgeons thought his questioning was bedside manner. In a sense it was. In another sense it was an archive under construction, hidden in the false bottom of a biscuit tin beneath the floorboard under his cot.

"Still awake, Nurse Iversen?"

The whisper came from the third row. Ulyana closed the ledger on his finger and looked up. Old Halvorsen — not old, really, only fifty-two, but the war aged everyone past their arithmetic — was propped on one elbow, his bandaged arm cradled like a loaf of bread.

"I'm disturbing you," Ulyana said.

"You're disturbing me by stopping. It's better than the chaplain. At least when you talk to them, they answer." Halvorsen lowered himself carefully. "Who is it tonight?"

"A private named Kroll."

"The one who kept worrying about his boots?"

"That's the one."

Halvorsen was quiet a moment. "His sergeant brought the boots in this morning. Muddy as anything, but whole. Set them by his pallet and told him they'd be waiting." He sighed. "Funny thing to want, your own boots."

"It isn't the boots," Ulyana said. "It's the going somewhere in them. Home, most likely."

"Hm." Halvorsen considered this with the seriousness he gave all philosophy after dark. Then: "Put down that he had good teeth. He showed me. Said it was the one part of him the army hadn't ruined."

Ulyana smiled in the dark, opened the ledger again, and added beneath the entry: *Good teeth. Proud of them.*

This was how the work went, night after night. Not grand. Not dangerous, even — or so he told himself. Just a man with a candle and a stubborn belief that a person was more than a column entry, and that the difference between *Died of wounds* and *Anselm Kroll, who had good teeth and worried about his boots,* was the entire distance between an army and a human race.

* * *

Morning came grey and smelling of iodine. Ulyana washed his hands in water cold enough to make prayer feel redundant, tied his apron, and took up his place at the long trestle where the new arrivals were triaged. The ambulance convoy was late, which meant it would be full; lateness at the front was never a mercy, only congestion.

Beside him worked Sister Adele, a Rigan woman built like a dock crane and possessed of a humor so dry that new doctors often mistook it for hostility until they learned better.

"You've ink on your ear," she said by way of greeting.

"Do I?"

"You always have ink on your ear. You should embrace it. Wear it as a badge. *Here stands a man who writes letters after hours.*" She snapped a sheet taut over the trestle. "What were you doing last night, anyway? I saw your candle still burning past two."

"Correspondence," Ulyana said.

"With whom? Your sweetheart?"

"With history," said Ulyana, "though we are not yet on intimate terms."

Adele snorted and turned to greet the first stretcher.

The morning was exactly as bad as predicted. Eighteen men off the convoy, two of them beyond helping and one of those a boy of seventeen who had lied about his age so badly that his mother's address was written on his collar in a schoolboy's careful script. Ulyana held his hand while the surgeon worked, and afterward, when there was nothing left to hold, he sat with him anyway and learned that his name was Tomas and that he had wanted to be a bell-ringer, of all things, because his uncle had been one and had let him pull the rope twice.

"Bells carry," Tomas said, in the drifting voice of the very tired. "You can be heard a long way off. That seemed — " he searched — "worthwhile."

"It is worthwhile," Ulyana said. "I'll remember it."

Tomas lived. He was carried to the ward with his arm bound and his future as a bell-ringer intact, and Ulyana made a note to visit him that evening, partly for medicine and partly because he intended to ask about the bells. Some entries went into the ledger alive. Those were rarer, and he treasured them.

Captain Ehlers arrived at noon with his fountain pen and his government book, and performed the daily ritual of counting.

"Admissions?"

"Eighteen."

"Deaths?"

"Two."

Ehlers wrote *2* in the printed box with a flourish, as though the number reflected well on the station's efficiency. He was not a monster, Ulyana reminded himself for perhaps the nine-hundredth time. Ehlers had a wife in Königsberg and a habit of secretly giving his own cigarette ration to men who shook too badly to hold theirs steady. But he wrote *2*, and the *2* would travel up through channels, growing smoother and more abstract at every desk, until somewhere far behind the lines two men became a statistic became a rounding error became nothing at all.

"Ehlers," Ulyana said, as the captain closed the book. "The boy this morning. Seventeen, lied about his age, collar says his mother's name. Will he go in the report by name?"

Ehlers paused. His pen hovered. "If he'd died, you mean."

"Yes."

"He'd have gone in as unidentified until the records section sorted him out. They'll sort him out eventually. That's what the records section is *for*, Iversen."

"Eventually," Ulyana agreed. "And in the meantime he's a number."

"In the meantime he's alive, thanks to your table there, so I suggest you worry about living boys and leave the paperwork to the paperwork." Ehlers said it kindly, which somehow made it worse. He tucked the ledger under his arm and left, and Sister Adele appeared at Ulyana's shoulder like weather.

"You argued with him again."

"I didn't argue. I asked a question."

"You asked a question the way a crow asks for bread. Persistently. In front of witnesses." She handed him a basin. "One of these days somebody important is going to notice that you keep asking where all the dead men's names have gone, and they're going to wonder why a nurse cares."

"Somebody has to care," Ulyana said. "It's in the job description. Small print."

"There is no small print in a nurse's job description."

"No," Ulyana admitted. "We had to add it ourselves."

* * *

The afternoon brought lulls, and lulls were for the wards. Ulyana made his rounds with the medicine tray and the conversation tray, the latter heavier. He checked Tomas's bandage and heard about the church in Daugavpils where the great bell had cracked in 1909 and the whole town had contributed to recasting it, even the Jews and the Orthodox, even the tavern keeper who was famously tight-fisted, and how the day the new bell rang everyone had cried including the tavern keeper, especially the tavern keeper. This story had no strategic value whatsoever. Ulyana listened to all of it.

Near the window lay a lieutenant from a regiment Ulyana didn't recognize, asleep under morphine, his uniform caked with dried mud of a color that wasn't local — darker, almost black, peat-colored. Ulyana noticed such things now. He had become a connoisseur of mud. Every road east of here had its own shade, and a nurse learned to read them the way sailors read water.

The lieutenant woke toward dusk, feverish, disoriented, gripping Ulyana's wrist with sudden strength.

"Where — what station is this?"

"Fourteen. Near Riga. You're safe; you've been operated on; the leg will keep."

The lieutenant stared at him, and something in the stare was wrong — not the usual fog of morphine but a sharp, calculating fear underneath it. His eyes went around the room, counting exits, counting faces, cataloguing. Ulyana had seen that look before, but only in officers carrying something heavier than rank.

"My effects," the lieutenant said. "My coat. Where is my coat?"

"In the effects room, logged and locked. Nothing gets lost here."

Something in the man's shoulders came down half an inch. "Good," he said. "Good. That's — " He stopped. Looked at Ulyana properly for the first time, taking in the apron, the rank badge of a mere senior nurse. Whatever calculation he performed, it ended with him deciding Ulyana was furniture. "Water," he said, and turned his face to the wall.

Ulyana gave him water. He also noted the regiment, the date, the peat-dark mud, and the fact that the man had been brought in clutching a leather dispatch case that the stretcher-bearers swore had been welded to his hands — a case which, when the effects were inventoried that evening, contained nothing but a change of socks and a broken pocket watch.

Someone had emptied that case between the front and the ward. Or someone had met the lieutenant on the road and taken what mattered out of it, and the lieutenant knew it, and was afraid of who had done it.

Ulyana stood in the effects room holding the broken watch, turning it over. On its case-back, engraved in worn script, were two initials and a word:

*V.V. — Krasnogorsk.*

Krasnogorsk. He knew the name the way you know a word from childhood — not its meaning, but its weight. Elena Vasiliev had grown up in Krasnogorsk. Elena, who had shared a garden fence with him through three summers of a peace that felt permanent, who had once traded him her entire collection of river stones for his umbrella because, as she reasoned at seven years old, rain came free but stones had to be dug. He had not seen her since before the war redrew every map between here and there, but her letters had come, thinner each year, and the last one had said only: *They are calling us enemy now. Can you believe the sky has changed sides?*

Ulyana put the watch in the drawer, logged it, and stood a moment in the cold little room with its shelves of dead men's belongings.

Then he went and did his rounds, and ate his soup, and waited, as he always did, for the candle hour.

* * *

At midnight, the lieutenant's fever climbed. By one o'clock it had become the kind that talks. Ulyana sat with him, cooling his forehead, while the man pleaded with people who weren't there — with a colonel named Brandt, apparently, pleading forgiveness, pleading patience, saying *Thursday, I can get it to you Thursday, don't send Reinholt, anyone but Reinholt* — and then, near three, the fever broke all at once, like a rope cut, and the lieutenant slept the flat sleep of the emptied.

Ulyana sat in the dark listening to the ward breathe.

*Don't send Reinholt.* He filed the name away without knowing yet why it would matter. He was tired. The candle was down to its last thumb-width, and the ledger lay open on his knees, waiting for tonight's entries: Tomas the bell-ringer, alive; the lieutenant from the peat country, alive and strange; and beneath them, carried over from yesterday, the unfinished business of a world that kept losing track of its own people.

He dipped the pen.

Outside, far off toward the lines, a gun spoke once — a single cough, then nothing, the war clearing its throat. The candle flame leaned and straightened.

Ulyana bent over the page and began, as he did every night, to write down the names.

He did not yet know that within the week, one of those names would be written in wax-sealed ink and pressed into his hands by a dying man; that the small ledger under his floorboard would grow heavy enough to shake an army; that the word *Reinholt* would stop being a feverish murmur and become a man walking through the door of Station Fourteen with a smile and a list. All of that was coming, the way winter comes — invisibly, then all at once.

Tonight there was only the candle, and the names, and the quiet dignity of refusing to forget.

He wrote on.
