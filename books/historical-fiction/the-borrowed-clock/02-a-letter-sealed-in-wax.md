## 2. A Letter Sealed in Wax

The shelling stopped an hour past midnight, and in the silence that followed Ulyana Iversen could hear the things he preferred not to: the wind worrying the canvas of the ward tents, a mule stamping somewhere near the supply wagons, and beneath all of it, from the corner cot by the stove, the particular wet rattle of a lung filling up with blood.

He had heard it enough times to know what it meant. The body announces its departures honestly, if you listen. Men lie about everything else — how they were wounded, whether they're afraid, whether they have someone waiting — but the body tells the truth at the end without any embarrassment at all.

Ulyana finished writing the name he was writing — Private Anselm Kroll, twenty-two years old, killed carrying water, which was the sort of detail no report would ever contain — and blotted the page with his sleeve. Then he took the candle and went to sit with Colonel Brandt.

"You should be sleeping," Ulyana said.

"I am sleeping." Brandt's eyes opened halfway. He was sixty-one years old and looked tonight like a man carved out of something that had once been oak and had since been left out in the weather. "This is me asleep. You've never seen me asleep, so you don't know the difference."

"I've seen you asleep. At Riga you fell asleep sitting upright in a chair during my lecture on gangrene."

"That wasn't sleep. That was strategic rest."

"It was snoring."

Brandt smiled, which cost him something — Ulyana watched the effort move through his chest like a stone dropped down a well — and then the smile faded into the business of breathing. The wound was a bad one, taken three days ago at the river crossing: a shell fragment low in the abdomen that the surgeons had dug for and not found, or found and wished they hadn't. There was nothing left to do for it. Ulyana had known that from the first hour, and Brandt had known it too, because colonels always knew, and so they had entered together into that unspoken arrangement doctors and dying men make — no false comfort, no wasted morphine, just company.

Ulyana set the candle on the crate beside the cot and began, out of habit, checking the dressing.

"Leave it," Brandt said. "It's fine. Sit down. I want to talk to you, and I can only talk while you're sitting still. When you hover, I feel like a horse being appraised."

Ulyana sat. The stool creaked. Somewhere beyond the canvas, the mule stamped again, and a sentry told it, wearily, to shut up.

"Do you remember," Brandt said, "when you first came to this station? In the spring?"

"April. It was snowing in April."

"It's always snowing somewhere in April. It's the calendar's way of keeping us humble. You arrived covered in mud to the eyebrows and the first thing you did was correct my Latin."

"Your Latin was wrong."

"My Latin was *regional*."

"Vos was not the plural used in your sentence, Colonel."

"And there it is again." Brandt closed his eyes, and for a moment Ulyana thought he'd drifted off, but his voice came again, quieter now, stripped of the play. "You were born here, weren't you. In these parts. Before your family moved."

"Krasnogorsk. Forty kilometers east, a little south."

"The Vasilievs."

The name landed in the tent like a coin dropped on a floor. Ulyana kept his face still, the way you learn to keep your face still in a dressing station, where every flinch gets read as news.

"You know them," Brandt said. It was not quite a question.

"Everyone knows everyone in a town that size. Elena Vasiliev and I grew up on the same lane. She taught me to skate badly and to swim worse. Her mother made bread that I think about more than is entirely dignified for a man of my profession." He paused. "Why does a colonel from two hundred miles west know the Vasilievs of Krasnogorsk?"

Brandt was quiet long enough that the candle flame had time to lean and straighten twice.

"Because I grew up there too," he said finally. "Before any of this. My father managed the mill account for old Vasiliev. I haven't been back in thirty years, and now I find I'm going back the way everyone goes back eventually, which is not at all." He turned his head with visible effort and looked at Ulyana fully. "And because three weeks ago I signed a requisition report that I should have burned instead."

Ulyana did not say anything. In his experience, men who had decided to talk needed the silence more than the words. He poured a little water, held it to Brandt's lips, waited.

"They are going to hang it on the Vasilievs," Brandt said. "What happened with the winter convoys. The grain that never arrived, the boots that were straw-and-sawdust by February, the coal sold twice over before it ever reached the depot. Someone stole a winter, Ulyana. A whole winter, meant for whole regiments. And when the questions start — and the questions will start, because dead men's families ask questions even when governments don't — someone very high up has already chosen who will be standing under them when the answer comes down."

"The Vasilievs supply the eastern garrisons," Ulyana said slowly. "Their contracts—"

"Their contracts, their warehouses, their signatures on manifests they never saw. It's elegant, really. A family with foreign blood, in a border town, with property worth seizing. Enemy-adjacent, as our current language likes to put it. By spring they'll be traitors, by summer their house will belong to some general's cousin, and everyone will shake their heads and say what a shame, one never can tell about those people." Brandt's breath caught; he rode through it, jaw set. "Except one never *can* tell about those people, and what one can tell is that Anton Vasiliev counted sacks of grain like a monk counts prayers, and there is no version of God's green earth on which that man signed away a regiment's boots."

"How do you know all this?"

"Because I was asked to help arrange it." Brandt said it flatly, the way a man reports the weather. "A letter came through channels. Polite channels. Channels with wax seals. Asking me, as a former son of the town, to confirm certain irregularities in Vasiliev's wartime conduct — irregularities I would be well compensated for confirming. And I sat at my desk for two days with the letter in front of me, and I thought about Elena Vasiliev teaching you to skate, though she'll have forgotten me entirely, and I thought about what I would say at whatever accounting they give us at the end of all this, and then I burned the polite letter and started keeping a different kind of record."

He reached, with a hand that trembled not from fear but from weakness, beneath the blanket, and brought out a packet wrapped in oilcloth and tied with cord. It was thicker than a letter. It had the weight of a ledger.

"Names," Brandt said. "Dates. Convoy numbers. Copies of requisitions that contradict each other in instructive ways. The signature of a man signing for stores in two towns on the same morning, forty miles apart, which is a difficult thing to do honestly. Everything I could gather before—" He gestured vaguely at his own middle, at the fragment doing its slow work there. "Before the army reassigned me."

Ulyana took the packet. It was heavier than paper should be. He thought of his own ledger, hidden in the bottom of his kit bag, the names written small and neat against the day someone might ask.

"There's a letter inside the wrapping," Brandt said. "Sealed. For Elena. Not the tribunal, not the commandant, not the church — Elena. She's the spine of that family and always was, even as a girl; her brother got the height and she got the iron. She'll know what to do with the rest of it. Swear to me you'll put it in her hands."

"Colonel—"

"No." Brandt's voice cracked out with the last of the parade-ground in it, and the sentry outside shifted his weight. Then it softened. "No. Let me finish, because I have a limited quantity of finishing left. I know what I'm asking. I know you're a nurse at a forward station with a war on both sides of him and paperwork enough to bury a smaller man. I'm asking anyway. I have no right, and I'm asking anyway. That is what being old is. You spend your rights early and end up begging."

"You're not begging."

"I absolutely am. Look at me." He did look terrible; the candle was honest about such things. "I am a dying man begging a favor, and the favor is this: that a promise made in peacetime should hold in wartime. I heard you speak of her once — last autumn, the night of the bombardment, you were holding a boy's artery closed with your thumb and telling him stories about home to keep him conscious, and you mentioned a girl named Elena who could whistle through her hands. Nobody mentions peacetime people that warmly unless the warmth is real. So. Elena Vasiliev. Krasnogorsk. Her hands. Swear it."

Outside, the wind pushed at the tent walls, and the mule stamped, and far off — or perhaps imagined, which amounted to the same thing at night — the guns cleared their throats once and fell quiet again.

Ulyana looked down at the oilcloth packet in his lap. He thought about the road east in December. He thought about the word *desertion*, which had a shape, a cold rectangular shape, like a coffin lid. He thought about Elena whistling through her hands on the frozen pond, laughing at him as he went down on his knees into the snow for the fourth time in an afternoon.

He thought about the fact that none of those thoughts changed anything, and that he had known they wouldn't from the moment Brandt said *Krasnogorsk*, the way a man knows, hearing the wet rattle from the corner cot, exactly what the body has decided.

"If I do this," Ulyana said, "I do it properly. I don't hand it to a courier and hope. Her hands. As you said."

"Her hands."

"Then I swear it. On my mother's grave and on my own Latin, which is better than yours and therefore a more reliable oath."

Brandt laughed — a short, ruined sound, but a laugh — and closed his eyes, and for a moment he looked thirty years younger and thirty years more tired simultaneously, which Ulyana supposed was simply what honesty looked like on a face.

"There's a second matter," Brandt said, quieter now, the words coming slower, spaced out like a man walking on ice. "The man who wrote the polite letter. He signs himself as a major doing inventory work. Reinholt. He's thorough, Ulyana. Thoroughness is his religion and efficiency is his hymnal, and he is very good at noticing things. Things like nurses who write at night. Things like nurses who ask after the dead." His eyes opened. "When he comes — and he comes to stations like this the way winter comes, inevitably — be dull. Be gloriously, magnificently dull. Nod at the correct intervals. Have opinions about linen supplies. Do not be interesting. Interesting men get followed."

"You're giving me a great deal of advice for a dying man."

"Dying men give the best advice. We have no reputation left to protect, so we tell the truth." He fumbled weakly at the oilcloth knot. "Take it out of my sight. If I fall asleep holding it, I'll dream I still have to deliver it, and I've done my marching." He paused, and something gentler crossed the wrecked landscape of his face. "Tell her — no. Don't tell her anything about me. She'll have forgotten me. Better she remembers the town as it was, with all of us in it, before we learned to count things."

Ulyana rose, tucked the packet inside his coat, against his chest, where it sat with the strange warm heaviness of live things.

At the tent flap he stopped. "Colonel. The bread her mother made. What kind was it?"

Brandt smiled at the canvas ceiling. "Dark rye. With caraway. Slightly burnt at one edge, always the same edge, because old Vasiliev insisted the oven ran cold on the left and refused on principle to have it fixed." His voice was going soft and distant now, sliding toward sleep or toward whatever lies under sleep. "Forty years and I can still taste it. You see? That's why it matters who remembers. That's the entire war, right there. They steal the bread, and we keep the taste."

Ulyana stood a moment longer in the dark doorway with the packet against his heart, then let the flap fall behind him.

* * *

Brandt died at dawn, in the grey half-hour when the lamps burn yellow and useless and the wounded stir and the dead are carried out quietly so the living won't count. Ulyana closed his eyes, folded his hands on his chest, and wrote the entry in the ledger by candle stub: *Colonel Edmund Brandt, 61. Mill accounts. Dark rye, caraway, burnt on the left. Kept his word.* There was no column in any official form for any of it, which was precisely why the ledger existed.

By eight o'clock the station had resumed its machinery — stretchers in, stretchers out, the surgeons' saws keeping their horrible metronome — and Ulyana had sewn the oilcloth packet into the lining of his coat with the small neat stitches he usually saved for skin, working the needle with his head bent, humming, looking for all the world like a man doing nothing more remarkable than mending a torn pocket.

Which was when the staff car came up the road through the mud, flying the pennant of the quartermaster corps, and stopped at the gate with the precision of a signature on a line.

A major stepped out — tall, dry-eyed, gloves immaculate despite the season — and stood surveying the dressing station the way a man surveys a warehouse he intends to count down to the nail.

"Major Reinholt," announced the driver unnecessarily.

The major's gaze traveled across the yard, unhurried, itemizing. Tents. Wagons. Stovepipes. And then, briefly, resting on the figure by the entrance with the needle and thread — a nurse, bent over his mending, humming.

Ulyana hummed on. Dull as linen. Gloriously, magnificently dull.

But his heart, beneath the sewn-in letter, had begun — quietly, professionally, like a man taking another man's pulse — to race.
