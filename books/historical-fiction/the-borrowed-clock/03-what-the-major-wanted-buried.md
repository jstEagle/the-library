## 3. What the Major Wanted Buried

The major arrived on a Tuesday, which felt fitting, because Tuesdays at the dressing station were always the days when something went wrong that nobody had planned for.

Ulyana heard the motorcar before he saw it — a sound so alien to the muddy lane outside Riga that the orderlies stopped stacking firewood just to stare. The car was black and immaculate, which meant it had been washed somewhere far from the front, and it ground to a halt in a spray of half-frozen slush directly beside the latrine trench, an accident of geography that Ulyana would treasure privately for weeks.

Major Reinholt stepped out as though he had not noticed. That was the first thing Ulyana learned about him: he noticed everything, and pretended he hadn't, and you were meant to understand both facts at once.

"Which of you is Iversen?" the major asked, in the tone of a man asking which of you has misplaced my gloves.

"Iversen," said Ulyana, wiping his hands on his apron out of habit rather than need. His hands were never clean. Nobody's were.

The major looked him over the way a horse trader looks over a purchase with a suspected limp. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples in a way that seemed maintained rather than earned, and he wore his coat as if mud had personally insulted him. Behind him, two staff officers unloaded a leather portfolio, a portable typewriter, and — this detail Ulyana would turn over in his mind for days — a small brass scale, the kind used for weighing letters.

"Colonel Brandt's station," Reinholt said. It was not a question, but it wasn't quite a statement either; it was the kind of sentence that waited to see what you would do with it.

"The Colonel's station," Ulyana agreed. "He's sleeping. He took a wound yesterday — through the shoulder. I've dressed it twice."

"Then you'll wake him when it's convenient," the major said, and smiled, and the smile did not reach anywhere near his eyes, which stayed on Ulyana's face with the patience of a fisherman watching a bobber.

* * *

The inventory began immediately, and it was, in its way, more frightening than any bombardment Ulyana had lived through, because bombardments at least announced themselves.

Reinholt's clerks went through everything. Bandages counted by the roll. Morphine tablets counted by the tablet, each one logged against a serial number. The stove was inspected. The candles were counted — *the candles*, forty-one of them, and Ulyana watched a young clerk write "41" in a ledger of his own and thought, absurdly, *he counts them too, but for different reasons.* The clerk recorded names as well, but only the names of objects. There was something in that which made Ulyana's chest ache in a way he couldn't quite name.

"You're the one who writes things down," Reinholt said that evening. He had appeared in the doorway of the supply room where Ulyana was re-sorting dressings by size, a task that required no thought and therefore left all of Ulyana's thoughts unsupervised.

"I keep records, sir. As instructed."

"As instructed." The major let the words sit a moment, like a spoon in tea. "Brandt tells me you asked him about the men from the third company. The ones transferred out last month."

Ulyana kept his hands moving. Gauze, gauze, splint, gauze. "I asked where they'd gone. Their families write to us. Someone ought to be able to tell them something."

"And what did the Colonel tell you?"

"That they were reassigned."

"Mm." Reinholt picked up a roll of bandage, weighed it in his palm, set it down precisely where it had been. "And when he told you that, did he look like a man telling the truth?"

It was a trap laid so neatly that Ulyana almost admired it. Answer *yes*, and he was either a fool or a liar; answer *no*, and he had just told a staff officer that his commanding officer lied to him. He chose the third road, the narrow one.

"He looked like a tired man, sir, doing his best."

Something flickered across the major's face — not anger. Something more like disappointment, or perhaps recognition, the way one professional recognizes another's technique. "Your file says you were a schoolteacher before the war."

"In a village you've never heard of."

"I've heard of most villages." Reinholt turned a splint over, examining the wood grain. "Krasnogorsk? No — further east. Never mind. The point, Iversen, is that teachers are dangerous men in wartime. They believe names matter. They believe that if enough people know a thing, the thing becomes true." He set the splint down. "Out here, only numbers become true. Numbers, and what the General Staff says they mean."

"And what do the numbers say, sir?"

The major looked at him then — really looked, for the first time — and Ulyana had the distinct sensation of being read like a document found in a dead man's pocket.

"They say," Reinholt said mildly, "that the third company never existed. And by Thursday, neither will your questions."

* * *

That night Ulyana sat with Brandt while the fever climbed.

The wound should have been clean. It should have been simple — a shell fragment through the muscle of the shoulder, ugly but honest. But there was infection in it now, threading red up toward the collarbone, and the Colonel's skin had gone the color of candle tallow. Ulyana had lanced it, packed it, bathed it in carbolic until his own hands burned, and still the fever rose like water in a locked cellar.

"Reinholt," Brandt said, from somewhere deep inside the heat. "Was he here today."

"This morning."

"What did he want?"

"To count our candles."

Brandt laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough that went on too long, and Ulyana held the basin and hated how practiced his hands had become at this particular choreography. When it passed, the old man lay back and stared at the canvas ceiling.

"Counting," Brandt murmured. "Yes. That's what he does. He counts things so they can be made to disappear properly. You can't lose a thing, Iversen, unless someone has first written it down as present." His eyes moved, unfocused, finding Ulyana's face. "Where is your little book tonight?"

Ulyana went cold. "What little book, sir?"

"Don't." Brandt's hand found his wrist — hot, dry, grip still astonishing. "I've seen you writing by candlelight after every convoy. You think the officers don't notice because we don't say anything. We all notice everything. It's the one thing we're all still good at." A breath. "Names. You write their names. The ones the reports omit."

"I only—"

"Only nothing." The grip tightened. "Listen to me. There is a letter. In my dispatch case, under the false bottom, wrapped in oilcloth. Sealed in wax. Do you see the wax?"

Ulyana saw it, later — a heavy black seal, pressed with a crest he didn't recognize, some family arms worn smooth by time. But that was later. Now there was only the Colonel's voice, running downhill faster than Ulyana could follow.

"It goes east," Brandt said. "To Krasnogorsk. To the Vasiliev family. Do you remember the Vasilievs? From home? Elena — she'll be a woman now, she was a girl when—" The fever took him for a moment, and he wandered off into some other year entirely, saying a name — *Alyosha* — that Ulyana didn't know, and then coming back, gripping harder. "Promise me. Swear it. Not the army. Not the chaplain. You. Because you're the one who keeps the names, and God help me, that makes you the only honest man within thirty miles."

"Colonel—"

"Swear."

And Ulyana, who had sworn oaths before — to the crown, to the medical corps, to a promise made years ago under a summer sky to a girl named Elena Vasiliev who once shared her bread with him behind her father's orchard wall — found that this oath cost the least of any of them and frightened him the most.

"I swear," he said.

Brandt exhaled, long and slow, and for one terrible moment Ulyana thought it was the end. But the old man's breathing steadied instead, settling into the shallow rhythm of a man who had decided, for now, to stay. His eyes closed.

"Good boy," he whispered, already half asleep. "Now burn the wax seal off the case lock and take the letter somewhere Reinholt can't count it."

* * *

The next morning, the major invited Ulyana to breakfast.

This was such an unprecedented event that Sister Anka, the oldest nurse at the station, pulled Ulyana aside beforehand and gripped his sleeve with fingers like bird claws. "In forty years," she said, "I have known three majors to invite a junior orderly to eat from his own table. One wanted money. Two wanted lies. Check which one you are before you swallow anything."

Breakfast was served in the requisitioned farmhouse that served as Reinholt's office: real coffee, white bread, a small dish of butter — actual butter, yellow as sunlight, which alone nearly undid Ulyana's resolve. The major ate sparingly, pushed the butter dish toward his guest without comment, and waited until Ulyana had eaten enough to feel guilty about enjoying it.

"You wrote eleven letters yesterday," Reinholt said.

Ulyana's stomach, full of white bread and coffee, turned slowly over. "For the wounded, sir. To their families."

"For the wounded, yes. And four of those men died before the post cart came. Did you send them anyway?"

"Yes, sir."

"Knowing they'd arrive addressed to sons who no longer exist?"

"No, sir." Ulyana set down his fork with great care. "Addressed to men who existed. Which is the only address I ever had."

The major studied him for a long moment. Outside the frost-cracked window, an ambulance ground past, and somewhere a crow complained about the state of the world, which struck Ulyana as reasonable.

"Do you know what happens to a lie, Iversen, when it is written down often enough?" Reinholt said. "It stops being a lie. It becomes a record. Records are load-bearing. Pull one out and you never know what comes down on top of it." He leaned back. "You keep a second record. Don't bother denying it; Brandt confirmed it before I even asked, which tells me the old man trusts you beyond sense. Names of the fallen that our reports omit. Am I close?"

"You're close, sir."

"Why?"

It was such a direct question, and so nakedly curious, that Ulyana answered honestly before caution could catch up. "Because someone has to. Sir. If the report says a man deserted, and his mother reads it, she spends her life ashamed of a son who bled out carrying stretchers. That's not a small thing. That's a whole life, bent. A name costs nothing to write correctly and everything to write wrong."

Reinholt was quiet. Then he did something unexpected: he nodded, slowly, as if confirming a calculation.

"My grandfather was a parish registrar," he said. "Forty years of births and deaths in one district. When he died, my grandmother said the only thing he ever feared was losing the book. Not fire, not thieves. Losing the book." He smiled faintly. "I used to think it was sentiment. Now I know better. The book is the only place we actually live, Iversen. Everything else is weather."

"Then you understand why I—"

"I understand completely." The major stood, walked to the window, hands clasped behind him. "Which is why I'm offering you a commission."

The word hung in the warm air like smoke.

"A field commission. Second lieutenant, medical administration. Within the month, if you accept. Your duties would include casualty documentation for three stations instead of one. Official documentation." He turned. "Your pen, Iversen, would carry the weight of the General Staff behind it. Imagine what you could correct. Imagine whose mothers you could save from shame — properly, on the record, forever." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Provided the record contains only what the record is permitted to contain."

"And the little book, sir?"

"Becomes a bad memory. A youthful enthusiasm. Paper for the stove." Reinholt returned to the table and sat, folding his hands, and now at last the kindness drained away and Ulyana saw the thing underneath, plain as bone. "You misunderstand me if you think this is a threat. This is arithmetic. There is a scandal coming — a supply scandal, large, ugly, and already assigned its guilty parties. When it breaks, there will be a tribunal, and tribunals require witnesses, and witnesses come in two kinds: those who confirm the record, and those who contradict it. The first kind prosper. The second kind—" He spread his hands apologetically. "The second kind become entries in someone else's book, if you take my meaning. Deserters. Traitors. Men shot at dawn with their true service unrecorded, so that even their mothers learn the wrong story."

"And if I decline the commission?"

"Then you remain a nurse." Reinholt's voice softened into something almost gentle, which was somehow worse. "An excellent one, I'm told. With excellent habits. Habits that draw attention. And Iversen — attention, in wartime, is the one wound I cannot dress for you." He slid a folded paper across the table. "The commission authorization. Think it over. You have until the convoy arrives Friday."

* * *

Ulyana carried the paper back to the ward and put it in his pocket, where it sat against his hip like a stone from a riverbed, smooth and cold and heavier than its weight.

At noon, Sister Anka cornered him by the water boiler. She didn't ask what the major had offered; she had been alive too long for questions whose answers would only hurt. Instead she handed him a heel of black bread and said, "Whatever you decide, decide it before Friday. Convoy days, this place fills with strangers. Strangers are useful to a man who needs to be less himself."

"Sister," Ulyana said carefully, "why would I need to be less myself?"

She looked at him the way grandmothers look at boys pretending not to be in love. "Because you've been checking the road east since Tuesday," she said, "and because the Colonel's dispatch case is missing its false-bottom letter, and because I changed his dressings while you slept and he told me everything, the old fool, between fevers." She pressed the bread into his hands and closed his fingers around it. "Elena Vasiliev," she said. "That was the girl's name, wasn't it? He said it four times. Men only repeat names that matter."

Ulyana stood very still. Steam sighed from the boiler. Somewhere down the ward, a boy of nineteen was dreaming loudly about plum trees.

"Sister Anka," he said at last, "if I go — will you write to me? Care of the Krasnogorsk church? So I know you're well?"

She snorted, but her eyes shone wet and fierce. "I'll do better than that. I'll count the candles every day and send you the number. Forty-one today." She patted his cheek with a hand like dry paper. "So you'll know the counting hasn't won yet."

That night, by candlelight, Ulyana opened his private ledger and added the day's names — three of them, omitted again from the official report, three mothers spared a bent life — and beneath them, in smaller script, he wrote a single line:

*Krasnogorsk. Before the tribunal. Before Friday.*

Outside, the wind came down from the north smelling of snow and coal smoke, and far to the east, past the dark line of the front, a town he had not seen in nine years slept unaware that it had begun, quietly, to run out of time.
