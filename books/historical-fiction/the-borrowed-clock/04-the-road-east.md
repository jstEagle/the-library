## 4. The Road East

Desertion, Ulyana discovered, was mostly a matter of laundry.

That was the absurdity of it. He had spent two nights imagining barbed wire, sentries, the flat crack of a rifle in the dark — and instead his escape began with a requisition slip and a pile of soiled bandages. The supply sergeant at the Riga depot was a man named Oskars, whose nose had been broken so many times it resembled a piece of driftwood and whose attitude toward paperwork was that of a man who had made peace with all of life's disappointments.

"Four crates of gauze, two of carbolic," Ulyana said, setting down the slip. "For the forward station."

Oskars squinted at the paper, then at him. "You're the nurse who asks questions."

"I'm a nurse."

"That's what I said." Oskars stamped the slip with the air of a man sealing a tomb. "The major's people were here Tuesday. Asking about you, too. I told them you asked questions." He leaned across the counter, lowering his voice to something like gravel poured through a cloth. "I also told them you were too stupid to be dangerous. You're welcome."

"Thank you, Sergeant."

"Don't thank me. Thanking me makes it a conspiracy. Just take your gauze and be stupid somewhere else." He paused, and something almost human crossed the ruined landscape of his face. "East road's choked with refugees. Nobody looks twice at a cart in a crowd. West road's where they look."

It was as close to a blessing as the army issued, and Ulyana took it.

* * *

He had sewn the colonel's letter into his coat three nights earlier, by candlelight, with the fumbling reverence of a man who had stitched wounds but never secrets. His fingers, so steady over torn flesh, trembled over the simple work of a needle and waxed thread. The packet sat against his left side, over the ribs, where Brandt had pressed it — *here, nurse, where you'd feel a bullet* — and now it rode there like a second heartbeat.

The rest he packed according to a new arithmetic. He had spent years weighing the world in what could not be kept: men, limbs, letters home that arrived folded into casualty lists. Now he weighed it in what could be carried. A blanket. Two shirts. The ledger of names, wrapped in oilcloth and bound against his chest beneath the coat, because a man could survive losing everything except that. A tin of bread. A photograph of nothing in particular — he had no family photographs, and had taken, on impulse, the picture of the dressing station staff from the wall, eleven tired faces squinting into a summer that felt like it belonged to other people. If he was going to erase himself, he wanted one small proof that he had existed somewhere, among someone, once.

His own papers he kept. Forged ones would come later, if later came; for now he was Nurse Ulyana Iversen, Fourth Field Dressing Station, on legitimate transport duty, and the lie would hold exactly as long as nobody asked him anything twice.

At dawn he led the mule cart out through the gate, and the sentry — a boy of perhaps seventeen with a chilblained ear — waved him through without looking up from the fire he was feeding.

"Mind the road, sir! They say there's Cossacks turned bandit past Dvinsk!"

"They say a great many things," Ulyana said, and did not look back, because looking back is how a man turns into a pillar of salt, and salt was one thing the army never ran short of.

* * *

The east road was a river that had decided to walk.

He joined it outside the city, where the fields gave way to a churned brown thoroughfare, and for the first hour he thought it was a column — some unit retreating in good order. Then he saw it was not soldiers but everyone: farmers with their cattle, grandmothers with feather mattresses strapped to their backs like enormous pale humps, a man wheeling a piano on a handcart with the desperate pride of a man who has decided that if civilization ends, it will not end without music. Children trotted alongside the carts. Dogs wove between legs. Somewhere ahead a baby cried in a rhythm so relentless it became part of the road's noise, like wind.

Ulyana had expected to feel conspicuous — a deserter in an army coat, a criminal among civilians. Instead he found himself invisible, which was its own strange comfort and its own strange grief. In this river, everyone had left something burning behind them. His particular fire was simply better hidden than most.

A woman beside his cart was carrying a birdcage. Inside it, a single indignant canary.

"You'll want to eat that before long," said the man walking with her, presumably her husband, with the practicality of long marriage.

"He'll eat *you* first," she said, and adjusted the cloth over the cage against the wind, tender as a christening.

Ulyana found himself smiling, which startled him. It was the first smile since Brandt died, and it arrived, as smiles do, uninvited and slightly shameful. But there it was: the world ending, and still a man forbidden to eat a canary.

"What do you carry, nurse?" the woman asked, nodding at his cart. Everyone asked everyone; it was the convoy's whole conversation, the inventory of loss traded back and forth like currency.

"Medical stores," he said. It was even true. Four crates of gauze, two of carbolic, and underneath the false bottom of the third crate — which he had built himself, badly, at midnight — a space where he might, if pressed, hide a man-sized bundle. He had built it for the ledger and the letter both, then sewn them into his coat anyway, because a man trusts his ribs more than his carpentry.

"And where are you bound?"

"Krasnogorsk," he said, and heard his own voice go quiet around the name, the way one speaks of a grave or a sweetheart.

The woman clucked her tongue. "Long walk. God keep you."

God, Ulyana reflected, had a great deal of traffic to sort through just on this road alone. Still, he said amen.

* * *

They measured distance in what could be carried. That was the lesson the road taught him, day after day, and it reorganized something in his mind.

The man with the piano had carried music. The grandmother with the mattress had carried, apparently, the entire concept of a good night's sleep and refused to surrender it to history. The canary woman carried a song. A boy of about nine walked the whole second day carrying, with enormous care, a glass jar in which swam three minnows from a pond that no longer existed, refilling the jar each evening at whatever well or ditch offered itself, and every adult in the convoy pretended this was a perfectly reasonable thing to bring, because they understood — Ulyana understood — that the jar was not about fish. It was about the pond. It was about the fact that the boy had been the one who caught them, and that somewhere inside the glass, swimming in slow circles, was the proof that he had once been a boy in a place, rather than a boy on a road.

By the third night, Ulyana took out his ledger by the light of a shared cookfire, and an old man beside him watched him write and said, "You're keeping accounts?"

"Names," Ulyana said. "Of men who died. The reports leave some out."

The old man was silent a while. Sparks went up. Around them the convoy slept in heaps, humans and animals together for warmth, breath rising like a congregation.

"My son," the old man said finally. "Mikals Ozols. Second Latvian Rifles. Killed — they told my wife — at some place with a name nobody can pronounce. No body. No papers came. Just the telling." He looked into the fire. "If a man's name is written nowhere, nurse, did he happen?"

Ulyana opened the ledger to a fresh page and wrote, in his careful hand: *Ozols, Mikals. Second Latvian Rifles. Son of the road.* And beneath it, because it seemed important: *Carried by his father, who carries him lightly.*

The old man read it upside down, slowly, moving his lips. Then he nodded once, the way men nod at a well-dug grave or a straight furrow, and lay down, and slept.

Ulyana understood then why Brandt had chosen him. Not for courage — he suspected he had less of that than most, merely a different arrangement of fears. But Brandt had known what Ulyana knew: that a name written down is a small stubborn act of war against forgetting, and that wars are lost and won in such acts long before the treaties are signed.

* * *

On the fifth day, the weather turned, and the road taught him fear again.

Snow came sideways off the flats, fine as flour, erasing the horizon until the convoy moved through a white blindness, carts creaking, drivers calling to one another in voices flattened by wind. And it was in that whiteness that Ulyana heard the hoofbeats.

Patrol. Two riders materializing out of the murk like bad thoughts, army greatcoats, carbines slung — checking papers, turning back stragglers, hunting, among other things, a nurse who asked questions.

He had rehearsed this. He had rehearsed it so many times that when the moment came, his body simply performed it: he pulled the cart to the side, hauled the tarpaulin loose, and bent over the third crate with the false bottom, making a show of prying at the lid. When the first rider drew level, Ulyana was swearing at the crate in the heartfelt, profane language of every medic who has ever fought army packaging.

"Trouble, brother?" the rider called down.

"Lid's swollen," Ulyana grunted. "Carbolic leaks. Smells like the whole war pickled in vinegar. You want to check my papers, check them, but stand upwind."

The second rider laughed — the short bark of a man who has smelled carbolic and sympathizes. The first dismounted, glanced at Ulyana's documents, glanced at Ulyana's face, and found there exactly what the face was offering: boredom, cold, and mild grievance.

"Nurse, are you?"

"Unfortunately."

"Going where?"

"Forward station near Krasnogorsk, if the road ever decides to exist again. Though why anyone wants that posting—" he let the complaint trail off into the snow, a masterstroke of authenticity.

"No one wants any posting," the rider said philosophically, and swung back into the saddle, and the two of them dissolved into the white as if the war had briefly reconsidered and then changed its mind.

Ulyana stood in the road with his heart hammering so hard he was sure it had loosened the stitches over the letter. The canary woman appeared at his elbow.

"They didn't look in the crates," she observed.

"No."

"Pity. Then the vinegar story was wasted." She patted his arm and walked on, leading her husband, who rolled his eyes heavenward.

But that night, in the lee of a collapsed barn, Ulyana did not sleep. He lay listening to the convoy breathe and understood that the fear had changed shape. It had been a fog before, general and directionless. Now it had a name and a face: Major Reinholt, riding somewhere behind him along these same roads, patient as winter, needing the Vasilievs convicted before the clock ran out. Every day Ulyana traveled east was a day Reinholt traveled east too. They were, in some terrible sense, traveling together.

And the armistice rumors were already moving down the road like birds ahead of a storm — whispered at wells, repeated at fires, denied by officers, believed by everyone. *Weeks,* people said. Maybe sooner. Which meant Reinholt was not patient at all. Patience was a luxury of men with time.

Ulyana pressed his hand flat against his ribs, against the stiff rectangle of waxed paper and thread, and felt the letter's edges through the cloth. Brandt's last handwriting. Elena Vasiliev's fate folded to the size of a psalm. *No way back,* he told himself, and found that the words had stopped tasting like dread and started tasting like bread. Plain, necessary, daily.

Purpose, it turned out, was just fear that had been given somewhere to go.

* * *

On the seventh day the road crested a long rise, and below it the land opened — black fallow fields under a low sky, a frozen river stitching through them, and far off, hazed with woodsmoke, the roofs of a town.

"There's Krasnogorsk," said the canary woman, drawing up beside him. She studied his face, then added, gently, in the tone women use when they have already understood a thing and are only permitting you to catch up: "Someone waiting for you?"

"Someone I promised," Ulyana said.

She nodded as if that were answer enough, because it was, and offered him half of a dried apple, which he accepted, because on the road east you learned to accept what was given.

Below, the town waited under its smoke, and somewhere in it — guarded, confined, declared enemy — a house he had played beside as a child, and a woman named Elena who had once made him swear a promise in peacetime, back when peacetime had seemed like the permanent condition of the world.

Ulyana clicked his tongue at the mule and started down the hill, the letter riding warm against his ribs, the ledger riding warmer against his heart, and the snow beginning again behind him, filling in his tracks as fast as he made them — as if the road itself had agreed that no way back should be visible from anywhere.

He did not yet know that the town below had eyes at its gates, or that among the carts and bundles ahead of him, a girl with quick eyes was already watching the nurse who asked questions, deciding — the way couriers decide — whether he was cargo worth carrying.

But the road knew. Roads always find out first.
