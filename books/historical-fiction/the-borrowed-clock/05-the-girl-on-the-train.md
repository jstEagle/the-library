## 5. The Girl on the Train

Marta had learned to read a train car the way other people read a newspaper.

This one — a troop transport with its windows fogged by forty soldiers' breath and one broken stove doing its inadequate best — told her several things before she'd been aboard ten minutes. It said: *we are going east slowly and unhappily*. It said: *the man in the corner by the sacks has bread hidden somewhere, because he keeps looking at it the way you look at something you love and are ashamed of loving*. And it said, in letters as clear as any printed page: *the tall fellow pretending to sleep near the door is not sleeping at all*.

That last message was the interesting one.

She had boarded at Zilupe with her bundle of letters sewn into the lining of her coat — eleven of them this trip, from families scattered like dropped cards, addressed to sons and husbands and brothers whose regiments moved around the map faster than the postal service could follow. Officially, Marta Ozolina was fourteen years old, an orphan traveling to her aunt in Rezekne, carrying nothing but a parcel of mended clothing for her poor relations. Unofficially, she carried more news than the entire military post office between here and Daugavpils, and she carried it better, because nobody searched a girl who looked bored.

Boredom was armor. She had perfected it. She could produce an expression of such profound, bone-deep tedium that sergeants' eyes slid off her like rain off oiled canvas. Her aunt — not a real aunt, but the woman who ran the courier line and whom everyone called Aunt Ilze because "handler" sounded like something out of a bad novel — had taught her that. *Look at them the way you look at a fence post,* Ilze said. *Nobody interrogates a fence post.*

So Marta sat on her bundle, produced her fence-post face, and studied the man by the door who was failing so spectacularly at sleeping.

He was big — not soldier-big, exactly, though he had the shoulders for it, but big in the hands especially, hands folded over his chest like they didn't quite know what peacetime work to pretend toward. His coat was good quality, army issue but well kept, and there was a smell about him that took her a moment to place and then made her go still inside: carbolic soap and iodine. Hospital smell. A medic, then, or a nurse. A nurse traveling alone on a troop train, heading east away from every front he should be attached to, with eyes that opened every time the conductor came through and closed again only when the man's boots had passed.

*Deserter*, she thought, without much judgment. Half the men on the train probably wished they had his nerve.

Or not a deserter. Or something stranger. He had the look of a man carrying something heavier than his kit bag — the particular carefulness of a person transporting an idea. Marta knew that look. She saw it in mothers passing their babies over crowds, in old men handing her letters that trembled. People who carried things that mattered held themselves differently, as if the cargo were glass and the whole world were gravel.

The train lurched. The stove smoked. Somewhere behind her a soldier began, for the third time, to tell a story about a goat, and the other soldiers groaned with the weary affection of men who had heard the goat story across four hundred kilometers and would riot if it ever stopped.

The nurse's eyes opened.

Not at the conductor this time. At *her* — briefly, assessing, the way she herself assessed. Then closed again, too late.

Well, thought Marta. That was rude of him. You weren't supposed to catch each other at it. There was a whole etiquette to pretending, and he'd just broken it.

She decided she liked him already.

* * *

The opportunity came two hours later, outside Daugavpils, when the train stopped for no announced reason in the middle of a white field, which trains did now the way old men stopped mid-sentence to remember why they'd stood up.

Soldiers piled out to stretch their legs and smoke. The stove situation having deteriorated — someone had fed it a boot, apparently in an experimental spirit — the car emptied except for a few sleepers and the nurse, who finally abandoned his pretense and sat up, rubbing his eyes.

Marta slid down the bench until she was beside him. Close enough to talk, far enough to deny everything.

"You breathe wrong for a sleeper," she said.

He looked at her. Up close he was younger than his hands suggested, maybe twenty-five, with a tiredness on him that went deeper than the skin — the kind you couldn't shave off. "I beg your pardon?"

"Sleepers breathe slow and stupid. You breathe like a man counting things." She nodded at his coat. "You're also sitting like there's something in the left inside pocket you don't want pressed. And you smelled my ink."

His hand moved — a small involuntary twitch toward the left breast of his coat, the tell of all tells — and then he caught himself and turned it into adjusting his collar, badly. "I don't know what you—"

"Carbolic and iodine," Marta said. "You're medical. But no unit markings patched on, and your papers — I saw them when the conductor checked — are signed by a supply officer, which is what people use when they've got papers that wouldn't survive a real inspection. And you're going east." She let that sit. "Everybody with honest business goes west right now. East is where people run *from*."

The train sighed. Snow ticked against the black window.

"You're very observant," the nurse said at last. His voice was even, but she watched his hands settle flat on his knees, deliberate, the way a man plants himself before a wave hits. "For a girl traveling to her aunt."

"I have a lot of aunts," Marta said. "It's a large family. We travel constantly."

Something happened at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile — more like a smile being considered by a committee. "Do you have a name, this large family?"

"Marta." She stuck out her hand, because Ilze said courtesy disarmed people, and also because she liked watching grown men grapple with whether to shake a child's hand seriously. He shook it. His grip was careful, a bandager's grip. "And you're a liar with nice handwriting. I could see your ledger through the gap in your coat when you shifted. You write in columns. Lists. Nobody lists anything for pleasure except clerks and liars, and you're too tired to be a clerk."

"And what am I, then?"

"Someone keeping accounts," Marta said. "The kind somebody doesn't want kept."

Outside, the soldiers were laughing at the goat story's finale. The snow kept ticking. The nurse looked at her for a long moment — and here was the thing Marta noted for later, the thing that decided her: he didn't do what men did when caught, which was either bluster or patronize. He simply looked tired, and then, underneath the tired, relieved. Like a man setting down a sack he'd been told to carry smilingly.

"Ulyana," he said. "Ulyana Iversen."

"That wasn't so hard, was it?"

"It was extremely hard." Now the smile actually arrived, brief and crooked. "You understand I'm obliged to point out that if you're a police informant, this has been a masterful performance."

"At my age?" Marta snorted. "They don't recruit informants under sixteen. Something about the corruption not taking properly." She leaned closer and lowered her voice to the register she used for business. "Here's my thinking. You need to get somewhere specific, quietly, and your papers will get you onto trains but not past checkpoints, because checkpoint men look at paper men carefully and at girls not at all. Am I close?"

Ulyana said nothing. His silence had a shape, though, and the shape was *yes*.

"There are back roads," Marta went on. "Cart roads, forest tracks, a ferry at Kraslava that doesn't ask names because the ferryman owes my family. If you stay on the rails, you'll be stopped twice between here and wherever you're going. If you come off the rails with me, you'll walk three days and arrive smelling like a barn, but arriving."

"And why," Ulyana said slowly, "would you offer that? What does a girl with many aunts want with a deserter's troubles?"

"I never said deserter."

"You didn't have to. Your face did."

Marta grinned. Then she sobered, because the next part mattered, and she'd rehearsed it on the platform at Zilupe without knowing yet who she'd say it to. "I carry letters," she said. "Eleven on this trip. Wives to husbands, mothers to sons. The mail takes months or never, so we take it in days. My family has been doing it since the war started. Do you know what happens to couriers who are caught?"

"I can imagine."

"Don't imagine. They hang some. The ones they don't hang they question first, and questioning is worse." She held his eyes. She'd practiced that too; her father, before the war ate him, used to say she had her mother's stare and it could hold a horse. "I'm telling you this because you now know something that gets me killed, and I want us square. You know my risk. I know yours. That's the whole contract between strangers on a train."

"That's a terrible contract," Ulyana said. "You've given me power over you and asked for nothing."

"I haven't asked for nothing." Marta glanced at the left side of his coat. "I want in."

"In where?"

"Your ledger." She nodded at it. "I've seen how you keep it. Columns, dates. Names?"

Something crossed his face then, quick as a fish under ice, and Marta understood she'd guessed truer than she knew. "Names," he said quietly.

"The dead," she said. "The ones the reports leave off. I've heard about men like you — there was a chaplain near Vilani who kept one, till they transferred him somewhere cold. Everyone whispers about it. The forgotten dead." She pulled her coat open, deliberately, and showed him the lining, the flat stitched packets. "Every letter I carry is a name trying not to disappear. A wife writing a husband isn't sending words. She's sending proof he existed, that they existed together. That's all any of it is — proof against forgetting." She met his eyes again. "I read them sometimes. Not to pry. To remember them myself, so they're written twice. So put me in your book, Ulyana Iversen. Marta Ozolina, courier, daughter of Riga, carrier of eleven letters and more than eleven secrets. Write me down alive. And when I'm old, or dead, or hanged from a telegraph pole outside some town that's already forgotten why — there'll be a column somewhere with my name in it, and I'll have existed properly."

The train whistle shrieked far down the line. Soldiers began climbing back aboard, stamping snow, complaining about the cold with the relish of men grateful for something to complain about.

Ulyana was silent for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was rough at the edges. "How old are you really?"

"Fourteen. Nearly fifteen."

"Nearly."

"Since March."

He exhaled something that was half laugh, half prayer. "In my ledger," he said, "the pages aren't happy ones, Marta Ozolina. Nearly-fifteen. You may be asking to live next to grief."

"I live next to grief already. It pays poorly and snores." She shrugged. "But it's honest company. Better than most."

He reached into his coat — she politely examined the ceiling while he did it — and brought out a small notebook, swollen with use, its cover soft as cloth from handling. He opened it to the back, where the columns ran thin and the entries were fewer, and uncapped a stub of pencil with his teeth.

"*Marta Ozolina*," he wrote, murmuring it as the nib scratched. "*Courier. Carrier of letters. Keeper of her own remembering.*" He paused, then added something else, small, beneath. He tilted the book so she could see: *alive, and meant to stay so.*

"That last part," Marta said, throat unexpectedly tight, "isn't standard format."

"No," Ulyana agreed. "New column. Started today."

* * *

The train moved again, slower, the wheels finding a rhythm like a tired heart. The goat storyteller had fallen asleep mid-epic. Someone had retrieved the boot from the stove, and the stove, sulking, produced a little heat after all.

They talked in low voices while the soldiers snored — or rather, Marta talked and Ulyana listened, which she suspected was his professional habit and his personal refuge both. She mapped the back roads for him in the condensation on the window, drawing with one fingertip: the cart track south of the rail line, the farm where a widow sold hot milk and asked nothing, the frozen ford, the ferryman. He absorbed it the way sponges absorb, nodding, asking precise questions — How many men at the Kraslava checkpoint? Does the ferryman take money or favors? Which roads flood in thaw? — and his questions were good ones, a traveler's questions, a survivor's.

"And where is it you're going, exactly?" she asked, at last, because he'd answered everything except that.

Ulyana looked out the black window a moment, at the world rushing past unseen. "Krasnogorsk," he said.

Marta's finger stopped on the glass. Even she, who was fourteen-nearly-fifteen and officially interested in nothing, had heard the name whispered along the courier lines for weeks. *Krasnogorsk. The Vasiliev affair. They're going to hang it on the family, watch.* Aunt Ilze had marked it on her mental map the way sailors mark reefs.

"Well," said Marta Ozolina, and smiled her father's smile, the one that held horses. "Then you'll definitely want the back roads."

Somewhere ahead, in the dark, the train began to slow — and through the fogged glass, faint and getting fainter, came the sound of a church bell in a town they hadn't reached yet, ringing out of hour, ringing as if for someone.
