## 6. Krasnogorsk in Winter

The road into Krasnogorsk announced itself the way old roads do — by the things it stopped pretending to be. For three days the track had been mud and cart-ruts and the frozen hoofprints of an army that had passed through going the other way, but at a certain bend past the birch woods it widened, straightened, and became a road again, with ditches cut on either side and a milestone worn smooth by a century of thumbs. Ulyana stood on it in the grey light of late afternoon and felt something in his chest loosen and tighten at once, the way a fist does around a thing it is afraid of dropping.

Krasnogorsk. He had rehearsed the name so many times since Riga that it had begun to feel less like a place than a password. But here was the place itself, sitting in its shallow valley under a lid of cloud, smoke rising from chimneys in the unhurried columns of a town that did not yet know what winter intended for it.

He had changed since he left the front. Not his coat — the coat still carried Brandt's letter sewn flat against his ribs, beneath the lining, beneath the wool, a small stiff rectangle like a second heart — but his manner of walking. A nurse walks quickly, head down, useful. Ulyana had taught himself to walk slowly, head up, bored. Boredom, Marta had told him on the train, was the finest disguise ever invented. "Nobody stops a bored man," she'd said. "They stop frightened men, and they stop running men. Nobody has ever once asked a bored man for his papers." She was fifteen and knew more about survival than most generals, and he missed her already, which was a foolish thing to miss someone you'd known for four days.

He walked into town like a bored man. He was, privately, terrified, and so perhaps the disguise held better than he deserved.

* * *

The first thing he noticed was that the church was still there, which surprised him more than anything else about the view, because in his memory Krasnogorsk had been assembled almost entirely out of the church. The whitewashed tower, the green roof gone black with age, the bell that Elena Vasiliev had once dared him to ring with a stone thrown from the wall — he could still hear the outrage of it, the whole village coming out, the priest shaking his fist while two children aged eleven lay flat in the long grass by the cemetery wall, laughing until they couldn't breathe. He had been punished with a week of hauling water for the sexton. Elena had been punished with nothing at all, because her father had laughed too, quietly, where only his daughter could see.

That was the summer world. This was the winter one.

Ulyana went up the hill toward the churchyard because it was the highest ground and because his feet, having made this climb a thousand times as a boy, made it now without consulting him. The gate hung as it always had, one hinge complaining. Inside, the graves lay under their first snow, tidy and white, the stones standing up out of the drifts like teeth.

From here he could see all of Krasnogorsk laid out below: the market square, the mill, the long street called — absurdly, in this landlocked country — Harbour Street, because some ancestor had named it in optimism or drink. And there, at the end of Harbour Street, set back behind its linden trees with the frozen river glinting beyond —

There it was. The Vasiliev house.

His breath went out of him in a long white ribbon.

It was not burning. That was the first thing, and he made himself notice it properly, because he had spent four hundred kilometers imagining fire. The house stood. Its windows were dark, but windows go dark in every house at dusk. Smoke rose from its chimney, thin but real. Someone was keeping a stove lit in there. Someone was alive in there.

And around it, arranged with the neatness that made them worse than any mob, were soldiers.

Six of them, by his count, though the failing light argued with him. Two at the gate, wrapped in greatcoats, stamping their feet. One pacing the river side. Others at intervals along the fence line, posted the way you post men when you are guarding something — or when you are guarding people, and want the difference understood. A supply wagon stood in the lane, unremarkable except that no one was loading or unloading it. It was simply there, the way furniture is there in a room you've decided not to enter.

Confined pending tribunal. The words had traveled faster than he had; a woman at the last village had spoken them over her bread like a proverb. *The Vasilievs. Thieves, they say. Stealing army supplies while boys freeze at the front.* She had said it without heat, the way you repeat something you don't believe quite enough to defend and can't quite afford to doubt. That was how poison worked in a town. Not a knife — a slow pour, into the well everyone drank from.

Ulyana crouched behind the low wall by the cemetery's oldest section, where the eighteenth-century stones leaned together like conspirators, and watched, and let the grief arrive on its own schedule.

Because here was the thing he had not prepared for, though he should have: it hurt to see the house guarded. Not the way he'd expected — he'd expected fear, urgency, the cold arithmetic of rescue. Instead what came was a boyhood, entire, arriving all at once. The lindens bare now but present, the same lindens under which Elena had taught him to whistle through a blade of grass (he never learned; she mocked him for a decade). The kitchen garden gone to snow, but he could map it blind — the row of currant bushes, the frame beds where old Vasily grew tomatoes too ambitious for the climate and covered them with glass bells every night like a man tucking in infants. The stable door hanging slightly crooked since the storm of '09, never mended because Elena's father said a crooked door kept honest hours.

All of it visible. None of it touchable. He might as well have been looking at a photograph, or a grave.

"You're a long way from anywhere," said a voice above him.

Ulyana did not jump, which cost him something. He turned his head slowly — boredom, boredom — and found an old man standing among the graves with a shovel over his shoulder and a sack of birdseed under his arm, regarding him with the frank, unimpressed stare of a man who has buried half the parish and expects to bury the other half without interference.

"Resting my legs," Ulyana said.

"Hm." The gravedigger — for who else carries a shovel to churchyard in December? — came closer, unhurried. "Legs. On a wall. In the snow. Facing the Vasiliev house."

"The view is good."

"It is." The old man set down his sack, and sparrows began arriving as if summoned by name. "I knew a fellow used to sit right about where you're sitting. Years back. Boy from the doctor's family, always with the little Vasiliev girl. They'd watch the town from here and make up stories about everybody. Terrible slanderers, both of them. Had the miller's wife married to a bear, I recall."

Ulyana kept his face doing nothing at all, which took practice. "What happened to him?"

"Went off to be a nurse, they said. Family thought it a strange choice. Kind hands, his mother said. Too kind to waste." The old man scattered seed with a flick of the wrist, economical, a man who wasted nothing, not even gestures. "Town's full of ghosts this year. Half of them haven't died yet."

"They may yet avoid it," Ulyana said quietly.

"That would be new." The old man looked at him a moment longer — a long, weighing look, the look of a man pricing a horse — and then shrugged, and picked up his shovel, and added, not turning back: "Guards change at six. The river-side one is young and stupid and walks the same twenty steps every time, counting them wrong at seventeen. The gate pair plays cards after dark and cheats each other, badly. If a man wanted to know things, a man could do worse than ask the sexton. Sexton sees everything. Sexton hears everything. Sexton, being eighty-one, forgets most of it immediately, which is why everyone tells him things."

"I'll remember," Ulyana said.

"Don't remember too loudly." And the old man crunched away through the snow, whistling at his sparrows, leaving Ulyana alone with the astonishing suspicion that he had just been given directions by the graveyard itself.

* * *

He found a room above the tavern on Harbour Street, paid for a week in advance under a name he invented on the stairs — Anders Vik, a traveling buyer of hides, a profession chosen because nobody questions a hide-buyer and everybody pities him — and settled in to learn the town again from a window.

Krasnogorsk had grown poorer. That was the honest accounting. The paint on the shopfronts had gone chalky; two of the market stalls stood empty with their awnings furled like closed fists; and there was a new poster on the customs-house wall, pasted over an older poster, its ink still sharp:

*CITIZENS ARE REMINDED THAT HOARDING AND THE TRAFFIC IN ARMY SUPPLIES IS TREASON AGAINST THE PEOPLE AND WILL BE PUNISHED ACCORDINGLY.*

Below, in smaller type, the tribunal's schedule. Ten days hence. Town hall. The names of the accused printed in bold: VASILIEV, E. — VASILIEV, M. — VASILIEV, S.

Three names. Mother, father, daughter. Ulyana read them until the letters swam, then read them again. There was a particular violence in seeing a person reduced to type — Elena, who had once argued a schoolmaster into admitting that winter existed on other planets, compressed into a single upright word between rules and serifs. He thought: they have made her a heading. He thought: I will make her a story again, if it is the last work of my life.

The tavern below was warm and loud and mercifully indifferent to him. Hide-buyers, it turned out, were invisible in exactly the way Marta had promised. Ulyana sat in the corner with a beer he barely touched and listened, and within an hour he had the shape of it — the town's mind, moving like weather.

The scandal had arrived in autumn with the requisition officers. Convoy wagons meant for the eastern garrison had gone astray — grain, boots, medical stores, a quantity of morphine that made even the tavern go quiet when mentioned. The trail, according to the official account, led directly to the Vasiliev warehouse lease and to signatures that looked very much like signatures of Vasilievs. Documents, the officials said. Signed documents. Produced, conveniently, from the files of a command office two hundred miles away, examined by no one local, explained by no one at all.

"And old Vasily signed them himself?" a carter was saying, incredulous. "Man counts his spoons before bed. You're telling me he signed away army grain?"

"Paper says what paper says," answered his neighbor, a lean man with the flat voice of someone repeating a lesson. "Tribunal will sort it."

"Tribunal," snorted a third voice, a woman's, dry as August. " Tribunal will find what it's sent to find. My cousin saw the guards going into the chapel cellar last week with lanterns. Chapel cellar. What's in the chapel cellar, I wonder?"

"Rats," said the flat-voiced man.

"Big rats," said the woman. "Rats that sing hymns."

Laughter — quick, guilty, smothered laughter, the laughter of people testing whether a joke is safe. It was not entirely safe. That was the measure of the town: it still laughed, but it checked the door first.

Ulyana drank his untouched beer and felt hope move in him like a live fish. Because that was the thing the major in his fine uniform had never understood, back at the dressing station — the thing you could only learn by listening in taverns. Fear silences people individually. Collectively, they cannot help talking. A town is a ledger that writes itself, and somewhere in Krasnogorsk tonight, in kitchens and cellars and behind the steam of bathhouses, entries were already being made.

* * *

Near midnight the tavern emptied and the cold climbed through the floorboards, and Ulyana went out because sleep was impossible and because the house at the end of the street pulled at him like a tide.

He told himself it was reconnaissance. He was honest enough, walking, to admit it was also longing.

Harbour Street at midnight belonged to the snow. His boots left prints and he hated each one, evidence marching behind him, but the night was his ally too — moonless, the clouds low and fat with more of the same. He passed the guard at the river side, a shadow pacing its twenty steps, and counted along with it silently, and smiled despite everything when the boy lost his place at seventeen exactly as promised.

Closer. Close enough now to see the house properly, the dark bulk of it, the chimney breathing its thin gray thread. A lamp burned in one upstairs window — the room that had been Elena's, he was nearly sure, the room where she'd kept books under the floorboard because her mother believed novels rotted the character and Elena believed her mother was entitled to her beliefs. A light in that window meant someone awake in there. Someone reading, maybe. Or someone sitting very still in the dark beside a lamp, the way people do in the last hour of a day that has taken everything.

Elena. Were you afraid? Of course you were afraid. Are you furious? Certainly you are furious. Are you — and this thought arrived gently, like a hand on the shoulder — are you, at this moment, planning?

He knew her answer before he finished asking. Somewhere behind those walls, in a house turned prison, a woman who had once run a household, a dispensary, a flood-relief effort, and a spirited campaign against the unfairness of school examinations was not waiting to be rescued. She was making lists. She was remembering who owed her favors. She was, at this precise minute, probably inventing a use for the guards' stupidity that would have made the gravedigger proud.

The trouble was time. Ten days. A tribunal already built, a verdict already drafted somewhere in a clean office, a general — Brandt's letter had named him, and Ulyana had read the name so many times the syllables had worn grooves in him — who needed the Vasilievs convicted before anyone looked too closely at whose signature actually moved the grain. Guards, documents, distance, and a town afraid to speak: these were the walls. And he was one tired man in a borrowed name with a dead colonel's letter sewn against his ribs.

But he was also — and the thought steadied him like a handrail — a man who kept ledgers. And he was in a town that remembered him. The gravedigger knew. The gravedigger, being eighty-one, would forget by morning, but knowing was a door once opened.

A floorboard shifted in the house. Or he imagined it. Then, unmistakably: the upstairs lamp went out, and in the darkness Ulyana felt rather than saw a figure come to the window — a slight silhouette against the lesser dark, standing quite still, looking out over the snowy garden, over the guards, over the sleeping town. Looking east, toward the road he had come by, though she could not know that. Looking at nothing. Looking, the way the confined always look, at the possibility of elsewhere.

Don't see me, he willed her, pressing into the shadow of the linden. Don't see me yet. Give me ten days, Elena Vasiliev. Give me your stubbornness and your lists and your terrible slanderous imagination, give me the girl who rang the church bell and got away with it, because I have crossed a collapsing country carrying a dead man's promise and I am not — I am *not* — going to stand in a tribunal hall and watch them make you a heading.

The figure at the window lingered a moment longer. Then it withdrew, and the curtain fell, and the house was a house again, guarded and dark and alive.

Ulyana turned back toward the tavern, and it was then, passing the mouth of the mill lane, that he heard voices — low, urgent, wrong for the hour — and saw by the shuttered glow of a lantern two figures at the chapel door: a soldier in a greatcoat, and a townsman he half recognized, bent close together. The soldier handed the man something folded. The man took it with both hands, the way you take a summons or a debt.

"—before the tribunal convenes," the soldier murmured, "or the major says the offer closes. Names, dates, where the goods went. Full pardon for cooperation. After—" the lantern guttered, throwing their shadows tall up the chapel wall, "—after, there isn't an offer. There's just the list."

The townsman said something Ulyanna couldn't catch. The soldier clapped him on the shoulder — comradely, almost gentle, kindness with a knife behind it, a gesture Ulyana had seen before on another man in another town — and the two parted, one toward the guard post, one stumbling home through the snow like a man carrying something breakable.

Ulyana stood in the black mouth of the lane and understood, with the clarity of cold air, that Reinholt's hand had reached Krasnogorsk ahead of him — and that the town's fear now had a clock attached.

Ten days. Fewer, if the major chose to hurry.

He pulled his collar up and went to find the sexton.
