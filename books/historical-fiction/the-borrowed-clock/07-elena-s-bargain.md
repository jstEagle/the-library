## 7. Elena's Bargain

The bread had gone stale again, which meant the guard on the east window was new. Elena Vasiliev had learned the household's small grammar of survival in three weeks of confinement: fresh bread meant Sergeant Orlow at the gate, a man who still believed in decency and looked away when her mother crossed herself too loudly; stale bread meant his replacement, a boy named Piotr who saluted too often and read the letters he confiscated by candlelight, moving his lips.

She broke the loaf and set half aside for Dmitri, who was sulking in the corner behind the piano as though the instrument might shelter him from history. It could not. Nothing could. But she let him believe it for another hour, because he was eleven, and because an hour of belief is not nothing.

"Mama says the tribunal will be fair," Dmitri announced to the piano's underside.

"Your mama says that so you'll sleep," Elena said. "Fairness is a word officers use when they want something."

"You're very grim today."

"I'm always grim. You only notice on Tuesdays." She smiled despite herself — he counted the days aloud now, having nothing else to count, and had decided Tuesday was the day bad news arrived because the arrest had been a Tuesday. A child's theology. She did not correct it. Theology, even a wrong one, kept a boy upright.

The letter came to her on Thursday, which unsettled her more than any Tuesday could have.

* * *

It arrived the way contraband always arrives: absurdly. Marta — a girl of perhaps fifteen with a chapped face and the unblinking confidence of a sparrow that has learned no one bothers catching sparrows — appeared at the kitchen door claiming to deliver a clock for repair.

"We have no clock," said Elena's mother, who had taken up lying with the enthusiasm of a woman discovering a late vocation.

"The order says this house," Marta insisted, holding up a broken mantel clock as evidence of itself. "Signed by a Herr Brandt."

The name went through the room like cold water through cloth. Elena's mother sat down abruptly on a stool. Dmitri, mercifully, was in the yard counting soldiers' buttons through the fence.

"Bring it in," Elena said. "Carefully. It belonged to my grandmother."

It had belonged to no one; it had been born, apparently, three days ago in some watchmaker's back room, hollowed and lined with oilcloth. Elena knew this because she had once hidden banknotes in exactly such a clock for her father, in the year of the last scandal, before the war made scandals of everything. Her father had called it "the family archive." He had died before the family needed archiving quite so badly.

Marta watched her open it with professional interest. Inside lay a letter sealed in black wax, and beneath it a second packet, heavier, wrapped in sacking: a ledger, or part of one, in a cramped hand Elena did not know but somehow recognized anyway — the handwriting of a man who had spent years writing down things other people preferred forgotten.

"There's a message," Marta said. "Verbal. I'm to say it exact." She closed her eyes, reciting. "*Tell Frau Vasiliev: Brandt is dead. He kept the promise. So will I.* That's all." She opened her eyes. "There was supposed to be more but the man cried a little and lost his place."

Elena turned away toward the window, toward the frozen garden where she and Ulyana Iversen had once played at being explorers mapping a country made entirely of snowdrifts. He had promised, at nine years old, never to lose anything of hers. He had grown into a man who kept promises across a war. She pressed her thumb against the wax seal until it cracked.

*Frau Vasiliev,* the letter began, and then, in a shakier line, *Elena — forgive the formality; old habits are the last things to die and they die slowly, like the rest of us.*

She read it twice. Then she sat down beside her mother and read it aloud, because her mother's eyes were failing and because some things must be spoken to become real.

Brandt wrote of convoys — dates, numbers, wagon counts — grain and coal and medical stores that had left Krasnogorsk's depot and arrived nowhere, vanishing between ledgers like water into sand. He wrote of requisition orders signed with a flourish he described, with a dying man's precision, as *theatrical*. And he wrote a name.

Not Reinholt. Reinholt was a hand, a glove, a major who signed what he was told to sign. The name at the bottom of the theft, the name above it, the name whose signature flourished like a curtain rising:

**General Hess.**

"Hess," her mother repeated faintly. "He dined here. Before the war. He complimented my plum brandy."

"He would," Elena said. "Men like Hess compliment everything they intend to take."

*They need the Vasilievs guilty before the armistice comes,* Brandt had written. *A conviction freezes the story. Peace melts it. They are racing the calendar, Elena, and they mean to win by sacrificing your name. I am sorry. I am a soldier who has finally done one honest thing, and I can only do it while dying, which I suspect tells you everything about our army.*

Her mother wept quietly, the way she did everything — thoroughly and without drama, a woman built for endurance rather than performance. Elena folded the letter along its creases and found her hands were steady, which surprised her. She had expected trembling. Instead there was something almost like clarity, the clean air that follows a storm front.

"They'll hang Papa's memory on this," her mother whispered. "And yours. And the boy's."

"No," Elena said. "They'll try."

* * *

That night she wrote her reply by the light of a shuttered lamp, and it was short, because the girl would have to carry it in her shoe or her hair or wherever sparrows keep their secrets.

*To the nurse who keeps promises: I have read it. I know the name. Come to me through Marta — I cannot come to you, they count my steps to the well. And bring your ledger. If we are to fight men with armies, we will fight them with arithmetic.*

Then she hesitated, pen hovering, and added the sentence that would decide everything:

*I will not run. Do not ask it of me. Read this twice if you must, then accept it, as you accepted a dying man's letter. Some of us are the town. If the town runs, there is nothing left worth saving.*

She thought about that sentence for a long time afterward, sitting in the dark with the lamp cold. It was easy to write bravery. Bravery on paper cost nothing, weighed nothing, could be carried in a shoe. Living it was another arithmetic entirely — one where every number was a neighbor.

Because that was the truth she could not put in the letter: she had already counted them. Old Fenya at the bakery who had slipped loaves under the fence since the first day of confinement. The Kroll brothers, who shoveled her walk though their sons wore the other uniform. The schoolmaster Petrov, who had taught her Latin and now stood in the square each morning glaring at the guards as though conjugating them into shame. Four hundred souls in Krasnogorsk who had watched her family arrested and had not — not one of them — spat, or pointed, or looked away. In a winter when looking away was the national pastime, four hundred people had chosen to look.

If she fled east with Dmitri and her mother and a broken clock full of papers, those four hundred would be left standing in the square alone when the tribunal came asking questions. And the tribunal would come asking questions. Men like Hess did not build stage sets and then leave them empty.

"You're doing the face," said Dmitri from the doorway. He had escaped the piano. "The thinking face. Mama says your thinking face frightens the icons."

"Your mama flatters me."

"Are we going to be hanged?"

"Dmitri."

"Piotr told the baker's boy we'd be hanged. He said it like it was already decided. Like weather." The boy's voice didn't waver, which was worse than wavering. He had begun to collect terrible information the way other children collected stamps, pressing each horror flat between pages so he could look at it later without flinching.

Elena crossed the room and took his hands. They were cold and chapped and certain.

"Listen to me. There is a man coming — a friend, from long ago, before you were born. He is bringing proof that none of this is our fault. Real proof, with names and dates. And when he brings it, we are going to do something much more frightening than being hanged."

"What's more frightening than that?"

"Winning," Elena said. "Winning means they'll have to explain themselves. Hanged people get sympathy. Winners get audited."

Dmitri considered this with the gravity it deserved. "Papa would have liked that joke."

"Papa invented it." She kissed the top of his head. "Now go to sleep. Tomorrow I need you to do something dangerous."

His eyes lit up in a way that confirmed he was, unfortunately, entirely his father's son. "What?"

"I need you to be bored. Thoroughly, visibly bored. Bored boys get ignored, and ignored boys see everything. Count the guards' boots. Tell me how many pairs change each day."

"That's not dangerous."

"It will be, if you smile while doing it."

* * *

Marta returned on Sunday with snow in her eyelashes and Ulyana's answer folded inside her mitten, which explained why the girl held her right hand slightly apart from her body all evening, like a woman carrying a sleeping bird.

*Understood,* he wrote. *I won't ask you to run. I remember what happens when people ask you things.* — and here Elena laughed out loud, alone, at midnight, a sound so foreign in that house that her mother called out anxiously from the next room — *but understand me in turn: I buried a colonel two weeks ago who also refused to leave his post, and I have no intention of adding you to a ledger. Bring me the town instead. Every witness, every farmer who saw the wagons leave at night, every clerk who stamped the false manifests and hated himself for it. Hess stole from everyone in Krasnogorsk. Everyone in Krasnogorsk is therefore a witness. We will make the trial so crowded with truth that their stage play collapses under its own audience.*

*P.S. The plum brandy compliment was reported to me verbatim by a dying man. Your mother's cellar has better intelligence value than the general staff.*

So it began.

Elena worked the way she imagined engineers work — quietly, load by load. Marta carried notes sewn into hem linings. The baker's daughter carried bread with messages baked under the crust, a system Elena improved upon after the first loaf was nearly eaten by a hungry sergeant. Old Fenya, it turned out, had been watching the depot road from her kitchen window for two years and kept a memory like a parish register: *the third convoy went out on a Thursday, forty wagons, came back twelve, and General Hess's aide drank my husband's slivovitz and called it 'requisitioned hospitality.'*

The schoolmaster Petrov came to the fence on Wednesday and stood in the falling snow pretending to admire the Vasilievs' bare linden trees.

"Madam," he said formally, "I am given to understand you require assistance with... classical studies."

"I require witnesses, Herr Petrov."

"Ah. Witnesses. Yes. I have several former students in the quartermaster corps. Boys who once copied their homework from one another and can now be persuaded to copy the truth instead." His breath steamed. "It will be dangerous."

"Yes."

"Good." He nodded once, sharply, a man closing a lesson plan thirty years overdue. "Danger clarifies the curriculum."

By Friday she had eleven names. By the following Tuesday, nineteen: farmers, clerks, a railway porter, two deserters hiding in the tannery, and one astonishingly candid customs officer who had been waiting three years for permission to be angry.

Nineteen names. Elena wrote them in the back of Dmitri's arithmetic primer, in pencil, in Latin abbreviations Petrov would appreciate — a private language, a schoolgirl's cipher, the rebellion of educated women everywhere since writing was invented.

She looked at the list and felt the fear she had been postponing arrive at last, punctual as the bread. Nineteen people, and every one of them a door the major could kick in. Every one of them someone's child, someone's father. She was not gathering witnesses; she was gathering hostages and calling it hope.

Her mother found her at the table at dawn, the primer open, the pencil worn to a stub.

"Well?" her mother asked.

"I've just volunteered nineteen families for a firing squad."

"Mm." Her mother poured tea, unperturbed, a woman who had survived two wars, one revolution, and Elena's grandfather. "And the alternative?"

"The alternative is we flee, and the town hangs alone, and I spend the rest of my life alive."

Her mother drank her tea. Outside, the guards changed, and Dmitri — bored, magnificently bored — counted their boots.

"Nineteen," her mother said at last, "is a respectable congregation. Your father started his business with six." She rose, kissed the crown of Elena's head, and added, in the tone she reserved for final rulings: "Besides, if we must be ghosts, we will be the kind that haunt. Now finish your tea. The sparrow girl comes at noon, and you look like a woman planning something, which is precisely the face you must never wear in this house again."

But that afternoon, as Marta slipped away through the gate with the primer's pages copied into her shoe, one of Piotr's replacements — a new guard, tall, careful, with the eyes of a man taking inventory — watched the girl go. Not idly. He watched her the way Major Reinholt's superiors had taught him to watch: noting the direction, the gait, the hour.

Elena saw him seeing, and felt the first cold thread of what was coming, and lifted her chin anyway.

Let them take inventory. She was keeping one too — and hers was longer.
