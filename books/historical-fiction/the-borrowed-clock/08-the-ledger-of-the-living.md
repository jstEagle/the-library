## 8. The Ledger of the Living

Marta had carried a great many things in her thirteen years — bread wrapped in a shawl, letters sewn into doll's bodies, a live hen once, for six kilometers, under her arm and inside her coat at the same time, which she still maintained was a feat no soldier could match. But she had never before carried hope, and it turned out hope was heavier than the hen.

It was heavier because it came in pieces.

"Read it back to me," Elena Vasiliev said.

They sat in the back room of the bakery that belonged to Frau Ostrowski, who was conveniently deaf whenever soldiers asked her questions and miraculously hearing whenever Elena needed a table. The room smelled of yeast and lamp oil. On the table between them lay two ledgers: Ulyana Iversen's small black book, soft-cornered from years of pockets, filled with names of dead men; and Elena's own papers, which were not a book at all but a sheaf of scraps — the backs of envelopes, the margins of hymnals, one piece of wallpaper with a floral border — covered in a neat, urgent hand.

"The two lists," Elena said. "We have to make them one."

Marta picked up the black ledger. She had seen it before, on the train, when she'd guessed what the quiet nurse carried. She had thought then it was orders, or codes. Now she knew what it was, and knowing made her handle it like something that could bruise.

"Yankov, Pavel," she read. "Private, second company. Died of his wounds October fourth. Not listed." She looked up. "Not listed where?"

"Anywhere," Ulyana said from the corner, where he stood with his arms folded like a man guarding a door only he could see. "His officers reported him missing. Missing is cheaper than dead. Dead requires a pension."

"And this one — Sorokin, Mikkel. Farmer. Shot at the crossroads, November ninth. Witness to—" Marta stopped. "This is the same name."

"It is the same man," Elena said. "That's the whole point, Marta. Sit down properly; your neck will thank you. Pavel Yankov drove supply wagons out of the Krasnogorsk depot for two years. He told his wife the wagons left empty and came back full, and full of things that never reached the front. Then he died, officially missing, unofficially buried behind the dressing station near Riga — because this man here wrote him down."

Ulyana didn't move, but Marta saw something pass over his face like weather.

"So the dead man testifies," Marta said slowly. It sounded like something out of a story her grandmother told, saints and candles and justice arriving on a pale horse. Except here it arrived on paper, in pencil, in a girl's handwriting.

"The dead man corroborates," Elena corrected. "The living ones swear to it. That's why we need both books." She spread her scraps flat. "I've spent three weeks doing what I do best, which is talking to people until they forget they're frightened. There are forty-one names here. Farmers who saw the wagons. A clerk from the depot who kept duplicate receipts because he didn't trust his major, God bless suspicious clerks. Two soldiers from the escort detail. A woman whose husband loaded crates marked 'grain' that rang like they were full of tin."

"Forty-one," Marta repeated.

"Forty-one witnesses. Some will refuse. Some are already gone. Some will be too afraid when the day comes." Elena's voice stayed level, but Marta noticed her thumb pressing hard against the edge of the wallpaper scrap, as if pinning it down against a wind. "So we need more than forty-one. We need enough names that striking through any one of them doesn't matter. That is how you beat powerful men, Marta. Not with one hero. With arithmetic."

* * *

The arithmetic took all night.

They worked by two candles, and Frau Ostrowski brought them bread at intervals without being asked, setting it down and leaving as silently as a woman feeding ghosts. Somewhere past midnight Marta invented a system, because the lists refused to agree — names appeared in one book and not the other, dates contradicted, and Private Yankov turned out to have been recorded by Ulyana under a spelling that would have made Yankov himself laugh.

"Here." Marta pulled a fresh sheet toward her and drew three columns with the ruler she used for schoolwork, back when there was schoolwork. "Dead who witnessed. Living who witnessed. And the third column —" she hesitated, pencil hovering, "— living who might witness if someone sits with them long enough and reminds them they're not alone."

Elena looked at the third column for a long moment.

"You've been listening to me," she said.

"I listen to everyone. It's my job. People tell couriers things they don't tell their priests."

"Then put yourself in the third column," Elena said. "Because you'll be the one sitting with them."

"I can't testify. I'm nobody. I'm fourteen next month and I deliver laundry tickets."

"Elena means," Ulyana said quietly, "that you'll carry the summons. Not sign them." He crossed to the table, and up close Marta could see how tired he was, the kind of tired that lives behind the eyes and doesn't wash off. "A summons isn't an order. It has no seal. It's a letter that says: you saw what you saw, and you are asked to say so, and here is where, and here is when, and here is a person who will stand beside you while you tremble. It has to reach thirty houses without the major learning it happened. Can that be done?"

Marta thought about her routes. The laundry circuit, the flour delivery, the errands for the widow Brandt — no relation to the colonel, though she'd decided not to mention that coincidence aloud. The checkpoint at the mill bridge where the sentry checked baskets but never checked the girl carrying them, because after eleven weeks he'd stopped seeing her as anything but furniture.

"Yes," she said. "But not as one route. If I visit thirty houses in three days, even the furniture gets noticed." She took the pencil back and began drawing lines between names. "You sort them by street. I fold them into my ordinary deliveries. Frau Lindemann gets her soap and a note. Old Tomas gets his tobacco and a note. Nobody receives anything unusual. Hope travels better disguised as errands."

There was a silence. Then Elena laughed — a short, surprised sound, the first Marta had heard from her, and it changed her whole face, made her briefly a girl herself.

"Hear that, Ulyana? Justice arrives disguised as soap."

"My grandmother always said revolutions smell of bread," Ulyana said. "She may have undersold the soap."

* * *

By dawn the single list existed. Marta copied it twice — once fair, once rough — and burned her practice pages in the oven, watching the names curl and vanish, each one going somewhere safer: into memory, into ink, into a girl's satchel.

The summons itself was Elena's masterpiece, and Marta admired it the way other girls admired embroidery. It was half a page, written to be memorized and destroyed:

*You are asked to speak of what you saw in the matter of the supply convoys, before Judge Lindqvist, at the town tribunal. You will not stand alone. Come by the way you know. Tell no one, including those you love most — love is the easiest door for fear to knock on.*

"That last line," Marta said. "Did you make it up just now?"

"I made it up twenty years ago," Elena said, "when I learned that my father's secrets were safest kept from my mother precisely because she loved him. Fear doesn't torture people, Marta. It tortures people about people."

Marta folded the first summons into the false bottom of her basket, beneath a layer of laundry tickets, and went out into the grey morning feeling taller than she had any right to feel. The snow had stopped. The town lay white and hushed, chimneys standing up out of the drifts like candles on an enormous cake, and somewhere in that cake lived thirty-one households who didn't yet know they had been counted, summoned, believed.

She delivered soap to Frau Lindemann, who talked for ten minutes about her rheumatism and then read the note twice, went very still, and said, "Tell her yes. Tell her I've been waiting two years for someone to ask."

She left tobacco for old Tomas, who couldn't read and made her read it aloud four times, and on the fourth reading his chin began to shake, and he patted her hand and said, "My eyes aren't what they were, but my memory is a bastard. It won't let go of anything."

By the third day she had covered nineteen houses. Nine had said yes outright. Six said they needed to think, which Marta had learned to translate as *yes, later, after I've argued with myself and lost.* Three had said no, and those she wrote down anyway, in her own private margin, because Elena had taught her that even a refusal was information — it told you where the fear was thickest, and therefore where the major's shadow fell longest.

One house frightened her. The clerk from the depot, Herr Weiss, the one with the duplicate receipts. His shutters had been closed for two days, and his neighbor mentioned, casually, terribly, that some officers had come round asking questions about him. Just questions. Just so far.

Marta sat on Weiss's step for a quarter of an hour composing what she'd say, and in the end slipped a blank summons under the door — no name, just the meeting place and time — because Elena's rule was that a man must choose freely, and a named letter pinned to a watched house was not freedom, it was bait.

* * *

On the fifth evening they gathered again at the bakery, and Marta laid out her tally like a general reviewing troops: nineteen visited, fifteen committed, three refused, one silent and possibly hunted.

"Fifteen," Elena said, and wrote the number large, as if the numeral itself needed encouragement.

"Plus your original forty-one, minus the gone and the frightened," Marta said. "Twenty-two confirmed. Twenty-six probable. And the dead ones don't count toward attendance, but they count toward the argument."

"The dead ones," Ulyana said, "are the spine of it. The living give dates. The dead give the reason dates matter." He touched the black ledger, lightly, the way a man touches a shoulder. "Thirty-eight names. Every one omitted from official reports. Every one connected, by road or rail or grave, to those convoys."

Marta watched him and understood, suddenly, something she hadn't on the train. He wasn't keeping the ledger out of defiance, or not only. He was keeping it out of arithmetic too — the same arithmetic Elena believed in. One forgotten man was a tragedy that could be explained away. Thirty-eight was a pattern, and patterns could not be explained away by anyone, not even generals.

"When does the tribunal convene?" she asked.

"Twelve days," Elena said. "Judge Lindqvist arrives in nine."

"Nine days, thirty houses' worth of walking, one courier, and one very suspicious major," Marta counted off on her fingers. "I've carried worse odds. I once carried a hen."

"You did mention the hen."

"Because it bears repeating."

But the laughter faded quickly, because underneath it all three of them could hear the same clock ticking. Nine days was nothing. Reinholt was everywhere and nowhere; the shuttered house of Herr Weiss proved the net was already closing. And Marta, walking home through the blue snow-dark with her empty basket swinging, felt the weight of the remaining twelve summons in the false bottom like a heartbeat against her hip.

She had just turned onto her own street when she saw the sleigh.

It stood outside the Weiss house, dark and official, horses steaming, and two figures in officer's coats were knocking on the clerk's door with the unhurried patience of men who already owned the answer. As Marta froze in the shadow of a woodpile, the door opened a crack, and lamplight spilled out, and she heard — faintly, but unmistakably — a voice she'd come to dread say, pleasantly, the way a knife goes in pleasantly:

"Herr Weiss. So good of you to be home. We'd like a word about your bookkeeping — and about your friends."

Marta pressed back against the woodpile, heart hammering, and did the fastest arithmetic of her life: twelve undelivered summons, eight days, and now the major had begun visiting names from the list — which meant the list was leaking, which meant someone had talked, which meant every house she hadn't reached yet was a race she was suddenly losing.

She turned and ran for the bakery, snow spraying, to tell Elena that the counting had become a countdown.
