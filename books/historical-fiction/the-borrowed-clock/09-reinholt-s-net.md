## 9. Reinholt's Net

Major Reinholt had a theory about fear, and the theory was this: fear was a liquid. It could be poured from one vessel into another. A frightened man would always find someone to frighten in turn — a wife, a clerk, a dog — and the liquid traveled downward until it pooled somewhere it could be examined.

He had learned this as a boy on his father's estate, where the gamekeeper taught him that a wounded hare runs in circles, and a frightened peasant runs toward whatever he loves. You did not chase the hare. You waited at the places it loved.

So when the schoolmaster's assistant — a damp, apologetic young man named Feller who smelled of chalk and terror in roughly equal measure — appeared at the provost's office an hour past midnight, wringing his hat into something resembling a pastry, Reinholt did not offer him a chair. Chairs encouraged people to settle in and elaborate. He let the man stand, which shortened confessions considerably.

"You came to me," Reinholt said, "which means you've already decided what you're going to say. Say it."

Feller said it. The Vasiliev woman — Elena Vasiliev — had been seen speaking with two men at the market, low-voiced, quick-handed. One was Anders Kroll, who kept the toll records at the eastern bridge. The other was Piotr Lem, who drove the mail cart twice a week to Novgorodsk and back. And there had been papers, Feller believed, though he couldn't swear to it. Papers passed hand to hand like something hot out of an oven.

"Did they see you watching?"

"I don't think so, Herr Major."

"Then they will see you again tomorrow, at the same hour, in the same place, doing exactly what you usually do. If either of them so much as nods to you, you will nod back. You will be friendly. You will be *boring*." Reinholt leaned forward. "Do you understand the difference between friendly and boring?"

Feller, visibly, did not.

"Good," said Reinholt. "Neither do most people. It makes you safe."

* * *

By seven in the morning, Kroll was in a cell under the town hall, and by nine, Lem's mail cart had been stopped at the Novgorodsk crossroads with its driver seated very politely in the snow while three soldiers went through his sacks with the delicacy of men looking for eggs.

They found no eggs. They found letters — ordinary letters, family letters, the sort of paper that keeps a war running almost as well as ammunition does — and they found nothing else, which troubled Reinholt more than contraband would have. Empty men confess easily. Empty carts are simply empty.

Kroll, in his cell, confessed everything except anything useful.

"Yes," he admitted readily, hands folded, the picture of a man relieved to finally set down a heavy bag. "I know Frau Vasiliev. Everyone knows Frau Vasiliev. She organized the bread distribution in the famine winter, she paid for Doctor Ammerling's stove, she once pulled my nephew out of the river when I myself was too fat and too frightened to take off my boots. Yes, we spoke at the market. About turnips, Herr Major."

"Turnips."

"The price of them has become criminal. I said so. She agreed. That is the entire conversation, and if it is treason to discuss root vegetables, then arrest me for the turnips and leave my character out of it."

Reinholt studied him. Kroll was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, with the particular stubbornness of a man who had spent thirty years writing down numbers other people told him to write down and had developed, as compensation, opinions. There was fear in him — Reinholt could smell it, faintly, under the cologne of righteousness — but it was fear of a specific shape. Not fear for himself.

Fear for the woman, or for what the woman was carrying. Or both.

"Where were you on Tuesday night?" Reinholt asked.

"At home. With my sister. Ask her; she never lies, she only exaggerates, and even her exaggerations are documented."

Reinholt asked the sister. The sister produced a ledger of her own, in effect: a verbal inventory of every hour, including the precise moment the cat had knocked over the inkwell and Kroll had used language unbecoming a toll officer. It matched. Of course it matched. People who lived their lives in registers were easy to verify and hard to break, because breaking a register only produced more registers.

Lem was worse. Lem said nothing at all. He sat in the second cell with his enormous mail-driver's hands resting on his knees, and he looked at Reinholt with an expression of such mild, patient interest that Reinholt found himself talking more than he intended, filling the silence the way one fills a silent room, and hating himself for it.

"You carried something for her," Reinholt said. "The third time I ask, it stops being a conversation."

Lem considered this. Then he said, in the tone of a man discussing weather: "My mother is eighty-one years old. She lives above the bakery on Kirchstrasse. If I am executed, she will not understand why. She will wait for me. Do you know how long an old woman waits? She'll wait longer than the war, longer than you, longer than the empire itself. She'll die waiting, and the last thing she'll believe is that her Piotr was coming up the road."

And then he folded his hands again and became furniture.

Reinholt left the cell and stood a moment in the corridor, listening to the building settle around him like a large animal digesting.

It was at this point — he would remember this precisely later, the way one remembers the first hairline in a windshield, invisible until suddenly all one can see — that the doubt arrived. Not about his work. His work was sound. But about the shape of the thing he was working for.

Because here was what bothered him, and he permitted himself, alone in the corridor, to phrase it honestly: the Vasilievs were *small*. Oh, the name was old and the house was fine, but the crime laid at their feet was theft of army supply convoys — convoys, plural, moving through military corridors, signed for by military hands. Civilians did not steal convoys. Civilians could not steal convoys. A convoy was not a purse left on a table; it was a column of wagons with armed escorts and a manifest countersigned at three stations. For the Vasilievs to have taken those supplies, the army would have had to hand them over, wagon by wagon, and sign politely each time.

Which meant either the tribunal was prosecuting the wrong people — impossible; General Hess had personally assured him of the case's soundness — or it was prosecuting the *convenient* ones, and the word *convenient*, once admitted into the mind, was a splinter that worked deeper with every step.

Reinholt was a loyal man. Loyalty was the whole architecture of him; take it away and he was mostly posture and good boots. But loyalty, properly understood, ran upward *and* downward. He had spent twenty-two years believing that these were the same direction. Standing in the corridor outside Lem's cell, listening to an old woman's name sit in the air like a struck bell, Reinholt felt the two directions separate by a degree — barely a degree, nothing a compass would register.

He dismissed the feeling. It returned. He dismissed it again. It was, he noticed, becoming better at waiting than he was at dismissing.

* * *

The notice went up by noon, printed in three languages because Krasnogorsk was that kind of town, and posted on the churchyard gate, the market posts, and the door of the town hall itself:

**NOTICE TO ALL PERSONS**

A deserter is at large in this district. The individual travels as a nurse and carries falsified identity documents. Description follows: male, approximately thirty years of age, medium height, light-brown hair, speaks German without regional accent, may present in nurse's uniform or civilian dress. This person is dangerous and is suspected of carrying stolen military property.

Any citizen providing information leading to capture will be compensated. Any citizen harboring this individual will be treated as an accomplice.

— Major E. Reinholt, Provost Command

Reinholt wrote the description himself, from the fragments Brandt's household staff had given the military police before the colonel died: the visitor, they said, who came the last week, who sat with the colonel behind closed doors, who wore the grays of a field nurse and whose hands were steady in the way that meant he'd held too many of them still over dying men.

He read the finished notice twice. Something in it nagged him — the phrase *stolen military property*, which was technically true of the ledger if the ledger existed and was nothing at all if it didn't — but notices were not written to satisfy their authors. Notices were written to make a town's eyes useful.

And a town's eyes, Reinholt knew, were the finest instrument ever devised. Better than cavalry. Cavalry got bored. Eyes took offense, grew jealous, remembered slights. Every town contained forty people who would sell their neighbor for a warm coat and another forty who would do it for free, out of principle.

Within a day, the reports began arriving. A nurse seen at the Novgorodsk depot — false, a Red Cross Sister of fifty-three years, verified and released, though she had been made late for her train and complained with such vigor that the checkpoint sergeant requested transfer to the front, where, he said, the people shot at you but did not *nag*. A nurse lodging at the inn near the mill — false again, a medical student home on leave, indignant, female, and armed with a father who owned half the flour contracts in the district. A nurse walking the river road at dawn — investigated; turned out to be a scarecrow that had lost its coat to the wind and stood, in the gray light, with a poignancy that embarrassed everyone involved.

Reinholt sorted the reports with the patience of a man shelling peas. False, false, false, plausible, false, absurd, plausible. Two plausibles. He sent men after both.

But underneath the shelling, the splinter worked.

Because here was the arithmetic that would not resolve. If the deserter carried proof of the convoy thefts — and Brandt had died with the urgency of a man carrying exactly that — then the proof named someone. Ledgers always named someone. And Hess wanted the Vasilievs convicted *before the armistice*, which meant the proof, if surfaced, would implicate someone the armistice would otherwise protect. Armistices protected victors and vanished crimes. Who was protected by an armistice?

Officers. Command. Men whose signatures were on manifests.

Men like—

Reinholt put down the report he was holding, walked to the window, and looked out at the snow-covered square with the expression of a man doing long division against his will.

No. He shut the thought away with both hands, the way one shuts a door against weather. He had a patron, and a patron was a roof, and a roof was not interrogated about where its beams came from. General Hess had taken an interest in him, promoted him, trusted him with this district, and a man did not audit trust. Trust was the currency that made the machine run, and machines did not run on suspicion.

Besides — he told himself, straightening — the case was nearly closed. Kroll and Lem in custody. The courier network, whatever its shape, amputated at the wrist. The deserter described in three languages on every post in the district. Within a week, perhaps less, the net would close, the tribunal would proceed, order would be restored, and he would stop having thoughts he didn't authorize.

He almost believed it.

* * *

In the evening, a runner brought word from the bridge garrison: a peddler crossing east claimed to have seen, four days prior, a man answering the notice's description traveling with a refugee convoy out of Riga — traveling, the peddler emphasized, in the company of a girl, maybe fourteen, sharp-faced, who had asked him questions about back roads.

Back roads. Reinholt read the word twice. Deserter nurses did not ask about back roads unless they had a reason to avoid roads, and reasons came in two varieties: guilt and destination. Everything about this man — Brandt's deathbed urgency, the forged papers, the flight east — pointed at destination. Pointed at Krasnogorsk.

Which meant he was not fleeing through the district. He was *arriving* in it. Perhaps already arrived. Sitting somewhere in the town's ten thousand shadows, holding whatever Brandt had pressed into his hands, waiting for the net to look elsewhere.

Reinholt gave the orders quietly: double the patrols at night, watch the churchyard, watch the chapel, watch the Vasiliev house itself — discreetly, no uniforms in sight of the windows. And add a question to every interrogation henceforth, one question, small and harmless-seeming: *Have you seen anyone asking about back roads?*

Then he sat alone with the day's reports, and the lamp, and the snow ticking against the glass, and permitted himself one minute of the honesty he rationed so strictly.

Kroll, who discussed turnips like a treaty. Lem, who spoke of his mother the way other men swore oaths. An old woman above a bakery who would wait forever. A girl of fourteen on the back roads. A dead schoolteacher's assistant who had come trembling in the dark to give up his neighbors, and whom Reinholt had thanked with a pat on the shoulder that Feller would probably treasure for years, God help him.

None of these people had stolen a convoy. None of them *could have*.

And yet the tribunal was scheduled, the verdict pre-written in Hess's own hand — Reinholt had seen the draft, had admired its cleanliness — and the machinery was turning, oiled and certain, toward crushing exactly these people.

*A roof is not interrogated about where its beams come from.*

He thought of his father saying it, or something like it, over the estate accounts, and understood for the first time that his father had been describing a house he knew was rotten and could not afford to say so aloud.

Reinholt snuffed the lamp and sat in the dark a moment longer than he needed to.

Outside, far across the sleeping town, a light moved briefly in the upper window of a house on the hill — the Vasiliev house, dark now for weeks, confined and shuttered. One candle, guttering, then gone.

Someone was inside who should not have been able to light a candle.

Reinholt reached for his boots.
