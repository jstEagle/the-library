## 10. The Chapel Cellar

The chapel of Saint Ansgar had no priest anymore. Father Ambrose had been conscripted two years past to serve as a stretcher-bearer at the front, and the townsfolk said — in the way townsfolk say things, half prayer and half gossip — that he carried men more gently than he had ever carried sermons. What he left behind was a building of grey stone and a cellar that smelled of turnips, candle wax, and forty years of honest damp.

Ulyana crouched in that cellar now, among eleven witnesses and a crate of pickled beets, listening to the sound of boots on gravel above them.

It is a strange thing, he thought, how much of courage is merely the discipline of not moving. He had learned it at the dressing station: when a shell landed close, the wounded who thrashed bled faster. Stillness was survival. Stillness was a skill, like bandaging, and you got better at it by practicing on your own terror until the two became indistinguishable.

Above them, Major Reinholt's men were conducting what the major would have called a thorough and orderly search, and what Marta — before she'd gone below with the others — had called *men kicking over other people's soup pots*.

The witnesses shifted in the dark. Old Halvard, the fishmonger whose testimony about the convoy dates mattered more than he knew, had a cough he was holding in his chest like a bird in cupped hands. Ulyana could hear him fighting it. Beside him, his wife knitted in the dark — actually knitted, needles clicking softly, because she declared that if she must hide from soldiers in a root cellar, she would at least finish her son's scarf while doing so. The clicking stopped whenever the boots came near, then resumed, faithful as a heartbeat.

Ulyana counted heads again. Eleven. It did not change. Counting was another form of stillness.

Elena Vasiliev sat against the far wall, her back straight even here, even in the dark, as though posture were a country she refused to surrender. She had not wanted this — hiding, waiting, the whole indignity of it. "A woman should not have to be smuggled through her own town," she had said, and Ulyana had answered, gently, that the town was not hers to walk through just now; it belonged to the major's search parties, and they were welcome to it until spring or until common sense returned, whichever came first.

She had not laughed. But something in her face had loosened, which from Elena was worth more than laughter.

The trapdoor opened an inch. A sliver of grey daylight fell across the beets, and Marta's whisper slid down through it like a thread through a needle's eye.

"Three of them at the churchyard gate. The major himself is standing by the lych-gate talking to the verger. They're going door to door after this." A pause. "And Ulyana — they've posted your description at the square. *Nurse, tall, light eyes, traveling under forged papers.* Your eyes are apparently very memorable."

"Tell them I've been ill," Ulyana whispered back. "Tell them my eyes are quite ordinary."

"They also say you have kind hands."

He looked down at his hands in the dark. Kind hands. Twenty years of bandages and splints and held-final-breaths had indeed left their signature on them — a nurse's hands could no sooner pass for a farmer's than a lark could pass for a crow. That was the trouble with being remembered kindly: kindness left marks.

"I need to leave," he said. "If they find me here, they find everyone here."

"We know," Marta said. "That's why I came down early. Watchmaker Sorel has been thinking."

Everyone in Krasnogorsk knew watchmaker Sorel, mostly because Sorel knew everyone else's business to the quarter-hour. If Sorel had been thinking, then a plan already existed, complete with contingencies, and probably a small brass fitting somewhere in it.

* * *

The plan, delivered an hour later in whispers through the trapdoor, was elegant in the way of things made by a man who repaired small mechanisms: it depended entirely on no one looking too closely.

There was to be a burial that afternoon. Old Widow Lundgren, dead three days and waiting, as the dead do patiently, for circumstances to permit her funeral. The gravedigger, Torsten, was a large man of seventy with a bad knee and a good relationship with schnapps, and Sorel proposed that Torsten spend the afternoon being indisposed while a substitute finished digging Widow Lundgren's resting place and walked out through the churchyard gate, spade on shoulder, head bowed, entirely unremarkable.

"You are six inches too short and forty pounds too light," Elena had said flatly, when the plan reached the cellar.

"I will wear his coat," Ulyana said. "And I will walk like the work matters more than the walking. Nobody studies a gravedigger, Elena. Gravediggers are the one profession the living agree not to look at."

"That is precisely what worries me."

But there was nothing else, and they both knew it, so the argument ended the way arguments end in cellars: quietly, and with someone knitting harder than before.

* * *

Torsten turned out to be exactly as advertised — enormous, wheezy, and delighted to spend an afternoon asleep behind his shed with a bottle for company, provided the stranger paid him in tobacco and flattery. His coat fit Ulyana the way a tent fits a chair, but a rope belt solved most of that, and the rest could be attributed to hard winters. Torsten's cap came down nearly to Ulyana's eyebrows, which was a mercy, and Torsten's spade was heavier than any instrument Ulyana had ever carried, which was a lesson.

Marta inspected him at the shed door with the seriousness of a general reviewing troops. She had taken to him months ago, in a train car full of strangers, with a frankness that had startled him then and steadied him since. Now she tugged the cap lower, smeared a thumb of grave-dirt along his jaw — "for atmosphere" — and stepped back.

"You look like a man who digs holes," she pronounced.

"I have never received higher praise."

"Don't be clever at the gate. Clever people get remembered. Be boring. Boring people survive wars." She hesitated, and for a moment the courier vanished and a fifteen-year-old stood in her place, frightened. "Walk slow. If you run, they'll know. Nobody runs carrying a spade unless they're guilty."

"Where did you learn all this?"

"My father was a smuggler," she said, with the pride other children reserved for admirals. "Of letters. Which is smuggling, only poorer." Then she was the courier again, brisk and bright. "Gate. Lych-gate. Major. Go."

* * *

The churchyard in late afternoon was the color of old pewter. Snow lay thin over the graves like a sheet drawn up to their chins. Ulyana dug — actually dug, because a hole half-finished would draw questions, and because the rhythm of the work was the only thing keeping his heart from hammering loud enough to be heard at the gate. Spade in, spade out. Earth, frozen at the crust, soft beneath. Somewhere under this yard slept half the town's history, including, he supposed, eventually, everyone currently arguing about supply convoys. There was a comfort in that. The ground kept better records than the army did.

Widow Lundgren deserved better than to have her grave dug by a fugitive in a borrowed coat, but Ulyana found he could apologize silently as he worked, and that the apology helped. He had buried enough men without names. This woman had a name, a plot, mourners expected tomorrow. He dug neatly, squared the corners, and thought: *I am getting strange satisfaction from proper grave-digging, and I will examine that later, if there is a later.*

Then the voice came, from the direction of the lych-gate, and every muscle he owned announced itself at once.

"Gravedigger."

He did not stop. That was the first rule, the one Marta had taught him without knowing she was teaching it: keep the rhythm. Spade in. Spade out. He straightened slowly, the way a tired old man straightens, letting the coat hang off him, letting the cap shadow his eyes, and turned.

Major Reinholt stood inside the gate with his hands clasped behind his back and his breath steaming in the cold air. Up close, and in this light, the major looked less like a hunter than like a man carrying furniture nobody wanted — burdened, upright, and privately tired. Ulyana noted it the way he noted symptoms: not with pity yet, but with diagnosis.

"The widow's funeral is tomorrow," Reinholt said.

Ulyana pitched his voice low and rough, a voice that lived in the throat and asked nothing of the world but to be left alone. "Ground's hard, sir. Starting early."

"Efficient." The major studied him. Not the way a soldier studies a suspect — worse, the way a quartermaster studies inventory, checking each item against a list. "You're not Torsten."

The words went through Ulyana like cold water through cloth. Spade in. Spade out. Keep breathing like a man with a bad knee and a bottle behind a shed.

"Torsten's hip," Ulyana rasped. "His nephew sent me. From Velling." He let a pause sit there, the kind of pause a dull man makes while assembling simple sentences. "Paid in tobacco."

"Hm." Reinholt walked a slow half-circle around the open grave, and Ulyana understood suddenly why wolves circled: it wasn't cruelty, it was geometry. You circle to see what the thing does when approached from angles it didn't choose. "There's a man we're looking for," the major said conversationally. "A nurse. Tall fellow. Light eyes. Very kind hands."

"Kind hands, sir?" Ulyana bent, took up a clod of frozen earth, crumbled it between his palms — a gravedigger testing soil, nothing more, though the gesture cost him everything he had. "Wouldn't know. Hands out here are all the same by February. Frozen."

Something flickered across Reinholt's face. Not suspicion — recognition. The particular expression of a man hearing an echo he can't place. Ulyana watched it arrive and watched the major refuse it, and thought: *he almost knows my voice. He heard it at the dressing station, asking after the dead.* But a gravedigger's rasp is a good disguise, and fatigue does wonders for the memory, and the major had, in the last weeks, heard a great many voices asking inconvenient questions.

"Do you see many people crossing this yard at night, gravedigger?"

"Dead ones, sir. Mostly."

Reinholt almost smiled. Ulyana saw it happen — a small crack in the official face, quickly mortared over — and understood that this was the hairline fissure Elena had spoken of, the doubt growing in the loyal man like frost in a wall. The major did not want to believe the town was hiding anything. Believing it would cost him something he hadn't finished counting.

"If you do," Reinholt said, turning back toward the gate, "there are men at the guardhouse who would want to know. And there is a reward for useful information. Enough to winter on."

"Winter's nearly done, sir."

"So it is." The major paused at the lych-gate, one hand on the iron, and looked back — at the grave, at the bowed figure with the spade, at the whole grey quiet scene — and said, softly, almost to himself: "Everything ends, doesn't it. One way or another."

"Yes, sir," said Ulyana, keeper of names, digger of borrowed graves. "That's rather the point of the yard."

The major held the look a moment longer. Then he nodded, once — to the gravedigger, or perhaps to the grave — and went out through the gate, and his boots faded down the frozen road, and Ulyana stood in the churchyard with the spade trembling very slightly in his hands and did not allow himself to shake until the sound was entirely gone.

Courage, he decided, leaning on the spade and breathing the grey air, was mostly this: the ability to keep walking while terrified, and the wisdom to wait until you're alone to fall apart. He gave himself ten seconds of falling apart, kneeling beside Widow Lundgren's neat square hole, forehead against the spade handle.

Nine seconds were enough. He saved the tenth.

* * *

By dark he was back in the chapel cellar, coat exchanged for Torsten's bottle and gratitude, and the witnesses emerged into the vestry one by one to hear the news. Reinholt's men had searched the baker's, the schoolhouse, the cooperage, and the inn, finding nothing but dust and one incriminating quantity of hidden schnapps belonging to the mayor. The net had closed on empty air. For tonight.

Elena listened, arms folded, and when he finished she said, "He spoke to you. Face to face."

"He complimented the yard's honesty."

"And you answered him."

"Gravediggers answer, Elena. Silence gets remembered."

She studied him a long moment, and he saw her doing arithmetic — the risk he'd carried, the seconds at the gate, the distance between his voice and discovery. Whatever sum she reached, she kept it to herself. "Tomorrow," she said instead, "we move them north, in twos and threes. Marta knows the routes. And Ulyana—" she stopped, and for once the stubbornness in her face gave way to something plainer, older. "Thank you. For keeping walking."

"It's the only thing I'm reliably good at."

"No," she said. "It isn't. But it may be the most important."

They were still arranging the first departures when Marta came down the chapel steps two at a time, breathless, snow melting in her hair, and the look on her face stopped the room. She had carried terrible news before; this was different. This was news arriving like weather.

"There's a chaplain," she said, catching her breath. "Father Anselm, from the garrison hospital — he came to the watchmaker's shop at dusk, wouldn't come in, talked through the door. He says Colonel Brandt wrote a second letter. Before he died. He gave it to the chaplain for safekeeping." Her eyes went around the room and settled on Ulyana. "He says it names the convoy. Dates, manifests, everything. He wants to know — he asked specifically — whether anyone can get it to Judge Lindqvist before the tribunal opens."

The cellar air seemed to change pressure. Ulyana felt the ledger against his ribs, Brandt's first letter long since passed to Elena, and thought of the dying colonel pressing wax into his hands — *one letter, nurse; I was only able to write one clearly* — and understood at last what the colonel had meant.

Brandt had known. Dying, hunted, certain of nothing, he had built a second door into the wall, and trusted it to a priest.

"Tell the chaplain," Ulyana said, and heard his own voice steady in a way his knees were not, "that yes. Someone can."
