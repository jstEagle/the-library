## 11. A Name Struck Through

The cellar smelled of turnips and candle smoke, and Elena Vasiliev had begun to think of it as a second home, which was either an adaptation or a surrender, depending on the hour.

She sat at the trestle table beneath the chapel with her lists spread before her like a general's maps, though they were nothing of the kind. They were names. Farmers' names. Clerks' names. A widow who had signed for flour that never came. A railway porter who remembered the convoy dates because they were the same dates his daughter was born, which was how men like him kept history — stitched to the small private calendar of their lives.

Ulyana sat across from her, copying in his careful nurse's hand, the ledger open beside her papers so that the dead and the living lay together on the table, shoulder to shoulder, as if sharing a bench.

"You've written 'Karl Ostrin' twice," she said.

"He's important enough to say twice."

"Everyone is important enough once. Twice is favoritism."

"I favor the ones still breathing," Ulyana said mildly, striking out the second entry. "It's a prejudice I've developed."

Elena almost smiled. Almost. The smile had been rationed lately, like everything else in Krasnogorsk — bread, lamp oil, hope. Three days since Reinholt's men had taken Henrik Ostrin and old Master Alvars from the bakery where they'd been collecting witness statements, three days since the town had learned what it cost to look at her too long in the street. People crossed now when they saw her coming, not out of rudeness but out of arithmetic. They were calculating whether knowing her would be worth their sons' conscription, their daughters' reputations, their shops' licenses. She did not blame them for the sums. She only wished the answer were easier to arrive at than it evidently was.

Marta came down the ladder with the particular lightness she used when the news above was bad — Elena had learned to read the girl's feet before her face. Marta landed on the packed earth, brushed off her skirt with great ceremony, and announced, "Nothing."

"Nothing," Elena repeated.

"The major's office has shut its windows. There are two soldiers at the schoolmaster's house and one at Ostrin's shop. And Frau Lindt says—" Marta hesitated, and for once the courier discipline failed her; her voice dropped into something younger. "Frau Lindt says there were lights on in the guardhouse all night. And shouting."

No one spoke. In the candlelight, the ledger pages looked very white.

"They're old men," Elena said at last, and heard how thin her own voice sounded, like paper worn at a fold. "Ostrin is sixty-one. Alvars taught half this town its letters. He used to rap my knuckles for writing *v* backward." She flexed her fingers as if the sting might still be found there, forty years on. "He'd have been within his rights to do it again. I wrote his name into this business."

"You wrote his name into history," Ulyana said.

"It's remarkable how often those turn out to be the same list," she said, "and how differently the entries read."

* * *

They learned what happened in the guardhouse the way the town learned everything: sideways, in fragments, through mouths that would deny having spoken.

Frau Lindt brought it herself, under cover of delivering bread to the chapel — the priest's household still had standing to receive charity, one of the few privileges of the confessional economy. She set the loaf down, glanced at the ladder, and said to the air in general, in the loud clear voice women use when they wish to be overheard by exactly one listener:

"My cousin cleans for the major's clerk. The old teacher — they questioned him two nights. He would not give them the other names. Not one." A pause, the loaf adjusted. "His heart gave out. That is what the report will say. His heart."

"And the report will be true enough," Ulyana said quietly, when she had gone. "A heart can be given out by others."

Elena stood very still among her lists. She had imagined this moment, in the way one imagines all the costs of a thing beforehand precisely so that they will feel paid for in advance, and she discovered that imagination had been useless. Imagined grief is a coin that turns out not to be legal tender when the bill arrives.

Alvars. Master Alvars, who had kept order in a classroom of forty boys with nothing but a ruler and a raised eyebrow. Who had told her father, at some village supper Elena barely remembered, that a town gets the government it deserves but never the spelling it deserves, and everyone had laughed, and she had been proud without knowing why, being seven. He had been the first adult who treated her mind as furniture meant to be used rather than dusted. And he had gone into Reinholt's guardhouse carrying her summons in his coat pocket, and he had come out carried.

She heard herself saying, "I want to see him."

"Elena—"

"Not the body. I know better than to ask for the body. I want to know where they've put him. I want to know that he isn't lying on a floor somewhere waiting for a clerk to fill out a form." Her hands had closed on the edge of the table hard enough to whiten. "We write down every name we save. It seems obscene not to know where the one we lost is *lying*."

Ulyana looked at her for a long moment. Then he began rolling down his sleeves. "Gravediggers go where clerks don't," he said. "Wait for dark."

* * *

What he brought back, near midnight, was worse than a location and better than nothing, which was the narrow band within which all news now arrived.

"They have him in the chapel crypt," Ulyana said, crouched by the candle with snow melting off his shoulders. "Not the churchyard — the crypt. Behind the old iron gate. The sexton told me the major ordered no burial yet, pending — and here I quote the sexton, who quotes the corporal, who quotes God knows whom — *pending administrative resolution.*"

"They're keeping him as evidence."

"They're keeping him as leverage. A dead man you haven't buried is a question you haven't answered. Reinholt knows families make noise about graves. Noise draws priests. Priests draw congregations." Ulyana warmed his hands at the flame. "There's a chaplain attached to the tribunal staff now. Father Anselm. Young, frightened, and — the sexton thinks — honest. Honest young chaplains are the most dangerous people in any army, including to themselves."

Elena sat down slowly. "You've thought about this a great deal. Where to put bodies. Which priests can be trusted."

"I was a nurse at a dressing station for two years, Elena. Everything I know is about where to put bodies and whom to trust." He said it without self-pity, the way one reports weather, and somehow that made it land harder. "But yes. I've thought about it."

"And?"

"And nothing. Yet." He met her eyes. "Yet."

* * *

That night she could not sleep, and so she went back down into the cellar alone, because the lists did not sleep either, and someone ought to sit with them.

By candlelight she reread the page bearing Master Alvars's name. He had given it willingly — she remembered the evening, only eleven days ago, an eternity ago, when he had come to the house by the garden wall after dark and listened to her explanation with his head tipped, the way he had listened to recitations, letting the errors accumulate visibly before correcting them all at once.

"You understand," he had said, when she finished, "that I am too old for this to cost me much. They can take my pension, my house, my library. My remaining assets are opinions and a bad knee. But you should count the cost for the others, girl. You should count it *before*, not after. Counting after is what governments do."

"I'm counting," she had told him.

"Mm," he had said, in the tone that meant *we shall see*, and had signed his name in his beautiful schoolmaster's copperplate, the *A* formed like a little roof over the rest of the word, sheltering it.

Now she dipped the pen and drew a single line through the name. Not to erase it. To mark it, the way her ledger-keeping partner across the table marked his — struck through meant finished, meant concluded, meant *entered into the permanent record*. But her hand shook on the stroke and the line went crooked, and crookedness in that page felt like a confession.

She heard the ladder creak and knew it was Ulyana before his boots touched earth. He came and stood beside her, reading the page upside down with the ease of long practice.

"You struck him through."

"He's finished."

"He's *transferred*." Ulyana pulled out the chair opposite and sat, drawing his own ledger toward him, opening it to the section Elena tried not to look at, the growing section, the section that had started as a handful of omitted names at Riga and become a census of everything this war preferred to forget. "In mine, no one is ever struck through. They're only added. Watch."

He wrote, in that unhurried hand: *Alvars, Tomas. Schoolmaster. Taught the town its letters. Refused to speak. Died refusing.* He turned the book around so she could read it.

"That's not an entry," she said, when she could. "That's an epitaph."

"All my entries are epitaphs. That's rather the point. The army writes reports; I write the other thing." He blotted the line. "Reports are what happened according to authority. This is what happened according to witnesses. History is just the argument between them, carried on longer than anyone enjoys."

"And which side wins?"

"Whichever keeps better records."

She wanted to laugh at that and found instead that her throat had closed. She pressed her knuckles to her mouth for a moment, an old habit from funerals, from her mother's funeral specifically, and when she lowered them she said, in a voice she did not recognize as her own:

"I keep telling myself it's worth it. I've been telling myself for weeks. Every name gathered is a nail in Hess's coffin, every witness is a stone in the wall against Reinholt, and it's all arithmetic, clean arithmetic, and then an old man dies on a guardhouse floor with my handwriting in his pocket and the arithmetic stops being clean. It stops being arithmetic at all. It becomes just — *cost*. Cost paid by people who trusted me." She looked at the crooked strike-through. "For the first time, Ulyana, I don't know if I'm building something or merely spending people."

Ulyana was quiet for a while. The candle fluttered in the cellar draught, and somewhere overhead a beam ticked as the frost worked at the chapel walls.

"When I was at the dressing station," he said finally, "there was a surgeon named Weiss who drank. Badly, openly, the whole staff knew. One night I asked him how he stood it, and he said something I've carried ever since. He said: *Nurse, the question is never whether you can save them all. The question is whether the ones you cannot save died for something or for nothing. Because that part is up to you.*" He closed his ledger. "Alvars didn't die for your lists. He died because Reinholt needed a lesson taught and chose the oldest, gentlest man available, on the theory that gentleness breaks fastest. He was wrong about that. Alvars held. Two nights, and he held. So the only question left is yours: does it mean something, or nothing? And I've watched you work, Elena Vasiliev. I already know your answer. I think you do too. You're just angry that the answer comes with a price tag."

"It shouldn't be *him*," she said, and hated the childishness of it even as she said it. "It shouldn't be the kindest ones first."

"It's always the kindest ones first. Kindness volunteers." He rose and took the candle, and in its shifting light his face looked both very tired and very steady. "Grief and fury, Elena. Keep both. They're fuel. But don't let them talk you into quitting, because that's precisely what they're for — theirs, I mean. Reinholt's. Grief that makes you stop is a weapon he fired."

She sat alone a while after he climbed up. Then she took the pen again and, beneath the crooked strike-through, added a line of her own, small, in the margin: *Counted before. Counted after. Both counts stand.*

It wasn't arithmetic. But it was hers.

* * *

Morning brought Marta down the ladder two rungs at a time, breathless, snow in her hair, holding a scrap of paper like it might hatch.

"There's a printer's boy paying taverns to say a thing," she gasped. "Same words in every tavern — Frau Lindt's cousin confirmed it. He's saying the war ends inside a month. Armistice. Real talks, real dates, maybe less than a month." She caught her breath. "And Elena — the boy's other message, the part they're paying him extra to spread quiet, in the right ears — is that once peace is signed, the tribunal dissolves. All charges lapse. Everyone walks free."

For one wild heartbeat Elena felt relief rise in her like floodwater — *everyone walks free, Henrik walks free, the town walks free* —

And then she saw Ulyana's face, and the floodwater went cold.

"No," she said slowly, working it through. "No — if the tribunal dissolves, the verdict dissolves. If there's no conviction, there's no crime on record. The theft simply... never happened. Hess keeps everything, Reinholt gets his promotion for closing the file, and the Vasilievs aren't cleared. We're *unrecorded*." Her hand found the table edge again. "Peace doesn't save us. Peace erases us. They need us convicted before the clock runs out — and now they'll know we know the clock is running."

Ulyana was already pulling his coat from its nail.

"Where are you going?"

"To find Father Anselm," he said, buckling it. "Because if the tribunal dies in a month, then Brandt's proof needs to reach Judge Lindqvist in days, not weeks. And I think I know which honest young chaplain can carry it past the guards — if we can convince him to endanger himself, which, as I recall, honest young chaplains find very difficult."

He paused at the ladder, looking back at her, and for a moment neither of them moved.

"A month," Elena said. "Maybe less."

"Then we'd best be quicker than the peace," Ulyana said, and climbed.
