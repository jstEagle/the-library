## 12. The Armistice Rumor

Major Reinholt had a theory about rumors, and the theory was this: a rumor was a fire, and fires could be starved. You did not fight them by shouting them down — shouting was oxygen. You starved them. You controlled the bread, the post, the church bells, and the mouths of men who drank too much at the Golden Stag, and eventually the rumor suffocated in the dark like everything else.

For three years, his theory had held.

On the morning of December 4th, it failed him completely.

He knew it had failed before he reached the town square, because he heard the rumor being discussed by two of his own military policemen, who were supposed to be starving it, and who were instead feeding it buttered rolls.

"—weeks, I heard. Maybe three. The Americans are coming and everyone's tired—"

"Three weeks? My cousin says before Christmas."

"Your cousin says a lot of things."

"Your mother says a lot of things," said the first policeman, with the serene confidence of a man whose mother did, in fact, say a great many things, mostly accurate.

Reinholt cleared his throat. Both men snapped to attention so fast that one of the buttered rolls performed a small, tragic arc and landed in the snow, where it was immediately claimed by a crow that had clearly been waiting for exactly this moment and considered it a professional arrangement.

"Sergeant Kroll," Reinholt said. "Private Danner."

"Sir."

"Sir."

"A word about what your mothers and cousins are saying."

* * *

The trouble, he reflected later, pacing his office above the requisitioned bakery — the whole building smelled of yesterday's rye, which made every report he read feel faintly like a menu — was that you could not court-martial a rumor. He had tried, in a manner of speaking. He had ordered the telegraph clerk to stop repeating anything he hadn't read himself. He had reminded the provost that loose talk was treason. He had even, in a moment of weakness he was not proud of, offered the baker's wife a ration of coffee to keep her customers' ears full of prices instead of politics.

None of it mattered, because the rumor was true. That was the unbearable part. A false rumor you could strangle; a true rumor had roots, and roots went down through the whole soil of a thing, past every layer you controlled, into bedrock you didn't.

The armistice was coming. Perhaps in weeks. Perhaps sooner. And when it came, the machinery of justice — such as it was, such as it had been built to be — would grind to a halt mid-turn, and everything inside it would simply... remain there. Unresolved. Unpunished. *Unproven.*

Reinholt stood at his window and watched the snow come down on Krasnogorsk with the patient malice of weather that had all winter and no opinions.

Below, across the square, the Vasiliev house sat under guard. He could see the sentry stamping his feet, see the thin smoke from their chimney, see the dark shape of Elena Vasiliev moving behind the frosted glass of an upstairs window — a woman doing something domestic, something ordinary, watering a plant perhaps, or straightening a curtain, as if she were not the most dangerous object in the town.

That was what he had never managed to explain to Hess, back when Hess still took his calls: the danger wasn't in what she'd done. It was in what she *was*. A woman who watered her plants while awaiting trial for treason had either already surrendered or had decided the trial was beneath her, and Reinholt had known Elena Vasiliev for eleven days now, long enough to rule out surrender.

* * *

The telegram came at four o'clock, and it came, as all important things seemed to lately, through channels that insulted him personally.

It was not addressed to him. It was addressed to the garrison commandant, a pleasant half-wit named Oberst Feller whose principal qualifications were a fine mustache and a family connection to someone important, and it was marked FOR EYES OF SENIOR STAFF, which meant Feller read it, understood perhaps a third of it, and then walked it over to Reinholt with the expression of a schoolboy delivering homework he hoped would not be examined.

"You'll want to see this," Feller said, holding it out at arm's length as though it might bite. "It has numbers in it."

"It has numbers in it," Reinholt repeated.

"Several." Feller lingered. "Also, the baker's wife says you've been buying coffee off her, which is technically hoarding, and I've decided not to care, but I thought you should know I know. As colleagues."

"Thank you, Oberst. Your discretion is a monument."

Feller left, satisfied, and Reinholt read the telegram twice, standing very still, while the smell of rye bread rose around him like the ghost of a normal world.

The substance was simple. The armistice negotiations were progressing; a cessation of hostilities might be signed within the month. All pending tribunals were to be concluded *before* signature wherever conviction was material to ongoing operations. In plain language: finish the Vasiliev case first. Make it stick. After the guns went quiet, no one in Berlin or anywhere else would spend political capital un-convicting two civilians over a supply ledger, and General Hess's exposure would be sealed under the armistice forever — buried, like everything else, in the great forgiving snowfall of peace.

There was a second paragraph. It contained numbers, as promised. It also contained a sentence Reinholt read three times:

*General Hess will arrive in Krasnogorsk to oversee the conclusion of proceedings personally.*

Hess. Coming here. To his town, his case, his careful, methodical, nearly-finished work.

Reinholt set the telegram down on his desk and discovered his hand was not entirely steady, which interested him. He was forty-six years old. He had been shot at in '14, shelled in '15, and verbally destroyed by a quartermaster-general in '16 in a meeting he still thought about at night. Very little made his hand shake.

But Hess coming to Krasnogorsk was not a visit. It was an inspection. And inspections were how loyal men learned what they were worth.

* * *

He worked until midnight, because work was the only conversation that never lied to him.

The case file lay open under the lamp. Two witnesses in custody — the cooper Anselm Brandt (no relation to the colonel, though Reinholt had checked twice, hoping) and a farmhand named Piet Lukas. The cooper was frightened and holding. The farmhand was frightened and crumbling; you could see the crack running through him like a flaw through ice, widening daily. Reinholt had interrogated enough men to know that Piet Lukas would talk within the week, and that what he said would lead to the chapel cellar, and that the cellar would give up the rest of them like a pocket turned out onto a table.

So why did it feel like losing?

He poured himself the last of the baker's-wife coffee and admitted the thing he had been not-admitting since the telegram arrived: because the clock was running both directions now, and he could suddenly hear it.

Every day the tribunal did not convene was a day closer to the armistice — but also a day closer to whatever Elena Vasiliev and her invisible nurse were building in the dark. He had lost people before. Not to bullets. To paper. A name moved from one column to another; a witness transferred to a garrison two hundred kilometers away; a report filed under the wrong date, innocently, regretfully. He had done it himself, early in the war, before he understood that paperwork was just violence with better posture. Someone was doing it to him now. Somewhere in his tidy machine there was a seam, and through the seam things were escaping: letters, lists, witnesses, hope.

And now the deadline. If the armistice arrived before the verdict, the crime evaporated — not forgiven, merely *forgotten*, which was worse, because forgiveness at least required someone to remember. But if the verdict came first, if Hess got his scapegoats convicted and shot the record closed—

Then the theft became history. Then it became *true*, in the only way that mattered: written, stamped, archived. Then Colonel Brandt had died in a dressing station with his proof burning a hole in a nurse's coat for nothing.

Reinholt caught himself on that thought and stood very still.

*For nothing.* Where had that come from? Since when did he carry opinions about what Brandt's death was worth?

He sat down heavily. The chair creaked its familiar complaint. Outside, the snow kept falling, and somewhere below in the square the sentry stamped his feet, and behind the frosted glass the dark shape of Elena Vasiliev moved through her ordinary evening, watering whatever needed watering.

Here was the truth he kept circling and refusing to land on: he had believed in this case once. Not in the scapegoating — he was a career officer, not a monster, and he had told himself the comfortable lie that he merely executed policy — but in the *order* of it. Someone stole supplies meant for men freezing at the front. Men died of that theft; Reinholt had signed the casualty reports; he knew the arithmetic of missing coats in February. Somebody had to answer for it. He had genuinely believed the somebody was the Vasilievs, because believing that was easier than investigating upward, and because the evidence — Hess's evidence, curated, immaculate, arriving always exactly on time — had been so beautifully complete.

Beautifully complete. That phrase again. He had used it in his own report, in September. *The documentary record is beautifully complete.*

In twenty-five years of service, Reinholt had never once seen a real case that was beautiful. Real cases had gaps like missing teeth. Real cases smelled of rye bread and panic and contradictory statements taken down in pencil. Beautiful cases were written backward, from the verdict to the evidence, by men who already knew the ending.

Colonel Brandt had known it too. That was the thought that finally landed, cold as the window glass. Brandt had been Reinholt's instructor once, years ago — map reading, logistics, the moral geometry of supply lines. A dry, decent man who marked papers honestly and once told a young Reinholt, apropos of nothing, that an officer's hardest duty was not obeying orders but noticing which orders deserved obedience. Brandt had died passing a letter to a battlefield nurse. Brandt had died *running*. From what?

From Hess.

* * *

At six in the morning, having not slept, Reinholt did something he had never done in his career.

He opened the interrogation files and read them looking for holes.

Not for use — not yet, not consciously. Just to see whether the holes existed. Whether the beautiful case had gaps after all that he had trained himself not to see, the way you can train your eye not to see the crack in a wall until someone points at it, and then it is all you see.

The holes were everywhere. The convoy dates in the prosecution's exhibits matched the theft dates perfectly — too perfectly, no fog delays, no clerical drift, no human mess. The Vasilievs' alleged motive rested on a single letter whose handwriting analysis had been performed by an expert whose other clients, when Reinholt checked, included two firms that supplied the army under contracts Hess had approved. The informant who had first named the family was dead — conveniently, of "pneumonia," in a garrison hospital, in August.

Reinholt closed the file. His hands were steady now. Steadier than they'd been all week, in fact, which was its own kind of alarming.

He thought: *I could stop.*

He thought: *I could slow down.* Lose a document. Misplace a date. There were ways; he knew all of them, had invented several. The armistice would arrive like weather, dissolve the tribunal, and the whole filthy business would melt into the general thaw of forgetting, and the Vasilievs would walk out of confinement into a ruined, forgiving world, and Hess would have to live with it.

He thought about that for a long time, watching the sun come up gray over the square, watching the sentry change shift, watching the smoke rise from the Vasiliev chimney — a home still making breakfast, against all instructions.

Then he thought about the telegram's second paragraph. *Hess will oversee proceedings personally.* And underneath the dread of inspection, he found the colder fact waiting: if Reinholt slowed the machine, Hess would notice. Hess noticed everything except guilt. And a major who obstructed a general's case in the final weeks of a war would not face a tribunal. He would face a transfer, a disgrace, a quiet end — or less quiet, if the schoolmaster who died under questioning this winter was any measure of what the patron's patience cost.

And beneath even that, honest at last in the empty office that smelled of bread: he was afraid not of failing Hess, but of finding out, in front of Hess, that he had spent a year building a lie — and that some part of him had known it the entire time, and built it anyway, because building is what he was *for*.

The clock on the mantel — requisitioned, like everything, from a house whose owners had fled — ticked with a small irregular hitch, a hesitation on every fourth second, as though it too were checking its conscience.

Reinholt stood, buttoned his tunic, and gave the order that would make the next days run like a blade down a rail.

"Doubled patrols. Telegraph under my personal seal. The farmhand Piet Lukas — bring him in today, not tomorrow. And find me the nurse." He paused. "No more description. No more posters. Put men on the roads out and the chapel in, and put a man on the gravedigger, because a man hiding in a town always ends up visiting graves."

His adjutant wrote it down, saluted, and left.

Alone, Reinholt gathered the telegram, folded it along its creases, and slipped it inside his tunic, against his chest, where he could feel it like a second heartbeat — faster than his own, counting down.

Outside, the snow had stopped. The sky was hard and clear, the kind of sky that carried sound for miles, and across the frozen square he could hear, distinctly, the clock in the church tower striking seven — each note falling on the silence like a stone into a well.

Everyone in Krasnogorsk could hear it that morning. He was almost certain of it. The baker, the sentries, the woman behind the frosted glass, the nurse with the ledgers, the witnesses in their cellar — the whole town listening to the same ticking, each person praying for a different hour.

Reinholt pulled on his gloves. On the road from the west, right on schedule, a staff car appeared through the morning haze, flying a general's pennon, coming toward the town at a speed that announced its passenger considered arrival itself a formality.

Hess was here. The play was about to begin, and Reinholt — to his horror, to his lasting shame, and with a small traitorous flutter of relief he would examine never — realized he was glad.
