## 13. Marta's Long Night

The summons weighed less than a feather. Marta knew this because she had weighed it — held it in her cupped palm like a moth, feeling almost nothing at all — and then sewn it into the lining of the clock case anyway, because almost nothing could still hang you.

It was folded to the size of a stamp. Judge Lindqvist's own seal on it, pressed in green wax that had cracked in the cold and been mended with a drop of candle tallow by Elena Vasiliev herself, who had laughed a small, tired laugh while she did it and said, "There. Now it looks worse than it is. Nobody suspects anything that looks mended."

Marta had memorized the words inside before folding it. Not because she expected to forget them — her memory was the one part of her that never failed, the way other girls' hair never frizzed — but because Papa had always said that a courier who knows her cargo by heart can lose everything else and still arrive. *To Captain Iversen, care of the chapel. The tribunal convenes Thursday. Bring every name.*

Thursday. Two days away.

The clock sat in her arms now, heavy as a sleeping cat. It was a good clock for lying about: an old mantel piece with a fat brass face and one hand snapped off at the root, its case scarred where some previous owner had set a hot kettle down without thinking. She had practiced the story walking from the Vasilievs' kitchen to the edge of town — out loud, at first, until a woman pulling a sled of turnips gave her an odd look, and then only inside her head, where nobody could hear her stumble.

*I'm taking it to old Fenner the watchmaker. My grandmother sent me. It stopped years ago and she wants it fixed for Easter, except we couldn't come sooner because of the roads.*

The trouble with the story was that there was no grandmother. Marta had invented her on the spot three nights ago and had grown so fond of her since that she sometimes caught herself worrying about the woman's cough.

The checkpoint stood where the mill road met the river bridge: two sawhorses, a lantern guttering in the wind, four soldiers and a sergeant who looked like he'd rather be anywhere else, including the front, which was saying something. Beyond them the bridge planks shone wet and black under the moon, and beyond the bridge the road climbed toward the chapel hill, where somewhere in the dark a cellar full of witnesses was waiting for a piece of paper no bigger than a moth.

Marta adjusted her grip on the clock and walked forward.

"Halt." The sergeant raised his lantern. "What's in the box?"

"It's not a box," Marta said, "it's a clock." And then, because the terror in her chest had to go somewhere and her mouth was nearest: "It's broken. That's rather the whole point of it."

One of the younger soldiers snorted. The sergeant did not.

"Open it."

*Don't open it.* Her heart performed a maneuver that would have interested a physician. But her hands — her hands were steady, because Papa had trained those too, had made her carry full water buckets across the courtyard at eight years old with orders not to spill, and had said, when she complained, "Your hands will learn what your head already knows, little one: fear is just weather. You walk through it."

So her hands opened the clock case, tilted the works into the lantern light — gears, weights, the pendulum rod bare where the bob should have hung — and said, in the voice of a girl who found all adults faintly disappointing:

"You can look, if you want. There's nothing in there but time that doesn't work."

The sergeant peered into the mechanism. The summons lay against the case lining, behind the strike train, wrapped in oilcloth thin as onion skin. He looked directly above it. He looked directly beside it. His eyes passed over the exact spot where the paper was the way water passes over a stone.

"Whose clock?"

"My grandmother's. In Krasnogorsk. She wants it fixed for—" *Easter,* said the story. "For spring. She's particular. It belonged to my grandfather, and he wound it every Sunday of his life, and when it stopped she took it as—" Marta paused, and here the invented grandmother reached up out of pure invention and steadied her granddaughter's voice: "—as a bad sign. With the war and everything. So she wants it going again."

Silence, except the river under the bridge and the wind worrying the lantern.

"Where's your pass?"

"In my left coat pocket. Under the mittens. You'll have to take the mittens off yourself, my hands are occupied with the clock, and Grandmother says if I break it again she'll—" She stopped. "She won't really. She cries more than she shouts."

The sergeant fished out the pass. It was genuine — Elena had seen to that, a child's travel permit stamped by a clerk who owed the Vasilievs forty years of kindnesses — and it said what it needed to say: *Marta Lindholm, age fourteen, resident of Krasnogorsk, traveling to the watchmaker Fenner, Mill Road, and return.* The sergeant read it twice. Then he looked at her over the top of it.

"Lindholm."

"Yes."

"That's a northern family."

"So is winter," Marta said, "but nobody asks it for papers."

This time both young soldiers laughed, and quickly pretended they hadn't. The sergeant's mouth twitched in a way that suggested it wanted to laugh too but had signed papers promising not to.

"Grandmother," he said. "In Krasnogorsk. What street?"

Here was the trap, laid flat and obvious as a rake in grass. Marta had known it would come; she and Ulyana had rehearsed it by lamplight two nights ago, him playing the soldier, being patient and kind and therefore much harder to lie to than any shouty man. *They'll ask you something easy,* he had said. *That's how you'll know it's the real question. Easy questions are doors. Hard ones are only walls.*

"The church street," Marta said. "The blue house with the crooked gate. Everyone knows it because the gate has fallen over twice and my grandfather refused to fix it properly, on principle."

"What principle?"

"He said a gate that leans keeps out people who are in a hurry, and anyone worth letting in will wait."

Another silence. Somewhere behind the sergeant, the youngest soldier was grinning openly now, and even the fourth man, who hadn't moved or spoken the entire time, shifted his weight in a way that might have been amusement or might have been the cold.

The sergeant handed back the pass. Then he stepped aside from the gap in the sawhorses, and Marta felt her whole body go light, the way snow feels when it lets go of a roof.

"Wait." He crouched suddenly and squinted at the clock face. "That hand. Snapped clean off. What happened to it?"

And here Marta made her mistake, or what felt like one: she told the truth.

"I broke it," she said. "Last winter. I was supposed to be dusting it and instead I was reading, and I knocked it off the shelf with my elbow. Grandmother didn't scold me. That was somehow worse. She just picked up the hand and put it in her sewing drawer and said, 'Well. Now it matches the times.'"

The youngest soldier turned away and shook silently. Even the sergeant exhaled through his nose, which from him was practically applause.

"Go on, then," he said. "Mind the ice on the bridge. And tell your grandmother—" he hesitated, as if the sentence had arrived without permission— "tell her clocks can be mended. Most things can. It's only men who insist otherwise."

"I'll tell her," Marta said gravely. "Though she'll say a soldier wrote that, and then she'll cry again."

She crossed the bridge. Her legs held. The ice did crackle, and once her boot went through the crust to the ankle, and she did not fall, because falling would have meant putting the clock down.

* * *

Fenner the watchmaker lived above his shop on the Mill Road, and he was awake, because watchmakers are always awake; sleep, in Fenner's opinion, was a habit for people whose livelihoods didn't depend on noticing small movements. He let Marta in through the back, unbolted the door himself, and looked at the clock, then at her, then at the clock again.

"Well?" he said.

"Behind the strike train. Oilcloth. Don't cut it, it's not yours."

"No," Fenner agreed, "it's nobody's. That's the trouble with paper these days." He carried the clock to his bench, lit a lamp with the economy of a man who resented lamps, and extracted the summons with tweezers, holding it up to the light like a specimen. "Green wax. Mended with tallow. That'll be Mrs. Vasiliev's work."

"You know her sealing habits?"

"I repaired her mother's carriage clock for thirty years. A woman's wax tells you more than her letters do." He set the summons down flat beneath a glass weight. "You gave this to me. I never saw you. You came for your grandmother's clock, which is not yet ready, and you went home disappointed."

"That's the saddest thing I've ever heard."

"It's the safest thing I've ever heard. Sadness is invisible. Everybody walks past it." He glanced at her then — really looked, the way he looked at escapements, checking for wear. "Your hands were steady?"

"They were steady."

"And your mouth?"

"My mouth said my grandmother lives on the church street in the blue house with the crooked gate, and that she cried when the clock stopped, and that a leaning gate keeps out people in a hurry."

Fenner was quiet for a moment. "That last bit," he said. "Yours?"

"His," Marta said. "My grandfather's. Before he died."

"Mm." Fenner turned back to his bench. "Good principle. Wrong war for it." He began, delicately, to reassemble the clock around the space where the summons had been. "Sit down, girl. You're white as the flour they've stopped selling. There's tea. It's terrible. Drink it anyway."

The tea was terrible. Marta drank it anyway, holding the cup in both hands, and only then noticed that her knees had opinions of their own, conducting a private argument about whether to hold her up. She sat on Fenner's stool and watched his fingers move among the gears — quick, sure, unbothered — and thought about how strange it was that the worst hour of her life had taken exactly one hour, door to door, checkpoint to bench, and that the world had not changed shape at all afterward. The river still ran. The lamp still hissed. Fenner still disapproved of lamps.

"Papa used to say fear is just weather," she said.

"Your papa sounds like a wise man."

"He was a postman."

"Some of the wisest men I've known have been postmen," Fenner said. "They know what matters enough to carry, and they know most of what people send each other is love, badly packaged." He fitted the pendulum rod into place, minus its bob, and swung it gently with one finger. Tick. Tick. "There. A clock with no bob keeps no time, but it remembers how. Same as people."

Marta looked at him. "Did everyone in this town agree beforehand to talk like that? Was there a meeting?"

"There was a war," Fenner said. "It does the same work."

* * *

She was to sleep in the shop's back room and return home at first light, when checkpoints grow bored and sleepy-eyed, but sleep declined to come. She lay on the narrow cot under a quilt that smelled of machine oil and pipe smoke, and listened to the house tick and settle, and did the arithmetic she had forbidden herself from doing on the bridge.

One hour. One sergeant. Four soldiers. One wrong word about a street, a grandmother, a gate — any single hinge of the lie, and it all swings open. And inside the clock, the whole plan: Lindqvist's summons, Thursday, *bring every name*. The witnesses in the cellar. Elena's stubborn, luminous refusal to run. Ulyana with his ledger of the dead, walking around Krasnogorsk disguised as a gravedigger, of all the professions a poet could have chosen.

If the sergeant had opened the case another inch. If he had asked what her grandmother's maiden name was, instead of what street she lived on. If the youngest soldier had been the suspicious one, instead of the laughing one.

Her hands, under the quilt, had finally begun to shake. Fine, she told them. Shake now. Do it thoroughly. Be finished by morning.

But underneath the shaking, something else had settled in and was refusing to leave, warm and unfamiliar and slightly embarrassing: pride. Not the showy kind — the other kind, the quiet kind, the kind Papa must have carried all those years walking his rounds in rain and snow with other people's news strapped to his back. She had done it. The smallest courier in the network, fourteen years old, armed with a broken clock and a grandmother she had invented out of thin air and loved completely, had walked the heaviest hour in Krasnogorsk straight past a loaded rifle, and the rifle had blinked first.

Tomorrow the summons would travel on — Fenner knew the route, or said he did, in the manner of men who know more routes than they admit. Tomorrow Ulyana would read it by candlelight and nod and begin the final counting of names. Tomorrow Elena would stand in her guarded parlor and be brave in the specific, exhausting way she was brave, which was to say entirely.

And Marta would be a schoolgirl again, carrying bread, asking after neighbors, invisible. Sadness is invisible, Fenner had said. So, it turned out, was courage. It dressed in whatever was hanging by the door and walked out into the weather like anybody.

She slept eventually, near dawn, and dreamed of nothing at all, which was itself a gift.

* * *

She woke to knocking — not at the shop door, but at the back entrance, soft and rhythmic, the knock of someone who had done this before. Fenner was already moving. Through the floorboards Marta heard the bolt slide, low voices, and then footsteps on the stairs, and then Ulyana was standing in the doorway of the back room in his gravedigger's coat, frost in his hair, holding something wrapped in sacking.

He looked at her for a long moment. She couldn't read his face — he was better at that game than she was — but there was something in it that made her sit up too fast.

"Fenner says you went through the mill checkpoint last night," he said quietly.

"Does he also say I was magnificent? Because I was. There was a grandmother. She cried."

"Marta." He crossed the room and crouched to her eye level, and now she could read him after all, and what she read made her stomach drop through the cot, through the floor, through the frozen ground beneath it. "The mill checkpoint isn't Reinholt's. It's Hess's own men, seconded yesterday afternoon. They've been stopping everyone coming off the bridge road since dusk."

"But they didn't stop me."

"No," Ulyana said. "They didn't stop you." He set the sacking bundle gently on the cot between them and unwrapped one corner of it: the broken clock's missing brass hand, polished bright, as if someone had found it and kept it. "Which means either their sergeant is the laziest man in the army — or he let you through on purpose, and now he knows exactly what a girl with a crooked-gate story looks like. They took the watchmaker's apprentice an hour ago for questioning. He came back white and silent and wouldn't say why."

Outside, far off across the frozen town, a whistle blew — twice, then once more. Ulyana stood, and his face had gone the color of the frost on the window.

"Get your coat, Marta. Whatever that sergeant decided about you last night — I think we're about to find out which way he decided."
