## 14. Hess Arrives

The general came to Krasnogorsk on a Tuesday, which Ulyana thought was fitting, because Tuesdays were market days, and Hess arrived the way merchants did — smiling, shaking hands, and counting everything.

Ulyana saw him first from the churchyard wall, where he had spent enough mornings that the sexton had stopped asking questions and simply left a space for him on the bench by the bell tower. From there he could see the whole of the square: the fountain with its frozen spout, the baker's awning sagging under snow, the guarded door of the Vasiliev house, and beyond it the town hall where, in nine days' time, a tribunal would convene to decide whether Elena Vasiliev's family had robbed an army or been robbed of their good name.

The motorcar was black and long, absurd on these cobbled streets, like a whale that had wandered into a trout stream. It stopped in front of the inn, and out stepped a man in a gray greatcoat cut so precisely it seemed less sewn than grown.

"General Hess," said the innkeeper's boy, running past the bench with the news before anyone had asked him for it. "He's come early! They said he wouldn't come at all!"

"They say a great many things," said the sexton, who was seventy and had learned that most announcements were weather forecasts for other people's storms.

Ulyana sat very still.

*Silver-haired,* Colonel Brandt had described him, in one of the long fevered nights before the letter. *You'll know him by the hands. He has beautiful hands, Ulyana — a pianist's hands. He'll shake yours and you'll think, what a kind man. That's the trick of it. The kindness is real. He genuinely likes people. He just likes winning more.*

The general crossed the square, and Ulyana watched the hands. Hess stopped at the baker's stall, where Frau Lindemann stood frozen behind her loaves like a rabbit deciding between two directions of fatal mistake. He picked up a loaf — the dark rye, Ulyana noted; even generals knew the rye was better here — weighed it in those elegant fingers, and said something that made her laugh, actually laugh, her shoulders coming down from around her ears.

Then he paid for it. In full. With exact change.

"Oh no," Ulyana murmured.

"What?" said the sexton.

"He's charming."

"That's usually considered a virtue."

"It's considered a virtue the way a flood is considered irrigation," Ulyana said, "depending on where you're standing." He pulled his cap lower. As far as Krasnogorsk knew, he was Anton Reiter, a gravedigger's cousin from two towns over, come to help with the winter burials because the ground was hard and the old gravedigger's back was harder. His papers were forged, his name was borrowed, and his face was known to exactly one man in this region — Major Reinholt, who had posted his description on every gatepost between here and the front.

So far the description had proved uselessly specific. *A nurse traveling with forged papers.* Half the displaced men in the province had soft hands from hospital work, and the other half had forged papers. Ulyana had dirt worked so deep into his knuckles by now that even he sometimes doubted he'd ever held anything gentler than a shovel.

But Hess was another matter. Hess had never seen him. And Hess, according to Brandt, missed nothing.

* * *

By noon, half the town had been charmed and the other half was in queue.

Hess did what occupying generals almost never bothered to do: he walked among them without escort, or with only the thinnest pretense of one, two officers trailing him at a distance calculated to look casual and fail. He visited the school and complimented the children's singing, which was terrible, and told them so with such warmth that they laughed and sang worse. He inspected the granary and pronounced its stores adequate, which was a lie everyone recognized as generous, since the stores would not have fed a large family through February. He bought ribbons at the haberdasher's for his nonexistent daughters — *three of them, back home, the image of their mother, thank God* — and when the haberdasher's actual daughter, a girl of six, corrected his German pronunciation of *Krasnogorsk*, he repeated it wrong again on purpose until she beamed.

"He's not what I expected," Marta said that evening. She had appeared in the churchyard shadows beside Ulyana with the particular silence she reserved for moments when being seen mattered. She was carrying bread, which she divided without asking — three ways, though there were only two of them. The third portion went, as always, to whatever hungry thing might pass. In Krasnogorsk that usually meant the sexton's cat, but Ulyana had begun to suspect Marta kept the habit for larger hungers.

"No," Ulyana agreed. "He's worse."

"You wanted him to be a monster."

"I wanted him to be easy to hate. Hate is efficient. You can carry it a long way without getting tired." He tore his bread. "This man you have to keep reminding yourself about. Every hour. Like taking medicine."

Marta chewed, considering. Below them the lamplighters were working the square, and the Vasiliev house windows glowed their supervised, rationed glow. "Elena says he came to the guard post today and spoke to the family through the fence. Politely. He apologized for the inconvenience."

"The inconvenience."

"That's what she called it too. She used his own word against him. He laughed. She says he has a lovely laugh and she'd like to put something through it."

"There's the medicine," Ulyana said. "Take your dose."

* * *

The trouble with watching a performance is knowing you're the only one who didn't buy a ticket.

For three days Hess performed Krasnogorsk the way a man waters a garden he intends to dig up: thoroughly, gently, and with his eye already on the harvest. And each night, while the town slept off its flattered exhaustion, the harvest proceeded.

Ulyana learned of it piecemeal, through the network he and Elena had stitched together over the winter — Marta's couriers, the sexton's gossip, the baker's delivery boy who noticed everything and reported it in the tone of a sports commentator. On the first night, two gendarmes arrived on the eastern road and took rooms at the inn without registering. On the second, the town clerk — a nervous man named Pfeiffer whose loyalty to truth was roughly equal to his fear of whoever was nearest — drew up a fresh list of names at Hess's request. On the third night, the tailor Grosse, who had signed Elena's witness list in October with the bravado of a man signing a petition against bad weather, closed his shop and did not reopen it.

"He's gone," Marta reported, breathless, arriving at the churchyard after curfew with snow in her hair. "His wife won't say where. She keeps saying he's visiting his brother, but his brother's dead, everyone knows it, she says it anyway, louder each time someone asks."

Ulyana felt the cold go through him that had nothing to do with December. "Who else?"

"So far? Nobody missing. But the list—" she pressed a scrap of paper into his glove, folded to the size of a stamp. "Pfeiffer copied it. Elena has the original. These are the ones marked."

In the blue light of the sexton's shuttered lantern, Ulyana read the names. Nine of them. Farmers, a clerk, the schoolmaster's widow — no, not the widow; the schoolmaster himself, old Herr Albrecht, who had testified once and survived it and been warned twice and signed again anyway, because, as he'd told Elena, *a man my age should spend his remaining credibility on something.* Beside each name, in the clerk's tidy hand, a date. All the dates fell within the week before the tribunal opened.

"They're going to arrest them quietly," Ulyana said. "One or two at a time. Before anyone notices a pattern."

"And if they're all detained when the tribunal begins—"

"Then the tribunal hears from no one. The case runs as written. The Vasilievs confess to a crime they didn't commit in front of an audience that's already been charmed into gratitude." He folded the paper away. "It isn't even corruption anymore, Marta. Corruption implies effort. This is theater. The play was finished before we got to town. We're just set dressing that hasn't been informed."

Marta was quiet a moment. Then, in the small fierce voice she saved for injustice: "I hate being set dressing."

"I know," Ulyana said. "So do I. But hating it loudly is how dressing gets burned." He looked out over the sleeping square, at the guard post's single lamp, at the inn where unregistered men slept above registered ones. "We need to get word to the marked ones tonight. Tomorrow may be too late for some of them."

"Marta can move faster than tomorrow."

"Marta," Ulyana said, "is fourteen years old and it is minus eleven degrees."

"Marta," said Marta, "has delivered in worse," which was true, and which was also, Ulyana had learned, the entire architecture of her courage: a true statement, repeated until it became load-bearing.

He gave her four names and four addresses and watched her fold herself into the dark like a letter into an envelope. Then he sat alone on the churchyard bench with the lantern shuttered, doing the arithmetic that had been sitting in his chest all day.

Here was what he understood now, watching General Hess feed the town compliments like sugar to a horse being led somewhere it wouldn't want to go: the tribunal was lost. Not endangered — lost. Nine witnesses, nine dates, a judge whose honesty was rumored rather than confirmed, a major who needed the conviction before any armistice dissolved it, and a general who had crossed half a country to attend in person because some crimes are too important to delegate. Against this: a nurse with a shovel, a girl with a broken clock, a woman behind a fence, and a stack of papers that proved everything and could reach no one who mattered.

Brandt had died believing a letter could fix this. Ulyana had crossed a collapsing front believing it too, and he still believed it — believed in the ledger, in the names, in the stubborn arithmetic of memory. But belief, he was beginning to understand, was not a plan. Somewhere between the churchyard and the tribunal there was a seam in the machine, and he had nine days to find it, or the play would run as written, curtain, applause, and the Vasilievs would walk out of history wearing someone else's crime.

A sound made him look up. A figure at the churchyard gate — not Marta returning; taller, slower, wrapped in a coat that predated several wars. The sexton's cousin, the old gravedigger himself, come shuffling up the path with his lantern swinging.

"You're up late, cousin," the old man said, lowering himself onto the bench with the sound of a folding chair. For a while they sat in companionable silence, two fake relatives keeping vigil over nothing.

Then the gravedigger said, without looking at him: "There was a chaplain here this morning. Asking after you, Anton. Or asking after somebody. Said a dying colonel sent him."

Ulyana's heart performed a maneuver it had not performed since the front.

"Said he'd come back," the gravedigger went on, yawning. "Said it couldn't wait past Sunday. Said—" the old man squinted, retrieving it, "- 'tell him Brandt wrote twice.'"

The wind moved through the bare lindens, and somewhere across the square the guard lamp guttered and steadied, and Ulyana Iversen, gravedigger's cousin, keeper of the forgotten, sat very still in the cold and understood that the play might have a second act after all — if he could find the chaplain before Sunday, before the arrests, before the curtain.

"Twice," he said softly.

"Aye," said the gravedigger. "Whoever this colonel was, cousin, he doesn't sound like the sort who wastes his words."
