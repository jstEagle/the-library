## 15. What Brandt Knew

Elena Vasiliev had learned, in the six weeks since her family's confinement began, to measure time by the guard at the gate.

He changed shifts at seven, at three, and at eleven. The morning man was a boy named Pavel who blushed when she said good morning and could be trusted to look away while Marta's notes passed through the gap in the fence where the ivy had died. The afternoon man was older and deaf in one ear, which made him either useless or ideal depending on the day. The night man she never saw, only heard — boots on gravel, a cough like a saw going through wet wood.

It was not freedom. But it was a schedule, and Elena had discovered that a woman with a schedule could survive almost anything. Her mother had taught her that during the famine years: *when they take everything, take back the hours.* So Elena took hers back. She baked bread in the kitchen the guards permitted her to use — the smell of it drifted over the wall and made even the hard-faced sergeant shift his weight — and she mended uniforms for half the garrison at rates so modest it was practically charity, which was precisely the point. A town that owes you favors is a town that will carry messages for you.

On the morning this chapter concerns, she was beating dough with more force than the dough deserved when Marta came through the kitchen door with snow in her hair and an expression Elena had learned to read the way sailors read clouds.

"You've found something," Elena said.

"I've found Father Anselm," Marta said, shaking off her cap. "Or he found me. He's been asking after you for three days. He says—" she dropped her voice, glancing at the window out of pure habit, though the nearest guard was forty meters away and watching a cat "—he says he has something of Colonel Brandt's."

The wooden spoon stopped mid-stroke.

Brandt's name was spoken in their house now the way one speaks of weather — carefully, and with an eye on the sky. His first letter, delivered through Ulyana's coat seam and Marta's stubborn courage, had named General Hess as the thief of an entire winter's supply convoy and the Vasilievs as the convenient scapegoat. It was enough to know. It was not enough to prove. Elena had spent weeks assembling witnesses the way her grandmother assembled quilts — one scrap at a time, each too small alone, all of them together maybe warm enough to matter.

"Did he say what?"

"He said," Marta recited, clearly enjoying the theater of it, "*tell Mrs. Vasiliev that the colonel kept two keys, and I have been keeping the second one in my desk drawer for a year, waiting for someone who would know what to do with it.*" She wrinkled her nose. "Chaplains talk like that. Everything's a parable."

"That isn't a parable, that's a letter," Elena said. Her heart was doing something undignified against her ribs. "A second letter. Marta — Brandt wrote a second letter."

"Then we should go hear it before the major hears it first."

* * *

The chapel of Saint Olga sat at the crooked end of Krasnogorsk's high street, leaning eastward like an old man straining to hear good news. Father Anselm had been its priest for thirty-one years, and in that time he had perfected the art of appearing slightly deaf whenever soldiers asked him inconvenient questions and remarkably lucid whenever anyone needed bread, blankets, or a cellar with a hidden door. He received them in his study, a room that smelled of candle wax, ink, and the particular dustiness of books nobody had dared throw away.

He did not speak until Marta had checked the window twice and pronounced the street empty except for a goat with ambitions.

"Sit down, child," he said to Marta. "You hover like a moth."

"Moths are quiet."

"Moths are nervous. Sit." He waited until she perched on the edge of a chair, then turned to Elena, and the mildness fell away from his face like a cloth pulled from furniture. What remained underneath was not unkind, but it was serious in the way that men become serious when they have decided to spend something they cannot replace.

"A year ago last autumn," he said, "Colonel Brandt came to me at night. You'll remember the weather — rain for a week, the roads a swamp. He looked like a man carrying a piano up a hill. He told me nothing. That was the arrangement. But he left a sealed letter with me, and he gave me instructions I have followed to the letter ever since." The old priest smiled thinly. "Which is ironic, given the letter's contents. To the letter. Yes."

"What were the instructions?" Elena asked.

"To give it to no one from the army. To no one from the government. To no official person of any kind whatsoever — and Brandt was specific, he'd clearly rehearsed it — *no matter how honest they claim to be, and especially if they claim it loudly.*" Anselm folded his hands. "The letter was to go only to a member of the Vasiliev family, or, failing that, to someone acting in their interest whom I judged trustworthy. And I was to hold it until either the family asked for help or the tribunal convened. Whichever came first."

"The tribunal convenes Monday," Elena said quietly.

"Yes. Which is why the goat outside is the only creature in Krasnogorsk having a peaceful morning." He rose, went to the bookshelf, and ran his finger along a row of theological volumes until he reached a fat commentary on the Epistles. From behind it came a packet wrapped in oilcloth and tied with string, and beneath the wrapping, when he laid it on the desk, a letter sealed with wax gone dark as old blood.

Elena looked at it and did not touch it.

"Father," she said, "if this is what I think it is, then everyone who touches it is in danger. You most of all. You've kept it safe for a year. Handing it over doesn't end your part in this — it confirms it."

"My dear girl." Anselm's voice was dry as communion wafer. "I am seventy-three years old. I buried your father, I baptized you, and I once watched you steal apples from Deacon Petrov's tree with such poor technique that I felt obliged to teach you afterward. If Major Reinholt wishes to hang a priest for delivering a dead man's mail, he will find my funeral very well attended and my eulogy extremely long." He pushed the packet across the desk. "Open it. I confess my curiosity has been eating me alive for twelve months."

She broke the seal. The paper inside was written in Brandt's small, precise hand — the hand of a man who had spent decades signing things that mattered — but it wavered toward the end, the letters leaning like tired soldiers. Elena read it once in silence. Then she read it aloud, because some things need air to be believed.

"*To whoever holds this,*" she read. "*If you have this letter, then my first letter has found its way, and you already know the shape of the crime. This letter is its bones.*

*"Between the twelfth and nineteenth of October of last year, three supply convoys departed the central depot at Velmara bound for the Third Army's winter positions. Officially they carried flour, tinned meat, medical stores, and coal. Officially they arrived short — losses attributed to enemy action and spoilage. Unofficially they never went east at all. They were diverted at the Velmara junction under requisition orders signed with the authority of General Hess, commanding, and sold through a civilian broker in Riga named Aldis Krūmiņš. I have seen the requisition orders. I copied the numbers myself: convoy designations 4-117, 4-118, and 4-121, dated October 12th, 15th, and 19th. The sale receipts passed through Krūmiņš's office; I have copies of three, bearing his signature and the depot stamp.*

*"When the shortage was discovered, Hess required culprits. He chose the Vasilievs, whose warehouses stood nearest the junction and whose name was foreign enough to be useful. The evidence against them has been manufactured — I know because I was asked to sign part of it, and I refused, and that refusal is why I am writing this instead of sleeping.*

*"There is one man on the tribunal I believe incorruptible: Judge Lindqvist. He is old, he is slow, he asks questions that make important people uncomfortable, and for these reasons they consider him harmless. They are wrong. Get this letter into Lindqvist's hands — not before the trial opens, mind, but early, so he knows what questions to ask. A judge who knows the answers in advance can lead a witness anywhere he likes, and Hess's witnesses are liars, and liars can only keep their footing on ground they've prepared.*

*"I was a soldier for thirty-four years. I obeyed many orders, some of them bad. Consider this my last order, and the only one I ever gave on my own authority: let the truth arrive on time.*

*"— Colonel Henrik Brandt."*

The study was silent. Outside, the goat had apparently achieved whatever it had been attempting, because it stopped bleating.

Marta spoke first, in the practical voice that continually astonished people who underestimated her. "Convoy numbers. Dates. A broker with a name and a city. Copies of receipts." She counted on her fingers. "That's not a story. That's a case."

"That's a conviction," Elena said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears — steadier than she felt. "If it reaches Lindqvist. *If.*" She set the pages down and pressed her palms flat against the desk, as if the wood might lend her its patience. "Reinholt will have watchers on every road into the town hall by Saturday. He knows Brandt corresponded with someone. He's arrested Tomas and old Yuri already. If he learns a second letter exists—"

"Then it mustn't learn it slowly," Anselm said. "It must move fast, and it must move disguised." His eyes went to Marta, and there was apology in them alongside the request. "Child. You've done more running for us than any horse in this town."

"I know," Marta said. "Go on."

"Lindqvist lodges at the widow Halm's house, on Kirch Street. Every evening at six he walks to the chapel for vespers — alone, without his clerk, because the man snores through the liturgy and Lindqvist finds it restful to pray somewhere the snoring is voluntary. If the letter is placed in his pew before he arrives—"

"No," Elena said.

All three of them looked at her. Even the goat, briefly.

"Forgive me, Father," she continued, "but a letter in a pew proves nothing about who put it there and gives Lindqvist no way to verify any of it. Brandt himself said it — get it into his hands. Hands, plural. There should be a conversation. There should be a face he can question, a person whose testimony anchors the document. Otherwise Reinholt dismisses it as an anonymous forgery before the second verse of the opening hymn, and we've burned our one copy of the truth for nothing."

Anselm stroked his beard. "And who do you propose carries it? You are confined to your property pending tribunal. Your nurse friend is the most hunted man between here and the coast. And this one—" he nodded at Marta, not unkindly "—is fourteen."

"Fifteen in March," said Marta.

"And therefore ideally suited to being overlooked," Elena said. "Which is exactly why it shouldn't be her. Not for this. If Reinholt catches a child carrying this, he won't just detain her. He'll make an example. No." She picked up the letter and held it against her chest for a moment, feeling the paper's small weight, the weight of everything Brandt had spent his last strength to say. "Lindqvist walks to vespers at six. Which means at half past five, Kirch Street belongs to anyone who looks like they belong there."

"Elena." Anselm's voice was gentle. "You are the most recognizable woman in this town. Half the garrison eats your bread."

"Half the garrison eats my bread," Elena agreed, "and the other half wears uniforms I've mended. I won't be sneaking anywhere, Father. I'm going to deliver laundry." She allowed herself the smallest smile. "The widow Halm sent out three shirts and a tablecloth Tuesday last. Nobody has returned them. It's a scandal. Someone simply must walk to Kirch Street and see to it personally."

For a moment the old priest stared at her. Then he laughed — a short, startled bark of laughter, quickly strangled, as if even joy might be reported to the authorities. "Thirty-one years in this parish," he said, "and I still forget that your family prays the way other people gamble."

"We prefer 'negotiates,'" Elena said. She wrapped the letter back in its oilcloth, and then, after a moment's thought, slid it inside the hollowed spine of a hymnal from Anselm's shelf — a battered thing, its cover stamped with musical notes, the kind of object no soldier in history has ever voluntarily opened. "Marta. Tomorrow at five you'll walk past the widow Halm's house twice, once from each direction, and count the guards. Don't stop, don't look at the windows, and if anyone speaks to you, complain about your feet. At half past five I'll come up Kirch Street with a laundry basket. And at six o'clock, Judge Lindqvist will kneel down to pray and find that God has provided him with homework."

"And if Reinholt's men stop you?" Marta asked. "A basket full of shirts is fine. A basket full of treason is a different inventory."

Elena thought of her mother again, of the famine years, of the hour-by-hour arithmetic of survival. She thought of Ulyana, wherever he was, carrying names the way she carried bread. She thought of the schoolmaster — they had all heard by now, and none of them said it aloud, but he was present in every room lately, a silence shaped like a kind old man.

"If they stop me," she said, "then I will stand in the street and insist, loudly and at length, about the disgraceful state of army laundry, until they wish they had shot me on sight. And while they're wishing it, Marta will be standing behind them with the hymnal, because a plan where only one of us can be caught isn't a plan. It's a sacrifice. We're not doing sacrifices anymore. We did that with the schoolmaster, and I intend for it to have been the last tuition paid on this particular education."

The priest closed his eyes briefly, the way men do when they want to object and know it's useless. "Vespers is at six," he said finally. "God willing, so is justice. I'll light a candle for the both of you. Two candles. Three — one for the goat, it's had a trying day."

* * *

That night Elena sat at her kitchen table long after the house slept, copying nothing and destroying nothing, just holding Brandt's words in her memory the way she'd once held her daughter through fevers — steadily, patiently, refusing to let go until the danger passed. Convoy 4-117, October the twelfth. Convoy 4-118, the fifteenth. Convoy 4-121, the nineteenth. Three dates, three numbers, a broker named Krūmiņš, a general named Hess, and one slow old judge who asked uncomfortable questions.

For a year the machine had seemed seamless to her — a great rolling engine of paperwork and rank that crushed whatever it rolled over and called the crushing procedure. But machines had seams. Brandt had found one with his dying breath. Tomorrow, God and clean linen willing, they would drive a wedge into it.

At half past five the next evening, Elena Vasiliev walked up Kirch Street with a laundry basket on her hip and murder in her heart — the tidy, lawful, well-documented kind — and did not once look back to see whether the girl with the hymnal was following.

She knew better than to doubt Marta. The smallest courier in Krasnogorsk had never yet missed an appointment with history.

But halfway to the widow Halm's gate, Elena saw the black motorcar parked outside it, and the uniformed adjutant climbing the steps with papers under his arm, and understood that the machine had noticed its seam — and had sent someone to sew it shut before six o'clock.
