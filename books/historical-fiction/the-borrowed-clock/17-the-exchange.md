## 17. The Exchange

Marta had decided, somewhere in the third hour of her imprisonment, that the chair was the worst part.

Not the questions. The questions she could manage. She had been managing adults and their questions since she was eleven years old, when her mother first pressed a letter into her hand and said, *Don't read it, don't lose it, don't be interesting.* Three rules. Marta had broken only one of them in her life, and that was by being born with a face people remembered, which hardly seemed fair to count against her.

No — the chair. It was a kitchen chair, wooden, missing one slat so that every time she shifted her weight it gave a small reproachful creak, as if to say, *again? really?* And the room smelled of cabbage and lamp oil and, underneath it all, the particular staleness of a building where men had been afraid for a long time. She had counted the floorboards (forty-one), the cracks in the ceiling plaster (nine, plus one shaped like a hare if you squinted), and the times Sergeant Dorn had walked past the door and pretended not to look at her (eleven, and getting less convincing each time).

She was on the twelfth non-look when the door opened and Major Reinholt came in.

He did not look like the stories. The stories — passed between couriers like contraband, whispered at way-stations — described him as a wolf, a spider, a man made entirely of winter. In person he was disappointingly ordinary: a tidy man with tidy hands and the tired eyes of someone who had once believed in something and had kept the receipt.

"Marta Lindt," he said, reading from a paper as though he didn't already know it by heart. "Seventeen years old. No fixed address. Detained at the mill checkpoint carrying—" he turned the paper over, as if the back might contain something more alarming than the front— "a clock."

"A broken clock," Marta corrected. "There's a difference. A broken clock is honest about being broken."

Reinholt looked at her for a long moment. Somewhere behind his eyes, a very small door opened a very small crack, and Marta saw — because she was good at seeing things, it was practically her trade — that he was not looking at a prisoner. He was looking at an instrument. A lever. Something with which to move a larger object.

And the larger object, she understood with a cold settling feeling in her stomach, was Ulyana Iversen.

"You were delivering it," Reinholt said. "For repair."

"Yes, sir."

"To whom?"

"The watchmaker, sir. Herr Brandl. On Kessel Street. It's his own clock; I borrowed it last autumn when mine stopped and never returned it, and now it's stopped too, and my mother says a girl who borrows a clock ought to have the decency to return it mended." She delivered this with the flawless boredom of a child reciting homework, and watched the sergeant by the door nearly smile before remembering not to.

"That's a good lie," Reinholt said quietly. "Well constructed. Believable details. Your mother is a nice touch." He set the paper down on the table. "But you know what I know, and I know what you carry, and we are both wasting a beautiful evening pretending otherwise."

Marta said nothing. Rule two: don't lose it. Rule one: don't read it. There had never been a rule three for this.

"Here is what will happen," Reinholt went on, sitting down across from her with the air of a man settling in for a game of cards he had already rigged. "Tomorrow morning, a nurse named Iversen — though he may call himself something else; gravedigger, lately, I'm told; a waste of a good uniform — will receive an offer. The papers in his possession, in exchange for you. Unharmed. Fed, even. I am told you haven't eaten since yesterday, and frankly, girl, it shows."

"It shows?" Marta lifted her chin. "I've been told I look positively robust."

Something flickered across the major's face that might, in a different man, in a different life, have been amusement. It was gone quickly, folded away like a letter he couldn't afford to be caught holding.

"You're not afraid," he observed.

"I'm cold," Marta said. "Fear is different. Fear makes your hands shake, and my hands don't shake. Ask anyone whose letters I've carried."

"And how many is that?"

"More than you've arrested."

The sergeant by the door made a sound that might have been a cough or might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Reinholt did not turn around. He studied Marta the way she imagined he studied maps: noting the roads, the bridges, the places where resistance would form and the places where it would crumble.

"Go to sleep," he said at last, standing. "Eat whatever they bring you. Tomorrow, someone decides whether you go home or stay here. It isn't your decision, and it isn't mine, and there's a kind of mercy in that, if you look for it."

"There isn't," Marta said.

"No," Reinholt agreed, at the door. "There isn't. But it's polite of both of us to pretend."

* * *

They brought her bread and soup and a blanket that smelled of another person's whole life, and she ate mechanically, because rule four, unwritten but absolute: eat whenever food appears, because you don't know when it will again. Then they left her alone with the chair and the hare-shaped crack, and she did what she always did when the world got too large and too heavy: she shrank it down to tasks.

Task one: survive tomorrow. Task two: whatever came after task one.

It was strange — she had known, abstractly, that she might someday be caught. Every courier knew it. They joked about it, even, in the grim shorthand of children doing adult work: *if they take me, swallow the names; if they ask, be stupid; stupidity is free, everyone believes it.* She had rehearsed interrogation in her head a hundred times, casting herself always as brave and quick and faintly luminous with defiance.

What she had not rehearsed was the waiting. Waiting, it turned out, was its own country, with its own weather, and the weather was cabbage-scented and endless. Waiting let all the cleverness drain out of you and left only the cold facts: seventeen years old, no fixed address, a chair with a missing slat, and somewhere out in the dark a man in a gravedigger's coat who had written her name in his ledger of the remembered, and whom she had, with a broken clock and a child's lie, gotten herself captured in front of.

*He won't come,* she told herself firmly. *He'd be a fool to come. He'll send someone. He'll do the sensible thing.*

Then she thought of Ulyana Iversen's face when he talked about the names in his ledger — the quiet, stubborn tenderness of it — and she thought of Elena Vasiliev refusing to flee her town, and she thought of the schoolmaster who had died asking nothing, and she revised her estimate downward considerably.

*Oh no,* she thought, with a strange, floating clarity. *He's going to come himself. He's absolutely going to come himself. He's going to walk up to the front gate with his terrible forged papers and his nurse's hands and his ledger full of dead men's names, and he's going to trade the most important document in the province for me, because that is exactly the sort of thing he does, and I have delivered letters for forty-seven families and not one of them ever mentioned how infuriating it is to be loved by honorable people.*

She fell asleep at some point, sitting up, and dreamed of clocks — hundreds of them, all ticking slightly out of time with one another, none of them agreeing on the hour.

* * *

They woke her before dawn and moved her to a room with a window, which she understood immediately as stage-setting. You did not give a prisoner a window unless you wanted someone outside to see the prisoner through it. She was arranged — there was no other word for it — at the sill, with bread and jam placed beside her like props, and downstairs in the yard she watched soldiers sweep snow that did not need sweeping, and she thought: *a theater. They've built me a little theater.*

Through the window, past the gate, the town lay gray and blue under early light. And there — she had to bite the inside of her cheek to keep her face still — there was a figure in a gravedigger's coat standing at the fence with the patient, hunched stillness of a man who has dug many holes and expects to dig many more.

Ulyana. Of course. Walking straight into it, exactly as predicted, exactly as feared, exactly as — she admitted it, furious at herself — hoped.

She could not hear the words exchanged at the gate. She didn't need to. She knew the shape of them. *The papers for the girl.* She watched Ulyana's hands — steady, always steady, those hands that bandaged men at a dressing station while shells came down — and then she watched him reach inside his coat, and her heart did something complicated and painful, because she knew what he carried and she knew what he was about to give away, and she also knew — because she was Marta Lindt, and knowing things was her trade — that Ulyana Iversen was many things, honorable and stubborn and infuriating, but he was not a fool either.

Which meant the packet he handed over the fence was probably not what Reinholt thought it was.

Which meant she was being traded for a copy.

The relief hit her sideways and made her dizzy. She gripped the windowsill. *He kept the original. He kept it, the madman, he kept it and gave them paper and ink and a fair imitation, and now they'll find out, sooner or later, and when they find out—*

When they found out, everything would come apart. Whatever thin thread of trust or exhaustion held this arrangement together would snap, and the major who needed a conviction before the armistice would stop trading and start hunting in earnest, and Ulyana would have lost his surprise — the one thing he had — to buy her freedom. She understood the arithmetic perfectly. She had spent two days in a chair computing it. He had spent, apparently, no time at all.

*You idiot,* she thought at the gravedigger's coat, with a love so fierce it embarrassed her. *You wonderful, hopeless idiot.*

A soldier came up the stairs, took her elbow — not roughly, which somehow made it worse — and walked her down through the yard. The snow-sweepers paused to watch. At the gate, the world resolved: the fence, the frost, and Ulyana's face above the collar of the coat, older-looking than she remembered, eyes going over her quickly the way he must once have gone over wounded men — counting limbs, checking for damage, triaging.

"They fed you," he said.

"Once. The soup was an insult to soup everywhere."

"Good," he said gravely. "Insulting soup is a sign of recovery."

And that was all. That was the whole homecoming — no embrace, no speeches, because the gate stood open between them and eyes were watching, and some things you saved for later or lost forever. A soldier walked her through. The gate closed behind her with a sound like a book shutting.

She made it eleven steps past the fence before her legs informed her, without consultation, that they were done. Ulyana caught her arm without comment and steered her into the alley behind the chandler's shop, where she sat down on an overturned crate in the snow and shook — not from fear, finally, fear having used itself up somewhere around hour five of the chair, but from something else, something with no name yet, the residue of two days of being an object in other people's negotiations.

"You traded the real letter," she managed.

"I traded a letter," Ulyana said.

"A copy."

"A very good copy. I stayed up half the night on it. My handwriting is better than Brandt's, actually, though don't repeat that; it feels disrespectful to the dead."

"How long do they have?"

"Days. Maybe less. Long enough." He crouched in front of her, and for the first time she saw how tired he was, worn through like a coin passed over too many palms. "Long enough for it to matter. I hope."

"But they'll check. Sooner or later someone compares it to the second letter, or Hess remembers a date wrong, and then—" She stopped. Her voice had come out smaller than she intended.

"Then they'll know I deceived them," Ulyana said simply. "Yes. I know. I bought you, Marta, and I paid with the element of surprise, and I'd do it again before you finished asking. But it means the next thing we do has to happen soon, and in the open, where copies and tricks can't save anyone." He paused. "Are you all right? Truly. Not the brave answer."

Marta considered the question with more honesty than she had given anyone in two days. "My hands don't shake," she said at last. "But there's a part of me that's still sitting in that chair. I think it might stay there a while. I think—" she frowned, working at it, "—I think freedom is supposed to feel bigger than this. This just feels like a different-sized room."

Ulyana nodded slowly, the nod of a man who had sat in many chairs of many sizes. "Rooms get bigger," he said. "Slowly. With help."

* * *

Elena Vasiliev received them at the chapel cellar with a pot of barley soup and the ferocious hospitality of a woman who had learned that feeding people was the one rebellion always available. Marta ate two bowls before she could speak in complete sentences, which the household treated as a medical milestone.

The cellar was fuller than she remembered — witnesses moved again, whispering again, the schoolmaster's empty place at the table marked by nobody and seen by everybody. News traveled down the cellar stairs in fragments: the armistice rumor had grown legs and was running through town faster than any courier Marta had ever employed; the tribunal had been moved up; the trial of the Vasiliev family would open within the week, in the town hall, with General Hess himself presiding from the front bench like a patron at a play he had written.

"He'll want it clean," Elena said, ladling soup with the rhythm of someone thinking aloud. "Quick, tidy, convincing. A conviction before the peace comes and takes his crime away with it."

"Then clean is what we ruin," Ulyana said. "In front of everyone."

Marta listened, warm for the first time in days, and felt the chair-room inside her expand by a few inches. Because this was the thing about the work — the thing nobody told you when they handed you your first letter and said *don't be interesting*: it was not made of secrets. Secrets were just the wrapping. Underneath, it was made of this — a cellar, a soup pot, a woman who refused to vanish, a man who traded away his only advantage for a girl with shaking-not-shaking hands — and it was worth every checkpoint, every lie, every hour in every chair.

That night, wrapped in a blanket among strangers, Marta took out the stub of pencil she had hidden in her boot sole — they had missed it, or hadn't bothered — and opened the small oilcloth packet that was hers alone. Inside were her own lists: deliveries made, families served, debts of gratitude owed across half the province.

At the bottom, in careful letters, she added a line:

*O.I. — traded the truth for me and called it nothing. Owed: everything.*

She looked at it for a long moment. Then, beneath it, because a courier's ledger must be accurate even when it breaks her heart a little:

*Also owed: one clock. Still broken. Still his fault, somehow.*

She smiled — small, crooked, real — and closed the packet, and slept without dreaming of chairs.

Above ground, in the town hall, carpenters were already building the tribunal's platform, and Judge Lindqvist — summoned by summons Marta herself had once carried in a broken clock — arrived on the evening coach, unaware that in three days' time a nurse in whites would walk into his courtroom carrying a ledger full of the dead, and that the whole machinery of the province would grind against him, and that he would need every friend he had.

Among them, though he did not yet know her name, would be a girl with a pencil stub in her boot.
