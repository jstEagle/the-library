## 18. The Tribunal Opens

The town hall of Krasnogorsk had been built for weddings and harvest festivals, which is why it made such a poor theater for a trial. Its windows were too tall, letting in a thin winter light that fell on everyone equally — the guilty and the accused and the merely curious — as though the building itself refused to take sides. Someone had hung a military banner over the stage where the tribunal sat, and it did not quite cover the faded bunting from some older celebration, so that behind Judge Lindqvist's head there hovered a ghost of red ribbon, like a wound that hadn't finished deciding what it was.

Ulyana stood at the back in his nurse's whites, freshly laundered by Sister Anneli, who had pressed them with an iron heated on the rectory stove and said nothing at all except, "Stand straight. Lies travel faster through a crooked spine."

They were not lies, precisely, what he intended to say. But they would be called lies by men whose business was calling things lies, and so he took the advice.

The hall was fuller than he expected. Word had traveled — Marta's network, or simply the town's hunger for something other than ration bread — and every bench held faces he half-knew. The baker's widow. Two of the schoolmaster's former pupils, sitting together the way children sit when they have agreed not to cry. Old Ferencz, who had broken in the cellar and given up the chapel's secret; he sat near the door with his hat crushed in his fists, and when Ulyana's eyes passed over him, the man flinched and then, remarkably, nodded — a small, terrible nod that said *I know what I cost you, and I am here anyway.*

Ulyana nodded back. There was no time in war or tribunals for anything gentler.

On the stage, three chairs behind a long table draped in gray cloth. Judge Lindqvist occupied the center chair, a narrow man with spectacles that kept sliding down a nose built for better-fitting glasses, and the particular stillness of someone who has decided to be surprised by nothing. To his left, Major Reinholt — uniform immaculate, hands folded, wearing the face of a man attending a colleague's funeral: solemn, correct, and privately relieved. To Lindqvist's right, General Hess.

Hess was the surprise.

Ulyana had prepared himself for the general the way one prepares for a storm — braced, grim, expecting thunder. Instead Hess sat like a favorite uncle at a christening, silver-haired, blue-eyed, radiating the calm of a man who has never once been late for anything. He wore no decorations beyond what modesty permitted, and even his boots seemed apologetic about their shine. When the crowd murmured, he smiled at them as if they were grandchildren reciting poems badly.

*That*, Ulyana thought, *is the most dangerous thing in this room.* Not the guards at the doors. Not the stenographer with her careful hands. The smile.

And below the stage, at two small tables facing the tribunal like penitents at confession: the Vasilievs.

Elena sat with her spine straight and her hands quiet on the table, wearing her good winter coat because she had apparently decided that if they meant to try her, they would do it while looking at a woman, not a prisoner. Beside her, her father Dmitri Vasiliev had aged ten years since the arrest, his hands trembling until Elena covered one of them with hers, at which point they stopped, as if her palm were a paperweight set down on a windy day. Her mother was not present. Illness, Marta had reported. Or perhaps the tribunal preferred its accused in manageable numbers.

Ulyana looked at Elena and found that his heart performed a foolish thing entirely unsuited to the occasion: it leapt. Forty years old, trained to keep steady hands while men died, and his heart leapt like a boy's because a woman across a room lifted her chin. He breathed it back down. Later, he told it. If there is a later.

Lindqvist opened proceedings by reading the charges, and it was, Ulyana admitted, beautiful work — if you admired craftsmanship divorced from truth.

The case against the Vasiliev family ran like this: that in the autumn of 1916, supplies bound for the Third Field Hospital — grain, medical stores, coal, and winter kit — had been diverted from their convoy and sold through intermediaries, and that the manifests bearing the Vasiliev trading house's seals were proof of complicity. That Dmitri Vasiliev, a supplier of long standing, had used wartime scarcity to enrich himself while soldiers froze. That the family's flight from certain debts, and their known correspondence with persons now classified as enemy nationals, completed a picture no reasonable tribunal could misread.

It was seamless. That was the horror of it. Every document genuine — the seals were real, the signatures real, the convoy numbers real. Only the story wrapped around them was false, and a false story built entirely of true bricks is the hardest thing in the world to knock down. You cannot call the bricks liars.

Reinholt presented the documentary evidence himself, walking the stenographer through each exhibit in a voice pitched for grief. He was good. Ulyana watched him and felt something almost like respect curdle into something else. This was the man who had offered promotion for silence, who had posted a description of a nurse with forged papers, who had interrogated an old schoolmaster past the point where old hearts stop. And here he stood mourning the thefts he knew his own patron had committed, with the sincerity of an actor who has played the role so long the tears come unassisted.

"Major," Lindqvist said at one point, "the manifest dated the fourteenth of October — the diversion is recorded here as a routing error corrected retroactively. Who authorized the correction?"

"The regional supply office, Your Honor." A pause measured to the half-second. "An office since dissolved in the general reorganization. Unfortunately, the relevant officers are unavailable."

Unavailable. Ulyana turned the word over. Some of those officers were dead — he knew, because their names were in his ledger, entered by candlelight with the causes listed honestly, unlike the reports. Dead men were very convenient witnesses. They never contradicted you, and they could be promoted posthumously into whatever villainy the narrative required.

Through the morning the testimony rolled forward. A quartermaster testified to receiving diverted goods and identifying the Vasiliev seals. A clerk confirmed the correspondence. Each witness answered smoothly, each answer fitted the last like joinery, and Ulyana understood with growing coldness that this had been rehearsed — not crudely, but the way a choir rehearses, until the harmony sounds inevitable. Somewhere, weeks ago, in a warm room, these men had practiced a trial.

He glanced at Hess. The general listened with his chin propped lightly on two fingers, nodding occasionally, the picture of a man pained by necessity. Once, when the quartermaster stumbled slightly, Hess leaned toward Lindqvist and murmured something, and Lindqvist — Ulyana watched closely — did not smile, but something around his mouth went carefully neutral, the way a face goes when it is declining an invitation.

*A seam,* Brandt's letter had called it. *There is a seam in every machine. Find the honest man and pull.*

Was Lindqvist the seam? Or was the neutrality just another kind of tailoring?

At midday the tribunal recessed, and the hall emptied slowly, the crowd shuffling out into the snow to eat whatever the town could still provide. Guards remained at the doors. Elena was permitted to stand, escorted, near the window, and Ulyana — keeping to the shadowed side of the hall, in whites that any soldier would assume belonged to the medical detail — managed to pass within six feet of her.

She did not look at him. She looked at the window, at the falling snow, and said, very quietly, to no one in particular:

"He's going to win, isn't he? By evening."

"Not yet," Ulyana murmured, not turning his head, adjusting a nonexistent bandage roll under his arm. "Stories only look finished before the last page."

"You always did read ahead in books. It used to infuriate your teachers."

"It infuriated everyone. It's why I became a nurse. Nobody checks whether you've read ahead when you're holding a basin."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, but the ground where a smile might take root — and then the escort touched her elbow and she was walked away, straight-backed, and Ulyana went to find the water pump and drink until his hands stopped shaking.

* * *

The afternoon session began with the defense, and the defense was a formality conducted by a court-appointed officer named Vogel, a young man with the defeated posture of someone handed a losing hand and instructed to play it beautifully. He called Dmitri Vasiliev, who testified in a cracked voice that the seals had been stolen, that the correspondence had been forged or intercepted, that he had reported the discrepancies to the authorities in November and received no reply. All of it true. All of it sounding, in that room, like exactly what a guilty man would say.

"And who," Reinholt asked gently, "would profit sufficiently to arrange so elaborate a forgery? Name the person, Herr Vasiliev. You accuse shadows. Shadows make poor defendants."

Dmitri's hands trembled. Elena's hand moved toward his and stopped halfway — she could not reach him at the witness table — and the distance between her fingers and her father's knuckles was perhaps the cruelest image the day produced.

"I don't know names," Dmitri said. "I know convoys. I counted crates for thirty years. I know what was on those trucks, and I know it didn't go where the papers say—"

"Conjecture," Reinholt said, without heat, without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "The tribunal thanks you."

Lindqvist made a note. Hess examined his cuff. The clock on the town hall wall — borrowed, like everything in that building, from some private parlor requisitioned for the war — ticked with the patience of machinery that had seen empires change hands and expected to see more.

And Ulyana, standing at the back among the guards, felt the moment arrive the way you feel a hemorrhage begin before any blood shows: a wrongness in the air, a certainty that the body of the day was bleeding out internally and would be dead by supper unless someone applied pressure *now*.

Vogel rested his case. Lindqvist consulted his papers. Outside, the light was already going the color of pewter; the tribunal would close today, verdict tomorrow morning, tidy as a made bed. Hess had scheduled justice the way he scheduled everything — punctually, before the armistice could arrive and dissolve the whole proceeding like sugar in rain.

Ulyana thought of Brandt dying by inches in the dressing station, pressing wax-sealed paper into a nurse's hands because a nurse was the only man he trusted with the weight. He thought of the schoolmaster, whose name he had written in the ledger with a steadiness he had not felt. He thought of Marta, somewhere in the town with bruised wrists and a silence she wouldn't explain, who had risked a checkpoint with summons hidden in a broken clock because she believed names mattered. He thought of his own name, struck from the registers, his papers burned to ash in a chapel stove — a man who officially did not exist, testifying before a tribunal of men who officially existed very much indeed.

Then he thought of Elena's palm on her father's hand, stopping the trembling.

Sister Anneli had told him: stand straight. Lies travel faster through a crooked spine.

So does courage. It travels the same road, and it travels fast, and mostly it gets there before you've agreed to send it.

Ulyana stepped out from the wall and walked down the center aisle of the Krasnogorsk town hall, in nurse's whites ironed on a rectory stove, and every eye in the room turned to follow him — the crowd first, then Reinholt, whose face performed a small, controlled catastrophe, then Hess, whose smile stayed perfectly in place the way a mask stays in place, and finally Judge Lindqvist, whose spectacles slid down his nose as he looked up, and who did not push them back.

Ulyana reached the space before the tribunal, stopped, and inclined his head with the precise courtesy due to rank and none whatsoever to fear.

"Your Honor. Members of the tribunal." His voice carried further than he expected; the tall windows gave it back to him. "I request permission to testify."

Reinholt was on his feet. "This witness is not recognized by the court. He has no standing. He is—" and here the major caught himself, visibly recalculating, because to say *he is a deserter with forged papers* would be to confirm the man existed, "—he is unknown to this proceeding."

"He is dressed as a medical orderly," Lindqvist observed mildly.

"I am a nurse," Ulyana said. "Eleven years' service, field stations along the Riga line. And I come before this tribunal in a capacity the court has not yet heard from, though heaven knows it should have: I am a keeper of casualty records."

A murmur ran the length of the benches.

"Every report filed through our station passed through officers who decided what the army would remember," Ulyana continued, before anyone could stop him, and he heard his own voice grow calmer as it grew louder, the way it did over dying men. "Some of what they decided to forget, I wrote down. Names. Dates. Convoy numbers — because the dead travel on manifests too, Your Honor, and I kept both ledgers. The one the army signed, and the one it didn't."

"Your Honor," Reinholt said sharply, "this is an irregularity. This man is self-declared, unverified—"

"So was every witness you presented this morning," Lindqvist said.

The hall went very quiet. Lindqvist removed his spectacles, polished them with a handkerchief that had seen better decades, and put them back on, and looked at Ulyana over the top of them for a long moment — a look that weighed, that measured, that asked a question no one spoke aloud: *How much of this is going to hurt, and are you prepared for how much?*

"State your name for the record," Lindqvist said.

And Ulyana, who had burned his documents, who had buried his rank, whose name had been struck from every register the army kept — Ulyana Iversen opened his mouth to give the tribunal the one thing he had left, the one possession no order could confiscate except by taking everything—

"Ulyana Iversen," he said. "Nurse, First Class. And I have brought the names of forty-one men this tribunal's reports declared missing rather than dead — and the dates on which the supplies meant to save them changed hands."

From the corner of his eye, he saw Hess lean forward at last, the uncle's smile gone, replaced by something older and colder — the expression of a man watching a debt walk into the room and introduce itself by name.

Lindqvist folded his hands.

"Then," the judge said quietly, "you may begin."
