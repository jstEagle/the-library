## 21. Exile, Lightly Carried

The verdict was read on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday the town had already begun to forget it.

That was the thing about Krasnogorsk that Elena Vasiliev understood now, standing at her own kitchen window with a cup of tea going cold in her hands: a town could acquit you and still convict you, all in the same breath. The tribunal had dismissed the charges — Judge Lindqvist had said the words plainly enough, "cleared of all wrongdoing," and old Fenn the clerk had written them into the record with his careful schoolmaster's hand — but the words did not reach the eyes of the neighbors. The eyes were their own tribunal. They convened daily, on doorsteps and outside the bakery, and their verdicts never changed.

Mrs. Halvorsen had nodded to her yesterday across the market square. A nod! After twenty years of borrowing sugar and returning it with interest, after funerals attended and children nursed through scarlet fever, Mrs. Halvorsen gave her the sort of nod one gives a stranger who may or may not be dangerous.

"They're afraid," her father had said, when she'd told him. Old Vasili Vasiliev sat by the stove with his blanket over his knees like a king holding court over six feet of floor. "Afraid people are like weather, Elena. You don't blame the rain for being wet. You just stop planning picnics."

"That's very philosophical for a man who shouted at the rain last autumn."

"The rain shouted first."

She laughed despite herself — her father could always do that, extract laughter from her like a splinter, painful and relieving at once — but the laugh didn't hold. Nothing held lately. The house had been returned to them, technically, the guards withdrawn, the seals peeled off the doors. But the guards' boots had left marks on the floors, gray crescents of mud ground into the pine boards in the hall, and Elena found she couldn't look at them without seeing the night they'd come. She'd scrubbed the hall twice. The crescents remained, faint as fingerprints on glass.

Some things scrubbed out. Some things only got paler.

* * *

It was Marta who finally said what everyone was thinking, because Marta was fourteen and therefore constitutionally incapable of leaving anything unsaid longer than an hour.

"You're leaving," she announced, arriving at the kitchen with the particular breathlessness of a girl who had run most of the way and would deny it if asked. She dropped into a chair as though the chair owed her money. "Everyone says so. Papa says so. The watchmaker says so, and the watchmaker knows everything, he just pretends he only knows clocks."

"Does he," said Elena, pouring tea. "And why do you say so?"

"Because you've started giving things away." Marta wrapped both hands around the cup — she held tea like it was a small animal she was rescuing from the cold. "You gave Mrs. Lindqvist your good copper kettle yesterday. I saw. Nobody gives away a kettle unless they're leaving. Kettles are forever. That's the whole point of kettles."

Elena sat down across from her. Outside, February was doing its slow gray work on the snow, and somewhere down the street a dog was barking at nothing with tremendous conviction.

"Where would we even go?" Elena asked. It wasn't really a question for Marta. It was a question for the ceiling, for the house, for the whole idea of home.

"East," Marta said promptly. "Papa has cousins near Tobolsk. Everyone has cousins near Tobolsk; it's where cousins go when things happen." She drank her tea, then added, with the gentleness that always startled people who thought of her as all elbows: "It doesn't have to be forever. Places stay. People can come back."

"People can come back," Elena repeated. "That's what my mother used to say about the river flooding. Right up until the flood took the bridge."

"So build a boat." Marta shrugged. "I'm just saying what everyone knows. You'd rather know it too. You hate being the last one told. Ulyana told me — you once made him walk back half a mile because he'd heard a piece of news secondhand and hadn't told you immediately."

"I made him walk back half a mile because the news was that the Lindqvists' cow had calved, and he let me worry for three days thinking something serious had happened."

"It *was* serious. Twin calves. That's practically a festival."

* * *

They decided in the end the way most families decide things — not in one grand conversation but in a hundred small ones, over dishes and boots and the packing of trunks, until one evening her father set down his spoon during supper and said, "Well then. Tobolsk," as if announcing the weather, and that was that.

Ulyana came the next day. He came quietly, the way he did everything now; his name had been struck from the army registers, his rank dissolved, his uniform replaced by a civilian coat that hung on him like a borrowed opinion. Stripped of rank yet carrying a new ledger — the town gossips described him this way with relish, having run out of fresher material. He looked, Elena thought, lighter than he had any right to look. A man who had traded everything away and somehow ended up holding more.

He stood in the doorway of the kitchen, hat in hand, and looked around at the crates.

"So it's true," he said.

"Marta says kettles are forever and I've given mine away. Apparently that settles it."

"Marta is rarely wrong about objects. It's people she misjudges." He came in, sat down heavily in the chair that had been his since they were both young enough to get scolded for putting elbows on this exact table. For a while neither spoke. The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs, her father was singing — badly, deliberately badly, an old song about a fisherman and an unreasonable sea, the kind of singing a man does when he wants the people downstairs to know he's there and content.

"You could come with us," Elena said.

There. She'd said it. She'd been carrying the sentence around for two days like a stone in her coat pocket, turning it over, and now it was out in the open air between them, and she found she could breathe.

Ulyana looked at her for a long moment. Then he smiled — not the sad smile she'd expected, but a real one, crooked and warm, the smile of the boy who had once walked half a mile twice in one afternoon over a cow.

"Elena. My name isn't anywhere anymore. Not in the registers, not in the rolls, not on any list the government keeps. If I board that train with you, I'm not traveling under a name. I'm traveling under a rumor." He spread his hands. "Besides — someone has to stay and keep the ledger."

"The ledger of the living. Yes. And who will you write in it, here? Half the town won't meet your eye."

"Half the town wouldn't meet my eye before, either. I'm a nurse. We're used to being ignored until urgently needed." He leaned forward. "But listen — listen. You think exile is the punishment. It isn't. Look at your father up there, singing badly on purpose. Look at Marta, who carried a summons inside a broken clock and came out of that checkpoint room taller than when she went in. Belonging was never the house, Elena. It was never this street, or the churchyard, or Mrs. Halvorsen's terrible nods. It's the people who helped. The farmers who hid witnesses in their hay. The chaplain who kept Brandt's letter against his heart for a month. The watchmaker, who fixed a clock he knew had been used to deceive him and asked no questions except whether it kept good time now." He tapped the table. "That list goes with you wherever you go. That list *is* where you live."

"And yours?"

"Mine stays here. Someone must remember this place kindly, or it'll only be remembered the way Reinholt remembered it — as a problem to be solved." He paused. "Though I confess I may travel a bit myself. There are names in my ledger whose families were never told how their sons died. There are widows in three provinces who think their husbands deserted. I intend to spend a year or five correcting the record. It seems I'm unemployable in every respectable capacity, so I might as well be employed in an honest one."

Elena laughed, and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and pretended she hadn't.

* * *

The last week passed the way the last week of anything passes — too quickly and not quickly enough, full of small ceremonies nobody planned.

The watchmaker came to the house with a parcel wrapped in brown paper and string, and refused, with great ceremony, to accept payment for anything ever again. Inside the paper was the clock — the broken clock, the clock that had hidden the summons through a checkpoint and a interrogation and a child's lie. He had repaired it properly. New mainspring, cleaned works, a fresh face of glass.

"A clock should keep time honestly after that," he said, "or it's no use to anyone. Besides—" and here the old man's voice went sly— "it owes you an hour. It spent one of yours lying to a major. I've adjusted it. It runs one minute fast. Perpetually. So it's always paying you back."

Her father wept exactly once, privately, and was heard doing it, and pretended not to have been, which was the family tradition. Mrs. Halvorsen appeared at the gate on the final morning with a jar of preserved plums and an expression like a woman crossing a frozen river, and thrust it at Elena and said, "For the road. Your mother's recipe was better but mine keeps longer," which in the currency of Mrs. Halvorsen amounted to an apology, an obituary for the friendship, and a promise of its resurrection, all at once. Elena took the jar and kissed her cheek, and the old woman fled.

Marta did not flee. Marta planted herself in the middle of the kitchen on packing day and declared, arms crossed, "I'm coming to the station and you can't stop me, and furthermore I've started a ledger of my own."

"A ledger of what?"

"Of everyone who helped *you*. Ulyana thinks he invented remembering people, but he didn't. I've got thirty-one names already. Thirty-two counting the watchmaker's apprentice, who carried flour to the cellar people and thinks nobody noticed. I notice everything. It's my worst quality and my best one."

Elena looked at her — this girl who had been stopped at a checkpoint with a nation's worth of hope concealed in a broken clock, who had been questioned and frightened and had come out the other side still willing to carry things for strangers — and felt her chest do something complicated.

"When you're older," Elena said, "and the world is less mad, you should write all of it down. Properly. The whole story. The clock, the cellar, the schoolmaster—"

"I know." Marta's chin lifted. "I'm going to call it something grand. I haven't decided yet. Something about time."

* * *

The morning of departure came clear and hard, the sky the blue of new steel, the cold honest for once — the kind of cold that doesn't sneak up on you but introduces itself formally and shakes hands.

The whole platform, or what felt like it: the watchmaker and his apprentice, the chaplain, the Lindqvists with the twin calves' owner in tow (the calves had become a local legend), a dozen others whose names were in Marta's ledger and Ulyana's and, Elena suspected, in each other's by now, the lists cross-pollinating like gardens. They did not cheer. This was not a cheering kind of town anymore. But they stood close, closer than they had stood in months, and that was its own kind of anthem.

Ulyana carried her father's trunk as if it weighed nothing, which it did not, and settled the old man by the window where the sun would reach him. Her father gripped Ulyana's wrist and said something too quiet for Elena to hear, and Ulyana nodded once, gravely, as though accepting a commission.

Then Elena stood facing him on the platform, and the whistle sounded, long and low, and everything that needed saying had been said except one thing, which she said anyway:

"Come find us. When the ledgers are done. Tobolsk has terrible winters and excellent people, and there will always be a chair, and my father will sing."

"Heaven forbid," said Ulyana, and hugged her, brief and fierce, the hug of people who had learned that time is borrowed. "I'll write. Carefully. Through channels Marta approves of."

"She'll inspect your envelopes," Elena said. "She told me."

"She told me too."

The train began to move. Elena climbed aboard and leaned from the window, and the platform slid backward — the faces, the breath-clouds, the town beyond them with its crooked streets and its watched windows and its stubborn, surviving kindnesses. Her father raised his hand. Marta ran alongside the train for twenty glorious strides, ledger clutched to her chest like a heart outside her body, shouting something lost entirely to the wind and entirely meant regardless.

And Ulyana stood where he stood, hat in hand again, getting smaller, getting stiller, keeping his post.

Elena turned at last and sat beside her father, the repaired clock ticking faithfully on her lap, one minute fast, always paying back. Ahead lay a thousand versts of winter and a town that had never heard their name.

"Well," said her father, settling back, closing his eyes, already composing, by the look of him, some fresh atrocity against the fisherman's song. "Tell me about these cousins in Tobolsk. Are they musical?"

"Dreadfully," said Elena, and found she was smiling. "Everyone we're about to meet is."
