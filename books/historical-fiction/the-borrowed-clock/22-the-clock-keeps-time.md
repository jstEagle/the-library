## 22. The Clock Keeps Time

The snow had stopped in the night, and Krasnogorsk woke under a sky so clean and pale it looked rinsed. Ulyana stood at the edge of the station square with his collar up and his hands in his pockets, watching the steam rise off the roofs of the town as though the whole place were exhaling after holding its breath for a year.

The tribunal had been over for six days. Hess was dead, Reinholt was a witness instead of a hunter, and the Vasilievs were free — free to stay, the judge's ruling said, in ink that would outlive everyone who signed it. But freedom on paper and freedom on a street were different animals, and the Vasilievs had looked down their own lane at the shuttered windows of neighbors who had watched everything happen and said nothing, and they had made their choice the way Elena made every choice: quickly, quietly, and without asking anyone's permission to feel it.

So: exile. Lightly carried. A train east at ten minutes past nine.

Ulyana checked his pocket watch — Brandt's watch, actually; he'd inherited it along with everything else the colonel could no longer use — and saw that he had forty minutes, which meant he had time to stand still for a while, which was itself a kind of luxury he hadn't permitted himself since autumn.

The station square was busier than it had any right to be. Word traveled strangely in small towns; it seemed to travel fastest when it had no official reason to exist. Half of Krasnogorsk had turned out, ostensibly to go about their ordinary business near the railway, and they stood about in clusters pretending to discuss coal prices while watching the Vasiliev family load four trunks and a crate of books onto a cart. Nobody spoke to them. Nobody quite looked away, either. It was the peculiar cowardice of people who wanted, very badly, to have been brave all along and hoped proximity might count retroactively.

Elena noticed it too. Ulyana watched her notice it — a small tilt of her head, the same look she used to give the neighbor's dog when it stole bread off her windowsill: not anger, exactly, more an accounting. She said something to her mother, and Mrs. Vasiliev laughed, which startled three separate clusters into silence. Whatever the joke was, Ulyana suspected it was about him. Most of Elena's jokes that winter had been about him. He had learned to accept this as a form of affection.

"You're hovering," said a voice at his elbow, and there was Marta, hands jammed in the pockets of a coat two sizes too large for her, hair escaping from under her cap in every direction like she'd lost an argument with the wind and refused to concede.

"I'm waiting."

"Waiting is hovering with dignity." She fell into step beside him, matching his pace toward the platform. "You've been standing by the fence for eleven minutes. I counted."

"Of course you did."

"Somebody has to keep records around here." She grinned up at him, and for a moment — just a moment — the grin was entirely the old one, the one from the train, quick and crooked and slightly illegal-looking. Then it settled back into what it had become since the checkpoint: a grin worn over something quieter. She didn't talk about those hours between detention and release, and no one asked, and the silence around it was not empty but full, the way a cellar is full — of things stored carefully in the dark.

They reached the platform. The eastbound train sat hissing, patient as an old horse. Somewhere behind them the station clock struck the quarter hour, and Ulyana felt Marta go still beside him, just for a heartbeat, before moving again.

"Here," she said abruptly, and thrust something into his hands.

It was wrapped in brown paper and string, and it was heavier than its size suggested, and he knew what it was before he finished unwrapping it. The broken clock. The one she had carried through the checkpoint with summons hidden inside its hollow case, the lie that had saved the last witness and nearly cost her everything.

"The watchmaker fixed it," Marta said. Her voice was doing something careful, walking on ice. "Old Pavel. I went to him last week and told him the truth — well. Some of the truth. I told him I'd lied to him about whose clock it was, and that it mattered more than any clock had ever mattered, and I couldn't say why, and he looked at me for about a year—"

"He's good at that look," Ulyana said. "He repaired my mother's sewing machine once and managed to make me feel guilty about how I'd oiled it."

"—and then he cleaned it, and put in a new mainspring, and charged me nothing, and told me to tell whoever it belonged to that clocks are for keeping time, not for hiding in." She shrugged, elaborately casual. "So now it keeps time. And it doesn't belong to anyone. So."

Ulyana turned it over in his hands. It was a plain thing, walnut-cased, brass-faced, the sort of clock that had sat on ten thousand mantelpieces in ten thousand rooms where ordinary people drank tea and argued about weather. The glass over the face was new — Pavel must have replaced it — and the hands moved now, small and steady, ticking against his palms like something alive.

"It stopped during the war," Marta said, quieter now. "I mean — it never ran. It was already broken when I got it. Everything was broken then. And now—" She gestured vaguely at the train, the town, the entire catastrophe of the past year. "Now it isn't. That seemed worth saying properly, even if it comes out stupid."

"It doesn't come out stupid."

"Well. Good." She scuffed a boot against the platform. "I also brought you this, because apparently today is the day I give away everything I own."

The second object was a notebook. Not new — the cover was soft with handling, the spine mended with thread — but empty. Ulyana opened it and found clean pages, unruled, smelling faintly of the watchmaker's workshop: oil and metal shavings and, inexplicably, cloves.

"Pavel's son kept accounts in it until he emigrated," Marta said. "Pavel says ledgers should go to people who'll fill them. He said—" and here she faltered, because she had clearly rehearsed this part and the rehearsal was failing, "he said he'd heard about what you read out. At the tribunal. Everyone heard. And he said a man who keeps names like that needs somewhere to put the living ones too."

Ulyana held the notebook and the clock together and found, to his considerable annoyance, that his throat had closed. He was thirty-four years old and had held dying men steady with both hands, and a repaired clock and an empty book were undoing him on a public platform.

"Marta," he managed.

"Don't," she said. "If you thank me I'll have to hit you with the clock, and then it'll be broken again, and we'll have wasted a perfectly good mainspring." But she stepped in close and bumped her shoulder against his arm, briefly, hard, which from Marta was equivalent to weeping openly.

*

They boarded at five minutes to nine.

The Vasilievs traveled as they did everything — with more dignity than luggage. There were only the four trunks and the books and Mr. Vasiliev, Elena's father, who insisted on carrying a single potted plant that had no business surviving the winter and looked, frankly, delighted to be proving everyone wrong. Elena had changed out of the grey dress she'd worn through the tribunal into traveling clothes, and her hair was pinned plainly, and she looked like what she was: a woman leaving a country that had tried to erase her, carrying herself anyway.

She came to find Ulyana on the platform while the others settled in. They walked to the far end, past the water tower, where the noise of the crowd thinned and there was only the tick of the clock in his coat pocket and the wind coming down the line.

"So," she said.

"So," he agreed.

"You could come with us." She said it looking at the rails, not at him, which was her way of making it easy — or trying to. "There's room. There's nothing for you here anymore. Your name's struck from the registers, your post is gone, your—" she waved a hand at his uniform, or rather at the civilian coat that had replaced it, "—your country has formally declared that you don't exist. Which, I'm told, is a terrible inconvenience. You'd have to travel as someone else. Again."

"I've had practice."

"That's what worries me." Now she looked at him. Her eyes were the same ones from the churchyard, from childhood summers, from the promise made in peacetime that neither of them had known would cost so much. "You've spent so long being useful to other people's lives, Ulyana. The dead, the family, the witnesses. I watched you do it all winter and I never once saw you ask what you wanted."

"I want the Vasilievs alive," he said. "That worked out."

"And after?"

The question hung there. Down the platform, the conductor was calling something; a whistle sounded, far off, the first of the warnings. Ulyana thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty and he owed her at least that much truth among so many borrowed ones.

"After," he said slowly, "I think I want to write down some names. Living ones. Starting with yours." He took out the notebook, the soft-covered one, and opened it to the first page. "Marta thinks I should. Pavel thinks I should. Apparently the whole town has opinions about my future except me, so I've decided to let the town win."

Elena laughed — really laughed, the sound cracking open the cold air — and then pressed her hand flat against his chest, over the pocket where the notebook sat, as though taking its temperature.

"Write us often," she said. "Not about us. To us. We'll be somewhere with a bad address and worse mail service, and letters will take months, and I expect every single one of them."

"You'll get them."

"And you—" She stopped herself. Steadied. Elena Vasiliev did not cry at stations; that was for people with less spine and more privacy. "You will find something that is yours. Not Brandt's. Not mine. Not the dead's. Yours. Promise me that one, and then I'll board this train, and that will be the end of promises between us forever, because I refuse to owe you another."

"I promise," Ulyana said.

She kissed his cheek, briskly, the way she'd done when they were nine years old and she'd dared him to climb the churchyard wall and he'd fallen off it. Then she walked back down the platform with her chin up, and Mr. Vasiliev handed her the potted plant through the carriage door like passing over a national treasure, and the whistle blew again, longer this time, and the great iron animal began, grudgingly, to move.

Ulyana stood and watched the train go. The windows passed one by one — Marta's face in one of them, her hand raised, her mouth shaping words the wind took; Mrs. Vasiliev's kerchief; the plant, absurdly, held up to the light like a torch — and then the tail of the train, and then the track, curving away east through the snow fields until it was only a dark line and then a suggestion and then nothing at all, just the space where the future had gone.

He stood there long after. The station clock struck nine-thirty.

Then he took out the clock, set it on the platform bench beside him where it could see the light, and opened the ledger to its first page.

*

*The Ledger of the Living*, he wrote at the top, because a man ought to name what he builds, and then, beneath it, because there was no point pretending otherwise:

*Vasiliev, Elena — living. Exile, chosen. Laughs the same way she did at twelve.*

*Vasiliev, Andrei — living. Carries a plant that has outlasted an empire.*

*Marta — living. Courier, liar, hero. Owes me nothing, insists otherwise.*

He sat back. The pen hovered. The clock ticked on the bench, small and stubborn, marking out seconds that belonged to no army and answered to no general.

There were more names, he knew. There would always be more names. The schoolmaster's widow in the blue shawl, who had brought bread to the cellar every night knowing what it was for. Old Pavel, who fixed clocks and charged nothing and understood what they were for. Judge Lindqvist, who had accepted a nurse's notebook into evidence over the objection of a general, and would pay for it in ways quiet and slow. Even Reinholt — and Ulyana paused over this one a long while, pen scratching nothing — even Reinholt, who had finally spoken the truth when speaking it cost him everything he'd built his life on, and who now lived in a small room in his own garrison waiting to learn whether truthfulness was a virtue or merely a late symptom. Perhaps that name would wait. Perhaps some pages were better left blank until they knew what they were.

But the first page was full, and the first page was enough, and the first page was the truest thing he had ever written, because every name on it was breathing somewhere in the world at this exact moment, and no register, no order, no struck-through line in any ministry could confiscate that.

A shadow fell across the page. Ulyana looked up.

Marta stood there. On the platform. Holding a second bag.

He stared at her. The train was a mile gone, maybe two, a smudge of smoke on the eastern horizon.

"You missed your train," he said, stupidly.

"No, I didn't." She sat down beside him, dropped her bag between her boots, and helped herself to the pen out of his hand with the proprietary ease of someone repossessing property. "I got off at the water stop. Everyone gets off at the water stop, it takes twenty minutes and the tea is terrible. I told Elena I was staying."

"She let you?"

"Elena said—" Marta bent over the ledger, reading his entries upside down with frank disapproval of his handwriting, "—Elena said, *finally, someone sensible is staying with him,* and then she gave me this." She produced, from her coat, a letter. Sealed, addressed in Elena's upright hand: *For Ulyana. Open when you have written the tenth name.* "And then the train started moving and I got off, and that's the whole story, and if you make a speech about it I'm taking the clock back."

Ulyana looked at her — sixteen years old, half a war survived, a courier's instincts and a soldier's scars and the nerve of a burglar — and felt something in his chest that had been clenched since August loosen, one notch, like a mainspring settling into working order.

"The watchmaker needs an apprentice," he said. "Someone reliable. Someone who can lie convincingly to officials and count accurately."

"Pavel already asked me." She smirked. "I told him I'd think about it. I wanted to see your face first."

"My face."

"When you realized I wasn't on the train." She tapped the clock on the bench, where its hands stood at twenty minutes to ten, running perfectly, keeping time like it had never once stopped. "Worth it. Your face was worth the whole walk back."

The wind came down the line from the east, carrying the last smell of coal smoke. Somewhere in the town behind them, a door opened and shut; life going on, indifferent and miraculous. Ulyana picked up the pen Marta had stolen and took it back, and she let him, which was her way of apologizing, and wrote beneath the Vasilievs' names:

*Marta — living. Staying.*

Then he closed the ledger, put it in his pocket next to the clock, and the two of them walked out of the station together into the washed morning, unhurried, with nowhere they were required to be and all the time in the world — borrowed time, kept time, theirs.
