## 1. The Hour That Would Not Hold

The clock had been dying for eleven days, and the whole city of Marseille knew it without knowing why.

They felt it the way one feels a missed heartbeat—a small wrongness in the morning, a tram that arrived a half-minute early, bells across the Vieux-Port disagreeing with one another like quarrelling brothers. The observatory's master clock governed them all. From its brass-and-oak heart, once each second, an electrical impulse ran down buried copper to every public dial in the arrondissement, and when it faltered, the city stumbled after it, polite and confused.

Odile Delacroix had read about the failure in *Le Petit Marseillais* over his coffee and hard bread, and by noon he had been summoned—by telegram, by the observatory's registrar, in language so curt it might have been composed by the clock itself: *Send Delacroix. Tuesday. Come alone.*

He came alone. He came alone everywhere; it was the first rule of his life, learned young and rehearsed daily, the way other men rehearsed their signatures.

He climbed the hill of Saint-Marcel in the grey light before seven, his tool case heavy against his hip, and paused where the road bent to look back at the city unspooling below him—the terracotta roofs, the masts of the harbour standing up out of the mist like the ribs of some enormous drowned animal, the sea beyond it all the colour of hammered pewter. A beautiful city, Marseille. It had never once looked at him closely enough to know him, and he loved it for that.

Above him, the observatory stood on its bald crown with the dome catching the first pale light, and beneath the dome, somewhere in the building's stone bones, the great clock ticked wrong.

* * *

The registrar who met him at the door was a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and the air of someone perpetually late for something more important than whatever he was doing. He looked at Odile's papers without reading them, which suited Odile perfectly. Papers that are read are papers that can be remembered.

"Delacroix."

"Yes."

"The clock is in the east range. You will be given the key and you will return the key. You will not enter the dome. You will not enter the library. You will not speak to the Director—he is ill—and you will not speak to anyone of the Director's family, who arrive Thursday. If you require assistance you may apply at this desk between ten and ten-thirty." He held out a heavy iron key on a loop of wire. "Do you understand?"

"Perfectly," Odile said. It was true. He understood men like this the way he understood escapements: they wanted only one thing from him, motion without noise. He was very good at giving both.

The corridor to the east range smelled of stone dust and lamp oil, with underneath it something faintly sweet and clinical—the smell, he would learn later, of a sickroom kept just out of sight. His boots rang on flagstones worn into shallow valleys by a century of feet. Through tall windows the morning came down in long dusty blades, and he walked through them one by one, feeling the day warm and cool against his shoulders like slow applause.

Then he opened the clock room, and forgot, for a while, to be careful.

* * *

It was beautiful. He had expected a machine; he found instead an argument between beauty and duty that beauty was losing.

The master clock filled the far wall of a high room lit by a south-facing window, its case of dark walnut rising nearly to the ceiling, its dial painted white as an eggshell, its hands of pierced gilt brass reaching toward numerals painted in a fine old-fashioned script. Below the dial, behind glass, the pendulum hung in its long patient swing—two metres of steel rod and gilded bob, breathing in and out, in and out, the way the sea breathes, the way the world does if the world has anything to say about it.

And yet the clock was failing. Odile could hear it from the doorway: a hesitation at the top of each minute, a fractional catch in the train, like a singer drawing breath a moment too late. The city below had been living on borrowed seconds for eleven days, and no one had thought to send for help until the newspapers began to mock.

He set his case on the trestle table provided, opened it, and let his hands do what they always did—move first, think alongside, worry later or not at all. There was a comfort in this that he trusted more than he trusted most people. Tools have no memory of your face. A movement that comes apart under your fingers tells you everything about itself and nothing about you, and asks, in exchange, only that you be honest with it. Honesty with machines was the only kind Odile had ever been able to afford.

His papers said *Odile Delacroix*, journeyman horologist, of the Rue des Repentirs. The papers were good work. He had paid well for good work, because bad work gets read twice. But the name inside them belonged to a man who had died in a lodging house in Lyon nine years ago, a man two years older than Odile and considerably less careful, and every time Odile handed those papers across a counter he performed a small silent arithmetic: how much attention, how much risk, how much forgetting could be purchased for the price of competence.

Competence bought a great deal. Competence was why the registrar hadn't read his papers. A man who can fix what others cannot is granted a strange immunity—people need his hands too badly to study his face. Odile had built a life in that blind spot, and he meant to die there, quietly, decades hence, mourned by no one and owed nothing.

That was the plan. It was a good plan. He reviewed it often, the way one checks a fuse box.

* * *

By nine o'clock he had the side panels off and the trouble half-diagnosed, and it was worse and better than the newspaper implied.

The going train was sound. The escapement—deadbeat, jewelled, a lovely piece of thinking in brass—was sound. What had failed was the contact assembly that sent the minute impulse down the line to the city dials: a spring-loaded arm whose silver contact point had oxidised, then arced, then begun to pit, so that each minute the clock reached out to touch the world and the world received only part of the greeting. Eleven days of half-handshakes. The clock itself kept perfect time, faithful as scripture; it was only its voice that had gone hoarse.

"Of course," Odile murmured to it, kneeling with a loupe screwed into his eye. "You were never sick. You were only not being heard."

There was, he reflected, cleaning the contact point with the finest emery and a drop of oil applied like medicine, a sermon in that. He declined to write it down.

The repair would take until evening: clean, reseat, adjust the spring tension, burnish the points, test through several hours of impulses, watch the current with a galvanometer borrowed—without asking, because asking invited conversation—from a shelf of instruments along the north wall. He worked in the rhythm he loved best, the long unhurried rhythm of a thing done properly, and the room filled around him with its own small weather: the tick, the swing, the dust turning gold above the window, the distant sound of the city waking into its ordinary noise.

Once, passing the window for a rag, he saw a girl in the observatory courtyard below—small, perhaps five years old, dark-haired, standing quite still in the middle of the gravel as though she had been set down there like a parcel awaiting collection. He watched her for a moment. No one collected her. A gardener passed within three metres of her and did not appear to see her at all, and Odile, who knew a great deal about not being seen, felt something cold move through him—not pity exactly, but recognition, which is colder and lasts longer.

Then she was gone behind the wing wall, and he returned to his work and told himself it was none of his affair. It was none of his affairs. None of his affairs had ever been any of his business; that was the architecture of his safety, load-bearing, tested daily. He cleaned another contact point. He adjusted the impulse spring by a quarter-turn. The clock ticked on, keeping perfect time for a city that couldn't hear it.

At half past four, with the light going amber and the repair nearly done, he heard footsteps in the corridor—slow, uneven, dragging slightly on the left side—and a knock at the open door.

An old man stood there in a dressing gown far too grand for his shrunken frame, leaning on a cane, breathing with the effort of having crossed the building. His face was grey as ash but his eyes were extraordinary—dark, burning, appalled by their own weakness. Two things happened in Odile at once: the trained reflex that said *the Director, ill, do not speak to him*, and something older than training, some animal instinct that looked at that burning gaze and understood that here was a man spending his last coins of strength on whatever he had come to say.

"You're the clockmaker," the old man said. Not a question.

"I'm repairing the clock," Odile said carefully, "sir."

"They're the same thing, usually." The old man came a step further into the room, and his eyes went past Odile to the great pendulum swinging in its case, and for a moment his ruined face went soft with something like love. "Eleven days I've listened to it hesitate. They told me I imagined it." He laughed, a dry papery sound with no humour in it. "I built half of that train with my own hands forty years ago. One doesn't imagine one's own heart stumbling."

"It's repaired, sir," Odile said. "Or nearly. By tonight the city will hear it clearly again."

"Good. Good." But the old man wasn't looking at the clock anymore. He was looking at Odile—really looking, the way almost no one ever did, straight past the competent hands and the careful face—and Odile felt the familiar cold arithmetic begin, the calculation of exits. And then the Director said, in a voice gone suddenly quiet and urgent, gripping his cane:

"How long have you been in my city, Monsieur Delacroix?"

And Odile, who had answered that question a hundred times with a lie polished smooth as a jewel, heard himself hesitate for the first time in nine years.

* * *

The old man smiled at the hesitation. It was not a cruel smile. It was the smile of a man who had spent a lifetime reading instruments and knew a falsified reading when he saw one.

"No matter," he said. "No matter. I didn't come to interrogate you. I came because—" He stopped. Something moved across his face like cloud-shadow, and he gripped the doorframe, and for a moment Odile thought he would fall, and stepped forward without deciding to. The old man waved him off with two fingers, furious at his own body. "Because tomorrow night there will be a storm. They say a storm. And there is—" His jaw worked. Whatever he had come to say had teeth, and he was afraid of being bitten. "There is something at this observatory that my family will want erased. Erased thoroughly. Professionally. They are thorough people, my family. They keep historians the way other families keep dogs."

Outside the window, the light was failing over the harbour, and the first wind of the promised storm came up the hill and pressed against the glass like a hand.

"Sir," Odile said slowly, "I'm a clockmaker. I've been engaged to repair a clock."

"Yes," the old man said. "And you've repaired it. And tomorrow night, when the storm comes, you'll find that the clock is not the only thing in this building that keeps a record." He turned himself around with visible effort, cane tapping, and paused at the threshold without looking back. "Remember what you see tomorrow night, clockmaker. Whatever they tell you afterward—remember it. Memory is the one archive they haven't learned to seal."

Then he was gone, his uneven footsteps fading down the corridor, and Odile stood alone in the lamplit room with the great clock ticking clean and true beside him, keeping perfect time, and outside, far below in the darkening courtyard, he could just make out—though he told himself he could not, though he tried very hard not to—the small still figure of a child, waiting at the observatory door as the first rain began to fall.
