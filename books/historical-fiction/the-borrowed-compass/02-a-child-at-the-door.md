## 2. A Child at the Door

The storm came up from the sea the way debts come for the poor—suddenly, and all at once.

Odile Delacroix felt it in his teeth before he heard it in the wind, a dull ache behind his jaw that he had learned, in nine years of bending over watch movements, to trust as he trusted any instrument. By four o'clock the light through the observatory's tall windows had gone the color of pewter. By five the first rain struck the dome above him like handfuls of gravel thrown by an impatient god. By six, when he should have been walking home along the Corniche with his collar up and his eyes down, the streets had turned to rivers and the tramway had stopped running, and Odile stood in the vestibule of the Marseille Observatory with his hat in his hands, listening to the concierge argue with himself about whether anyone could be spared to see him out.

"No one is spared," the concierge concluded, as though delivering a verdict that pleased him. "You'll wait. The storm will pass."

Storms did not pass in Marseille in October. They settled in like relatives, took the good chairs, and stayed until they had eaten everything in the house. But Odile said nothing. He was very good at saying nothing; it was, after the repair of mechanisms, his principal accomplishment. He set his hat on the bench beside him, folded his hands, and became the kind of man one forgets five seconds after looking away. He had practiced this so long it required no effort at all—only a certain loosening of the shoulders, a certain flattening of the gaze, a willingness to be furniture.

It was not a small thing, being furniture. Furniture was safe. Furniture got paid, got fed, got forgotten by the police, by the landlords, by the men who had once known a boy named Odile Vasseur in Avignon before that boy stopped existing. Furniture did not have papers worth checking. He had spent nine years building this invisibility plank by plank, and he guarded it the way other men guarded gold.

The master clock, at least, had approved of his work. Three days now since he had first opened its case in the tower room, three days of cleaning the crusted oil from its escapement, truing its pallets, replacing the cracked suspension spring with one he had cut and filed himself from a strip of steel he carried always in his coat pocket, because a clockmaker without spare springs was a surgeon without thread. The great regulator had begun, at last, to keep honest time. That afternoon he had compared it against the transit observations logged by the assistant astronomers, and it had agreed with the stars within a tenth of a second. A tenth of a second. Somewhere in Paris, men were deciding what time all of France should hold, and here in Marseille it would be his hands that held it local.

He was thinking about this—the small, clean pride of it—when the knocking began.

At first he took it for the storm: the loose iron band on the cistern, the shutter on the east gallery, all the observatory's old complaints made vocal by wind. But then it came again, and it was wrong. Storms do not knock. Storms have no rhythm. This had a rhythm—faint, arrhythmic, stubborn—and it was coming from below, from the great door at the foot of the entrance stair, the door that had been bolted since the assistants went home.

The concierge had gone down to the cellar for coal twenty minutes ago and not returned. Odile stood alone in the vestibule with his hat and his silence.

He told himself, quite reasonably, that whoever it was would go away. People knocked at doors; doors did not answer; people went away. It was the natural order. He had survived nine years precisely by letting doors stay shut.

But the knocking continued, and beneath it now he could hear something else—a thin sound, high and wavering, almost lost under the rain. Not a voice calling for help. Something smaller than that. The sound a person makes when they have given up expecting help but cannot quite stop making noise, the way a dying fire keeps smoking.

Odile looked at the bench, at his hat, at the dark window full of rushing water. *Go home,* he told himself. *Wait out the storm. Whatever is at that door belongs to someone else's life.*

His feet, which had never once disobeyed him in matters of self-preservation, carried him down the stairs.

* * *

The vestibule of the main entrance was a cavern of cold stone, lit by a single gas jet that guttered every time the wind found the gap under the door. Odile unshot the bolt—it weighed as much as a child, he thought absurdly, later, remembering—and pulled the door open against the pressure of the gale.

Rain drove in past him across the threshold stones and spattered the mosaic floor. For a moment he saw nothing but the night itself, black water falling out of black air, the harbor lights smeared into long red wounds across the bay. Then he looked down.

There was a bundle against the doorpost, wedged into the angle where the stone met the wall, tucked as carefully as if someone had set it there to shelter it from the worst of the weather. Someone had. There was a dry patch under the overhang exactly large enough for a small body.

The bundle moved.

Odile knelt. His knees hit the wet stone and he barely noticed. The bundle was a child—a girl, perhaps four years old, maybe five, wrapped in a wool shawl gone gray with rain and soaked through despite every care her abandoner had taken. Her hair was plastered to her skull in dark ropes. Her face was turned toward the door, toward him, and her eyes were open, and they were enormous, and they were watching him with the flat, patient attention of a creature that has passed beyond crying into some colder country.

She had been crying for a long time before that. Her face showed it. But she wasn't crying now. She was simply looking at him, and waiting to find out what he was.

"Dieu," Odile said uselessly. "Dieu tout-puissant." He reached for her and she flinched—not away from him, but tighter into herself, a contraction, an economy of motion learned young—and something in Odile's chest, some mechanism he had kept oiled and silent for nine years, jumped its escapement entirely.

"It's all right," he said. "It's all right. Come inside. Viens. Come."

She did not resist when he gathered her up. She weighed nothing. That frightened him more than anything else—the lightness of her, the way she came off the stone like something already half-ghost. Her clothes beneath the shawl were good once: a dress of dark blue wool with fine stitching at the hem, shoes that had belonged to a household with money. Whoever had dressed her that morning had expected her to come home that evening.

He got the door shut and the bolt thrown and stood in the vestibule dripping, the child against his chest, and only then did he feel the paper.

It was pinned to the shawl at her shoulder, a square of heavy cream stock, folded twice, weighted against the rain by a knot tied with more skill than panic. He worked it free with numb fingers and held it to the gaslight. The hand was fine, educated, unhurried even now:

*Forget this face.*

No name. No date. No plea, no explanation, no threat beyond the instruction itself, which was threat enough. Just those two words in ink the color of dried blood, written by someone who had sat down—sat down, deliberately, with pen and paper and a steady hand—before carrying a sleeping or drugged or simply exhausted child through a city drowning in rain, and left her at the door of the one institution in Marseille whose business was the keeping of exact records.

Odile read it three times. The words did not change. Words rarely did; it was people who changed around them.

"Forget this face," he said aloud, and the girl looked up at him, and lightning came through the fanlight above the door and lit her whole face white for one instant, and in that instant Odile saw—

He saw the portrait. The oil portrait in the director's anteroom, the one of old Director Aubertin at forty, the one every visitor walked past without seeing: the same wide-set eyes, the same improbable gravity in a young face, the same line of the jaw that no artist could have invented twice. He had studied that face for hours over three days, not for its likeness but because the sitter's pocket watch hung from the frame on a ribbon and Odile had wanted to know its maker.

The girl had Director Aubertin's face. Not resembling it. *Having* it.

She sneezed, a tiny furious convulsion, and the moment broke, and Odile discovered that he was shaking—from the cold, he told himself, from the wet and the cold—but his hands, his reliable hands, were trembling like leaves, and would not stop.

*Forget this face.* As if a face could be forgotten by decree. As if ink could reach back into a man's blood and erase it. Someone powerful had written this. Only the powerful wrote instructions instead of pleas.

And somewhere upstairs—or not upstairs; across the city, in the Hôtel des Aubertins, behind gates—somewhere there was a man dying who had this same face, and a family arranging itself around his deathbed like creditors around a coffin.

"Right," Odile said to the child, because she was still watching him, still waiting to learn what he was. His voice came out steadier than his hands. "You're frozen. We're going upstairs where it's warm, and we're going to find you something dry, and—" He stopped. He had been about to say *and then we'll sort everything else*, and he knew, with the certainty of a man reading a balance spring, that nothing else would ever be sorted. He was holding a document now. A living one, warm and sneezing, that certain people had already declared void.

He climbed the stairs with her pressed against his shoulder. At the top, he retrieved his hat from the bench, and paused, and looked back down the stairwell toward the bolted door, and understood that he was standing at a border. On one side of this night was the man he had built: Odile Delacroix, hands for hire, no history, no entanglements, safe as furniture. On the other side was whatever you became when you picked up a child out of the rain because her face was a face you refused to forget.

The girl yawned against his collar, a vast, trusting, boneless yawn, and gripped a fistful of his coat as if filing a claim.

Outside, the storm leaned on the dome, and the great clock in the tower—his clock, repaired by his hands, keeper of the city's official hour—ticked steadily on into the dark, counting seconds that someone, somewhere, was already spending to make this child never have existed.

Odile carried her up toward the warmth of the kitchens, and did not look back again.
