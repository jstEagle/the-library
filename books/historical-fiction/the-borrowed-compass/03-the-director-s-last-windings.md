## 3. The Director's Last Windings

The girl slept through the night in the clockmaker's workshop, curled on a folded blanket atop the bench where Odile usually laid out his finest tools. He had cleared them away without thinking, sweeping the jeweler's screwdrivers and the tiny files into a drawer so she would not roll against them, and only afterward understood what he had done: he had made room. In twenty-six years of living under borrowed names, he had never once made room for anyone.

He told himself it was temporary. He told himself that in the morning he would carry her down to the Hôtel-Dieu, or to the Sisters of Sainte-Marthe, and hand her over to institutions built for exactly this purpose, and return to the master clock with its failing escapement and its patient, mechanical grief. He was a repairer of mechanisms. He did not repair lives.

But in the morning she woke, looked at him with eyes the color of wet slate, and said nothing at all — which he found, absurdly, easier to bear than weeping. She ate the bread and honey he set before her with the grave deliberation of someone who had learned not to trust that there would be more. When she finished, she wiped her fingers carefully on her ruined dress and watched his hands as he worked.

No one had ever watched his hands like that. Clients watched the clock. Apprentices watched for mistakes. She watched his hands the way he himself watched them: as though they were the only honest part of a man.

"You'll want a name," he said, mostly to fill the silence. "For the sisters. They'll ask."

She considered this with the seriousness of a magistrate.

"Céleste," she said finally, pointing one small finger up through the high window toward the pale morning sky, where the observatory dome caught the light like a second sun.

Céleste. Of course. A child abandoned at an observatory door, naming herself after heaven. Odile felt something turn over in his chest, some mechanism he had not known he possessed, engaging with a soft click he could almost hear.

"That's not a name you were given," he said carefully. "That's a name you've chosen."

She shrugged, the universal gesture of the very young who have already understood more than their years allow. And then, because children are merciless, she pointed at him and raised her eyebrows.

"Delacroix," he said. "Odile Delacroix." The false name sat in his mouth like a coin of doubtful metal. He had carried it for nine years, since Lyon, since the fire, since the night he had understood that the name on his true papers could be read by men who wanted him dead or worse. *Odile Delacroix* was a coat he wore so long he sometimes forgot it was not his skin.

Céleste repeated it once, mangled it charmingly, and went back to watching his hands.

It was while he was fitting a new pallet stone into the master clock's escapement that afternoon — Céleste perched on a stool nearby, solemn as a foreman — that old Bastide came hobbling down from the director's apartments with a face like a man carrying bad news in both hands.

"The Director's awake," the caretaker said. "Properly awake, first time in a fortnight. Doctor says it's the last flare of the lamp before the oil's spent. He's asking—" Bastide paused, glancing at Céleste, and something flickered across his weathered face. "He's asking for the clockmaker. Said if the clock's being mended, he wants to speak to the man mending it."

Odile wiped his hands slowly on his apron. "I'm only the apprentice sent from Vernet's shop—"

"He said *the clockmaker*," Bastide repeated, with the emphasis of a man repeating words that had been spoken with unusual weight. "His exact phrase. Come on, then. He hasn't got long, whatever the doctor pretends."

Director Aubertin's rooms occupied the eastern wing, where the windows faced the sea and the morning light entered like a benediction. Odile had expected grandeur; instead he found austerity touched with decay. Books everywhere, stacked in leaning towers. Charts of the heavens pinned three deep to the walls. And on the great bed, propped against pillows, a ruin of a man whose eyes were still startlingly alive — dark, quick, hungry eyes set in a face gone gray and hollowed, the flesh fallen away as though the disease inside him were consuming its own house.

Odile bowed. "Monsieur le Directeur. I'm Delacroix, from Vernet's. The master clock—"

"I know why you're here." The voice was a whisper with iron in it. "I asked for you because you are the only man in this building whose profession is *truth*. Do you understand me? The astronomers measure what they hope to see. The priests measure what they fear. But a clockmaker—" a thin hand lifted from the coverlet, trembling, "—a clockmaker cannot lie. A gear either meshes or it does not. An escapement either releases or it holds. You deal in hours, monsieur, and hours do not flatter anyone."

Odile stood very still. He had been complimented before, but never quite like this — never as though his trade were a form of virtue.

"Sit," Aubertin said. "No — bring the chair to the window, where I can see your face properly. Light is everything. My family taught me that much: whatever you wish to hide, hide it in shadow."

The words landed strangely. Odile sat.

For a moment the old man simply studied him, and Odile had the uncomfortable sensation of being read like a document — every forged line, every borrowed date noted by those tireless dark eyes. Then Aubertin's gaze slid past him, to the doorway, where Bastide hovered.

"And the child?" the Director asked. "The one found at my door?"

Odile's blood went cold. "You know about her?"

"I know everything that happens under my roof, even now. Especially now." The dark eyes came back to Odile's face. "Describe her to me."

"Small. Perhaps four years old. Dark hair. Gray eyes. There was a note ordering whoever found her to forget her face."

"Forget her face." Aubertin laughed — a terrible sound, half breath, half rust. "As if a face could be forgotten by decree. As if my own family had not tried exactly that with other faces before hers." He closed his eyes. When he opened them again, they were wet, and he did not trouble to hide it. "Does she have a mark? Behind the left ear. A small birthmark, shaped like a crescent."

Odile thought of bathing the girl's grime away the previous evening, of the small warm weight of her, of the crescent-shaped shadow behind her ear that he had taken for a smudge of soot until it would not wash off.

"Yes," he said quietly.

The sound the old man made then was not a word in any language. It was the sound of a mechanism releasing after years under tension — a spring let go all at once, the whole works shuddering with it. His hands gripped the coverlet. For a long moment he simply breathed, and Odile sat motionless, understanding that he was witnessing something that had no place in any probate record: a dying man receiving absolution of a kind.

"She is mine," Aubertin said at last. "My daughter. Conceived in honesty, which is more than can be said for anything else my family has produced in a century. Her mother is dead — fever, in Toulon, in the winter. Before she died, Hélène wrote to me. Begged me to acknowledge the child. And I was a coward, monsieur. I drafted the letters. I signed them. And then my brother's son Gaspard heard rumors, and I thought of the scandal, and I thought of my name, and I put the letters away and told myself there would be time." His laugh again, that rusted hinge. "There is never time. That is the one law even astronomers cannot repeal."

Outside, gulls wheeled past the window, crying over the harbor. Somewhere below, the master clock ticked on, keeping the city's official hour whether anyone listened or not.

"They will come for her," Aubertin said. "Gaspard will come, and he will bring lawyers, and worse, he will bring historians. My family does not kill, monsieur — we are too respectable for killing. We *edit*. We remove. A person inconvenient to the Aubertin legend simply ceases to appear in it. Births unrecorded. Marriages unparsed. Whole lives struck from the ledger as cleanly as a clerk striking an error." His eyes fixed on Odile with sudden ferocious intensity. "You found the note at your feet. *Forget this face.* Now you know whose hand wrote it. Mine did not."

"What do you want of me?" Odile asked. His voice sounded distant to his own ears.

"I want you to remember." The old man pushed himself higher against the pillows, and for a moment the dying fell away and something formidable looked out — the man who had run France's greatest observatory for thirty years. "When they seal the archives — and they will seal them, within days of my death, I give it a week — when every paper bearing my signature passes into Gaspard's custody, there will be no proof left that Céleste Aubertin ever existed. Except in the memory of people like you. People no one thinks to buy or frighten, because no one sees you at all." A ghost of a smile. "Invisibility is a weapon, monsieur. I have spent my life in plain sight and envied the invisible everything."

Odile's hands had gone cold in his lap. Nine years of careful anonymity rose up in him like a tide, whispering all its old arguments: *stay small, stay silent, stay hired and unhunted.* And against that tide, the image of a small girl eating bread and honey with grave deliberation, wiping her fingers, watching his hands as though they were the only honest thing in the room.

"I am nobody," he heard himself say. "I mend clocks. Whatever I remember will weigh nothing against a family like yours."

"It will weigh everything," Aubertin said, "if you put it in the right place." He gestured feebly toward the wall, toward the whole vast building around them. "Do you know why I summoned a clockmaker from Vernet's, and not the firm that has served us for decades? Because the master clock is the one witness in Marseille that cannot be bribed. Every official hour in this city issues from it. Every ship's chronometer is rated against it. Every court, every notary, every contract bears its time." His voice dropped, urgent, rasping. "And I have hidden things inside it, monsieur. Over the years. Things my family must never find, and things they must. If my mind clears again — if God grants me another lucid hour — I will show you myself. But if He does not—"

A spasm took him then, bending him sideways, and the formidable thing fled his face and left only the ruin behind. Bastide lunged forward; the bell-pull was yanked; somewhere above, footsteps hurried. Through the commotion the old man's hand shot out and seized Odile's wrist with a strength that astonished him.

"The clock," Aubertin gasped. "What the records must show — I wound it into the works themselves. Look where the truth keeps its time. Promise me you'll look."

"I promise," Odile said — and heard, with a strange detachment, how easily the vow left him, as though some part of him had been waiting nine years for a reason to break cover.

Footsteps in the corridor now, voices, the doctor's brisk tones. Odile rose, bowed to the panting old man, and slipped out through the servants' stair as the room filled with white cuffs and concerned murmurs.

Down in the workshop, Céleste looked up from the stool where she had been arranging his spare screws into a perfect spiral.

"Well?" she demanded, in the imperious tone of four-year-olds everywhere.

Odile knelt beside the master clock's open case, its brass heart gleaming in the lamplight, and thought of the old man's grip on his wrist, of the words *look where the truth keeps its time.*

"Nothing," he said. "Yet."

But his hands were already reaching for the casing panels, and behind him, unnoticed by either of them, Bastide lingered in the doorway — listening.
