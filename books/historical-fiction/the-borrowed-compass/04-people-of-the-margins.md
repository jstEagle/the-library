## 4. People of the Margins

The cook found them first.

Odile heard her before he saw her—the particular heavy tread of Madame Roux coming down the service stair, the one that announced, to anyone who knew how to listen, that she was carrying something and did not intend to be stopped by doors. He had three days' acquaintance with that tread and had already learned to fear it in the way one fears weather.

She appeared in the workshop doorway with a covered basket and the expression of a woman who had made a decision at four o'clock in the morning and would not be unmaking it before supper.

"The child," she said, without greeting. "Where is she sleeping?"

Odile set down his file. Céleste, who had been curled on a pallet behind the clock case among the packing wool, sat up with her hair in magnificent disarray and regarded the cook with open assessment, the way she assessed everything now—as though the world were an instrument delivered for repair and she intended to find fault with it.

"She sleeps where I can hear her," Odile said carefully.

Madame Roux snorted. She crossed the room, lifted the edge of the blanket, inspected the child as one inspects a cut of meat at market, and pronounced judgment. "She is thin. She has been crying in her sleep—I heard it through the floor last night, don't look at me like that, boy, this building talks to anyone who feeds it." She set the basket down on his workbench, displacing screws with the serene authority of a force majeure. "Bread still warm. Cheese. And there will be soup at six, and she will eat it at my table, because no child of mine eats alone in a machine room."

"She isn't—" Odile began.

"I know what she isn't." Madame Roux straightened, wiped her hands on her apron, and looked at him with eyes that had seen forty years of Marseille's hungry come through kitchens. "I have worked in great houses all my life. I know what it looks like when someone decides a person never existed. It looks exactly like a basket left at a door in the rain." She turned for the stairs, then paused. "Her name?"

"We've been calling her Céleste."

Something moved across the cook's face—brief, quickly mastered. She nodded once, as if a ledger had balanced, and went up.

Céleste tugged Odile's sleeve. "Was I hers?" she asked. Children ask such questions with terrible precision. "In the story you tell me. Was I hers first?"

"You're ours now," Odile said, and heard himself say it, and could not take it back.

* * *

Éloise Vasseur came down from the dome at dawn, as she always did, when the sky surrendered its work to the daylight men who pretended she did not exist.

She had been dismissed twice already—once formally, by the director's brother-in-law, with much talk of propriety; once informally, by simple silence, the committee declining to put her name on any roster while continuing to accept her reductions of the night observations under the initials *E.V.*, which everyone agreed stood for nothing at all. She stayed because the stars did not care about committees, and because leaving meant they had won, and Éloise Vasseur lost nothing, not even arguments.

So she was in a mood already when she smelled the soup.

The kitchen at half past six held an assembly that should not have existed: Madame Roux stirring with the aggression of a woman defending territory; the child on a stool too tall for her, eating with both hands; the clockmaker standing at the edge of the room like a man waiting out a storm under an awning; and Théo Marchand, the archivist's assistant, who was not supposed to arrive until eight and who had clearly altered his route for the smell of bread.

Théo was twenty-two and deaf since scarlet fever at nine, quick-eyed, fluent in the particular sign language he and old Perrin the archivist had built between them over thirteen years—a language part standard, part invention, entirely their own. He read lips beautifully but only when he chose to, which Éloise considered the most honest form of listening she had ever encountered.

He caught her eye across the kitchen and signed, lazily: *Who is the small person?*

"A problem," Éloise said aloud, because Théo liked to practice reading her.

*You are smiling,* he signed. *Problems do not make you smile.*

Éloise looked at the child—Céleste—who had paused mid-bite to stare up at the astronomer with the frank, unblinking interest children reserve for people they have decided are interesting.

"What do you see at night?" the child demanded.

"Stars."

"Are they on time?"

Éloise blinked. Of all the questions ever put to her about her work—and she had endured decades of *isn't it cold up there* and *can you wish on them*—no one had ever asked if the stars kept time.

"They are the only things," she said, "that are always exactly on time."

The child nodded gravely, satisfied, and returned to her bread.

Éloise sat down. It occurred to her, sitting, that she had just joined something. She examined the feeling the way she'd examine an anomalous reading—with suspicion, then fascination, then a kind of fierce unwillingness to let it be explained away.

* * *

It was Théo who understood the shape of the danger first.

He had been shelving correspondence in the archive that afternoon when Gaspard Aubertin's letter arrived—not the heir apparent himself yet, but his instructions, sent ahead like cavalry scouts. Perrin read it aloud in his cracked voice out of habit, though Théo only watched his face; and Théo watched everything, always, having learned in childhood that the world gives itself away to those who watch.

The letter ordered a full inventory of the observatory's records in advance of the family historian's visit. It ordered, further, that *all materials pertaining to the Director's personal affairs be segregated for private review.* And beneath that, in a postscript whose casualness fooled no one who watched faces:

*Should anything irregular have come to the household's attention in recent weeks, it is to be reported directly and immediately.*

Perrin's hands shook slightly as he set the letter down. Théo touched his elbow. The old man looked at him—looked at the doorway, at the window, at the space where a small girl might have been seen crossing the courtyard two nights past—and Théo saw the exact moment the old man decided to have seen nothing.

But Théo also saw what Perrin could not help doing afterward: pulling certain drawers an inch closed, turning certain folders face-down, moving with the guilty economy of a man hiding something he had not yet admitted he was hiding.

That evening, Théo climbed to the workshop where the clockmaker worked by lamplight with the child asleep against his shoulder, and he told them. Not in words—he had words, he wrote well enough—but in the swift, urgent language of his hands, which the clockmaker, to his credit, followed with only occasional loss.

*They are coming,* Théo signed. *The family. A historian. They will write the house's history, which means they will decide what happened in it. And there is a question in the letter about a child.*

Odile went very still. The lamp flame bent in a draft from the dome stair.

"How do you—" he began aloud, and Théo cut him off with an impatient flick.

*I watch. You hide a child in a clock tower for three days and think no one counts footsteps? I count. It is what I am for.* His hands slowed. *I was invisible once too. Deaf boys in archives learn to be furniture. But furniture hears everything.*

He reached into his coat and produced a notebook, cheap paper, pencil-bound, and set it on the bench between them.

*If we are going to do this,* he signed, *we do it properly. Everything remembered. Everything written. Dates, times, who saw what. If they erase her, we will at least be able to prove she existed.*

Odile looked at the notebook. Then at the sleeping child, her breath fogging faintly in the cold tower air, her hand curled around a spare escapement wheel she had claimed as a treasure and refused to surrender even in sleep.

"Yes," he said. And then, because Théo deserved it said properly, he fumbled through the signs he'd been learning these three days from watching, clumsy as a child's first letters: *We. Together.*

Théo's face did something complicated and bright.

*Your accent is atrocious,* he signed. Then he pulled up a stool, opened the notebook, and wrote the date at the top of the first page in a careful archival hand.

* * *

By the end of the week, the observatory's margins had organized themselves into a garrison.

Madame Roux established the supply lines: extra bread appeared in places it had no business appearing; the child ate at her table nightly under the fiction of being "the clockmaker's niece from Toulon," a fiction so flimsy that Madame Roux defended it with the added armor of making it boring—she spoke of the niece's health, the niece's appetite, the niece's delicate constitution, until the servants' hall lost interest in the niece entirely. Boredom, the cook understood, was the best camouflage in any great house.

Théo built the record. Each evening he brought the notebook to the workshop, and each evening Odile dictated and the child contributed testimony of variable reliability ("I saw a rat wearing a hat"—*noted*, wrote Théo, deadpan, *unverified*). Between them they compiled the beginnings of an account: the note in Odile's keeping, *Forget this face*, pressed flat between pages; the date and hour of her abandonment, cross-checked against the master clock's own log; the director's lucid hour, recorded in Odile's careful words with the old man's grip still ghosting his wrist.

And Éloise—Éloise took the watches.

It was she who mapped the building's rhythms as precisely as she mapped the sky: when the day staff left, when Bastide the steward made his rounds, which corridors had sightlines and which had shadows, at what hours a small girl could cross from the workshop to the kitchen unseen. She drilled Céleste in it playfully, a game of *starlight and shadow* played on the dome stairs, the child shrieking with delight as she learned to freeze behind pillars, to move only when Éloise's lantern swung left.

"She's better at it than the new assistants," Éloise remarked one night to Odile, watching the girl crouch motionless behind the meridian circle, glowing with pride.

"She shouldn't have to be good at it," Odile said quietly. "Children shouldn't have to be good at disappearing."

"No," Éloise agreed. "They shouldn't. But the world being wrong about that is rather the whole point of us, isn't it?" She gestured—at herself, at Théo signing accounts in the corner, at Madame Roux's fortress-kitchen below, at the clockmaker with his forged papers and careful hands. "The people nobody bothered to keep records of. We know exactly how erasure works. Which means we know exactly how to fight it."

Below them, the master clock ticked through the small hours, governing Marseille's time, holding its secret in its brass ribs. Céleste emerged from behind the instrument, declared victory over the darkness, and demanded to be carried to bed—an honor contested, that night, between three adults who had been strangers eleven days ago and who now, none of them quite admitting it, arranged their lives around a four-year-old's bedtime.

It was Théo who saw the letter next morning, before anyone else—Théo, who watched, who counted, who read rooms the way other men read weather.

He came up the tower stairs two at a time and burst into the workshop without knocking, which he had never done, and thrust the note at Odile, his hands moving fast and hard:

*Gaspard Aubertin arrives tomorrow. With the historian. And he is asking the household one question first—*

Odile read the copied line in Théo's neat hand and felt the floor tilt.

*Has anyone seen the Director's daughter?*
