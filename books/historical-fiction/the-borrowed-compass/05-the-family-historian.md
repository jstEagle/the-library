## 5. The Family Historian

They came on a Tuesday, in a carriage too fine for the road up to the observatory, and Odile knew before it stopped that everything had changed.

He was in the clock room, third tier of the tower, elbow-deep in the great movement's striking train, when he heard the horses laboring on the grade. He had learned the observatory's sounds the way a blind man learns a room: the cook's bell at six, the night astronomer's boots descending the iron stair at dawn, the wind worrying the dome's shutters like a dog at a bone. This sound was new. Wealth has its own acoustics—oiled leather, patient hooves, the particular silence of men who expect the world to make way.

He wiped his hands on his apron out of habit, though no one would look at them anyway. That was the whole art of being invisible: you did not hide, you simply arranged yourself among things people already ignored. A workman at his bench was furniture. Odile had been furniture for eleven years, since the day he had walked into Marseille with a dead man's papers and a living man's fear, and he had come to find a certain peace in it.

The carriage door opened below. Two men descended. The first was broad, expensively coat-ed, with the ruddy confidence of someone who had never once been told no by anything but weather. Gaspard Aubertin. Odile recognized him from the director's fevered descriptions—the nephew, the heir apparent, the man who had not visited his dying uncle once in four months but had arrived within a day of learning the old man might amend his will.

The second man was different. Thin as a compass needle, gray at the temples, carrying a leather satchel the way a priest carries a pyx. He looked at the observatory the way other men looked at women—not with desire exactly, but with an appetite for what could be taken from it and written down.

"The historian," Odile murmured to himself, and felt the first cold thread of dread go through him.

* * *

He heard most of it from the gallery above the entrance hall, where he had gone ostensibly to inspect the regulator clock mounted there. In truth he stood where the stone balustrade threw his shadow into shadow, and he listened.

"—a comprehensive record," the historian was saying. His voice was dry, precise, the voice of a man reading aloud even when speaking. "Monument Aubertin deserves nothing less than a definitive account. I have compiled family histories for three of the great houses of Provence. Accuracy, Monsieur Aubertin, is a form of respect."

"Accuracy." Gaspard's laugh was short. "Yes. Within reason."

Odile watched the historian's face do something very small and very telling: a flicker, quickly smoothed, of a man who understood exactly what *within reason* purchased and had decided long ago what his conscience cost per page.

"I'll want access to the correspondence," the historian said. "The director's letters, his journals. And the household staff. Servants remember things."

"Servants remember wrong things." Gaspard waved a gloved hand. "My uncle's last months were confused. Fever, morphine, sentimentality. Whatever he may have said or written in that state—" he smiled, and it was a beautiful smile, the kind that had opened doors his whole life, "—the record should reflect the family as it truly is. As it will be remembered."

"As it will be remembered," the historian repeated, writing it down.

Odile's hands, resting on the cold balustrade, curled slowly into fists. Below him, Céleste was asleep in the cook Martine's room, behind a false wall of flour sacks, her breath making a small warm sound that only three people in the world knew existed. Three days ago there had been two. The circle was small because it had to be, and here came a man whose profession was enlarging records until they swallowed whatever they covered.

* * *

Gaspard found him that afternoon—or rather, Gaspard's gaze passed over him and snagged, briefly, the way a fisherman's line snags on a sunken log.

"You. Clock man."

Odile straightened from the escapement, keeping his eyes down and slightly to the left, the posture of a man with nothing behind his face. "Monsieur."

"My uncle's clock. It keeps the city's time, yes? The port bells, the customs house, all of it?"

"It does, Monsieur."

"Then it keeps records of its own, doesn't it. A log. Winding times, adjustments." Gaspard said it idly, testing the weight of the thought. "A machine that writes things down. My uncle always did love that about it."

"There is a maintenance ledger, Monsieur. Dates of service. Nothing more."

"Nothing more." Gaspard studied him a moment longer than was comfortable. Up close he had his uncle's jaw—the same jaw Céleste had, small and stubborn under her chin—and seeing it on this man's face made something turn over in Odile's chest, half grief and half fury. "You've been here how long?"

"Nine days, Monsieur. Sent by Breguet's workshop."

"And before that?"

"Other workshops, Monsieur. Marseille, Toulon." Lies worn smooth as river stones. He had told them so many times they had stopped feeling like lies.

"Hm." Gaspard turned away, then turned back, and now the idleness was gone from his voice. "If you find any papers in the mechanism—notes, letters, anything my uncle might have hidden in one of his sentimental fits—you'll bring them to me. Directly. There's a bonus in it. My uncle hid things, you understand. Old men do. And a house should be emptied properly before strangers go poking through it."

"I understand, Monsieur."

"Do you?" For a moment something moved behind Gaspard's eyes—suspicion, or its cousin, fear—and then it was gone, banked like a fire shuttered for the night. "Good man."

Odile bent back to his work and did not look up until the footsteps faded. His heart was going hard against his ribs. *Any papers in the mechanism.* Gaspard did not know yet. But he suspected. Somewhere, somehow, a thread had reached him—a servant bribed, a letter intercepted—and he had come to the observatory not merely to curate a legacy but to hunt one down.

And Odile had spent two nights with his arm inside the master clock, reading the director's letters by lamplight, touching paper that smelled of camphor and dying ink, learning that a little girl asleep behind a wall of flour sacks had a name in her father's hand: *my daughter, my Céleste, my late repentance.*

* * *

The historian worked through the evening in the library, and Odile carried lamps to him.

It was the perfect errand—invisible men carry things; no one remembers the lamp—and Odile used every minute of it. He memorized the historian's method: the way he laid documents in rows, photographed them with his mind, annotated a card index in a hand so small it was nearly a code. He memorized which drawers the man opened and which he avoided. Most of all he memorized the questions, asked casually over supper, of Martine:

"A child, was there? The director spoke of a godchild, perhaps, in the spring. Someone must have seen her."

"No child here," Martine said, stirring a pot with the serene face she showed to power. "Unless you count the astronomer's cats."

The historian smiled thinly and wrote something down anyway. That was the thing Odile learned about him that night: he wrote down the denials too. He was building a record of absence, a map of every place the child was not, and a map of absences, read carefully, points straight at what is missing.

Later, past midnight, Odile stood in the dark of the clock room with the pendulum breathing beside him and forced himself to think like the enemy.

Gaspard would search. Not today—he was performing magnanimity for the staff—but soon, thoroughly, with the historian cataloguing whatever was found. The letters in the casing were not safe. Nowhere in the building was safe. And the probate hearing loomed, weeks away at most, at which point the family's version would be finished, bound, gold-tooled, and true by sheer weight of print.

Against that: a maintenance ledger, a deaf archivist's assistant who read lips through walls, a cook with a spine of forged iron, a dismissed astronomer who still had keys, and a clockmaker with a forged name and a real memory.

It seemed a thin arsenal. Then Odile thought of the escapement log—the daily record he himself kept, in his own careful hand, of every adjustment to the master clock. The city's official time flowed from this machine. Every date the historian wrote could be checked against it. Every lie about when the director signed what, when the child was born, when the letters were written—all of it would have to pass through time itself, and time, Odile realized, his dread sharpening all at once into something harder and cleaner, time was the one thing in this house that answered to him.

He took out the ledger and, by the light of a single shaded lamp, began to write—not the truth, not yet, but the scaffolding of it: dates, windings, the precise hours of his service, each entry a brick in a wall that could not be quietly demolished, because it bore the seal of the observatory and the rhythm of the city's bells.

Below, in the courtyard, a carriage door closed. Through the window he saw Gaspard pause beneath the tower, look up at the lit clock face, and stand there a long moment, motionless, like a man doing arithmetic.

Then Gaspard turned and gave an order to the historian, and though Odile could not hear the words, he saw the man open his satchel and take out fresh paper.

In the morning, Odile decided, he would move the letters. Tonight he would sit with the ledger until dawn if he had to.

The pendulum swung. Somewhere below, a child slept behind a wall of flour, breathing in time without knowing it, and Odile Delacroix—for the first time in eleven years—wrote his own name in the margin of a page, small, where only someone looking very closely would ever find it.
