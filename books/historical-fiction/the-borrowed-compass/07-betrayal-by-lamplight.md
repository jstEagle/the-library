## 7. Betrayal by Lamplight

Sylvie Marchand had spent eleven years learning to read the sky through a keyhole. They had taken her title first—*assistant*, then nothing at all—and then her salary, and finally even the fiction of employment, so that now she climbed to the dome each night as a trespasser in her own life, admitted by a cook who left the side door unlatched and an archivist's boy who pretended not to see her pass. She had thought herself beyond surprise. She had thought that after the Academy refused her paper on variable stars because it arrived under her own signature, after Director Aubertin wept with frustration and could do nothing, after she learned to love the heavens precisely because they did not know she was a woman—she had thought no institution could humiliate her further.

Then came the carriages.

She was in the meridian room, collating a night's observations of the star Mira by lamplight, when she heard the wheels on the gravel—not the soft rattle of a delivery cart but the deliberate crunch of money arriving, four horses at least, and more than one vehicle. She pinched out her lamp on instinct, an old habit from the years when being found in the observatory after midnight had been grounds for scandal, and moved to the window.

Three carriages stood in the forecourt below, their lanterns burning yellow against the rain-slicked stones. Men were dismounting in fine wool coats, and among them, unmistakable even in bad light, walked Gaspard Aubertin—the nephew, the heir apparent—with his gloves already off, as though he considered Marseille too provincial for them. Behind him came a smaller man carrying a leather satchel: the historian, Vidal, whom Sylvie had watched for two days now moving through the archives with his pencil and his flattery.

"It's past two," she whispered to no one. "No family visits at two in the morning unless they mean to catch something sleeping."

She was down the spiral stair before the thought finished forming, skirts gathered in one fist, taking the steps two at a time in the dark the way she had done as a girl in her father's house in Toulon. The kitchen was warm and smelled of yesterday's bread. Margot the cook was already awake—of course she was; the woman slept like a cat with one ear standing—and Céleste was there, curled on the pallet behind the flour sacks where the child had slept for three weeks now, her dark hair spread across the sacking, one thumb near her mouth.

The resemblance stopped Sylvie's breath every time. The same heavy brow as the dying man upstairs. The same stubborn chin. God had been careless with his likenesses, or else merciful.

"They've come," Sylvie said. "Gaspard. In force."

Margot did not waste breath on questions. Twenty years feeding astronomers had taught her that panic wasted heat. "The letters," she said instead. "Are they still in the clock?"

"I don't know what Odile's done with them. He said the mechanism itself would keep them safe." Sylvie crouched and touched Céleste's shoulder, and the girl woke the way children wake in dangerous houses—all at once, silently, eyes wide and already listening. "Céleste. We have to move you."

"The clock room," the child said. Her voice was small and level. She had learned in three weeks not to cry, and the learning of it broke something in Sylvie every time she witnessed it. "Monsieur Odile said if ever anyone came, I should go to the clock."

"He said that?" Margot muttered. "That man thinks of everything except his own neck."

They went up through the service passage, Margot carrying a covered basket as though this were any ordinary errand, Sylvie holding Céleste's hand, the child's fingers cold and gripping hard. Above them, boots were already crossing the entrance hall. A voice—Gaspard's, smooth and carrying—said something about *thoroughness* and *my uncle's wishes*, and then, distinctly, terribly: "Begin with the servants' quarters."

They reached the clock room. Odile was not there—he had gone down before midnight to check the escapement, Sylvie remembered—but the great master clock filled the wall like a cathedral organ, its pendulum breathing its slow iron breath, and the whole room smelled of oil and metal and the particular cold of instruments. Odile's tools lay in their felt roll, ordered as always.

"In here," Sylvie said, and opened the low door in the casing where Odile had shown her the mechanism's heart weeks ago, when the four of them had still believed cleverness might be enough.

But Céleste stopped. The child turned her head toward the corridor, and Sylvie heard it a half-second later: footsteps, several pairs, unhurried. Not searching yet. Patrolling.

"There's no time to hide her properly," Margot breathed. "The flour sack trick won't survive a lamp held to it."

"Then she doesn't hide." Sylvie looked at the narrow stair at the room's far end—the workman's stair, the one that wound up through the tower's bones to the dome itself, built for men who carried oil cans to the telescope's bearings and never meant to be seen. Rain hammered the dome above them. "The dome. There's the gallery behind the telescope pier—the old storage loft. They won't look up. Nobody ever looks up."

Margot gripped her arm. "You can't take her up there in this weather—it'll be freezing, she's six years old—"

"And if she stays here, she's in a record by breakfast. Go back to your kitchen, Margot. Be asleep when they open your door. Be innocent. One of us has to be."

For a moment the cook's face did something complicated—grief and fury and the arithmetic of sacrifice—and then she pressed the basket into Céleste's arms, kissed the top of the child's head hard, and said, in a voice only slightly roughened: "There's bread and cheese under the cloth. Don't come down till someone whistles the way I do. Three notes, low ones."

Then she was gone, and Sylvie took Céleste's hand again, and they climbed.

* * *

The dome at night in a storm was a country Sylvie knew and loved and had never once been able to claim. Wind found every seam in the copper and moaned along them. Rain came through somewhere near the shutter track and fell in cold threads across the gallery floor. She settled Céleste in the corner behind the pier where decades of disused chart tubes made a windbreak, wrapped her in Sylvie's own shawl, and put the basket within reach.

"Is this where you watch stars?" the child whispered.

"When they let me. When nobody remembers I'm here."

"Odile says remembering is important."

"Odile is right about most things," Sylvie said, "except how much danger he's in." She glanced toward the stair. Down below, muffled by stone, doors were opening. Voices. The particular tone of authority performing its own innocence—*we regret the hour, we act only for the family's good*. She had heard that tone her whole life, in committee rooms and Academy halls, always just before something was taken away.

She heard them reach the clock room.

She heard Gaspard Aubertin say, pleasantly, "What's this casing doing open?" and then a silence that lasted too long, and then the sound she would hear for the rest of her life: paper, drawn from the heart of the machine. Several pages. The whisper of vellum being counted by gloved hands.

"No," she breathed. Beside her Céleste had gone rigid, understanding without being told that the papers were about her, were *her*, were the only proof in France that she had existed before tonight.

Below, Gaspard's voice rose with satisfaction. "My uncle's hand. Dated March. Well. Vidal will want to examine these. And the workman who kept them here—Delacroix, is it? Where is Delacroix?"

"Not at his bench, sir," said a stranger's voice.

"Find him. And the cook—this kitchen has been feeding an extra mouth for weeks if the laundress is to be believed. Margot—" the name pronounced like something scraped off a shoe. "Pack her things. She leaves this morning. My uncle will be told she stole silver; it saves explanations."

In the darkness of the gallery Sylvie felt fury arrive in her body like weather—a hot front moving through her chest, her throat, her hands. *It saves explanations.* That was all it ever was, she thought. Power did not need to burn books; it needed only to reassign blame, adjust a date, dismiss a cook, rename a daughter into a rumor, and the record closed over the truth like water over a dropped stone. They had done it to her with a signature. They were doing it to a child with a satchel.

Céleste's hand found hers in the dark. Small fingers. Steady.

"Will they take Monsieur Odile?"

"I don't know."

"Will they take me?"

Sylvie knelt so their faces were level, rain dripping somewhere behind her, the great telescope looming over them both like a patient giant. "Listen to me, little one. Those men downstairs can seize papers and ruin cooks and rewrite ledgers until doomsday. But they cannot rewrite the sky. Your father showed me that sky once, when he still had strength to climb these stairs, and he pointed at the stars and told me they were indifferent, and I told him he was wrong—they're not indifferent, they're *faithful*. They return. Every single night, they return." She tucked the shawl closer. "So do we. Whatever happens tonight—we return. Do you understand me?"

The child nodded slowly. Then, in the smallest voice: "I heard Grand-père say that once. About the clock. He said clocks are promises."

Down below, the search went on. Sylvie heard them turn over Odile's tool roll, heard a drawer break, heard Vidal's dry voice reading aloud a date from one of the seized letters while another voice wrote it down—transcribing, absorbing, digesting the evidence into the family's own account where it would be digested into nothing. She heard Margot's door open and Margot's voice, calm as Sunday, professing ignorance of everything, and she heard the lie fail.

And then—footsteps on the workman's stair. Coming up.

Sylvie pulled Céleste deeper behind the chart tubes and pressed one finger to the child's lips. Lamplight wobbled up the stairwell. Two men, by the sound—one heavy, one quick. The heavy one swore at the narrowness of the steps.

"Dome's locked from inside anyway in this weather," the quick one said. "Nothing up there but the big scope. Vidal says check it anyway. He says the astronomer woman's been seen here nights."

Something went cold down Sylvie's spine. *Seen.* So they knew about her. Of course they did; Gaspard had been asking about missing children for two days, and a woman who officially did not exist climbing into the observatory after dark was exactly the kind of loose thread such men pulled first.

The lock rattled below. The heavy man cursed the mechanism. And Céleste, practical as her father, tugged Sylvie's sleeve and pointed—not at the stair, but at the maintenance ladder bolted to the dome's inner curve, the one that ran up to the shutter platform where the observatory's oldest secret waited: the outside gallery, the ledge that ringed the cupola beneath the eaves, built for cleaning crews and abandoned for thirty years. Open to the storm. Open to a drop of sixty feet onto wet flagstones.

"She can't," Sylvie whispered—to herself as much as to the child. But Céleste was already looking at the ladder with the flat, unchildlike assessment of someone who had been abandoned once before and had decided it would not happen twice.

Below, the lock gave with a crack of old iron.

Sylvie made her choice in the space between heartbeats. She swung Céleste onto her back, tied her shawl around them both in a rough harness the way she'd carried flour sacks down from the market carts of her girlhood, and took the ladder as the lamplight spilled into the dome behind them.

Wind hit them at the top like a wall. Rain drove sideways across the ledge. Sixty feet of black air breathed beneath the narrow footing, and somewhere below, faint through the storm, she could hear the observatory's bell beginning to count the small hours—four strokes, five—and beneath that, rising through stone and rain, the sound of Margot's voice breaking at last, and of men giving orders.

Sylvie pressed herself and the child flat against the cold copper of the dome and began, rung by rung, to move.

They had taken the letters. They had cast out Margot. They would come for Odile by morning, and seal the archives within three days, and by week's end there would be no child anywhere in any record of the Aubertin family at all.

Unless someone stood up and made the record answer for itself.

*Hold on,* she told the small weight on her back, feeling Céleste's arms tighten around her neck, feeling the child breathe against the storm. *Hold on, little star. We know people the powerful never bother to learn—the invisible ones, the dismissed ones, the ones who keep the time. And time, my darling, keeps everything.*
