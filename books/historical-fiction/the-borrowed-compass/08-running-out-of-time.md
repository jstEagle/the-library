## 8. Running Out of Time

The cook's kitchen smelled of scorched milk and rain. Odile sat at the scarred table with the clock's escapement log spread before him, and for a long moment he could not make his hands move.

Three days. The words had a weight like wet stone. In three days the Aubertin family historian would seal his record—the dates already inked, the omissions already elegant—and whatever truth remained would live only in the memories of people no court believed: a dismissed woman astronomer, a deaf archivist's boy, a cook without references, and a clockmaker whose own name was a forgery.

He turned the log toward the lamp. His own handwriting marched down the columns in its careful, unremarkable script—*escapement adjusted, third bell, error two seconds slow; remontoire cleaned; pendulum re-rated against sidereal transit.* He had kept the log because keeping logs was what careful men did, because a record that existed could not be accused of lying.

That was when the thought arrived—not gradually, but all at once, the way a mainspring lets go.

"The historian's record," he said aloud. "It has dates in it."

Madame Vasseur, the night astronomer, looked up from her charts. Her eyes were red-rimmed; she had not slept since the raid, since Gaspard Aubertin's men had carried off the director's letters in a leather case as casually as men carry away a hat. "Dates," she repeated flatly. "Records generally have them."

"But which dates?" Odile leaned forward. "A family history is full of them—births, deaths, commissions, donations. And every date that touches this observatory touches *my* clock. The master clock governs official time for Marseille. Every transit logged here, every observation certified by the director, every donation witnessed at a stated hour—it was all measured against one mechanism. A mechanism I have spent three weeks inside. I know its errors to the tenth of a second."

Silence. Then Madame Vasseur pulled out a chair so fast it shrieked against the tile.

"You're saying the record can be *checked*."

"I'm saying it can be checked against something no one thought to seize." He tapped the log. "Gaspard took the letters. He didn't take time itself."

* * *

They worked through the night, and the work had the particular fever of desperation dressed up as method.

Odile sent Théo—the deaf archivist's assistant, who read lips across a room and moved through corridors unseen—to fetch every published almanac, gazetteer, and newspaper he could carry without being noticed. Meanwhile Madame Vasseur wrote out, from memory, everything she knew of the observatory's public chronology: the dates of the meridian observations printed in the *Sémaphore de Marseille*, the ceremony when the new transit instrument arrived, the evening the director had demonstrated the clock to the Chamber of Commerce.

"Here." She stabbed the page. "March 1870. The director received the delegation at nine in the evening, after the transit of the moon. It was in the papers. If the historian's record places that visit in the afternoon—"

"Then it contradicts the very clock the delegation came to see." Odile almost smiled. "And I can prove what the clock read. Not just that night. Every night. Because an escapement doesn't drift randomly—it drifts according to temperature, wear, oil. I've reconstructed its entire curve since January. If anyone claims the director signed a document at an hour when the clock demonstrably stood still for repairs—if they claim a letter was written on a day the observatory was closed for the equinox adjustment—"

"The record hangs itself," Madame Vasseur finished. "With its own dates."

But there was a flaw, and Odile saw it before dawn did. He set down his pencil.

"It isn't enough to show their dates are wrong. Wrong dates happen. Clerks err. We need to show they were wrong *deliberately*—that the historian changed dates to bury Céleste. Which means we need to know what the original documents said." He looked at the empty chair where the letters had been, before Gaspard's men took them. "We need the archive."

Théo, who had been mending the fire, went still. He turned and signed rapidly, and Madame Vasseur translated, her voice low: "He says the probate clerk's office keeps duplicate filings. Anything the director registered—a deed of gift, a baptismal dispensation, anything—would have a counterpart filed within the week. The historian can burn the observatory copies. He cannot reach the city's."

"He'd need someone on the inside," Odile said.

Théo's mouth curved. He signed again: *I worked there for two years. They never once remembered I was in the room.*

* * *

The second day was for the clock itself.

If Odile was going to stand before a magistrate—or worse, before Gaspard Aubertin's lawyers—and swear to the machine's accuracy, the machine had to be beyond reproach. He climbed into the great casing at first light and did not come down until dark, except once, when small feet appeared on the ladder and Céleste's face rose through the hatch like a moon.

"Is it sick?" she asked. She had been kept below since the raid, pale and quiet, playing with the cook's wooden spoons. Since the cook's dismissal she barely spoke above a whisper.

"Not sick," Odile said. "Being made honest."

"What's honest?"

He considered the question with more seriousness than he had ever given any technical problem. "Honest is when the hands say what the wheels have done. No more, no less." He held out his hand, and she took it, and together they watched the pallets catch and release, catch and release, the heartbeat he had been listening to all his life without knowing it was teaching him something.

"My papa wound it," she said suddenly. "I remember. He let me hold the key. It was heavy and warm."

Odile's throat tightened. The director had told him the same thing, in his last lucid hour—that the child had stood on a stool beside him every Sunday, that he had written her name in the margin of his private almanac where no historian would think to look. That almanac too was gone now, seized with the rest.

"Then you'll hold the key again someday," Odile said. "When it's yours to wind."

She looked at him with the director's grey eyes, and nodded once, gravely, as if accepting a contract.

Below, Madame Vasseur called softly that Théo had returned. Odile lifted the girl down the ladder himself, feeling her weight—so little, so absurdly little to hang a rebellion on—and followed.

* * *

Théo laid his findings on the kitchen table like a man laying down cards he had stolen rather than been dealt. Three documents, copied in his beautiful, invisible hand.

"A deed of guardianship, filed June 1869, naming the child Marie-Céleste Aubertin, born February of that year. A letter of provision, filed October 1870, assigning an annuity. And—" he paused, and even his steady fingers trembled slightly as he signed, "—a notice of intended legitimation, filed last spring. The director was preparing to acknowledge her publicly. He died before he could."

Madame Vasseur read the copies twice. "Now compare." She spread the historian's draft pages beside them—pages Odile had memorized during those long, dreadful days of playing the invisible workman while Gaspard's hired pen scratched out the family's glory. "In the historian's account, the annuity appears dated March 1871. After the director lost his reason. They've moved it forward so it looks like a deathbed charity, not a father's provision. And the guardianship—they've dated it November 1869, five months late, and attributed it to the *family*, not the director. As if the child were a ward of charity, not blood."

"And the legitimation notice," Odile said quietly, "is simply absent."

"They couldn't explain why a man who meant to disown her had filed papers to name her heir." Madame Vasseur laughed, but there was no warmth in it. "So they erased the paper instead of answering it."

Odile drew the escapement log toward him and began, line by line, to build the bridge between the two records. Here: the clock's certified reading on the true filing date, corroborated by the probate office's own timestamp. There: the historian's claimed date, on which the master clock had stood stopped for eleven hours—eleven hours Odile himself had spent elbow-deep in its gears, recorded in his own hand, witnessed by the night astronomer's observing book. A document allegedly signed at the observatory on a day the observatory's heart did not beat.

"No honest clerk could have made these errors," he said at last. "Every mistake moves in the same direction. Away from the child. Toward the family."

"Can you prove the log wasn't forged?" Madame Vasseur asked. "They'll say you wrote it yesterday."

It was the question he had been dreading, and he had an answer, and the answer frightened him more than the question. "Not with my word. My word is worth nothing—I'm not who my papers say I am." He met her eyes. "But the clock's rate curve matches the weather. Temperature, barometric pressure, the mistral days—I can show its errors tracking the season like a tide. You don't forge a tide. And the observing books—the transits you logged, independent of me, in your hand, countersigned by the director—those fix the clock's readings to the sky itself. The stars don't perjure themselves."

She was silent a moment. "You understand what you're describing. To present this, you'll have to testify. Under oath. Under your real name."

The kitchen was very quiet. Rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere above them the master clock struck the hour, unhurried, indifferent, true.

"I've spent six years being no one," Odile said. "It kept me safe and it kept me nothing. They took the letters, they took the almanac, they took the cook's livelihood—for a child who never harmed anyone. If the price of giving her back her name is paying with mine—" He found, to his surprise, that his hands were steady. "Then it's the cheapest thing I'll ever buy."

Madame Vasseur reached across the table and gripped his wrist, brief and hard, the way sailors grip.

"Then we have one day left," she said, "and one problem. The hearing is at ten tomorrow morning. The probate clerk will want the originals, not our copies—and the originals sit in Gaspard's lawyer's strongbox until the seal goes on at noon." Her eyes went to Théo, then to Odile, and something grim and bright kindled in her face. "Which means somewhere between nine and noon, someone is going to have to open that strongbox in front of God and the magistrate. And the only person in Marseille with a legitimate reason to demand it opened—"

"—is the person claiming to be the heir," Odile finished slowly.

They both looked toward the stairs, where a small girl sat in the lamplight, practicing holding an imaginary key, turning it in the air with great solemnity, winding some invisible clock toward morning.
