## 1. The Weight of Sealed Things

The letter was warm from Mr. Halloran's hands.

Casper noticed that first — the way the envelope seemed to hold the heat of the man who had sealed it, as though the words inside were still cooling, still setting like poured wax. He took it with both hands, the way one takes a live coal, and felt the weight of it. Not much. A single sheet, folded twice. But weight in the way a stone dropped into deep water has weight: you feel it pulling long after it leaves your fingers.

"Magistrate Vasseur's office, and nowhere else." Halloran's voice had the flatness of a man reciting scripture he did not believe but had memorized all the same. "You do not stop. You do not speak to anyone along the way. You hand it to the magistrate himself, or his clerk if the magistrate is occupied, and you bring back nothing."

"Yes, sir."

"And Winterbourne—" Halloran paused at the ledger desk, his ink-stained thumb resting on a column of figures as if the numbers might bolt. He was not an unkind man, exactly. He was a careful man, which in the Meridian Trading Company amounted to nearly the same thing. "You are three years indentured. Two remain. A boy who delivers where he is told and asks nothing finishes his term with a reference worth having. Do you understand me?"

"I understand, sir."

Halloran nodded once and turned back to his columns, and Casper went out into the gray morning with the letter pressed against his ribs like a second heartbeat.

The harbor was already awake. Gulls wheeled screaming over the fish market; the ferry coughed its way across the channel trailing smoke; somewhere beyond the breakwater, a ship's bell counted out the hour. Casper walked through it all the way he had learned to walk these past three years — head down, shoulders narrow, present and invisible at once. It was a skill he had perfected young, first as a diver's son on the wrong side of the quay, then as a debtor's body in company livery, and it had kept him whole when wholeness was not guaranteed.

But the letter would not stay quiet against his chest.

It was the seal that undid him. He had carried dozens of envelopes for the company — invoices, contracts, threats dressed in fine paper — and he had never once been tempted. Sealed things were sealed for reasons, and a boy two years from freedom could not afford reasons. But this envelope had been closed carelessly, hurriedly, the wax applied thin and cracked along one edge where Halloran's thumbnail had caught it. And when Casper shifted it beneath his coat to shield it from the drizzle, the crack widened by a hair's breadth, and through that hair's breadth he saw a single line of ink.

*Re: the property of A. Okonkwo and household—*

He stopped walking.

The world went on around him — cart wheels, gull cries, the slap of dirty water against pilings — but Casper stood fixed on the cobbles of Customs Row with the name ringing in his skull like a struck bell.

*Okonkwo.*

Mrs. Okonkwo, who had set a bowl of pepper soup before him eleven winters ago without asking his name, because a hungry boy at her table needed feeding before he needed naming. Mr. Okonkwo, who mended nets on their porch and taught Casper to read the water the way other men read scripture. Adaeze, who was six years old when he first came to their door and who used to fall asleep against his shoulder during the long sermons of her father's stories. They had fed him through the winter his father drowned and his mother's lungs gave out, fed him when the Meridian men came to collect his father's debt and found instead a fourteen-year-old they could bind to the ledgers instead of the grave.

They had given him belonging when belonging was the one thing the world refused to sell him.

And now the company wanted them named in a magistrate's letter.

*Property.* As though a family were a thing to be seized.

Casper walked on. What else could he do? His legs carried him down Customs Row because legs obey training even when the rest of a man has come apart, and he delivered the letter to the magistrate's clerk, a dry-lipped man who signed a receipt without looking up, and Casper walked back to the company offices feeling the whole time as though he were carrying something home from his own execution.

* * *

That night he could not sleep.

His room above the cooperage was cold enough to see his breath, and the candle stub he allowed himself — carefully, frugally, because candles cost money and money was debt — sat on the table untouched while he lay staring at the rafters, listening to the wind worry the harbor.

Three years of silence. That was the bargain he had made with himself the day they inked his name into the indenture book: keep your head down, keep your mouth shut, count your days like coins, and walk free at twenty. Silence had kept him fed. Silence had kept him unwhipped, unbeaten, unmarked except in the ways no constable could see. He had told himself silence was survival, and survival was not cowardice.

But silence, he understood now, lying in the dark, was also a kind of agreement. Every day he said nothing, he agreed. He agreed to whatever crossed those desks in sealed envelopes. He agreed to whatever names appeared in whatever lines of ink. He had agreed for three years, and tonight the agreement had broken against a single word, and he could no more un-break it than he could un-drown his father.

Before dawn, he lit the candle.

* * *

The copying took four nights.

Not the letter itself — that was gone, delivered, beyond reach — but what lay behind it. On the third evening after the delivery, Halloran sent him to the records room to fetch the quarterly manifests, and Casper saw the file left out on the reading table: a folder of foolscap bound in red tape, *Vasseur / Okonkwo* written across the tab in Halloran's cramped hand. Manifests. Ledger extracts. A deposition, unsigned. Pages that together built a story: cargo moving through Meridian channels that belonged to no manifest, duties never paid, and the Okonkwo family's small chandlery standing accused as the front for all of it.

It was nonsense. Casper knew it the way he knew tide tables — in his bones, from years of watching. The Okonkwos moved rope and lamp oil and salt fish to harbor folk at prices harbor folk could pay, and if they sometimes looked away from a crate or asked no questions of a friendly skipper, it was kindness, not commerce. They were being made into a doorway so that someone else's smuggling could have somewhere to stand.

Someone at Meridian was doing the smuggling. Someone whose name would be in these pages, or just behind them.

He should have closed the folder. He stood there with his heart going like a drum and his hand actually on the cover, feeling the pull of two years of careful silence, and then he thought of Mrs. Okonkwo's fifth bowl, always set without being asked, always waiting, and his hand did not close the folder. His hand opened it.

That night he began to copy.

By candlelight, in his cold room, with a stolen pen nib and sheets of cheap paper bought with coins meant for bread, Casper Winterbourne committed his first crime. His handwriting shook at first — the letters of a boy who'd learned his letters late, from Mr. Okonkwo's patient finger under a lamplight — and every sound from the street below made him blow out the flame and sit in blackness listening until his ears rang. A constable's boots. A drunk singing. The cooperage dog worrying something in the yard.

But the terror, he found, changed shape as the nights went on. It did not shrink. It settled. It became something he worked alongside, the way divers work alongside fear of the deep — acknowledging it, respecting it, refusing to let it steer. Each page he copied was a page that could hang him. Each page was also a page that might save them. He could not seem to weigh those two facts in any scale that favored his own skin.

On the fourth night, he finished the last of the deposition and sat back, rubbing eyes that burned like coals. Eleven pages of borrowed truth lay before him, and beside them, the greater problem: the originals themselves. Copies could be dismissed as forgery, as the spite of a debtor boy with a grudge. Only the real pages, with Meridian's own watermarks and Halloran's own initials in the margins, would carry weight enough to stop what was coming.

And what was coming was coming soon. The deposition bore a date. Before New Year's Eve, the warrant would be signed, the case sealed forever, and the Okonkwo family would be taken apart the way the company took everything apart — legally, thoroughly, without anyone raising their voice.

Fourteen days.

Casper gathered the pages, folded them flat, and slid them inside the lining of his coat, where they lay against his heart like a second, heavier seal. Then he blew out the candle and let the dark take the room, and in the dark he made the only prayer he knew anymore, the one Mr. Okonkwo had taught him over net-mending and lamplight: *the sea keeps what is true; let me be true enough to keep.*

Tomorrow he would need help crossing the customs house. There was only one person he trusted enough to ask — Tomas Reyes, three years at the clerks' desks, who owed Casper a debt of his own and knew the checkpoint men by their first names.

He only hoped trust would be enough.
