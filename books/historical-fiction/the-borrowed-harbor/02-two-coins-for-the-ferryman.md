## 2. Two Coins for the Ferryman

The evidence file lived in a locked drawer in Merrow's office, and the lock was older than Casper's indenture.

He had learned that in three years of carrying keys he was never meant to use, of standing in doorways with ledgers under his arm while clerks muttered combinations over his head as though a diver's son were deaf rather than merely lowborn. The lock on the third drawer of the company secretary's desk was a Corbin & Hale, brass-bellied, its mechanism worn smooth by forty years of idle thumbs. Casper had watched Mr. Merrow open it a hundred times without ever seeming to touch it — the way old men do, by feel, by habit, by the arrogance of never imagining that anyone else might be learning the same lesson from the shadows of the room.

On the fourth night after copying the letter, Casper opened it with a fishhook bent to the shape of the wards and a patience learned from his father, who had once waited out an oyster for two hours because the pearl inside would not forgive haste.

The drawer held more than the letter. That was the first thing that frightened him — properly frightened him, down where the cold water lives. The letter naming the Okonkwos was only the surface catch. Beneath it lay the whole net: manifests with dates altered in a hand Casper recognized as Merrow's own, bills of lading stamped with the customs house seal but signed by men whose names did not appear on any crew list, and a page of correspondence between Meridian Trading and an office in the capital that used words like *arrangements* and *continuance* the way smugglers use *rum* and *tobacco*. And at the bottom, folded twice, a draft warrant already made out in the magistrate's name — already made out, though no hearing had been called, no defense heard — condemning Adaeze Okonkwo and her sons to transportation for conspiracy.

They had decided before they had accused. The paperwork was not evidence of a crime; it was the crime, wearing evidence's clothes.

Casper stood in the dark of the records room with the file against his chest and felt the building breathe around him — the tick of the case clock, the settle of timber, somewhere below the lap of harbor water against the pilings on which all of Meridian's respectability was built. He thought of Mrs. Okonkwo's kitchen, the pepper soup she had set before him that first winter without asking how he'd come to be starving, only pushing the bowl closer when his hands shook. He had been eleven. She had fed him every night for four months and asked nothing back except that he learn her sons' names so he could call them properly.

If he put the file back, closed the drawer, walked home, and kept his silence, the silence would hold. It had always held before. Three years of holding it, and his debt stayed manageable, his body stayed unmarked, his father's grave stayed quiet.

But silence, he understood now, standing there with another family's ruin in his arms, was not safety. Silence was just the sound a drowning man makes from far away.

He took everything.

* * *

Tomas Reyes found him at dawn on the coal stairs behind the counting house, which was where Tomas found everyone, because Tomas had a gift for being exactly where a thing was about to happen.

"You look," Tomas said, taking the last steps up and settling beside him, "like a man who hasn't slept."

"I haven't."

"Because of the letter." Not a question. Of course not a question; Tomas missed nothing that moved through this building, and he had watched Casper carry the sealed packet to Vasseur House and come back with his face rearranged into something worse than grief. "You read it."

"The seal was loose."

"The seal was never loose, Casper. You broke it." There was no accusation in it. That was the terrible comfort of Tomas — he never wasted breath pretending. He lit a cigarette against the wind coming off the water and offered one, and Casper took it, though he didn't smoke, because refusing would have ended the conversation and he needed it to go on.

"There's more than the letter," Casper said.

"I know."

"You know?"

"I file the copies, remember? I've known something was wrong since August, when the *Sable Ann* docked light and left heavy and nobody wrote down why." Tomas exhaled toward the gray water. "I didn't know it had the Okonkwos' names in it. That part I'm sorry for."

"They'll hang them. Or send them to the cane fields, which is slower and counts as mercy here." Casper's voice came out flat, which surprised him; underneath it was everything else, roaring. "There's a warrant drafted already. There are manifests. If this goes through on the thirty-first, it's done forever — sealed, archived, buried. After New Year's no one can reopen it without the magistrate himself."

"So you're going to steal it." Tomas looked at him then, fully, and Casper saw his friend's eyes move to the satchel at his feet — the oilcloth bundle inside it, the shape of it unmistakable now that it was named. "And you're telling me because—"

"Because I can't cross the customs house alone." The words came out of him like blood from a reopened cut. "First checkpoint I can manage — the pier gate knows my face, knows I run errands. But the second is the customs house itself. They search clerks. They don't search couriers traveling under a delivery chit for Vasseur House, but I can't get a chit — only a company man with standing can sign me through, and if I ask anyone senior, I'm dead by noon. You have standing. You have the Reyes name and your uncle's commission and—" He stopped. He had planned the speech and now found it obscene, all of it, the whole architecture of what he was asking. "I'm asking you to help me betray the company that owns us both. And I know what that costs. Whatever it costs, I'll carry my half."

The wind pushed smoke sideways between them. Down on the quay, gulls argued over fish guts, and a steamer moaned its way out past the breakwater, and the world went on being ordinary while Casper's heart stood still.

"You understand," Tomas said slowly, "that if we're caught, they won't void your contract. They'll transfer it. There's a plantation upcoast that buys debts like ours."

"I know."

"And the Okonkwos — you understand they may not even thank you. People in danger often resent the rope thrown to them. It splashes."

"I know that too."

Tomas smiled then — the easy, warm smile that had gotten Casper through his first year of ledgers, that had shared stolen oranges and forged excuses and every small treason of friendship. "Then yes," he said. "Of course, yes. My uncle keeps a signing stamp in his coat. I'll take the chit from his desk tonight. Meet me tomorrow at the Ferryman's Rest at six, before the shift changes, and we walk through together like two bored clerks delivering nothing worth a glance."

It was too easy. Some drowned instinct in Casper noted that even as gratitude drowned it out — noted how quickly Tomas had agreed, how completely, how he had not asked what was in the file beyond what Casper told him. But Tomas had always agreed quickly. That was his nature. Quick to laugh, quick to help, quick to stand too close to trouble because trouble was where the interesting people were.

"Two coins for the ferryman," Casper said, because his father used to say it, because some old country superstition wanted saying before a crossing. "One to get across. One to get back."

Tomas laughed and clapped his shoulder. "You'll need both hands free, brother. I'll carry the satchel at the crossing — less to explain if they pat you down."

* * *

The Ferryman's Rest at six in the morning smelled of tallow and spilled gin, its windows fogged with the breath of dockmen warming themselves before the whistle. Casper arrived early and waited by the door, watching the street, watching the fog lift off the harbor in slow rags.

Tomas came at half past, coat buttoned to the throat, chit in hand, and he was not alone in his manner but he was alone in fact, and Casper let himself believe it. They drank one coffee each, standing. Tomas talked about his uncle's stamp, about the new sergeant at the customs gate who patted down everyone regardless of paper, about the best hour to cross. His voice was steady. His hands were steady. Only his eyes, once, flicked to the door and held there a half-second longer than the room deserved — and Casper, watching him, chose not to see it, because seeing it would have meant having nowhere left to turn.

"It's time," Tomas said.

They stepped out into the blue-gray morning. At the corner of Rope Walk, where the lamplighter was snuffing the last of the night's flames, Casper stopped and untied the satchel and held it out — the whole weight of it, the manifests and the drafts and the truth of nine months of theft, passing from his hands to his friend's.

"Hold it until we're through the second checkpoint," Casper said. "Then it goes to the magistrate's own hands, or to Elena Vasseur if her father won't hear it. No one else."

"No one else," Tomas agreed, and took it.

And then he did not turn toward the customs house. He turned up Harbor Street, toward Vasseur Hill, toward the magistrate's door, walking fast and straight as a man with an appointment, and the satchel swung at his side like a hanged man's lantern, and Casper stood in the middle of the street with empty hands and two coins in his pocket and understood, with the slow sick certainty of deep water closing overhead, that he had just delivered the evidence to the enemy.

"TOMAS!"

His friend paused. Half-turned. For one heartbeat their eyes met across twenty feet of cobblestone and morning fog, and whatever was in Casper's face made Tomas flinch — actually flinch — before he pulled his collar up and walked on, quicker now, not running, running would be proof of something, but walking like a man who had never been caught at anything in his life.

Casper ran the other way, toward the water, toward the third checkpoint, toward the only person in this city who might still be reachable before the thirty-first — and above the rooftops the fog tore open, and the first hard rain of the storm came down like a verdict.
