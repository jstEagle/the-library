## 1. The Weight of Sealed Things

There is a particular way to carry a sealed dispatch, and I had learned it the way one learns anything worth knowing: badly at first, then with the weary competence of a woman who has done a foolish thing so many times it has become a profession.

You do not clutch it. Clutching is a confession. You do not forget it, either, because forgetting is a career with a very short arc and an abrupt ending. You carry it the way you carry your own name—constantly, invisibly, with the assumption that no one will ever ask you to produce it. The dispatch rode in the false bottom of my document case, beneath a sheaf of perfectly genuine shipping manifests for Vessarine grain that would never be shipped to anyone, because the grain had been requisitioned by an army with better handwriting than manners. My diplomatic papers rode in my coat, against my ribs, where I could feel them warm and absurd, like a second heartbeat with better manners than my own.

The border crossing at Serev Ford was a tollbooth that had been promoted beyond its abilities. Two wooden barriers, a guardhouse with a stovepipe leaning like a drunk against a lamppost, and a flag—the occupation's flag, the grey double-tower on a field the color of old dishwater—hanging from a pole that had clearly once flown something else. You could still see the ghost of the Vessarine colors bleeding through the grey cloth where the dye had thinned. Someone had thought that was a kindness. It was not. It was the kind of thing you notice and then spend the rest of the day trying not to notice.

"Purpose of travel," said the guard, without looking up. He had the face of a man who had been told he was a border guard and had decided to be nothing else for the rest of his life.

"Cultural liaison," I said, and offered the papers with the flat wrist of a woman handing a clerk a laundry list. "Correspondence between the Meridian Institute and the University at Vessa. Antiquities. Very dull."

Dullness, I have found, is the most reliable disguise ever devised. Guards do not search for dullness. Dullness is the one cargo that passes every border in the world unexamined.

He stamped. I took the papers back. I walked across the ford, and the Republic of Vessar closed over my head like water.

* * *

Let me tell you my creed, since you will need it to understand everything I did afterward and everything I failed to do. It is a simple creed, and I held it for forty-one years with the devotion of the truly comfortable: *history happens to other people.*

I believed it the way farmers believe in weather—acknowledging its existence, respecting its power, and constructing my life entirely on the assumption that it would not, this year, rain on me. Wars were things I read about in dispatches I myself carried, which is a joke I only appreciated later. Famines were statistics with good posture. Massacres were rumors, and rumors, in my experience, were ninety percent inflation and ten percent weather. I was a courier, not a witness. I moved sealed things through unsealed places and told myself that the sealing was a kind of mercy: what I didn't read, I couldn't be haunted by. Neutrality was not a policy for me. It was a temperament. It was my native language.

The Meridian Institute paid me for this, and paid me well, because the Institute understood something the governments it served did not: that a woman with no flag is worth ten with one, and that indifference, properly tailored, opens more doors than any uniform ever has. I had crossed eleven borders in six years. I had been waved through, searched once (they found my sewing kit and apologized), and offered tea twice. History, in my professional experience, was a thing that happened behind the doors I was politely not looking at.

The road from Serev Ford to the town of Kelvane ran along the river for nine miles and then climbed through orchards gone wild with neglect. My briefing—composed in a warm office in Meridian City by men who believed their own maps—had promised me traffic. Grain carts, troop rotations, the ordinary arterial business of an occupied province. What the briefing had not promised me was the emptiness.

I passed, in the first hour, one ox cart driven by a boy of perhaps twelve, who looked at my traveling coat the way people look at weather they don't trust. I passed a shrine at the second milestone with its niche smashed in and the saint's hands broken off—the hands always go first; I have never learned why. I passed a burned farmhouse that had been burned thoroughly and long ago, the char already softened by rain, and no one working the fields around it, though the fields were the kind that make a farmer's hands itch just to look at them.

By the third hour I had counted four living persons and eleven crows, and the crows were winning on points.

This is the moment I should describe honestly, since honesty is the one luxury I could not afford at the time and can only afford now, in retrospect, where it costs nothing. The emptiness made me uneasy. Not afraid—afraid would have required imagination, and my imagination, like the rest of me, was on salary to the Institute and kept strictly to its duties. But uneasy, the way you are uneasy when a room you know is missing its furniture. The briefing said traffic. The road said otherwise. And a courier who trusts her briefing over her own eyes is not a courier; she is a parcel.

I recalibrated, as I had been trained to do. Roads empty in winter, I told myself. Harvest's long gone, planting's months off, and no one drives an ox cart for the pleasure of the company. The burned farmhouse was old news. The broken saint was politics, and politics, thank the nine dull gods, was not my department.

I was very good at telling myself things. It was, in its way, my finest professional skill.

* * *

Kelvane announced itself before I saw it, by smell—woodsmoke and wet stone and, underneath, the river's particular mineral cold. Then by sound: a garrison drum somewhere, beating the change of watch with the mechanical cheerlessness of all military music. Then by sight: the town folded into its river bend, slate roofs, a bridge, and on the bridge—this I noted with the mild interest of a tourist—scaffolding. Repairs. The bridge had been damaged and was being repaired, and I thought, *good, someone is keeping the infrastructure up,* and filed it under dullness, and moved on.

I have thought about that filing many times since. The mind is a customs house of its own, and mine waved the scaffolding through without inspection.

The boarding house on Tanner's Row was called the Sign of the Lantern, which pleased me, because my mother had kept a lamp in every window of my childhood and I am sentimental in ways I disguise even from myself. The innkeeper was a woman named Corva—broad, grey-streaked, with the forearms of someone who has lifted a great many things she did not respect—and she took my coin, gave me the corner room with the good lock, and asked no questions with a fluency I should have recognized as professional courtesy between colleagues, though I did not, then.

"Supper at seven," she said. "Curfew at nine. Don't be charming with the patrol; they're not equipped for it."

"I'm never charming," I said.

"Then you'll get along famously." And she was gone, back to her ledger, and I went up to my room, and I did the small rituals that make a courier feel like a person: I checked the false bottom of the case, I checked the papers against my ribs, I counted my coin, I looked out the window at the street.

The street was empty in the way streets are empty at dusk in winter everywhere, and I told myself that too, and believed it, because I was excellent at believing.

* * *

Supper was barley soup, black bread, and a pickled something that had given up its identity years ago. I ate in the common room with a peddler who talked at me about cloth tariffs until I performed the small yawn that ends conversations, and with two garrison clerks who spoke in the low voices of men who had recently learned that walls in Kelvane had opinions. I caught fragments. *Requisition. The mill. Not on the manifest. The General's own signature.* I did not lean in. Leaning in is a confession too.

I went up at half past eight, well ahead of curfew, and I sat by the window with the lamp turned low, and I did what I always did at the end of a leg: I rehearsed tomorrow. The university contact in Vessa, the manifests, the road north, the second checkpoint at Marlowe's Gate where the guards were said to be bored rather than cruel, which is the best kind. I rehearsed it all with the smooth efficiency of a woman who has never once in her life been surprised by anything she couldn't file under dullness.

Below, in the street, the lamplighter made his rounds. Kelvane still had a lamplighter; the occupation had not yet figured out how to requisition him. He went from lamp to lamp with his long pole, and the flames caught one by one down Tanner's Row, and I remember thinking—wryly, comfortably, with the full weight of my creed behind it—that whatever history was doing in this town, it was doing it to other people. To the peddler and his tariffs. To the clerks and their walls. To the innkeeper with her careful ledger. Not to me. I was a visitor from the country of afterward. I carried sealed things; I was not one of them.

I blew out the lamp. I went to bed.

I was woken, much later, by a knock. Not at my door—at the street door, low and quick, the knock of someone who has practiced not being heard. I lay in the dark and listened to Corva's footsteps cross the hall, and the street door open a hand's width, and a woman's voice, pitched so low I caught only its shape and none of its words.

Then footsteps on the stairs. Then a pause outside my door that lasted three heartbeats—three, I counted, because I had stopped breathing and the heart makes its own arithmetic—and then nothing at all.

In the morning, when I went down, the hall was empty and the street door was locked from inside, exactly as it should have been, and everything in the Sign of the Lantern was exactly where it should have been.

Except that on the mat by my door, in the grey hour before dawn, there was a child.
