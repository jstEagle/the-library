## 2. A Knock Before Dawn

I have been mistaken for a nun twice, a tax inspector once, and a widow of no particular grief so many times I've stopped correcting people. Invisibility is a craft, and I am good at my work. A woman who belongs to no one draws no eyes; a woman with sealed dispatches in her valise draws a firing squad. So you learn to be furniture. Furniture does not get interrogated. Furniture does not get followed home.

Which is why the child on the mat was not merely an inconvenience. She was a professional insult.

She was perhaps seven years old, small for it or old for it, hard to say, wrapped in a coat two sizes too large and sitting with the terrible stillness of a child who has been instructed to sit still. Not crying. Not looking at me either. Looking, in fact, at the middle distance, as though I were also furniture and we had finally found our level.

"Hello," I said, in the tone one uses for stray dogs and census-takers.

Nothing. Not deafness — I could see that much; her eyes flicked to my mouth when I spoke, tracking, cataloguing. Muteness, then, or something adjacent to it. The kind of silence that is a decision rather than a condition.

I looked up and down the corridor of the Sign of the Lantern. Grey light through the stairwell window, the smell of last night's cabbage, Mrs. Havel's clock ticking downstairs with the smug regularity of a thing that has never had to lie about anything. No mother lurking. No note pinned to the child's collar, which would at least have been conventional.

There was, however, a note folded into the child's fist. It took some doing to extract it; the fingers were small and stubborn and did not want to let go, and I confess I used more force than the situation strictly required because irritation is easier than the other thing, the cold thing, the thing climbing my spine on soft feet.

The note said: *Forget the face.*

That was all. No name, no apology, no instructions regarding feeding, which struck me even then as a gross dereliction on someone's part. Just four words in a careful hand, the kind of handwriting belonging to a person who has learned that everything they write may outlive them.

"Well," I said to the child, "you can tell whoever left you that flattery gets you nowhere."

The child continued to belong to no one, which was, I noted with a sourness I didn't examine too closely, my line.

* * *

Here is what I should have done, and I want it on the record that I knew it even as I failed to do it. I should have carried her down the stairs, set her on the front step, knocked on Mrs. Havel's door, and announced that an orphan had arrived, wasn't that dreadful, surely the constabulary handles such matters, and gone back upstairs to my dispatches and my practiced indifference. That was the protocol. That was the creed. History happens to other people; children on mats are history's business, not mine.

Instead I stood there holding a note that said *forget the face*, and thought: someone went to considerable trouble to find the one woman in this town whom nobody looks at, and gave her a child precisely *because* nobody looks at her. My anonymity, cultivated over years like an orchid in a dark room, had just been harvested by a stranger.

It's a peculiar feeling, being weaponized by trust. You don't feel trusted. You feel seen — thoroughly, invasively seen — by someone who never showed their face. Whoever she was, she had watched me long enough to know I'd hesitate. She had bet my hesitation against a child's life and, judging by the empty corridor, won before I even knew the game was running.

"You might at least have asked," I muttered, and picked the girl up, because her boots were wet through and standing on a cold mat in February is how children become corpses, and corpses draw inquiries, and inquiries draw soldiers. I told myself it was all logistics. I have told myself many things over the years. I'm told I'm very convincing.

* * *

She ate like a small wolf and drank tea like a much older one, hunched over the cup in my room with both hands, steam fogging her face. I had bought the bread downstairs with my best innocent-foreigner smile, and Mrs. Havel had asked no questions, because Mrs. Havel asks no questions of guests who pay in advance, which in occupied Vessar is practically a civic virtue.

The child did not speak. I tried Vessarine, then trade tongue, then a few words of northern dialect I keep for border crossings. Her eyes stayed on her bread. Fine. Silence, I decided, was a language I happened to be fluent in.

It was while she ate that I noticed the coat.

Two sizes too large, wool, good quality once, now mended in three places by someone whose stitches were neat but hurried. And along the inner seam of the lining, a ridge. I have spent eleven years feeling for ridges in linings — customs men teach you that, or rather, they teach everyone, and the ones who survive are the ones who learned fastest. A ridge in a seam is either nothing or everything.

I looked at the child. The child looked at the wall.

"This is yours?" I asked, touching the coat. Nothing. "May I?"

Nothing again, but she stopped chewing, which from her constituted a signed consent form.

I unpicked the seam with a hairpin. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth and sewn flat against the wool where a shoulder pad might sit, was a packet of papers. Not diplomatic correspondence. Not codes. Paper of the cheap grey sort used by clerks and archivists, covered in a tight, orderly hand, with columns — names, dates, and numbers. Numbers that were far too large for the space they occupied.

I read the first page standing up. I read the second page sitting down, because the floor seemed suddenly to have opinions about my balance.

Kelvane Bridge. The date was six weeks past, a market day, and the document was a tally. Not a report — reports have adjectives, reports have authors defending their careers. This was an inventory, compiled the way an accountant compiles stock, by someone who had apparently decided that if the occupation was going to treat human beings as cargo, then cargo would at least be counted honestly. Names. Ages. Where each had stood on the bridge when the shooting started. Which ones went into the water and which ones the water refused and were finished on the bank. A column marked simply *witnesses remaining*, and the number in it, page after page, getting smaller.

The final entry was a list of names in a different ink, fresher, added later. Children's names, mostly. With a notation beside each: *memorized*.

I looked up. The girl was watching me now — not the wall, not her bread. Me. And her expression was not the blankness of a mute child. It was the patience of a courier waiting for confirmation of delivery.

"Oh," I said. My voice came out wrong, so I tried again. "Oh, you clever, appalling little creature."

She blinked. Once. Somewhere below us, Mrs. Havel's clock ticked through another minute in which nothing happened, officially.

* * *

I want to describe what those documents did to me, and I find I can only do it sideways, the way you look at an eclipse.

My briefing in the capital had mentioned Kelvane Bridge exactly once, in a paragraph of bureaucratic weather-report prose: *incidents in the river districts, ongoing stabilization measures*. Stabilization. I had read the word over breakfast. I remembered thinking the coffee was bitter. That was the entire moral event of my morning — bad coffee — while forty miles away someone was writing down the ages of the dead in a mended coat.

This is what neutrality is, in case you wondered. It is reading about a massacre over breakfast and noticing the coffee.

And here was the same massacre, folded small enough to hide against a child's shoulder blade, delivered to my door by a woman who had chosen me — chosen *me*, with my forged calm and my unremarkable face and my dispatches for a government that I was beginning, in the grey light of that morning, to suspect existed mostly on letterhead. She hadn't asked my permission. Trust, I was learning, rarely bothers.

I sat for a long time with the papers in my lap and the child across from me, and I did the arithmetic I had trained myself never to do. If the woman who left this child was found, she would give up my boarding house under less pressure than it takes to bend a spoon. If the child was found with me, my diplomatic papers would become a curiosity examined by men whose curiosity is fatal. Every hour she sat eating my bread, the noose drew a little tighter around a neck that was, until this morning, entirely decorative.

"Forget the face," I read aloud again. "Whose face? Yours? Hers? Mine?" 

The child put down her crust. Then, slowly, deliberately, she raised one small hand and touched herself — cheek, hair, chin — and shook her head. Then she pointed at the papers in my lap.

I felt the cold go through me like a stitch pulled tight. Not *forget your own face*. Forget *the faces in the file*. The dead of Kelvane Bridge were not evidence to this child. They were homework. Her mother — I was sure now it was her mother, sure the way you're sure of weather — had made her memorize names instead of carrying them, because paper burns and a seven-year-old who keeps silent does not.

Except that the paper existed anyway. Both. Belt and suspenders. A woman planning for every failure except the one she couldn't plan for.

"All right," I said. It came out steadier than I deserved. "Here's what happens now. I am going to think, which will take some time, as thinking is not my strongest suit — my strongest suit is looking boring. You are going to finish your bread. And when Mrs. Havel knocks, you are my niece from the eastern provinces, shy, simple, and dull beyond description. Can you manage dull?"

For the first time that morning, something moved behind the girl's eyes. Not quite a smile. The ancestor of a smile. She nodded.

I repacked the oilcloth with hands that were almost steady and began, with a hairpin, to sew the seam closed again — and that was when I heard it. Boots on the street outside. More than one pair, unhurried, in the rhythm of men who stop wherever they please because stopping is what power sounds like. They paused by the boarding-house door. A voice, low. The scrape of paper — a notice being pasted to the glass, or taken down, I couldn't tell which.

The child went absolutely still, her hand halfway to her mouth, and I understood that stillness. It was not the stillness of obedience. It was rehearsal.

On the mat by the door, in the grey hour before dawn, someone had left me a child, a massacre, and a note. Now, before I'd even threaded the needle twice, somebody outside was already asking questions about the Sign of the Lantern.

I blew out the candle and listened.
