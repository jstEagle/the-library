## 4. Checkpoint One: The Paper Face

There is a particular way a bureaucrat holds his chin when he believes the world owes him patience. I had spent eleven years studying that chin from across desks in three capitals, and on the morning we approached the first checkpoint at Vosk Hollow, I adopted it as my own.

The trick, if you want to call it a trick—and I prefer to call it a costume, because costumes can be taken off and lies stick to the skin—is to be mildly inconvenienced. Not afraid. Not eager. Inconvenienced. A woman who is afraid has something to hide. A woman who is eager has something to deliver. But a woman who is inconvenienced is simply a woman who expected the world to run more smoothly, and no one in the history of occupations has ever been arrested for expecting too much of the world.

Mira walked beside me with her hand tucked into my elbow, which she had not done voluntarily two days earlier. I had rehearsed her the night before in the boarding-house room, using a hairbrush as a prop and my most tedious official voice.

"You are my sister's daughter," I told her. "Your name is Annel. You are seven—"

She held up four fingers.

"Fine. You are four and small for your age, which is a tragedy your mother weeps over, and you have a condition of the throat." I tapped my own neck. "You do not speak. You do not nod at questions. You look bored. Boredom, Annel, is the great camouflage of the innocent. Can you look bored?"

She looked at me with an expression I can only describe as a masterclass, and I understood that of the two of us, I was the amateur.

Her silence, it turned out, required no acting at all. Whatever had closed her throat had closed it thoroughly. The doctor in me—the part trained by years of watching people lie with their whole bodies—wondered sometimes whether the muteness was grief or strategy, whether her mother had instructed her in it the way she had instructed her in everything else. *Forget the face.* Well. We were forgetting faces together now, hers behind boredom, mine behind paperwork.

* * *

The checkpoint at Vosk Hollow sat where the north road pinched between a granite ridge and a frozen creek, three sandbagged stations and a striped pole, the kind of architecture that exists purely to remind you that your movement is a privilege granted by strangers with rifles. Snow had been trampled into gray slush by the boots of a garrison that clearly had nothing better to do than stand around being cold and important.

A sergeant met us at the first station. Young, chapped, with the particular resentment of a man who had joined an army to be a hero and had been issued a clipboard instead.

"Purpose of travel?"

"Consular escort," I said, and produced the folder with the languid motion of someone who produced folders for a living. "Diplomatic pouch, sealed material, transiting to Corvane. My niece." I tipped my head at Mira without looking at her. Looking at children invites conversation about children.

He flipped through my papers with the speed of a man who could not read half of them and did not want anyone to know. This is the secret weakness of every occupying force: they staff their checkpoints with boys, and boys are terrified of looking foolish in front of women who sound like they know what a consular pouch is.

"Sealed material," he repeated, as though tasting it.

"Which means," I said pleasantly, "that if you open it, you will have to write a report explaining why you opened it, and I will have to file a complaint explaining why you shouldn't have, and somewhere a major will spend his afternoon reading both. Is that how you'd like to spend his afternoon?"

I watched him perform the arithmetic. It took perhaps four seconds. The arithmetic of empire is always the same: the cost of diligence exceeds the reward.

"Marta!" he called instead, because there is always a Marta—a senior woman at every checkpoint whose entire function is to be the person the boy hides behind. "Documents check."

Marta was fifty, built like a well-used door, and she read my papers the way I imagined she read everything: once, completely, and without mercy. She checked the seal. She checked the transit stamps. She checked my face against my photograph with the frank scrutiny of a customs officer who had seen ten thousand liars and found most of them forgettable.

I gave her forgettable. I gave her the chin, the mild impatience, the small sigh of a professional whose time was being consumed by smaller professionals. Mira leaned against my hip and examined her own mittens as though they contained a lecture.

"Business in Vessar?" Marta asked.

"Passing through. The Republic's business concluded itself some months ago; I'm told what remains is mostly weather."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not a smile, exactly. Recognition, maybe, of a fellow traveler in the profession of not saying things. She stamped the folder. Twice. The second stamp was unnecessary and I understood it as a gift: redundancy as kindness, the only kind available at a checkpoint.

Then the boy ruined everything.

Not maliciously. That's the thing about catastrophe—it rarely arrives wearing malice. It arrives wearing diligence.

* * *

It happened at the third station, where a corporal with ink-stained fingers was logging transit manifests, and where one of my misdirected forms—the requisition sheet I'd forged in Corvane to give my route the texture of bureaucratic inevitability—listed its supply origin point.

Kelvane Bridge Depot.

I had chosen it because it was plausible. Depots are plausible. Bridges are plausible. Nobody interrogates plausibility.

Except this corporal did not read forms the way the sergeant read them, skimming for danger words like *sealed* and *complaint*. He read them the way Marta read them, or worse—he read them the way a man reads when he has been ordered to memorize a list.

His pen stopped.

"Kelvane," he said.

"Is that a question?" I asked, chin up, mildly inconvenienced.

"The bridge depot." He looked up at me, and his face had gone carefully blank in the way faces go blank when the mind behind them is moving very fast. "Supply traffic through Kelvane was suspended in the autumn. You wouldn't know. Foreign posting."

"I'm sure it was suspended for good reason," I said. "Requisitions outlive reasons. That's rather the nature of requisitions."

He smiled at that—actually smiled—and something in the smile was worse than suspicion. It was complicity. It was the smile of a man who knows exactly why the traffic was suspended and finds it darkly funny that a paper trail survived when the town did not.

He stamped the form. Logged it. Waved us through with the striped pole lifting like a small ceremonial surrender.

And it was only when we were forty yards past the barrier, Mira's hand tightening on my arm with a grip far beyond four years old, that I understood what had just happened.

He hadn't suspected me. He had recognized the name.

Somewhere in the occupation's machinery, a list existed. Not of smugglers, not of spies—of places. Places whose names on documents triggered a second reading, a flag, a quiet note passed up the chain. Kelvane Bridge was on that list because Kelvane Bridge was a wound the occupation was trying to close without anesthesia, and here I came walking through the snow with the wound's name typed neatly in a requisition box.

They weren't looking for a woman and a child. Not yet. They were looking for paper—any paper—that mentioned the massacre, because somewhere above the checkpoints, above the sergeants and the Martas and the ink-fingered corporals, someone had decided that the dead of Kelvane Bridge were a filing problem.

And a filing problem, unlike a massacre, gets solved.

I kept walking. One foot, then the other, at the pace of a woman with nowhere urgent to be. Behind us the pole dropped, the next traveler was waved forward, the machinery resumed its grinding. Ahead the road ran white and empty toward the river country, toward the second checkpoint, toward whatever waited between here and the coast.

"They'll pull the manifest tonight," I murmured, so quietly it was barely breath, more to myself than to her. "Cross-reference the transit number. Find out whose folder carried a requisition from a depot that officially never existed." I swallowed. "And then, my dear silent Annel, someone is going to start looking for us specifically. Not for couriers. Not for refugees. For us."

Mira looked up at me. She didn't speak—she never spoke—but her eyes said something anyway, something old and unchildlike, and I suspect it was the same thing her mother had known when she left her on my doorstep:

*I chose you because you belong to no one. Now make yourself useful.*

The road bent north. Somewhere behind us, a telegraph office was about to receive a very short message, and somewhere ahead—I would learn this later, though I couldn't have known it then—an old friend of mine was about to be handed a sketch of a woman's face and asked if he recognized it.

Life, I have often thought, is a comedy written by someone who despises subtlety.

I bought Mira a sugared bun at the next village, because if we were going to be hunted, we would be hunted on full stomachs. And I began, in the privacy of my own skull, to compose the lie I would need for checkpoint two—one good enough to survive a man who read lists for a living.

It would have to be better than the truth. The truth, these days, was contraband.
