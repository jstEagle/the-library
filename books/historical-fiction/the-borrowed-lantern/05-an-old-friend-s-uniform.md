## 5. An Old Friend's Uniform

The trouble with being invisible, I've found, is that it only works until someone decides to look.

We had been on the road from the lighthouse keeper's cellar for two days, and Mira had developed a theory about checkpoints. She communicated this theory entirely with her eyebrows, which had become, in the absence of speech, a full parliament. The left one said *guards are stupid*. The right one said *but you are more stupid for pretending to be a bureaucrat when you could be pretending to be anything else*. Together they constituted a motion of no confidence in my methods, and it passed without debate.

"You're welcome to walk back to Kelvane," I told her. "See how your eyebrows fare there."

She looked at me with an expression that was either forgiveness or indigestion, and trudged on through the slush beside me like a small, judgmental shadow. Somewhere south of us, behind two checkpoints and a flooded river, lay everything my mother had taught me about keeping my head down. Ahead of us lay the town of Vessarine Proper's last functioning rail junction, and somewhere in that town, if the rumors on the barge were worth anything, was a man whose job description had recently expanded to include looking for a woman traveling alone with a child.

I had decided not to think about that. I was thinking about it constantly, which is nearly the same thing but less useful.

* * *

Tomas Brandt had been in the occupation's service for eleven months, and he had learned three things in that time.

First: the paperwork was always worse than the war. Second: the men who shouted loudest about loyalty were the ones who kept escape routes memorized. Third—and this was the lesson he returned to nightly, turning it over like a stone to see what lived underneath—he had not become a monster. He had become something more efficient than that. A monster at least knew what it wanted.

He sat in his office above the rail junction checkpoint with a folder open in front of him and did not read it. Outside, snow came down in the flat, indifferent way of snow that has no opinion about who wins.

The folder contained a sketch. An artist at Checkpoint One worked fast and better than his pay grade suggested: a woman, thirty-something, dark hair pinned up with what appeared to be a pencil or a very thin knife, wearing the gray coat of someone who dressed to be forgotten. Beside her, a girl, perhaps nine, mute by all reports, with eyebrows the artist had rendered with suspicious tenderness.

The report attached described her papers as flawless. That was the detail that had snagged Tomas like a fishhook. Flawless papers at Checkpoint One were rarer than honest generals. Someone had coached her. Someone had spent real effort making her boring, and people who invested effort in boredom were carrying something.

He should have forwarded the sketch days ago. He had not forwarded the sketch. He had told himself it was because the likeness was poor, which was true, and because Checkpoint Two's flood had scattered everyone's schedules into chaos, which was also true. Both things were true and neither was the reason.

The reason was that when he'd first unrolled the sketch across his desk, before he'd read a word of the report, some animal part of him had already known the set of the jaw, the particular stubborn angle of a woman who had once, at fourteen, dared him to swim the Vessa in April and then, when he refused, swum it herself while he stood on the bank rehearsing reasons why that had been the intelligent choice.

"Ulyana Eastwick," he said aloud, to the empty office, and the name sounded like a door closing.

There were procedures for this. There were forms. He was, professionally speaking, a man made almost entirely of forms. He took out the requisition sheet for a regional alert—the document that would put her face in every guardhouse between the river and the coast—and held his pen over it for a long time.

Then he wrote instead: *Likeness inconclusive. Requesting secondary verification before distribution.*

Which meant he would have to verify her himself.

He told himself this was diligence. He was getting very good at telling himself things.

* * *

The boarding house in Vessar Town had the particular smell of places where the rent was paid in silence: boiled cabbage, wet wool, and the faint industrial tang of the stove polish they used to make the parlor look maintained. Mira and I shared a room under the eaves with one bed, one chair, and a window that looked out on a courtyard where someone had hung laundry that had frozen mid-drip, little stalactites of ice swinging like tiny hanged men.

Mira sat on the bed cross-legged and watched me repack the satchel for the hundredth time. Documents against my spine. Dispatches—what remained of them—in the false bottom of the case. The massacre file wrapped in oilcloth inside her coat lining again, because no one searches a child twice.

"They're asking after a woman and a girl," I said. I had heard it from the landlady, who traded information the way other women traded recipes, and who had charged me two marks for the sentence. "A courier and her silent niece." I paused. "That part's accurate enough."

One eyebrow rose. *And the rest?*

"The rest says the woman may be traveling under diplomatic cover." I tied off the satchel. "Which means whoever wrote that description knows what my papers look like. Which means—" I stopped, because finishing the sentence meant saying *someone I passed at Checkpoint One* and saying that meant admitting the whole apparatus of my invisibility had developed eyes.

Mira finished it for me with a slow, terrible nod. Nine years old, mute since Kelvane, and she understood the arithmetic better than half the ministers whose signatures I used to carry.

"There's a train tomorrow morning," I said. "Six-forty. We'll be at the gate by six. After that—" I didn't finish that either. My plans had lately developed a habit of dying young.

She reached over and, with great seriousness, straightened the collar of my coat. It wasn't crooked. She straightened it anyway, and I understood it was not about the collar. It was the closest thing to speech she had given me, and I received it as such, and pretended to blow my nose so she wouldn't see what it did to my face.

* * *

Tomas walked the checkpoint floor at five-thirty in the morning because he did not trust the morning shift to recognize subtlety if it handed them its papers personally.

The gate was a wooden frame bolted into the station arch, lit by two lamps that burned with the orange resignation of lamps in a losing war. Travelers shuffled through in ones and twos: a fish merchant with ice in his beard, two sisters carrying a trunk between them like a coffin, an old man arguing with a stamp.

At six-twelve, she came through the crowd.

She had done something to herself—dulled the hair, aged the coat, adopted a stoop that said *I am tired and unremarkable and please do not perceive me*—and none of it mattered. He knew her the way you know a song from three wrong notes. His heart performed a maneuver he chose not to examine.

He stepped into the queue ahead of her, turned, and said, "Papers, madam," in the voice he used forty times a day, and watched her decide whether to know him.

It took perhaps two seconds. He counted them. In the first second she was a stranger, bored and cold. In the second, recognition arrived—not in her face, which stayed perfectly bureaucratic, but in her hands, which went still on the documents she was passing forward.

"Tomas Brandt," he said quietly. "You can drop the stoop. It's terrible."

"Dominic Crane," she said. "You can drop the uniform. It's worse." Then, because twenty years will outlast any war: "You look awful."

"You look like a woman traveling with her silent niece." He glanced down at Mira, who was regarding him with the flat appraisal of a customs official. "Hello."

Mira said nothing. Her right eyebrow performed a slow ascent that Tomas, despite himself, respected.

"Step aside with me," he said, low. "Both of you. There's a room off the platform. If you argue, I'll do it loudly, and loud costs more than quiet."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I arrest you, and we both find out what I'm made of." He kept his voice level. "I'd rather not. I'm told it shows."

* * *

The room off the platform had once been a telegraph office and now contained a desk, a stool, and a poster reminding citizens that loose talk costs lives, which struck Tomas as the occupation's single honest piece of advice. He closed the door. Ulyana stood with her back to it, Mira beside her, and the three of them occupied the little room like a truce nobody had signed.

"The sketch," he said. "From Checkpoint One. It's on my desk upstairs."

"I wondered who'd drawn those eyebrows with such affection."

"The artist flatters everyone. It's why he's still employed." He sat on the stool because standing felt like a position and he didn't have one. "Ulyana. What are you carrying?"

"Church donations. For my niece's confirmation."

"Try again."

"Carpentry invoices."

"Third attempt, and then I start opening things." He nodded at the satchel. "You know I'll know whatever it is within the hour whether you tell me or not."

She studied him, and he felt the study like weather. This was the thing about Ulyana Eastwick that the sketch could never capture: she weighed people. She had weighed him at eight years old over a stolen apple, and he had confessed before she said a word.

"You were at my mother's funeral," she said finally. "You cried into your hat and blamed the wind. There was no wind."

"It was a very emotional hat."

"Tomas." Her voice dropped the wryness like a coat dropped on a chair. "Why haven't you filed the sketch? The landlady says alerts go out in hours. It's been four days. You're not a slow man."

He looked at his hands. They were a bureaucrat's hands now, ink-stained, soft at the pads. They had stamped transit passes for three hundred people this month, and he had stopped asking where the trains went, and every pass he didn't ask about was a small stone he'd laid in a road he told himself he wasn't building.

"If I file it," he said, "you'll be taken. Not arrested—taken. There's a difference and you wouldn't enjoy learning it." He met her eyes. "If I don't file it, I'm a traitor, and they hang traitors from the station gantry, and I'll deserve it, probably, though I notice the men I'd be loyal to hang people who never did anything at all, so the word has lost its precision."

Mira tugged Ulyana's sleeve. Something passed between them—a whole conversation conducted in the currency of eyebrows—and Ulyana shook her head almost imperceptibly.

"Don't tell her anything," Ulyana said. "Whatever you decide, don't let her hear it. She carries enough."

"She can't hear anything. She doesn't talk."

"She hears," Ulyana said, "everything."

Tomas looked at the girl, and the girl looked back, and there was something in her stillness that reminded him of the Vessa in April—water that looked calm and was moving hard underneath.

"Six-forty train," he said. "It leaves in eleven minutes. I'm going to walk out that door, and I'm going to stand at the gate, and I'm going to watch the departure board for exactly as long as it takes you to board—or not board—at my discretion." He stood. His legs felt borrowed. "I don't know yet which way I'll report it. Neither do you. That's the honest answer, and it's the last one I have."

"That's not an answer, Tomas."

"No. It's a delay." He opened the door. Cold air came in off the platform, smelling of coal and iron. "Delays are all anyone has left."

* * *

Ulyana watched him go, and hated how well she knew the shape of his back, the set of his shoulders, the exact way he squared himself before doing something he despised. He had squared himself that way at fourteen, on the frozen bank of the Vessa, watching her swim water that could kill her, unable to stop her and unwilling to follow.

Some men spend their whole lives on that bank.

"Eleven minutes," she said to Mira, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "New plan. We don't board the train."

The left eyebrow climbed. *Explain.*

"Because he's watching the departure board, and whichever way his conscience breaks, it breaks in front of witnesses. If we vanish from the platform, his report stays unwritten another day. If we board—" She touched the satchel. "If we board and he's already decided, we're a name on a manifest going to a station with his colleagues on it."

Mira considered this with the gravity of a supreme court, then tilted her head toward the far end of the platform, where a freight clerk was wrestling a handcart of crates toward the coastal line, swearing at the ice in fluent, inventive Vessarine.

Ulyana smiled, and it felt strange on her face, like a coat that hadn't fit in years.

"Yes," she said. "Exactly that. And Mira—if a man in a gray coat follows us onto that freight platform—"

The girl patted the oilcloth packet inside her lining, once, and looked up at her with an expression that needed no translation at all:

*I know what I'm carrying. I know what it weighs. And I know, better than you do, that some things are worth more than the person carrying them.*

"No," Ulyana said sharply, gripping the small shoulder. "Not that. Never that. You are not paper, Mira. You don't get burned to protect the message." She pulled the girl close and said it into her hair, fierce and quiet, the first true thing she'd said aloud in weeks: "The message burns first. From today, that's the rule."

On the platform behind them, the six-forty began to move, empty of them, and somewhere in the station a man stood watching a departure board and deciding, minute by minute, what he was made of.
