## 6. The Lantern Keeper

I had not spoken in forty-one days, which is a long time to keep your mouth shut when you are nine years old and the world keeps doing things that beg to be commented upon.

The lighthouse keeper's cellar smelled of onions and old rope. There was a barrel of salt herring that watched me with one cloudy eye whenever I moved, and I had decided it was my enemy. I named him General Herring, after the man whose photograph Mama once burned in our stove. This was the kind of joke I would have told aloud, before. Now I kept jokes like coins in a shoe.

Above me, through the floorboards, I could hear Ulyana Eastwick arguing with the lantern keeper about whether we were staying one night or two. She argued the way she did everything—politely, precisely, as if the world were a customs form she intended to fill out correctly. The keeper's voice rumbled back, low and sea-worn. Somewhere between them was the price of our hiding, which everyone pretended not to be discussing while discussing nothing else.

I sat on a crate with my knees up and practiced being furniture.

Mama had taught me that. *Be furniture, Mira. Furniture is never interrogated.* She said it the week before Kelvane Bridge, when she sewed the papers into my coat lining and made me recite names instead of sleeping. She said a mute child is invisible, and an invisible child is safe, and so I became mute—not because I couldn't speak, but because speaking was the one thing left that I could give her, and giving it felt like keeping her alive. Every day I didn't talk was a day I was still obeying her.

But forty-one days is a long time. And the herring barrel was winning the staring contest. And upstairs, the woman who was supposed to be only a courier was learning, I think, that I was not a parcel.

* * *

She came down the cellar stairs with a candle and two bowls of barley soup, and she set them on the crate beside me without demanding anything, which was her whole method with me and why I had begun, against every rule Mama gave me, to trust her.

"You should eat while it's hot," she said. "Cold barley soup is punishment for a crime nobody committed."

I looked at the soup. I looked at her. In my head I said: *General Herring has been demoted to private.* Out loud I said nothing, because saying nothing had become a wall, and walls get taller the longer you lean on them.

Ulyana sat down on the opposite crate with the graceless sigh of a woman whose spine had opinions. She blew on her soup. The candle flame stood very still, the way flames do in cellars where the sea can't reach them.

"I spoke with the keeper," she said, conversationally, to the soup as much as to me. "His name is Anselm Voss. He kept this light burning through the bombardment of Harrow Point, all three nights, because he said a dark harbor kills more sailors than any navy ever did." She took a spoonful. "He asked if you'd like to see the lamp room tomorrow. He says children always want to see the lamp."

I did want to see the lamp. I wanted it so badly my chest hurt. But wanting things out loud is how you lose them, and also how you lose *yourself*, if you're a girl carrying a massacre in her coat lining.

So I shrugged. It was a masterpiece of a shrug. I had been perfecting it for weeks.

Ulyana studied me over the rim of her bowl with those gray eyes that missed nothing, which I resented deeply. "You know," she said, "when I was your age, I decided not to speak for a month. To see if anyone would notice."

This was so obviously a lie that I almost laughed. Almost. My mouth did something dangerous at one corner.

"It's true," she said. "My father was a diplomat, and diplomats' houses are full of adults talking about important things, and I concluded that silence was the only way to make them look at you." She stirred her soup. "It worked. They noticed. Then they sent me to a doctor, who charged my father a great deal of money to inform him that I was willful. So consider yourself warned: silence is expensive."

*My mother paid more than a doctor,* I thought. *She paid everything.*

And then—I don't know why, except that the cellar was warm and the soup smelled like someone had actually thought about a child while cooking it, and General Herring's cloudy eye seemed less menacing by candlelight—the words came up out of me like water finding a crack.

"She made me memorize them."

Ulyana went very still. Stillness, I had learned, was her native language; mine now too.

"The names," I said. My voice came out rusty, small, like a door opening in a house long shut. "Not just the papers. Mama said papers burn, papers get taken, papers lie about what they contain. But names—" I pressed my fist against my chest, against the place where they lived. "Names you have to take out of a body one at a time, and there were sixty-one of them, and I know all of them. I know their trades. Ferenc Dalos was a ferryman. The Ostmere twins were eleven. Eleven," I repeated, because the number required witnesses. "Younger than me."

Ulyana set down her spoon. Very carefully, the way you put down something breakable.

"Mira," she began.

"Don't say it doesn't matter," I said, and my voice cracked on the last word, which infuriated me, because I had wanted to sound fierce and instead sounded nine. "Everyone says that. Everyone will say, oh, poor thing, what a burden, let the grown-ups carry it now. But Mama didn't give me the names because she thought I was strong. She gave them to me because I'm *small*. Nobody searches a small girl properly. And because—" Here was the part I had never said to anyone, the part that had been sitting in my stomach since Kelvane Bridge like a swallowed stone. "Because she knew she might not live to deliver them herself. She wasn't hoping. She was *planning*. She kissed my head and told me to be furniture and walked toward the bridge and she knew."

The candle flame trembled. Or maybe that was me.

"Sixty-one people crossed that bridge expecting to cross back," I said. "Someone counted them first. That's what Mama did. She was the counting kind. Someone has to be the counting kind or else they're just numbers, and numbers don't have trades, and twins who are eleven just become *casualties*, which is a word that means nothing on purpose."

I ran out of breath. I hadn't spoken that many words in six weeks, and they had used up some supply I hadn't known I possessed.

Ulyana Eastwick, courier of sealed dispatches, believer in neutrality and correct forms and history happening to other people, looked at me across a barrel of hostile herring, and I watched something change in her face. It was like watching ice decide to become water. Not dramatic. Just a slow, structural surrender.

"They're not numbers," she said quietly. "You've seen to that."

"And you'll carry them," I said. It wasn't a question. Mama had taught me that too: when you need something from an adult, state it as fact, because questions invite negotiation. "North. Past the last checkpoint. To someone who prints things."

"That is not what my dispatches say I'm doing."

"Your dispatches are for a government that's already dead," I said. "Mama told me about you. She chose you because you belong to no one. She said belonging to no one means you can choose anyone." I picked up my soup, finally, because my hands needed a job. "So. Choose."

* * *

Later—much later, when the lamp above us had begun its slow turning and the ceiling flickered with its patient heartbeat—Ulyana came down again. She didn't pretend it was about soup this time. She brought a blanket and sat closer than before, and for a while she just listened to the sea throwing itself against the cliff like a debtor at a locked door.

"When I cross into the northern provinces," she said at last, "there will likely be shooting. Or worse, paperwork. Both are bad." She pulled the blanket around my shoulders without asking, tucking it with a briskness that fooled neither of us. "If you come with me, you come as mine. Not as cargo, not as evidence. If anyone asks, you're my niece, and your name is Mira Crane, and you are a terrible liar and an excellent memorizer, which runs in the family."

*Mine.* The word landed somewhere under my ribs and stayed there, glowing like a coal.

"I'm not terrible at lying," I whispered. It was the second-longest sentence of my new life.

"No?" One eyebrow went up. "What color are my eyes, then?"

"Gray," I said. "Like weather that hasn't decided yet."

Something happened to her mouth that she would probably deny was a smile. "We'll work on it," she said.

Upstairs, the lamp swept its arm across the water, again and again, lighting ships it would never meet. I lay down on the cot she'd arranged and closed my eyes and recited names, all sixty-one, silently, the way Mama taught me—ferries and twins, bakers and schoolteachers, each one a door I could open and close.

Somewhere north, there was a printing press waiting to be fed. Somewhere behind us, men were drawing sketches of a woman and a silent girl.

And in the morning, Anselm Voss would show us the lamp, and Ulyana would ask him, casually, the way she asked about everything, exactly where the coastal road met the river—and I would hear his answer, and I would understand from the way her hands went still that the road we needed was no longer safe.
