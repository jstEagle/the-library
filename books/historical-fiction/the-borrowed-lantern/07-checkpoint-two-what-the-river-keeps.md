## 7. Checkpoint Two: What the River Keeps

The river had eaten the road.

That was how the ferryman put it, gesturing with a pipe stem at what had been, according to my map and my briefing and my generally optimistic view of geography, a perfectly serviceable bridge. In its place stood a brown expanse of moving water wearing the road's corpse as a hat—fence posts, a wagon wheel, half a chapel roof all drifting eastward with the serene confidence of things that had made their peace with impermanence.

"Three days," he said. "Maybe four. Water's still coming down from the hills."

"We don't have four days."

"Everybody says that." He shrugged with the magnificent indifference of a man whose livelihood depended on catastrophe. "The river doesn't care what you have."

Beside me, Mira stood very still in her oversized coat, watching the water the way she watched everything—with that unnerving, unblinking attention of hers, as though the flood were a document she intended to memorize. She hadn't spoken since the lighthouse cellar. I no longer believed she couldn't. I suspected she'd simply decided, with a child's ruthless efficiency, that words were a currency best hoarded.

I checked the sky behind us. Empty. That meant nothing. For two days now I'd had the sensation of being read—a page someone else was turning—and every time I looked up, the reader closed the book.

* * *

The barge was called the *Merciful Ann*, which told you everything about its owner's sense of humor and nothing about its seaworthiness. It sat low in the flooded current like something pretending to be a rock, and its captain was a woman built on similar principles: broad, weathered, immovable.

Her name was Odile Voss, and she looked at us the way an appraiser looks at a piece that might be fake.

"Crossing's not free," she said. "And it's not cheap. The bridge guards are watching this bank because they think everyone stupid enough to use it is worth catching. My price reflects the danger."

"I have money."

"I have money." She said it the way one might say *I have air*. "What I don't have is immunity. You're carrying something. Don't insult me by denying it—I've been running this crossing for eleven years and nobody pays my price to transport a hatbox and a quiet child. So here's the arrangement. Half now. And the other half—" she held out a hand, palm up, patient as gravity, "—is whatever's in your satchel."

Mira's small hand found mine. Cold fingers. A grip like a decision.

"The satchel stays with the child," I said.

"Then the child stays on this bank."

We looked at each other for a long moment. Rain came in sideways off the water, finding every gap in my coat with the precision of a customs inspector.

"You misunderstand your position," Odile said pleasantly. "You need me more than I need you. There are three boats on this stretch of river. One is mine. One sank Tuesday. One belongs to a man who sells his passengers to the garrison and calls them volunteers for labor. Do the arithmetic."

I did the arithmetic. The arithmetic said I was standing on a drowned road with a child who knew the names of forty-seven dead men, a bag full of evidence that could hang a general or gut a resistance, and exactly one bargaining chip I was willing to spend.

Just not the one she wanted.

* * *

Here is a truth about lying that no one tells you: the best lies aren't inventions. They're arrangements of the truth, the way a florist arranges flowers—the same materials, a different shape. I had spent nine years as a courier of sealed dispatches learning to say true things in false orders.

So I opened the satchel—slowly, letting her see the leather, the wax seals, the weight of officialdom—and lifted out the bundle that had been my whole purpose when I crossed into Vessar. Dispatches. Diplomatic correspondence for a government that was, by my last reliable count, a government mostly in memory. Sealed with the crest of a republic that existed now chiefly as ink.

"Half payment," I said. "These. State papers. If the occupation finds them on your boat, you're a smuggler of secrets. If you deliver them across, there are people on the far bank who will pay you more than your asking price just to know they arrived."

Odile turned one seal over with a thick finger. "Dead government's paper."

"Paper doesn't drown."

"No. But it burns beautifully." She looked up, and her eyes were shrewder than her voice let on. "This isn't the thing you're protecting. This is the thing you're spending so you don't have to protect the real thing. Which means the real thing is worth a great deal, which means I should raise my price, which means we're going to stand here in the rain while you decide whether to lie to me better."

There was a moment—I'll admit it—when I considered simply telling her everything. Trust is seductive when you're tired. But trust is also a door, and doors open in both directions, and Tomas Brandt had taught me that lesson years before either of us understood we were teaching each other anything.

"There's a woman," I said, and it was almost true. "She's dead now. She left something with the girl, and the girl can't carry the weight of knowing it. I'm taking them north. That's all."

"All," Odile repeated. She chewed the word like gristle. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed—a short, bark-like sound aimed at the river. "You're good. You're genuinely good. Every word true and the whole of it a lie." She pocketed the dispatches. "Fine. Half paid. But hear me, courier: if trouble comes to my boat, I throw trouble overboard. Child included. I've buried a husband and two sons in this water; I won't add myself out of sentiment."

"Understood."

"And you—" she pointed the pipe stem at me, "—you'll sleep in shifts, because the man on the south bank who sold you that map has been selling maps to the garrison office since autumn. Whatever you think is following you is following you faster than you think."

* * *

The crossing took the whole night. The *Merciful Ann* wallowed through the flood like a drunk leaving church, and Odile's crew—two silent brothers with forearms like mooring cable—poled us through water where fields used to be. I lay in the hold with Mira under my arm, both of us pretending to sleep, listening to the hull groan its opinions.

Somewhere past midnight, in the dark, she spoke.

"He's close."

I went cold from the boots up. Not *they're close*. Not *someone's following us*. *He.* As though she knew him. As though her mother had described him to her the way she'd described everything else—in names, in specifics, in the grammar of testimony.

"Mira." I kept my voice level. In the dark, a level voice is the only furniture you have. "What do you know about him?"

Silence. Long enough that I thought I'd imagined it.

"Tomas," she said finally. Just the name, set down between us like a stone.

"How do you know that name?"

Another silence, longer. When she answered, her voice was small and precise, a recitation worn smooth by repetition. "Mama said, *if the tall one comes, the one who knew you before the war, run even from him. Especially from him. Love is the easiest door to open.*" A pause. "She said it twice so I wouldn't forget. She made me say everything twice."

I lay there in the black hold of a smuggler's barge with a dead woman's instructions echoing in my skull, and understood, with the clarity that only arrives at the worst possible hour, that Sera Eastwick—or whoever the woman at my door had been—had known about Tomas. Had known him by name. Which meant she'd known *me*, too—not as a stranger, not as a convenient neutral party, but specifically, deliberately, the way you choose a lock you've already studied.

My invisibility hadn't been stumbled upon. It had been selected.

And somewhere behind us, moving through the rain and the flooded country, was a tall man who had known me before the war, closing the distance at whatever speed duty allowed him.

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin over the far bank, and with it came trouble, punctual as taxes.

Two riders on the ridge above the landing. Not moving. Watching. The distance was too great to be sure, but one of them sat a horse with a particular stillness I recognized—the stillness of a man who has done nothing but wait his entire life and is very good at it. An agent's patience. Tomas trained them himself; I'd heard him joke about it once, years ago, back when jokes between us were still possible. *Anyone can chase. The art is in arriving after they've stopped running.*

"They'll ride for the next crossing," Odile said quietly beside me. "Cut ahead. Or they'll wire the garrison at Marren Ford, and then the net's set and we're the fish." She spat into the water. "Your half-payment bought you the crossing, courier. It didn't buy you a future. So. Last offer, freely given: give me the real cargo, let me take it north with my regular freight where nobody looks twice at my holds, and you and the girl walk away light. Nobody hunts a woman with empty hands."

It was tempting. God help me, it was tempting—the clean solution, the professional solution, the one where Ulyana Eastwick the courier does her job and goes home and history continues to happen to other people.

But I had read the ledger. I had heard a child recite the dead twice over so she wouldn't forget. Names weren't cargo. Cargo could be lost, seized, burned, and replaced. These names, carried in this specific mute stubborn girl, were irreplaceable—which meant the girl stayed with them, which meant I stayed with the girl, which meant Odile's clean solution dissolved the moment you actually looked at it.

"No," I said.

"No?"

"The cargo travels with the witness. That's not negotiable. That's the whole point."

Odile studied me for a long moment, rain dripping off her chin, the riders motionless on the ridge like punctuation at the end of a sentence neither of us wanted to finish reading.

"You know," she said, "for a woman who claims to belong to no one, you argue like someone with a great deal to lose."

"I never claimed to belong to no one. Other people claimed it for me. They were wrong."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth that was not quite a smile. She jerked her head toward the gangplank. "Marren Ford is six miles north. Garrison checkpoint. Your friends on the ridge will have wired ahead by noon, so you have until then to become somebody else entirely." She paused, and the not-smile faded into something harder. "And courier—if you're carrying what I think you're carrying, burn whatever papers say who you used to be. Dead governments make poor armor, and living ones make worse."

I climbed the muddy bank with Mira's hand in mine, and behind us the *Merciful Ann* slid back into the brown water, taking my dispatches—my mission, my purpose, the last official version of Ulyana Eastwick—to wherever dead governments' paper goes.

Ahead, six miles north, a checkpoint waited with my name already traveling toward it.

Six miles to invent a new woman. I'd better start walking.
