## 8. The General's Invitation

They send a car for me, which is the first sign that something is wrong. In the two years I have served the Provisional Authority in Vessar, no one has ever sent a car. There is a tram that runs from the interrogation annex to the governor's compound, gray and punctual and smelling of boiled cabbage, and everyone of my rank rides it with their hands folded and their eyes on their boots. A car means either a promotion or a pit. Given the season, I had money on the pit.

The driver doesn't speak to me. He has the particular silence of men who have been instructed not to answer questions they haven't been asked yet. We go through the occupied quarter of Vessarine City, past the shuttered print shops and the café where I once drank coffee that cost three currencies depending on who was asking, and out along the river road toward the General's residence—a former sanatorium the Republic built for its tubercular officers and which the occupation now uses for healthier kinds of consumption.

At the gate they check my papers twice. The second check is new. So is the way the guard looks at me: not at my face but slightly to the left of it, as though the man he's actually inspecting is standing just over my shoulder.

I have spent eleven years reading people for a living. I know what it means when a guard won't look at you directly. It means your file came up this morning and nobody's told you yet.

* * *

General Aldous Reiner receives me in a room that used to be a solarium. The glass ceiling has been sandbagged—there are still partisans with mortars in the hills, though fewer every month—and the light comes down filtered through burlap, brown and dim, like tea gone cold. The General sits behind a desk that was clearly carried up from some other, better office, because it does not match anything else in the room, and he does not ask me to sit.

"Tomas Brandt." He says it like he's checking the spelling.

"Sir."

"You've been with us since the crossing."

"Since the second winter, sir."

"And before that?"

"I was born here, sir. Left at nine. Came back when the army did."

He nods slowly, the way a man nods when he already knows everything you're saying and is simply measuring how much of it you'll volunteer. Reiner is shorter than his reputation. Photographs make him look carved out of something geological; in person he is a compact, ordinary man with a farmer's hands and the eyes of someone who has learned to find weather coming a long way off. That's the trick of him, I think. Everyone expects a monument. What arrives is a man who notices things.

"Do you know why you're here?"

"No, sir."

"Liar," he says pleasantly. "Sit down anyway."

There is one chair. It faces the desk at an angle calculated—I would bet my pension on it—to make a man feel both invited and off balance. I take it. On the desk between us lies a folder, thin, the kind that holds a single report. My name is on the tab. Underneath the folder sits an ashtray shaped like a swan, looted, I'd guess, from a house where someone once thought swans were funny.

Reiner opens the folder, reads a line he surely knows by heart, and closes it again without showing me a word.

"Checkpoint Two," he says. "The river crossing. You flagged a courier. Female, foreign credentials, traveling with a minor. Your agents lost her on the water."

"The barge captain claimed engine trouble. We believe she transferred to a cart on the north bank."

"We believe that too." He lets the *we* hang there a moment, generous as a host pouring wine. "You wrote in your assessment that the courier was likely carrying diplomatic pouch material. Routine. You recommended surveillance rather than interception."

"She had legitimate papers. Interception creates incidents. Incidents create inquiries. Sir."

"Inquiries." He turns the word over like a coin he suspects is counterfeit. Then he opens a drawer, takes out a photograph, and lays it face down beside the swan ashtray. "Tell me about Kelvane Bridge, Brandt."

The room goes very quiet. Outside, somewhere below the sandbags, a crow is arguing with another crow about something neither will win.

"Kelvane Bridge was a security action in the autumn," I say. "A column of refugees failed to halt at a controlled crossing. There was an exchange of fire."

"An exchange." Reiner smiles for the first time, and it is the worst thing I have seen all year, because it is patient. "That's what the communiqué says. Here's what the file says." And he slides the photograph across the desk, turning it face up as it comes.

It's a bridge. I knew it would be, and still my stomach drops, because knowing a thing and seeing it are separated by a distance no training ever closes. The bridge is choked with bodies. Not soldiers. Coats, shawls, a handcart on its side, a child's boot caught upright in the railing as if the owner had been pulled out of it mid-step. The river under the span carries a pale sediment that the photograph renders in grainy gray and that I do not need rendered in any other color.

"This arrived by courier three days ago," Reiner says. "One copy. Our friend from the river made a second before we took the original back." He watches me. He is very good at watching; I recognize the craft the way a locksmith recognizes another man's picks. "You're wondering how I know about the second copy."

"Yes, sir."

"Because the archivist who compiled it kept duplicates the way other women keep buttons. Because her husband talked under interrogation—he lasted four hours, which for an accountant is practically heroic—and told us she'd seeded copies with couriers moving north. And because, Brandt"—and here he leans forward, farmer's hands folded, weather-finding eyes steady—"you flagged a woman at the river crossing whose description matches a courier our own records place in contact with that archivist six weeks ago. You found her. You were holding the thread. And then you recommended *surveillance.*"

I keep my face doing nothing, which is the hardest work I've done in eleven years.

"Sir, if I may. If the objective is recovery of the document, a quiet tail preserves more options than a stop."

"The objective is not recovery of the document." He says it flatly, without drama, which is somehow more terrible. "The document is already recovered. This is the only copy outside this room. The objective is the *courier.*"

I look at the photograph. I make myself look at it properly—the way they train you to look at evidence, systematically, corner to corner—because looking away now would be a confession and I am not ready to confess to anything yet, least of all to myself.

"Every person who has touched this material is dead or will be shortly," Reiner continues, conversational, almost gentle. "The archivist. Her husband. The printer in Halven who set the type. Two couriers. A ferryman." He pauses. "You'll notice the ferryman is on the list, and the ferryman never saw the document. He saw the woman. That is the standard, Brandt. Contact is contamination. You understand."

I understand. God help me, I understand perfectly. He is telling me, in the language of administration, that the list has room on it for a woman with diplomatic papers and a silent child, and that my name sits directly above theirs on the same page.

"And my role, sir?"

"Yours is delicate." He stands, finally, and comes around the desk, and stands looking down through the sandbagged glass at the brown light. "You grew up here. You know these people—their habits, their hiding places, their sentimentality. The courier is using routes only a native would use. I want you to lead the search personally."

"Personally, sir."

"Your agents report to you. You report to me. No intermediaries." He turns. "And Brandt—one more thing. The sketch from Checkpoint One. The one your artist circulated."

My mouth has gone dry. "Sir?"

"It's quite good. Captures her exactly." He taps the closed folder with my name on it. "You recognized her immediately, according to your own field notes. An old acquaintance, your notes say. A childhood friend." He watches me with those weather-finding eyes, and there is nothing in them, nothing at all, and I understand that this is the test and it has been running since the moment I walked in. "In my experience, old friends make the worst hunters and the best bait. But I'm told you're thorough. Prove it. Dismissed."

* * *

The car takes me back along the river road. I don't remember getting into it. I sit with my hands flat on my knees like a schoolboy and watch the water slide by, brown and slow, carrying ice toward the delta, and I do arithmetic.

Here is what I know now that I didn't know at breakfast. Ulyana Eastwick is alive, and she is carrying the Kelvane file, and the General has a copy, and the copy changes nothing, because the point was never the paper. The point is the people. A massacre survives as long as its witnesses do; therefore the occupation's entire strategy, from the archivist down to the ferryman, is a strategy against memory, conducted with ledgers and firing squads and a swan-shaped ashtray. They don't need to suppress the document. They need only to kill everyone who could vouch for it, and then, in ten years, when some foreign journalist asks what happened at Kelvane Bridge, there will be an official communiqué and no one left to contradict it.

History, it turns out, is not written by the victors. It is written by whoever is left standing with a pen. Reiner understands this better than any historian I ever met.

And I am supposed to be his pen.

I think about Ulyana as a girl, because I can't help it, because memory is the one thing they haven't figured out how to requisition. Ulyana at eleven, standing on the breakwater at Sarnholt with the wind flattening her hair, announcing that she would never take sides in anything, that history was a fight between other people's fathers and she intended to spend her life writing it down from a safe distance. She said it so fiercely, so seriously, that I laughed, and she pushed me into the harbor, and I forgave her before I finished drowning.

She believed neutrality was a kind of armor. We both did, in our ways—hers paper, mine uniform. Two children agreeing that the world could be observed without being owed anything.

Now she is on the list. Above the ferryman, above the child, and I am the instrument being sharpened against her, and the General watched me the whole time to see whether the steel trembled.

Did it? That's the question I can't answer honestly, sitting in this car. Eleven years of interrogation teach you every technique of evasion except the one that matters: lying to yourself. When Reiner showed me the bridge, my first thought—for one unguarded half-second, before duty and doctrine came rushing in like bailiffs—was *Ulyana must never see this.* Protect her from the knowledge, protect her from the grief. And my second thought was that protecting Ulyana Eastwick from grief is like protecting the sea from salt, and my third thought—

My third thought was that I had just, in the privacy of my own skull, committed treason. Because wanting her warned is already the crime. The act is only paperwork.

The car stops at the annex. The driver doesn't speak. I climb out into the cold and stand on the curb while the traffic goes by, and I take out my notebook—the little gray one, official issue—and I hold it, and I think: one telephone call. One note left in a dead-drop. One sentence whispered to a clerk I trust, and it travels north faster than any soldier. *They know the routes. They know the faces. Get off the roads.*

Treason, says the oath I signed. Duty, says the oath.

But I stood in that solarium and looked at a bridge full of the dead while a man with a farmer's hands explained that killing witnesses is policy, and I understood something with total clarity, the way you understand fire is hot: whatever the law is now, it isn't justice, and hasn't been for a long time, and the gap between them is exactly wide enough to bury a republic in.

I put the notebook away. There is a tavern two streets from the annex where the barkeep owes me, where the walls are thick and the regulars are deaf by profession. There is a dead-drop in the old customs house that the resistance thinks I don't know about, and I've known about it for a year, and never used it, saving it, the way you save anything you're afraid to want.

Ulyana will be at the third checkpoint within two days. The order will come down tomorrow morning: let her run, take her at the gate, quietly, completely.

Which gives me tonight.

I turn up my collar and start walking, and I tell myself I'm going to think it over, and I know I'm lying, and for the first time in eleven years the lie doesn't fit.
