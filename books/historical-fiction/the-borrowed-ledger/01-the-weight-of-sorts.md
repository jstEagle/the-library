## 1. The Weight of Sorts

The wedding announcement for Master Corvin Hale and Miss Sera Duvall was set to be the finest piece of work the Nakamura press had produced that season, and Jonah Nakamura intended it to be so — not out of love for either party, whom she had never met, but because a job done beautifully was the best argument she knew against the world coming apart.

She stood at the composing stone with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and her father's leather apron cinched at her waist, sliding brass-bodied type into the stick one letter at a time. *The families Hale and Duvall announce with joy the betrothal—* No. That was wrong. The Hales had paid for the grander formulation: *with exceeding joy.* Jonah pulled the offending word back out with her bodkin, dropped it in its case, and set the longer version in its place.

"Exceeding," she muttered. "As though joy were measured by the ounce."

Her mother's voice drifted up from the kitchen below, where the smell of rice and scorched ginger suggested that breakfast was once again an experiment rather than a meal. "Jonah? Is that you talking to the letters?"

"They listen better than people do."

"Then tell them we need more firewood."

"I'll put it in writing."

From the kitchen came her mother's laugh, low and dry as a well-oiled hinge. It was the same laugh Mira Nakamura had given every joke for as long as Jonah could remember — rationed, like everything else in her mother's life, but never withheld entirely.

Jonah returned to the stone. The shop around her was a country unto itself, and she was its cartographer. She could have walked its length blindfolded: the two presses, the big iron hand-press her father had bought secondhand from a bankrupted shipping concern, and the smaller proofing press he'd rebuilt himself the year Tomas was born; the racks of paper along the east wall, wrapped against damp; the ink stones; the drying lines strung overhead like laundry for ghosts. And everywhere, everywhere, the type cases — a hundred shallow wooden drawers fanned across the walls, each divided into compartments, each compartment holding its small metal population: capital A's crowded together in their box like anxious relatives, lowercase e's overflowing because e was always wanted, always needed, always being used up faster than any other letter in creation.

It was the e's that had taught her to read the world.

That was how her mother put it, anyway, when visitors asked where the girl had gotten such strange eyes. Most people looked at a street and saw buildings, carts, faces. Jonah looked at a street and saw sorts — movable pieces, each with a weight, each with a place it belonged, each capable of being lifted out and rearranged into something else entirely. A fishmonger's stall was a case of herring, easily redistributed. A neighbor's argument was a line badly justified, spacing gone uneven, needing only a patient hand with a bodkin to set right. The Merchant Assembly's new customs house, rising on the waterfront with its pale stone and its fat columns, was a very large sort indeed, recently placed, displacing everyone who'd been standing where it now stood.

And soldiers, when they came — if they came — would be sorts too. Heavy ones. The kind that got moved without anyone's consent.

She caught herself doing it again before breakfast was even finished: measuring the house by what could be carried.

This was her mother's arithmetic, learned in another country under circumstances Mira Nakamura did not discuss except after the third glass of plum wine, and even then only in fragments. *Everything in this house,* her mother had told them, again and again, in three languages for emphasis, *you must be able to answer one question about: can it go out the door in ten minutes or not?* Her father, rest him, had found this morbidly funny. "Mira," he'd say, "the man is asking to borrow our ladder, not invade Vellamare." But he'd learned the arithmetic too, in the end, and he'd taught it back to her: a printer's trade, at least, was made of portable things. Paper burned, yes. Presses stayed. But type — type went anywhere. A case of letters weighed less than a child and contained every word ever written or yet to be written. When their father died two winters ago, it was his type cases the family grieved over most tenderly, dusting each drawer, oiling the wood, keeping the brass bright.

Jonah finished the announcement's first paragraph and paused, thumbs pressed into the small of her back, and let her eye do its rounds.

The front door: solid oak, bolted top and bottom, and behind it the long counter where customers waited. The counter could not be carried. Neither could the presses. The paper stock could, if you sacrificed the wrapping shelves and stuffed it into sacks. The cashbox, obviously — that went first, always first, it lived by the door precisely so that it would never have far to travel. The ledgers of accounts, second. The photograph of her father at the press, third, though her mother would argue it should outrank the money and her mother would win, because her mother always won arguments conducted in the space between heartbeats.

And then the type cases. Which ones?

This was the question she turned over most mornings like a coin worn smooth. If it came to flight — and rumor said the docks were restless, that the Assembly's tax collectors had begun arriving with escorts, that men who talked politics in taverns had stopped talking politics in taverns — which cases would she take? The fourteen-point Caslon, surely; it was the family's finest face, the one her father had cut special ligatures for, those joined letter-pairs that made his work recognizable across half the city the way handwriting is recognizable. The italic, certainly, all of it, because italics were for names and names were the first thing worth saving. The ornaments and fleurons — no, those could burn, pretty useless things. The numbers, though. You couldn't leave numbers behind. Dates lived in numbers. Debts lived in numbers. She'd take the numerals over the fleurons without a moment's regret, and she suspected her mother would do the same, and she suspected her mother would never say so aloud, because there were things you didn't teach your children directly, you just arranged the world so they'd figure it out themselves.

"Jonah." Tomas came down the back stairs two at a time, as he did everything, as if the stairs might change their minds about existing. Nineteen years old, built like a capstan, already smelling of tar and salt from his morning shift at the chandlery. He kissed the top of her head, smearing faintly of rope, and peered at the composing stick over her shoulder. "Who's getting married?"

"Hale and Duvall. Exceeding joy."

"Exceeding." He snorted. "Old man Hale owns three warehouses and a grudge against every dockworker in Vellamare. Joy will exceed itself the day he pays a fair wage."

"Then perhaps the announcement is aspirational."

Tomas grinned and stole a slice of bread off the plate their mother had sent up, chewing as he wandered the shop with the proprietary air of a man inspecting a ship. He stopped at the window and looked out at the street, and Jonah watched his shoulders do a thing they'd been doing more often lately — settling, bracing, the posture of a man listening for weather that hadn't arrived.

"Docks were quiet today," he said, too casually.

"That's good."

"Mm. Quiet like a held breath." He turned. "There's talk the Assembly's doubling the harbor levies. And there's a fellow at the Chandlery Arms saying collectors came to Saltgate Wharf yesterday wearing sidearms. Sidearms, Jonah. For collecting taxes on dried cod."

"People say a lot of things at the Chandlery Arms."

"People said things at the Chandlery Arms last spring too, and then the Assembly arrested the whole fish-cutters' brotherhood in a single night." He said it lightly enough, but his hand had found the doorframe and gripped it. "Mama won't hear it. I asked her again this morning. I said, Mama, inland is four days' walk and Auntie Yara has the farmhouse, and she looked at me like I'd suggested selling Papa's bones."

"She won't leave the shop."

"She won't leave the shop," Tomas agreed, and the two of them stood in the middle of it together, surrounded by everything that could and couldn't be carried, while downstairs their mother hummed something old in a language neither of them spoke fluently anymore but both understood perfectly when she was sad in it.

"Well," Jonah said at last, because someone in the family had to be practical, "if soldiers come, you can carry the big press."

"The big press weighs as much as a horse."

"Yes. But you're showy, and it would impress them."

He threw a wad of waste paper at her head. She ducked; it scattered across the floor, and she gathered the sheets up afterward without thinking, sorting them by size the way she sorted everything, because waste paper was still paper and paper was still worth something, and a house that kept track of its own weight kept track of itself.

* * *

Midmorning brought customers, which meant coin, which meant the day improved.

First came Mistress Okonkwo from the candle shop, wanting fifty price cards, who paid promptly and complained cheerfully about the cost of tallow and the cost of everything else, "which is to say the Assembly, dear, because everything expensive in this city has the Assembly's fingerprints on it somewhere." Jonah agreed diplomatically and quoted her a fair price and did not write the woman's opinions down anywhere, though she noted privately that Mistress Okonkwo's voice had dropped on the word *Assembly*, the way voices in Vellamare were learning to drop.

Then came a boy with a note from the harbormaster's office requesting two hundred copies of the new mooring regulations, payment on delivery, and Jonah read the regulations twice and found them reasonable, which worried her more than if she'd found them outrageous. Reasonable regulations, she had learned, were often the prelude to unreasonable enforcement — the soft word before the hard knock.

And then, near noon, came Rell.

Rell was sixteen, the apprentice, all elbows and ink stains, with a cowlick that defeated every comb in the household and a talent for composition that made Jonah quietly jealous. His hands knew the cases the way musicians' hands knew strings. He'd been with them two years now, sent by a cousin of his late father, and he was diligent and quick and funny in a shy way, and his brother served in the Assembly's harbor watch, and none of that was strange, because half the city had a brother or a cousin or a debt entangled with the Assembly these days. You couldn't hold it against a boy whose family ate.

"You've got a smudge," Jonah told him, nodding at his cheek, "the size of Vellamare."

"It's a beauty mark."

"It's a county."

Rell scrubbed at it and failed, and took up his place at the second stone, where he began setting the smaller notices — births, funerals, a lost dog answering to the name Admiral ("a dog named Admiral," Rell said, delighted, "what happened, did the crew mutiny?"). The shop settled into its working rhythm, the comfortable clatter of metal and breath, two pairs of hands building sentences out of lead.

It was, Jonah thought later, the kind of morning she would remember. Not because anything happened in it, but because nothing did. Because the light came through the south windows and lay in warm bars across the type racks, and the smell was ink and wood polish and her mother's ginger drifting up the stairs, and the worst news of the day concerned a dog.

She was locking up the chase on the wedding announcement — wedges driven tight, the whole block of type firm as masonry — when her fingers found the ligature.

*fi.* Father's cut. He'd carved the joined letters himself, decades ago, because he said the standard fi looked like a man falling downstairs, and his ligatures had become the quiet signature of the Nakamura press. Every printer in the district knew them. Jonah ran her thumb over the little brass body, feeling the nub of the maker's mark her father had scratched into the side of every custom piece, and felt the usual ache, familiar now as an old scar, and set the thought aside with the practiced motion of a woman who had other work to do.

"Rell. Look at this and tell me if the justification's true."

He leaned over the chase, lips moving as he read, and nodded. "True as Sunday. Old man Hale will weep with exceeding joy."

"One hopes. Exceeding joy is expensive; I charged accordingly."

They laughed, the two of them, in the lamplit shop full of ten thousand letters, and upstairs the floorboards creaked as their mother moved through the rooms above, and outside in the street a cart rolled past toward the harbor carrying — Jonah noticed, because she noticed everything, because her mother had trained her to notice everything — carrying crates stenciled with the Assembly's crest, and drawn by hired men who did not look like hirelings but like guards, and going not to the customs house but to the records annex, where no goods had any business being delivered.

She watched the cart out of sight. Then she went back to her type.

But that evening, when she locked the shop, she did something she hadn't done in months. She counted the type cases twice, and chose, silently, in the privacy of her own head, which six would go out the door first.

And it was the very next dusk, with the announcement delivered and paid for and Master Hale's exceeding joy presumably underway, that the courier came to the door wearing Assembly colors, carrying a letter under seal.
