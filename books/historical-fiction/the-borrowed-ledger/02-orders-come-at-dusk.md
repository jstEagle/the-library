## 2. Orders Come at Dusk

The trouble with rumors, my father used to say, is that they set faster than lead.

He'd meant it as a joke about the docks — how a story could travel from a sailor's mouth to a fishwife's ears to a magistrate's desk in less time than it took Jonah to lock up a chase of type properly. But lately she thought about it differently. Lately everything in Vellamare seemed to be setting itself into some new shape without anyone's permission, and no proofreader anywhere with the authority to pull the whole page apart and start again.

Jonah stood at the stone sink behind the shop, scrubbing ink from her knuckles, watching the harbor through the window that had gone cloudy with salt age. The evening tide was coming in, carrying with it three merchantmen and, apparently, an entire day's worth of bad news, because the dockworkers unloading them kept stopping to talk, and the talking had that particular quality — hands moving fast, heads turning toward the Assembly Hall on its hill — that meant whatever they were saying was not about cargo.

"Jonah!" Tomas's voice from the front of the shop, pitched in the register he used when he wanted to sound like he wasn't panicking, which was the register he only ever used when he was. "Customer!"

"We close in ten minutes."

"He says it's urgent."

"Everyone who comes here at dusk says it's urgent. Last week urgent was a man who needed four hundred funeral notices by morning because his uncle couldn't decide to stay dead until after the weekend."

But she dried her hands and went out anyway, because Tomas had that look — the one where his jaw was set but his eyes were asking questions — and she'd learned years ago that when the two of those appeared together, someone on the docks had said something worth hearing.

The customer was a stevedore named Brannoc, built like one of the pilings he worked around, currently holding his cap in both hands and wringing it like laundry. He glanced at Tomas. He glanced at the door. He glanced at the press, which stood in the corner like a sleeping ox, and then leaned across the counter as though the words themselves might need to be smuggled.

"They're calling back the Harbor Watch," he said. "All of them. Off the piers, up to the Assembly quarter."

"That's not so strange. There's always some—"

"It's not for a festival, miss. They're pulling them *in*. And the customs men were told to stop issuing permits past Friday." He swallowed. "And there's a list."

"A list?"

"Of names. Being read out at the harbormaster's office. I didn't hear mine on it," he added quickly, "but I heard enough others that I stopped standing where I could hear it, if you take me."

Jonah took him. She took him all the way down to the cold place behind her sternum where such news tended to settle, like silt.

"What do we owe you?" she asked. "For coming by?"

Brannoc blinked. "I hadn't — I mean, I've no job for you. I just thought you'd want—" He stopped, and something shifted in his weathered face, and instead of finishing he reached into his coat and put two coins on the counter, copper, worn nearly smooth. "For the wedding announcements. For old Nakamura's girl. You did good work, him and you. Somebody ought to say it while it can still be said out loud."

Then he put his cap on, ducked his head, and left, walking quickly, not running — nobody ran anymore, that drew the eye — but walking with the particular speed of a man who has somewhere else he urgently needs to not be.

Tomas looked at the coins. Jonah looked at the coins.

"He paid *us*," Tomas said.

"He paid us in warnings," Jonah said. "That's the third this week. Mistress Corvel gave me a discount on her order and told me not to walk home by the Customs House. Old Piet brought eggs and mentioned that his nephew saw soldiers measuring the width of the bridge gates." She turned the coppers over. "Coin's getting strange, Tom. It still spends, but it doesn't feel like money anymore."

* * *

Their mother came down at dusk, as she always did, to bank the stove and check the type cases, and found them both standing at the counter not working, which in the Nakamura household constituted an emergency.

Mira Nakamura was a small woman who moved through the shop the way water moves through a ship — everywhere at once, touching everything, holding everything up. She had ink permanently shadowed under her fingernails in a way that no amount of pumice would ever fully lift, and she wore her gray hair pinned with a composing stick, because she said hairpins rolled away and composing sticks stayed where you put them. She looked at her children. She looked at the coins.

"Bad news?" she asked.

"Rumors," said Tomas.

"Worse than rumors," said Jonah. "Paid-for rumors."

Their mother picked up the coppers, held one to the lamp as though reading a date on it, and set them both in the dish where honest money lived. Then she untied her apron, folded it over her arm, and sat down on the stool by the stove with the air of a woman settling in for a long siege.

"Tell me all of it," she said. "Slowly. In order. Your father always said fear gets worse when you let it set out of order."

So they told her: the Harbor Watch recalled, the permits ending, the names being read at the harbormaster's office, the soldiers at the bridge gates with their measuring strings. Mira listened with her hands flat on her knees, and Jonah watched her mother's face do what it always did with bad news, which was nothing at all — go smooth and still as a locked case, every sort in its compartment.

"The Saltgate families have been grumbling since spring," their mother said at last. "Grain tariffs. The press gangs. The Assembly taxed the fish catch twice and called the second tax a 'harbor improvement.'" Something dry flickered at the corner of her mouth. "I have improved no harbors. Have either of you?"

"No, Mother."

"Then perhaps the Assembly should print fewer promises and pave more streets." She said it lightly, but her eyes didn't lighten with it, and Jonah understood that the joke was armor, and that underneath the armor their mother was counting things. She was always counting. Every jar in the pantry, every coin in the till, every stick of furniture in the house above — measured, weighed, assessed against some private ledger whose units were not crowns but *carrying*.

What could be carried. That was how Mira Nakamura had taught them to see the world. Not what a thing cost, or what it was worth, or what it meant to you — but whether, if the knock came at the door in the night, you could get it out into the dark and down the road before the door came off its hinges. The good spoons: yes, wrapped in a towel, forty seconds. The walnut chest: no. Father's portrait: yes, small, flat, slides between the mattress and the frame. The big press: God help them, no.

Jonah had grown up thinking this was simply how mothers thought. She had been perhaps twelve before she understood that other children's parents spoke of *someday* and *savings*, and hers spoke of *routes* and *what fits in a satchel*, and that the difference between them had a name, and the name was the country her mother had come from, which no longer existed except in Mira's accent and a single wooden type case that traveled with the family like a dowry, or a wound.

"Tomas," their mother said now, "you've been chewing on something since Brannoc left. Spit it out before it swallows you."

Tomas looked at Jonah. Jonah looked at the stove. Coward, she told herself, and made herself look too, because if their brother was finally going to say it, he deserved witnesses.

"I think we should go inland," Tomas said.

The words landed in the room and lay there.

"To Aunt Serene's, in Marrowfield. Or further, if we must. While the roads are still open, while we still have coin, while—" He stopped and started again, quieter. "Mother. There are lists. Names being read out loud. We're printers. We've printed pamphlets for the dock committees, the grain petition, the — Mother, our type has touched half the seditious paper in this city, and everyone knows where we sleep."

"Our type has touched wedding announcements too," their mother said.

"And funeral notices! That's the point, that's exactly the point — we print *everything*, for *everyone*, and right now that's a fine thing to be and in six months it will be a hanging thing to be, and I would rather we be alive and boring in Marrowfield than dead and interesting here."

It was the most words Tomas had strung together about anything since he'd argued with the priest about tithes, and Jonah felt something twist in her chest, because the worst part was that he was right. She could see it laid out in front of her the way she saw a page before it was set — clean, logical, line after justified line. Flee. Go inland. Wait out the storm. Come back when the printing was safe again.

Their mother let the silence sit for a moment. Then she rose, went to the shelf above the sink, and took down the small wooden box that no one touched. Inside it, Jonah knew, was a letter — old, yellowed, sealed with wax that had been broken and broken again over thirty years of being looked at. Their mother had never read it aloud to them. She had only ever said, once, when Jonah was small and bold enough to ask: *A long time ago, someone sent my family a letter that said we were enemies of people we had never met. By the time we understood what the letter meant, the road was already closed.*

She didn't bring the box down now. She just rested her hand near it, the way you'd rest a hand near a scar.

"When your father bought this shop," Mira said, "the roof leaked in two places and the press had a cracked bed that every repairman in Vellamare said was beyond saving. Everyone told him to buy the shop on Cordwainer Street instead. Newer. Better district. Safer." She turned. "Your father walked into this one, put his palm flat on the press like it was a horse he was gentling, and said, *This one has work left in it.*"

"Mother—"

"Your father died at that counter." Her voice did not waver; it simply got very precise, each word set and spaced like display type. "Not dramatically. His heart, at the end of an ordinary Tuesday, with the forms for the almanac half-locked and waiting for him. I found him with a quoin key still in his hand." She looked at Tomas, and then at Jonah, and her voice softened by one degree, which from Mira was a flood. "This shop is where he worked. Where he laughed. It is the last place in the world his hands were. And you are asking me to measure it — measure *him* — by what can be carried."

"He isn't here, Mother," Tomas said, and his voice cracked on it. "The shop is wood and iron. *We* are what's left of him, and I'd like to keep us."

Jonah watched her mother absorb that blow — watched it land, watched her take it, because it deserved to be taken honestly — and then watched her, infuriatingly, impossibly, stand straighter under it.

"Yes," Mira said. "You are what's left of him. All three of you, and this shop besides, because your father's hands are in every crack in that press bed, and his eye is in every punctuation cut in those cases, and I will not run ahead of the knocking." She held up a finger before Tomas could speak. "*But.* Hear me. If it comes to soldiers at the door — real soldiers, with the lists in their fists — then we go, and we go that night, and I will not argue with any of you, and you will not argue with me. The shop can burn. The press can rust. What cannot be replaced goes in the cases, and the cases go out the window, and we live to print another day. That is the bargain. Staying is not the same as dying, and leaving is not the same as being saved. Do we understand each other?"

Tomas's jaw worked. "And if staying is what kills us?"

"Then we will have died in our own house," their mother said, "with our own type in our own hands, and I find — I find, after everything — that I can live with dying better than I can live with being *carried off*." A ghost of her old dryness surfaced. "Besides. Someone in this city must keep setting the wedding announcements. Imagine the scandal."

Despite everything, despite the lists and the recalled Watch and the cold place behind her sternum, Jonah laughed — a short, cracked sound, but a laugh. Tomas didn't laugh, but the fury went out of his shoulders by half a degree, which from Tomas was surrender, provisionally, until the next battle.

Their mother banked the stove. Tomas climbed the stairs to the rooms above, his boots saying everything his mouth wouldn't. And Jonah stood a moment longer at the front window, looking out at the street where the lamps were being lit one by one, each flame a small stubborn argument against the dark.

Across the harbor, the Assembly Hall glowed on its hill, all its windows lit, burning through candles at a rate that meant clerks working late. Working late on what? On lists? On orders?

On pages, Jonah thought. Everything, in the end, came down to pages. Somewhere up there, someone was setting the next chapter of Vellamare in whatever type they pleased, and no one had been consulted, and no proofreader would dare mark it.

She turned down the lamp and stood a moment in the dark, listening to the tide come in, measuring the room behind her without meaning to — press, too heavy; cases, eight of them, heavy but possible; mother, non-negotiable; brother, furious but portable —

— and that was when she heard the knock at the front door. Three slow raps, deliberate, unhurried, in the deep of dusk when no honest customer came.

Through the glass, by the light of the street lamp, she could see a figure holding something out toward the shop. An envelope. Sealed. And on the flap, catching the lamplight — a crest she knew from the headers of a hundred official broadsheets they'd printed over the years, pressed into red wax.

Nobody official delivered letters by hand after dark. Nobody official knocked like that.

Jonah's hand found the door latch, and found, to her surprise, that it was shaking.
