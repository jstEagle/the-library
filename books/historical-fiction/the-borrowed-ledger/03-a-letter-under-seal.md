## 3. A Letter Under Seal

The morning the letter came, Jonah was arguing with a comma.

It sat in her composing stick like a small stubborn animal, and she glared at it over her loupe while the shop smelled of ink and yesterday's coffee and the particular warm-dust sweetness that lived in the type cabinets the way it had lived there since before she was born. The comma in question belonged to Signor and Signora Bellandi's wedding announcement, which was due at the Herald's office by noon, and which currently read:

*Together with their families, the Bellandi family requests the honour of your presence at the marriage of their daughter Lucia to Marco Ferro, merchant of good standing.*

Merchant of good standing. Signor Bellandi had insisted on the phrase, twice, and paid for it in coin, which lately made him nearly unique among their customers.

"He means himself," Tomas had said last night, chin propped on his fist at the kitchen table. "Good standing. His own standing. He wants everyone reading it to know his daughter isn't marrying down."

"He wants the *Ferro* family to know it," Jonah said. "Which is worse, because they'll know he's worried about it."

Their mother had said nothing at all, which from Mira Nakamura was a whole paragraph.

Now, in the grey-gold light coming through the front windows — light that caught the hanging rows of dried peppers and the dust motes above the press and turned even the shop's shabbiness into something almost holy — Jonah considered the comma. It sat after *families*, where no comma should sit, because whoever had set this line before her — and it had been set before her; the announcement had come back once already for corrections, which meant someone at the Bellandi house had opinions about typography stronger than their sense of grammar — had put a comma there, and the correction proof had come back with the comma *still there*, underlined, as though the Bellandis were defending it personally.

She could take it out. She could leave it in. Her father had taught her that a printer's first loyalty was to the reader, but her mother had taught her that a printer's first loyalty was to staying alive, and these days those two lessons pulled in directions Jonah didn't always have the strength to chart.

In the end she took it out, slid the corrected line into the chase, locked the form, and felt the small deep satisfaction that came from imposing order on one square inch of the world. It was a satisfaction she rationed carefully now. Order in one square inch was about all Vellamare could guarantee anybody.

* * *

Her father used to say the city was a page that somebody kept resetting without telling the printers. He'd said it laughing, usually, usually with wine. He had been dead four years, drowned when the ferry *Cormorant* went down in a squall with eleven others, and Jonah still caught herself turning to tell him things — the comma, mostly, the comma would have made him laugh — before remembering, and the remembering never got lighter, only more practiced.

What he would have made of this season, she couldn't imagine. The docks had gone strange the way a face goes strange before crying. Cargo lay unclaimed on the quay because the Assembly had levied a new tariff and the harbor guilds had answered by simply — not. Not loading, not paying, not showing up. Soldiers drilled in the Customs House yard each morning, and each morning a few more of them than the day before. And the customers — the customers had changed in a way Jonah felt in her hands before she understood it in her head. They stopped paying in coin and started paying in looks: a glance toward the door, a lowered voice, an envelope of banknotes handled like contraband. Old Widow Sereno had come in Tuesday for funeral notices — her third husband, poor man, and Jonah had printed all three with equal tenderness — and pressed Jonah's hand across the counter and said, "Keep your shutters closed after dark, girl," and would not explain, and left.

Tomas wanted them gone entirely. He said so at breakfast, every breakfast, with the tireless devotion of a man who had found one idea and intended to marry it.

"Inland," he'd say, gesturing with his spoon as if inland were a place you could point to from a kitchen. "Past the terraces. Aunt Sefia's village has room. We lock the shop, we go, we wait out whatever this is."

"And open what, when we come back?" their mother asked, not looking up from her mending. "A shop with no shop in it?"

"The press—"

"Is bolted to the floor through beams your father laid himself." Mira bit her thread. "You may go, Tomás. You are a grown man with strong legs. I will help you pack."

It was not an invitation anyone took, because nobody ever did. That was the shape of their mornings: Tomas proposing flight, Mira declining it in the passive voice, and Jonah between them with the coffee pot, pouring, refilling, being the territory on which the argument was conducted. She had begun to think of herself less as a sister than as a border.

* * *

The courier came at mid-morning, which was itself wrong.

Couriers did not come to the Nakamura shop at mid-morning. Couriers came at closing time, or at dawn, or during the lunch hour when the street was empty — the Assembly's runners had a genius for arriving when witnesses were scarce, and everyone in the print quarter knew it the way farmers know weather. But this one came at ten o'clock with the sun full on the street and the fishwife shouting her prices two doors down, and he stood in the doorway in Assembly grey with a leather dispatch case and the flat courtesy of a man delivering something unpleasant on schedule.

"Nakamura?" he said, though the sign above his head said NAKAMURA & SONS in letters her father had cut himself, and beneath that, smaller, in Mira's hand-painted addition, *& Daughters, Obviously* — a joke from better years that now hung in the window like a small act of defiance nobody had the heart to take down.

"That's us," Jonah said. She was wiping ink from her fingers and made herself do it slowly. "Which one?"

"There's only one."

"There's three of us."

He looked at her the way men looked at arithmetic they resented. Then he drew out an envelope — heavy paper, cream-colored, sealed with wax the color of dried blood — and a second, flatter package beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with official twine. He held out a ledger board for signature.

Jonah signed. Her hand wrote *J. Nakamura* in the neat slanted script her father had drilled into her at age six — *your name is the one piece of type you carry everywhere, little one, guard its kerning* — and the act of writing it in an Assembly courier's book felt suddenly like setting her own name in someone else's forme, to be printed at their pleasure, for purposes unknown.

The courier left. His boots went down the street at an unhurried clip. Somewhere the fishwife was still shouting about sardines, and the ordinary world went on making its ordinary noise, and Jonah stood holding two objects that seemed to pull heat into themselves like stones off a forge.

"Tomas," she called, and was proud that her voice came out level.

* * *

They opened it at the big worktable, the one scarred with forty years of nicks and burns, the one with the brass hook where her father used to hang his apron. Mira came in from the back with her sleeves rolled and her hands still wet from the garden tap, and when she saw the crest on the seal she went very still, and then she went very busy, drying her hands with tremendous thoroughness, taking her time, buying seconds the way other people bought bread.

The crest was the Merchant Assembly's: the scales and the ship, embossed, with the motto *ORDO ET FIDES* curled beneath in a ribbon. Ordo et fides. Order and fidelity. Jonah had set those words a hundred times for Assembly broadsheets back when the contracts came honestly, before the tariffs and the crackdowns, and she knew their weight in lead better than most priests knew their psalms.

"Break it," Mira said quietly. "Seals don't keep. Only knowing does."

Tomas broke it with his thumbnail. Inside were two documents.

The first was a letter, typeset, on Assembly stock. Jonah read it aloud, because her voice was steadier than her hands and because saying a thing out loud sometimes showed you whether it was real:

*"To the household of Nakamura, printers: Let it be known that by testimony gathered under lawful authority, the persons herein named — Mira Nakamura, widow; Jonah Nakamura, printer; Tomás Nakamura, laborer — are recorded as signatories to a compact of sedition against the lawful commerce and governance of Vellamare, entered into on the night of the ninth of the Harvest month, at the house of one E. Vane, archivist, dismissed. Evidence is enclosed. You are required to present yourselves at the Hall of Scales within fourteen days to answer these charges. Failure to appear shall be taken as confession, and proceedings shall follow accordingly."*

The room was very quiet. Outside, the sardine woman had moved on; a cart rattled past; life continued its shameless business.

"E. Vane," Tomas said finally. "Do we know an E. Vane?"

"No," Jonah said. "And we weren't anywhere on the ninth. Mama was sick; I sat up with her. You were at the docks till dawn."

"I know where I was," Tomas said. "I'm saying it doesn't matter where we were. That's the point of it not mattering."

"It matters to *me*," Jonah snapped, and immediately wished she hadn't, because her voice cracked on *me*, and fear that shows is fear that spreads. She took a breath. "I'm sorry. Read the second one."

The second document was longer, and it was the one that made the cold arrive.

It was titled, in a bold centered heading, *CONFESSION OF SIGNATORIES TO THE COMPACT OF SALTGATE*, and beneath it ran three paragraphs in which the Nakamura family confessed, in careful legal language, to conspiring with dockworkers' delegates and a dismissed archivist to seize the customs houses, burn the grain ledgers, and invite foreign ships into the harbor against the Assembly — a confession dated, witnessed, and signed with three names that were almost theirs.

Almost.

Jonah read it twice. The first time she read it as a person, with her stomach rising and her mouth dry. The second time she read it as a printer, and that was when the cold came, the true cold, the kind that starts behind the sternum and moves outward with terrible patience.

"Mama," she said. "Look at the ampersand."

Mira leaned over. Tomas looked too, uncomprehending; Tomas could set a straight line of text if watched closely, but the finer organs of the craft were Jonah's inheritance.

"In the second paragraph," Jonah said. "'Customs houses *and* grain stores.' Look at the crossbar of the ampersand. See how it dips? There's a nick in the lower bowl, right side. A chip in the metal. Our ampersand. Number thirty-four in the italic case, father's casting, the one he dropped the winter the roof leaked. It's been chipped for nine years and every job we've run since carries that nick, and I've cursed it on every one, and—" She stopped. Made herself breathe. "And here it is. In a document we never set. On paper we've never bought. With ink I'd swear is Grebe's house black."

She put her finger on the page, under the ampersand, and her finger was not quite steady.

"It's our ligature in 'confession' too," she went on, faster now, the words tumbling the way they did when she was frightened and hiding it inside expertise. "The *ss*. Father cut that pair custom because the foundry's standard ss sat too wide for his eye. Nobody else in Vellamare has it. Maybe nobody else in the province. It's — Mama, it's like finding our door key in a stranger's pocket."

Mira had not spoken. She stood with both hands flat on the table, looking down at the forged confession, and her face had done something Jonah had seen only twice before: it had gone smooth, all expression drawn inward like water receding before a wave, leaving behind a surface too calm to be calm.

"When was the ninth of the Harvest month?" Mira asked. Her voice was mild. Mildness, in their mother, was a weather warning.

"Eleven days ago," Tomas said.

"Who has been in the shop in eleven days?"

Jonah made herself think. It hurt to think; her mind kept sliding off the question like a proof sliding off a damp tympan. "Customers. The usual ones. Widow Sereno. The Ferro boy picked up the engagement notice. Rell swept the cases Tuesday and Wednesday, he's been — Rell's been here every day, he lives half here anyway—"

She heard herself say it and stopped.

Rell. Fifteen years old, quick and quiet, apprentice going on three years, whose brother Dario wore Assembly grey and had worn it since the boy was twelve, and who had learned to set type with a patience that had always struck Jonah as slightly older than fifteen. Rell, who knew every case, every quoin, every idiosyncratic cut in the shop — including, certainly including, the chipped ampersand and the custom ss, because Jonah herself had shown them to him as a teaching curiosity, the way you'd show a child a birthmark. *These are ours. No one else has these. Learn to see them.*

"Someone with clean hands set that page," she heard herself say, in a voice she didn't recognize. "Someone who knows our furniture. Someone who could get in without breaking anything, because nothing *was* broken, look — the case slides are aligned, the ink stone's been rinsed. Whoever it was worked here like they belonged here."

"Don't," Tomas said.

"I'm not saying—"

"You're saying it with your whole face."

"I'm saying someone did it, Tomás! Someone stood at my father's stone and set our names into a lie, and used the cuts he made with his own hands, and either they climbed through a window like a thief or they walked in through the door like family, and I need it to be a thief. Do you understand? I need it to be a thief, because a thief I can bar against."

The cart had passed. The street noise filled the pause like water fills a hull.

Mira lifted the confession page closer to the light. For a long moment she studied it, and Jonah realized her mother wasn't reading the words; she was reading the *work* — the spacing, the justification, the slight uneven pressure where a roller had been rocked by someone not yet fully grown.

"This was set fast," Mira said at last. "Under time. The word spaces are tight in the third line and loose in the fifth; a practiced hand under hurry. And the impression is uneven — lighter at the foot of the page. Whoever worked the press didn't brace it properly." She set the page down. "Our press braces against the north wall. Anyone who grew up at this press braces it without thinking. This person didn't grow up here. But they've been taught here."

The logic closed around Rell like a trap, and Jonah hated how neatly it fit, and hated more that some cold practical part of her — the part her mother had built, the part that measured everything by what could be carried — noted that it also fit half a dozen other people, and that fear was a poor compositor, apt to set panic wherever truth should go.

"We present ourselves at the Hall of Scales," Tomas said. "We answer the charges. We say: we were home, ask anyone—"

"Ask whom?" Mira said gently. "The Harbor Guard has lists. The lists will say what they're told to say." She touched the forged confession once, lightly, the way you'd touch a wound to learn its depth. "This paper is not an accusation, my loves. It is a *sentence* wearing an accusation's clothes. Fourteen days is not a deadline for us. It is a deadline for them — time enough for whatever they mean to do in this city, with our names pre-printed to hang it on."

Jonah looked at the confession again — at the chipped ampersand, the borrowed ss, the three signatures in a hand that mimicked hers well enough to fool a clerk and not well enough to fool a sister — and felt the fear change shape. It stopped being a cold and became a resolve, hard and narrow, like a quoin tightened.

Someone was printing with their hands. Someone was writing them into history — the wrong history, the convenient one, the kind that gets typeset before the blood dries so no one ever has to ask afterward. Well. Her father had raised a printer. And a printer knew better than almost anyone alive that ink was never final. Ink could be reset. Every page, no matter how official its crest, was only type in a chase, and type could be broken apart and set again.

Fourteen days.

She was still standing there, the confession in her hands, when the knock came at the door — three raps, soft, deliberate, nothing like the courier's official rap — and a voice from the street, low and roughened, said:

"Signora Nakamura? My name is Elias Vane. I believe you've recently received mail with my name in it. I'd very much like to come in before someone follows me here — and someone will."

Mira and Jonah looked at each other across the table, across the forged confession, across fourteen days.

Then Mira went to unbolt the door.
