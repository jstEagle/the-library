## 5. Ligatures Don't Lie

The confession lay on the composing stone between them like something dead that hadn't finished dying.

Jonah had not slept. She had lit every lamp in the shop at some point during the night and then, embarrassed by the extravagance, put most of them out again, so that by dawn the place had the atmosphere of a chapel after a very anxious service. Elias Vane sat on the apprentice's stool with his long legs folded up like a compass, turning the forged page toward the light and then away from it, as though it might behave differently when unobserved.

"You're certain," he said. Not a question, really. A man hoping to be contradicted.

"I'm certain the way you're certain about your own handwriting." Jonah pulled her father's specimen book from under the counter — a fat, grease-spotted volume where Haruki Nakamura had printed every quirk of his craft for thirty years, because he believed a printer should know his own face better than his own reflection. "Look. I'll show you. But first, tea. You've been sitting there all night looking like a man being audited."

"I have been audited," Elias said. "Twice. It's how they got me to resign the first time."

"Then you know the posture." She set the kettle on the small stove behind the type racks, the one her father had installed so that no proofing session ever had to end merely because someone was cold or stubborn. In this shop, arguments were conducted over tea or not at all.

While the water grumbled, Jonah laid out her evidence the way she'd been taught to lay out a job: everything in its place, nothing accidental.

* * *

"Every punch-cutter has habits," she said, when Elias had a cup cupped in both hands and steam rising into his gray-stubbled face. "Most of them don't even know what their habits are. My father knew his. He used to say a typeface is a confession you make ten thousand times without noticing."

She opened the specimen book to a page near the back, marked with a ribbon gone soft as cloth from handling. "Here. The semicolon."

Elias leaned in. "It looks like a semicolon."

"It looks like my father's semicolon, which is a different animal entirely." She set the forged confession beside it. "See the dot above the tail? Everyone cuts the dot round. He cut it slightly oval, tilted left — he said it gave the mark 'a sense of hesitation.' Which is exactly the sort of thing a man says when he's been cutting semicolons alone for twenty years and has started having opinions about them."

A ghost of a smile crossed Elias's face and was immediately arrested for loitering. "And the forgery?"

"Tilted left. Oval. Hesitant." She turned pages. "Now the question mark. Look at the curve of the hook — see how it doesn't quite close? There's a gap at the top, thin as a hair. That's not damage. That's the cut itself. He dropped the graver once, in — Mother says it was the year of the dock strike — and instead of recutting the punch, he decided the flaw gave the mark 'a sense of inquiry.' My father could find a philosophy in any mistake he didn't feel like fixing."

"Inquiry," Elias repeated softly.

"Now look at the confession's question marks." She laid the two pages side by side, angled toward the lamp. "Same gap. Same hair-thin opening at the same point in the curve. And here—" she tapped a line near the bottom, where the forged signatories had pledged their names to sedition in beautifully regular columns, "—the ampersand. Most founders use the Roman form. My father cut a hybrid, half Roman, half italic, because he couldn't decide and refused to ask anyone's opinion. There are maybe three presses in Vellamare with that ampersand. Two of them bought their sorts from him. This one was cut by him — see the little spur on the lower bowl? Only exists on the original punches. Which live—"

"—in that case," Elias said, "behind you."

"Which lives in that case," Jonah agreed, "behind me. Which has been locked since the night he died, except that locks in this shop are a courtesy, not a promise. Rell has the key. Tomas has a key. I have two keys because I lost one and found it and kept both out of spite."

The kettle shrieked. Jonah got up to tend it, grateful for a moment when her hands had an honest job and her mind could run ahead of her mouth. But the mind kept arriving at the same place, and the place was ugly.

"So," she said, pouring. "Either someone stole our father's punches, which would require knowing they existed, where they were kept, and why the second drawer sticks — or someone used them here, in this shop, on this press, at night, and carried the confession out through a door that four people can open."

Elias set down his cup. "You're counting your apprentice among the four."

"I'm counting everyone among the four. That's the arithmetic." She heard how flat her voice had gone and disliked it. "Rell's brother wears an Assembly livery, Elias. Second Secretary's office. Rell told us that himself in his first week, like an honest man declaring contraband at customs. And now a document comes out of this shop accusing my family of treason, set in my father's type, with my father's hesitant semicolons, and I'm supposed to notice everything except the obvious?"

"Is it obvious?"

"It's obvious the way a bruise is obvious. It's also the shape of exactly what someone wanted me to see." She rubbed her eyes with the heels of her hands. "That's what frightens me. If Rell did it, he's a traitor and I've been feeding him and teaching him and laughing at his terrible jokes for two years. And if Rell didn't do it, then someone wants me to believe he did, which means they know this shop well enough to frame within a frame, and that is worse. Much worse."

Elias was quiet for a while. Outside, the street was waking in the wrong way — too few carts, too many boots, a fishwife arguing with someone in the low urgent voice people used now instead of shouting. The city had begun speaking in whispers the way a sickroom does.

"Show me the spacing," he said at last. "You printers read a page the way I read a ledger. Show me what else it says."

* * *

What else it said took the rest of the morning.

Jonah walked him through it piece by piece, and the more she showed, the stranger the document became. The justification of the lines was too perfect — every right edge true as a drawn knife — which meant the compositor had taken enormous care, or had all the time in the world, or both. The word spacing tightened almost imperceptibly in the final paragraph, the classic signature of a compositor running short of a letter and squeezing to finish without unlocking the form.

"He ran out of e's," Jonah said, tapping the last lines. "Or t's. See how the words huddle together here? Nobody plans that. You plan margins, you plan leading, but you run out of letters because you're working fast and alone and you don't want to stop and redistribute the case."

"Fast and alone," Elias said. "At night."

"At night, in a hurry, using a locked case he knew how to open, on a press he knew how to feed." She looked at the window, where morning had arrived gray and unwelcome. "And here's the part I keep circling. The quads. Look at the line ends."

Elias squinted. "They're... larger than the others?"

"They're mine." Her stomach said it before she could stop it. "Father cut a set of oversized quads his last year — bad eyes, wanted furniture he could find by touch in the dark. There are eleven of them. Eleven. I could count them asleep. Four of them are in this form." She laughed, and it came out with no humor in it whatsoever, a sound like a type case dropping. "So whoever set this confession used my father's punctuation, my father's ampersand, and my father's dying-year quads, and spaced the lines like someone who learned composition standing at that exact stone, where I'm standing now, being taught to justify a line by feel."

"Someone trained here."

"Trained here. Recently." She set down the forged page with exaggerated gentleness, the way you put down something you'd otherwise throw through a window. "There are three people alive who learned composition at this stone. One is me. One is Tomas, who sets type the way a mule pulls a cart — honestly and badly, and he was at the docks until midnight, I'll swear it on Father's press. And one—"

She didn't finish. The shop finished it for her: the bell over the door rang, light and cheerful, a sound that belonged to a different year.

Rell came in with flour on his sleeves and a paper bag of morning buns held up like a peace offering to whatever mood he found. He was nineteen, quick-moving, with his brother's dark brows and none of his brother's polish, and he stopped just inside the door the way he always did, reading the room first — a habit, Jonah realized with a fresh chill, that she had always found endearing.

"You're both up early," he said. "Or still up late. Either way — buns. The baker had extras because the Assembly man didn't come for his order, and when the Assembly stops taking free bread, that's—" he paused, weighing, "—that's weather worth noting."

"Weather worth noting," Elias murmured, and made a small note of it, actually writing it down, which Jonah could have killed him for.

Rell's eyes went to the composing stone. To the confession lying there. Something moved across his face — she watched for it deliberately, hating herself for watching — and what she saw was recognition, plain and unguarded, the look of a boy seeing a familiar thing in an unfamiliar place.

"That's the letter," he said. "From yesterday. The one naming you." He set the bag down carefully. "Why is it on the stone?"

"Because we're examining it," Jonah said. Her voice sounded normal. She was almost proud of that. "Type examination. Provenance. It's a thing archivists do."

"And printers," Rell said. He wasn't stupid; he had never been stupid; that had always been the trouble with him. His gaze went from the confession to the open specimen book to the locked case against the wall, and Jonah watched him do the same arithmetic she had done, arrive at the same sum, and go pale in the same places.

"Mira-san isn't up yet?" he asked. Too casual by half.

"Not yet."

"Right." He untied and retied the string on the bun bag for no reason at all. "I'll — the ink orders. From Ferro's. I said I'd go this morning."

"We're closed today," Jonah said. "Shop business."

Something flickered then — hurt, or fear, or the specific misery of a young man realizing the temperature of a room has changed and not knowing which of his sins caused it. "Right," he said again. "Of course." He left the buns on the counter anyway, which was either innocence or craftsmanship, and the bell rang behind him, cheerful and useless.

Through the window they watched him go — not toward Ferro's ink shop, which was two streets east, but west, toward the harbor district, walking quickly, glancing back once at the window where they stood.

"Toward the harbor," Elias said quietly. "Where his brother keeps office hours."

"Or toward the ferry landing. Or toward a friend's house. Or anywhere a frightened nineteen-year-old might go to think." Jonah pressed her forehead briefly against the cool glass. "I used to be able to read people the way I read type. Every mark in its place. Now everyone's justified too perfectly and I can't find the natural spaces anymore."

Elias gathered the specimen book and the confession and squared their edges together, an archivist's tic, order against chaos. "For what it's worth," he said, "I've spent twenty years watching guilty men walk, and I never once learned anything useful from watching them leave a room. Guilt is invisible. Fear isn't, and he was afraid, but this whole city is afraid, including me, including possibly your mother, who shows it by reorganizing the pantry."

"She does that."

"Last night she alphabetized the dried goods in a language I don't speak."

Despite everything, Jonah laughed — a short, cracked sound, but real. Then it died, because the arithmetic hadn't changed. Someone had stood at her stone, in the dark, and set her family's name among traitors using her father's hesitant semicolons, and the list of people who could have done it was short enough to memorize and included nearly everyone she loved.

"There's one more thing," she said. "One more mark. I didn't want to say it in front of—"

She stopped. Listened. Above them, faint through the floorboards, came the sound of Mira Nakamura's door opening, and her slow step on the stair, and then her voice, calling down in the tone she reserved for announcements that were not negotiable:

"Jonah. Come up. Bring the letter."

Elias raised his eyebrows. "Does she know?"

"She knows everything," Jonah said. "It's her worst quality." But her chest had gone tight, because her mother's voice had a crack in it she had heard only twice before — once at the funeral, and once, years ago, over a story about another country and another sealed letter that Jonah had always assumed was just a story.

She picked up the confession, and followed her mother's voice up the stairs, toward whatever her mother had been carrying all these years in a case no one was allowed to open.
