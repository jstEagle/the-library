## 7. The Printer's Mark

The watermark was the kind of thing you'd only notice if you had spent your whole life listening to paper the way other people listened to music.

Jonah held the forged confession up to the morning light slanting through the shop window and tilted it, slowly, the way her father used to tilt sheets to check for even ink coverage. There — faint as a breath on glass. A crest pressed into the fibers themselves, not printed on top of them. The Merchant Assembly's scales-and-crown, rendered in that pale gray ghost-shade that only appeared when a sheet had been made on a specific mold, at a specific mill, by a specific hand.

"You see it?" she asked.

Elias Vane leaned in from his chair by the cold stove, where he had been sitting since dawn with a cup of tea he kept forgetting to drink. He squinted. He took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve with the fussiness of a man who had spent thirty years handling documents worth more than his life, and put them back on.

"Yes," he said, after a moment. "No. Possibly. Point again."

Jonah pointed. His eyebrows climbed.

"Ah." He said it the way scholars say *ah* — meaning not *I understand*, but *I have just been handed something I will be thinking about for weeks*. "That's a Harrowgate mold. Or a copy of one."

"Harrowgate closed two years ago," Jonah said. She set the confession down on the composing stone, carefully, as if it were a sleeping animal that might bite. "When the Assembly started buying its paper from the south mills. Papa bought their last three reams because they were cheap and he liked the weight." She paused. "We have maybe forty sheets left in the back room."

"So the forger either bought from us—"

"Or stole from us. Or—" Jonah stopped. Her mouth had gone dry in the particular way it did before bad news, a sensation she had come to think of as her body's own proofreader flagging a line before her mind could read it. "Or someone has been in our shop who shouldn't have been."

Elias set down his tea at last. It had gone cold hours ago; he drank it anyway, which told Jonah something about how seriously he took what came next.

"Then we follow the water," he said.

"The water?"

"Paper is honest, Miss Nakamura. Ink lies, signatures lie, men lie beautifully and fluently and all the way to the gallows. But paper remembers where it was made, and molds remember where they were carried, and if your father bought the last of the Harrowgate stock, then somewhere between your back room and that seal, someone carried it." He stood, joints crackling like a poorly bound spine. "And mold-makers keep records. Mill agents keep records. Everyone in this trade keeps records, because no one trusts anyone else in this trade, which is the single most reassuring thing about printers."

Jonah looked at him. "You're enjoying this."

"I am a dismissed archivist whose entire career ended because I declined to burn evidence," Elias said mildly, reaching for his coat. "This is the first time in four months that knowing things has been useful instead of fatal. Let me have my little joy before someone shoots us."

* * *

They went out through the print quarter in the gray middle of the morning, when the streets belonged to delivery carts and shopkeepers sweeping yesterday's mud into new configurations of mud. Vellamare was pretending, as cities do, that nothing was wrong. Fishmongers shouted prices. The ferry bell clanged its flat note across the harbor. Two Assembly constables stood outside the customs house looking bored in a way that struck Jonah as rehearsed, like boredom worn as a uniform over alertness.

"Walk like we're arguing about type sizes," Elias murmured.

"Why?"

"Because everyone ignores two people arguing about type sizes. It's the most boring subject known to mankind. Even soldiers would rather imagine being anywhere else."

"We *are* arguing about type sizes."

"Then we're perfectly disguised. What size is the confession's body text?"

"Pica. Obviously. With Papa's em-dash cuts, which is a problem I haven't solved yet."

"See? Boring. No one will ever suspect us of anything."

Their first stop was the paper agent's office on Cordwainer Lane, a narrow establishment run by a woman named Bettine Oss, who had sold paper to Nakamura & Son — well, Nakamura & Widow & Daughter now, though the sign hadn't changed; there was no money for repainting, and anyway her mother said the sign could stay until the grief wore off or the paint did, whichever came second.

Bettine received them in a back room stacked to the ceiling with reams, each wrapped and labeled in her tiny ferocious handwriting. She was a square woman with ink-stained cuffs and the general air of someone who had heard every excuse a debtor could invent and found most of them typographically flawed.

"Nakamura," she said. "You want credit."

"I want information," Jonah said. "About the Harrowgate stock."

Bettine's expression shifted from suspicion to a kind of professional delight. Information about paper movements was, in her economy, currency of the highest denomination.

"Who else has asked?"

"That's my payment question," Elias said smoothly, "and mine is: has anyone bought Harrowgate stock in quantity since the mill closed?"

Bettine looked them both over — the young printer with her father's hands, the disgraced archivist with his coat too fine for his current fortunes — and made the calculation of a woman deciding whether a secret was safer kept or traded.

"One buyer," she said. "Grebe."

The name landed in Jonah's chest like a piece of type dropped from a high shelf.

"Aldous Grebe," she repeated.

"He bought out the whole remaining lot. Eleven reams. Paid cash, which should have told me everything, because Grebe never pays cash for anything he can pay late." Bettine shrugged. "He said he was doing commemorative work. Anniversary editions. Very sentimental, Aldous. Sentimental like a knife is sentimental about cutting."

"Do you know where he's printing?" Elias asked. "His old shop on Tanner's Row burned out—"

"It didn't burn out. It was *relocated*. He moved to the old salt warehouse behind the Customs Annex. Big space. New press." Bettine lowered her voice with the reverence of a true believer discussing heresy. "A double-cylinder, imported. You could print a thousand sheets an hour on a machine like that. You could print *lies* at a thousand an hour." She caught herself. "Not that I'm saying he is."

"You're not saying anything," Elias agreed. "You told us nothing. We forced our way in and you bravely resisted."

"That's the spirit. Get out."

At the door, Bettine caught Jonah's sleeve. Her voice dropped to something almost gentle.

"My girl. Whatever you're following — follow it home before dark. Things are moving on the docks. My cousin loads ships, and he says officers are drawing up lists. Real ones, with seals. Not the usual lists of smugglers." Her eyes were steady and unhappy. "Lists of names."

* * *

The salt warehouse behind the Customs Annex was a long low building the color of old teeth, its windows recently reglazed with glass so clean it looked ashamed of itself among the sootier buildings around it. Jonah and Elias approached from the canal side, where a loading dock faced the water and a single guard dozed against a bollard with the practiced artistry of a man paid to appear awake only when observed.

"How do you want to do this?" Elias whispered.

"Walk in the front and ask to see him."

"That's a plan?"

"It's the plan where nobody gets shot. Papa always said the best way into any printer's shop is to admire his press. Printers are vain about exactly one thing."

"And that's why he's dead," Elias muttered, but he followed her up the dock stairs.

Inside, the warehouse had been transformed. Where salt barrels had once sweated brine into the beams, there now stood rows of drying lines hung with broadsheets, racks of fresh paper, an office partitioned off in varnished pine, and — dominating the center floor like a cathedral altar — the press. It was beautiful. Jonah hated how beautiful it was. The double-cylinder gleamed with oil and care, and beneath it, stacked in wooden cases with brass corner fittings, lay the type.

She didn't need to get closer. She knew those cases. She had grown up sleeping under the sound of them being sorted.

Aldous Grebe emerged from his office wiping his hands on a rag, a stout man with side-whiskers groomed into two aggressive commas and the smile of a man who had once been told he had a trustworthy face and had been milking the compliment ever since.

"Visitors! And during working hours, too. Miss Nakamura, isn't it?" His smile widened. "Your father's daughter. You have the look. How is the old shop? Still standing, I hope. These are uncertain times for small operations."

"Very uncertain," Jonah said pleasantly. "Especially for small operations whose paper keeps walking out the back door."

Something flickered behind the trustworthy face — quick as an ink fleck, gone just as fast. "I'm afraid I don't follow."

"Eleven reams of Harrowgate stock," Elias said, producing the forged confession and holding it up with the flourish of a conjurer. "Closed-mill watermark, Assembly seal. Bought by you, per Bettine Oss's books, which I'll note she showed me voluntarily and at some personal risk, because she is braver than either of us. This document — naming the Nakamura family as conspirators against the Assembly — was set on a press using this paper. And these ligatures—" he tapped the page, "—are not Grebe foundry cuts. They're custom. Hand-cut. Worn at the shoulders from years of use."

"They're ours," Jonah said. "My father cut them. I could find them blindfolded. Someone took them from my family's shop, along with forty sheets of our paper, and set a confession on them that will get my mother hanged."

The warehouse went very quiet. On the drying lines, a hundred fresh broadsheets stirred in the draft from the door — and Jonah, tilting her head, read one upside down from ten paces. *Loyalty Register of the Merchant Assembly: Citizens of Proven Fidelity.* Names in columns. Rewards listed at the bottom.

Grebe sighed. It was a performance of a sigh, deep and regretful, and he delivered it beautifully.

"You've got it backward, Miss Nakamura. Entirely backward." He spread his hands. "Yes, I hold Assembly contracts. Yes, I print their registers, their proclamations, whatever keeps my workers fed. That's not conspiracy, that's commerce. As for your paper — I bought it fair from Oss, and if some of it found its way to... other parties..." he let the sentence trail off into a shrug so innocent it was practically criminal, "...paper moves. People move it. I couldn't say who."

"But you could say who *paid* you to not ask," Elias said quietly.

Grebe's smile cooled several degrees. For a moment the mask sat crooked, and underneath it Jonah saw the thing her father had always warned her about in rival printers — not malice, exactly, but appetite. A man who wanted the Assembly's contracts more than he wanted anything else in the world, including other people.

"Here's what I know, Vane," Grebe said, dropping the warmth entirely. "I know you were dismissed from the Archive for theft. I know the Nakamura shop harbors dissidents and seditious correspondence — half the quarter knows it, they're simply polite enough not to say it aloud. And I know—" he produced a folded sheet from his waistcoat, unfolded it with unhurried care, "—that the Assembly's Commission of Public Order finalized its arrest schedule three days ago."

He turned the sheet around.

It was a list. Typeset, sealed, official. And there, in the fourth column under *Sedition — Print Trades*, Jonah read:

*Nakamura, Mira — proprietor.*
*Nakamura, Jonah — compositor.*
*Nakamura, Tomas — associate (docks).*
*Vane, Elias — fugitive archivist.*

Her own name. In someone else's type. Set by a stranger's hands, on a stranger's press, to end her.

"There's a reward attached for verified locations," Grebe said, folding the list away again. "I'm telling you this as a courtesy, Nakamura, because your father was an honest craftsman and I respect the trade, whatever else happens. You have perhaps two days. Maybe less, once the crackdown starts. Go inland. Disappear. Let sensible people write the history and leave the ink to professionals." He smiled again, and this time there was nothing trustworthy left in it at all. "History gets written by whoever owns the press. Your family taught this city that. It seems a shame you've forgotten the lesson."

* * *

They walked out through the front door because running would have meant admitting fear, and Grebe's guard was watching, and pride is cheaper than bullets. Only when they had put three streets between themselves and the Customs Annex did Jonah stop, beside a fish stall, gripping the edge of a barrel hard enough to whiten her knuckles.

"My name," she said. "It's on a list. My *mother's* name. Tomas." Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which surprised her; inside, something was moving very fast, like a press at full cylinder speed. "He's going to hand that list to the Commission. Tonight. He was smiling while he read it."

"He was smiling because he thinks he's already won," Elias said. He had gone pale, but his eyes had gone bright — the brightness of a man whose worst suspicions had just been promoted to facts. "Listen to me. Grebe made one mistake. He showed us the list."

"Why is that a mistake? Now we know we're hunted."

"No, child — now we know the *shape* of the hunt. Dates, columns, categories. Arrest schedules aren't guesses; they're documents, and documents have masters. Someone above Grebe drafted that. Someone who wants arrests ready the moment the revolution gives them an excuse." Elias pulled his spectacles off and cleaned them, though they were clean, a habit Jonah was beginning to recognize as his version of pacing. "Which means the forgery isn't Grebe's idea. It's his *commission*. And commissions leave trails — drafts, payments, instructions. Paper, Jonah. Everything leaves paper."

Jonah stared at the harbor. The ferry bell clanged. Somewhere beyond the breakwater, gray and patient, the Assembly's patrol gunboats sat at anchor like punctuation at the end of a sentence the city hadn't finished reading.

Two days. Her mother in the shop, sorting type by lamplight. Tomas on the docks. Rell sweeping the floor, humming, trusting them.

Dread sat in her chest, cold and complete. And then, beneath it — she felt it settle, the way a forme settles into its bed when every quoins are tight — something harder. Not courage exactly. Closer to composition. The certainty that a page is not finished merely because someone else has decided how it ends.

"Elias," she said. "How fast can you forge a merchant's letter of introduction?"

He blinked. "Reasonably. Why?"

"Because Grebe answered to someone when he commissioned that confession. And whoever holds his contract keeps copies of everything — orders, payments, drafts. Men like Grebe never destroy proof that they can later use against each other." She pushed off from the barrel and started walking, fast, toward the print quarter, toward home, toward the two days that were already burning down like a candle measured in minutes. "We're going to steal the paper that proves who wrote my family's death warrant. And then—"

She stopped at the corner. Down the street, the shop's windows glowed warm in the graying afternoon, and in the doorway her brother Tomas stood talking to a dockhand, gesturing urgently, and even at this distance Jonah could see that his face was the color of ash.

"—and then," she finished quietly, "we'd better hurry."

Tomas saw her and began to run.
