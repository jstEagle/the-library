## 8. Rell's Two Brothers

Rell had two brothers in the way that a man can have two hands: one he trusted to hold things, and one that kept making fists.

The first was Dario, who was not his brother by blood at all but by seven years of apprenticeship — the Nakamura shop's other boy, gone now these three months to the Assembly garrison because a printer's wage fed one mouth and a soldier's fed three. The second was Ferro, who shared his blood and his mother's jaw and none of his sense, and who served the Assembly as a clerk in the Office of Public Order, which was the sort of name that meant the opposite of what it said.

Rell thought about this often, that both his brothers wore uniforms now. Dario's was brown wool and smelled of gun oil. Ferro's was grey broadcloth and smelled of nothing at all, which Rell found somehow worse.

He thought about it most on nights like this one, when he was supposed to be sweeping the shop and instead stood in the doorway watching Jonah Nakamura read a letter she'd taken from under her own door.

She didn't know he could see her face. That was the thing about Jonah — she thought her face was set type, locked tight in its chase, nothing shifting without her say-so. But Rell had spent four years learning to read proofs, and reading proofs was mostly learning to see what the compositor didn't mean to show you. A hairline crack in a worn ligature. An upside-down *n* hiding in a line of small caps. And right now, in the lamplight, the look on Jonah's face was the typographical equivalent of a dropped form: everything she'd built coming apart all at once.

Rell tightened his grip on the broom and swept harder, because sweeping was what he did when his chest got too full, and Mrs. Nakamura said a boy who swept angrily still ended up with a clean floor, so no harm done.

* * *

Here is what Rell knew, arranged in his head like sorts in a case, each piece in its compartment:

In the upper-left, where the capitals lived: someone had forged a confession naming the Nakamura family as conspirators, and had set it on the Nakamura press, or one exactly like it, down to Papa Nakamura's custom punctuation cuts — the ones the old man had carved himself, with a jeweler's file, because he believed every shop should have a voice you could recognize across a room.

In the middle rows, the lowercase of it: whoever had set that forgery had needed access. Not just access to the press — access to the *type*, to the particular worn ligatures, to the quoin key that only fit their frames. And there were perhaps six people alive who could walk into this shop after hours without breaking anything.

Rell was one of them.

Dario had been one of them.

And in the lower compartments, the punctuation marks, the small sharp pieces: Ferro had come to him eleven days ago, standing under the awning across the street in the rain, not crossing over, saying through the drumming water, "There's a list, Rell. Names get on it or names don't. I can keep ours off if you keep your eyes open. You're in a house full of sedition and you don't even have to do anything. Just tell me what you see."

That was the whole cruelty of it. Ferro hadn't asked him to forge anything, or steal anything, or lie about anything big. He'd asked him to *see*. As though seeing were free. As though eyes came without a price.

* * *

"You sweep like the floor insulted you," Jonah said, without looking up from the letter.

"The floor knows what it did."

That got half a smile out of her, which was the most anyone had gotten out of Jonah in two days, and Rell felt the small warm victory of it even while the rest of him stayed cold. This was the trap of the Nakamura shop, the thing nobody warned you about when you signed your indenture papers: they were *kind* to you. Mira Nakamura fed you until you ached. Tomas taught you dice games and let you win one time in five, which was exactly the number that kept a person coming back. And Jonah — Jonah treated you like a colleague, like your opinion about kerning mattered, like you were a person who happened to be fifteen and poor instead of a fifteen-year-old poor person.

It would be much easier to betray people who weren't kind.

Not that Rell was betraying anyone. He told himself this nightly, with the fervor of a man reciting a catechism he didn't quite believe. He hadn't given Ferro anything yet. He hadn't refused, either, which was its own kind of answer, and he knew it, and Ferro knew it, and the silence between them had begun to accrue interest like a debt at a merchant bank.

* * *

The arrest list came to him on Thursday, folded inside a delivery of ink.

This was how Ferro communicated — never letters, never anything with a seal, always contraband tucked into legitimate shipments, because Ferro had learned tradecraft from the same novels Rell had, except Ferro had apparently believed them. The ink order was real; Rell checked the invoice twice. Inside the wrapping paper, in Ferro's cramped clerk's hand, was a single sheet:

*By order of the Office of Public Order, the following persons are to be detained upon declaration of martial law.*

And then names. Dozens of them. Dock organizers. A schoolteacher. Two priests who had preached something inconvenient about the Saltgate tolls. Elias Vane, the disgraced archivist, whose name Rell recognized from the gossip sheets — *the man who wouldn't burn what he was told to burn*.

And, third from the top, in the same cramped hand as the rest:

*Nakamura, M. — printer. Nakamura, J. — printer. Nakamura, T. — laborer.*

Rell sat down on the ink crate and read the names four times, as though repetition might change the type. It did not. Type never changed. That was the whole point of type, and the whole horror of it too: what was set stayed set, unless someone broke the line and redistributed it, and no one was going to break this line.

Ferro had sent it deliberately. Rell understood that with the sick clarity of a man reading his own obituary proof. This was Ferro saying: *look what I can show you, look how far inside I am, look what happens if you're not useful.* And underneath that, in the subtext, in the fine print: *I marked our name for you, little brother. I put us on the list so you'd know I could take us off.*

Or so Rell hoped that was the message. There was another possible message, the one shaped like a knife: *this is what you get.*

His hands were shaking. He noticed them shaking with a strange detachment, the way you notice a misprint in someone else's book.

* * *

He almost told Jonah that night.

They were alone in the shop, the two of them, pulling proofs of a funeral notice for old Signora Vashti, who had died at eighty-nine and left instructions that her notice contain the phrase "finally, peace and quiet," which Jonah had set with a perfectly straight face and which Rell had laughed at for ten minutes until she threatened to make him set the whole notice himself.

"Jonah," he said.

"Mm." She was cleaning the rollers, sleeves pushed up, ink to her elbows. She had ink on her cheekbone too, a small black crescent, and Rell thought absurdly that if the soldiers came for her they would find her already wearing the mark of her trade, like a saint with an attribute.

"There's — I heard —" His tongue, which could set a line of eight-point Baskerville faster than anyone in Vellamare under twenty, turned to loose type in his mouth. Everything spilled and jumbled. "The docks. There's talk of lists. Arrest lists. People are saying—"

"People say a great many things." She looked at him then, and her eyes were tired and sharp at once, and Rell understood suddenly that she was not going to ask him what he knew, because asking would make him choose, and she was — God help him — she was *protecting him from having to choose*, the way you'd protect an apprentice from a job too heavy for his arms.

"I'm not a child," he said.

"No," she agreed, turning back to the rollers. "You're an apprentice. It's different. Apprentices get to be surprised by the world a few more years. It's one of the benefits. Terrible wages, long hours, but the surprise allotment is generous."

He wanted to laugh. He wanted to put the paper on the bench between them and watch her face do what faces do. He wanted Ferro to be someone else's brother.

He said none of it. He finished the funeral notice, and walked home through streets that smelled of woodsmoke and fear, and lay awake listening to Ferro breathing in the next room — Ferro, who slept like the innocent, flat on his back, hands folded, a clerk even unconscious — and hated him with a love so tangled Rell couldn't find either thread's end.

Because here was the compartment Rell kept locked, the sort he never let himself reach for:

Rell knew who had set the forgery.

Not Dario — he'd interrogated that fear a hundred times and Dario came up clean, Dario who wrote him letters full of misspellings and homesickness, Dario who wouldn't know a custom ligature from a hole in his boot. No. It was the man who had come to the shop in the autumn, the journeyman printer named Corvess, hired for three weeks during the almanac rush, who had worked the night shifts, who had praised Papa Nakamura's punctuation cuts a little too warmly, who had asked once — casually, over dinner, so casually that only later did the question grow teeth — *"and who else has keys to this place?"*

Corvess, who had left before his last week was paid, and whose references, when Rell had finally thought to wonder, had been written on paper bearing a watermark Rell now suspected belonged to Aldous Grebe's press.

Rell knew. He had known for nine days. And he could not say it, because saying it meant explaining how he knew, and explaining how he knew meant telling Jonah that he'd recognized Corvess's habits in the forgery — the double space after a semicolon, a habit so specific it might as well be a signature — and that he'd recognized it *nine days ago* and said nothing while suspicion curdled around the shop like milk in heat.

And worse, deeper, the true locked compartment: saying Corvess's name meant saying how he'd confirmed it, and he'd confirmed it through Ferro, and Ferro had made him promise, and Ferro had said — laughing, easy, the way he said everything — *"Corvess works for people who'd kill me for knowing his name, Rell. You give me up, you give up your own brother. Think of it as family loyalty."*

So Rell held the name in his mouth like a hot coal and waited for it to cool, and it did not cool, and he went on holding it.

* * *

On Saturday night he copied the list.

He did it in the shop, after hours, which was itself a betrayal of a kind — using their paper, their ink, their press — but his hand couldn't be trusted with a pen and he knew it. He set the names in twelve-point, the body size of ordinary notices, and he set them fast, the way Papa Nakamura had taught him to set when speed mattered: don't read the words, read the shapes. If you read the words you'll weep or hesitate, and hesitation shows in the spacing.

He read the words anyway. He couldn't help it. *Nakamura, M. Nakamura, J. Nakamura, T.*

Tomas's name hurt worst, somehow. Tomas, who owed the Assembly nothing but his taxes and his contempt. They'd put him on the list just for sharing a roof, the way you'd confiscate a whole shipment because one crate was contraband.

He printed forty copies. Then, hating himself, he printed sixty more, because if the list was real then other families deserved warning too, and if the list was a test then he was damned either way and might as well be damned generously.

Then he stood in the empty shop with a hundred thin sheets of other people's doom and did not know what to do with any of it.

* * *

What he finally did was this: he took one copy — one — and folded it into eighths, small as a communion wafer, and at dawn on Sunday, before the city woke, before Mira Nakamura came down to stoke the stove, he knelt at the shop's back door and slid it under, into the gap beneath the door where the draft came through in winter.

Then he ran. There was no dignity in it. He ran the way boys run, all elbows and guilt, three streets before he let himself stop and breathe.

Let them think it came from a friend. Let them think it came from anywhere but the apprentice who slept under their roof and ate their bread and carried his brother's secret like a stone in his shoe. Let Jonah puzzle over it with her sharp tired eyes and reach whatever conclusion spared everyone — or spared Ferro, which was the only conclusion Rell could afford.

He told himself it was enough. One copy, anonymous, deniable. The rest of the hundred he hid in the hollow beneath the floorboards of his cot, wrapped in oilcloth, and every day that passed without disaster he told himself the danger was draining out of them like ink blotted from a proof.

But paper doesn't drain. Paper keeps. That was the first lesson of the trade and the last lesson of his life, maybe: whatever you set stays set. Somewhere in the dark under his cot, a hundred copies of the list lay waiting to be read, and somewhere across the city, Corvess was setting more lies on stolen type, and somewhere in a grey office Ferro was sharpening his kindness into something with an edge, and Rell walked to work each morning whistling, because whistling was the only lie he was good at.

* * *

On Tuesday evening, the bells rang wrong.

Rell was stacking fresh paper when he heard it — not the measured hour-chime but the rapid, ragged peal of the alarm bells, all of them at once, from the cathedral down to the harbor chapels, overlapping and urgent. He went to the window. Down the street, people were stopping in the doorways of their shops, looking at each other, asking questions with their eyebrows.

Then the smell reached him. Smoke, but not hearth-smoke — something bigger, angrier, burning wet wood and tar. From the direction of the docks rose a column of black, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the sound of a crowd that had stopped being a crowd.

Mira appeared in the stairwell with a lamp, and behind her Jonah, and behind Jonah Tomas, and for one moment the four of them stood in the shop's doorway like figures in a frieze, listening.

"They've declared it," Tomas said quietly. "Martial law. It's started."

Rell's hand went, without his willing it, to his pocket, where the folded copy of the list was not — where only the memory of it was, a hundred copies strong, hidden under his cot in a room he might not sleep in again.

And across the street, under the awning where Ferro had once stood in the rain, a figure in grey broadcloth was watching the shop.

Watching Rell.

Raising one hand — not a wave, not quite. A reminder.
