## 9. Curfew of Ash

The bell above the door had not rung all day, and Jonah found she missed it the way one misses a clock that has stopped — not the sound itself, but the proof that time was still passing in the ordinary way.

By dusk the proclamation had gone up on the Assembly's boards, pasted over the wedding announcements and auction notices in great grey sheets: *By order of the Merchant Assembly, a state of martial law is declared within the city of Vellamare. All persons are confined to their dwellings from nightfall. Gatherings of more than three persons are forbidden. This order takes effect immediately.*

Jonah read it twice, professionally. The type was set badly — cramped leading, a widow on the last line, and someone had spelled *martial* as *marital*, which under other circumstances would have been the funniest thing to happen in Vellamare all year. She wondered if Grebe's press had set it. She hoped so. She hoped Aldous Grebe lay awake tonight knowing his marital law hung on every corner of the city, and that somewhere a schoolboy was giggling at it.

"Enjoying the literature?" Tomas asked, coming in with an armload of shutters he'd begun nailing up before the ink on the proclamation was dry.

"The kerning is criminal," Jonah said. "Also they've outlawed gatherings of more than three people, which means Mother can no longer scold us properly. We'd constitute an unlawful assembly."

Tomas grunted — his version of laughter these days — and fitted the last shutter into its frame. Through the gaps between boards, the street showed in slats: the lamplighter making his rounds faster than usual, a fishwife hauling her cart home mid-sale, and at the far end of the lane, where the road bent toward the harbor, a glow that was not sunset. It had the wrong color for sunset. Sunset was gold. This was orange, the deep honest orange of things burning that were never meant to burn.

The barricades had started going up at noon. By four there were three of them visible from the upper window — furniture, paving stones, a dismantled cart, someone's entire dining table standing on end like a shipwrecked hull. By six, the smoke. The Assembly had sent soldiers to clear the Saltgate quarter, and the quarter had declined to be cleared, and now the two refusals were meeting each other in fire.

Inside the shop, Mira Nakamura was doing something worse than watching the smoke. She was counting.

Jonah knew the count by heart because her mother had taught it to her before she could write her own name. *What can you carry?* Not what do you love — what can you carry. The type cases, yes, though only half of them; the good ones, father's ones, with the worn ligatures. The hand press could stay. The paper stock could stay. The ledger proofs, the true pages Elias had brought folded inside his coat like contraband scripture — those went wherever the cases went.

Her mother stood now in the middle of the composing room with a candle in one hand, surveying the racks the way a general surveys a map, and Jonah watched her lips move through the inventory.

"We hollow them tonight," Mira said, without turning around. She had heard Jonah come down the stairs; she always heard everything. "While we still have lamplight."

"Hollow what?"

"The type cases." Mira set the candle down and drew one of the shallow upper cases from the rack, the kind used for em-quads and spacing material — the drawers nobody loved, full of blank metal. "A case is a box that already has compartments, Jonah. No soldier in Vellamare will look twice at a printer's drawer. They'll see sorts. They'll see nothing." She turned, and in the candlelight her face was calm in the way that Jonah had learned, over nineteen years, to read as anything but. "But first we take out every sort that matters and we hide *those* separately. If they take the cases, they must not take our alphabet."

It was, Jonah thought, the most motherly sentence ever spoken: *hide the alphabet.*

* * *

They worked by three candles and the last grey light through the shutter slats, and it might have been any busy evening at the Nakamura press if you didn't listen too hard. Tomas pried the false bottoms off the old spacing cases while Rell fetched and held, and Elias Vane sat at the stone bench with the true ledger pages spread before him, sorting them into piles with an archivist's terrible tenderness.

"These go together," Elias murmured, mostly to himself. "The requisition orders, the bribes, the — no. Wait." He held two sheets up to the candle, frowning. "These are dated wrong. Or I am."

"You're tired," Jonah said. "Everyone dates things wrong when they're tired. That's why printers exist — to fix what tired people wrote."

Elias looked at her over the rims of nothing, because he did not own spectacles but squinted anyway, an affectation he'd picked up from actual scholars and refused to surrender. "Is that what printing is? Fixing?"

"That's what printing *is*. Everything else is just ink."

Rell snorted from the corner, then went very still, the way he'd been going still all day whenever a door opened or a voice passed outside. He had not spoken since morning except to answer direct questions, and his answers had the flat politeness of a boy carrying something heavy behind his teeth. Twice Jonah had caught him looking at the shuttered door as if he could see through it — or as if he feared something on the other side could see in.

"Rell," she said gently, "hand me the wax, would you?"

He handed her the wax. Their fingers touched on the little pot, and he flinched, and pretended he hadn't, and she pretended she hadn't noticed, which was its own kind of lie, and she was getting practiced at lies today and hated every one of them.

The work itself was simple, once you accepted its absurdity. Each spacing case came apart along seams Tomas swore their father had built for exactly this purpose — and when Jonah raised an eyebrow at that, Tomas said, "Don't look at me. Ask him," gesturing vaguely upward at whatever part of eternity contained Kenji Nakamura — and beneath the tray was a cavity two fingers deep, running the length of the case. Into each cavity went the ledger pages, rolled tight, wrapped in oilcloth against damp and worse. Then the tray back, the sorts poured loosely on top to give it weight and honesty, the lid nailed, the seams waxed and dusted with wood shavings so the fresh carpentry wouldn't show.

Six cases. Six hiding places sitting in plain sight on the rack, among thirty others exactly like them.

"It's not enough," Elias said quietly, when the sixth was done. "If they search thoroughly—"

"Then we lose the copies and keep the memory," Mira said. "That's why there are four of us who've read every page. Five, counting Rell." She glanced at the boy, who stared hard at the floor. "Paper burns, archivist. People are harder to burn. Though the Assembly keeps trying."

It was meant as a joke. Nobody laughed, least of all Mira, whose jokes always sounded like prophecies.

* * *

The knocking came at half past nine.

Not the polite knock of customers — those had stopped days ago, replaced by payment-in-warnings, dockworkers pressing coins and rumors into Jonah's hands with equal solemnity (*they're massing at Northgate, miss; the Fourth Regiment won't fire on the crowd, my cousin swears it; buy salt now while you can*) — but the flat, rhythmic knock of authority. Three blows. A pause. Three more.

Tomas blew out the candles with one sweep of his arm, which was either discipline or panic, and in the sudden dark Jonah heard her own heartbeat and her mother's voice, low and even: "Lamps stay out. Everyone to the wall. Say nothing unless asked directly. Answer briefly. Be boring."

*Be boring.* It was the whole Nakamura survival doctrine in two words. Her mother had crossed a border once with a forged name and a type case, and had apparently decided en route that the safest thing a person could be was forgettable.

Tomas opened the door on the chain, and lantern light fell through the gap in blades.

There were five of them. Four soldiers in Assembly grey with bayonets fixed — fixed, at a print shop, which told Jonah everything about the quality of the evening — and one officer holding the lantern, a young man with the unfortunate complexion of someone who had joined the army to see the world and had instead seen Vellamare.

"Open fully. Inspection."

"On whose authority?" Tomas asked, mild as milk.

The officer produced a paper. Even by lantern light Jonah recognized the layout — she'd have known it blindfolded by the smell of the ink — and felt a small vicious satisfaction noting that whoever had set the warrant had used a hyphen where an em-dash belonged. Amateur. *Grebe's shop,* she thought. *Has to be.* Then the satisfaction curdled, because the warrant also bore a list of names, and the officer was reading them aloud in a bored recitation: "Nakamura. Mira. Jonah. Tomas. Also one Rell—" he squinted "—surname unrecorded."

"He's apprenticed here," Tomas said. "Family matter, the surname. His people are inland."

The officer made a note. Jonah filed away the fact that Rell's surname being unknown was currently saving him, and that this could stop being true at any moment, and saw Rell file away the same fact, his face a study in stillness lit by the swinging lantern.

The soldiers moved through the shop the way soldiers move through anywhere: loudly, incuriously, breaking nothing yet but touching everything, lifting lids, prodding sacks of rags, kicking open the privy door and regretting it. One of them ran a finger along the press and looked at it the way men look at machines they don't understand — suspicion without vocabulary. Jonah stood where her mother had placed her, hands folded, face arranged into the expression of a woman whose most urgent concern in life was whether the wedding announcement for the Corvane-Vessel wedding would go out on time.

In fact — and this was the thought that got her through it, the absurd lifeline she grabbed — in fact that announcement was *still in the chase*, locked up in the press, waiting. Two hundred invitations to a wedding that might now happen during a siege, set in 14-point Baskerville with the couple's names in the small caps her father had cut himself. Somewhere in the city the bride was presumably also crouched behind shutters listening to boots on her floorboards, and Jonah felt a strange sisterhood with a woman she'd never met, united across the burning city by the shared experience of having one's life interrupted by history while one's stationery waited patiently.

"Here." A soldier had pulled one of the modified cases halfway from the rack.

The room did not breathe.

He tilted it. The loose sorts shifted and rattled — the sound Jonah had spent an hour perfecting, pouring quads and spaces over the hidden cavities until the cases sounded *full*, sounded honest, sounded like nothing but metal and routine — and the soldier peered into the compartments, saw spacing material, saw the most boring objects in the trade, and shoved the case back with his knee.

"Printers," he said, to nobody, with the disgust of a man who had hoped to find weapons.

"Type is heavier than it looks," Elias offered from the bench, in his best pedantic warble. "You'd be surprised what a man can do with a font of 12-point. Whole governments have fallen over less."

The officer looked at Elias for a long moment — long enough for Jonah to feel the temperature of the room drop — and then, mercifully, decided the old man was simply mad, which was the correct reading, and moved on. Elias caught Jonah's eye with the faintest twitch of his mouth. *Later,* the twitch said. *I'll tell you later how many archives I've talked my way out of by being tedious.*

At the door, the officer turned. "Curfew holds till dawn. Anyone seen abroad is shot. Anyone harboring seditious materials—" he tapped the warrant "—is shot slightly differently. Good evening."

The door closed. The lantern light slid away under the shutters like water down a drain.

For ten full seconds, nobody moved. Then Mira relit the candle, and the flame stood up straight and small and stubborn, and Jonah discovered she was shaking, and that her mother was too, and that both of them pretended otherwise, which was the family trade after all — setting other people's words, wearing other faces.

"They weighed the cases," Tomas said hoarsely. "Did you see? He checked the weight."

"I saw."

"And the rattling fooled him."

"The rattling," said Mira, blowing out the match, "was your father's idea twenty years ago. He hid smugglers' pamphlets the same way during the Grain Riots. He always said—" her voice caught, just once, a hitch small enough to deny later "—he always said the best place to hide a letter is inside a case full of letters."

Jonah looked at the rack. Thirty-one cases, six of them pregnant with the truth, all of them identical, all of them smelling of ink and oilcloth and her father's hands. Terror was still in the room — it would be in the room all night, curled in the corners like the dark itself — but around it, impossibly, the craft held. The craft always held. That was the secret her mother had never said outright but had taught her every day of her life: you cannot stop the soldiers, but you can make the hinges quiet; you cannot stop the fire, but you can choose what goes in the case.

*Be boring,* Jonah thought. *And keep the alphabet safe.*

* * *

Past midnight, with the family bedded down in the back room and the city outside punctuated by musket-cracks and that same deep orange flicker through the slats, Jonah stayed up at the bench. She couldn't sleep. Sleep felt like desertion.

She worked by a stub of candle shielded behind a stack of paper, doing nothing useful — unlocking the wedding announcement from the chase, just to touch the sorts, just to feel the small leaden certainty of letters that said what they meant. *Together with their families, the families announce the marriage of…*

She was so absorbed she didn't hear the floorboard until it creaked.

Rell stood at the edge of the candlelight, barefoot, coat already on, a bundle under his arm that Jonah recognized with a lurch: his spare shirt, his awl, his dead mother's ring on its cord.

"Rell." She kept her voice below the cannon-thump rolling in from the harbor. "Where are you going?"

His eyes went to the shuttered door. To the street beyond it, where a figure in grey broadcloth had stood all afternoon, raising one hand — not a wave, not quite. A reminder.

"I have to check on something," he whispered. "Before it's too late to check on it."

"Rell—"

"If I'm not back by dawn," he said, and his voice cracked down the middle, young and terrified and resolved all at once, "tell your mother the cases were my idea. Tell her I'm sorry about the ligatures."

Then he was at the door, lifting the bar with the silence of a boy who'd spent three years learning every noise this shop made — and Jonah, frozen at the bench with a wedding announcement in her hands, understood suddenly and completely what he'd meant by *the ligatures*, and what it was he had to go and check on, and why he couldn't say it aloud.

The door clicked shut behind him.

Outside, the smoke glowed. Somewhere toward the river, a drum began to beat.
