## 10. Reading by Lamplight

The ledgers smelled of tallow and secrets.

Elias Vane had spent nineteen years in the Assembly's archive, and in all that time he had never once been permitted to read a ledger straight through. He was allowed to *file* them, to *index* them, to carry them from shelf to shelf like a midwife who is never permitted to see the child's face. The reading was done by clerks with better bloodlines and worse handwriting, men who had been appointed to their posts the way one appoints a horse to a carriage — for breeding, not for understanding.

So there was a particular, unholy joy in what he was doing now: sitting at Jonah Nakamura's worktable at half past midnight, with three stolen Assembly ledgers open before him and a cup of Mira's barley tea going cold at his elbow, reading forbidden numbers aloud to an audience of two printers and a nervous apprentice who kept pretending to sweep the same corner of floor.

"Listen to this," Elias said, tapping a column with one finger. "Entry dated the ninth of the harvest month. 'To Captain Ondrej Vasko, for services rendered in matters of dock security.' Four hundred crowns."

"That doesn't seem so bad," Tomas said. He was mending a fishing net by the stove, though Elias had noticed the net had been in need of mending for three days and no closer to being mended. It was, he suspected, a net whose true purpose was to keep Tomas's hands busy so his mind wouldn't strangle him.

"It wouldn't," Elias agreed, "except that Captain Vasko's regiment was disbanded six months ago. And except that four hundred crowns is roughly eight times what a captain earns in a year. And except that this entry appears again — different month, different clerk, same captain — eleven times." He turned the page. "And here, my favorite part. In the margin, in pencil, someone has written '*paid in full, do not enter in public accounts*.'"

Jonah looked up from the type case she was sorting. She had been sorting that case since sundown, and Elias suspected she wasn't really sorting it at all; she was thinking with her hands, which was, he had come to understand, how the whole family thought. Even the mother. Especially the mother.

"Pencil," she said. "They used pencil for the shameful parts."

"They always do. Ink is a commitment. Pencil is a coward." Elias pulled the second ledger toward him. This one was older, its spine cracked, its pages soft as cloth. "Now. Debts. Would you like to know who owns the harbor?"

"I own the harbor," said Mira from the doorway. She had come down with a lamp and a shawl, though it was not cold. Elias had learned that Mira Nakamura descended into the shop at night the way other people checked on sleeping children — quietly, often, and without announcing it. "I have owned it since before your Assembly existed, young man. They merely rent it from me and have forgotten to pay."

Tomas snorted. Rell stopped sweeping.

"I believe you," Elias said, and found that he did. "But the ledger agrees with you in spirit if not in law. Here — the Harbormaster's fund. Money collected from every crate, every hull, every barrel that touches the quay, ostensibly for the maintenance of wharves and lighthouses. Do you know how much went to wharves last year?" He turned pages with a scholar's delicacy. "Sixty crowns. Sixty. There is a wharf on the east side that has been 'under repair' for nine years; the repairs consist of a rope and a sign apologizing for the rope."

"And the rest?" Jonah asked.

"The rest bought votes. Here, look — payments to Assembly members marked as 'consulting fees.' One hundred and forty crowns to a man whose consulting, as far as I can determine from his own correspondence, consisted of agreeing with Aldous Grebe over dinner." He paused. "Which reminds me. Grebe's press has been paid handsomely out of this fund too. 'Printing services, miscellaneous.' Miscellaneous is a wonderful word. It can mean anything except the truth."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft from under the door, and everyone watched it steady itself. Outside, the city made its nighttime noises — a dog, a cart, somewhere far off a whistle that might have been a patrol or might have been nothing. For a week now, the noises had been getting harder to sort into *nothing* and *something*, and everyone in the room had privately stopped believing in *nothing* altogether.

"There's more," Elias said, quieter now. "There's a section at the back of the third ledger, unbound — loose leaves, folded in. I nearly filed past it out of habit. Twenty years of habit is a hard thing to break." He unfolded one of the leaves and laid it flat, weighting the corners with type sorts because the paper wanted to curl back on itself, the way paper does when it knows it's been read too soon.

It was an order. Not a suggestion, not a minute from some meeting — an order, signed, countersigned, and initialed in three places, as though the magnitude of the thing required triple confirmation, like a delivery of gunpowder.

Elias read it silently first. He had learned, long ago in the archive, to read terrible things silently first, the way one tests bathwater. Then he read it aloud, because silence would have been a kind of collaboration.

"'On the first day of the rising, should the docks fall to the mob, the garrison is authorized to clear Saltgate Wharf by any means necessary. No quarter is to be given to armed persons. Unarmed persons are to be moved to the customs house for processing. Any person resisting processing may be treated as armed.'" He stopped. "There's a final line. Listen. '*The Wharf District is to be reported as lost to rebel violence regardless of actual circumstances.*'"

Nobody spoke. The stove ticked. Rell had stopped sweeping entirely and stood holding the broom the way a man holds a spear.

"Reported as lost," Jonah said slowly, "regardless of actual circumstances."

"Yes."

"So they intend to burn it themselves."

"They intend to do worse than burn it. Burning is quick. What they intend—" Elias tapped the order. "Read the phrasing. *Clear.* *Process.* *Treat as armed.* This is not rage; rage is honest. This is bookkeeping. Someone sat at a desk with a cup of coffee and worked out, in advance, exactly how many people could die at Saltgate Wharf and how the deaths could be entered into history as the rebels' doing." He took off his spectacles and rubbed the bridge of his nose. "I catalogued orders like this for nineteen years. I filed them under *internal correspondence, security matters*. Do you know where they sit now? On shelves. Alphabetized. Waiting for someone like me to be told to burn them, and for someone like me to refuse, and then for soldiers to come asking where I put things."

"You refused," Jonah said.

"I refused," Elias confirmed. "It seemed like the correct administrative decision at the time."

That got a small sound out of Tomas — not quite a laugh, but the shape of one, which in that house, that week, counted as laughter.

Jonah came around the table and bent over the order. Elias watched her read it the way she read everything — as movable pieces. Her eyes went down the lines and then back up, and he could see her resetting the words, seeing how they'd been built.

"This is typeset," she said. "Not handwritten. Look at the spacing — machine-set, or set by a very fast compositor. And this em-dash here." She pointed. "Somebody cut that dash custom. It's too heavy for standard stock. Somebody's press made this order official."

"Does it matter whose press?" Tomas asked.

"It always matters whose press," Jonah and Elias said together, and then looked at each other, and Elias felt something shift in his chest that he decided, for professional reasons, to ignore.

"It matters," Elias said, recovering himself, "because a forged confession requires a printer, and a printed massacre order requires a printer, and I am beginning to suspect they are the same printer, working for the same masters, building both halves of the story — the lie about us, and the lie about them. You don't need two presses to write history. You need one obedient press and enough ink."

Mira set her lamp down on the table with a small decisive click, the click of a woman who has made up her mind about something and will not be discussing it further. "Then we are agreed," she said. "This goes in the ledger."

"What ledger?" Rell asked.

"*The* ledger, child. The one we are making. The one with the debts and the bribes and this—" she touched the order with one finger, gently, as if it might bruise "—and whatever else these men wrote down while they thought no one was keeping score. My daughter sets it in type. Your archivist reads it aloud. And someday, when this city has finished burning itself, someone will stand in front of somebody else and say: *here. Here is what happened. Read it yourself.*"

"Mira," Elias said carefully, "if the Assembly wins, printing this kills us. If the rebels win—"

"If the rebels win," Mira said, "then we shall see what rebels are made of. That is rather the point of writing it down, isn't it? So that we find out?"

She picked up her lamp and left, because Mira Nakamura did not linger after delivering verdicts. At the top of the stairs her footsteps paused, resumed, paused — checking on her sleeping children, all three of them, including the sixty-year-old archivist at the kitchen table.

* * *

They worked until the fourth hour of morning.

It was Elias who insisted on method. He had spent two decades watching documents become lies through simple disorder — a date removed here, a signature transposed there — and he would not contribute to history by accident. Every fact went onto a slip of paper first. Date, source, ledger, folio. Then Jonah set it in type. Then Elias proofed it against the original. Then, and only then, did it join the growing stack of printed slips in the hollowed type cases beneath the floorboards.

"Names," Jonah said, around the second hour. She was setting the bribe entries now, and her fingers moved through the cases like water finding its level. "We should print the names of the clerks too. The ones who wrote the entries."

"Why? The clerks didn't decide anything."

"No. But they wrote it down honestly, at the time, before anyone told them to be ashamed of it. That's worth something. A record should have witnesses, not just criminals." She glanced up. "Besides — someday one of those clerks is going to be an old man telling his granddaughter he was only following orders. Let him open a broadsheet and see his own name next to the number he entered, and let him decide whether he can still say it."

Elias looked at her for a moment longer than the proofing required.

"You would have made an excellent archivist," he said.

"You would have made an excellent printer."

"We're both wasted, then. Tragic." But he wrote down the clerks' names.

It was near the end, as the candle stubs drowned in their own wax, that Elias did the arithmetic he had been avoiding all night. He did it the way he did everything — silently first.

Saltgate Wharf, on a market morning, held perhaps two thousand people. Dockworkers, fishwives, carters, children running messages between the boats. The garrison had three hundred muskets and, per the order, authorization to treat anyone who objected as armed. The customs house mentioned in the order had a documented capacity, per the port registers he had indexed years ago, of four hundred souls.

He set the figures side by side in his head and found that they did not fit, and understood that this was precisely the point. The order was not designed to save anyone. It was designed to fill the wharf with bodies and the history books with blame.

"Elias?" Jonah was watching him. "You've gone grey."

"I've been grey for years. It's one of my finest features." He took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, and put them back on, buying himself the length of a breath. "I'm trying to imagine tomorrow. Or next week. Whenever it starts. The rising happens — it *will* happen, the whole city is one dry field waiting for a match. The Assembly clears Saltgate. Hundreds dead. And then the fighting ends, one side or the other, and the historians begin."

"Whose historians?"

"Whoever wins. Always whoever wins." He laid his hand flat on the loose-leaf order. "Do you see the trap? This document says *the Wharf District is to be reported as lost to rebel violence regardless of actual circumstances.* That sentence was written before the first shot. They've already drafted the history, Jonah. Somewhere in the Assembly's archive — I guarantee it, I know that building the way you know your type cases — there is a finished account of a massacre that hasn't happened yet, blaming it on the very people it will murder. All that remains is for events to cooperate."

"Then we steal the account too."

"We steal the account too," Elias agreed. "But hear the rest, because it's worse. Suppose we succeed. Suppose we print everything — the bribes, the debts, this order, all of it — and scatter it through the city. The rebels win anyway. And the new government, the victorious one, sits down to write its official history, and it finds our broadsheets *inconvenient*. Because the truth says the Assembly planned the massacre, and the new heroes would rather the story be simple: wicked old regime, noble rising, happy ending. Truth is rarely simple. Truth has footnotes." He smiled without any humor in it. "I have spent my life among footnotes. I know how they suffer."

"So we're not printing this for the winners."

"No."

"Then who?"

Elias looked at the stack of printed slips, each one small as a prayer card. Two nights of work. Nineteen years of filing. One refusal, one flight across the city with documents inside his coat, and now this kitchen table, this lamplight, this girl with ink to her wrists.

"For the ones on the wharf," he said. "For the fishwives and the carters and the children. Whoever wins, they'll be called rebels or traitors or regrettable casualties, and none of those words will be true. The only true word will be their names, and nobody will know those unless we write them down." He tapped the ledger. "We're going to be the last witnesses, Jonah. Everyone else who knows this — the clerks, the captains, the Assemblymen — has a reason to forget. We're the only people in Vellamare with a reason to remember. That's not a job. That's a weight. And I want to be honest with you about how heavy it is before you spend your life carrying it."

Jonah was quiet for a long moment. Then she reached into the nearest type case, and her hand came back with a single piece of type, which she set upright on the table between them, standing on its feet like a tiny soldier.

An asterisk.

"My father kept one of these loose in every case," she said. "He said it was the most honest mark in the box. It doesn't argue. It just points down and says: *there's more. Keep reading.*" She pushed it toward him across the scarred wood. "That's us. That's what we're for. We point down and say there's more."

Outside, the sky had begun to go grey at the edges, the particular grey that means the night is losing and everyone knows it. Somewhere toward the docks, a bell rang once — not the hour, not the alarm. Just once, like a man clearing his throat before saying something difficult.

Elias looked at the bell-tower's direction, then at Jonah, then at the asterisk standing guard between them on the table.

"Get your mother," he said. "And wake Tomas. I think the dry field just found its match."
