## 11. Saltgate

Tomas Nakamura had spent his whole life learning to read the harbor the way his mother read a room — quickly, quietly, and with an eye for what was about to go wrong.

He read it now from the roof of the salt warehouse at the corner of Brine Street, and what he read made his mouth go dry.

The docks at Saltgate Wharf were supposed to be empty by noon. That had been the whole point of the morning's labor: get the dockworkers out before the Assembly's marines arrived to make an example of the district that had stopped paying its levies. He'd spent three hours going door to door along the quay with his cousin Devi and a borrowed handcart, saying the same thing in as many ways as he could manage — *there's a ship at the coal pier, there's room, bring the children first* — until his voice had gone rough as rope.

And it had worked, mostly. He could count the families already aboard the *Kindly Meridian*, her deck crowded with blankets and birdcages and one enormous, indignant goose that belonged to the Osei family and refused all persuasion. He could see old Marta Ferro locking her chandlery with the ceremony of a woman burying a king. The wharf was thinning out the way a crowd thins after fireworks.

But not everyone had gone.

A knot of perhaps forty men still lingered near the fish market at the wharf's far end. Dockworkers, mostly, and their sons, standing in that particular posture Tomas knew too well — arms crossed, weight on the heels, the stance of men who have decided that running is a thing that happens to other people.

"They won't board," Devi said beside him, low. "I told them. I *begged* them."

"You can't beg a man off his own pride," Tomas said. "It's load-bearing."

Devi snorted despite himself, which Tomas counted as a small victory. Humor was a tool like any other in the Nakamura kit; his father had used it to keep apprentices calm around the press, his mother used it like a knife wrapped in cloth, and Tomas had learned to use it the way sailors use songs — to keep time while something terrible approaches.

What approached now came up the coast road in columns of gray.

"Marines," Devi breathed. "That's — Tomas, that's two companies. For a *fish market*?"

Tomas didn't answer. He was counting again, because counting was what he did when fear tried to do arithmetic of its own. Two companies, say four hundred men. Muskets. Two of the small field guns the Assembly had bought from Vellamare's own foundries, back when buying them had seemed like civic pride instead of a threat delivered in advance.

The columns halted at the mouth of the quay. An officer rode forward on a gray horse, and even at this distance Tomas felt his stomach drop through the roof boards and into the sea below, because he recognized the man. Captain Aurel Voss. Everyone on the docks knew Voss; he'd collected the levies for six years, always polite, never cruel, which in the end had made him worse than either.

Voss raised a hand, and a herald bawled the proclamation: dispersal order, ten minutes, resistance to be treated as rebellion against the lawful Merchant Assembly, penalties accordingly.

Forty men stood in the fish market and did not disperse.

Tomas watched the ten minutes drain away like water from a cracked cistern. He watched some of the forty slowly, grudgingly begin to drift toward the side streets — pride bending, finally, to the arithmetic of muskets. Maybe twenty remained. Fifteen. They were arguing among themselves; he could see it, the shoving gestures, the stubborn planted feet.

Then he saw the thing that would follow him for the rest of his life, though he did not yet know it would be the rest of his life, or that he would one day set the whole of it down in type and hang it on a burned wall for a city to read.

He saw a second group of soldiers come down onto the wharf from the seaward stairs — and they wore no gray at all.

They wore the blue sashes of the rebels.

* * *

"No," Devi said. "No, those are ours. Those are — where did they get sashes? Half the city wears those now, kids tie them on fence posts—"

"Look at the boots," Tomas said. His voice came out flat and strange, as if someone else were speaking through him. "Look at the marching."

Because that was the thing. The men in rebel colors moved like marines. Shoulders square, step matched, wheeling in formation around the fish market with a crispness no dockside mob had ever achieved in the history of mobs. And moving among them, directing them with small economical gestures of a riding crop, were officers in plain coats with the Assembly's crest worked in silver thread at the collar — visible only because Tomas was above them, looking down, close enough to see the thread catch the light.

Rebel uniforms. Assembly officers. A stage set, dressed by professionals.

Below, the fifteen men in the fish market had noticed the blue sashes too. Tomas saw one of them laugh — actually laugh, slap his neighbor's shoulder — and call something up that the wind carried in pieces to the roof: *"—thought you'd never come! Where's the rest of you? We held the quarter, we held it—"*

They thought it was reinforcements.

"Oh," Devi whispered. He had seen it too. "Oh, gods. Oh, *gods*."

"Get down," Tomas said, and pulled him flat behind the parapet, and in the same heartbeat the first volley tore the morning open.

There was no order to fire that he heard. There was simply the sound — a long tearing rip across the whole width of the quay — and then the fish market was a different place than it had been. Men folded. Men spun. One boy, sixteen maybe, stood untouched for a full second amid the falling, looking around with an expression of pure bafflement, as if the world had played a joke whose punchline hadn't reached him yet. Then he went down too.

The second volley came before the echoes of the first had finished bouncing off the warehouses. Then a third. Then the field guns spoke, firing case shot into the covered arcade where a handful of the forty had taken shelter, and the arcade's old stone, which had stood through three storms and two fires, gave up its dignity along with its windows.

Tomas lay flat with his hands over his ears and discovered that his body was making a sound he couldn't stop, a thin sustained note through clenched teeth, and that Devi was shaking him, shouting his name, shouting that they had to move, that the marines were sweeping the side streets and the roof wouldn't stay safe past five minutes.

He got up because his legs decided to without consulting him. They went down through the warehouse — past sacks of salt that smelled absurdly, obscenely ordinary, past a cat asleep in a ray of light through a broken skylight — and out the loading door into a street that had become a river of people.

The volleys had done what the proclamations couldn't. Every soul left within earshot was fleeing, and the streets funneled them, and somewhere behind them the guns kept speaking in their calm, measured way, like clerks stamping documents.

*This is what it's for,* Tomas thought, wild and clear-headed at once, shouldering through the crowd with Devi's fist knotted in his coat. *All those ledgers, all those levies, all those polite years of Voss collecting his taxes with a smile. This is the document they were drafting all along.*

* * *

They might have made it clean out of the district if not for the child.

She was wedged under a fish cart near the tannery lane, maybe seven years old, hands over her head, utterly silent in the manner of children who have learned already that silence is survival. Her mother was nowhere. The crowd poured past her like she was a stone in a stream.

Tomas didn't remember deciding to stop. He was simply there, hauling her out by the shoulders, and she bit him — hard, on the wrist, drawing blood — without ever opening her eyes or making a sound, and he held on anyway, the way you hold a nettle, and said the only true thing available: "I've got you. I've got you, little fish. Bite all you like."

Something in the nonsense of it got through. Her eyes opened. She let him lift her onto his back, and locked her arms around his neck with a grip he would feel for weeks, and then Devi screamed *"Down!"* and shoved them both into the tannery doorway as a squad of marines came up the lane at double time.

Not after them. After a man ahead — a big dockworker, one of the forty who had run late, bleeding from the arm, limping badly. The marines took him at the mouth of the lane. Tomas pressed the girl's face into his shoulder so she wouldn't see, and could not press his own face anywhere, and so he saw all of it.

But it was afterward that mattered. Afterward, when the squad formed up to march their prisoner back toward the quay, an officer stepped out of an alley to meet them — plain coat, silver-threaded collar, riding crop — and spoke to the marine sergeant in a low voice. And the sergeant nodded, and the prisoner was marched not toward the Assembly garrison but toward the *seaward* stairs, the same stairs the false rebels had come up by, and Tomas understood with a cold, complete clarity that the dead in the fish market were only half the accounting. The other half would be reported as rebels killed by loyalists, or loyalists killed by rebels, whichever the final draft required.

History, being written in advance. Ink before blood. He didn't have the phrase yet — that belonged to Jonah, later, over lamplight — but he had the shape of it, heavy as an anchor, and he swore then, silently, into his sister's future ledger, that the shape would not hold.

The officer turned to go back down his alley, and the light caught his face, and Tomas's blood went from cold to boiling in a single pulse.

Captain Voss. Directing the costume parade personally.

"I'm going to follow him," Tomas said.

"You're going to get us *killed*," Devi hissed.

"Yes," Tomas agreed. "Both possible. Take the girl to the coal pier. Tell the Oseis their goose has been avenged."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny." It wasn't. But saying it steadied his hands, and his hands needed steadying, because they had already closed the girl's arms loose from his neck and passed her to Devi, and his feet were already moving.

* * *

He followed Voss the way his mother had taught him to move through a hostile city decades before he was born: close to walls, in the rhythm of other footsteps, never hurrying because hurry is the loudest thing a body does. Mira Nakamura's lessons lived in his bones even when his mind was elsewhere. *Walk like you belong to whatever street you're on,* she used to tell them, setting type one-handed while she talked. *Belonging is a costume anyone can wear. I've worn six.*

Voss crossed a yard, ducked through a cooperage, and emerged at the base of the seaward stairs, where a clerkish sort of man waited with a leather satchel and a lantern, though it was broad daylight. Papers changed hands. Orders, presumably. Voss read them, gave instructions Tomas was too far to hear, and the clerk hurried off toward the garrison.

And then Voss stood alone for perhaps thirty seconds at the foot of the stairs, checking a pocket watch, and the war handed Tomas a chance he neither wanted nor felt entitled to refuse.

There was a coil of mooring line on the piling beside him, and a boat hook, and thirty seconds.

He did not, in the event, need any of it. As he stepped from cover Voss turned — fast, a soldier's turn, hand going to his pistol — and the two of them recognized each other at the same instant. Six years of levy collection. Six years of *good afternoon, Master Nakamura, your father's shop keeps regular hours, I trust.* Voss's eyes went wide with something worse than fear: with being *seen*, with the whole arrangement of his life tipping over.

"Tomas Nakamura," Voss said. "You don't want—"

Tomas hit him. Once, with everything the morning had put in his arm. Voss went down against the stair rail and did not get up, and Tomas stood over him breathing like a bellows, trembling, discovering with distant surprise that he was crying and furious about both.

He hadn't meant to kill anyone today. He wasn't sure he had; the man was breathing, shallowly. What Tomas meant to do, he realized, was rob him.

The dispatch book was inside Voss's coat — a slim, oilcloth-bound volume with a pencil on a string, the kind officers carried for orders too sensitive to trust to couriers. Tomas took it. He also took the fallen papers the clerk had delivered, folded into his shirt, and Voss's crest-ring, on a sudden instinct he couldn't explain, and then he ran, because shouts were rising from the direction of the quay and his luck had been spent to its last coin.

* * *

They caught him at the boundary of the district, or nearly did.

A marine picket at the tannery arch saw him sprinting and fired, and the ball took him high on the left side, a blow like a maul wrapped in fire, spinning him into a stack of barrels that broke his fall and several of his ribs besides. He rolled into the drainage culvert beneath the arch — the one Rell knew, though Tomas didn't know yet that Rell knew it, the one that would matter so much in the days to come — and slid down its slick stone throat into darkness while shots sparked off the arch above him.

He crawled. It took him the better part of an hour to cover ground he could normally walk in five minutes, one arm useless, the dispatch book clamped under his chin like a terrier's bone, and every yard of it he repeated the same thing to himself in his mother's voice: *measure life in what you can carry, measure life in what you can carry.* He was carrying testimony. He was carrying names. He was carrying proof that the massacre had been staged, directed, scripted — and if his body failed between here and Brine Street, the proof would die in a ditch with him.

So he didn't fail. Stubbornness is a Nakamura inheritance deeper than any trade.

Devi found him at the culvert's mouth behind the ropewalk, white-faced, soaked in blood and drainage water, and for one horrible moment Devi thought he was carrying a corpse. Then Tomas said, through teeth gritted against the pain, "Is the goose safe?" and Devi laughed and wept at once, which Tomas considered the correct response.

They got him to the shop by back ways, by darkening lanes, by the kindness of strangers who owed the Nakamuras favors going back years. Mira met him at the door, took one look, and issued orders with the calm of a woman who had fled one country already and did not intend to lose this one to a flesh wound. Jonas's old workbench became an operating table. Jonah held the lamp. Elias Vane, pale as paper, boiled thread and needle.

And through all of it — the probing, the stitching, the biting down on the leather strap Jonah put between his teeth — Tomas kept one fist clenched around the oilcloth book, and when at last it was done and he was laid out on the cot by the press, gray-faced and alive, he opened his fingers one at a time and pushed it across the blanket toward his sister.

"Saltgate wasn't a battle," he rasped. "It was a *performance*. Assembly officers in rebel sashes, shooting dockworkers for an audience that never came. Voss ran it himself." He coughed, winced, went on. "The book's his dispatches. Every order, every date. And Jonah—" He caught her wrist, hard, with his good hand. "Whatever you find in there. However bad it is. It goes in type. All of it. Every name. Promise me."

Jonah looked down at the book, then at him, and he watched his sister's face change the way type changes when the ink first kisses it — something latent becoming legible forever.

"Open it," she said. "Let's see what we're setting."
