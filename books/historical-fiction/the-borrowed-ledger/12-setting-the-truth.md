## 12. Setting the Truth

The first shell landed somewhere past the tanneries at half past nine, and the whole shop shivered like a dog shaking off rain.

Jonah didn't stop setting type. She'd learned that trick years ago, during the dock riots of her girlhood: keep your hands moving and your body remembers it is working, not afraid. The composing stick sat steady in her left palm. In the right, a lowercase *a*, then an *r*, then an *m* — *armory requisition, dated the ninth* — each letter lifted from the case with the small practiced click that had been the sound of her entire childhood.

"You're slanting," said her mother from the proofing table.

"I am not slanting."

"Third line from the bottom. It leans east."

"Mira," Elias Vane said mildly, without looking up from the ledger he was transcribing, "I have watched your daughter set type for six days now under conditions I would describe as apocalyptic. If she leans a hair to the east, I suggest we forgive her."

"My daughter does not lean." Mira Nakamura held the galley up to the lamplight, squinted at it with the severity of a magistrate reviewing a death warrant — which, in a sense, she was — and pronounced judgment. "Unless she's tired. Sit down, Jonah. Drink something."

"I'll sit when the Saltgate page is locked in the chase."

"You'll sit when you fall over, more likely, and then who will lean my type?"

Another shell, closer this time. The windowpanes buzzed in their frames, and a fine sift of plaster came down from the ceiling and settled on the forme like gray snow. Rell, who had been sweeping the same corner of the floor for what Jonah suspected was the better part of an hour because sweeping gave him something to do with his hands that wasn't trembling, looked up at the ceiling and then at her.

"That's the third time it's snowed in here," he said. "Do we... should we be worried about the roof?"

"The roof has stood through two riots and one wedding where the groom's family got into a fistfight over the seating," Jonah said. "It can stand through this."

"It's not the shells I'd worry about," Elias said quietly. "It's what comes after them."

Nobody answered that, because nobody needed to. They all knew what came after shells. What came after shells was men with lists, and they were, at this very moment, by lamplight, making their own list longer.

* * *

The true ledger of Saltgate Wharf lay open on the big stone in the center of the room — the flattening stone their father had hauled up three flights of stairs on his back, swearing cheerfully the whole way, because a print shop without a proper stone was just a room full of alphabet soup. On top of it: the Assembly's own ledgers, stolen and copied, and beside them Tomas's dispatch book, recovered from a dead officer's coat, its pages stiff with someone else's blood.

Elias had spent the day turning those pages into plain words. Now the words waited in a neat stack of foolscap, and the work of the night was to turn them into metal.

Names first. That had been Mira's ruling, delivered hours ago with a finality that ended all debate before it began. *Every name. Every one. Spelled correctly.* Not summarized, not counted — the Assembly was very good at counting; forty-one dead could become "an incident involving casualties" in the space of a single well-set sentence — but named. Given their letters. Given the dignity of being typeset in the same face as everything else, no larger, no smaller, because the truth did not need to shout. It only needed to be legible.

"There are forty-one names," Elias had said, when she first said it. "Some of them we only have in the officers' records. Spellings may be approximate."

"Then we make them exact." Mira had been cleaning the press rollers at the time, and she had not stopped cleaning them, but Jonah had seen her hands go still for half a beat, the way they always did when she was about to say something that came from further away than this room. "I once received a document with my name spelled wrong. *Mira Nakamora.* One letter. And do you know what that one letter meant? It meant whoever wrote it had never bothered to learn how my people write ourselves. It meant I was a mistake to them before I was anything else." She'd resumed cleaning the roller with slow, even strokes. "These people died because of documents. We will not let them die twice because of one."

So: names first, exact, checked against the wharf guild rolls that Rell had fetched — at considerable personal risk, though he'd waved off the risk with a shrug that fooled absolutely no one — and cross-checked again by Elias, who had the archivist's gift for remembering where a fact lived even when the building it lived in was currently on fire.

Forty-one names. At roughly four minutes a name, with setting and correcting, that was most of a night, and the ink was already low.

* * *

"Ink," announced Rell at midnight, holding up the pot and tilting it so the lamplight showed the sad black slick at the bottom. "We have enough for perhaps... two pages?"

"We need five," Jonah said.

"Then we have a problem."

"We have a solution. You're going to walk to the chandler on Rope Lane and buy every stick of lampblack he has."

Rell stared at her. "Rope Lane is being shelled, Jonah."

"Rope Lane is *occasionally* being shelled. There's a rhythm to it. Twenty minutes between falls, sometimes thirty if they're reloading. My father used to say you could set a watch by artillery if you were foolish enough to stand near it."

"Your father also died in this shop, so perhaps his advice—"

"He died at his case, at seventy-two, with his boots off," Jonah said, "which is more than most of us will get, and he would have been the first one out the door for lampblack. Take the tunnel as far as the cooper's yard and come up behind the chandler's. And Rell—" He had his hand on the door already, and she caught his sleeve. "If the rhythm changes, you come home. The ink is not worth you."

Something crossed his face then — that flicker, quick as a moth, that had been crossing his face for days whenever anyone said the word *home* or *brother* or anything in between. "Right," he said. "Ink. Rhythm. Home." And he slipped out into the dark like a boy who had done exactly this sort of errand many times before, which, Jonah reflected as the door clicked shut, he probably had.

Mira watched the door for a moment after it closed. Then she went back to the case.

* * *

The hours found their shape. They always did, in a print shop, no matter what was falling on the city outside. Jonah worked the upper case, names and capitals; Mira worked the lower, text and punctuation; Elias read aloud in a low, level voice, feeding them lines from the transcription, and corrected galleys as they pulled proofs on the little hand press in the corner, the one they called *the widow* because it had outlived three owners.

"*Marcus Ilven,*" Elias read. "Wharf warden. Confirmed dead at the third warehouse. His name appears in the requisition ledger of the twelfth — the Assembly purchased, in his name, four hundred pounds of chain shot two days before he was killed by it."

"Say that again," Jonah said.

"Four hundred pounds of chain shot, purchased under Marcus Ilven's account."

"So they didn't just kill him. They made him pay for it." Her hands had stopped. She noticed, and made them start again. *I-l-v-e-n.* Into the stick. Each letter a small cold weight, and each weight somehow heavier than lead had any right to be.

"They were thorough," Elias said. There was no admiration in it. There was something worse: recognition. He had spent twenty years in the government archive, and Jonah understood by now that thoroughness was the thing he feared most in the world, because thoroughness was what he himself was made of. "That's what people don't understand about evil done on paper. It isn't sloppy. Sloppy evil gets caught. It's the careful kind that wins."

"Then we'll be careful too," said Mira, without looking up.

Another shell. Closer. The lamp flame bent sideways and the shadows swung around the room like startled birds, and when the light steadied, everyone was still in their places, which felt like a small victory. Jonah thought: *this is what Father meant.* She had spent her whole life hearing him say that type was honest work because the letters don't care who's in power — an *h* is an *h* for kings and rebels alike — and she had nodded along the way you nod at things your parents say, and only now, at twenty-six, with the windows rattling and her brother bleeding upstairs, did she feel it land in her chest like a struck bell.

They weren't printing a pamphlet tonight. They were printing a witness. Metal doesn't burn easy, doesn't forget, doesn't change its story when the new government offers it a pension. Whatever happened tomorrow — and none of them said the word *tomorrow* aloud, but it sat in the room with them, large as the press — these letters would still say what they said.

"Tomas is awake," Mira said suddenly.

Jonah looked up. Her mother had a way of knowing things about her children across distances and through walls, and sure enough, when Jonah climbed the stairs, there was Tomas propped against the headboard, gray as uninked paper but conscious, one arm bound and splinted, watching the ceiling as if reading it.

"You look terrible," Jonah said.

"You should see the other rooftops." He tried to grin and got about halfway there. "Is it true? Are you putting the names in?"

"All forty-one."

"Good." He was quiet a moment. The cannons muttered far off, arguing with themselves. "There was a woman on the quay," he said finally, to the ceiling rather than to her. "She was carrying a fish basket. Afterward the basket was standing upright next to her and she was—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "Her name is on your list. Ansa Corvel. Make sure the *r* and the *l* are both in it. People drop the *l*. It matters to the family. It mattered to her."

Jonah sat down on the edge of the bed and took his good hand. "Both letters," she promised. "*A-n-s-a C-o-r-v-e-l.*"

He nodded, and closed his eyes, and within a minute he was asleep, because the body of a man who has carried a dispatch book through a burning city takes its sleep wherever it can get it. Jonah sat a moment longer, memorizing nothing in particular, just the shape of her brother breathing. Then she went back downstairs and set *Corvel* with both letters, and pressed her thumb briefly against the finished line before moving on, the way her father used to touch a finished forme, as if to seal it.

* * *

Rell came back at three in the morning with lampblack, a split knuckle, a story he told in eleven words ("chandler's son, loyalist, we reached an arrangement"), and, inexplicably, a wheel of hard cheese.

"Why cheese?" Elias asked.

"He wouldn't take coin. Said it'd be worthless by Friday either way. So I took cheese." Rell set the pot of fresh ink on the stone with the reverence of a man laying down a reliquary. "Also I may have mentioned we print wedding announcements, and he wants one done for his niece, no charge, when all this is over."

"When all this is over," Mira repeated softly. She was mixing the new ink with the old, folding black into black with a leather brayer, and the motion seemed to comfort her. "Everyone says that. *When this is over.* Nobody ever says what they think 'over' looks like." She tested the ink against her thumbnail, nodded. "Jonah. Come look at this."

Jonah came. On the stone lay the locked forme of the second page: the orders themselves, the ones Elias had deciphered from the dispatch book, set clean and square. *By authority of the Merchant Assembly, Office of Internal Security, directive countersigned...* The date. The place: Saltgate Wharf. The instruction, in cold official prose: *clear the quarter.*

"Read it back," Mira said.

Jonah read it back. Her mother listened with her eyes closed, the way she listened to music, or to arguments she intended to win.

"Now read me what's missing."

Jonah scanned the line. "Nothing's missing. Names, dates, the counter-signature block—"

"The counter-signature." Mira opened her eyes. "In the original, the signature is a stamp, yes? A stamped seal, not written?"

"Yes. All the directives are stamped."

"Then our printed version must show a stamp. Not handwriting. Not a signature line. A *stamp*." She tapped the forme. "Because the moment the new historians want to call this forgery — and they will, Jonah, believe a woman who has been called a forgery herself — they will look for the one detail we guessed at instead of copied. We copy the smudge. We copy where the stamp sits crooked, here, see, the left edge heavier than the right. Truth lives in crooked edges. Forgers make things too tidy."

Elias had gone very still. "Your mother," he said to Jonah, "has forged documents before."

"Elias," said Mira pleasantly, "you are a guest in my shop."

"I mean it as the highest compliment available to me."

"I know. That's why I'm allowing you to live." But the corner of her mouth moved, and Jonah saw — not for the first time that week — the flash of the woman her mother must have been at twenty, crossing a border with a type case on her back and a lie in her pocket, and it steadied something in her chest. They came from stubborn stock. Stubbornness, in the Nakamura family, was practically a trade skill.

They cut a facsimile of the stamp from soft type-metal, Mira guiding the knife, copying the crooked edge exactly, the worn spot where the seal had been pressed a thousand times until its eagle's wing blurred. When Jonah pulled the first proof of the finished directive and held it up beside Elias's tracing of the original, even Elias — who trusted nothing, professionally speaking — let out a long breath.

"If I showed this to my old colleagues," he said, "they would weep. Half with fury, half with envy."

"Fury we can use," said Mira. "Envy we can print."

* * *

The ink ran out again at dawn. Of course it did.

Jonah scraped the last pots, thinning the dregs with the lampblack-and-oil mixture, and the color came out brown-black and slightly wrong, and Mira declared it acceptable in the tone of a woman declaring a storm acceptable, meaning: *we survive it.* They pulled the fifth and final page — the colophon page, the one Elias insisted on, though Jonah privately thought it mad — and there at the bottom, in small type, the line they had argued about for an hour:

*Set and printed by the Nakamura Press, Candlemakers' Row, in the sight of God and the Assembly, who may verify either.*

"Giving them the address," Rell had protested. "That's giving them the address."

"It's giving them the *truth*," Mira had said, "with an invitation."

Now the sheets hung drying on the lines strung across the shop, forty copies of five pages, names and dates and crooked stamps, swaying gently every time a distant gun went off, like a congregation nodding agreement. Jonah walked down the row slowly, reading names upside down and backward, the way a printer reads — *Ilven, Corvel, Osei, Brandt, the two Ferris sisters* — forty-one people rendered in metal and oil, permanent as anything humans make.

Elias stood at the window with the shutter cracked, watching the sky go the color of dirty pewter over the harbor. "The council will convene within days," he said. "Maybe hours. And the moment they do, their historians start writing. We have a window, Jonah. Small. Closing."

"Then we go through it."

"Through it," he agreed. "But not necessarily *out* the front door." He turned from the window, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before — not the weary archivist, not the guilty man, but a conspirator, and an oddly cheerful one. "Which reminds me. How many wedding announcements did you say your father taught you to set?"

Jonah looked at him. Then at the drying sheets. Then at the drawer where the old announcement borders lived, the doves and the laurels, the ornamental rules her father had loved beyond all reason.

Somewhere across the city, a church bell rang the hour, and the guns, for one strange minute, held their breath — and in that minute Jonah heard, very faintly, from the direction of the Assembly quarter, drums. Many drums. And beneath them, another sound she liked considerably less: voices. A great many voices, moving this way.

Rell appeared in the doorway from the stair, white-faced. "There's a crowd forming on Candlemakers' Row," he said. "And at the front of it—" he swallowed— "there's a man carrying a truce flag, and I'd swear on every type case in this room it's Aldous Grebe."
