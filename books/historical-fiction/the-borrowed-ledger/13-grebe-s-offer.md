## 13. Grebe's Offer

The truce flag was a tablecloth.

Jonah saw it from the upstairs window, bobbing above the crowd like a small white surrender tied to a broom handle, and for one absurd moment she thought of the wedding announcements — how her father had always said a good printer could make even a bedsheet look ceremonial. Aldous Grebe held the pole himself, which told her everything. A man who delegated his violence but carried his own flag wanted to be seen carrying it.

He stood at the mouth of Printwright's Lane with four men behind him and perhaps two hundred people beyond them, packed shoulder to shoulder in the grey morning light, their breath rising in clouds. Some of them held torches, unlit, which struck Jonah as the most Vellamare thing she had ever witnessed: a mob that hadn't decided yet whether it was a mob.

"He came at dawn," Mira said quietly beside her. Her mother's hands were folded, ink-stained as always, as though she were about to set a line of type rather than face down a crowd. "That's not courage. That's scheduling."

"Mother—"

"He wants you frightened before you've eaten. It's an old trick. Older than he is." She turned from the window. "Put water on for tea. If we're going to be threatened, we'll do it properly."

* * *

They received him in the front room of the shop, the one with the customer counter and the specimen books, because Jonah refused to negotiate in the back room where the ledger proofs lay hidden inside hollowed type cases under the floorboards. Grebe entered wiping his boots, which was either genuine politeness or a performance of it, and Jonah noted that his men stayed outside. So did the crowd. The lane held its breath.

Aldous Grebe was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with a printer's forearms and a merchant's belly, and hands that were beautifully kept — too beautifully, for a working pressman. He wore his ink like jewelry these days, a smudge on one cuff placed just so. His shop across the river did the Assembly's proclamations, its tax notices, its elegant little executions of reputation. Jonah had grown up hearing her father call him *a decent compositor with a landlord's soul*, which she now understood to be the kindest sentence anyone had ever constructed about him.

"Mistress Nakamura." He bowed slightly. "Mistress Nakamura the elder. You'll forgive the hour."

"You'll forgive the tea," Mira said, pouring. "We're low on everything except opinions."

He smiled the way men smile when they have rehearsed warmth. "I'll come directly to it, then, since I suspect we're all short on time. The city is about to change hands. Perhaps today, perhaps tomorrow. When it does, there will be accounts to settle, and I would prefer — genuinely prefer — that yours settle gently." He set a leather folio on the counter between them. "This is a list. Your names are on it. There is a second copy at the Assembly barracks, and a third in a place I won't describe. Arrest lists travel in threes; everyone knows this."

"And if we hand over what you want," Jonah said, "the lists burn."

"All three copies. I'd do it personally, with witnesses." He opened the folio and turned it toward her. There they were: *Nakamura, M. Nakamura, J. Nakamura, T.* And beneath them, in smaller type, *the archivist Vane, E. — harboring.* Someone had set Elias's name with care, giving him no dignity of abbreviation.

"What exactly is it you believe we have?" Jonah asked, and was proud of how level her voice came out.

Grebe looked at her with something almost like respect. "Mistress Nakamura. I have been a printer for thirty years. When a rival shop suddenly goes silent for three days and three nights, burning lamp oil at a rate no honest jobbing work could account for — I know what a press sounds like when it's running something long. And I know what runs long, at a moment like this, in a city about to fall." He tapped the counter once. "You're printing the truth. All of it. Names, dates, orders. And the moment those sheets leave your shop, every gallows in Vellamare becomes a very busy place."

"The truth tends to have that effect," Mira said mildly.

"It has *consequences*," Grebe said, and here his voice changed, dropped into something lower and more sincere, and Jonah realized with a cold turn in her stomach that he believed what he was about to say. "I have children, Mistress Nakamura. Two sons. One of them is fourteen and already taller than me. When the rebels take this city — and they will — someone will have to write down what happened. That is not a threat. That is arithmetic. History gets written by whoever is standing when the smoke clears, and I intend to be standing, and I am offering to make sure *you* are standing next to me." He spread his soft, beautiful hands. "Hand over the ledgers. Let reasonable people shape the record. Live."

There it was. Not cruelty — worse. Kindness, of a sort, the kind that had done its arithmetic correctly and arrived at cowardice. Jonah looked at the arrest list and felt the temptation settle over her shoulders like a coat cut precisely to her measure. Tomas was somewhere in the city with a wound in him. Her mother was sixty-one years old. Rell was seventeen and terrified. Hand over the paper, burn the lists, live — open a shop in some quieter town, set wedding announcements until her hands gave out, tell her grandchildren nothing.

It would be so easy. That was the obscene part. He wasn't asking her to betray anything she didn't already doubt at three in the morning.

"No," she said.

"Jonah," Grebe said gently.

"No," she said again, and found that the second one was easier than the first, which was not at all what she'd expected. "You've made an error, Master Grebe, and I'd like to point it out while the tea is still warm. You think you're offering us our lives. But we printed those pages knowing what they'd cost. We priced them already. What you're actually offering is a refund."

Something flickered across his face — annoyance first, then, briefly, something more wounded. A craftsman hearing his work called clumsy.

"You mistake my purpose," he said. "I'm not the villain of whatever pamphlet you're planning to print about this conversation. I'm a tradesman. I keep my shop running through every regime. My father kept it running through the last upheaval, and his father through the one before that. Presses outlive principles, girl. Somebody always needs announcements, notices, receipts. The dead need funerals printed and the living need weddings. I serve the city, not its masters."

"My father served the truth," Mira said, "and the city served him badly, and he never once confused the two."

For a moment the room was very quiet, and Jonah heard the crowd outside — a low murmur like the sea deciding something.

Then Grebe sighed, and folded his hands, and became a different man. Not softer. More precise.

"I had hoped to spare you the rest of this conversation," he said. "But you should understand your position fully. Outside that door are two hundred people who were told this morning, by reliable voices, that the Nakamura shop harbors Assembly agents and prints treason against *both* sides. That story is ready. It is typeset. It costs me nothing to release it, and by noon your mother will be a collaborator and your brother a spy, and no one in this district will lift a finger when the fire starts — indeed several will carry the torches gladly, because you will have made them feel foolish for ever having trusted you." He stood, buttoning his coat. "Give me until sundown. Think about what your name is worth. Bring the ledgers to the old customs house, or don't, and live with the arithmetic."

"There's one more thing I'd like to know," Jonah said, before she could stop herself. "Your story about us. The one that's typeset and ready. Who wrote it? Because the phrasing — *harborers of sedition, enemies of order* — that's not a tradesman's phrasing. That's not even a magistrate's. That reads like someone who loves the sound of his own sentences."

Grebe paused at the door, and Jonah watched him weigh the question, watched him decide — fatally, wonderfully — that flattering his employer cost him nothing.

"Cassian Merle," he said. "The Assembly's historiographer. He's been drafting the official account of this whole miserable year since last autumn — before the massacres, before the barricades, before any of it needed writing. Do you understand? The history is already finished, Mistress Nakamura. It sits in locked cases in the Assembly archive, set in type, awaiting events. Saltgate, the arrests, your family — all of it, composed in advance, waiting only for the dates to be filled in." He smiled thinly. "You can't beat a finished book. You can only hope to be mentioned kindly in it. Sundown."

And he was gone, the little white tablecloth bobbing away down the lane, the crowd parting around him like water around a barge.

* * *

Elias came up from the cellar gray-faced, having heard everything through the floor. "Merle," he said. "Cassian Merle. I know that name. I processed his requisitions for years — paper allowances, plate storage, private archive access. I always assumed he was a minor functionary with a vanity project." He sat down heavily on a type case. "He's been setting the official history in advance. Do you comprehend what that means? Events stop being things that happen. They become things that are *confirmed*. The massacre at Saltgate isn't waiting to be recorded — it's already recorded. It happened months ago, on paper. The world is just catching up to the manuscript."

"Then the world can catch up to ours instead," Jonah said.

"With what hours?" Elias asked. "Sundown, he said. And he wasn't bluffing about the story being released — I've seen that mechanism from the inside, Jonah. A rumor like that doesn't need to be true. It needs eleven hours to become true, which is all any rumor needs."

Jonah looked at the window, where the grey light was slowly warming toward gold. She counted the way her mother had taught her, measuring the day in portable things: hours left before sundown, sheets still drying in the cellar, names not yet set, a tunnel Rell knew, checkpoints between here and the wharf. Everything in the house weighed and measured, and none of it enough.

"Then we don't wait for sundown," she said. "We move tonight — no. We move *now*. Rell knows the tunnels. Elias, pull the proofs. Mother—" 

Her mother was already folding her apron, already moving toward the cellar stairs, and she paused only to say, with terrible calm:

"I'll pack the type. We were always going to carry it, Jonah. The only question was ever what else we'd be holding when we went."
