## 14. The Mob at the Door

Mira Nakamura had survived three wars, two famines, and one marriage to a man who alphabetized his spice jars, and she had learned this much: a crowd is only ever one story away from becoming something else.

She was in the kitchen when she heard them come — not the shouting first, but the sound underneath it, the shuffling of many boots finding a common rhythm. She knew that rhythm. It was the same in every country. She set down her knife, wiped her hands on her apron out of forty years of habit rather than any real need for cleanliness, and listened.

"Mother." Jonah appeared in the doorway, ink to her elbows, her face gone pale in the way it did when she'd been reading something she shouldn't. "There's a crowd on Corvane Street. Fifty of them, maybe more. Torches."

"At midday?" Mira asked.

Jonah blinked. "Yes."

"Then they're amateurs," Mira said, and went to get her shawl.

* * *

The shop smelled of lead and linseed oil and the particular dust that lives inside type cases, a smell Mira had carried across an ocean once and would carry into whatever came next. Through the front windows — shuttered now, since Grebe's visit — she could hear the crowd gathering itself like weather.

"They're saying we forged the confession," Elias said from the corner where he'd been cataloguing ledger pages with the nervous precision of a man who organized his fear into columns. "Grebe's people went through the market stalls this morning. By noon there were handbills." He held one up. It was crude work, Mira noted — crooked margins, ink too thick, the sort of printing done by someone who thought a press was a weapon rather than an instrument. *THE NAKAMURA TRAITORS OF CORVANE STREET*, it said. *COME SEE THE FORGERS HANGED.*

"Hanged," Tomas repeated from the stairs, where he stood with his arm still bound from Saltgate. "They've skipped trial entirely. That's efficient, at least."

"Tomas." Mira said his name the way she said it when he was being clever instead of useful.

"I'm going out the back," he said. "I can draw some of them off—"

"You will stay in this house with your torn shoulder and your jokes," Mira said. "Jonah, how many copies of the true ledger are finished?"

"Two hundred broadsheets. The rest is still in type."

"Then it stays in type. No one carries anything out tonight. Tonight—" she tied her shawl at her throat, "—I am going to have a conversation."

Jonah caught her arm. Her daughter's hands were strong; she got that from her father's side, all those years of hauling the press bed. "Mother. They have torches."

"Yes," Mira agreed. "And I have three languages and sixty years of practice making men ashamed of themselves. I like my odds."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't meant to be. Although," she added, because Jonah's face needed it, "if I am burned as a traitor, do put it on my stone that I died before finishing the mending. Let history record I finally escaped it."

* * *

The door of the Nakamura print shop opened at four o'clock in the afternoon, and out of it came a small woman in a gray shawl, carrying nothing but a wooden box the size of a bread loaf.

Mira had chosen the box deliberately. It was her first type case, the one she'd fled with, its brass corners worn bright by her own two hands. She held it against her hip the way other women held infants, and she walked down the three steps of her own doorstep and stopped at the bottom, and she looked at the crowd.

They were not monsters. That was the first thing, the thing she always saw and they never expected anyone to see. They were fishwives and dockhands and two boys from the tannery who couldn't have been more than fourteen, and a baker she'd sold wedding announcements to for years — Master Odell, flour still on his sleeves, holding his torch as if it had been pressed into his hands and he hadn't yet decided what to do about it. Their faces were the faces of people who had been told a story that made their hunger make sense. Hunger always wants a story.

"Well," Mira said, in Vellamarese, the port tongue, loud enough to reach the back row. "You've come a long way to burn down a print shop. I hope you brought your own paper. We're short."

A ripple of confusion. Somewhere in the crowd, someone laughed and then choked it down.

"You want traitors," she went on. "Very well. I will give you one. But you must let me tell it properly, because I learned this story in another language, and translated things lose something if you rush them."

Master Odell shifted. "Woman, stand aside—"

"I *am* standing aside," Mira said. "This is my doorstep. You're the ones standing in my street. In Vellamarese, we say: the guest who brings fire should also bring bread, or he is not a guest, he is only fire." She let that sit. Then she switched tongues.

The second language was Kessari, her mother's tongue, the flat northern vowels that had followed her across the sea like a dog that wouldn't be left behind. She spoke it loudly, deliberately, so they could hear the shape of words they didn't know — and then she gave it to them again in Vellamarese, sentence by sentence, the way she used to translate for the dock clerks, the way she'd taught both her children that every language is just another font and the meaning lives underneath:

*"Forty-one years ago, a girl stood on a dock in a city whose name no longer appears on maps, because the men who won that war decided the map looked better without it. She was seventeen. She owned one thing worth stealing: a case of metal letters, her father's trade, which he pressed into her arms at the gate and said, 'Go. Words weigh little. Carry them.'"*

She lifted the wooden box so they could see it.

*"This is that case. Everything else she owned stayed behind and burned — her bed, her mother's rings, her sister's letters, the garden with the plum tree she'd climbed since she could walk. The soldiers who burned it carried papers too. Official papers. Sealed documents naming her family enemies of the state. She never learned who wrote them. She suspects they were printed on a good press, by a careful man, with clean hands."*

In the crowd, faces had begun to turn toward each other. The word *sealed* moved along the rows like a current. They had all seen the sealed letter naming the Nakamuras. They had all heard it described.

Mira changed to the third language — the dock cant, the rough braided speech of sailors and stevedores, half Vellamarese and half everywhere else, the language of people paid by the crate. She spoke it with a stevedore's bluntness, and she watched the dockhands straighten, because here was a gray-haired woman speaking their tongue like she'd hauled rope beside them.

"Now listen, because this part is plain talk. Somebody came to this city with a story: that the family on Corvane Street forged a confession. Ask yourselves — who profits? Not us. We printed wedding announcements. We printed funeral notices. My husband spent thirty years setting type for other people's happiest days and saddest ones, and he died at that press with ink under his nails, and in all that time he printed exactly one lie, which was telling your mother-in-law that her son's announcement was the finest he'd ever set." A laugh went through the crowd this time, a real one, and Master Odell's torch dipped. "A printer who lies with his press is worse than a murderer, in our trade. A murderer kills a body. A liar kills the truth, and the truth doesn't grow back."

"So if we forged it," she said, returning to Vellamarese, stepping forward until she stood close enough to smell the pitch on their torches, "then read it. Bring me the confession. I will show you the ligatures — the joined letters, cut by my husband's own knife, worn in a pattern as personal as a fingerprint. If our type made that document, I will light the fire myself and stand inside it. And if it didn't — if some other press, some other careful man with clean hands made it — then you will have burned the house of the only people in Vellamarese who can prove you were lied to."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

It was one of the tannery boys who broke it. He couldn't have been fourteen. He looked at the torch in his own hand as though discovering it there. "My da said you printed his marriage lines," he said. "Said you didn't charge full price."

"We charged what he could pay," Mira said. "That was the arrangement."

Another voice, from deeper in: "My brother's name was on the arrest list. The real one. The one that got passed around before Grebe's handbills." A dockhand, big through the shoulders, his face working. "If the Nakamuras are traitors, why was *he* on it?"

And that was the crack, the hairline fracture that runs through a story when it meets a better one. Mira didn't push. She had learned long ago that you don't shove at a doubting crowd; you stand very still and let the doubt do its own work, the way water finds the seam in stone.

Master Odell lowered his torch. He looked at it, then at her, then at the type case against her hip, and something in his flour-dusted face folded. "My wife ordered our announcement from you," he said gruffly. "Twenty-two years ago. You added a line in the border. Little birds. She kept the proof copy in her jewelry box until the day she died." He turned and pushed back through the crowd, and the crowd parted, and where a man goes, others follow.

They thinned the way fog thins — not all at once, not in order, some drifting, some lingering, two boys snuffing their torches against the cobbles with the exaggerated casualness of the young pretending they were never part of anything. Within the hour the street held only smoke-smell and a few handbills trampled into the gutter, where the ink ran in the evening damp and the words *TRAITORS* blurred into something unreadable.

Mira stood on her doorstep a while longer, holding the case, watching the last of them go.

* * *

Inside, Jonah met her at the door and did not hug her, because neither of them was built that way; she took the type case from her mother's arms instead, which was the same thing in their family.

"Fifty people," Jonah said. "You talked down fifty people with a bread box."

"Type case."

"With a *type case* and three languages and a joke about mother-in-laws."

"The mother-in-law was true," Mira said. "All the best ammunition is."

Elias emerged from the back room looking as though he wanted to take notes and knew it would be inappropriate. "Grebe won't stop," he said quietly. "A crowd is one tactic. He has others. And the council — the rebel council — they'll have heard the handbills by now too. Stories travel faster than corrections."

"Then we print faster," Jonah said, and there it was, the set of her jaw that was her father's entire face reborn. "Elias — the broadsheets. The two hundred finished ones. How do we move them past the checkpoints?"

Mira watched her daughter's eyes go distant, calculating, reading the world as movable pieces the way she'd been taught — and then Jonah's gaze dropped to the stack of old job tickets on the counter, the wedding announcements and funeral notices waiting for delivery, and Mira saw the idea arrive in her daughter's face like a letter coming under a door.

"Mother," Jonah said slowly. "How many wedding announcements do we have queued for the west district?"

Mira smiled, and began untying her apron.
