## 15. Borrowed Names

There was a particular joy in setting a wedding announcement that Jonah's father had tried to explain to her a dozen times and that she had only come to understand the week he died. *Anyone can print a lie,* he'd said, locking up the chase on some happy couple's names in ornamental script. *It takes real craft to make the truth beautiful enough that people want to carry it home.*

He had loved this work best — not the pamphlets, not the Assembly contracts, but the small announcements. Weddings. Births. Funerals. The little formal fictions of ordinary life, set in type so lovely that families pinned them to walls for decades. He used to say a printer who could set a funeral notice with dignity could set anything, because grief forgave no clumsy hand.

Now Jonah stood at his old case at two in the morning, cannon light flickering orange against the shutters, setting a funeral notice for a woman who had never existed, and understood him completely.

"Read it back," Elias said from across the room, where he was folding sheets by lamplight with the fussiness of a man who had once been paid by the government to be fussy.

"*Dearly mourned, Seraphine Oduya of Candlemaker's Row, taken from us on the fourteenth by the sudden violence of these troubled days,*" Jonah read. "*Her family receives condolences at the house of her aunt, Marisol Vane, Quay Street.*"

"Vane," Elias said. "You've made me an aunt."

"You make a very convincing aunt. You have the disapproving mouth for it."

"I am thirty-nine years old."

"Aunts come young in wartime." She blew on the wet proof and held it up. "The mourning border is Father's. The one with the weeping willow corners. Nobody questions a notice with weeping willows. Grief makes customs officials polite."

Mira came down the stairs with a basket of laundry that contained no laundry — inside it, wrapped in a chemise, lay forty copies of the true account of Saltgate, folded to the size of a funeral card. She set it on the counter and looked over Jonah's shoulder at the type.

"The spacing is too generous," she said.

"It's a funeral, Mother. Funerals breathe."

"Funerals are also read carefully, which means they must be set tightly, or the reader notices the gaps before she notices the words." Mira reached past her daughter and slid two spaces out of the line with a fingertip, the way she had been doing since Jonah was six. "There. Now it grieves properly."

Jonah looked at the corrected line and felt something loosen in her chest. It was absurd, she knew — the city burning, armies in the eastern hills, their names on arrest lists — and yet here were three Nakamuras and one disgraced archivist hunched over a composing stone at midnight arguing about word spacing, and there was nowhere else in Vellamare she would rather be standing.

"How many?" Tomas asked from the doorway. He was bandaged at the shoulder where the Saltgate shrapnel had caught him, and he moved like a man carrying a sack of wet sand, but he was upright, and upright was more than half the dockworkers he'd pulled off that wharf could say.

"Forty funeral notices for the west district," Jonah said. "Thirty wedding announcements for the north gate. And twelve birth announcements for the harbor checkpoints, because nobody searches a man carrying news of a baby."

"A birth announcement," Elias repeated. "What does a birth announcement say?"

"'*Born to the household of Aldous Grebe, a son, eight pounds, healthy, loud.*'"

Elias stopped folding. "We're announcing Grebe's child?"

"We're announcing Grebe's *fictional* child, which I think is fitting, given how much practice he has with fictional documents." She tapped the stack. "And tucked behind each announcement is the ledger page. Same paper stock as the announcement itself — Grebe's own overrun stock, which I liberated from his pressroom — so if a soldier holds it to the light, it all looks like one sheet. The truth hides inside a lie about somebody's happiness."

Tomas laughed, then winced and held his ribs. "Father would have wept."

"Father would have complained about my kerning first, and then wept."

* * *

The problem, as always, was delivery. Paper could pass a checkpoint wearing a disguise; people could not. Jonah's face was known now — Grebe's men had seen to that — and Elias's face had been known since the Assembly dismissed him, and Mira's face had apparently been known since before either of them was born, a fact she declined to elaborate on.

Which left Rell.

He arrived an hour before dawn, coming through the cellar door instead of the street door because the street door was being watched by a man eating roasted chestnuts with the patience of someone paid to stand still. Rell was seventeen and had the particular gray exhaustion of a boy who had not slept properly in nine days, and when Jonah put the disguised broadsheets into his hands he stared at them for a long moment.

"This one says I'm delivering wedding announcements."

"You are delivering wedding announcements. They're just also the true history of the Saltgate massacre. Read the fine print."

Rell read. His lips moved slightly, which he hated and which everyone pretended not to notice. When he got to the list of names — the dead of Saltgate Wharf, spelled correctly, every one of them, because Mira had checked them three times against the dispatch book — his hands went very still.

"My brother isn't on this list," he said quietly.

"No. He's alive."

"He was there. At Saltgate. On the wrong side of it."

"I know," Jonah said. "And when this is over, he'll have to answer for it. But he'll answer to a court that's read the truth, not to a historian who's buried it. That difference is the whole reason we're carrying paper through tunnels at night instead of sleeping like sensible people."

Rell nodded slowly, and folded the announcements into the false bottom of the letter satchel he'd used for two years to smuggle messages between himself and his brother — letters of warning, mostly, each boy trying to save the other from the side they'd chosen. He knew every checkpoint clerk in the west district by name, knew which ones took bribes, which ones took flattery, and which one, a widow named Perpetua, took neither but could be moved to tears by any document involving children.

"Perpetua gets the birth announcements," Rell said. "She'll cry, and while she's crying she'll wave us through, and she'll never once look in the satchel. She lost a son in the dock riots. A baby announcement goes through her like a knife, poor woman."

"That's cold," Elias said.

"That's *accurate*," Rell said, with a shrug that was pure Mira, though he didn't know it yet. "My brother taught me: don't hate the checkpoint. Learn it. Every wall has a seam."

* * *

The tunnel under Tanner's Lane had begun its life as a drainage culvert, been promoted briefly to a smuggler's run during the tobacco wars, demoted again to drainage, and was now enjoying what Rell described as its golden age. It ran four hundred paces from the cellar of a shuttered tannery to a coal chute behind a chandler's shop on the far side of the first checkpoint line, and it smelled like exactly what it was.

"Mice," Elias said, holding the lantern high. "Or rats. Something with ambitions."

"Don't insult the residents," Rell said. "They held this tunnel longer than the militia did."

They went in threes — Rell first with the satchel, Jonah second with a second satchel strapped flat beneath her coat, Elias last with the lantern and a running commentary on the structural integrity of eighteenth-century masonry that Jonah eventually paid him to stop. The ceiling was low enough that even Rell stooped. Water seeped somewhere to their left with the sound of patient applause.

Halfway through, Rell stopped at a junction where three passages met, and put his hand on the wall, on a mark scratched there years ago: two strokes and a circle. His brother's sign, Jonah realized. The code of two boys who had turned a city's underground into a private post office.

"He marked every route," Rell said. "In case one of us forgot, or in case—" He didn't finish. "This junction means left is flooded, right is watched, straight goes through. Straight always goes through. He called it the honest road."

"And the dishonest roads?"

"Faster." Rell smiled in the dark, and for a moment he looked less like a frightened apprentice and more like whatever he was going to become. "But we take the honest road tonight. The paper deserves it."

Jonah touched the scratched mark herself, lightly, the way you touch a lintel you're asking permission from. Somewhere above them, artillery spoke twice, distant and unhurried, like a landlord knocking.

* * *

The chandler's shop on the far end belonged to a widow named Odile who owed the Nakamura shop a debt going back to a funeral notice printed twenty years ago at no charge, and who now kept the coal chute oiled and asked no questions, which Jonah considered the finest interest any debt had ever accrued.

By noon they had a system. Rell ran the tunnel with the satchels. Odile's grandnephew carried bundles from the chandler's to the market stalls in baskets lined with fish, because no soldier in the history of armies had ever volunteered to search under fish. From the market stalls, the announcements went out with honest peddlers to honest customers, wedding cards to the north district, funeral cards to the west, birth announcements through Perpetua's checkpoint in ones and twos.

And in kitchens and taverns and prayer houses across Vellamare, people opened a card that promised them a marriage or a mourning, and found instead the names of the dead of Saltgate Wharf, and the orders that had killed them, and the signature of the officer who'd given them — in Assembly dress, worn over rebel uniforms, exactly as Tomas had sworn.

"They'll figure it out," Elias said on the second evening, sorting returned sheets. Some came back to them — people passed them hand to hand, and a few copies drifted back to the shop bearing wine rings and candle burns and, on one, a child's careful crayon copy of the willow-corner border. "Grebe's not stupid. Sooner or later someone opens a wedding announcement at a checkpoint and finds a massacre inside."

"Then we change the disguise," Jonah said. "That's the whole art, Elias. Father taught me that too. The Assembly thinks power is owning the press. Power is owning the *forms*. Anyone can seize a print shop. Nobody can seize the idea that truth travels better dressed as something ordinary." She held up the child's crayon copy. "Look at this. Somebody's daughter copied our border. By next month there'll be a dozen shops running willow-corner funerals, and Grebe won't know which one to burn."

Elias looked at the crayon for a long time. "When the new government writes its history," he said finally, "I hope they write about the fish baskets."

"The new government," Jonah said, "will write about whichever generals are standing closest. That's why we keep printing."

* * *

On the third night it nearly ended at Perpetua's checkpoint, and it ended instead with laughter, which Jonah counted among the strangest gifts the war gave her.

Rell told it later, sitting on the cellar stairs with his boots off, wringing river water out of his sock. He'd come through with twelve birth announcements — Grebe's fictional son was now, per the running joke, nearly two years old and walking — and Perpetua had waved him through with red eyes as always. But a new officer had been assigned beside her that evening, a young lieutenant with the Assembly crest still bright on his collar, and he had demanded to inspect the satchel.

"So I open it," Rell said, "and he takes out a birth announcement, and reads it, and his face does something complicated. And then he says, very official, 'Aldous Grebe? The printer?' And I say yes, sir, the printer's household, a healthy boy. And the lieutenant says—"

Rell had to stop and laugh into his sleeve before he could finish.

"—he says, '*That man owes me money.*' And he waves me through. Fast. Like the announcement might ask him for a loan."

Even Mira laughed, a short surprised sound she immediately pretended she hadn't made, and Elias raised his lamp in salute to the late lieutenant, wherever he was, and Jonah sat down on the stair beside Rell and felt the whole strange machine of it humming around her — the tunnel, the fish, the widow, the debt-ridden lieutenant, the willow borders multiplying across the city like frost.

Her father had set ten thousand announcements in this shop. Weddings, births, funerals. Small true things dressed in borrowed beauty. He had never once imagined the trade running backward — lies of ordinary life carrying the truth inside them, out through the seams of a dying city.

Somewhere below them, faint through the floor, the tunnel breathed its damp breath. Somewhere above, a patrol went by singing. And on the counter lay the night's last bundle, already addressed: twelve copies for the harbor district, disguised as—

Jonah frowned, and picked up the top sheet, because it was not her type. The paper was right, the willow border was right, the ink was even right — but the hand that had set it was not hers, and not Rell's, and not anyone's in this house. The announcement read:

*Wedding announcement: Miss Verity Nakamura to Captain Aldous Grebe, ceremony to be held at the Merchant Assembly hall —*

And beneath it, in smaller type, almost hidden in the flourish: *Bring your ledgers. We know where the presses sleep.*

Mira read it over her daughter's shoulder. Then she walked to the window, drew back the curtain one inch, and looked out at the chestnut-seller's empty brazier across the street.

"He's stopped watching the front door," she said quietly. "Because now he knows we come out the other way."
