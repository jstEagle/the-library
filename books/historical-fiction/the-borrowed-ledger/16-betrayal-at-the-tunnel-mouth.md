## 16. Betrayal at the Tunnel Mouth

The trouble with tunnels, Rell had always thought, is that they only go two ways. In. Out. There was no clever third direction, no side door for a boy whose heart was being pulled apart like wet paper. A tunnel was the most honest thing in Vellamare, and that was exactly what made it unbearable.

He walked ahead of them with a candle stub cupped in his palm, counting arches under his breath the way his brother Dain had taught him when they were small and smuggling letters through these same brick throats — love notes mostly, at first, then debt notices, then the little poisoned papers the Assembly's clerks liked to pass hand to hand so nothing incriminating ever traveled by road.

*Forty-one arches from the tannery grate,* Dain used to say, tapping Rell's nose with a finger that smelled of ink and pipe smoke. *Count them twice and you'll never be lost. Count them three times and you're frightened.*

Rell was on his fourth count.

Behind him came Jonah Nakamura, carrying the satchel the way she carried everything — close, quiet, as though the whole weight of the city could be balanced on one hip if you just found the right fulcrum. Then Mira Nakamura, who moved through the dark like a woman strolling through her own kitchen, unhurried, unbothered, occasionally reaching out to steady herself against the damp brick with the tips of her fingers, as if greeting old acquaintances. And last, limping slightly but refusing to say why, Tomas, who kept glancing over his shoulder toward the distant orange glow bleeding down the tunnel mouth behind them.

"Fire's reached Cooper's Row," Tomas said, for the third time.

"I heard you," Rell said.

"Just noting it."

"You've noted it twice already."

"Well, now I've noted it thrice," Tomas said, "and we all feel better informed."

Mira made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a prayer; with her it was often both. Rell felt something in his chest loosen by a single degree. That was the cruelty of it — he loved these people. Not the way an apprentice loves a trade, which is really a love of one's own hands, but the actual way, the dangerous way. He loved Jonah's stubborn chin and Mira's three-language scolding and even Tomas's terrible jokes, which were so bad they circled back around to being load-bearing. And because he loved them, he had led them into the one place in all of Vellamare where his brother might be standing.

Because Dain would be standing there. Rell had known it since sundown, since the note slipped under the shop door in handwriting only Rell could read — the looping, backward-tilted script their mother had beaten into them both before she died: *Tunnel mouth at Saltgate Rise, midnight. Bring yourself or don't come.* Dain did not write *or else*. Dain never wrote *or else*. That was somehow worse.

The dispatch book pressed against Rell's ribs where he'd tucked it inside his coat. The officer's book. The one Tomas had bled for on a rooftop while the wharf burned below. It sat there like a second heart, beating out of rhythm with his own.

They passed the forty-first arch. Rell counted it twice, as instructed. He did not count a third time, because he was not frightened, he told himself, he was merely walking very carefully, which any sensible person would do in a tunnel full of gunpowder smoke and history.

* * *

The exit grate came into view at last — a circle of gray light freckled with drifting ash, like looking up at a dirty moon. Beyond it, Saltgate Rise climbed away in terraces, most of its windows dark, a few burning cheerfully, the way buildings did when soldiers were feeling thorough. Somewhere above, a bell rang without conviction, the kind of ringing that said *curfew, probably, unless you have better plans.*

Rell stopped. Held up his hand.

Jonah bumped into his back. "Sorry. Why are we stopping?"

"Resting," Rell said.

"We've rested four times."

"And this is the fifth. Rests are cumulative. Ask any printer."

Mira, behind them, said mildly, "In my country we had a saying about boys who lie badly."

"What was it?" Tomas asked.

"That they should become printers, because at least the lies would be spelled correctly."

Rell laughed despite himself, and hated himself for laughing, and then heard the sound that ended everything easy.

Boot heels on stone. Measured ones. The particular measured tread of a man who has been told to walk somewhere and is walking there.

The grate above them went dark.

For a moment nobody breathed. Then a lantern flared at the tunnel mouth, held high, and the light fell down the shaft in a long yellow sheet and landed on the face of Rell's brother.

Dain looked terrible. That was the first thing. The second thing was that he looked magnificent, which was worse — uniform brushed, boots polished, jaw set in the exact configuration their mother had called *your father's stubbornness wearing my son's face*. There was ash in his hair and a fresh cut over one eyebrow, and his hand rested on his pistol the way a man rests his hand on a doorframe: not threateningly, just to remind everyone the door exists.

"Dain," Rell said. His voice came out younger than he wanted.

"Rell." Dain's eyes went past him, cataloguing: Jonah, Mira, Tomas. The satchel. The satchel especially. "You brought them."

"You told me to bring myself."

"I told you to bring yourself *without* the entire criminal enterprise of the printing quarter." Dain sighed, and for one flickering second the officer fell away and he was just a tired older brother standing in a doorway at midnight. "Hello, Mistress Nakamura. You look well, considering."

"I look terrible," Mira said pleasantly, "and so do you. Come down here where your mother can see your face properly."

"Mira—" Dain began, and stopped, because Mira Nakamura had been calling herself his mother in moments of maximum tactical advantage for six years, and it worked every time, and they both knew it.

He came down anyway. Boots on rungs, lantern swinging, until he stood in the tunnel among them, and the space that had seemed large enough suddenly was not.

* * *

What followed was, Rell would think later, the strangest negotiation conducted in Vellamare during the whole war, and possibly in the whole history of arguments: four people and a soldier, by lantern light, in a tunnel that smelled of tallow and rat, while a city burned overhead.

"You know what I'm ordered to do," Dain said quietly. "There's a list. Their names are near the top. Anyone leaving the district with printed material is to be detained and the material burned. Those are the orders. I didn't write them."

"No," Jonah said. Her voice was steady, but Rell saw her knuckles white around the satchel strap. "You just carry them. There's a word for the part someone plays after the writing is done, Dain. We set it in type once. It had a lot of letters."

"Jonah." Dain rubbed the bridge of his nose. "I pulled your brother out of a press gang when he was fourteen. I stood between your father and an Assembly inspector for two hours on a dock in the rain. Don't quote type at me."

"He's right, he did," Tomas offered helpfully. "It was a very long rain."

"Tomas," said three people at once.

But something in the mention of the dead man — of Jonah's father, of the shop, of the worn ligatures that had betrayed them all — softened the air. Or hardened it. Rell couldn't tell which. With family, he thought, those were often the same thing.

"The satchel," Dain said finally. "Give it to me."

"No," said Jonah.

"Jonah—"

"No." She stepped forward, and Rell saw her plant herself the way she must have planted herself at the case a thousand times, feet square, chin level. "You'll burn it. Not because you want to. Because if you don't, someone else will, and you'd rather it be your hands than theirs, because at least yours would shake. I know you, Dain Ferrow. Your brother talks about you the way other people talk about saints. Saints burn things too, when they're ordered to."

"That's not fair," Rell burst out, and then, horribly, "both of you, that's not — neither of you is being fair, and also you're both completely right, and I can't—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "I'm the one who copied the arrest list. I'm the one who knows Grebe's compositor. I'm the one who's been passing letters for both sides since I was eleven years old, and I am *tired*, and I would like everyone to stop pretending this is simple."

Silence. Even the fire outside seemed to lean closer to listen.

Dain looked at his little brother for a long moment. Then he did something no officer in the Merchant Assembly's service would have done: he took his hand off his pistol, sat down on a wet step, and put his head briefly in his hands.

"She's right," he said, muffled. "My hands would shake."

"Dain—"

"They shook at Saltgate, Rell." He lifted his head, and his eyes were wet and furious and young, suddenly, all the polish burned off him. "You weren't there. I was. In uniform, watching men in our uniforms do what was done, and holding a dispatch book I wasn't supposed to read, and reading it anyway, because I'm a coward in exactly the direction that matters." He looked at Tomas. "Is that the one? The book from the rooftop?"

Tomas's hand drifted toward his coat. Mira's hand drifted toward Tomas. Nobody moved fast.

"It's mine to take," Dain said. "Not to destroy. To take. There's a difference, and the difference is the only decent thing I've got left."

* * *

Here is what Rell understood then, standing in the tunnel between his brother and the people who had become his other family, watching the lantern flame bend in the draft: justice was a clean thing in stories. In stories, the good evidence went to the good people and the bad people got arrested in orderly rows. But real justice, the kind you could actually hold in your hands at midnight, came apart in your grip like cheap paper. You had to decide which half to keep and which half to trust to somebody else, and every choice cost somebody something they loved.

Dain wanted the dispatch book. Of course he did. It was proof of Saltgate, proof of the massacre, proof of the officers in rebel colors — and it was also, if the wrong person learned he had it, a death warrant with his name pre-printed. But more than that, Rell realized, watching his brother's face: Dain wanted it because taking it was the only way he knew how to stand on both sides of the door at once. Family duty pointed one way. The truth pointed the other. The book let him hold one in each fist.

And Jonah — Rell saw her working it too, her printer's mind moving behind her eyes, weighing, measuring, the way she measured everything by what could be carried. The dispatch book was heavy in every sense. If Dain took it, the proof split in two. Half in the satchel going to the wharf, half in a soldier's coat going God knew where. Two halves of the same truth, each one incomplete alone, each one safer apart than together.

She was quiet for a long time. Then she said, "You'll copy it."

"What?"

"Every page. Tonight. Before dawn. You keep your copy, we keep the original. If anything happens to either half, the other survives." Her chin came up. "You taught Rell to count arches twice. Consider this counting twice."

Dain stared at her. "You want me to sit in a tunnel and forge documents."

"Not forge," said Mira. "*Copy*. There is a difference, and my children can tell you the difference in three languages."

A sound escaped Dain that in any other man would have been a sob and in him was a laugh, barely. "You are the most infuriating woman in Vellamare."

"I have raised two apprentices and buried one husband. Infuriating is the least of what I am."

So it was decided, the way things were decided in families — not by agreement exactly, but by everyone getting tired at the same moment. They spread the dispatch book open on a dry stretch of step. Dain produced paper from his coat, because of course he did, an officer always carried paper for writing down other people's crimes. Rell held the lantern. Jonah read dates aloud in a low clear voice, and Mira corrected Dain's numerals twice, and Tomas kept watch at the tunnel mouth making comments about the quality of the burning that nobody asked for.

Rell watched his brother write. Watched Dain's hand move through the names of the dead — the orders, the times, the officer's signature — copying each one faithfully, and understood that this was the choice, happening right in front of him, in real time, in bad light. His brother was choosing. Not between family and duty. Between the version of duty that obeys and the version that witnesses. It was costing Dain everything visible and probably several things invisible, and he kept writing anyway, tongue caught between his teeth the way he'd done since childhood over any difficult letter.

"You still do that," Rell said softly.

"Hm?"

"When you concentrate. The face."

Dain didn't look up. "Shut up and hold the lantern straight."

"Yes, sir," Rell said, grinning, meaning none of it, meaning all of it.

When the last page was copied, Dain blotted the sheets, folded them into his breast pocket over his heart, which was where he kept everything he couldn't afford to lose, and closed the original dispatch book. He held it a moment. Then he handed it back to Tomas.

"There," he said. "Split. Half with you, half with me. If the council on the wharf wants the whole truth, they'll need us both alive, so try not to die, all of you. It would make my paperwork impossible."

"And you?" Jonah asked. "When they ask what happened at the tunnel mouth?"

Dain stood, brushed off his knees, and became an officer again from the boots upward — but slowly, reluctantly, like a man putting on a coat that no longer fit. "They'll ask what happened at the tunnel mouth," he agreed. "And I will report that I searched it thoroughly and found nothing but rats and sentimental printing apprentices." He looked at Rell. "Which reminds me."

He crossed the space between them in two steps and hugged his brother, hard, briefly, the way Ferrow men did everything — all at once, without warning, never mentioning it afterward.

"Go," Dain said into Rell's hair. "Out the grate, left along the wall, third drain, the low one. Don't stop till the wharf. And Rell—" He pulled back, hands on his brother's shoulders. "Whatever they print about tonight, whoever wins — you make sure it's spelled right. Promise me."

"Count it twice," Rell said.

His brother nodded once, turned, and climbed the rungs into the ash-falling dark, and the grate scraped shut above them, and the gray moon-circle of light returned, and he was gone.

Nobody spoke for a moment. Then Tomas shifted the dispatch book inside his coat and said, "Well. I've been shot at, robbed a corpse, and witnessed a family argument in a sewer. This revolution has everything."

"Up the ladder," said Jonah. "All of you. The council reads at dawn."

Rell went first, as always. Forty-one arches behind him, one brother ahead of him somewhere in the burning city, and half a truth tucked against his ribs like a second heartbeat — enough to end a war, maybe, but only if the other half survived the night.

Above him, through the grate, the sky over the Assembly archive glowed a strange, hungry orange, brighter than any fire he'd seen yet.

Someone, he thought, climbing, was burning paper.
