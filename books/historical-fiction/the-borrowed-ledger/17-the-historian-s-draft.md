## 17. The Historian's Draft

Elias Vane had spent thirty-one years in the Assembly's archive, and in all that time he had learned one lesson above all others: no one guards a room full of paper as carefully as they guard a room full of gold. Gold could buy a knife. Paper could rewrite the knife's victim out of existence entirely. And yet the archivists of Vellamare had always been treated as a kind of furniture — necessary, dull, beneath suspicion.

Which was why, on the morning the regime collapsed, Elias walked through the Assembly's front doors wearing his old gray coat and carrying a ledger satchel, and not one of the panicked clerks gave him a second look.

The building was dying in stages. In the great hall, undersecretaries were feeding documents into braziers with the grim efficiency of men who understood exactly which signatures would hang them. Down the west corridor, someone had already pried the brass nameplates off the doors — Aldous Grebe's patron, First Secretary Ombre Vail, had fled two days prior, though his door still bore the ghost of his title in the faded varnish. Somewhere above, a window had been left open to the smoke drifting off the harbor, and ash fell gently onto the marble floor like the world's most ominous snowfall.

"Master Vane." The doorkeeper, old Sef, looked up from a half-packed crate of candlesticks and blinked at him with genuine surprise. "I thought they'd dismissed you months ago."

"They did," Elias said. "I've come to return my keys."

It was even true. He had the keys. He had simply neglected to mention that he'd had copies made before his dismissal, or that he intended to use them for purposes the Assembly would have found considerably less tidy than key-returning.

Sef waved him through with the weary courtesy of a man who had decided that whatever happened next was above his pay grade. "Mind the third floor. The historians are up there packing."

"The historians," Elias repeated, "are *packing*."

"Boxes since dawn. Very particular about it, too. Wouldn't let the porters touch anything."

Elias climbed the stairs slowly, letting the familiar creaks count themselves under his feet. Third floor: the Office of Records and Commemoration, established eleven years ago by decree, its purpose stated in language so smooth it took three readings to find the teeth in it. *To ensure that the historical record of Vellamare reflects the settled conclusions of the Merchant Assembly.*

Settled conclusions. Elias had spent his career among unsettled ones, and he had come to believe that the phrase should be printed on every tyrant's letterhead, right beneath the crest.

* * *

The Office of Records and Commemoration smelled wrong.

Elias noticed it before he saw anything — ink, yes, and the waxy smell of fresh stereotype plates, but underneath it something sweeter, newer. The archive proper, down in the vaults, smelled of dust and patience and the slow chemistry of things aging honestly. This room smelled of haste.

Rows of composing stones stood where the reading tables used to be. Galley trays lined the walls, each labeled in a neat clerk's hand. And at the far end, beside the plate-press, sat the thing he had come to find — though he did not know its shape until he saw it.

A book. Typeset, locked into its chase, ready for plating. Not a manuscript awaiting approval. Not a draft circulating for comment. A finished object, galleys imposed and corrected, waiting only for metal and paper and time.

*The True History of the Late Unrest in Vellamare,* read the running head. *Compiled by Authority of the Merchant Assembly.*

Elias set down his satchel very carefully, the way one does when one's hands are shaking and one wishes to pretend otherwise.

He began, as he had begun ten thousand times in his career, at the beginning. The title page. The preface, four pages of it, written in that particular prose he thought of privately as "marble veneer" — everything polished, nothing load-bearing. He skimmed. Then his eye caught a date, and stopped skimming.

Saltgate Wharf. The ninth day of the rising.

He read the paragraph twice, because the first time his mind refused to accept what his eyes reported. *At Saltgate Wharf, rebel agitators — having seized the harbor gates and taken the customs officers hostage — turned their cannon upon the crowded quay. Some four hundred souls perished, martyrs to the rebels' ambition. The Assembly's forces, arriving too late to prevent the atrocity, could only mourn.*

Could only mourn. Elias thought of Tomas Nakamura, carried back to the shop with a hole in him and an officer's dispatch book in his bloodied coat, describing what he had seen from the rooftop: Assembly officers in rebel uniforms, giving the order in Assembly voices. Four hundred people. He had helped set their names in type by lamplight while cannon shook the windows, Mira insisting on every spelling, Jonah's hands steady over the case even when the glass cracked in its frames. Every name spelled correctly. That had been the whole liturgy of those nights — *get the names right, get them right, get them right* — and here was this book, this beautiful, finished, patient book, prepared to get every single one of them wrong forever.

And then he found the Nakamuras.

He had known, in the abstract, that the forgery campaign would end somewhere like this. He had told Jonah as much, back when their alliance was still mostly suspicion wrapped in mutual guilt. But knowing a lie is coming and meeting it in print are different countries, and the crossing between them is bitter.

*Mira Nakamura,* the text announced, *matriarch of a family long suspected of sedition, perished in the fighting — by her own hand, some say, rather than face the justice she knew awaited her conspirators.* Her sons and daughter were listed among the *traitors whose names are struck from the rolls of honest citizens.* There was even a footnote, scholarly and precise, citing a confession *recovered from the family's own press* — the forged confession, the one bearing his father's worn ligatures, the document that had started all of it.

Martyred traitors. It had a certain poetry. It was also, Elias reflected, the kind of sentence that gets people killed after they're dead, which takes a special talent.

"You're not supposed to be here."

Elias did not jump. Thirty-one years in archives teaches you to be startled quietly, internally, and without spilling anything. He turned.

A young man stood in the doorway with an armful of galley proofs and the hunted look of someone who had been awake too long. Elias recognized him vaguely — Pellam? Pellar? One of Vail's bright young historians, the sort who quoted precedents at parties.

"And you're supposed to be where?" Elias asked mildly. "Everyone else has fled."

"I'm saving the record," the young man said, with the earnestness of the recently converted. "This history must survive the city's destruction. It's the most important document of our age."

"Mm," said Elias. "Tell me about Saltgate."

"What?"

"Saltgate Wharf. Who fired the cannon?"

The young man's jaw firmed. "Rebel agitators. As the text makes clear—"

"Because I've seen the Assembly's own dispatch orders," Elias said. "Signed orders. Countermanding nothing, explaining nothing, directing the wharf batteries to fire on the crowd. I've held them. I've read them aloud to a room of witnesses. So you understand my difficulty: either your book is wrong, in which case it is worthless, or the Assembly's orders are forgeries, in which case I'd love to hear how rebels managed to forge documents on Assembly watermarked stock, inside the Assembly's own countinghouse, days before the rising."

The young man stared at him. Something moved behind his eyes — not doubt, not yet, but the raw material of it, the ore before smelting. "You're Vane," he said suddenly. "The one who was dismissed. For refusing the burn directive."

"For refusing to burn things, yes. It's become something of a theme."

"They said you were—" He stopped. Recalibrated. Looked down at the proofs in his arms, and then, terribly, at the typeset book itself, and Elias watched him do the arithmetic that ruins careers: if this is a lie, then I helped set it.

"The plates," Elias said gently. "Where are the plates for the first printing?"

The young man didn't answer. He didn't need to. His eyes flicked — just once, just barely — toward the plate cabinet against the north wall, and then away, and he set his stack of proofs down on the nearest stone with the care of a man surrendering a weapon, and walked out of the room without another word.

Elias hoped, distantly and not unkindly, that the boy would land somewhere softer than disgrace. He himself had landed in a print shop that smelled of lamp oil and stubbornness, surrounded by people who measured life in portable things, and it had turned out to be the best posting of his career.

But first: theft.

* * *

Righteous theft, Jonah had called it, when he'd explained the plan to her the night before, and she'd laughed — actually laughed, the first time in days — and said, *Only you would steal something and file the guilt under virtue.*

"It's filed under necessity," he'd protested.

"Everything in an archive is filed under something," she'd said. "That's the whole disease."

Well. He opened the plate cabinet now and found the disease in its terminal stage. Dozens of stereotype plates, each wrapped in oiled cloth, each heavy as sin: the full first edition of the official history, cast in metal, meant to fix the lie permanently. Plates survived fires. Plates survived new governments. Whoever held these plates could reprint the false history in any city, any decade, under any flag — and metal does not blush, does not recant, does not confess its forger's ligatures.

He could not carry them all. He was fifty-eight years old and had the upper-body strength of a man who had wrestled ledgers, not cannons. He made the cold calculation of the archivist: take the chapters that kill.

Saltgate. The forged confession and its citation. The section on the Nakamura family, martyr-traitors, names struck from the rolls. And the appendix — he almost missed it — a list of persons designated for *historical correction*, which was a bureaucratic way of saying that certain living witnesses were expected to die inconveniently and be remembered wrongly. His own name appeared. So did Mira's, and Jonah's, and Tomas's, and, touching beyond measure, Rell's — the apprentice listed as *accomplice (minor), amend records accordingly.*

They had even planned the corrections in advance. Somewhere in this building there was presumably a schedule for the lies, the way other institutions kept schedules for deliveries.

Elias wrapped nine plates in the oiled cloth, packed them into his ledger satchel, and found, when he lifted it, that righteousness weighs roughly forty pounds. He adjusted the strap, straightened his gray coat, and paused — because an archivist's discipline dies hard — to leave a note on the empty shelf, in his small precise hand:

*Removed for correction. The error was yours.*

Then he heard boots on the stairs, many of them, descending fast from the fourth floor, and reconsidered several of his life choices.

* * *

There was, Elias would reflect later, a certain symmetry in escaping the Assembly's archive by way of the route he'd once mapped for smuggling documents *into* it — the service stair behind the plate room, down past the bindery, out through the loading yard where the delivery carts came and went. He had drawn that route in his head years ago, back when he still believed the system could be corrected from within. The system had declined correction. The route, however, remained excellent.

The yard was chaos. Carts being loaded with anything portable and valuable; a sergeant arguing with a clerk about whether official seals counted as loot; smoke thickening over the eastern wall where the harbor district burned. Elias pulled his collar up, became furniture, and shuffled toward the street with the unhurried gait of a man who belonged wherever he was standing.

"Hold!" the sergeant barked. "What's in the bag?"

"Records," Elias said. "Being evacuated for safekeeping. Assembly property."

The sergeant squinted at him, at the gray coat, at the satchel strap cutting grooves in Elias's shoulders. Forty pounds of stolen history, and the sergeant visibly concluded that whatever was in the bag, the man carrying it was too tired and too dull to be worth the trouble. "Get it out of my yard."

"Gladly," said Elias, who had never meant anything more sincerely.

The streets of Vellamare were coming apart in the orderly disorder of a city changing owners. Barricade crews waved him past when he showed them nothing but his empty hands. A baker pressed a heel of bread on him — "You look like a man who reads, and readers faint" — refusing payment with the fierce generosity of a man who had decided, today, that the old world owed him and the new one might yet owe him better. Elias ate the bread walking, and thought that if the revolution accomplished nothing else, it had produced excellent bread distribution, and that Jonah would find this observation both funny and damning.

By the time he reached the print quarter the light had gone coppery with smoke, and the first thing he saw was wrong. Wrong in the specific, practiced way that made his stomach drop: the angle of the smoke, too low, too black, coming from the direction of Nakamura & Sons.

He ran. An archivist's run — undignified, satchel banging, forty pounds of stolen certainty hammering his spine — past shuttered windows and a barricade half-built and abandoned, around the corner onto the shop's street.

Soldiers at the far end. Retreating column, moving fast, and one of them pausing at the corner with something in his hand — a flask, a taper, Elias couldn't tell at this distance, couldn't see, couldn't—

The man bent toward the dry timber of the cooperage across the lane, and flame caught, and the wind off the harbor did the rest.

Fire in the print quarter spreads like gossip. Elias knew this. Every printer in Vellamare knew this; it was why the quarter's buildings stood shoulder to shoulder and why the insurers had long ago given up on the block. He watched the flame take the cooperage and leap, hungry and delighted, to the chandler's eaves, and he ran harder, toward the shop, toward the light in its windows that meant someone was still inside.

Jonah was in the doorway when he arrived, soot-striped, hauling type cases into the street with her mother's efficiency, measuring the apocalypse in portable things. Behind her, the sky over the harbor glowed, and the fire came up the lane like a debt finally called in.

"Elias," she said, not slowing. "You're late. Tell me you got them."

He lifted the satchel, and she saw the weight of it in his arms and understood everything at once, the way printers understand — imposition, pressure, the whole truth borne on a single flat plane.

"Nine plates," he gasped. "The lie, cast in metal. All of it. Saltgate, the confession, your family — they'd already typeset the whole history, Jonah. Ready for printing. They wrote the ending before the battle was done."

For one moment she went very still, the way she went still over a misaligned line. Then she nodded once, sharp as a press stroke.

"Good," she said. "Then we carry it out. Because the fire doesn't care whose side the paper is on."

Behind her, inside the shop, the lamplight still burned over the composing stone where the true ledger lay half-set, and the flames turned the corner of the lane.
