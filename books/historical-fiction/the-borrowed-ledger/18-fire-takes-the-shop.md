## 18. Fire Takes the Shop

The first sign of trouble was not smoke but silence — the particular silence of a city holding its breath before it screams.

Jonah heard it from behind the composing stone, where she was setting the last page of the ledger proofs for drying. The cannonfire to the east had stopped an hour ago, and in her experience, cannonfire stopping was never good news. Cannonfire stopping meant someone had won, or someone had run out of ammunition, or someone had decided that burning buildings accomplished more than cannonballs. All three possibilities ended the same way: with soldiers, and with fire.

"Elias," she said.

He looked up from the Assembly ledgers spread across the proofing table, his spectacles pushed up into his hair where they always migrated when he forgot he was wearing them. "I heard."

"You heard nothing. You've gone deaf with reading. I said *Elias*."

"I said I heard." He took the spectacles down and polished them on his sleeve, which was his way of buying time to think. "The shelling's stopped. That means either the rebels have broken the eastern wall or the garrison is falling back through the print quarter."

"Through the print quarter," Jonah repeated. "Why through?"

"Because it's wide, and because retreating men burn what they can't carry. It's very old doctrine. The Assyrians did it. The Romans did it." He set the spectacles back on his nose. "Your mother taught you to measure everything in this house by what could be carried if soldiers came. I confess I thought it a quaint habit when I first arrived. A family superstition."

"It's not a superstition. It's arithmetic."

"Yes," Elias said quietly. "And tonight we do sums."

* * *

Mira Nakamura was already in the yard when Jonah came out, standing beneath the line where sheets hung to dry like pale flags of a country that existed only between clotheslines. She had changed her apron — put on the old one, the good one, the one with deep pockets — and she had the type cases stacked by the door in the order Jonah had known since childhood: the ones worth carrying first, then second, then not at all.

"You felt it too," Jonah said.

"I heard the cannons stop." Her mother's voice was level, the voice she used for pricing difficult jobs and for delivering worse news. "A stopped cannon is a door closing somewhere. Better to be on the right side of it." She pressed a hand flat against the nearest case, the one holding their father's display sorts, the big carved letters he'd cut himself the year Jonah was born. "We take these. We take the proofs. We take the ledger plates if they're cool enough to touch."

"The press—"

"Stays."

"Mama, the press is the shop."

"The shop is not the press." Mira looked at her daughter with something that was almost gentle and entirely immovable. "Your father could have told you. A press is wood and iron. Any port city has iron; any forest has wood. What cannot be replaced—" she touched the case again, "—is in here. Forty years of cuts. His hands are in these boxes. Hands travel better than machines."

Jonah wanted to argue, which was itself a comfort, because arguing with her mother was the oldest ritual in the house, older than the press, older than Tomas, possibly older than grief. But there was no time for ritual, because at that moment the sky over Harrow Street turned the color of a bruise, and the smell arrived — pitch and tar and burning paper, the smell of a print quarter learning what it was made of.

* * *

They worked. There is no other word for it, and Jonah would remember afterward that there was, strangely, laughter in it — the terrible gallows humor of people doing manual labor while the world ends around them, because the hands need something to do besides shake.

Elias carried cases two at a time and complained about his back with scholarly precision. "I catalogued the Vellamare civic archive for eleven years," he announced, staggering past with the case of small numerals. "Eleven years. Do you know what I lifted in eleven years? Documents. Documents weigh nothing. This is persecution of a secondary kind."

"You're carrying history," Jonah said.

"I'm carrying punctuation. History is in the proofs, which weigh four ounces, and which you have wrapped in oilcloth like a christening gown, which I note is the correct amount of reverence and also the correct amount of paranoia."

"Both are family traditions."

Rell appeared at the yard gate ashen-faced, soot already streaking one cheek, having run from the docks. "It's coming up Candlemaker's Row," he gasped. "The garrison's pulling out east — they're firing the shops as they go. Grebe's press is burning. I saw it." He paused, and something complicated crossed his face. "I saw it, and I didn't feel anything."

"That's shock," Elias said.

"No," Rell said slowly. "I think it's justice, and I didn't know what shape it would be." Then, because he was sixteen and the moment required it: "Can I help carry? My brother says I'm strong for my size, though he says most things through his teeth."

"You can carry the ink stones," Mira said. "And you can stop talking about your brother until we're past the wharf."

* * *

The fires moved like weather. That was the thing no one warned you about a city burning — it had wind patterns, currents, a tide. The flames came up Harrow Street in a slow front, house to house, and each shop went up with its own signature. The chandler's went fast and bright, all tallow. The bookbinder's went sullen and thick, glue and paste smoldering like a grudge. Aldous Grebe's fine new press — the press that had printed the forged confessions, the press that had nearly hanged them all — burned with a crackle Jonah chose to interpret as laughter.

She stood in the doorway of her own shop with a type case under each arm and let herself look at it. Really look. The composing stone where her father had set her name in type when she was four, so she'd understand what letters were before she understood what words were. The window seat where Tomas hid during storms and claimed he hadn't been afraid. The burn scar on the doorframe from the year the lamp fell, which her father had refused to plane away because, he said, a shop should remember its accidents the way a face should.

"You're doing the thing," Mira said beside her. "The measuring thing."

"I'm measuring."

"And?"

Jonah took inventory, the way she'd done every day of her life without knowing she was rehearsing for this. The press: too heavy. It stayed. The paper stock: burning nicely already, nothing to do about it. The finished wedding announcements still stacked in the corner — three months' work for a merchant who might or might not still be alive to marry anyone — those went up with everything else, and she found she didn't mind. Her father had loved that work, the ordinary work, the announcements of joy. Let them burn as incense.

What remained, what she held: forty years of type. Four ounces of truth in oilcloth. The ledger plates, warm against her hip through the sacking. An archivist who weighed more than he admitted. An apprentice who had chosen them over his own blood. A mother whose pockets had never once been empty.

"And?" Mira prompted again.

"And it fits," Jonah said, and was surprised to find her voice steady. "It all fits in four arms and a handcart."

Her mother nodded once, satisfied, the nod she gave when a job came out true on the first pull. "Now you understand."

"I understood before."

"No." Mira watched the fire take the window seat. "You obeyed before. Understanding is different. Understanding is when you stop grieving what can't be carried, because you finally see that everything that matters already has arms and legs." She adjusted the oilcloth bundle in Jonah's grip with two small tugs, the way she'd straightened Jonah's collar before every first day of school. "Your grandmother fled with a type case. I fled with a type case. You flee with a type case. Someday some fool historian will call us unlucky women. We were the luckiest printers alive. We learned early what the fire teaches late."

* * *

They got the handcart to the end of Harrow Street before the heat drove them into the alley behind the cooperage, and there Jonah allowed herself one look back.

The Nakamura shop went up the way her father had built it — honestly, solidly, taking its time. The roof went first in a bloom of orange, then the windows blew out in sequence like notes played down a scale, and then the whole front opened and she could see the press silhouetted inside, black and stubborn in the middle of all that light, holding its shape long after everything around it had surrendered.

"There it goes," Elias said softly, beside her. He had removed his hat, which for Elias was roughly equivalent to weeping.

"She stood forty years," Mira said. "She doesn't owe us anything."

Rell was crying and pretending the smoke was doing it, and everyone agreed loudly that the smoke was very thick tonight.

And Jonah — Jonah found that she was not crying at all, and examined this fact with a printer's curiosity, turning it over to see how it was made. She had expected grief to feel like loss. Instead it felt like clarity, sharp and terrible and clean as a freshly-cut sort: the understanding that a life could be reduced to what fit in a handcart and not be reduced at all. That the shop had only ever been the shell. That the press had only ever been the tool. That the thing her father had actually built, the thing that was walking away up the alley on six legs and two bad wheels, was a household that knew exactly what it believed and had already begun setting it in type.

"They'll say it was an accident," Elias was saying, practical again, wiping ash from the ledger proofs with his sleeve. "Retreating armies always file it as an accident. And tomorrow — tomorrow the council will want accounts. Witnesses. They'll want the Saltgate orders read publicly before the historians get their version stitched together."

"Tomorrow," Mira said, "we find a press that isn't ours and borrow it."

Jonah laughed — actually laughed, there in the alley, with her childhood burning fifty feet behind her — and her mother smiled the thin, fierce smile that meant the joke had landed exactly where it was aimed. Borrowed. Of course. The whole ledger had been borrowed from the Assembly's own archives; the press would be borrowed from whoever survived the night; even the names of the dead were borrowed, briefly, from oblivion, and it was a printer's entire sacred trade to return them legible.

They came out onto Saltgate Road as dawn considered arriving, the harbor below them gray-gold and crowded with boats, and the whole print quarter behind them glowing like a forge. On the quay ahead, figures were gathering — dockworkers, refugees, a knot of rebel soldiers around a banner Jonah couldn't yet read. Around one figure in particular, tall, bandaged, leaning on a makeshift crutch, waving with his good arm.

"Tomas," Jonah breathed.

"He's alive," Rell said. "He's alive and he's *waving*, why is he waving, does he know—"

"He knows," Mira said. "Look at his face. He knows something, and he's about to make it our problem." She picked up her end of the handcart. "Come, then. Let's go hear what your brother carried home."

Jonah shifted the oilcloth bundle high against her chest — four ounces of paper, forty years of type, one whole inheritance — and walked down toward the wharf, toward whatever her brother had seen at Saltgate, toward the reading that would decide whether the dead got their names back or lost them twice.

Behind them, the shop burned. Ahead of them, the crowd parted.

And in her arms, everything that mattered kept perfect weight.
