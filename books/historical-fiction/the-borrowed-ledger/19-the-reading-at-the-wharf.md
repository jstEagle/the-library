## 19. The Reading at the Wharf

**Jonah**

The wharf smelled of salt and char and, faintly, of tar where the fire had not finished its work. Jonah stood behind the rebel council's rough plank table — a door laid across barrels, which struck her as fitting; doors made fine tables when a city had burned its furniture — and tried to remember that her hands knew how to be steady. They had set ten thousand lines of type without trembling. Surely they could hold one sheet of paper.

The crowd frightened her more than soldiers had. Soldiers came in ranks you could count. This was Vellamare itself: dockworkers with bandaged hands, fishwives still in their aprons, clerks who had slipped out of the Assembly's offices with their collars askew, children riding their fathers' shoulders because from up there everything looked like a festival. Perhaps it was a festival. Festivals and funerals used the same flowers.

Elias stood at her elbow, gray and rumpled, clutching his satchel of stolen printing plates like a man holding a cat he wasn't sure wanted to be held.

"You'll do fine," he murmured.

"I'm about to read names to an armed crowd."

"Then don't read them *to* anyone. Read them *for* someone. Pick one face." He nodded toward a woman near the front, small and dry as driftwood, wearing a widow's black gone green with salt air. "Her. Read for her."

Jonah found her voice somewhere below her ribs, where it had been hiding since Saltgate.

"Citizens of Vellamare," she began, and the crowd's noise folded down into something attentive. "Three nights ago, at this wharf, two hundred and eleven people died. The Assembly's historians will tell you rebels did it. I have here the orders that say otherwise. I have the names."

She lifted the first sheet — the true ledger's opening page, ink still smelling of their shop, of her father's lamp, of every late night Mira had stood watch at the door.

"The first name is Ansel Corvane. Oyster dredger. Forty-one years old."

A sound went through the crowd like a tide changing direction.

* * *

**Tomas**

Tomas had thought he was done being afraid on rooftops. He had been wrong once already this week, so he supposed he could survive being wrong again.

He stood on the customs house roof — the same vantage from which he'd watched officers in rebel uniforms direct the massacre, the same vantage from which he'd carried away a dead man's dispatch book and most of his own blood — and looked down at his sister's small figure at the plank table. She looked, from up here, exactly as she had at nine years old, reading aloud to the household while their father fed paper through the press. Same tilt of the chin. Same habit of pausing before a name she wanted the room to hold properly.

Beside him, leaning against the parapet with his arm bound in a sling he clearly resented, Rell squinted against the wind. "She's doing it," Rell said. "She's actually doing it."

"She does everything actually," Tomas said. "It's her worst quality."

Below, Jonah read the fourth name, then the tenth, then the twentieth. She did not rush them. Each name got its full weight, the way type gets its impression — pressed deep enough to last.

Tomas's wound ached in time with the crowd's murmuring. He kept scanning faces instead of listening, an old smuggler's habit, looking for trouble in the crowd the way you read water for sandbars. He found some. Near the council table stood a knot of men in the new republic's colors, and among them, unmistakably, Aldous Grebe — broad, well-fed, wearing the expression of a man watching a rival's shop burn while assuring everyone how tragic it was.

"He's going to try something," Tomas said.

"Obviously," said Rell.

"You're very calm for an apprentice."

"I'm very calm because my brother is standing thirty feet behind Grebe," Rell said, "and my brother has been carrying something under his coat for two days like it was a hot coal. Whatever happens next, he's part of it."

Tomas looked. And there, indeed, pushing through the councilors' cluster with the stiff walk of a soldier off duty, came Denner Rell — no, Denner was the family name; the brother was called Marcus, Marcus with the Assembly insignia torn off his sleeve but the posture still bolted on — Marcus, walking toward the table with his jaw set like a man approaching his own trial.

"Ah," said Tomas. "That coal's about to land somewhere."

* * *

**Mira**

Mira Nakamura had attended public readings on three continents, and she could judge a crowd the way her husband used to judge ink — by touch, by smell, by what happened when you put pressure on it.

This crowd was nearly ready. Not for violence — violence had spent itself at Saltgate and in the burning quarter, and grief had come in behind it like a slow tide filling a emptied harbor. The crowd was ready for *truth*, which is a hungrier thing than violence and much harder to feed.

Her daughter's voice carried beautifully over the water. Mira had known it would. Jonah had inherited her father's instrument voice, the one that made even a wedding announcement sound like scripture. But it was the names that were doing the work. Names always did. You could argue with an argument, but nobody could argue with *Ansel Corvane, oyster dredger, forty-one years old*. Somewhere in that crowd was a wife, a mother, a daughter, hearing a name spoken aloud in daylight for the first time since the night it was taken.

Mira stood near the front — she had refused all offers of a seat; seats were for people whose legs hadn't crossed borders — and she held in her pocket the oldest letter she owned, the one from another country, another purge, another time when powerful men wrote lies in official typefaces. She had carried it across the sea inside a type case, wedged between the M's and the N's, and she had promised herself then that if she ever got the chance, she would stand in a free square and hear the truth read out loud.

Well. Here was the square. It smelled of smoke, and freedom had arrived looking disheveled and slightly criminal, but it had arrived.

Jonah reached a name and stopped — just a half-second hitch, the kind only family would notice. Mira followed her daughter's eyes and saw why: a girl of perhaps twelve at the crowd's edge, holding a candle stub in daylight, lips moving along as though she were checking the spelling.

*Even the victims' names must be spelled correctly,* Mira had insisted, three sleepless nights ago, ink to her elbows. *Especially then.*

Her daughter had spelled them correctly. All two hundred and eleven.

Mira found she was crying, quietly, without any intention of stopping or making a scene about it. There was a difference, she had learned long ago, between weeping and breaking. One you could do standing up.

* * *

**Rell**

Rell had never seen his brother cry, and now he was seeing it, and it was worse than anything at Saltgate.

Marcus had pushed through the councilors — past Grebe, whose hand shot out to grip his arm and was shaken off like a fly — and stood before the plank table, before the council, before the whole listening city, and held out the dispatch book wrapped in oilcloth.

"This is from the night of the massacre," Marcus said, and his voice cracked in the middle like a boy's, and Rell felt his own chest crack in sympathy. "I took it off a dead officer. My officer. Our side killed those people and dressed it in your uniforms, and this book proves it, and Aldous Grebe paid me to help forge documents saying otherwise, and I am done."

The silence afterward lasted four heartbeats. Rell counted them. He counted everything these days; it kept his hands from shaking.

Grebe recovered fastest, which told everyone everything. "This man is a confessed forger," he announced, spreading his arms, magnanimous as a festival banner. "His word is worth nothing—"

"His word," said Elias Vane, stepping forward with the unhurried menace of a lifelong bureaucrat, "is corroborated by the dispatch book, by the arrest list he copied and delivered under our door — yes, Grebe, we know — and by the watermark analysis I performed on your forged confession, which matches your press's stock to within three sheets. I was the government's archivist. I know what paper remembers." He smiled, thin as a knife's edge. "Paper remembers *everything*. That's rather the whole point of me."

The crowd made a noise then that Rell had only ever heard at executions and weddings — the same appetite, aimed differently.

Grebe looked around for support and found the council studying him with the flat expressions of men recalculating. One of them — the tall woman with the scarred cheek whom everyone called the Quartermaster — turned to Jonah Nakamura and said, loudly enough for the quay:

"Printer. Your sheets. Hang them where the people can read."

* * *

**Jonah**

There is a particular joy, Jonah thought later, in hanging laundry. Anyone who has done it knows: the snap of cloth, the sun through wet fabric, the small domestic satisfaction of order imposed on chaos. Hanging the ledger pages along the burned quay was laundry. It was also the most important typesetting verification in the history of Vellamare, because a crowd was proofreading.

She and Tomas and Rell worked down the line of iron rings left from the old cargo nets, clipping sheets with whatever they had — clothespins, fish hooks, bent nails. Each sheet held twenty names. Eleven sheets. Two hundred and eleven names, each with age and trade, each spelled correctly, because her mother would have risen from her grave — or more alarmingly, from her garden — otherwise.

The crowd moved along the line slowly, the way people move past graves. Reading. Some touched the paper. A fishwife — the driftwood-dry widow Elias had picked out — stood before the sheet bearing *Ansel Corvane* and read it aloud to no one, to everyone, her finger under each word like a schoolmistress, and other women gathered and read after her, passing the names mouth to mouth down the quay until the whole wharf was quietly murmuring two hundred and eleven lives back into existence.

Jonah stepped back beside Elias and watched.

"That," Elias said softly, "is a record no one can carry away."

"They could tear them down."

"They could. And tomorrow there'd be two hundred and eleven people who memorized their sheet." He glanced sideways at her. "You understand that's the trick? Ink is just the delivery method. What you printed today is in *them* now."

Jonah looked out at the crowd, at her mother standing straight as a mast, at Rell wiping his eyes and pretending it was sea spray, at her brother grinning despite his stitches.

Somewhere behind them, the council was arguing about Grebe — she caught fragments: *trial*, *the contracts*, *who approved the forgeries* — and she understood, with a printer's instinct for what comes next in the sentence, that this story was not over. Revolutions did not end at wharves with readings. They ended, if they ended at all, in archives, in committee rooms, in the quiet places where the next draft of history was being set in type.

The Quartermaster approached their little group, flanked by two guards.

"Nakamura. Vane. The council wishes to discuss the disposition of these materials. Tomorrow, the archive building, noon." Her gaze flicked to the hanging sheets, and something complicated passed over her face — gratitude wearing suspicion's coat. "You should know some of my colleagues consider broadsheets posted in public to be... destabilizing."

"My mother," Jonah said, "considers forgetting to be destabilizing."

The Quartermaster almost smiled. Almost. Then she was gone into the crowd, and Elias exhaled.

"Tomorrow," he said. "Noon. The archive." He patted his satchel of stolen plates. "I have a feeling they're going to want to talk about which pages stay."

Jonah looked at the eleven sheets snapping gently in the sea wind, at the names catching the light, and felt the old familiar weight settle onto her shoulders — not fear, exactly. Responsibility. The weight of sorts.

"Then we'd best decide tonight," she said, "which pages *we're* willing to lose."

Behind them, the widow in green was reading Ansel Corvane's name aloud again. She did not intend to stop.
