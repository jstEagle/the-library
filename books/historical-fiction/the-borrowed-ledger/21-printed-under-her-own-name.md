## 21. Printed Under Her Own Name

The amnesty came on a Tuesday, delivered by a young clerk who kept his hat in his hands the entire time, as though he expected someone to throw something at him.

Jonah read it twice at the counter of the rebuilt shop — not rebuilt yet, actually; the shop was still a scorched shell with a new roof and two salvaged windows, but she had already begun thinking of it as *rebuilt*, the way you think of a wound as healed long before the scar stops pulling. The document was typeset beautifully. She noticed that first, the way she noticed everything now: the even leading, the confident kerning, the crisp impression of a press that had never once been asked to print a lie.

*Be it known*, it began, *that Jonah Nakamura, having suffered grievously at the hands of the former regime, is hereby granted full pardon for any acts committed during the late disturbances, upon condition of her silence regarding matters of state.*

"Any acts," Jonah said aloud. "How generous of them."

The clerk shifted. "It's a good offer, miss. There's folk who'd give a year's wages—"

"I'm sure there are." She set the page down and smoothed it flat with both palms, the way her father used to smooth a sheet before pulling a proof. "Do you know what this is?"

"An amnesty?"

"It's a blank forme." She tapped the paper. "They've locked up all my words except the ones they approve, and then they've handed me the key and called it mercy. Whoever composed this understood type very well." She looked up. "Did you set it yourself?"

The clerk blinked. "I — yes, miss. Apprentice work."

"It's clean work. You should be proud of it." She watched him decide whether to be flattered or alarmed, and settle, visibly, on both. "Tell your masters I'll have an answer by week's end."

He left at nearly a run.

Mira emerged from the back room, where she had been sorting salvageable sorts from the melted ones — a grim arithmetic of lead, counting what the fire had taken letter by letter. "Well?" she said.

"They want my silence."

"And what did you tell them?"

"That I'd answer by week's end."

Her mother smiled the small, dry smile that Jonah had spent twenty-nine years learning to fear. "You already know your answer."

"I already know my answer."

"Then why make the poor boy wait?"

"Because," Jonah said, "I want them to spend six days believing they've won. And because I needed to know if the shop was watched." She nodded toward the window, where a man had been leaning against the lamppost across the street since morning, reading the same page of a pamphlet with the concentration of a scholar deciphering Linear B. "He's been there three hours. He turned the page twice."

Mira glanced out, unimpressed. "At least this city's spies dress better than the last lot."

* * *

Elias arrived at dusk, carrying his satchel the way he always did, close against his ribs like a child he wasn't sure he was allowed to keep. He had grown thinner since the wharf reading — the new government consumed its archivists the way the old one had, just with better manners — and there was a new gray in his beard that Jonah suspected had arrived all at once, overnight, sometime between the council's third "review" of the Nakamura broadsheets and its fourth.

"You got one too," he said, dropping onto the stool by the press. It wasn't a question. Everyone had gotten one. Half the witnesses of Saltgate had woken to find themselves pardoned for crimes they'd never committed, which was, Elias observed, a novel form of erasure: forgive a man into nothing.

"I got one." Jonah slid it across the counter.

He read it with the particular stillness he brought to documents, lips moving slightly, one finger tracing the margin. Then he laughed — a short, bitter bark that surprised them both.

"'Upon condition of silence,'" he quoted. "Do you know what they offered me? Not silence. A *post*. Keeper of the New Archive. My own office, my own staff, a salary that would make Grebe weep." He set the amnesty down. "All I had to do was leave out the pages where the Assembly's treasurer paid the Saltgate officers directly. And the page about the grain contracts. And, oh — the small matter of who ordered the massacre. Just those. A mere forty sheets out of four hundred."

"What did you say?"

"I said I would consider it." He rubbed his eyes. "Which is archivist for *no*, but buys me time to copy everything before they decide I'm insufficiently grateful."

"Elias." Jonah pulled the stool beside hers around so they sat knee to knee, the way compositors did over a difficult line. "They're going to bury it anyway. You know that. Every day the official history sets another line in stone. By winter, Saltgate will be a rebel atrocity in every schoolbook in Vellamare, and the only people who'll remember otherwise will be us, and we'll be old, and then we'll be dead."

"Yes," Elias said quietly. "I've done the arithmetic."

"Then we don't wait for permission. We reprint. All of it. The full ledger, the dispatch book pages Rell's brother finally surrendered, the names — every name, spelled right, the way Mama insisted. And at the bottom—" Jonah heard her own voice steady itself, the way it did when she locked a chase, when the forme was tight and true and nothing could shift it now. "At the bottom, under the title, we set one line. *Set by Jonah Nakamura.*"

Elias stared at her. "They'll take the shop."

"The shop is four walls and a press. They already burned it once; it didn't take."

"They could take more than the shop."

"They could," Jonah agreed. "But here's what I keep coming back to, sitting up at night doing sums like Mama taught me." She held up the amnesty. "This says they're afraid. Councils don't buy silence they don't need. And what are they afraid of? Not me. Not you. They're afraid of the crowd at the wharf — the ones who stood in the rain and read their own sons' names off our sheets and wouldn't leave until every name was down. You can arrest a printer. You can't arrest everyone who can read."

Elias was quiet a long moment. Outside, the spy turned his pamphlet page for the fourth time, an act of such flagrant idleness that Mira, passing, remarked that the man deserved a raise purely for persistence.

"You know," Elias said at last, "in thirty years of service I never once put my name on anything. Archivists don't sign. We're supposed to be invisible — the record matters, not the keeper." His hand drifted to the satchel. "When they dismissed me, when they told me to burn the ledgers, do you know what I felt? Not righteous. *Relieved.* Because burning them meant I'd never have to stand behind them. Never have to be the man who said *this is true, and I am the one saying it.*"

"And now?"

"Now I'm going to help you set your name under a document that will get us both hanged if the wind shifts." He smiled, and it transformed his whole tired face. "God, it feels *good*."

* * *

They worked nine nights.

The press was the old one, dragged half-melted from the ruins and coaxed back to life by Tomas, who had returned from the docks with a limp, a dispatch book's worth of nightmares, and an uncanny talent for machinery that had no business working. The type was a mongrel army — survivors of the fire, purchases from three shuttered shops, a case traded from a widow in exchange for printing her husband's memorial card free. Some letters were older than the revolution. One *Q* had, according to Mira, fled two countries.

On the fifth night, Rell came through the back door with flour on his sleeves and his daughter asleep against his shoulder.

"She wouldn't settle," he whispered. "Figured she might as well not-settle somewhere useful."

He was twenty-three now and ran his late brother's bakery, and he still moved through the world like a man perpetually listening for footsteps — but the footsteps were quieter these days, and some nights, Jonah thought, he almost believed they were gone. He settled the girl on a pallet of empty paper stock, tucked a compositor's apron over her like a blanket, and picked up a composing stick without being asked. His hands remembered. They always had.

"Page twelve," Jonah told him. "Start at 'Received of the Assembly treasury.'"

Rell set type the way other men prayed. Somewhere past midnight his daughter woke, watched them all working by lamplight with enormous serious eyes, and announced, "I'm going to learn letters."

"You will," Jonah said. "First lesson: never trust a *Q*."

* * *

The distribution went through the tunnels — of course it did. Some things you did the honest way, and some things you did the way that worked.

Rell knew the network better than anyone alive now, having spent his apprenticeship running letters to a brother on the wrong side of history, and he walked Jonah through it with the pride of a man showing off his childhood home: the cellar door behind the chandlery, the drain that wasn't a drain, the stretch beneath the Customs House where you had to walk bent double and think respectful thoughts about rats. At each checkpoint above ground, the broadsheets traveled disguised — wrapped in wedding announcements, tucked inside funeral notices, folded into the pages of almanacs. Her father's trade, keeping her father's truth alive. Jonah found herself talking to him in her head as she worked, the way she always did at the press. *Look, Papa. Look what the wedding announcements are carrying now.*

By the ninth morning, copies of the ledger hung along the burned quay at Saltgate, weighted with stones, fluttering in the harbor wind. By noon, people stood reading them in silence, and then not in silence. By evening, the council had convened an emergency session, and the lamppost spy outside the shop had been replaced by two considerably worse spies who kept checking a pocket watch that Jonah could see, from the window, was not running.

* * *

The council sent three men on the eleventh day. Jonah received them in the shop, among the type cases, and offered them tea, which none of them accepted, which she considered a tactical error on their part because her mother's tea was genuinely excellent and might have softened them up.

The senior of the three was a smooth, gray man named Corvane whom Elias had warned her about. "Madam Nakamura," he said, settling into the chair as though he owned it and merely tolerated her tenancy. "The council is prepared to be generous. The amnesty stands — expanded, in fact. A stipend. Restoration of your father's Assembly printing contract, posthumously honored. In exchange, the unauthorized broadsheets are withdrawn, the plates surrendered, and the matter of authorship quietly resolved."

"Quietly resolved," Jonah repeated.

"The document in question contains material prejudicial to civic order. Names of officials now serving in good faith. Allegations that cannot be substantiated to the council's standard."

"Substantiated," Jonah said, "by whom? The historians who already typeset the lie? I've seen the plates Elias pulled from your archive. Saltgate blamed on the rebels. My family listed as martyred traitors — martyrs, mind you, of a plot we supposedly joined. Someone set that type, Corvane. Someone chose each letter. And now you'd like to choose mine."

Corvane's smile thinned by a degree. "You are a printer, madam. You of all people understand that ink is cheap. That words can be reprinted, revised, superseded. Why destroy yourself over a document the city will forget by spring?"

"Because I measured it," Jonah said.

He waited. She let him.

"My mother taught me to measure everything by what could be carried if soldiers came. Type cases. Documents. Names. For thirty years this family carried the truth of other people's crimes in boxes small enough to lift. And I have learned — at considerable expense, believe me — that the things worth carrying are exactly the things you cannot put down." She rose. "Here is my answer to your generous council. The ledger stays. The name stays. *Set by Jonah Nakamura* — let every apprentice in Vellamare learn to set those four words, and let them ask, someday, what it cost."

Corvane studied her for a long moment, and Jonah saw the exact instant the calculation changed behind his eyes — saw him look past her shoulder, out the window, to the street beyond, where a crowd had begun to gather. Not an angry crowd. A reading one. Dockworkers and fishwives and two of the council's own clerks, standing in the lane, holding the broadsheets, simply *looking* at the shop that had printed them, the way pilgrims look at a shrine they're not entirely comfortable praying at.

Corvane stood. "The council," he said, with the weariness of a man recanting in advance, "will review the matter further."

"Do," Jonah said pleasantly. "Review takes time. Meanwhile, we're printing a second edition. Larger type. The elders complained."

* * *

They let it stand.

That was the phrase Elias used, later, shaking his head over his files: *they let it stand* — as though the truth were a building the council had declined, at last, to condemn. Not approval. Not courage. Arithmetic. Three thousand copies in the streets, a crowd that read instead of rioted, and a simple fact the new government understood in its bones: you could fight a printer, but you could not fight everyone who had ever traced a finger along her lines and found their own grief spelled correctly there.

On the night the second edition came off the press, Jonah pulled the last sheet herself, held it to the lamp, and read the line at the bottom one more time.

*Set by Jonah Nakamura.*

Not borrowed. Not anonymous. Not her father's ligatures or her mother's caution or some historian's convenient fiction. Hers.

Mira touched the wet ink, then her daughter's cheek, and said only, "Your father would have charged them double for the rush work."

Tomas laughed until he had to sit down. Rell's daughter, who had learned her letters after all, sounded out the title slowly, triumphantly, getting every word but stumbling gloriously on "Nakamura," and Jonah corrected her gently, and thought: *that's how it survives. Not in archives. In mouths.*

Outside, dawn was coming up gray over the harbor, and the smoke-stained frame beside the door — the original ledger sheet, the first one, hung there where the whole street could see it — stirred in the sea wind like a thing deciding to stay.

Jonah locked the forme, banked the lamp, and reached for her apron. There was, after all, always more to set.
