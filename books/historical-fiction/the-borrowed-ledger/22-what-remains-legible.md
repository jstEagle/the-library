## 22. What Remains Legible

The bell over the door of the Nakamura & Daughters Press had been cast from the melted hinges of the old shop door, and it rang with a slight flaw in its voice — a small flat note at the end, like a word someone couldn't quite finish saying. Jonah had refused every offer to recast it.

"It sounds broken," said the bell-founder's apprentice, years ago, apologetic.

"It sounds *true*," Jonah had told him. "Everything worth keeping has a crack in it somewhere. It's how you know it survived."

Now, on a morning in late spring — the harbor glittering beyond the window, gulls arguing over the fish market as though they had a grievance older than the revolution — that flawed bell rang twice, and two apprentices came in out of the light: Sefa, fourteen, all elbows and impatience, and Rell's daughter, Wren, twelve, who walked into the shop the way her father used to walk through checkpoints, quietly, watchfully, as if measuring which exits she might someday need to know.

Jonah was sixty-one now, or thereabouts. Her mother had always said that a woman who fled one country and buried one husband and outlasted one revolution had earned the right to be vague about her age, since the paperwork kept burning anyway. Her hands were the truest record of the years: ink in the creases that no pumice would lift, a scar across the left thumb from the night the soldiers came, knuckles gone broad and sure as quoins.

"Compositor's bench," she said, by way of good morning. "Both of you. Show me what you set last night."

Sefa slapped his galley down with the confidence of a boy who had not yet learned that confidence is the first thing type punishes. Wren laid hers down more carefully, squaring it to the edge of the stone, and Jonah felt something in her chest do the thing it always did when she watched a young person handle type with respect — the thing her father must have felt, standing behind her at this same height, on this same street, before any of it happened.

She read Sefa's work first. A handbill for the fishmongers' guild, requesting attendance at their autumn feast.

"Sefa."

"Yes, master?"

"What is this word?"

He squinted. "*Feast.*"

"Look again. Look at your third letter."

He looked. His face went through the stages of grief in the space of four seconds. "I put an *a* where the *e* goes."

"You did. And so instead of inviting the guild to a feast, you have invited them to a *faast*, which I believe is what happens when the Aldermoor family eats quickly." She let him suffer exactly long enough. "In my father's shop, a man once printed *publik notices* for eleven years without ever once spelling *public* correctly. The city thought it was charming. The city was wrong. Fix it."

"Yes, master." He was already turning back to the case, ears pink, and Jonah caught Wren's mouth twitching and gave her a look that said *you will be humble too, eventually, when I find your error,* because mercy distributed evenly among apprentices is the whole art of teaching.

Then Wren's galley. A funeral notice — real work, real grief; old Master Corvane of the chandlery had died in his sleep on Tuesday, full of years and beeswax, and his daughter wanted something dignified. Wren had set it clean. Spacing even, leads true, the widow's name spelled correctly — *Corvane* with the *e* that half the city dropped, because the family had come from the same coast Mira had, generations back.

"Well?" Wren said. She was trying not to hope, and failing beautifully.

Jonah held the galley up to the window light, tilting it so the shadows fell into every counter of every letter. This was how you found the flaws — not by looking at the type but by looking through it, letting the truth show itself in silhouette.

"Your justification is sound," she said. "Your spacing is generous without being wasteful. You remembered the accent on *Corvane*, which his own daughter forgets." She set the galley down. "It's good work, Wren."

Wren's shoulders came down half an inch. Twelve years old, and already she took praise the way her father had taken it — as evidence to be verified rather than comfort to be enjoyed. Rell would have been proud. Rell *was* proud, when he came by on Thursdays to fix the press and complain about the price of leather and drink one cup of tea that took him an hour to finish because he kept forgetting about it in favor of talking. He was a grown man now with gray coming in at his temples, and he still flinched, very slightly, whenever he heard boots on the stair outside. Some things a body keeps. But he laughed easily these days, more easily than Jonah did, and his daughter knew nothing of tunnels except as a story with a good ending.

That was the arrangement. That was the bargain they had all made, without ever speaking it aloud: the children would get the stories with the endings attached, and the adults would keep the middle parts, where the fear lived, the way a compositor keeps the sorts he uses rarely — up high, out of reach, dusted but never discarded.

"Now," Jonah said, clapping her hands, "the lesson."

She led them to the far wall, where the frame hung beside the door, as it had hung beside the door of the old shop, as it would hang beside whatever door came after this one, God and the harbor willing.

The original ledger sheet. The first proof pulled off the old press on the worst night of the war, when cannon fire shook the glass and Mira sat by the lamp insisting that every name be spelled correctly while the world outside tried very hard to make the names not matter. Its edges were smoke-stained still — brown fading to black, black fading to nothing, the way memory does. The ink had gone soft with age, sepia settling into the paper fibers like tea into a cup left too long. Behind the glass, forty-two names stood in columns. The victims of Saltgate Wharf. Set by hand. Set by a frightened girl who became, without ever quite deciding to, a keeper of accounts.

"Wren," Jonah said. "Read me the first name."

Wren didn't need to step close. Jonah knew — had known for three years now, since the day the girl had sounded out the title and stumbled gloriously on *Nakamura* — that Wren had memorized the sheet the way other children memorized songs.

"Aldous Vey," Wren said. "Dockworker. Age thirty-one."

"And why does it hang by the door?"

Wren considered. She was a girl who considered, which in Jonah's professional opinion was worth more than a girl who answered quickly. "Because everyone who comes in has to walk past it," she said finally. "And because—" She stopped, frowned at her own thought, then committed to it the way you commit a line of type: fully, no taking it back. "Because paper burns. So you don't keep it somewhere safe. You keep it somewhere *walked past*. Somewhere it gets looked at. Things that get looked at get remembered."

Jonah let the silence sit for a moment, the way you let ink sit on the plate.

"Elias couldn't have said it better," she said. "Which will annoy him enormously when I tell him."

*

Elias Vane came at noon, as he came every day, carrying his lunch wrapped in cloth and his grievances carried openly, having long ago decided that a man who had stolen printing plates from a collapsing regime had earned the right to complain about anything he pleased.

"The council meeting went badly," he announced, setting himself at the kitchen table in the back with the familiarity of twenty years' occupation. "Or well. Depending on whether you're a person who thinks history should be accurate."

"Those are usually the same meetings," Jonah said, pouring his tea.

"Indeed. They're debating the new monument for Saltgate. There's a design. It's very handsome. Granite, waves, a figure holding a lantern." He unwrapped his lunch with the air of a man unveiling evidence. "There are no names on it."

"No names."

"There's an *inscription*. Something about sacrifice and the tides of history. Poet Laureate wrote it. Very fine. Rhymes, even." He bit into his bread with the violence of a man biting a committee. "Forty-two people died, Jonah, and the granite remembers them as a *tide*."

"Mm." Jonah sat across from him. Outside the kitchen window, visible between the bean rows, her mother was on her knees in the garden with a trowel, arguing gently with a weed. "And what did you tell them?"

"I told them the truth, which is my character flaw." Elias wiped crumbs from his beard. "I told them I had personally seen the orders for Saltgate. That I had set some of them in type myself, in a manner of speaking, when we printed the ledger. That names are not decoration, they are the entire point. And Councilor Perrin looked at me over her spectacles and said, *Archivist Vane, monuments are for unity, not for accounting.*"

"What did Mother say when you told her that?"

"I haven't told her yet. I'm gathering courage."

"She's eighty-three, Elias. She'll eat you alive and then plant beans over the remains."

"I know," Elias said mournfully. "It's why I married her."

The joke was twenty years old and still landed, because it was barely a joke. Mira had buried Jonah's father and then, after the fire, after the loss, after the years of rebuilding, had somehow found room in her life for a skinny, guilt-ridden archivist who brought her documents the way other suitors brought flowers. He'd courted her with a first-edition broadside. She'd married him for his marginalia. Tomas had cried at the wedding, which he claimed was the onions.

Jonah looked out at her mother again. The garden lay where the forge had stood — the forge of the old shop, the iron heart of the place that burned. When they rebuilt, Mira had declared she wanted no forge, no iron, nothing that remembered fire. She'd wanted soil instead. Beans, tomatoes, marigolds, a fig tree that struggled heroically against the sea wind and produced, most years, four figs, which the family divided with ceremonial gravity. Where the fire had been, things grew. Mira tended them every morning, kneeling in the dirt, humming songs from a country whose name had changed twice since she'd left it.

Once, years ago, Wren had asked her why she gardened there specifically, of all places.

Mira had said: *"Because this is where the burning finished. Everything after this is growing. You garden where the burning finished, little bird, so the ground knows the story too."*

Wren had repeated this to Jonah later, solemnly, as a great secret, and Jonah had thanked her gravely and gone into the back room and sat down for a while.

*

The afternoon lesson was distribution — putting the type away, which apprentices always hated and which Jonah insisted was the soul of the trade.

"Anyone can set type," she told them, walking the length of the case rack while they groaned and picked up their emptied galleys. "Setting is excitement. Setting is making something new. Distribution is character. You learn who you are when you're putting the letters back where they belong."

"Master," Sefa said, sliding a *k* into the wrong box with tremendous stealth, "what if you put a letter in the wrong place?"

"Then the next person who reaches for it finds chaos," Jonah said, without turning around. "A missing letter here, a stranger there. A wedding announcement becomes a funeral notice. A name becomes another name. Whole sentences go wrong and no one knows why, and the printer tears his hair out blaming the typecase gods." She turned. "Typecase gods punish disorder, Sefa. Eternally. Ask your grandmother."

"My grandmother says the typecase gods owe *her* money."

"Your grandmother is correct. Put the *k* back."

He froze. Wren snorted. The bell above the door rang its cracked note, and Jonah turned expecting a customer and found instead Councilor Perrin herself, small, silver-haired, sharp-eyed, standing in the shop with her hands folded and her face arranged into the expression politicians wear when they've come to deliver news they've decided is good.

"Printer Nakamura."

"Councilor." Jonah wiped her hands on her apron out of habit. Apprentices scattered to useful-looking tasks with the speed of long practice.

Perrin's eyes went, inevitably, to the framed ledger by the door. They always did. Everyone's did. It was the reason it hung there. She regarded it for a long moment, the smoke-stained edges, the forty-two names, the small line beneath the title that read *Set by Jonah Nakamura.*

"You know," Perrin said at last, "when we were fighting, I thought that sheet was the bravest thing in Vellamare. I marched past it during the anniversary. I saluted it, once, when I thought no one was looking."

"And now?"

"And now it makes my colleagues nervous." Perrin turned back to her. "They say it's destabilizing. Twenty years, Nakamura, and they *still* say it. A piece of paper. Hanging by a door." She smiled thinly. "I told them: yes. Exactly. That's rather the point of it."

Jonah waited. Politicians who came bearing compliments always had the invoice tucked somewhere.

"The monument," Perrin said. "The one without names. The design commission wants to move forward next month. Vane has made himself unbearable, bless him. And there's a faction — I won't name them, they'd enjoy it — who want the inscription to describe the Saltgate dead as casualties of *rebel provocation*. The old lie. Grebe's lie, dressed in granite."

The name still worked on Jonah like a hand around her throat, even now, even with Grebe twenty years dead and unmourned by anyone including his own printers. She breathed through it the way her mother had taught her: in through the nose, slow, like pulling a press.

"They can't," she said. "The ledger exists. Thousands of copies. It's in archives in four countries."

"The ledger exists," Perrin agreed. "But granite is louder than paper. Granite doesn't need to be *read*, Nakamura. It just needs to be *looked at*. People will stand in front of it and absorb whatever it says, and their grandchildren will absorb it, and in a hundred years the ledger will be a curiosity scholars argue about while every child in Vellamare learns the wrong story from a beautiful rock." She paused. "Unless the rock tells the truth."

"And you've come to my print shop," Jonah said slowly, "because—"

"Because the commission is deadlocked, and deadlock favors liars. Liars only need the vote to fail. Truth needs to *win*." Perrin reached into her coat and produced a folded paper. "There will be a public hearing. At the wharf. Where else? Three weeks from today. Citizens may address the council on the inscription. Anyone may speak." Her eyes flicked to the ledger again, then back. "Anyone with something to say."

Jonah unfolded the paper. An official notice. She read it twice, automatically checking the typeface — old habits — and found it competent, municipal, unloved. Set by someone paid by the hour.

"You could speak yourself," Perrin said. "You're the obvious choice. The woman who printed it under her own name. The crowd would carry you to the podium."

"They would," Jonah agreed.

"But?"

Jonah looked toward the back of the shop. Through the doorway she could see the kitchen table, and Elias hunched over his lunch and his outrage, drafting rebuttal notes. Beyond him, through the window, her mother rose stiffly from the bean rows, brushed earth from her knees, and stood a moment looking at the garden — at the place where the burning finished — with her face tilted toward the harbor light.

Twenty years ago, Jonah had printed *Set by Jonah Nakamura* beneath a forbidden title, knowing it would cost her the shop, and had been right, and had never once regretted it. But she had learned something since, in the long peace, in the years of lessons and funerals and wedding announcements and figs divided five ways: that the truth did not live in her alone anymore. It lived in Wren's memorized columns. In Sefa's corrected *e*. In Elias's unbearable testimony and Mira's beans and Tomas's Thursday laughter and the cracked bell and the walked-past wall. She had spent her whole life learning that records survive in mouths, not archives.

"If I stand up there," Jonah said, "they'll make it about me. The brave printer, the martyr's daughter, the legend. And legends are just lies with better posture." She folded the notice closed. "Let the apprentices read the names. All forty-two. Out loud, at the wharf, in front of the granite proposal. Two children and a list. Let the council try to call *that* destabilizing."

Perrin stared at her. Then she began, helplessly, to laugh — a real laugh, startled out of her, the laugh of a tired woman remembering why she'd chosen her side in the first place.

"Oh, they'll *hate* it," she said, delighted. "Absolutely hate it. It's perfect."

"Of course it is," Jonah said. "I learned politics from a typecase. Everything important is made of small pieces, and everything depends on who puts them in order."

Perrin let herself out. The bell rang its cracked, honest note behind her, and the two apprentices emerged from their hiding places, and Wren looked at Jonah with wide eyes, having heard everything, having understood, as she seemed to understand everything, more than she should.

"You want *us*," she said, "to read the names. At the wharf. In front of the *council*."

"I want you to practice first," Jonah said. "Every day. Starting tomorrow. Because if you're going to speak forty-two names to a city, Wren Nakamura-Vane, you're going to pronounce every single one correctly."

"That's not my name."

"It will be," Jonah said, "if you keep setting type in this family long enough. Now — wash up. Your grandfather's making dinner, which means your grandmother is supervising, which means we'll eat late, and before that—" She nodded toward the case rack, toward the waiting sorts, toward the long unbroken habit of the work. "Before that, we distribute."

Outside, the light went gold over the harbor, and in the garden Mira straightened her back and picked the day's four figs, and inside the shop, in the warm smell of ink and oil, two children began putting the letters back where they belonged.
