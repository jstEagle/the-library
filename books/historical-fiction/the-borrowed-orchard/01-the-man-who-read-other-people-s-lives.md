## 1. The Man Who Read Other People's Lives

The kettle in the mews had a voice like an old woman complaining about the weather, and Oscar Delacroix had learned to time his mornings by it. Six o'clock: the complaint began. Quarter past: it reached its pitch, and he lifted it off the hob before Mrs. Prewitt could hear it from the kitchen above. He liked the small conspiracies of the early hour — the way he and the kettle kept secrets from the house, the way the mews held its breath between night and day, the gas lamps still burning their yellow vigil over wet cobblestones while somewhere behind him, in the great sleeping bulk of Ashcombe House, Lady Verity Ashcombe dreamed whatever patronesses of lost causes dreamed.

He polished the Daimler by lamplight because he preferred to be finished before anyone could watch him do it. A chauffeur who worked in public was a servant. A chauffeur whose car gleamed as though by miracle was a craftsman, nearly invisible, and invisibility was the whole of Oscar's ambition. He had refined it over eleven years the way other men refined golf swings or recipes. He knew precisely how far back to stand when delivering a message — near enough to be summoned, far enough to be forgotten. He knew how to arrange his face into a pleasant blankness that invited no conversation. He knew that the trick was not to avoid being seen but to be seen without being *noticed*, which was a subtler art entirely, and one at which he had become, if he said so himself, masterly.

Nobody ever did say so himself. That was rather the point.

By half past seven the car stood gleaming in the grey light like a black beetle, and Oscar went up the area steps with the post tray. The morning's letters had come with the milk — four for Lady Verity, two for the secretary Mr. Loxley, one circular addressed to *The Occupier*. He carried them through the basement passage where the smell of bacon and blacklead competed, up the back stairs, along the corridor that smelled instead of beeswax and cold marble, and laid them on the silver salver in the morning room, squared to the edge, largest beneath, smallest atop. It was a small ceremony and he performed it daily, and daily nobody remarked upon it, and daily he felt the peculiar satisfaction of a man who has done something perfectly and had it pass unobserved.

Then he took three of them back.

This was the habit. He would not have called it a vice; vices were drink, cards, women, the things that ruined men visibly. This was quieter than any of those. This was more like reading. And Oscar was, above all things, a reader.

It had begun innocently enough, as these things do, five years ago, with a letter left out by mistake and a name on the flap he recognized from the newspapers — some MP or other, a man whose photograph Oscar had seen pinned inside the office of his old garage employer. Oscar had told himself he only meant to carry it upstairs. Then he had told himself he only meant to look at the handwriting. Then he had told himself a great many things, and the telling had gotten easier each time, until now the ritual was as fixed as the polishing of the Daimler: three letters chosen at random — never all, never none, randomness itself a kind of courtesy — carried down to the mews, steamed open over the kettle's spout, read, dried flat beneath the iron Mrs. Prewitt used for collars, resealed with a stick of gum arabic he kept wrapped in oilcloth behind a loose brick, and returned to the salver before eight o'clock struck.

In eleven years he had never been caught. In eleven years he had never once acted on anything he learned.

That second fact was the foundation of everything. Other men might have sold what he knew, or blackmailed with it, or run chattering to the papers. Oscar did none of these. He simply *knew*. He knew that Lady Verity's brother in Shropshire had gambled away two hundred pounds and asked her not to tell their mother. He knew that the mother knew anyway. He knew that Mr. Loxley, the secretary, wrote love letters on Tuesdays to a woman in Camden who signed herself *Your own B.*, and that the woman in Camden also received letters on Fridays from a dentist in Ealing. He knew that Lady Verity sent money every quarter to a Mrs. Fennick in Battersea whose husband had been transported to Australia, and that the letters between them were careful and formal and full, underneath the formality, of something that looked to Oscar very much like grief.

He knew all this the way he knew the map of London: completely, uselessly, beautifully. Knowledge was his collection, and collections are not meant to be spent.

*You know everything,* his sister Rosa had told him once, home from service in Richmond with her hat askew, *and you do nothing with it. You're like a bank that won't lend.*

*A bank keeps money safe,* Oscar had said. *That's the whole virtue of a bank.*

*And pays you nothing,* Rosa had said, and gone off laughing, and he had thought about it for perhaps a week and then filed her remark away with everything else, in the vast unlabeled archive of his head.

The truth — and he permitted himself the truth only in the privacy of the mews, with the steam rising — was that knowing other people's lives was the next best thing to having one. His own life ran in grooves worn so smooth they barely registered. Up at six. Car polished by seven. Post by half past. Lady Verity driven to her committee at ten, to the Women's Social and Political Union offices at two, to whatever drawing room or lecture hall wanted lending her name at four. Home by seven. Supper in the servants' hall, where the talk was of prices and illnesses and the cook's sister's disappointing fiancé. Bed by eleven. And then up again, and the same again, and the same, until the years themselves had taken on the smoothness of the Daimler's bonnet — polished, gleaming, going nowhere.

Other people's letters were the windows out of that. Through them he had sat in hospital wards and prison visiting rooms and seaside boarding houses and the offices of men whose decisions moved armies. He had watched marriages begin and end in ink. He had read a will that cut off a son for marrying a Catholic and read the son's reply and read the father's second letter, written twenty years later, three lines long, saying only *I am old and I was wrong.* Oscar had read that letter standing at the kettle with his hands shaking slightly, and had put it back exactly as he'd found it, and had never mentioned it to a living soul, and had nevertheless felt for the rest of that day as though he'd witnessed something enormous — a cathedral, a coronation, a comet. History, he concluded, happened constantly, everywhere, in envelopes, to everyone; and the great advantage of his position was that history could not happen *to* him. He was not a participant. He was the audience, seated comfortably in the dark, watching the play unfold in other people's hands.

It was a comfortable doctrine. He had built his whole life on it the way a man builds a house on good stone, and he did not inspect the foundations, because a man does not inspect what he cannot bear to find cracked.

* * *

At ten minutes to nine the side door opened and Lady Verity came down into the mews with her collar up and her gloves already buttoned, moving with the quick decisive stride she used everywhere except in her own drawing room, where she slowed to the pace of a woman who wished to be thought unhurried.

"Good morning, Delacroix. Which of them is worse today — the Home Office or my sister?"

"Your sister, my lady. She writes on blue paper."

"Blue paper means tears." Verity settled into the rear seat and arranged her skirts with two efficient movements. "Home Office merely means fury, and I can drive fury out of my head. Tears linger." She glanced at him as he took the wheel, and her eyes — sharp, grey, forty-eight years old and missing nothing — rested on him a moment longer than usual. "You've heard nothing? Nothing odd?"

"Odd, my lady?"

"Odd. Strange. Out of the ordinary." She waved a gloved hand at the general world. "Men loitering. Questions asked. Anything."

"No, my lady."

"Hm." She faced forward. "Drive by the park, not the Embankment. I'm expected at the Strand at half past and I refuse to arrive flustered."

Oscar drove. The Daimler moved through the waking streets with its deep contented thrum, past the costermongers crying the first of the spring greens, past a dray horse stamping at the cold, past two boys chasing a hoop along the gutter as though the hoop owed them money. Verity sat straight-backed in the rear mirror's frame, reading her blue-papered letter with a face that grew grimmer by the furlong, and Oscar watched her in the mirror with the professional attention he gave to all human beings, cataloguing, filing, understanding, and doing precisely nothing about any of it.

She frightened him a little, Lady Verity. Not in herself — she was scrupulously fair with the staff, remembered names, paid above the going rate, and had once, memorably, stood in the rain for an hour outside Holloway with a placard and caught a chill that laid her low for a fortnight. What frightened him was her certainty. She believed things. Belief, in Oscar's observation, was expensive; it cost people their comfort, their reputations, occasionally their freedom. The suffragists who crowded her drawing rooms had a wild bright look about them, like people leaning into weather. Miss Pankhurst's women went to prison and came back thinner and harder and went again. They threw bricks. They chained themselves to railings. They starved themselves in cells while officials fed them through tubes, and the tubes, the women said when they spoke of it at all, were instruments of torture dressed as medicine.

Oscar knew all of this. He knew more of it than most of the members, because he read the letters — the furious ones from prison, the fundraising ones, the desperate ones from mothers whose daughters wouldn't come home. He had read accounts of the force-feeding written in hands that shook, and had set them down and eaten his supper and slept soundly, mostly. History happened to those women. He watched.

At the corner of the Strand a newsboy was shouting something about Parliament and the Conciliation Bill, and Verity lowered her window an inch to listen, and Oscar, waiting at the crossing, let his gaze drift to the letter lying open on the seat beside her — he couldn't help it; it was like water finding a low place — and saw, in the top corner, a date and a line that snagged on something in his memory. *Friday,* the line said. *Everything depends on Friday.* He filed it. Friday meant nothing yet. Friday would mean nothing tomorrow. Most dates in most letters meant nothing, forever.

They arrived at the Strand at half past exactly. Verity gathered her papers, paused with her hand on the door, and looked back at him with that searching expression again.

"You're certain there's been nothing, Delacroix? No strangers asking after the house? No letters you can't account for?"

"I account for all the letters, my lady," Oscar said, with a smoothness that would have astonished him if he had noticed it.

"Yes," said Lady Verity slowly. "I imagine you do." And she got out and went up the steps into the building where the future was being argued over, and the door closed behind her, and Oscar sat alone in the idling car with the strange feeling of having been weighed and found — what? He couldn't say. Found present, perhaps. Found *there*, which for eleven years he had made it his life's work never to be.

He shook it off. He drove home through the strengthening daylight, washed the car's windscreen, ate bread and cheese standing up in the mews, and took down the afternoon's letters when they came — five of them now, one bearing a foreign stamp, one smelling faintly of lavender, one thick and official-looking with a crest embossed in the flap.

He chose three. He always chose three.

The kettle was just coming to its complaining boil when he heard the sound that stopped his hand halfway to the envelope — footsteps in the mews entrance, uneven, dragging, the footsteps of someone exhausted or hurt, and then a voice, hoarse and low, addressing him directly, which almost never happened, which in eleven years had essentially never happened:

"You're the driver here? Then listen to me, mate, because I haven't got long." The man swayed against the doorpost, rain-soaked, gaunt as a November tree, clutching a bundle of letters wrapped in oilcloth against his chest as though it were a child. "I'm looking for the woman who keeps Edith's letters."

"Edith?" said Oscar. "I don't—"

"Edith Marchmont." The man coughed, a wet terrible sound. "And don't tell me she's dead. Everyone tells me she's dead. She's been dead two years, they put it in the papers." He held the bundle out with a shaking hand, and Oscar took it without meaning to, purely reflexively, the way a man catches something falling — and felt through the oilcloth the unmistakable shape of envelopes, a dozen of them, tied with string.

He knew that string. He knew that knot. And when the wrapping gaped a little at the corner, he saw the direction on the topmost letter, written in a hand he knew better than any living person's except perhaps his own — the hand he had studied for years across a kettle's steam, the tall ascenders, the fierce forward slant, the way the *d* looped back like a woman turning to look over her shoulder.

Lady Verity Ashcombe, Grosvenor Square.

Written by Edith Marchmont, who had died in Holloway Gaol in the winter of 1911.

The kettle screamed behind him. Outside, the rain came down on the cobblestones, and the gas lamps burned yellow over the wet street, and Oscar Delacroix — spectator, archivist, the man who knew everything and acted on nothing — stood holding a dead woman's mail, and felt the floor of his comfortable dark theatre tilt slowly, terribly, toward the stage.
