## 2. A Stranger at the Service Entrance

The rain began an hour before dawn and settled in for the day the way London rain does — not falling so much as residing, a grey tenancy over the mews that turned the cobblestones the colour of old pewter. Oscar Delacroix stood under the porte-cochère with his hands behind his back and watched it come down on the Daimler's long black bonnet, beading there like sweat on a sleeping animal.

He had washed the car twice already. It did not need a third wash, but washing was what he did while he waited, and waiting was most of what he did, and he had made his peace with that years ago. There was a comfort in the small economies of service: the cloth folded just so, the brass fittings polished until they held a warped little mirror of the world, the tyres checked each morning against a wear that would take months to arrive. A man could spend a whole life tending things that were not his, and if he was careful — if he was very careful — no one ever thought to ask him who he was.

At half past seven he carried the morning tray up to Lady Verity Ashcombe's study, knocked once with the back of two knuckles — the knock of a man who is furniture — and set it down beside her without disturbing the drift of her pen.

"Thank you, Delacroix."

"Ma'am."

Three letters waited on the salver for posting. He took them without being asked. One was from her solicitor; one bore the crest of the Women's Social and Political Union; the third had no crest at all, only a smudged postmark from somewhere in the north. All three went into his inside pocket rather than the letter box by the tradesmen's entrance.

This was the other economy of his life, the one nobody suspected because nobody imagined it was worth suspecting. Upstairs, in the small room over the coach house where he slept, there was a biscuit tin beneath a loose floorboard, and in the tin were forty-one letters — or the ghosts of them, transcribed in his own neat clerk's hand into a ledger he had bought for sixpence and labelled, absurdly, *Accounts*. He steamed them open over the kettle, read them, sealed them again with a dab of gum and the flat of a warm knife, and returned them before anyone was the wiser. In four years he had never been caught. In four years he had never *acted* on anything he learned either. That was the arrangement he had struck with himself, the bargain that let him sleep: knowledge was a currency he collected but never spent. He knew which of Lady Verity's nieces was engaged to a man already married in Canada. He knew that Mrs. Prewitt sent half her wages to a sister in Bristol who drank. He knew that the government had opened Lady Verity's mail twice in 1911 and once, clumsily, last March, and that she suspected nothing, or pretended to suspect nothing.

He knew, in short, everyone's life except the shape of his own.

The rain thickened toward noon. Oscar drove Lady Verity to a committee meeting in Kensington and waited outside it for ninety minutes, reading a newspaper he had already read, watching the pavement for the plainclothes men he had learned to recognise by their shoes — good shoes, too good for the men wearing them, who always stood as though standing were work they were being paid for. There were two today. He noted them the way he noted everything, filed them away in the tin-box of his head, and did nothing, because doing nothing was his profession and he was very good at it.

* * *

It was nearly five when the stranger came to the mews.

Oscar heard him before he saw him — footsteps in the alley with a wrongness to them, uneven, dragging slightly on the left side, the sound of a man walking on a leg that had been broken and set by someone in a hurry. Then the man came around the corner of the stable wall and into view, and stopped, and looked at Oscar for a long moment with the expression of someone checking a face against a description carried too long in a wet pocket.

He was gaunt the way famine is gaunt, not fashion — cheekbones standing out like tent-poles, skin gone the grey-white of paper left out in weather, lips cracked and dark. His coat had been good once, a decent tweed now soaked through to a colour like riverbed mud, and it hung off him as though it belonged to a larger man he used to be. Rain ran off the brim of a hat crushed flat at the crown. He swayed slightly as he stood, and Oscar, who noticed everything, noticed also the smell coming off him underneath the wet wool — fever, that particular sweetish sourness, unmistakable to anyone who had grown up poor.

"You're the chauffeur," the man said. Not a question. His voice was roughened, as if gravel had been poured through it.

"I'm Mr. Ashcombe's driver, yes."

"The house is yours to mind, then."

"It's not mine to mind anything," Oscar said carefully. "If you've business with the household—"

"I haven't." The man took a step forward and stopped again, and Oscar saw how much the step cost him, how his hand found the stable wall and gripped it as though the wall had been hired for the purpose. "I've business with a particular member of it. I was told—" He paused, and something moved across his face that might have been doubt, or exhaustion eating doubt's territory. "I was told to ask for the woman who keeps Edith's letters."

The rain filled the silence that followed. It came down steadily on the cobbles, on the roof of the car, on the canvas awning, a thousand small indifferent drummings, and inside that noise Oscar felt the world tilt very slightly, the way a carriage tilts when a wheel finds a rut you didn't know was there.

Because he knew that name. Of course he knew it. Every reader of every newspaper in England knew it, and Oscar, who read more than newspapers, knew it better than most. Edith Marchmont. Militant suffragist, organizer, the woman whose arrest in 1911 had been celebrated in the Tory papers and mourned in private rooms all over Belgravia; the woman whose hunger strike in Holloway had been written about in careful, euphemistic paragraphs — *her condition gave cause for concern* — and then written about no more at all. Dead two years this spring. There had even been a notice, brief and oddly uninformative, and Lady Verity had worn black for a month and burned no fewer than three bundles of correspondence in the study grate, and Oscar had stood in the yard watching the ash rise and thought: *there goes another chapter I'll never get to read.*

Dead women kept no letters. Dead women were safe. That was the whole function of death, as the authorities understood it — it closed the account.

"I don't know anyone by that name," Oscar said.

"Yes, you do." The man smiled, and it was a terrible thing to see, less a smile than a baring of teeth to prove they still worked. "You know exactly who I mean. You're the one who reads the post. Don't trouble yourself looking surprised — I was told that too. Told you'd deny everything, and lie well, because lying is the one trade this city teaches free of charge." He pushed himself off the wall and came closer, close enough that Oscar could see his eyes properly: light grey, burning, ringed with the bruised shadows of a man who had not slept in a bed so much as fallen into one and been thrown out of it repeatedly. "I don't have time for your denial, mister chauffeur. I've been followed since Leeds. I may have lost them at Peterborough, or they may have lost me deliberately, which is worse. I'm carrying something that has to reach Lady Ashcombe tonight, and I am—" he glanced down at himself, at the tremor in his own hand, with a kind of clinical detachment, "—evidently not going to remain upright much longer to deliver it myself."

"I can take a message," Oscar said. "I can't admit strangers to the house. You must see my position."

"My position." The man laughed — a dry, cracking sound, like a branch giving way. "My position is a cell in Holloway with my name scraped off the register. My position is two years running through the wet parts of England on money I stole from people I loved. But thank you for asking." He fumbled inside his coat, and Oscar tensed — but what came out was not a weapon. It was a bundle, oilcloth-wrapped, tied with butcher's twine, and it was dripping, and the man held it against his chest the way one holds a child, or a wound. "Look at it. Just look. Then tell me your position."

Oscar did not want to look. Looking was a commitment. He had built four years of peace on the principle that a man could observe anything provided he never touched it, and here was a stranger trying to put the whole weight of something directly into his hands in the rain in his own mews where any of the neighbours' men might walk past at any moment.

But he looked.

The oilcloth had been tied and retied many times; the knots told a story of opening and closing, opening and closing. At one corner the wrapping had loosened, and through the gap showed the edge of a packet of letters — cheap paper, soft with handling, and across the topmost envelope, in ink gone brown at the folds, a line of writing.

Oscar had never seen that handwriting before in his life.

That is what he told himself first, in the split second of pure self-protection. And then the second thought arrived, and it arrived like ice water finding the seam of a collar, because it was not true. He had seen that handwriting. He had seen it dozens of times, in dozens of envelopes, over four years of kettles and steam and gum and the flat of a warm knife — the distinctive forward-leaning script with its fierce ascenders, the way the writer crossed her t's like small accusations, the peculiar hooked capital E that looked ready to fly off the page.

Edith Marchmont's hand. Unmistakable. Intimate to him as his own signature, though he had never signed anything in her name and never would.

And Edith Marchmont was dead. There had been a notice. There had been a certificate. There had been a grave — Oscar had never seen the grave, but graves were the sort of thing that existed whether one inspected them or not, the way the far side of the moon existed.

The rain drummed. The Daimler gleamed. Somewhere beyond the wall, a milk cart rattled past, and the world went on conducting its ordinary Tuesday, and Oscar Delacroix stood holding a dead woman's handwriting in his master's mews and felt the floor of his convictions give way beneath him with a sound only he could hear.

"Where did you get these?" His voice came out lower than he intended.

"From her." The man said it simply, the way one states the time. "I'll say it plainly so we waste none of my remaining minutes: Edith Marchmont is alive. She did not die in Holloway. What died in Holloway was the truth about what was done to her, and it was killed on purpose, by men with titles, and buried under a certificate with lies on it. She wrote all of it down — names, dates, wards, witnesses. Everything. And these are her instructions for getting it into print before Friday week." He held the bundle out. His arm shook. The rain ran down his wrist and dripped from his knuckles between them like a slow metronome counting something down. "Take it to her ladyship. Tell her Crane brought it. Tobias Crane. She'll know the name if Edith ever trusted her enough to use it. Tell her—" He stopped. Swayed. Caught himself on the wing of the motorcar, and his palm left a dark print on the polished black lacquer, and some deep servant's nerve in Oscar flinched at the mark more violently than at anything else the man had said.

"Tell her what?" Oscar asked.

But the man's eyes had gone strange — fixed on some middle distance where the rain was not falling — and when he answered, his voice had begun to slur at its edges. "Tell her it's heavier than it looks. The paper. Tell her it weighs..." He frowned, hunting for the figure, and did not find it. "She'll know. She'll know what I mean."

Then his knees went, all at once, the way scaffolding goes — not bending but ceasing — and Oscar caught him, or mostly caught him, taking the man's full weight against his chest and staggering back a step into the shelter of the porte-cochère with the oilcloth bundle pinned between them, soaking through both their coats, cold as a delivered verdict.

"Mister Prewitt will hear about this," Oscar said automatically, and then corrected himself — "Mrs. Prewitt" — and got no answer, because the man called Crane had gone loose and heavy as sand, breath whistling thinly through cracked lips, fever-heat pouring off him through the wet wool like heat off a stone at dusk.

Oscar stood in the rain with a dying stranger in his arms and a dead woman's letters against his ribs, and understood, with the clarity of a man watching his own reflection shatter in the Daimler's door panel, that the spectator's seat he had occupied so comfortably for four years had just been pulled out from under him — and that he was now, whether he consented or not, upon the stage.

He looked down at the bundle. The loosened corner gaped. The hooked capital E leaned forward into the wet, flying off its page.

"All right," Oscar said quietly, to Crane, to the rain, to whoever was keeping the accounts. "All right. But you'd better not die in my mews."

He hooked the man's arm over his shoulder, gathered the bundle tight, and carried him through the tradesmen's door into the warm dark of the house — past the bell board with its neat ivory labels, past the coat stand, past the whole ordered architecture of his invisible life — toward the kitchen, where Mrs. Prewitt was boiling water for tea, and where everything, though he did not yet know it, was about to begin.
