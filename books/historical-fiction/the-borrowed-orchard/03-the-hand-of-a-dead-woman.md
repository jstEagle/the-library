## 3. The Hand of a Dead Woman

The rain had followed Crane into the house, or so it seemed; he left a dark trail across the flagstones of the service corridor, small wet prints like the marks of some animal that had wandered in from a harder world. Oscar walked half a step behind him, one hand hovering near the man's elbow, ready to catch him if he pitched forward. He did not need to look at the bundle in his own left hand to know it was there. He could feel it through the oilcloth, the way you feel a stone through your boot — its weight, its edges, its terrible particularity.

Mrs. Prewitt met them at the foot of the back stairs, keys at her waist, her face arranged in the expression she wore for tradesmen and bishops alike: courteous, impenetrable.

"He's asking for Lady Ashcombe," Oscar said quietly. "He says—" and here his voice did something he had not authorized it to do, something thin and strange— "he says he has letters from Edith Marchmont."

Mrs. Prewitt's keys did not so much as whisper against each other. That was how Oscar knew the name had struck her too. Twenty-two years in service had taught her that the face was a door and everything important happened behind it.

"Put him by the kitchen fire," she said. "I'll send up to her ladyship."

"She'll want to come down herself."

"She'll want a great many things." Mrs. Prewitt was already turning. "Sit him down, Mr. Delacroix. And don't let him die on the clean flags."

* * *

Lady Verity Ashcombe was sixty-one years old and had spent thirty of them learning to be unafraid, which is to say she had learned to be afraid properly — on schedule, with witnesses, and without letting it show above the collarbone. She was dressing for dinner when Mrs. Prewitt's knock came, and she said, "Come in," in the voice she used for the whole household, which was neither command nor invitation but simply the sound of someone accustomed to being obeyed.

She listened standing at her glass, her maid's hands still working pins into the grey coil of her hair. She did not interrupt. When the account was finished — the stranger, the mews, the bundle sealed with Edith Marchmont's hand — she said only, "Describe the hand."

"The hand, my lady?"

"The writing, Prewitt. The letters."

"Slanted," Mrs. Prewitt said. "Leaning hard to the right, like a woman hurrying. The capital E's are large — theatrical, almost. She used to draw them with a flourish."

Verity sat down slowly on the dressing stool. Her reflection sat down with her, and for a moment the two of them regarded each other across the gulf of what they both understood: that there were perhaps six people alive who could describe Edith Marchmont's handwriting, and one of them was standing behind her holding a hairbrush.

"Two years," Verity said. "They told us two years ago she was dead. There was a certificate. There was a notice in *The Times* — four lines, no photograph. They do that when they want a death to be small."

"Yes, my lady."

"And now a man comes out of the rain carrying her letters." Verity looked at her own hands, folded in her lap. They were steady. She made them stay steady. "Prewitt, fetch my black shawl. The one without fringe. I am going down to my kitchen, apparently, instead of dining like a Christian."

"You can't receive him below stairs, my lady. It isn't done."

"No," Verity agreed, rising. "It isn't. Which is why it will be believed."

* * *

Oscar had got Crane into a chair by the kitchen range and stripped off the ruined coat. Underneath, the man was all ridges — collarbones like oars, wrists like knots in rope. His skin had the grey-gold tint of old paper. Oscar had seen fever before, in the army hospital where he'd convalesced in '99, and he knew its signature: the bright eye, the too-quick speech, the way the body ran hot and cold in the same hour.

Crane gripped his arm with surprising strength. "You read them," he whispered. "I saw your face in the mews. You've read her hand before."

"I've seen it," Oscar said carefully. "On correspondence."

"On correspondence." Crane almost smiled. "That's a very careful way of saying you steamed open other people's post, chauffeur."

Oscar felt the floor tilt a degree. He had prided himself — had built an entire interior architecture around the fact — that his trespasses were invisible, unwitnessed, a private museum of other people's lives that harmed no one because no one knew it existed. And here was a dying man naming it within ten minutes of arrival, casually, between shivers.

"The letters," Oscar said. "How many?"

"Nine. Written over fourteen months. Hidden in the lining of a Bible, then in a loaf, then sewn into my coat." Crane's eyes closed. "I carried them out of England once. Carried them back. They weigh nothing, those letters. Nothing at all. But they're the heaviest thing I've ever held."

The door opened and Lady Verity came in wearing the black shawl over her dinner dress, which gave her the look of a woman dressed for two different emergencies at once. She crossed the kitchen tiles like a duchess crossing a station platform — unhurried, certain of her right of way — and stopped before Crane's chair.

"You have something belonging to me," she said.

"Not yours, my lady." Crane lifted his head with effort. "Hers. She said — she made me swear — they go to Lady Verity Ashcombe, because Lady Verity has money and a printing connection and no husband to be arrested on her behalf. Those were her words. I'm sorry. I was instructed to wound."

Something moved through Verity's face — grief, recognition, the particular pain of being accurately described by an absent friend. "Then give them to me," she said, "and sit still while I read."

* * *

Later — much later, though the clock in the hall would insist it had been less than forty minutes — Oscar would remember the opening of the oilcloth as a kind of ceremony. Verity broke the wax seal with her thumbnail, precisely, as if she had done it a thousand times, and unfolded the first page, and went absolutely still.

Oscar stood by the range. Mrs. Prewitt stood by the door. The rain worked at the window. And Lady Verity Ashcombe read the handwriting of a dead woman by the light of a kitchen lamp, and her breath changed — Oscar heard it change, one long inhale that never quite finished exhaling — and he knew, before she said a word, before she turned the page with fingers that trembled only slightly and then steadied themselves by force of will, that the hand was true.

"It's hers," Verity said at last. Her voice was level. Below the levelness, things were moving like ice on a river. "The slant. The E's. She underlines twice when she's angry, three times when she's furious, and here—" she touched the page— "here she has underlined the same sentence five times, which means she wrote it knowing she might not live to see it printed." She looked up. "Where did you get this?"

"From her," Crane said. "Eleven days ago. In a room above a chandler's in Hull."

"Hull."

"She couldn't come south. There's a description of her circulating — police circular, unofficial but thorough. So she wrote. Fourteen months, nine letters, and a tenth she finished the night I left." Crane coughed then, a deep tearing sound, and pressed his sleeve to his mouth, and when he lowered it there was color on the linen that had nothing to do with ink.

Nobody spoke about it. Everybody noted it.

* * *

Verity read aloud. She chose to — Oscar watched her make the decision, watched her glance at Mrs. Prewitt and at himself and decide that witnesses were a duty rather than a risk. Her voice was the reading-aloud voice of a woman who had read to children and to dying friends and once, memorably, to a crowded hall in Manchester when the microphones failed: clear, unhurried, giving every word its full value.

*"My dear V,"* the letter began. *"If you are reading this, then Tobias reached you, and if Tobias reached you, then God is not entirely a Home Office appointment.*

*I am dead, officially. You will have seen the little paragraph. I want you to understand exactly what that paragraph cost and who signed for it.*

*In February last I entered the eleventh day of hunger strike at Holloway. You know what they do — the tube, the gag, the chair. What you do not know is what happened on the twelfth day, because it was not permitted to happen. The doctor who attended me — a Dr. Loxley, whom I believe acted under instruction rather than appetite — damaged something in my throat. I could not speak above a whisper for three weeks. I heard the wardress say to another, 'If this one goes, we bury it quiet,' and I understood that 'quiet' was a policy, not a hope.*

*I did not die. Another did. A girl called Ada Quill, nineteen, third cell along. They force-fed her on the fourteenth day and she hemorrhaged and by morning there was a new certificate and a new story: pneumonia, contracted before admission, family notified, case closed. I saw the order that closed it. I will tell you whose signature was at the bottom of it, because signatures are how power signs its name when it thinks no one is looking.*

*Sir Roderick Vane, Home Office. He visited the prison twice in March. He examined me personally — asked after my health with the tenderness of a man checking a ledger — and pronounced me unfit for further publicity. Unfit, V. As though I were meat gone off. Two days later my death was announced, because a dead suffragist cannot testify, cannot sue, cannot stand on a platform and say what was done to her and to Ada Quill. They did not kill me. They killed my legal existence. It is tidier. It files better.*

*I have spent fourteen months proving that a woman declared dead can still hold a pen. Here is the proof. Publish it before Friday. Friday is when the Home Office closes its books for the quarter, when the transfer papers move, when I am moved — I know the schedule, V, I have bribed a laundress with my wedding ring, which tells you what my marriage was worth — and once I am moved to whatever institution they have chosen, the letters stop and the story dies with me in some renamed room in some unnamed place.*

*Publish before Friday. Not for me. For Ada Quill, whose mother was told a lie by men in good coats. And burn this letter after typing it, because I have died once already and found it inconvenient.*

*Yours, alive and furious,
Edith"*

* * *

The lamp hissed. Somewhere upstairs a clock struck the half-hour with the serene indifference of furniture.

"Vane," Verity said finally. "Roderick Vane. Under-Secretary. He chaired the committee that reviewed prison medical procedure — the committee I presented evidence to, two years ago. He shook my hand." She looked at her own hand as though it had been contaminated and needed explaining. "He shook my hand and told me the government took these matters with the utmost seriousness."

"My lady," Mrs. Prewitt said, in the tone of a woman reporting that the fish had arrived, "the gentleman is sliding out of his chair."

Crane had indeed begun a slow collapse, dignified even in failure, one hand still hooked over the chair's arm as though reluctant to surrender it. Oscar caught him under the shoulders; the man weighed less than the bundle of letters, or seemed to. His skin was furnace-hot and his lips had gone a cracked grey.

"Water," Oscar said. "And brandy if we keep any the master hasn't found."

"Boiled water," Mrs. Prewitt corrected, already moving. "Brandy is medicine and medicine is measured."

Crane's eyes opened, fixed on Verity with the desperate clarity of a man spending his last coins. "Friday," he rasped. "Not next week. Not when it's convenient. She's moved Friday. Everything ends Friday. Promise me you'll—"

"I promise you nothing," Verity said, kneeling so that her face was level with his, and Oscar, watching, understood that this was the kindest thing anyone had said to the man all night. "Promises are what people make when they want to stop thinking. I am not going to stop thinking until this is done. Prewitt — the blue room, the bed with the iron frame, and hot bricks for the feet. Send the boy for Dr. Ferris at first light and tell him it's gastric, nothing more. A friend of the family taken ill on the road."

"A friend of the family," Mrs. Prewitt repeated, filing the fiction away with the rest of her keys.

Verity rose, and in rising she became again the public woman, the patroness, the name on the letterhead — but her eyes, when they found Oscar's across the kitchen, belonged to somebody rawer and older than either.

"Well, Delacroix," she said. "You drive me everywhere. You hear everything. You see everyone. I have long suspected you are the best-informed man in this house." She held the letters against her chest with both arms, the way one holds a child or a weapon. "Tell me what a man does with information, when doing nothing has become the same as helping the men who wrote that certificate."

Oscar opened his mouth to answer and discovered that for the first time in his adult life, he had no secondhand material to offer. No borrowed opinion. No observed life.

"I don't know yet, my lady," he said honestly. "But I think I'm about to find out."

Above them, faintly, through the floorboards, the hall clock began striking eleven — and beneath its chime, from the blue room, came the sound of Tobias Crane beginning to cough blood into the dark.
