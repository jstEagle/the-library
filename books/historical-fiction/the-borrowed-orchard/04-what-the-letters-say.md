## 4. What the Letters Say

The library at Ashcombe House smelled that morning of coal smoke and camphor, and of something else beneath it — the faint medicinal sweetness of the ether Mrs. Prewitt had used on Tobias Crane's fevered brow upstairs. Oscar stood at the window with his hands behind his back, watching the mews below, where the rain had finally stopped and the cobbles shone like poured pewter. Somewhere a milk cart rattled past. The world was proceeding exactly as it always did, and he found this obscene.

Behind him, Lady Verity Ashcombe sat at her writing desk with the bundle of letters laid out before her like instruments on a surgeon's tray. She had changed out of last night's clothes into a high-collared grey dress that made her look less like a patron of lost causes and more like a magistrate. Her reading glasses were pushed up into her hair. She had not touched the letters in ten minutes.

"We should wait," she said, for the third time.

"He's sleeping," Oscar said. "Mrs. Prewitt gave him laudanum an hour ago. He won't know what we've read until it's done."

"That is not why I wish to wait." Verity removed her glasses entirely and set them down with a small, precise click. "Once these words are spoken aloud in this room, they cannot be unsaid. You understand that, don't you? Whatever is in them — whoever they name — we will be implicated simply by knowing. There will be no more standing apart from it. Not for you, not for me, not for Mrs. Prewitt, who has served this family for thirty years and deserves better than to have sedition read to her over breakfast."

At the sideboard, Mrs. Prewitt did not look up from the coffee she was pouring, but her chin lifted a fraction of an inch. "I buried a husband who came back from the Transvaal with his lungs full of somebody else's war," she said to the silver pot. "I've no illusions about what respectable men do when nobody's watching. Read them, my lady. I'll listen or I'll leave, but I'd rather know than wonder."

Oscar looked at Verity. She looked at the letters.

"Very well," she said. "But you read, Mr. Delacroix. My eyes are not steady enough this morning, and if I am to be implicated, I would rather implicate your voice than mine."

It was a joke, or near enough to one, and none of them laughed.

* * *

He broke the first seal carefully, though care hardly mattered now; Crane had already steamed these open somewhere between Holloway and the mews, and the envelopes had gone soft as old leaves. The paper inside was cheap, greyish, the kind sold in prison chapels by the sheet. Edith Marchmont's hand crossed the page in that slanting, impatient script Oscar knew better than his own — he had watched it arrive through Lady Verity's letterbox for three years before her death, had held it under steam and lamp, had read every word of every letter about committees and subscriptions and the moral bankruptcy of Asquith, and had sealed each one again and put it back in its tray feeling like a man who had eaten someone else's dinner and remained hungry.

This was different. This was not committee business.

*My dear Verity,* the letter began, *if indeed this reaches you, then God has finally taken an interest in suffragists, because I do not expect it to.*

Oscar cleared his throat and began properly, aloud.

"'My dear Verity — if indeed this reaches you, then God has finally taken an interest in suffragists, because I do not expect it to. I write from a place I will not name, in the company of a man I trust with my life because he has already spent it on me once. What follows is testimony. I ask you to publish it entire, unedited, under my true name, before Friday next. If it appears after Friday, Vane's people will have buried it, and me with it.'"

He stopped. "Vane," he said. "She names a Vane."

"I don't know any Vane," said Mrs. Prewitt.

"You wouldn't," Verity said quietly. "That is precisely the point of men like him. Continue, Mr. Delacroix."

He continued.

"'You believe I died in Holloway. So does the country; so, I am assured, does my mother, which is the cruelty I cannot forgive and will spend whatever remains of my life repaying. I did not die. I nearly did. In March of 1911, after eleven days without food, they decided to feed me whether I consented or no. You know how they do it — the gag, the tube, four wardresses to hold one woman down. I had endured it twice already. On the third occasion something went wrong. The doctor — I never learned his name, a nervous young creature with shaking hands — passed the tube wrongly. Into the lung. I could not breathe. I felt myself drowning on dry land, Verity, in a room with six people in it, all of them employed by His Majesty's Government, and not one of them willing to call for help because calling for help meant admitting what they were doing.'"

Oscar heard his own voice go flat and careful, the way it went when he drove on ice. He kept reading.

"'They carried me out of the wing at night. I learned later they told the other prisoners I had been transferred. In truth they took me to a private room in the infirmary where a man was waiting. I heard the wardresses call him Sir Roderick. Sir Roderick Vane, of the Home Office — the same who appears in the newspapers shaking hands with prison reformers, the same whose photograph stands beside the King's at the opening of the new wing. He stood over my bed and asked the doctor, in a perfectly pleasant voice, whether the woman could be expected to recover. The doctor said it might go either way. And Vane said — I will repeat it exactly, because I have repeated it to myself every night since — Vane said: "Then see that it goes the convenient way."'" 

The clock on the mantel ticked through four whole seconds. Outside, the milk cart had gone, and the silence it left behind seemed louder than the cart had been.

"'When it became clear I might live after all — my body, which they starved and broke and drowned, proved more obstinate than their intentions — another arrangement was made. I was registered dead. Death certificate number—'"

Oscar turned the page. "'—number 4471, signed by the same trembling doctor, cause of death given as cardiac failure following pneumonia. My name is written on it in ink. I have seen it. They showed it to me, Verity, do you understand? They showed me my own death certificate and told me that if I ever surfaced again — if I ever wrote, spoke, marched, or breathed too loudly in public — there were men whose business it was to make the certificate true. A dead woman has no rights. A dead woman cannot testify. A dead woman who inconveniently continues to draw breath is not a citizen but a problem, and problems of this kind are solved quietly and filed away.'"

He lowered the page. His hands were not entirely steady, and he was glad neither woman was looking at them. Mrs. Prewitt had stopped pretending to pour coffee. She stood at the sideboard with the pot still in her hand, coffee presumably flooding the saucer, staring at nothing.

"They certified her dead," Verity said. It was not a question; it was a woman testing whether the floor would hold her weight. "While she was alive. While she lay in their own infirmary listening to them do it."

"And the doctor signed," Oscar said. "A man with shaking hands signed anyway."

"Men with shaking hands sign the worst things," said Mrs. Prewitt. "It's the steady ones you want to watch."

Verity rose and walked to the window, arms folded tight, looking out at the wet mews as though the answer might be parked out there among the carriages. "Roderick Vane," she said. "I've met him. Twice. Once at a garden party in Richmond — he complimented my hat — and once at a Select Committee hearing, where he gave forty minutes of evidence on the humane treatment of hunger strikers. Forty minutes, Mr. Delacroix. He described the procedure as 'regrettable but medically routine.' He used the word 'maternal.' He said the force-feeding was administered, quote, with the tenderness one shows an ailing child." She turned around. "And Edith was lying in a room downstairs from him while he said it, listening, unable to move."

"There are two more letters," Oscar said.

"Then read them. All of it. If we are to be hanged, let us at least know the full text of our crimes."

* * *

The second letter was longer, and colder. Where the first had burned, the second was written in the level tone of a woman who had passed through fury into something harder and more useful.

"'You will want to know how it was managed,'" Oscar read, "'so here it is plainly. The doctor was paid — I saw the envelope change hands, and later I saw the man himself, drunk in a public house in Croydon, telling anyone who would listen that he had "retired early." The registrar at Holloway was told the prisoner Marchmont had died and was shown the certificate; he entered it in the ledger without examining the body, because examining bodies is not the registrar's habit and Vane's people made sure he had no reason to form one. A coffin was buried. Whether it contained anything at all, I cannot say. I was driven out of London in a furniture van belonging to a firm I will not name, in the company of the man who now carries this letter, and I have been a ghost ever since — moving lodging every few weeks, wearing other women's coats, signing other women's names. For two years I have watched my comrades mourn me. I have read my own obituary. Mrs. Pankhurst called me a martyr. Verity, I am not a martyr. Martyrs are permitted to rest. I am a piece of evidence that learned to walk.'"

Mrs. Prewitt sat down. She did not ask permission; she simply lowered herself onto the window seat as though her legs had submitted notice. "Go on," she said, when Oscar paused. "I'm listening. Go on."

"'Here is what I ask, and I ask it knowing what it costs. Publish the testimony. Name Vane. Name the doctor. Give the certificate number, give the date, give everything, in a paper they cannot suppress — not The Suffragette, they will raid the print works within the hour, but some small radical sheet they have never thought worth watching, and let it be picked up from there. Do it before Friday. On Friday the session opens at which Vane intends to present revised figures on prison mortality — figures which conveniently omit certain deaths, including, in a sense, mine. If the truth prints before Friday, he cannot stand in that chamber and lie with a straight face while the newspapers hold his falsification up to the light. If it prints after, he will have had time to destroy the registrar's ledger, buy the doctor's silence permanently, and come for me. He has men, Verity. I have seen them twice. Tobias has seen them more. We are being hunted by the machinery of the state, and the state believes — quite sincerely, with every legal justification on paper — that I do not exist.'"

Oscar set the second letter down. The third was short. He almost didn't read it aloud — it was addressed half to Verity and half to no one, scrawled quickly, the handwriting deteriorating toward the bottom as though written in haste or cold.

"'Last thing. I do not ask you to remember me kindly; I was never kind, only right. But remember this: history is not happening somewhere else, to other people, in rooms we are not invited into. It happened in that infirmary with six people holding me down and one man asking whether I could be expected to recover. It is happening wherever a document is signed that shouldn't be. The question was never whether we are spectators or players. The question is only which side of the desk we're standing on when the paper comes across it. — E.M.'"

* * *

For a long moment nobody moved. Then Mrs. Prewitt stood, walked to the mantel, and wound the clock, which did not need winding, because her hands required employment and that was the nearest honest work.

"My lady," she said, with her back to the room, "that coffee is ruined. I'll make a fresh pot. And then I think you'll want me to pack a bag, because whichever of us carries those letters to wherever they're going, they'll not travel light and they'll not travel alone."

Verity blinked. "Mrs. Prewitt, I haven't said—"

"You were going to. I've ironed your petticoats for thirty years, madam; I know what you sound like before you've decided things." She left, and the door clicked behind her with terrible competence.

Oscar gathered the letters, squared their edges, and set them in the center of the desk. His heart was doing something unfamiliar — not the pleasant spectator's flutter he had known for years, watching history through steamed envelopes, but a heavier motion, like an engine turning over that hadn't started in a long time.

"Friday," he said. "Three days."

"Two and a half, by the time anything reaches a printer." Verity picked up the third letter and read it again silently, her lips moving slightly. "She's right, you know. About the desks. I spent a decade believing that funding pamphlets and chairing meetings constituted action, and meanwhile men like Vane were deciding whether women lived or died and filing the decision under 'cardiac failure.'" She looked up. "She cannot come here. I am followed — you've seen the man outside the tobacconist, the one who reads the same page of the same newspaper every afternoon. If she surfaces anywhere near this house, they take her, and this time there will be no furniture van."

"So the letters travel without her," Oscar said. "To a printer who isn't afraid. Which means north — Manchester, possibly Leeds — which means checkpoints, because Vane will have wired ahead the moment he suspects these exist. Which means—"

He stopped. Verity was looking at him with an expression he had never received from an employer in eight years of service: not instruction, not gratitude, but a genuine, open question.

"Mr. Delacroix," she said. "I cannot go. Crane cannot stand, let alone drive. Mrs. Prewitt is seventy-one and the most valuable person in this conspiracy precisely because nobody suspects her, and I will not spend her. Which leaves—" 

"The car," Oscar said. "Nobody stops a chauffeur. Chauffeurs are furniture with engines." He heard himself say it, and heard, underneath the joke, the thing he had actually decided somewhere between the second letter and the third, without consulting himself. "Which car, my lady?"

"The Daimler draws eyes. Take the little Wolseley — it looks like a doctor's runabout." Verity pressed both hands flat against the desk, over the letters, as though taking an oath upon them. "There is a compositor in Manchester, Albert Fenwick, off Oldham Road — Edith trusted him absolutely. If anyone can set this type and print it before Friday, it's Fenwick."

Upstairs, faintly through the ceiling, came the sound of Tobias Crane coughing — deep, wet, wrong — followed by Mrs. Prewitt's footsteps crossing above them at a pace considerably faster than her years should allow.

Oscar looked at the ceiling, then at the letters, then at the window, where the mews lay bright and ordinary in the washed morning light, the world still pretending that nothing had changed.

"I'll need petrol," he said, "and a map, and someone to sit beside me who can talk her way past a constable. Because I've just realized, my lady, that I have never in my life told a convincing lie — and I suspect I'm about to learn."
