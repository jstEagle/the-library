## 5. Three Checkpoints

The letters lay on the library table like something dissected.

Oscar had arranged them himself — he could not help arranging things; it was the chauffeur in him, the man who believed every journey began with order — seven envelopes in a row, each one steamed open and flattened, each one bearing that impossible hand. *Edith Marchmont*, written in ink that had faded from brown to the colour of old tea. Two years dead. And yet here she was on paper, page after page of her, describing the cell in Holloway, the tube forced down her throat, the doctor who had signed his name to lies.

Lady Verity Ashcombe stood at the window with her back to the room. She had been standing there for some minutes, which for Verity was the equivalent of another woman weeping. Through the glass, Belgrave Square went about its morning: a hansom, a milk cart, two gentlemen in grey top hats walking with the unhurried certainty of men who had never once been afraid of a government.

"There is a constable," Verity said at last, "at the corner by the plane tree. He has been there since yesterday afternoon. He is not watching the square, Oscar. He is watching this house."

"Are you certain?"

"I am sixty-one years old and I have been a nuisance to the Home Office for twenty years. I know what their watchers look like. That one shifts his weight every four minutes because his boots pinch." She turned. Her face was composed in the way of a portrait, all the feeling pressed behind the varnish. "I cannot leave this house without them knowing. I cannot go to Manchester. I cannot even post a letter without it being read first — they will have a warrant by Friday if they don't already."

"Then we don't post it," Oscar said. "We carry it."

The words were out before he had decided to say them, and hearing them, he felt the peculiar sensation of a man who has stepped off a curb and found no pavement beneath his boot. He had spent eleven years in this household perfecting the art of not being noticed. He knew the precise angle of stillness that made Lady Verity look through him rather than at him. He had read every letter that crossed her desk — suffragist correspondence, parliamentary gossip, love notes from a married colonel in Shropshire — and he had never once acted upon any of it. Knowing things, he had long ago concluded, was a pleasure in itself. Doing things was how men got ruined.

And here he was, volunteering to drive four hundred miles with seditious documents hidden in his motor car.

Verity was looking at him now, fully looking, and there was something in her expression he had never seen directed at him before. Interest. As though he had been furniture and had just cleared its throat.

"You?" she said.

"I know the roads north," he said. "Better than anyone you could hire. I've driven you to Yorkshire twice, to Edinburgh once. I know where the petrol stations are thin and where the hills eat engines. And no one watches me." He almost smiled. "I have made a profession of that."

Mrs. Prewitt, the housekeeper, who had been hovering by the door with a tray nobody wanted, set it down with a clack. "If I may," she said, which meant she would speak regardless. "They stopped a lady's motor outside Northampton last month. There was a notice circulated. Papers demanded, boots searched, the lot."

"Two checkpoints," Oscar said. "Perhaps three if they're thorough." He had been thinking about it since dawn, when sleep had abandoned him and he had lain in his attic room above the mews listening to the rain and doing what he always did with information: sorting it, filing it, admiring the shape of it. Except now the filing cabinet had opened and the contents were climbing out. "Rural constabulary on the Great North Road — they stop anything fast or foreign-looking, mostly to gawp. Then there's the railway inspection if we try to send anything by train. Manchester station has had inspectors since the window-smashing campaign. They're looking for parcels."

"So we don't send parcels," said Verity. "We send a man who looks like exactly what he is."

"A chauffeur driving his mistress's car north for servicing," Oscar said. It came easily. It frightened him how easily it came. "Nobody interrogates a servant, my lady. Servants are weather. You note them and forget them."

"Is that how you think of yourself?"

It was an odd question, delivered without malice, and he found he had no answer for it, so he bent over the letters instead. The topmost one ended mid-sentence — *they believe me finished, and it suits them to let the world agree* — and below the signature, cramped into the corner as though written in haste or hiding, an address: *J. Whitlock & Sons, Printers, Ancoats, Manchester*. And beneath the address, in different ink: *Tell no one but the man who sets the type.*

"She names her printer," Oscar said quietly. "She planned this. Before they declared her dead, she was planning this."

Verity crossed the room and put her hand flat on the letter, not quite touching the words, the way one touches the glass over a relic. "Edith Marchmont," she said, "was the most methodical revolutionary I ever met. They broke her body and it never once reached her mind." She looked up. "Very well, Mr. Delacroix. You shall drive north. But hear me — you are not to be brave. Bravery gets people arrested. Be dull. Be invisible. Be the weather."

"I have been practising for years," Oscar said.

* * *

They spent the afternoon making maps of danger.

Mrs. Prewitt produced a road atlas from the estate room, pre-war, its cloth spine soft with handling, and they spread it across the kitchen table because the library curtains were too thin and the kitchen faced the mews rather than the street. A candle burned between them although it was barely four o'clock; October had begun pressing its face against the windows.

"The first checkpoint," Oscar said, tracing with one finger, "will be here. Alconbury. The constabulary keep a point on the Great North Road where it narrows past the church — I've been waved through it twice this year alone. They stop touring motorists, mostly. Ask where you're bound, glance at your tyres." His finger moved north. "The second is worse. Wetherby. There's a sergeant there named Hobbes — I remember him because he fined me four shillings in 1911 for a cracked lamp and wrote my name in his book very slowly, enjoying it. If our friends in London have telegraphed ahead, Hobbes is the one who'll be waiting."

"And the third?"

"Not a checkpoint at all, properly. The railway. If any part of this goes by train — and it may have to, if the car fails — then Manchester London Road station is where it dies. They've had women searched there. Strip-searched, Mrs. Prewitt tells me." He glanced up. Prewitt, folding linen with the violence of a woman strangling an enemy, nodded grimly.

"So," said Verity. She had come down to the kitchen against all propriety, in a plain cardigan Oscar had never seen her wear, her silver hair pinned carelessly. The surveillance at the corner of the square had changed something in her; she moved through her own house now like a guest. "We must assume every mile of that road has ears. Which means the letters cannot travel as letters."

"No," Oscar agreed. "They travel as something boring."

They considered it together, the three of them bent over the atlas, and it struck Oscar — absurdly, inappropriately — that this was the happiest hour he had spent in eleven years of service. Here was everything he had hoarded for so long, all his secret knowledge of roads and timetables and the habits of policemen, suddenly worth something. Information, it turned out, was like money in a country whose language you didn't speak: useless until the day you found the market.

"The spare tyre casing," he said at last. "The outer cover comes off the rim. Flat documents rolled tight will sit inside the casing against the rim, invisible unless a man takes the whole wheel apart, and no rural sergeant dismantles a lady's wheel on a public road — he'd lose half an hour and his dignity."

"And if they do take it apart?"

"Then I'm a chauffeur carrying his employer's private correspondence to her tailor in Leeds, and God help me, I'll have the tailor's name ready."

Prewitt snorted. Verity almost smiled.

"The rest," Oscar continued, "goes in the hymnal."

"What hymnal?"

"Mine. Every driver keeps a book in the door pocket — maps, a Bible, whatever. Mine happens to be a hymnal I bought for sixpence in a Woolwich church sale. A man searching a car expects to find reading matter in a driver's effects and thinks nothing of it. Pages can be loosened and re-glued. Four sheets fit between the boards of *Abide With Me* and *Rock of Ages* and no man alive will ever look."

He heard himself describing these arrangements and marvelled. This was smuggling. He was describing smuggling, calmly, in a kitchen that smelled of bread and beeswax, and his hands were steady. Somewhere beneath the steadiness, though — he could feel it the way you feel water rising through floorboards — was fear. Not the dramatic fear of penny dreadfuls but a low animal hum, the knowledge that men were looking for these papers, that men had already hurt a woman nearly to death to keep them quiet, and that on Thursday morning those men would be somewhere along four hundred miles of English road, and so would he.

"Mr. Delacroix," Verity said, and he realized she had said his name twice. "You need not do this. I want that understood before you commit. If you are caught, you will not be treated as a courier. You will be treated as a conspirator, and the men who treat you so will feel themselves justified. I have watched them do it to better people than either of us."

Oscar looked down at the atlas, at his own finger still resting on Wetherby, Sergeant Hobbes's territory, four shillings and a slow-written name.

"For eleven years," he said, "I have read other people's lives through their envelopes and told myself that knowing was enough. That watching was a kind of honourable neutrality." He straightened. "It isn't, my lady. I understand that now. So — yes. I'll go. And I'd thank you not to call it bravery, since you've just explained it isn't."

For a moment Verity Ashcombe looked at her chauffeur as though seeing a stranger in a familiar coat. Whatever she saw, she kept behind the varnish.

"Thursday, then," she said. "Before dawn. And Oscar—" she used his Christian name deliberately, he noted, the first time in eleven years— "come back."

* * *

He did not sleep Wednesday night.

He told himself it was preparation: checking the car by lamplight in the mews, testing the spare casing until his fingers were black with rubber and graphite, gluing four sheets of Edith Marchmont's testimony between hymns with flour paste stolen from the kitchen. He rolled the remainder tight as cigarettes and fed them into the tyre, and when he remounted the wheel it spun true and silent, carrying its cargo of sedition inside itself like a heart inside a chest. He packed a grip with collar studs and a spare shirt. He memorized the printer's address until it repeated itself behind his eyes like a heartbeat: *Whitlock, Ancoats, the man who sets the type.*

But past two o'clock, sitting on the running board with the mews dark around him and the rain starting again, he had to admit the truth: the hum underneath had become a roar. Fear sat in him like swallowed river water, cold and heavy, and every time he closed his eyes he saw a cell, a tube, a doctor signing his name to a lie. He saw handcuffs. He saw his own name written slowly in Sergeant Hobbes's book.

*You can still say no,* he told himself. *Verity gave you the door.*

He thought about taking it. He thought about it honestly, the way he had once thought honestly about nothing at all. Tomorrow night he could be sleeping in his attic bed, letters unread, conscience unbothered, history happening elsewhere to other people while he polished the mud off the mudguards. It was a good life. It had kept him alive and unbruised for thirty-four years.

Upstairs, a light burned in the servants' garret where it should not have. Prewitt, no doubt, packing. Or —

No. The light moved. Crossed the attic floor and paused, and a shadow blocked it briefly: small, slight, feminine in outline. Someone was walking about in the sealed east garret, the room Verity had ordered locked in September after the trouble at Holloway, the room Oscar had been told contained broken furniture and nothing else.

He rose slowly from the running board.

The shadow moved again, deliberate, unhurried — and then stopped, directly at the garret's one small window, and Oscar had the distinct, vertiginous certainty that whoever stood behind the glass was looking down at him, at the car, at the spare tyre he had spent the evening loading with a dead woman's testimony.

The light went out.

Oscar stood in the rain-soaked mews with his heart going like a piston, and understood that the house he thought he knew — its locked rooms, its silences, its careful arrangements — had one more secret than the ones he'd stolen.

Someone else was hiding in Lady Verity Ashcombe's house. And by Thursday's dawn, he might have to decide whether to trust them with his life.
