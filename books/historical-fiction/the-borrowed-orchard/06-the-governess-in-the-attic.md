## 6. The Governess in the Attic

The fever took Crane in the small hours of Wednesday morning, and it did not take him gently.

Oscar heard him through the wall of Mrs. Prewitt's linen room — where they had laid him on a cot between the sheets and the mangles — moaning in a register that had nothing to do with pain and everything to do with fear. Not the cry of a man whose body hurt him. The cry of a man running somewhere in his sleep and never arriving.

Mrs. Prewitt was already there when Oscar came down with the lamp, her grey hair plaited for bed, her sleeves rolled as though she had been expecting to fight a battle all evening and had only just been told which one.

"He's burning," she said, before he could ask. "And he won't take water unless I tell him it's from Edith Marchmont's own well."

"Does he say anything?"

"Names. Streets. A woman's name over and over." She wrung out a cloth in the basin. Her hands were steady; her voice was not. "Not hers. Someone else's. Someone called Nell."

Oscar held the lamp higher. In its light Crane looked less like a man than like the drawing of one, all angles and hatching, the gauntness of him carved deeper by sweat. His lips moved. Oscar bent close enough to smell the fever coming off him, sour and metallic.

"—told her the coal cellar," Crane whispered. "I *told* her the coal cellar, nobody looks in a coal cellar—"

Then his hand shot up and seized Oscar's wrist with a strength that belonged to some other man entirely, some healthier ghost inhabiting this ruined frame, and his eyes opened — not seeing Oscar, seeing through him, seeing two years ago.

"They're at the gate," he said clearly. "They're always at the gate. You have to go round by the wall."

And then he shuddered once, all down his length, and slept — truly slept — and Oscar stood there with his own pulse hammering and the cloth going cold in Mrs. Prewitt's hands.

"The gate," she repeated quietly. "Whose gate?"

"I don't know." Oscar set the lamp down on the shelf beside the camphor and the mothballs, among the ordinary furniture of an ordinary house, and felt how thin that ordinariness had become. "But I think we'd better find out what else he knows before anyone else does."

* * *

He came upstairs past two o'clock, meaning only to look in on Lady Verity and report — she had refused to sleep, had installed herself in the morning room with the letters spread before her like a general studying a map of a country she'd thought long lost — but he did not reach the morning room.

Because the door to the attic stair was open.

It was never open. It was kept locked; Mrs. Prewitt kept the key on the same ring as the still-room key, and Oscar had driven this house for four years and had never once seen that door ajar. Now it stood open onto darkness, and from the darkness above came a sound so small and so deliberate that he knew instantly it was meant to be heard: a floorboard, creaking. Once. Twice. The creak of someone deciding whether to run or stay.

Oscar stood very still. The lamp was downstairs. The corridor was black except for the grey line of window at its end, and against that grey line he could make out the shape of the attic stair rising into shadow, and — at the top of it, barely — the outline of a figure. Small. Still. Watching him watch her.

"You're the driver," said a voice. Young. Low, pitched to carry no further than the landing. "The one who came back late Tuesday with a stranger in the car."

"And you," Oscar said, "are not Lady Ashcombe's governess."

A pause. Then, unexpectedly, a breath of laughter — brief, dry, gone almost before it arrived. "No. Miss Fenwick, governess, gave notice in March. Very tearful. Very respectable. I've been Nell Barrow since the second week of April, and before that I was three other people, none of whom you'd have liked much either."

She came down two steps, into the grey. She was perhaps twenty-three, narrow-built, with cropped hair tucked under a cap that fooled no one in this light and plainly wasn't meant to — and she had come down because she had decided something, and the speed of her deciding showed in her face. She had the eyes of someone who counted exits as a habit of mind.

"You've been living in my employer's attic for five months," Oscar said.

"Eight weeks. Before that I was in the still-room loft, until Mrs. Prewitt found me stealing apples and decided, being Mrs. Prewitt, that the correct response was to feed me properly and tell absolutely no one." The girl shrugged. "Your housekeeper has a talent for finding fugitives and a stronger talent for keeping them. You didn't know?"

"I read other people's letters," Oscar said. "Apparently I don't read my own house."

That laugh again, quieter. Then it left her face altogether, and she descended the last steps and stood before him, and up close he saw what the dark had hidden: a thin white scar at the corner of her mouth, dragging it slightly downward, the kind of mark left by something forced between the teeth.

"You've got the Marchmont letters downstairs," she said. "I heard them talking. Verity reads aloud when she's working — she thinks no one listens." Her chin came up. "I need to see them. Tonight."

"Why?"

"Because if Tobias Crane carried them here, then the men hunting Crane will follow them here, and when those men come through that door, every scrap of paper in this house becomes evidence. And evidence can be taken away." Her voice didn't rise at all; that was the frightening thing about it. It simply went on, level as a spirit gauge. "Or it can be made safe. But someone has to know how. Do you?"

Oscar thought of the bundle upstairs in the morning room, sitting in lamplight on a lady's writing table, sealed with a dead woman's hand. He thought of himself, four years a spectator, expert in everything, actor in nothing.

"No," he admitted.

"Right," said Nell Barrow. "Come on, then. I'll show you."

* * *

Lady Verity met them in the corridor outside the morning room, dressing gown over her nightclothes, candle in hand, and she did not ask who the stranger was. She looked at Nell for a long moment — at the cropped hair, the scar, the eyes that counted exits — and then stepped aside and held the door.

"Mrs. Prewitt mentioned she'd taken in a governess who was afraid of dogs," Verity said dryly. "It occurred to me at the time that most people are afraid of dogs, and few of them require a room with no windows."

"I required a room with one staircase," Nell said, "and a landlady who doesn't gossip. Everything else was decoration."

In the morning room the letters lay where Verity had left them, weighted at the corners with the salt cellars she had requisitioned from the dining room, and Nell went to them the way a doctor goes to a wound — no ceremony, no hesitation. She drew the lamp close, turned the topmost letter toward the light, and studied the seal.

"Crane's work?" she asked.

"Edith's own," Verity said. "Sealed before—" She stopped.

"Before she died," Nell finished, flat. "Officially." She tilted the letter. "This wax is good. But the fold underneath has been opened and re-folded twice — he read them on the road, poor devil, checking they were intact. Look at the crease memory here, and here." She glanced up. "If any official opens these, even carefully, he'll see the paper remembers being handled. Paper always remembers. That's the whole trouble with paper."

"That," said Oscar slowly, "is why Crane brought them by hand instead of post."

"It's why anyone brings anything dangerous by hand." Nell was already unpinning her sleeves, rolling them back with brisk economy, turning the morning room into a workshop with three movements. "Now. What do we have for re-sealing? Wax, obviously. A candle. A knife — not that one, a thin one. And something smooth and slightly curved to press the wax with. A spoon works, if you warm the bowl first and don't rush it."

Verity fetched her seal ring — the Ashcombe crest, which she wore, Oscar realized, precisely because no one expected a suffragist patron to seal letters with a baronial griffin — and Nell shook her head.

"Too identifiable. We want boring. Boring survives checkpoints." She looked around the room, and her gaze snagged on the mantelpiece, on the little brass bell Verity rang when she wanted the carriage. "That. Turned face-down. A plain disc makes a plain seal, and a plain seal says *merchant's invoice* or *dress pattern*, and dress patterns get waved through everywhere in England."

It was nearly three o'clock when the work began in earnest, and Oscar would remember the texture of those hours for the rest of his life: the candle flame steadied against draughts, the wax pooling blood-red on the folded paper, Nell's fingers quick and certain as a surgeon's, the spoon-bowl pressing down with a soft hiss. She talked while she worked, in a low continuous stream that was half instruction and half something else — testimony, almost, offered without being asked for.

"Holloway taught me seals. They teach you everything backward, prison. I learned locks because I was locked in, and seals because I smuggled notes out in other women's laundry, and laundry must look untouched or the wardresses burn it. I learned to copy Edith's hand too — she had a loop on her *d* like nobody else, a sort of kick in it. You'll see it in every letter there." A pause; the spoon pressed; the wax set. "I was in the next cell to her for four months. I heard them force-feed her through the nose tube eleven times. I heard her count aloud while they did it, so the others wouldn't hear her cry. She counted primes. Two, three, five, seven. She said primes were numbers that refused to be divided."

The room was very quiet. Even the fire had burned down to embers.

"She didn't die," Verity said at last, and her voice was not steady now, and she did not try to make it so. "The certificate says she died. Sir Roderick Vane signed it. And she is alive, and writing to us from God knows where, asking us to spend her life buying the truth a printing."

"Then the truth had better be printed," Nell said, setting the last re-sealed letter on the pile and sitting back on her heels, "before Friday. Which gives us—" she glanced at the mantel clock— "a shade under sixty hours, minus whatever time it takes to move these north without anyone noticing a dead woman's handwriting crossing three counties."

Oscar heard himself say it before he had decided to say anything at all.

"I'll drive them."

Both women looked at him. He looked at the letters. His heart was going hard and fast, but his voice, when it came again, was surprisingly level — the voice, he realized with a strange inward jolt, of a man doing something rather than watching it.

"I drive all day, every day. I know the roads north better than the men who'd stop me. Nobody searches a chauffeur; servants are furniture, that's the one advantage of being furniture. And—" he hesitated, then finished honestly— "I have spent four years knowing things and doing nothing. I'd like to find out what the opposite feels like."

Nell studied him for a moment with those exit-counting eyes, and whatever she found there, she didn't laugh at it.

"Furniture," she said, "that drives."

Something passed among the three of them then, around that littered table at three in the morning — something Oscar had no name for yet, though he would later. It was not quite a plan and not quite an oath. It was the particular silence of people who have stopped being spectators together.

* * *

Near dawn, Crane surfaced from the fever enough to drink broth, and with the broth came words — broken, but coherent now, the delirium receding like a tide leaving wreckage behind. Mrs. Prewitt propped him upright; Oscar sat on the edge of the cot; Verity stood at the door with her arms folded, refusing to be anywhere else.

"Prison," Crane said, when Oscar asked him plainly how he had come to carry the letters. "Not as a convict. As the coal man. Holloway takes deliveries at dawn, and dawn men are invisible — I was invisible for six months, learning the yard walls, learning which wardress drank and which prayed. Edith Marchmont planned her own escape, madam. Everyone thinks some gallant gentleman arranged it." A wet, rasping laugh. "It was a dying woman in the infirmary wing who arranged it. She wanted out long enough to write the truth down before Vane's people buried her a second time."

"You got her over the wall," Verity said.

"I got her into a coal cart. I got her to a safe house in Camden. And then—" his face worked; the fever climbed back into it— "then they nearly had us at the canal bridge. There was a girl helped us across, a laundress, she held the sentry's lantern and walked it away down the towpath so we could cross in the dark." His hand closed on the blanket. "They caught her. They caught her and I ran, I ran with the letters because that was the mission, and I have been running ever since, and her name was—"

He stopped. His jaw trembled. When he spoke again it was barely above a whisper.

"Her name was Nell."

The silence lasted three full seconds.

Then the door opened and Nell Barrow came in with an armful of clean bandages she had no medical reason to be carrying, and stopped dead, and the bandages slipped from her arms to the floor, and neither she nor Crane moved at all.

"You held the lantern," Crane said hoarsely. "They said— I heard they put you in—"

"They put me in a cell," Nell said. Her voice was very quiet and very controlled, the control of someone holding something heavy at arm's length. "For six weeks. Then they released me, because a laundress isn't worth a trial, and they assumed terror would keep me quiet." She looked around the little room — at Crane, at Verity, at Oscar, at Mrs. Prewitt in the doorway behind — and something in her face let go, just slightly, like a knot loosening a single turn. "It did, for a while. Then Mrs. Prewitt fed me apples, and apparently I'm done being quiet."

Crane was weeping — silently, horribly, the way men weep when they have been alone with a thing too long — and Nell crossed the floor and picked up the bandages, and sat down on the edge of the cot beside him, and began, with great practicality, to wipe his face.

"Eat your broth," she said. "You're not forgiven yet. Forgiveness costs more than a lantern. But you're among friends, and the letters are sealed, and we have fifty-nine hours."

It was Mrs. Prewitt who broke the spell, arriving in the doorway with the poker in one hand and an expression of profound displeasure.

"Beg pardon, my lady," she said, "but there's a hansom cab stopped at the corner of the mews. Been there twenty minutes. Two gentlemen in it, not moving, not getting out." She paused, and her chin lifted. "Watching the house."

Oscar rose. Through the linen room's small high window, past the angle of the wall, he could see it: the dark shape of the cab, motionless in the blue pre-dawn, and the two shapes inside it, patient as stones.

Sixty hours, he thought. They hadn't even waited for breakfast.
