## 7. Vane's Men Call

The knock came at eleven minutes past four on a grey Thursday, and Oscar knew before he opened the door that it was trouble, because nobody knocked at the front of Ashcombe House at eleven minutes past four. Tradesmen came to the mews. Visitors came by appointment, announced by telephone and card. Only the police came to the front door unannounced in the middle of a working afternoon, and only the police stood with their hats held just so, their weight balanced on both feet like men who had learned that patience was a weapon.

There were two of them. The taller one had a face like a folded newspaper — everything creased and nothing legible. The shorter one was younger, pink-cheeked, with the kind of eyes that went everywhere at once and pretended to go nowhere at all. Neither wore uniform. Neither needed to. Plainclothes announced itself the way expensive cologne did: you couldn't name it, but you smelled it.

"Good afternoon," said the tall one. "Mr. Delacroix? Chauffeur to Lady Ashcombe?"

"That's me." Oscar kept his hand on the door, not barring it, not welcoming — merely present, the way a piece of furniture is present.

"Detective Sergeant Hobbes. This is Detective Constable Lyle. Metropolitan Police, Special Branch. Might we have a word?"

"About what?"

"A routine inquiry." The word *routine* arrived with a small smile attached, the way a bill arrives with a compliment attached. "Nothing for you to concern yourself over."

*Everything about this concerns me*, Oscar thought, and heard his own heartbeat, steady as an engine at idle. He stepped back. "I'm only the chauffeur. But I suppose there's no harm in a word."

He led them into the morning room — not the drawing room; the drawing room was Verity's territory and her letters were in it — and asked Mrs. Prewitt for tea, which she would bring and which nobody would drink. It was what houses did when police came. Houses performed normalcy the way actors performed grief: loudly, and hoping no one looked too closely.

Upstairs, behind two closed doors and a locked cedar chest, Tobias Crane lay burning with fever, and Nell sat with him, memorising a dead woman's testimony line by line into the archive of her body. Somewhere in this house were papers that could end a career, topple a ministry's under-secretary, and get every person under this roof transported or worse. And here Oscar stood, pouring sherry nobody wanted, being *only the chauffeur*.

He discovered, in that moment, that he had been rehearsing for this his entire life. Every letter he had steamed open, every secret he had read and never acted upon, every confidence he had carried silently through years of other people's crises — all of it had been practice for the single most important performance of his career: standing in a room with two policemen and being boring.

"So," said Hobbes, settling onto the settee without being asked. He produced a notebook but didn't open it. The notebook was furniture, like Oscar was furniture. Everyone in the room was pretending to be something they weren't. There was a strange comfort in that — a kind of equality. "You've been in Lady Ashcombe's service long?"

"Six years."

"And before that?"

"Delivery work. Grocer's cart, then a bakery van in Camden."

"Local lad, then?"

"Bethnal Green born." True. All of it true. He understood now why liars loved truth: truth was load-bearing. Build your lie out of true bricks and no inspector could pull one loose without bringing down the whole wall, including himself.

Lyle, the young one, drifted toward the window with elaborate casualness, looking out at the square. Oscar watched him watch the street and thought: *they've posted someone outside already, or they will tonight.* The house had been under observation for weeks — Verity said so herself — but observation was one thing. A visit was another. Observation was weather. A visit was frost.

"We're looking for a man," Hobbes said. "Might have passed through this neighbourhood recently. Tall fellow, forty-odd, gaunt. Been ill, we understand."

"What sort of man?"

Hobbes looked up from the notebook he still hadn't opened. For half a second the folded-newspaper face unfolded, and underneath it was something quick and hungry. Then it folded again. "A convict, Mr. Delacroix. Escaped custody some time ago. Convicted woman, actually — though she'd have help, wouldn't she, moving about? Men who carry things for people. Runners. Couriers." He let the words sit in the room like smoke. "You'd know if a stranger came round. Servants always know. Servants see everything."

*I do*, Oscar thought. *That's rather my whole profession.*

"There was a man came to the mews last week," Oscar said, and felt the thrill go through him like cold water — because it was true, and because he was choosing how much of the truth to give, and the choice itself was intoxicating. "Looking for charity. Lady Ashcombe gives to the prison funds, you see. Wives and children of inmates, that sort of thing. They come round."

"This fellow — describe him."

"Tallish. Dark coat, wet through. Spoke like the north." Also true. Crane spoke like the north; Oscar had merely declined to specify which north, or that he had spoken while collapsing into a fever at the service entrance.

"Did he leave a name?"

"He asked after the lady's secretary. I told him the household wasn't hiring and sent him off." A pause, measured to the length of a man remembering something trivial. "He had a cough."

Lyle turned from the window. "You gave him nothing, then. No food, no shelter?"

"I gave him sixpence and directions to the Salvation Army shelter on—" Oscar named a street three miles away, correctly, precisely, and watched the precision do its work. Liars who were vague got caught. Liars who were exact became authorities on their own fiction. He was becoming, in real time, an authority.

Hobbes wrote something. Or pretended to. "And Lady Ashcombe — she receives a great deal of correspondence, I imagine. From the movement."

"She gets bags of it. Suffragists, anti-suffragists, cranks, collectors, one gentleman who sends her pressed violets every week and signs himself 'A Well-Wisher.'" He shrugged. "Mrs. Prewitt sorts it. I only drive."

"You don't read her post?"

"No, sir." And here was the summit of the climb, the moment the whole structure stood or fell: Oscar looked Detective Sergeant Hobbes directly in his folded face and lied — flatly, cleanly, without a tremor — about the single habit that had defined his entire adult life. Six years of steaming kettles and lifted flaps and reading other people's lives by candlelight, and he said *no, sir* the way a churchgoer says *amen*: automatically, sincerely, with total conviction.

Something happened inside him as he said it. He had expected shame to arrive like a slap. Instead it arrived like a debt — distant, deferred, accruing interest. And alongside it, unmistakable, appallingly sweet: pleasure. The pure mechanical pleasure of competence. He could feel the lie leaving him well-made, well-balanced, roadworthy. It was literacy. That was the terrible discovery of that afternoon. All those years of reading — letters, faces, silences, the grammar of households — had taught him a language, and the language worked in every direction, including against the people who trusted him with their mail. Reading was power. He had always known that. He had simply never let himself know that writing was the same alphabet run backwards.

Mrs. Prewitt brought the tea. She poured it with the serene hostility of a woman who had survived three employers' scandals and considered detectives a lower form of life than fishmongers. Her hands did not shake. Oscar noted this and drew strength from it: the house was performing, and performing well.

"The convict we're seeking," Hobbes said, stirring tea he hadn't asked for, "was convicted under the Cat and Mouse Act. You'll have read about it. Hunger strikes. Temporary release. Rearrest." He smiled without warmth. "This one didn't come back for rearrest. Officially she's—"

"Dead?" said Oscar. "Yes. I read about it. Edith Marchmont. It was in the papers." He let a decent interval pass. "Sad business. The force-feeding, they say. Terrible thing, done to women."

"Terrible thing," Hobbes agreed, watching him. Watching him the way a man watches a gauge. "Some people say the record's not straight on it. Some people say there are documents."

"People say all sorts."

"They do." Hobbes closed the notebook at last — opened it, that is, for the first time, and wrote one line with great care, as though transcribing scripture. Then he stood. "Well, Mr. Delacroix. You've been very helpful."

"I haven't helped at all. I don't know anything."

"No," Hobbes agreed pleasantly. "Nobody ever does." At the door he paused, half-turned, and delivered it lightly, almost kindly: "If a gaunt fellow does come round again — coughing, northern — you'll send word to Scotland Yard directly. There's a reward in it, for reliable men."

"I'll bear it in mind."

"One more thing." The folded face unfolded fully now, and what lived beneath it was patient and vast and entirely without malice, which made it worse. "The car. The Ashcombe motor. It goes north sometimes, doesn't it? We keep records of such things. Long-distance motors. Roads being what they are these days — checkpoints, inspections. For everyone's safety." He replaced his hat. "Safe travels, Mr. Delacroix."

Then they were gone, and the door clicked shut behind them, and Oscar stood in the morning room among the untouched cups with his heart going like a hammer on sheet metal.

* * *

He found them all in the kitchen: Verity, Mrs. Prewitt, Nell come halfway down the attic stairs with Crane's fever still audible above them as a thin, cracked muttering. They looked at him the way sailors look at the horizon.

"Well?" said Verity.

"They're looking for a missing convict. They asked about strangers, about your post, about whether I read it." He heard his own voice come out level and was faintly disgusted by how level it was. "I told them about a beggar who came for charity. I gave them a cough and a wrong address and sixpence."

"You lied," Nell said. Not accusing. Weighing.

"For the first time in my life. Properly, I mean. Seamlessly." He laughed — one note, unamused. "Do you know what it felt like? It felt like driving at night with the lamps lit. Everything ahead of you suddenly visible and going past very fast."

Verity sat down slowly at the scrubbed table. Up close he could see what the surveillance and the fear had done to her — the fine tremor in her hands she disguised by folding them. "They mentioned roads. Checkpoints."

"They mentioned them the way you'd mention the price of coal. Casually. Which means they're not casual at all. They'll have notices circulated — the car's registration, likely my description. By tomorrow, possibly tonight." He looked around the kitchen, at the cedar-chest letters somewhere overhead, at the fever-muttering behind the ceiling. "We can't wait for Friday to come to us. We have to start north immediately. Before dawn, before they've finished filing whatever Hobbes wrote in that little book."

Silence. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere above, Crane said a name over and over — a woman's name, not Edith's — and stopped.

"They'll be watching the mews by morning," Mrs. Prewitt said practically. "Which means whoever leaves must already be gone when the watching starts."

Nell had gone very still. Oscar realised she was looking at him — measuring him, the way he had spent six years measuring other people. Whatever she found, she kept to herself. But when she spoke, it was decided. "Then we leave tonight," she said. "And you'll need a sister."

Oscar looked at her. "What?"

"You'll need a sister," Nell repeated, "because a lone man driving north with a fevered fugitive's testimony sewn into his coat is a story with one ending. But a man driving his invalid sister to Manchester for treatment — a man with papers, a purpose, a family resemblance sitting beside him—" she touched her own cheek briefly, and indeed, put side by side they might have passed for kin, both dark-haired, both hollow-eyed that season, "—that's a different story entirely. I can play a part. I played one for two years in a house in Hampstead under another woman's name." She paused, and something moved behind her eyes that was not quite a smile. "It seems lying runs in the family we're about to invent."

Verity stood. When she spoke, her voice had the ring it must have had on platforms, before Parliament and prison broke it down to whispers. "Then it's settled. Tonight. Oscar — pack the letters where a search won't find them, and pack yourself something warm, because the roads north in November are cruel." She gripped his arm, hard, once. "You understand what you're carrying."

For a moment Oscar was back in his old life — the spectator's seat, the warm dark of the wings — and he felt its whole architecture collapse quietly around him, beam by comfortable beam.

"Yes," he said, and found that it was true. "I'm carrying everyone."

Above them, delirious, Tobias Crane began to laugh.
