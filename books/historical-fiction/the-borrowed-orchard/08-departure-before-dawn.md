## 8. Departure Before Dawn

I had always believed that the smallest hours of the morning belonged to other people — to milkmen and bakers and the men who swept the crossings before the city woke to judge them. I had driven through those hours for years without once belonging to them. At ten minutes past three on a Thursday morning in October, standing in Lady Verity Ashcombe's mews with a bundle of dead woman's letters in my coat and a hymnal under my arm, I belonged to them entirely.

The rain had stopped an hour before, but the cobbles still held it, and the single gas lamp at the mouth of the mews turned every puddle into a small bronze mirror. The Daimler sat in its stall with its lamps dark, breathing cold like a horse waiting to be saddled. I had wheeled it out myself rather than ring for anyone. Some things a man should do with his own hands if he means to answer for them.

Nell came down the outside stairs from the attic with her collar up and her hair pinned beneath a cloche hat that Mrs. Prewitt swore was fashionable in 1909 and would pass for provincial now, which was the point. She looked smaller than she had at the kitchen table. She also looked, I thought, harder to knock over.

"You'll want to check the tyres," she said.

"I've checked the tyres."

"Check them again."

There was no arguing with a woman who had spent two years learning not to be seen. I fetched the lamp from the stall hook and knelt at the rear offside wheel, where the spare rode in its casing behind the bodywork, and I will admit my hands were not steady. Not from fear, or not only from fear. From the strangeness of it — that I, Oscar Delacroix, who had prided himself all his adult life on touching nothing, was about to commit treason with a tyre lever.

The casing was the old pattern, a leather-and-canvas boot that laced over the spare wheel like a corset. Crane, feverish but lucid in his odd intervals, had shown me the trick of it: unpick the rear seam, lay the letters flat against the inner canvas, restitch with waxed thread. It had taken me two hours by candlelight and cost me a needle through the meat of my left thumb, which throbbed now as I worked the laces loose. The letters were there. Eleven pages of Edith Marchmont's hand, folded into quarters, lying against the rubber of the wheel as snugly as if they had been born there.

"Still there," I said.

"Then do it up before your hands remember how to shake properly."

I laced the casing shut and stood, and my knee cracked loud enough to startle a cat off the wall opposite. Nell almost smiled. Almost. She had a way of letting you see the smile begin and then banking it, like a fire raked over for the night.

"And the others?" she asked.

I held up the hymnal. *Hymns Ancient and Modern*, bound in black cloth, its spine sun-faded, its corners soft with handling. It had come out of the servants' hall bookshelf and no one had mourned it. Inside, along the gutter where the signatures met the spine, I had slit the paper with a razor and fed four more leaves of Edith's testimony into the gap, then glued the seam with flour paste and pressed the book overnight between two iron pans. To look at it, it was only what it appeared: a hymnal gone grey with use. Open it, and the pages sang of Jerusalem and the sweet bye-and-bye. The words that mattered were locked in the binding, silent as seeds.

"Four pages in the book," I said. "Seven in the wheel. And one—" I touched my breast pocket, where the last letter, the shortest and worst of them, lay against my heart. "This one stays on my person. If they take everything else, they don't take this."

"Vane's name is in the pocket letter," Nell said.

"That's why it stays close. Paper can be burned. I can't be searched past my own ribs." I said it and heard how it sounded — brave, rehearsed, a line learned from a novel. Nell looked at me a moment too long, and I felt the line go thin as wire.

* * *

Verity was waiting in the morning room, which she had chosen, I think, because it was the one room in the house whose windows did not face the street. A single candle burned on the desk among the drifts of her correspondence — the real correspondence now, the suffrage committees and the parliamentary allies and the American journalists who wrote asking careful questions. Two years of surveillance had taught her to work in pools of light small enough to shade from a window.

She rose when we came in, and I saw that she had dressed properly — walking skirt, jacket, the brooch at her lapel worked in the purple-white-green of the cause. Not because she was going anywhere. Because she wanted us to see her standing upright when she said what she had to say.

"You understand what I cannot do," she began.

"Yes, my lady."

"Don't *my lady* me at three in the morning, Oscar, I haven't the patience for it." But there was no edge in it; there never quite was, with her. She came around the desk and stood close, and lowered her voice though the nearest listener was a floor away. "There is a man across the square who has sold chestnuts from the same barrow for eleven years. Since Tuesday he has been selling them very badly, and reading a newspaper he holds upside down. They are watching this house. That is why it must be you. That is why it must be tonight, before they decide the house itself is worth searching rather than merely watching."

"The letters will reach Manchester," I said.

"They had better," she said, "or the next woman they lock up will die in there and be buried under a stranger's name, and none of us will ever know enough to be ashamed." She looked at me then in the candlelight, and I saw something in her face I had never seen in nine years of driving her — she was frightened, and she was letting me see it, because she judged I had earned the sight. "Oscar. You have been the quietest man I have ever employed. I want you to understand that I am asking you to stop being quiet, permanently, and that there is no wage I can attach to that."

"There's no wage attached to most of what matters," I said. "You taught me that by accident, reading your post aloud at breakfast."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. "You weren't meant to hear half of those letters."

"No," I agreed.

For a moment neither of us said anything, and the candle flame leaned and righted itself, and somewhere out in the dark the chestnut man's brazier would be cooling to ash. Then Verity took both my hands in hers — hers were cold and dry as good paper — and said, "Bring yourself back. The letters matter. The truth matters. But bring yourself back, do you hear me? I have buried enough friends this decade. I am not burying my chauffeur."

"I'm nobody's friend, my lady. I read their mail."

"You're everybody's friend," she said, "which is why you never noticed." She released my hands and turned to Nell, and her voice firmed into the committee voice, the one that could carry across a Albert Hall aisle. "Miss Nell. You know the route?"

"North through Hertfordshire on the Great North Road. First checkpoint at Stevenage, second beyond Newark, third the railway inspection at Manchester London Road. We avoid stations until the last. He drives; I talk."

"He drives and you talk," Verity repeated. "God send you both the gift of boring conversation." She touched Nell's cheek, briefly, the way one touches something one expects to lose. Then, briskly, before feeling could ruin the moment: "Mrs. Prewitt has packed ham and a spirit stove. The ham is for you. The stove is for the ham. Do not attempt tea on the move; it ends in scalding."

* * *

We went down through the sleeping house. Mrs. Prewitt stood at the service door with a basket and an expression like a drawn blind — nothing readable in it except the white-knuckled grip she kept on the handle until both our hands had closed on the basket's, at which point she said, "Mind the ham," in a voice that shook exactly once, and shut the door.

The mews was empty. The bronze puddles had gone to pewter as the sky behind the rooftops began, very faintly, to consider itself. I cranked the Daimler alive and the engine caught on the second swing and settled into its low idle, the loudest thing in a listening city. I checked my watch against the stable clock. Half past three. Crane's forged papers were in the map case on the dashboard — motor carriage permits in the name of Delacroix, made plausible by a stamp Crane had owned since some earlier life, and a railway warrant for "Miss Delacroix, accompanying her brother north on family business." Family business. It had a fine solid dullness to it. Nobody asks questions about family business; they only offer condolences.

Nell climbed into the passenger seat and arranged herself with her hands folded and her chin level, and I watched her become someone else in the space of ten seconds — not the governess from the attic, not the Holloway survivor with the burn-scarred throat she hid under her collar, but a respectable young woman of no interest whatsoever, tired, mildly cross about the hour, thinking about an aunt.

"Aunt Agatha," she said, catching me looking. "Dying. Slowly. In Harrogate. We are driving north to arrange her affairs, and I find the whole business tedious and would rather not discuss it."

"Is she dying slowly?"

"She's invented. Everything else is true." She pulled her gloves tighter. "Drive, brother."

I let in the clutch and we rolled out of the mews into streets still wet and empty as riverbeds. Past the shuttered greengrocer. Past the public house with last night's chalked prices ghosting on its board. Past the chestnut barrow, where the bad newspaper reader had finally gone home and left the square to a single policeman who examined the sky with the expression of a man who has been awake too long to believe in anything. He did not look at us. Why should he? A motor car at dawn, a driver in uniform cap and goggles, a young woman beside him. London was full of such pictures, and none of them had ever contained me before.

At the Euston Road I turned north, and the city fell away behind us in stages — terraces giving way to villas, villas to hedgerows, gaslight to a grey that was almost, if you were generous, light. Nell did not speak for the first twenty miles. Neither did I. There is a silence that is absence and a silence that is presence, and ours was the second kind: two people in one machine, carrying the same cargo, each privately rehearsing catastrophe.

It was she who broke it, just past Hatfield, when the sun managed one thin stripe through the cloud.

"How long have you had the letters' weight on you?" she asked. "Since Tuesday. Three days. How does it sit?"

"Like a stone in the boot of a car," I said. "You feel it in every corner."

"It gets heavier," she said. "Or you get stronger. I've never determined which."

The Great North Road unspooled ahead of us, lined with elms going gold, and the hedges ran with late rosehips like drops of blood someone had flicked from a brush. A cart of turnips. A boy leading a pony. A world going about its Thursday as if the Home Office did not contain Sir Roderick Vane and the machinery he served. I found the ordinary-ness of it obscene and comforting in equal measure, which I suspect is the ordinary condition of the guilty.

Stevenage announced itself as a church spire and a smell of woodsmoke, and then as a barrier.

I saw it before Nell did — a trestle laid across the carriageway just short of the bridge, a lantern hung from it despite the daylight, and beside it a police bicycle propped with the studied carelessness of its owner. Two constables. One was talking to a farmer whose cartload of pigs was expressing opinions. The other stood at the barrier itself, a sergeant by his stripes, a broad comfortable man built along the lines of a sack of meal, who watched our approach with the keen interest of a man watching weather.

My heart went up into my throat and sat there like a swallowed egg.

"Easy," Nell murmured. "Bored men wave cars through. Boredom is the eighth sacrament of the English constabulary. Give him nothing interesting."

I slowed the Daimler to a walk. Up close the sergeant resolved into fifty-ish, red-faced, with the moustache of a man who had made peace with it decades ago. He raised one palm — not commanding, merely administrative — and leaned down to my window, and his eyes went over the bonnet, the plates, the windscreen, with the thoroughness of a man checking whether it might rain.

"Morning," he said.

"Morning, Sergeant."

"Where'd you spring from at this hour?"

"London. We're for the North — family business." I let the phrase sit there, dull as a paving stone. "An aunt, poorly."

"Ah." The word carried a whole funeral in it. He glanced past me at Nell. "Sister, is it?"

"Brother," Nell said, in a voice pitched precisely between patience and grief. "Our aunt is at Harrogate and the doctors give her weeks, so we're making what speed we can, Sergeant, if you'll permit it." Her gloved hands tightened once in her lap and relaxed. It was, I thought, the finest acting I had ever seen, and I had spent years watching people lie in letters for a living.

The sergeant straightened and surveyed the road ahead as though it might contain further aunts in need of condolence. "Everyone's got somebody poorly this month," he said, mostly to himself. Then, to me, with a jerk of his thumb: "On you go, sir. Mind the bridge arch — cart clipped it Tuesday and there's loose brick."

He waved us through. Actually waved — a rolling gesture, hospitable, a man ushering guests to a wedding breakfast. The trestle scraped past my wing. The pigs commented. And we were through, and accelerating, and the checkpoint shrank in the mirror until the lantern on the barrier was a bead of light no bigger than Edith Marchmont's signet ring.

Neither of us spoke for a mile. Then Nell let out a breath she seemed to have been holding since Holloway, and laughed — one short, disbelieving note, quickly smothered, as if laughter too were contraband.

"One," she said.

"One," I said.

The road bent north between the hedges, and the thin sun came fully out at last and lay along the bonnet like a blessing. For perhaps five minutes — I counted them, because I did not trust them — I allowed myself the luxury of believing that the worst was behind us. That the day would stay this shape: long, dull, survivable, ending in Manchester with ink on a compositor's hands and my name still nobody's.

Then Nell, who had been idly watching the mirror, said quietly, "Oscar. Don't turn your head. There's a motorcycle coming up fast behind us, and the man riding it is wearing civilian clothes, and he is reading our number plate."
