## 9. The Road Through Hertfordshire

The sun came up over Hertfordshire the way it always did — indifferent, gilding the hedgerows, laying long shadows across the empty road as though the world had not changed hands overnight. Oscar drove with his cap low and his eyes flicking between the mirror and the horizon, and beside him in the passenger seat Nell sat very straight, wearing Verity's grey travelling coat and the expression of a woman who had decided to be unremarkable and would succeed at it by force of will.

They had been on the road since four. The first checkpoint was two hours behind them, a memory of a bored sergeant and a stamped chit and Oscar's heart hammering so loud he'd been certain the man could hear it over the engine. Now there was only the car, the road, and the strange intimacy of two people who had met yesterday bound together by paper that could hang them.

The Daimler ran sweet. That was one mercy. Oscar had spent eleven years learning engines the way other men learned scripture, and he trusted this machine more than he trusted most people. He let it hum through the lanes of Hertfordshire, past fields where ploughmen were already out, past a church whose clock said seven, past everything ordinary and asleep, carrying in its spare tyre casing and its hymnal enough words to bring down a knight of the realm.

"You drive like you're apologising to the horse," Nell said.

It was the first thing she'd said in an hour. Oscar glanced at her. "There's no horse."

"That's why you're apologising."

He almost smiled. Then he remembered what he was carrying and stopped.

* * *

She made him stop outside Ware, at a common where sheep grazed beyond a rusted fence, because she wanted tea from the thermos Mrs. Prewitt had packed and because, Oscar suspected, she wanted to look at him properly for the first time without the pretext of watching the road. They sat on the running board, side by side, mugs steaming in the cold. A lark went up somewhere and kept going.

"Nell," she said, when he asked again what it stood for. "Eleanor Pargeter. But nobody's used the Eleanor since Holloway, and I don't intend to start again now."

"Holloway." He said it carefully, the way you'd handle something with an edge.

"Two years ago." She blew on her tea. "You'll have heard the stories. Everyone's heard the stories. They're told like ghost stories at suffrage meetings — *and then they held her down, and then they forced the tube, and then she screamed until they put the gag in.* People shiver and pass the collection plate." She took a sip. "The stories are all true and none of them are right. Do you see? It isn't the moment of it. Anyone can endure a moment. It's that it happened every day, and you knew it would happen every day, and you lay awake at night rehearsing tomorrow's fight before it came, and your body learned to dread breakfast more than death."

Oscar looked down into his mug.

"I went on hunger strike in March," she said. "For the vote, they'd say. For the principle. But if you want the truth, I did it because Edith Marchmont had done it, and Edith Marchmont was the only person who ever looked at me in that prison like I was still a person. So when they came for me with the tube — and they came, forty-one times in nineteen days, I counted because counting was the only arithmetic they couldn't take — I thought of her. I thought, *she bore this, so I can bear it, so we are both bearing it together, so it is being borne.*" She turned the mug in her hands. "Then they passed the Cat and Mouse Act and realised they could simply throw me out when I got too near dying and take me back when I healed, like a book returned to a library, and after the third round of it my sister paid my fine without telling me, to end it, and I have never entirely forgiven her either."

"The silence," Oscar said quietly. "Verity said — released into silence."

"They make you sign things. Or they don't make you sign anything; they just make it clear. Your name goes on a list. Employers get letters. Your landlady gets a visit from two pleasant gentlemen who ask about your habits. You can go free, Miss Pargeter, absolutely free — free as air, free as a bird in a room with no door. And every woman who'd been inside learns the same lesson: keep your mouth shut or go back." She looked at him at last, directly, and her eyes were the flat grey of the sky over the common. "So I kept my mouth shut. Eighteen months. I typed other people's letters in a solicitor's office and I watched the movement tear itself apart and I read about women dying in cells and I said nothing, because saying something meant the tube again, meant the cell again, meant my sister crying at my bedside again. And then Tobias Crane knocked on my door three weeks ago with a face like a dead man walking and said Edith Marchmont was alive, and I found out what my mouth was for."

The lark had come down. The sheep moved slowly along the fence line, grazing their way through the morning. Oscar found that his throat was tight and that the tea tasted of nothing.

"I have to tell you something," he said.

"You've been working up to it since Barnet. Go on."

He set the mug down on the gravel because his hand wasn't steady enough to hold it and he didn't want her to see. Then he decided that was cowardice too, and picked it back up.

"For eleven years," he said, "I've driven for Lady Ashcombe. And part of the work — a small part, a private part, a part nobody knows — is that I steam open her letters."

Nell said nothing. She was very good at saying nothing. He understood suddenly that this was what prison taught: how to wait a person out.

"Not all of them. At first just the ones from the movement — I was curious, that's all, curious the way a servant is curious about the lives above stairs, because those lives are the only novel we're allowed to read. But then it became — I don't know what it became. A habit. A vice. I read everything. Correspondence with MPs. Plans for processions. Money matters. Quarrels. Griefs." He heard his own voice going faster, the confession accelerating downhill. "I know who Verity loves and who betrayed her. I know which MPs are cowards and which ones' wives are in the movement against their husbands' knowledge. I have sat in that car for eleven years listening to history happen on the seat behind me, and I have read it happening on paper in my room at night, and in eleven years I did precisely nothing. Not once. Not a note passed, not a warning given, not a letter delivered early. I told myself I was careful. I told myself discretion was my contribution. What I was, Miss Pargeter — what I am — is a spectator who charged admission to himself and called it service."

The wind moved the grass. Somewhere down the lane a cart rattled past, the driver whistling, off toward some ordinary errand in some ordinary life.

"When the letters came about Edith," Oscar said, "I already knew her. Not her — her hand. Her voice on paper. I've read years of her writing. Her jokes. Her anger. The way she underlines things twice when she means them. When Crane showed me that bundle sealed in her hand I felt — " He searched for it. "I felt like a man who has watched a drowning from the bank for years and is suddenly thrown a rope and realises he never once intended to get wet. And I stood there in the rain and I thought, *here it is, the bill for all those evenings with the kettle and the letter-knife,* and I said yes. I volunteered. And I want to be honest with you, because everyone else in that house has bled for this cause and I haven't: I didn't say yes out of courage. I said yes because the vertigo of being asked was better than the hollowness of never being asked. Because for the first time in eleven years I was going to be *in* a story instead of reading it."

He made himself look at her.

"So that's my confession. I read every letter. I knew about Holloway before you went — I read the correspondence planning around it, I read Verity's letters about visiting you, I read the reply you weren't allowed to send and the one you managed anyway. I read about the force-feeding in a warm room with a cup of cocoa, and I felt appropriately horrified, and I slept well. I am complicit the way wallpaper is complicit. Present in the room for every crime, contributing nothing but the pattern."

* * *

Nell finished her tea. She did not answer at once, and Oscar, who had braced for contempt — contempt he could have borne, contempt would have been clean — found the waiting worse than any verdict.

"Do you know what I did with the tube in my mouth?" she said finally. "I sang. In my head. Hymns, mostly — I'm not religious, but the rhythms hold. *Jerusalem* mostly. *Bring me my bow of burning gold.* Forty-one times, Oscar. Nineteen days. And do you know what I thought about, afterwards, in the eighteen months of silence? Not the wardens. Not Vane — though I didn't know his name then, I knew his cologne, he visited the ward twice and wore something that smelled of cloves and money. I thought about the people outside. The ones who knew. The ones who read the same reports you read and drank the same cocoa." She looked at the sheep, not at him. "I hated them more than I hated the wardens. The wardens were the weather. You can't hate weather. But the people on the bank — the spectators — they chose the bank."

"Yes," Oscar said.

"But." She held up a hand, and he stopped. "Here's the other thing I learned in Holloway, and it took me longer to learn than the singing did. There were women in there who'd done nothing. Cooks and seamstresses and a girl of nineteen arrested for heckling, chained to me in the corridor during a search, sobbing with terror. And there were women who'd thrown bricks and cut pictures and burned pillar-boxes and gone back out and done it again. And the ones who survived best — not the bravest, mind, the *best*, meaning the ones who came out still human — were neither. They were the ones who could tell the difference between what they could carry and what they couldn't, and didn't lie to themselves about which was which." She turned to face him fully. "You're telling me you couldn't carry it. Eleven years of watching and you couldn't carry action. All right. That may even be true — most people can't, that's rather the point of tyranny, it runs on most people not being able to. What I can't stomach isn't that you watched. It's that you enjoyed the watching and called it humility. A man who knows he's a coward and hates it is halfway to not being one. A man who knows he's a coward and polishes the fact like a medal — that man I cross the street to avoid."

"I did polish it," Oscar said. His voice came out rough. "I'm not pretending otherwise. I'm telling you because you deserve to know exactly what sort of man is driving you north. If I die on this road it should be for something, and if I run from it you should know that was always in me too."

"And now?"

"Now?" He looked down the long straight road ahead, white in the morning light, running toward the second checkpoint like a sentence toward its full stop. "Now I find I can't go back to the kettle and the letter-knife. Whatever happens Friday — whether the testimony prints or the police burn the press — I've been onstage for three days and the theatre doesn't give refunds. I think that's what Crane meant when he looked at me like that. Like a man recognising another man who's just heard the curtain go up and knows he's in the cast."

Nell studied him for a long moment. Then she stood, brushed the gravel from Verity's coat, and held out her hand for his empty mug.

"I don't forgive you," she said. "Forgiveness is for sins against me, and yours weren't. You were wallpaper in rooms where I was tortured — that's not mine to forgive, it's Edith's, and Verity's, and every woman whose letter you read with your cocoa. But I don't condemn you either, and I'll tell you why." She carried both mugs to the car and set them in the footwell. "Because condemnation is easy, and I've had eighteen months of easy. And because yesterday you lied to a policeman's face without blinking and forged a signature on the chit, and this morning you handed me the truth like it was the last honest coin you had. A man can be wallpaper for eleven years and then decide to be a window. I've seen it happen. It happened to me." She got into the passenger seat and pulled the door shut. "Drive, Mr. Delacroix. We've lost twenty minutes and I'd rather not lose any more at Royston."

* * *

They drove. The road unwound through villages with names out of a primer — Braughing, Barkway, Barley — each one asleep, each one innocent, washing hung out already despite the cold, smoke rising straight from chimneys into windless air. Oscar drove faster now, not quite apologising to the absent horse, and Nell sat with her hands folded and her lips moving faintly, and he realised after a mile that she was reciting.

"What is that?" he asked.

She opened her eyes. "*And did those feet in ancient time.*" A thin smile. "Old habit. You never know when you'll need a hymn."

He laughed — actually laughed, a short surprised sound — and for half a mile the car was full of something that might, in poorer light, have been mistaken for peace.

Then they crested the rise outside Royston and saw the checkpoint.

It was not the first checkpoint. The first had been a sergeant and a sawhorse and a yawn. This was different. This was a proper cordon across the Ermine Street — two constables and a plainclothes man in a bowler hat who stood slightly apart from them in the way plainclothes men do, hands behind his back, watching the traffic come. A queue of three vehicles waited. And pinned to the board beside the barrier, catching the mid-morning sun, was a sheet of paper that gleamed white for half a mile in either direction.

Even at distance, Oscar could see it was a notice. Even at distance, he could see the plainclothes man was not reading the drivers' faces.

He was reading the number plates.

Oscar's hands went steady on the wheel — the strange borrowed calm he'd found at the first checkpoint settling over him like a coat — but his mind was moving fast and cold. Plates. Of course plates. A Daimler registered to Lady Verity Ashcombe of Kensington, driven north out of London the morning after two plainclothes officers called at her house asking questions. He could change the story. He could produce Crane's papers. What he could not do was change six inches of painted metal visible from fifty yards.

Beside him, Nell had gone quiet. Her lips had stopped moving. She looked at the notice-board, then at the plainclothes man — sharp-faced, thirty-five, patient as a cat at a mousehole — and then at Oscar, and something passed between them that needed no words at all.

"He hasn't seen us yet," she murmured. "There's a cart between us and the barrier. Thirty seconds, perhaps less. Tell me you have a story, Mr. Delacroix."

Oscar watched the bowler hat turn, slow as a lighthouse beam, toward the head of the queue.

"Working on it," he said.
