## 10. Checkpoint Two

The road out of Hertfordshire had been kind to them for ninety minutes, and Oscar should have known better than to trust it.

Kindness on a road was a trick of the light — the long green corridors of hedgerow, the drowsy villages with their church spires tilted like hats worn too far back, the occasional cart horse lifting its head as the car passed with that look horses gave motorcars: contempt without surprise. Nell dozed against the door frame in her borrowed sisterhood, Verity's grey travelling coat buttoned to her throat, her hands folded in her lap like a woman dreaming of nothing worse than a slow afternoon. Oscar drove with his elbows loose and his mind running the route ahead like a man counting stairs in the dark.

Then the road bent around a hill at Knebworth's edge, and he saw the barrier.

It was not the first checkpoint they'd met — Sergeant Hobbs outside Stevenage had been the first, a red-faced man more interested in his tea than their papers, who'd waved them through with a grunt and a remark about the weather. But this one announced itself differently. Two motorcycles stood propped by the roadside, engines still ticking. A canvas tent had been raised beside the barrier, and from it ran a wire — an actual telegraph wire, Oscar saw, lashed to a fence post and sagging toward the cottage behind. Lanterns hung unlit on the crossbar. It was barely past noon; nobody expected to work after dark.

A constable stepped into the lane and raised one hand, palm out. Behind him, a second officer stood at a trestle table with papers weighted under stones, and behind *him*, leaning against the tent pole with his arms crossed and his cap pushed back, stood a man who did not look bored at all.

Oscar felt the change before he could name it. The first checkpoint had smelled of rain and woodsmoke and indifference. This one smelled of purpose.

"Nell," he said quietly, not turning his head. "Company."

She came awake the way he was learning she did everything — instantly, completely, without any visible transition between sleeping and attention. Her eyes opened on the barrier and stayed there.

"Inspector?" she murmured.

"Don't know yet."

"The thin one. Against the tent." Her voice was flat, conversational, pitched for no one but him. "He's already looking at the plates."

Oscar looked, because not looking would have been worse. The number plate on the Ashcombe Daimler was painted in house colours, dark green ground with cream characters, and it was distinctive the way everything about Lady Verity Ashcombe was distinctive — a car that appeared in society pages, a car a sharp-eyed man might have seen described in a notice circulated that morning to every constabulary between London and the Trent. *Green Daimler, registration such-and-such, believed carrying contraband correspondence connected to the Marchmont case. Stop and detain occupants.*

He eased off the throttle. The car slowed with a sigh.

"We can turn," Nell said. It wasn't a suggestion or a plea; it was an inventory. She was laying out the options the way she laid out anything — neatly, without sentiment. "There was a lane back half a mile."

"There'll be a motorcycle in it inside four minutes if they've got a telephone at the cottage. And they have a telegraph, which is worse." He kept his voice level and found, to his mild astonishment, that it was level all the way down. Somewhere between Stevenage and here, fear had stopped being a wall and become a floor he could stand on. He did not examine this. He simply used it. "We go through."

"And if he knows the plates?"

"Then we give him something better to know."

* * *

The inspector — and he was an inspector; the moment he pushed off the tent pole and came forward, the constables rearranged themselves around him like iron filings finding a magnet — was perhaps fifty, dry-looking, with a moustache trimmed so precisely it seemed drawn on rather than grown. His eyes were pale and quick and went where he sent them: the plates first, then the windscreen, then Oscar's face, then Nell's, then back to the plates, completing a circuit he had clearly run a hundred times and never once found interesting until now.

"Afternoon," he said. He did not smile. "Lovely machine."

"It gets me from place to place," Oscar said, and heard himself add, easily, "when it's minded to."

The inspector almost smiled. Almost. "Name?"

"Delacroix. Oscar Delacroix. This is my sister, Miss Delacroix." Beside him, Nell produced a small cough — a sisterly cough, faintly put-upon — and looked past the inspector with the weary patience of a woman who had been travelling since dawn and wished very much to stop being spoken about as though she weren't present.

"Papers."

Oscar handed them across. Crane's work. The little smuggler, fevered and shaking in the attic of Verity's house, had spent his last lucible hours over a kitchen table with a pen knife and a bottle of gum arabic, and what he'd produced was good — good enough that Oscar had looked at his own name rendered in another man's hand and felt briefly unreal. *Permit to proceed, issued Hertford Constabulary. Delacroix, O., chauffeur, and companion. Business: private conveyance.* There was even a stamp, inked from a potato cut with a razor blade, blurred just enough to be convincing.

The inspector read them the way he did everything — thoroughly, unhurriedly, letting the silence do its work. Oscar let him. He had learned that much at the first checkpoint and learned it again watching Nell: silence was a currency, and whoever flinched first paid.

"Private conveyance," the inspector said at last. "To where?"

"Manchester."

"That's a long way to be conveying privately."

"My employer's business is my business only as far as the driving of it, sir."

"Mm." The inspector turned a page. Turned it back. His eyes came up. "You came through Stevenage this morning."

It was not a question, and that was the trap in it. Oscar understood the shape of the trap a half-second after understanding it existed: the sergeant at Stevenage would remember a green Daimler, yes — but the inspector's phrasing assumed knowledge Oscar couldn't verify. Agree too readily and he confirmed a story; hesitate and he contradicted one. Either way, the inspector held the thread and was watching to see how Oscar pulled it.

"Yes, sir," said Nell.

Both men looked at her.

"Our aunt," Nell said, and her voice changed. It didn't break — it *thinned*, the way a voice thins when it has been used all day and the day isn't done. "At Retford. We had a telegram last night." She reached into the coat, unhurried, and drew out a folded paper — Crane's forgery of a telegram form, aged with cold tea, folded and refolded so many times it had gone soft as cloth. "My brother drives me. I can't sit in trains, not today. Not—" She stopped. Looked down at the telegram in her lap. Pressed her lips together in exactly the way people do when pressing lips together is the only alternative to something worse.

The constable at the trestle table shifted his weight. Even the one holding the barrier glanced away, embarrassed, the way Englishmen are embarrassed by grief in public — not unsympathetic, merely unequipped.

The inspector did not glance away. He took the telegram when Nell extended it, read it, and read it again, and Oscar watched him do it and understood that the man was not reading the words. He was reading the paper. The fold lines. The tea stain at the corner. The way the ink had feathered where a thumb had passed over it once, twice, many times.

*He's checking our props,* Oscar thought, with a clarity that felt almost like calm. *And we haven't enough rehearsal.*

But Nell had rehearsed, in her way. Not with Crane at the kitchen table — she'd refused to perform for Crane, saying she'd spent enough of her life being watched while other people decided things. She had rehearsed alone, walking the mews at night, mouthing the story to herself, and what she gave the inspector now was not a performance. It was a true thing wearing a false coat: she *had* been force-fed in Holloway; she *had* lain awake nights composing herself out of ruin; and the trembling in her hands as she took the telegram back was not acting. The inspector could not know which parts were real. That was the genius of it. That was the horror of it. Every lie she told was mortared with truth, and the mortar was made of her own life.

"Retford," the inspector said. "That's Nottinghamshire."

"By way of Newark tonight," Oscar said. "We stop where she can stop. She shouldn't be left alone with—"

"I take your meaning." The inspector handed the papers back. Then he did not step aside. He walked slowly along the flank of the car, and Oscar sat very still and listened to his own pulse count the seconds. The inspector paused at the rear. Paused at the spare tyre strapped to the back — the spare tyre whose casing held, wrapped in oilcloth, forty-one pages of testimony in a dead woman's hand, naming names, naming dates, naming Sir Roderick Vane of the Home Office, and the falsified certificate, and the cell in Holloway where a hunger-striker had been broken and buried under another woman's name.

"Full spare," the inspector observed. "For a long run."

"For these roads," Oscar said. "I've had two punctures since Tuesday."

The inspector looked at him. The pale eyes were not unkind, which somehow made them worse. They were the eyes of a man doing a job well, and doing a job well meant not stopping when a kind man would stop.

"Open the boot, please."

* * *

Later — much later, at the inn at Newark, with the lamps lit and the fear finally draining out of his hands — Oscar would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and find that memory had refused the job. What remained were fragments, filed without order:

The boot lid rising. The suitcase on top, Verity's, leather and brass, containing nothing but shirts and a photograph of a dog.

The inspector lifting the suitcase, weighing it, setting it down.

His hand going to the spare tyre casing — resting there — the strap under his fingers —

And Nell, beside the car, saying, in a small clear voice that carried the way a bell carries across water: "Is it the telegram you doubt, Inspector? Because you may wire Retford yourself. Aunt Cissy will tell you herself, if she's awake. She's awake most nights now. The doctors say it's the morphine that does it — she talks and talks, poor thing, about the orchard, about my uncle, about things from fifty years ago, and none of it makes sense, and I sit there and hold her hand because that's all there is to do at the end of a life. Hold the hand. So if you'd like to delay us, Inspector, you may. And when you've finished delaying us, I'll get back in that car and drive to Retford and hold her hand, and you can explain to yourself why you needed to look inside a dying woman's luggage to feel you'd done your duty. I'm sure it's a very good reason. You seem like a conscientious man."

Silence.

A lark somewhere above the hedge, going up and up.

The inspector's hand came off the spare tyre casing.

"Miss Delacroix," he said, and for the first time there was something in his voice besides procedure — something tired, something old, something that might, in a different life, on a different morning, have been an ally. "I had a sister. Took bad at thirty-one. Consumption. I held her hand eleven weeks." He straightened his cap. "Go on, then. And mind the road through Newark — they're resurfacing past the market square. You'll want the north side."

He stepped back. He waved the barrier up.

Oscar let in the clutch. The Daimler rolled forward, gathered itself, and took the road north, and neither of them spoke for a full mile — not because there was nothing to say, but because speech would have admitted that their hearts were going like pistons and their hands were not entirely their own.

It was Nell who broke it, at last, watching the hedgerows stream past.

"He knew."

"What?"

"The plates. He knew the plates — that's why he wanted the boot. He was giving himself a reason to look." She turned from the window. Her face was pale but steady, and there was something in it Oscar hadn't seen before: not fear, not relief. Craft. The look of a woman discovering she was good at the thing that terrified her. "The telegram bought him a way to stand down without standing down. Men like that need permission to be decent. We handed him some." She looked forward again. "Next one won't want permission."

"You don't know that."

"I know what I know," Nell said, and said nothing more.

* * *

They crossed into Nottinghamshire as the light began to fail, gold going to copper going to grey, and the hills folded themselves around the road like hands closing. At a crossroads outside Southwell, Oscar pulled the car into the shadow of a barn and checked the spare tyre casing with fingers that shook now, safely, uselessly, after the danger had passed. The oilcloth was dry. The pages were all there. Forty-one testimonies, riding six inches from an inspector's fingertips, saved by a dying aunt who did not exist and an orchard Nell had invented on the spot — or had she? He thought of what she'd told him yesterday on the road through Hertfordshire, of Holloway, of the tube forced down her throat, of the years of silence afterward. Perhaps the orchard was hers. Perhaps every lie she told was a truth with the serial numbers filed off.

"You were extraordinary," he said.

Nell snorted — an unladylike, wonderful sound. "I was furious. It works nearly as well." She climbed back into the car. "Newark before full dark. We need beds and we need supper, and you need to stop driving like a funeral."

He laughed — actually laughed, a short startled sound — and swung the car back onto the road.

The lights of Newark came up an hour later, low and amber under a sky bruising toward night, market square, church, bridge over the Trent with the river running black beneath. The inn the guidebooks favoured was full; the one Crane's instructions named was not — a low timbered place called the Sign of the Wheat Sheaf, with a yard at the back where Oscar could put the car under cover and a landlady with a face like a closed book.

It was as he carried their single suitcase through the taproom that he felt it: the sensation of being watched, specific and directional, like sunlight through a lens. He did not turn his head. He set the suitcase down, asked for two rooms facing the yard, and let his gaze travel the room as if idly taking it in.

By the fire, a man in a brown suit was reading a newspaper. He held it a little too high. He had not turned a page since Oscar entered.

And when the landlady led them upstairs, Oscar glanced back once from the landing — down into the warm lamplight, at the man in brown folding his untouched newspaper with great care — and saw, on the chair beside him, a leather dispatch case of a kind Oscar had seen before, only once, only ever in photographs in the newspapers he wasn't supposed to read.

Government issue. Home Office pattern.

Nell touched his elbow. "You've gone white."

"One letter," Oscar said quietly. "Count them again. Count every page."
