## 11. The Inn at Newark

The inn stood where the Great North Road bent toward the Trent, its sign — *The Wheatsheaf* — swinging on rusted hinges that groaned like an old man climbing stairs. Oscar brought the car around to the yard at a crawl, gravel popping under the tyres, and sat for a moment with his hands on the wheel, listening to the engine tick as it cooled.

They had made good time since the checkpoint outside Retford. Too good, perhaps. The miles behind them had a quality he couldn't shake, like footsteps heard in a corridor after the walker has stopped.

"You're doing it again," Nell said from beside him. She had her bonnet off and her hair coming loose in dark wisps, and she was looking at him with that flat, unimpressed attention she reserved for his worst moods. "Driving the same ten feet of road twice in your head."

"I'm checking the light."

"There's no light. It's nearly nine."

He got out. The evening air smelled of coal smoke and river damp and, faintly, of the stables — horse and straw and something older underneath, something that belonged to every innyard in England. He went round to the back of the car and knelt by the spare tyre casing, working it loose with fingers that had gone clumsy somewhere south of Doncaster.

The letters were there. All but one.

He counted them twice. Then a third time, pressing each envelope flat against his palm as though the missing page might be coaxed into existence by touch. Crane had carried eleven. Eleven had been re-sealed by Nell's careful hands at the kitchen table in Belgrave Square — she had shown him how to soften the gum with a brush dipped in warm water, how to press a fresh sheet of tissue over the flap so the seal would take cleanly, so no one lifting an envelope to a lamp would see the seam. Eleven envelopes had gone into the tyre casing before dawn. Ten lay against his hand now, their edges soft from handling.

"Nell."

She came around the car. He held up the stack without a word, and watched her count, and watched her arrive at the same number he had.

"When?" she said.

"That's the question, isn't it."

* * *

They took a room — two rooms, registered as brother and sister, Mr. and Miss Delacroix of Harrogate — because turning back was impossible and driving through the night with a possible spy already marked on their trail was worse. The innkeeper's wife, a broad comfortable woman named Mrs. Tuke, took their coin and asked nothing beyond whether they'd want supper in the parlour or upstairs, and Oscar said the parlour, because a man who ate alone looked like a man hiding, and a man who hid invited watching.

He understood this now. It was one of the many things he had learned in three days that twenty years of reading other people's correspondence had never taught him: invisibility was not a cloak you wore. It was a performance, and performances could fail.

The parlour was low-ceilinged and lamplit, half full of commercial travellers drinking mild ale over their ledgers. A fire had been laid but not lit; the chimney drew badly, Mrs. Tuke said, until the frost came. Nell chose a table in the corner with her back to the wall — she always chose such tables now, and Oscar had stopped remarking on it — and ordered for both of them, mutton chops and bread and a jug of small beer, speaking to the serving girl in a voice pitched exactly as a tired sister's voice should be.

Oscar ate and did not taste anything. His mind kept returning to the second checkpoint. The inspector with the grey eyes and the notebook. The way the man had walked a slow circle round the motorcar, tapping the mudguards, asking questions about plates and permits while Nell spun her story about the dying aunt in York — God rest her fictional soul — and Oscar had produced Crane's forged papers and felt the whole rotten structure of his new life balance on the thickness of a signature.

Ten minutes, perhaps, they had been stopped. Ten minutes in which the inspector's gloved hands might have gone anywhere.

But the tyre casing hadn't been opened. He was almost sure. Almost sure meant nothing now; he had learned that too. The man could have palmed an envelope while pretending to check the registration. Or — another thought, colder than the first — the letter might have gone missing earlier, at the house, in the dark of the mews before dawn, when anyone might have come down the lane. Or later, here, in the minutes between their arrival and the carrying up of their bags, when the car had stood unattended in the yard for the length of time it takes a woman to register two names in a ledger.

"Stop," Nell said quietly.

"Stop what?"

"Counting backwards. I can hear you counting." She tore her bread and pushed half onto his plate. "Eat. If they've taken a letter, they've taken it. What matters is what we do before morning."

"And what is that?"

She considered the fire that wasn't lit, the travellers with their ledgers, the door to the yard standing open to the blue-black evening. "We decide who else knows," she said. "And then we decide what to do about it."

* * *

It was while they were finishing supper that Oscar noticed the lodger.

A man sat alone at the far end of the room, nearer the window, with a glass of whisky he had barely touched and a newspaper folded to a column of shipping news. He was maybe fifty, dressed in the drab brown of a provincial solicitor or a railway clerk promoted beyond his station — the kind of man whose profession is being overlooked. He had been in the parlour when they came down. Oscar was certain of that much, and uncertain of everything else, which was the trouble. Certainty had become a currency he couldn't trust.

The man turned a page. Turned it back. Looked out at the darkening yard, where the motorcar stood pale as a bone among the carts, and then — briefly, professionally, the glance of a man checking his watch rather than studying anything — at their table.

"He's been looking at the car," Oscar murmured.

"He's been looking at everything. So have you." But Nell's shoulders had changed; some tension had entered them that hadn't been there a moment before. She didn't turn her head. "Describe him."

"Fifty-ish. Brown suit, brown hat on the settle beside him. Boots are city boots, wrong for Newark. Whisky untouched since we sat down. Reading shipping movements in a landlocked town."

"Hm." She sipped her beer. "And his hands?"

Oscar looked. The man's hands were folded on the newspaper, and even across the room he could see the calluses were wrong for a clerk — thickened across the palm, the nails cut short and square. Hands that had gripped things. Tools, or bars, or other men's collars.

"Vane's men don't look like policemen," he said. "That's the point of them."

"No," Nell agreed softly. "The point of them is that they look like whatever you're least afraid of. A bored sergeant. A lost traveller. A clerk reading about ships." She set down her glass. "Go and ask him the time."

"What?"

"Go and ask him the time, Oscar. Politely. Like a man making conversation in an inn. And watch his eyes when you do. Men who are merely curious look at your mouth when you speak. Men who are hunting look past your shoulder, counting exits."

He wanted to argue. He wanted, absurdly, to go upstairs and bolt the door and stand guard over ten envelopes until dawn, as though vigilance were the same thing as courage. Instead he rose, straightening his jacket, and crossed the parlour with the unhurried gait of a tired motorist, and pulled out the chair opposite the brown-suited man without quite asking permission.

"Beg pardon, sir — my sister's train-obsessed and my watch has stopped. Might I ask the hour?"

The man looked up. His eyes were pale, washed-out blue, and they rested on Oscar's face for exactly the length of a courteous answer, and then moved — just once, just past his left shoulder, to the doorway, to the stairs, to the open yard door — and returned.

"Half past nine," he said pleasantly. "Your motor's a Darracq, isn't it? Rare thing, this far north."

"It gets me about."

"My cousin keeps one in Leeds. Says the carburettor chokes in cold weather." The man smiled. It was a good smile, practised, worn smooth as a stair tread. "You'll want to drain it tonight, if there's frost coming. Nothing worse than a dead motor on a morning you need to move."

Every hair on Oscar's arms lifted. *A morning you need to move.* There was no way the sentence meant anything. There was every way it meant everything.

"Thank you kindly," he said. "Good night to you."

He walked back across the parlour with his pulse hammering in his ears and sat down across from Nell, and neither of them spoke for a long moment, because the serving girl was clearing plates, and because some conversations must happen in silence or not at all.

"Well?" she breathed.

"He knew the make of the car. He told me to drain the carburettor before morning." Oscar turned his glass slowly on the wood. "Nell — if he knows the car, he knows the plates. If he knows the plates, someone gave him the number. And the only people who have that number are the men at the second checkpoint, or whoever they reported to, and that report goes—" He stopped. He couldn't say the name aloud in a public parlour, even a name stripped of context. Sir Roderick Vane sat inside the word *report* the way poison sits inside a well.

"—north," Nell finished for him. "To Manchester. To the printer." She was very still. "Or the line is already cut, and we're delivering testimony into a locked room."

* * *

They went up early. In the narrow room with its two beds and its water jug and its candle burning low, Nell took the letters from the tyre casing and divided them — four into the lining of her petticoat bodice, six into a oilskin packet that went, after a debate conducted entirely in glances, into the false bottom of Oscar's travelling case, beneath a layer of shirts. The eleventh letter's absence sat between them like a fifth person in the room.

"If they have it," Oscar said, "they know what we carry."

"They know what we *were* carrying. One letter, out of eleven, describing conditions at Holloway." Nell sat on the edge of her bed, methodically rebraiding her hair. "Edith wrote eleven letters precisely so that losing any one of them wouldn't kill the testimony. She said so — 'let them steal what they like, there will always be another copy of the truth.' She built redundancy into it like a bridge builder builds extra piers."

"She also assumed the letters would reach print by Friday."

"Then we'd best not assume otherwise." She tied off the braid and looked at him across the candlelight. Her face was young and old at once — twenty-five years old and carrying the weight of a woman who had been force-fed in Holloway and released into silence, who had spent two years learning that survival itself could be a kind of complicity. He knew that weight now. He shared it. "Here's what we do. We leave before the inn wakes. Six o'clock. You'll drain the carburettor tonight as our friend suggests — genuinely, because I don't put it past him to loosen something while we sleep, and a broken car keeps us here till noon with all the time in the world for visitors."

"And the letters?"

"Split between us, always. If they stop the car, we're a brother and sister going to a funeral in Harrogate. If they search one of us—" she touched the flat shape against her ribs, "—they get part of the truth, never all of it. That was Edith's design. We're just the last link in it."

Oscar sat down heavily on his own bed. The mattress creaked; somewhere below, the inn settled and ticked around its dying fires. "I keep thinking about the man downstairs. About his hands. I keep thinking — I read thousands of letters, Nell. Thousands. I steamed open other people's secrets for years and filed them away in my head like a magpie, and never once did I imagine what it would feel like to be the secret in somebody else's file."

"Now you know."

"Yes." He laughed, without humour. "Now I know. And I find I hate it, and I find I'm grateful for it, and I can't tell those two feelings apart anymore."

Nell looked at him for a long moment. Whatever passed behind her eyes, she didn't share it — she rationed herself, always — but she reached over and pinched out the candle between finger and thumb, leaving them in the dark with the sound of the wind rising off the Trent.

"Sleep," she said. "One of us should."

* * *

He woke at four by the church bell and lay listening.

The house was quiet — the particular quiet of a full building holding its breath, floorboards contracting, a pipe ticking somewhere in the wall. Below, in the yard, a cart creaked. Footsteps crossed gravel, unhurried, and paused — he was sure they paused — near the place where the motorcar stood.

Oscar rose without lighting a candle and went to the window, easing back the curtain one inch.

Moonlight lay thin on the yard. The Darracq stood silver-pale, hood latched, tyres shadow-dark. Beside it, a figure in a brown coat was standing very still, head tilted, in the attitude of a man examining something closely — or listening to something intently. As Oscar watched, the figure raised one hand and knocked, twice, softly, on the car's flank. Then he took something from his pocket and bent toward the front of the machine, and was lost below the line of the bonnet.

For ten seconds Oscar stood frozen, his mind sprinting down every corridor at once — the carburettor, the plates, the tyre casing, the papers in Crane's forged hand, the six envelopes in the travelling case three feet from where Nell slept. Then the figure straightened, replaced whatever it held, and turned — and the moon caught his face, and it was not the brown-suited man at all. It was younger, rougher, a stablehand's cap pulled low, and he was grinning at the car with the simple pleasure of a boy admiring a machine, and he spat on his palm and rubbed a smear of mud off the headlamp with his sleeve, tenderly, the way one polishes a trophy.

A boy. Just a boy, cleaning the lamp of a fine motorcar because fine motorcars were beautiful.

Oscar let the curtain fall and stood in the dark with his heart going like a hammer and understood, with terrible clarity, that this was what the rest of his life would be: unable ever again to tell the boy from the hunter, the innocent from the instrument, the knock on the car's flank from a signal tapped in code. Paranoia, Mrs. Prewitt would have called it, and clucked, and poured more tea. But paranoia, Oscar thought, gripping the cold windowsill, was only literacy in a language you wished you'd never learned.

He dressed fully, in the dark. He woke Nell with a hand on her shoulder, and she came awake the way soldiers come awake — instantly, silently, reaching for the bag before her eyes were open.

"Six o'clock," she whispered.

"Sooner. Now. While it's dark."

They carried their bags down the creaking stairs, past the bar-room where one lamp still burned and a cat blinked at them from a bench, and out into the yard where the cold hit like a slap. Oscar drained the carburettor by feel, muttering, hands shaking so badly he flooded the ground twice. Nell checked the tyre casing, the tool locker, the space beneath the seats. Everything where it should be. Everything accounted for except the letter that wasn't, and the man in brown, whose bedroom window — Oscar checked as they loaded — stood dark, the curtains drawn, the room either empty or occupied by a man sleeping very quietly indeed.

The gates of the yard opened onto the Great North Road running white under the moon, northward, toward Manchester, toward Friday, toward the printer's devil waiting in his cellar.

As Oscar swung the car out onto the road and opened the throttle, Nell leaned close against the wind and shouted the thing they had both been thinking and neither had said:

"The letter they took — Oscar — what if it was the one that named the route?"

He pressed harder on the accelerator, and behind them the lights of Newark fell away, and ahead of them, at a crossing two miles north, a lantern swung — once, twice, three times — and went dark.
