## 12. Crane's Confession

The fever had its own geography. Verity Ashcombe had sat through three nights of it now, in the narrow room above the stables where Mrs. Prewitt had laid Tobias Crane on a horsehair mattress that smelled of saddle soap and old wool, and she had learned the map of his delirium the way one learns a hostile coastline — by wreck after wreck. He spoke to people who were not there. He apologized to walls. And twice each night, at hours Verity could have set a clock by, he said the same word in the same broken voice: *Milly.*

"Milly," he said again now, at half past four on Thursday morning, his eyes open but fixed on the ceiling as though reading something written there in a hand only he could decipher. "I told her the wall was low. I told her it was low because I needed her to believe it. Do you understand? It wasn't cowardice. It was *arithmetic.*"

Verity leaned forward in the chair she had dragged from her own drawing room, still upholstered in peacock silk, absurd here among the trunks and harness leather. She had not slept. Her hands, folded in her lap, were cold as the coins she'd once handed beggars outside St. Martin's — the practiced charity of a woman who had never, until this week, been asked to give anything that cost.

"Mr. Crane," she said quietly. "You're dreaming."

"I'm remembering." His head turned toward her with terrible slowness, and his eyes — fever-bright, sunk deep in a face gone the colour of candle tallow — found hers and held them. "There's the difference between me and you, Lady Ashcombe. You dream of what's coming. I remember what I did."

* * *

Two hundred miles north, in the dark before dawn, Oscar Delacroix drove through rain that fell not in drops but in long grey ropes, and tried not to think about checkpoints.

Nell slept against the door of the motor car, or pretended to. He had watched her for the last hour, the way her breathing stayed too even, too deliberate, and understood that she was keeping watch behind closed eyelids — that sleep, for her, was a country she could enter but never fully surrender to. Holloway had taught her that. Force-feeding had taught her that. He thought of the tube, the gag, the funnel of liquid poured into a body that refused it, and his hands tightened on the wheel until the leather creaked.

"You'll wear out the steering," Nell said without opening her eyes.

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be awake." She opened her eyes then, and looked out at the rain-blurred road unspooling ahead of them, silver in the headlamps' reach and black beyond it. "We're past Stevenage. The second checkpoint is somewhere before Baldock. They'll be watching for plates."

"I know."

"You know everything, don't you." It wasn't a question, and it wasn't kind. "That's what you told me yesterday. You read every letter that came into that house for six years, and you knew everything, and you did nothing. I've been turning it over all night like a stone in my pocket." She turned to look at him properly now, and in the dashboard's amber glow her face was young and hard and very tired. "What I can't work out is whether you're a coward or a man who was waiting."

Oscar kept his eyes on the road. The wipers swept, swept, swept, metronomic as a heart.

"What's the difference?" he asked.

"Cowards never wait for anything. They only hide." She settled back against the door. "Keep driving, Oscar. If we're stopped, I'm your sister Ada, and our aunt is dying in Leeds, and you are too grief-stupid to lie well — which is fortunate, because lying badly is more convincing than lying well. Everyone believes a bad liar. Nobody believes a good one."

He almost smiled. Then the road bent, and the trees closed overhead, and he saw the lantern-light of the checkpoint ahead, and there was no room in him left for anything but fear.

But that was north. This is the story of the south, of the stable loft, of the word *Milly*, and of how Verity Ashcombe spent the last dark hours before Friday learning that survival is never free — it is only paid for by someone else.

* * *

"Who was Milly?"

Verity had asked the question an hour ago, when the fever first named her, and Crane had answered with silence so complete she'd thought he'd slipped under again. Now, as the grey light of Thursday morning began to seep through the small window over the tack wall, he answered.

"She was a nurse." His voice had changed — steadier now, as though confession were a cold compress laid against the burn of him. "At Holloway. A little woman from Norfolk with a voice like a church bell rung softly. She was assigned to the ward they put Edith on after—" He stopped. Swallowed. "After the worst of it. After Vane's doctors had finished their experiments in patience."

"The force-feeding."

"Worse than the feeding." Crane's hands moved on the blanket, plucking at it, arranging and rearranging nothing. "You've read the letters. You know what's in them. But letters are polite, Lady Ashcombe. Letters are the version of a fire that fits inside a matchbox. What Milly saw — what Milly helped me carry Edith out past — none of that would fit in any matchbox in England."

Verity rose and crossed to the basin on the washstand, wet a cloth, wrung it, and brought it back. She pressed it to his forehead with a tenderness that surprised her; she had not known her own hands contained it. He flinched, then went still beneath the coolness like a man granted a pardon he hadn't requested.

"Tell me anyway," she said. "Not the version that fits in a matchbox."

* * *

The checkpoint at the crossroads outside Baldock was a plank barrier, two constables, and a brazier smoking under a tarpaulin strung between trees. Oscar brought the car to a stop with the smoothness of eight years' practice and felt Nell go rigid beside him — not visibly, not in any way the constables could see, but he felt it the way a driver feels a change in the engine, some note in the machine gone taut.

"Papers," said the older constable. Rain ran off the brim of his helmet. He looked bored, and bored men, Oscar knew, looked either closely or not at all, with no middle ground, and no way to predict which.

Oscar produced the documents Crane had forged in the lucid hour before his collapse — a hire-car contract, two railway excursion tickets, a letter from a fictitious uncle about a fictitious estate in Hertfordshire. His hands were steady. He noticed this with a strange detachment, as though observing another man's hands, some other chauffeur's hands, and thought: *so this is what it feels like. So this is what I've been avoiding all my life.*

The constable read. The younger one circled the car, boots squelching, and paused at the rear — at the spare tire casing, empty now, its secret surrendered to the train at King's Cross — and Oscar's heartbeat climbed into his throat and sat there, enormous, blocking all traffic.

"Aunt ill, is she?" the older constable said, looking up at Nell.

"Dying," Nell said. Her voice cracked precisely where a real sister's would crack — grief performed so honestly that Oscar, knowing it false, felt his eyes sting anyway. "They sent for us Tuesday. We may be too late already. We may—"

"There, there." The constable stepped back, embarrassed, the way men always were in the presence of a weeping woman, retreating into the rain as if it offered cover. "Get on, then. Mind the fog past Letchworth."

The barrier lifted. Oscar drove through it at exactly the speed of a grieving man, neither hurrying nor dawdling, and held that speed for two miles before he trusted his voice.

"You didn't tell me you could act."

"I'm not acting." Nell looked out at the hedgerows streaming past. "I had an aunt. She died while I was inside. They wouldn't let me go to the funeral. So no, Oscar — that isn't acting. That's borrowing." She turned her face away to the window. "Everything I am now is borrowed from something they took."

He drove on through the rain and said nothing, because there was nothing in him equal to that, and he knew it.

* * *

In the loft, the cloth had cooled on Crane's forehead, and Verity knelt to refresh it, and Crane spoke into the space above her bowed head as though dictating to a shorthand clerk he couldn't see.

"Milly Ashford. Twenty-three years old. Norfolk. She had a sweetheart who worked the herring boats, and a mother she wrote to every Sunday, and she was the only person in that entire building who treated the suffragettes as though they were made of the same substance as other human beings." He laughed — a dry, awful sound, like paper tearing slowly. "For that, they broke her. Not with violence. With paperwork. There was a girl on our ward, a Welsh collier's daughter, seventeen, who died of septicaemia after the tube tore her. Officially she died of pneumonia. Milly knew it wasn't pneumonia. Milly signed the chart anyway, because the alternative was losing her post, and her post was the only thing standing between the doctors and the girls on nights when the doctors got — inventive."

Verity's hands had stopped moving. The cloth hung dripping in her fingers.

"And you knew her," Verity said. "Before the escape."

"I was the coal merchant." Crane's mouth twisted. "Every prison has a hundred deliveries a day and a hundred men who come and go, and no one looks at a coalman's face because no one wants to imagine coal dust anywhere near their dinner. I'd been running messages in and out since the spring of '11. Notes wrapped in oilskin sewn into my coat lining. Then out of the coat entirely, into bread loaves. Then into the ash bin behind the laundry, which Milly controlled, because Milly had decided — God help her — that what was being done in that place was wrong, and that wrong things require witnesses."

He closed his eyes. The fever light in his face guttered and burned lower, and for a moment Verity feared he had gone under again. But his voice continued, quieter, levelled down to bedrock.

"In September of '11 they decided Edith Marchmont was going to die. Not by decree — nothing so honest as a decree. By neglect dressed up as treatment. Her heart was failing from the hunger strike, and Vane's men wanted a death that looked natural, because a martyr on a platform is worth ten thousand pamphlets, and they knew it better than we did. Milly heard the order given. She came to the ash bin that evening instead of leaving word, and she said to me — I will hear her voice until I die — she said, 'It has to be tomorrow. Before the change of shift. And I'm coming with you.'"

Verity rose slowly from her knees. The peacock-silk chair stood empty behind her, absurd and ornate, and she did not look at it.

"She helped you take Edith out."

"She planned it." Each word cost him visibly now, drawn up out of the mine-shaft of him one bucket at a time. "A convalescent transport was scheduled for the morning — a covered wagon taking four women to the infirmary annexe. Milly falsified the manifest. Put Edith's number on the list of the walking wounded, bribed the orderly with savings she'd meant for her mother's rent, and walked beside the wagon to the gate in her nurse's cape, carrying Edith's papers in her own name. At the gate the sergeant knew her. Wished her a good morning." Crane's breath caught, held, released. "And she wished him one back. And that was the last time anyone wished anything at that gate, because forty seconds later the discrepancy in the manifest was discovered, and the alarm went, and Milly — Milly had promised me she'd follow the escape route herself within the week, that she'd stay one more shift to destroy the manifest and the chart both, to leave no trail—"

He stopped. The silence filled the loft like water filling a hull.

"She didn't make it out," Verity said.

"They took her that afternoon." Crane opened his eyes at last, and they were clear now, terribly clear, the fever burned down to a cold steady flame. "Not arrested. Taken. There is a difference, Lady Ashcombe, and I have spent two years learning the grammar of it. Arrested men get trials. Taken women get obituaries written by the men who took them. Three weeks later the *Norfolk Chronicle* printed that Nurse M. Ashford of Wells-next-the-Sea had died of typhoid fever contracted in service. There was no typhoid. There was no service. There was a grave in an unmarked plot that I visited once, at night, through a hole in a wall, and there was a certificate bearing the signature of Sir Roderick Vane — the same hand, do you understand, the *same pen, the same ink, the same lies* that killed Edith Marchmont on paper two years ago."

Verity's legs carried her to the window without consulting her. Below, the mews lay washed clean by the rain, ordinary, glistening, innocent-looking as all complicit things look in the early morning.

"You told no one," she said.

"I told no one because telling would have gotten Milly's family visited by Vane's gentlemen, and her mother buried beside her inside a month. I told no one because Edith begged me — on her knees, Lady Ashcombe, on her knees in a safe house in Islington — she said, 'Her death buys my testimony. Don't spend it cheap.' And I have carried that sentence for two years like a stone in the boot." He pushed himself up on one elbow, and the effort shook him, but his voice rang. "Well. I am done carrying it. If the price of the truth is that everyone who paid it stays anonymous, then Vane wins even when he loses. Milly Ashford's name goes in the testimony. Her mother gets to know how her daughter died. Let them visit me for it. Let them take whatever's left."

* * *

North, past Letchworth, the rain thinned to mist and the fields opened out, wet green under a sky the colour of dishwater, and Oscar pulled the car onto a lane to let a farm cart pass and found Nell watching him.

"At Newark tonight," she said, "we sleep in separate rooms and bar the windows, and we take turns. You first watch, then me. Agreed?"

"Agreed." He eased the car back onto the road. "Nell — the letters. If something happens to me between here and Manchester—"

"Nothing is happening to you."

"If something does. You know what's in them. Every line. You copied them twice."

"I know them better than I know the psalms," she said simply. "I could recite them in the dark. I have recited them in the dark — every night since Tuesday, to keep myself from being afraid, which is the only use fear ever has if you catch it in time." She looked at him, and something in her face softened by a degree, a single degree, the ice sheet shifting. "You want me to promise to carry them if you fall. All right. I promise. But understand what you're asking, Oscar. Carrying them means living them. There's no putting them down again. Once you've spoken Edith Marchmont's truth aloud, you belong to it, the way a bell belongs to the ringing."

Ahead, the road climbed toward the ridge and the country beyond it, and the mist parted just enough to show a line of hills far off, blue-grey, patient.

"That's what I'm afraid of," Oscar said. "That it's already true. That I've belonged to the ringing since the first envelope I steamed open, and I just never heard myself."

Nell said nothing to that. But she reached over, briefly, and rested her gloved hand on his sleeve — two seconds, no more — before returning it to her lap, and he drove on with the warmth of it still there, faint as a coal's last heat, all the way to Newark.

* * *

In London, the morning came fully, and Mrs. Prewitt brought tea, and Verity sat by the bed while Crane slept — truly slept now, the confession having drained the fever's worst poison out of him, the way a wound drains. On the table beside the basin lay the bundle of copies, and atop them, in Edith Marchmont's unmistakable hand, the covering letter whose final instruction Verity now reread with new ears:

*Add what he knows. Add the names he carries. A testimony with the dead unnamed is only half a resurrection.*

She looked at the sleeping smuggler, at the hollows of his cheeks, at the stone he had finally set down, and she understood that the document they were racing toward Friday was larger than any of them had believed — that it was not one woman's account but a ledger of debts, and that every page of it had been purchased at full price.

Then she heard the automobile return to the mews below — earlier than expected, engine idling too fast, a sound with urgency in it — and Mrs. Prewitt appeared in the doorway with her face gone white as flour.

"Lady Ashcombe," she said. "It's the boy from the station. He says there's trouble on the northern line."
