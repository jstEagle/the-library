## 13. The Railway Trap

Dawn came to Newark the colour of dishwater, seeping through the curtains of Room 4 like something ashamed to be seen. Oscar had not slept. He had sat in the chair by the window with his boots off and his coat on, listening to the building settle around him — the tick of pipes, the groan of floorboards, Nell breathing evenly in the bed by the wall, or pretending to. He had learned by now that she slept the way soldiers slept: lightly, armed, one ear open for the world's intentions.

The missing letter sat in his mind like a stone in a boot.

He went over it again, as he had gone over it a hundred times since midnight. Eleven letters had left London. He had counted them at every stop, the way a miser counts coins, the way a guilty man counts sins. At the second checkpoint, outside Grantham, Inspector Hargreaves — sharp-eyed, soft-spoken, dangerous — had asked to see inside the motor. Oscar had handed over the forged papers, Nell had performed her dying-aunt grief with a subtlety that still made the back of his neck prickle with admiration, and they had passed. By minutes. By inches. But while Hargreaves's constable had stood at the running board with his hand resting casually on the spare tyre casing, and while Nell had wept beautifully about Aunt Ada's dropsy, someone — Hargreaves himself, most likely — had been standing very close to the valise on the back seat. Close enough. Long enough.

One letter gone. Ten remained, wrapped in oilcloth inside the hollowed casing of the spare tyre, and two more pressed flat in the torn spine of Verity Ashcombe's hymnal, which rode in plain view on the seat between them because the boldest hiding place was the one no policeman bothered to search. *Abide With Me*, embossed in gold. Who searches a hymnal?

Someone who knows what to look for. That was the arithmetic that had kept him awake. If Vane's men had lifted a letter at Grantham, then they knew the letters were moving north, and they knew the shape of the cargo, and it would take them no time at all to circulate a description — a chauffeur, a young woman posing as his sister, a motor car with London plates — to every station yard between Newark and Manchester. The railway was the throat of England. All traffic passed through it, and all of it could be gripped.

Below in the yard, a milk cart rattled past, and somewhere a train whistled — a long, low note that seemed to pass through the walls and through Oscar's chest, going north without him, indifferent, punctual, free.

Nell stirred. "You haven't slept."

"How can you tell?"

"Because when you sleep you look like a man being forgiven," she said, sitting up, her hair loose and dark against the pillow. "And you don't look forgiven now."

* * *

They took breakfast in the coffee room downstairs, at a table by the window where Oscar could watch the street. The inn was half-empty at that hour: a farmer arguing with his porridge, two commercial travelers comparing samples, and in the corner, the lodger.

Oscar had noticed him the night before — a lean man in a brown suit, thirty-five perhaps, with the kind of face designed to be forgotten, drinking one whisky slowly over three hours and reading the same page of his newspaper. He had watched them come in. Not stared — watched, the way a man watches weather. And this morning he was there again, in the same corner, with the same paper, and when Nell rose to fetch more tea he let his eyes follow her the way a ferret follows a rabbit into cover.

"Don't look," Oscar murmured.

"I'm not looking." She sat down again. "Is it him?"

"It might be nobody. It might be everything."

The man folded his paper, rose, dropped a coin beside his cup, and walked out through the entrance hall without a glance at either of them. Through the window they watched him pause in the street, consult a pocket watch, and set off toward the station at an unhurried walk.

"He's going for the Manchester train," Nell said.

"So are half the people in this town."

"Yes," she said. "But he checked his watch first. People going places check the time. People checking on other people check the time too — but differently." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, a small precise gesture, and Oscar thought, not for the first time, that whatever Holloway had done to Nell Barrow it had also made her, honed her like a blade is honed, and that the sharpening had not been kind. "Oscar. We have to decide before that train leaves."

He looked at the hymnal, sitting in her lap where she'd been holding it as though it were scripture indeed. Two letters inside. Eight in the tyre casing under the car. The whole testimony of Edith Marchmont — the hunger strikes, the cell, the doctor's careful lies, the name *Sir Roderick Vane* written in a dead woman's hand — divided between a rubber tyre and a book of hymns, in a market town in Nottinghamshire, with the net drawing closed around them by the hour.

"If they're watching the station," Oscar said slowly, working it through aloud, "then they're watching for us. For the car. For my face, if Hargreaves sent a description, and hers. The car can't go near the railway. But—" and here the idea arrived, the way ideas do, unwelcome and fully formed, "—the letters don't have to travel with us. They only have to travel."

Nell went very still. "You want to put them on the train anyway."

"I want to put *some* of them on the train. In someone else's hands. Someone they aren't looking for." He was speaking faster now, the plan assembling itself out of panic and geometry. "We split the risk. The hymnal goes north by rail, carried by a stranger — a traveler, a clergyman, anyone with an honest face and a ticket to Manchester. Meanwhile you and I drive on, empty, clean, nothing to find even if they take the car apart bolt by bolt. If the train gets through, the letters get through. If we get stopped, we lose nothing but our freedom, and freedom's already a borrowed thing."

"And if the train doesn't get through?"

"Then we've lost two letters and gained a lesson."

"No," Nell said. "You'd lose two letters. I'd lose two letters I know by heart." She said it flatly, without emphasis, and then seemed to hear herself, and stopped, and looked down into her tea. Oscar caught it — the slip, the strange phrasing — and turned it over in his mind, and found he had no time to ask. The station clock, visible through the window across the square, stood at twenty to nine. The Manchester express left at nine.

"Who carries it?" she asked.

"Not me. Not you. Someone local, someone ordinary, someone whose face isn't on any notice." His eyes swept the coffee room. The farmer, no — a farmer bound for Manchester with a stranger's hymnal would remember this day all his life. The travelers, no — men with cases and routes and employers who asked questions. Then the door opened and the answer walked in wearing an apron.

She was young, the girl who had brought up their supper the night before — sixteen at most, red-knuckled, quick-eyed, with the permanent wary cheerfulness of someone who worked for tips in a town full of strangers. She came to clear the next table, stacking plates with a clatter.

"You," Oscar said quietly. "What's your name?"

"Ada, sir." A curtsy, automatic. "Is summat wrong with the room?"

"Nothing's wrong. I want to ask you a favour, and I want to pay you for it, and I want you to decide quickly." He laid the hymnal on the table between them, wrapped now in brown paper and string, tied like a parcel of sermons. "I'm asking you to carry this on the nine o'clock to Manchester. There's a gentleman will meet you on the platform — tall, grey beard, spectacles, carrying a rolled newspaper. You give him this parcel, he gives you this—" a fold of notes, generous enough to make her breath catch, "—and you come home. That's all. You never opened it, you never saw us, and if anyone ever asks, you carried it for your auntie in Manchester because she's poorly and fond of hymns."

Ada looked at the parcel. Then at the money. Then, unexpectedly, at Nell — and something passed between the two women, some current Oscar couldn't read, some recognition that had nothing to do with him.

"My cousin's in Holloway," Ada said. "Since March. For breaking windows at the War Office." She picked up the parcel and tucked it under her arm as naturally as a loaf of bread. "Nine o'clock, you said. Tall, grey beard, newspaper."

"That's all."

"Right." She straightened her apron. "I'll want the money first. No offence meant."

"None taken," said Oscar, and meant it, and thought: *this is what trust looks like now. It looks like a girl who wants paying in advance.* He counted the notes into her palm and watched her go out through the yard door toward the station, brown parcel under her arm, walking at the pace of the utterly unremarkable, and he felt hope and dread rise in him together like water in a double lock.

* * *

They watched the departure from the bridge over the line, where the smoke of the express drifted up around them smelling of coal and rain. Oscar had insisted on seeing it with his own eyes — had told himself it was prudence, knowing it was cowardice, wanting to see the letters safely away the way a man wants to see his child onto the boat.

The platform below was crowded and ordinary. Porters wheeled luggage. A guard walked the length of the train with his flag furled. And there, third carriage from the front, Ada climbed aboard with the parcel under her arm, paused on the step to say something laughing to a porter, and vanished through the doorway. On the far end of the platform, leaning against a pillar with his rolled newspaper, stood a tall man with a grey beard and spectacles — Crane's arrangement, wired ahead from London through two intermediaries to a Manchester bookseller who owed Verity old debts. The receiver was in place. The chain was complete.

The whistle blew. Couplings clanked down the platform like a run of dominoes. The train began to move — gathering itself, gathering speed, smoke rolling back along the roofs — and Oscar gripped the cold iron rail of the bridge and watched the carriages slide beneath him, thinking: *Edith Marchmont is crossing England at fifty miles an hour in a hymn book carried by a girl called Ada,* and felt something dangerously close to joy.

Then, as the last carriage passed, he saw the man in the brown suit.

He was on the platform. Not boarding — standing well back, hands in pockets, watching the train go with his head tilted slightly, the attitude of a man confirming rather than witnessing. As the tail lamp dwindled toward the junction he turned, unhurried, and spoke to a uniformed inspector by the barrier, and the inspector nodded, and blew his whistle, and pointed — not after the train, but toward the telegraph office at the end of the platform.

"Oh God," Nell said softly. "Oh, Oscar."

"They might just be reporting a suspicious parcel—"

"They're wiring ahead." Her knuckles were white on the rail. "Every station to Manchester. The train will be met. She'll be taken off at Retford or Doncaster with that parcel in her arms and she won't know why, poor lamb, she'll think it's a mistake—" She stopped. Turned to him. "We have to warn her. We have to—"

"We can't." His voice came out flat and terrible. "If we show ourselves at that station we're finished, and the eight letters in the tyre are finished, and everything is finished. That's the whole design of it, Nell. That's why they hunt in chains. So that saving one link costs you all the others."

She stared at him. Below, the telegraph office window glowed with its small busy lamplight, clicking out its dots and dashes into the morning, and Oscar understood with perfect clarity that he was watching the exact moment the trap closed — that somewhere down the line, at some station whose name he didn't yet know, a porter and a policeman were already being told to look for a parcel and a girl, and that Ada, sixteen years old, red-knuckled, brave for four shillings and her cousin's sake, was riding unknowing into it.

"We should never have—" Nell began.

"I know."

"—you used her. We used a child as a shield."

"I know." He said it twice because once wasn't enough to bear the weight. "Come on. The car's been standing too long."

* * *

They drove north out of Newark on the Great North Road with the hood down and the morning wind stripping the fear off them in layers, and for two hours neither of them spoke. The hedges ran green and gold. Rooks argued over stubble fields. The world went about its business with the serene indifference it had shown all century, and Oscar drove with his hands at ten and two and his mind in the telegraph office at Newark station, hearing the click-click-click of his own ruin being spelled out letter by letter.

It was near Doncaster, stopping for petrol at a road-side pump, that they bought the evening edition of the *Yorkshire Post* almost by reflex — Oscar's new habit, born two days ago, of reading every word of print within reach as though the truth might be announced in advance. They stood by the car in the forecourt, shoulder to shoulder, and unfolded it, and there it was. Page five. Four lines.

*DETENTION AT NEWARK STATION. A young woman was yesterday morning removed from the Manchester express at Retford following a telegram from the Nottinghamshire Constabulary. A parcel in her possession was seized and examined by Superintendent's officers and found to contain nothing of interest. The contents — described as a book of hymns — were destroyed on the spot as a precaution against the conveyance of seditious material. The woman was released without charge.*

*Nothing of interest.*

Oscar read it three times. The words did not change. The hymnal was burned unread — *destroyed on the spot* — and Edith's two letters with it, her account of the cell and the tube forced down her throat, her careful furious handwriting, gone up as ash in a Yorkshire siding, and the girl Ada released without charge, spared, lucky, saved by the sheer blind stupidity of the men who had seized the wrong half of the cargo. Because of course they had searched the parcel and found only hymns. Of course they had. The letters in the tyre casing were still in the car behind them, still safe, still secret — except that Oscar had sent them away that morning, hadn't he, had loaded them into the spare tyre of a stranger's plan, and the plan had failed, and the letters—

No. He made himself think it through, standing in the petrol-smelling forecourt with the paper shaking faintly in his hands. The tyre casing had stayed with the car. Only the hymnal had gone. Only two letters. Two of ten. The mathematics of the disaster assembled themselves slowly, and with them came the thing underneath the mathematics, the thing he was ashamed of even as he felt it flood through him, warm and vile as brandy:

*Relief.*

They had taken the wrong parcel. They had burned the wrong letters. The trap had sprung and he was not in it. He was standing in the sun at a roadside pump with a full tank and an empty car and his skin intact, and some animal chamber of his heart was singing *thank God, thank God, thank God*, and he hated the song and could not stop humming it.

"They'll wire the description now," Nell said. Her voice was steady, which frightened him more than tears would have. "A chauffeur, a girl, a motor car, heading north. Every road from here to Manchester by nightfall. And we've nothing left to protect, Oscar. Do you understand? The hymnal's gone. Whatever's in the tyre is all that's left of Edith's hand in the world, and it's riding in the one vehicle in England they're hunting for."

He folded the newspaper. His hands were steady too, and he distrusted them for it.

"Then we get rid of the car," he said.

"Get rid of—"

"At the first town big enough to lose it in. We sell it, we abandon it, we leave it in a lane with the keys in it and walk away. From this moment we're a brother and sister travelling to a funeral, by train if we must, by foot if we have to, with nothing in our pockets but papers and grief." He heard himself say it and marvelled, distantly, at the man saying it — the man who had spent eleven years believing that survival meant invisibility and invisibility meant never deciding anything. "Empty-handed north. It's the only way."

Nell looked at him for a long moment. The wind moved the paper in his hand. Somewhere beyond the hedge a lark went up, singing its thread of song into the enormous empty sky, and he thought absurdly of Edith Marchmont in her cell, two years dead to the world, alive, waiting for her words to arrive.

"There's something you should know," Nell said quietly. "Before we go any further. Before you decide what we do when the tyres are gone and the paper's gone and there's nothing left between us and Manchester but our own wits."

"What?"

"What's in those letters," she said. "All of it. Every word." She held his eyes, and the wind pulled at her hair, and she said, in the voice of someone laying a card face-up on a table:

"I memorised them, Oscar. All ten. During the nights we copied them at Verity's — every line, every name, every date. You've been driving north with a spare tyre and a prayer." Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile; the place where a smile would go, once there was anything to smile about. "I *am* the cargo."
