## 14. Empty-Handed North

The engine ticked as it cooled in the lay-by off the Great North Road, and Oscar sat with his hands still on the wheel as though the wheel were the only thing holding the day together.

"You memorised them," he said.

It was not a question, but it was not quite a statement either. It was a man testing ice with one foot.

"All ten," Nell said again. She had not moved from her seat; she sat with her hands folded in her lap like a woman in church, which was, Oscar thought wildly, exactly where she ought to be saying this — from a pulpit, to a congregation, because it was nothing short of a miracle and miracles deserved their proper setting. "Every line. Every date. The names of the nurses who held her down. The name of the doctor who signed things he never saw."

"The missing letter." His voice came out cracked. "The one lifted at the checkpoint — the one Vane's men may have—"

"Was the fourth," she said. "Dated the ninth of March, 1912. It described the feeding tube and the officer who turned his back so he wouldn't have to watch. If they've read it, they know what we already know. They don't know anything new." A pause. "And even if they burn everything else, even if they burn *me*, they can't unread what's in here." She touched her temple with two fingers, lightly, the way you'd touch a wound that had stopped bleeding but hadn't healed.

Oscar got out of the car because he could not sit still. He stood in the grey light at the edge of a turnip field with the wind flattining the hedgerows flat and felt something move through him that had no decent name — part awe, part shame, part the vertigo of a man who has spent years believing history was a play performed behind glass and has just been told the understudy is standing beside him in a borrowed coat.

Ten letters. Edith Marchmont had written ten letters in a hand like wire, and Crane had carried them out of a prison system designed to make women disappear, and Verity's kitchen table had become a scriptorium by lamplight while Mrs. Prewitt kept watch at the window, and all the while Nell — quiet Nell, plain Nell, the governess nobody looked at twice because looking at governesses was a habit people had unlearned — had been doing the one thing no warrant could touch.

"How?" he asked, when he could ask anything.

She came to stand beside him at the fence. A cart went by on the road behind them, the driver not sparing them a glance.

"When I was in Holloway," she said, "they took my books. That's the first thing they do — or did, before the rules changed and then changed back. You have your Bible and you have your head and if you're lucky the head is better furnished than most." She watched the crows working the far field. "I learned passages. Whole speeches. Mrs. Pankhurst's words, Keir Hardie's, chunks of Milton because it was the only book they let me keep and I refused to let it be the only thing I owned. And when I got out and Verity took me in, and the copying began—" she shrugged, a small economical motion, "—it was the same work, only now the words mattered more. So I did what I'd trained myself to do. I read each page until I could see it with my eyes shut. Then I recited it back to myself while I braided my hair. While I scrubbed the step. While I lay awake listening for cars in the lane." She glanced at him. "You were there some of those nights. You watched me copy. You never asked why I never once consulted the original after the first reading."

"I thought you were being careful."

"I was being careful. Careful and thorough are sisters." She smiled then, properly, briefly, and it transformed her face the way a struck match transforms a room. "You've been treating those letters like relics, Oscar. Paper to be guarded, hidden in tyres, sewn into hymnals. Paper is what they can take from us. It's the first thing they take and the last thing they understand, because men like Vane believe a thing exists only if it can be seized." She tapped her temple again. "This they cannot seize. They tried, in Holloway. They tried very hard."

* * *

They drove north through the afternoon with the light going pewter-grey over the Wolds, and Oscar made her do it — made her recite, letter by letter, while he drove, partly to test her and partly because he needed to hear them aloud in daylight, in motion, stripped of candlelight and dread, to believe they were real.

She gave them without hesitation. Not fast — she delivered them the way Edith must have written them, level and precise and terrible, a woman dictating evidence rather than pleading for pity. The dates fell in order. The names: Warder Hobbes, Dr. Selkirk, the matron who wept once and never again. The measurements of a cell. The weight Edith had lost, in pounds, counted weekly like coins. And then the machinery above it all — the signature on a certificate, the arrangement between officials, the sentence Oscar had read four times and still could not read calmly:

*I am dead on paper, and the paper is the lie, and the lie has a name, and the name is Sir Roderick Vane.*

Nell reached that line at the crest of a hill with the whole vale of York spread below them, smoke rising from farmsteads straight as plumb-lines, and her voice didn't waver, and Oscar pulled the car onto the verge because his hands were shaking and he would not shake at sixty miles an hour.

"Say it again," he said.

"Which part?"

"The name."

"*Sir Roderick Vane,*" she said, clear as a bell across the empty country, and somewhere south of them, Oscar thought, that name was sitting in a warm office under a green lamp, believing itself safe, believing that fire consumed testimony the way fire consumes paper — completely, finally, leaving ash and silence.

He laughed. It surprised him coming up out of his chest — not a happy sound, more the sound a rope makes when a great strain comes off it.

"What?" Nell said.

"He thinks he's won," Oscar said. "We're driving north empty-handed and he thinks he's won, and the entire case against him is riding in the front seat eating an apple."

She had, in fact, an apple. She finished it while he gathered himself, and ate it without hurry, and threw the core into the hedge with an accuracy that he chose to find symbolic.

* * *

But the fear did not leave him; it only changed rooms.

That evening, in a low-beamed inn parlour near Thirsk with rain starting against the windows, Oscar laid out what remained of their situation on the scrubbed table like a losing hand of cards.

"The printer expects papers," he said. "Mr. Aldous set aside type-space on the strength of documents. Tomorrow we walk into Manchester with nothing but a story. What if he won't set a word of it on say-so? What if he thinks we're police provocateurs — bait dressed as couriers? It happens. It's happened before."

"Then he'll want proof," Nell said. "So we give him proof he can check. Names and dates he can verify against what he already knows — the hunger strike, the dates of the force-feeding, the warders' names, the transfer records. Any comrade who knew Edith will know pieces of this. When the details match, piece by piece, he'll know it's true, because a liar invents smoothly and the truth comes in fragments." She turned her teacup a quarter-turn, considering. "And if he still hesitates, we wire Verity. She'll confirm. Her word carries weight with that crowd."

"Assuming the wires aren't watched."

"Assuming they are, we use the code she and Edith used years ago. I know it. Garden flowers for people, weather for danger, prices for money." She looked up. "You keep planning for the moment everything goes wrong. Plan also for the moment it goes right. You'll be worse prepared for that one."

He stared at her. "When did you become the strategist?"

"About the same time you became the driver," she said. "Somewhere around Hertfordshire."

Mrs. Prewitt's phrase came back to him — *that girl has more spine than a whale* — and he almost smiled, and then didn't, because through the parlour window he caught the reflection of the public bar beyond: three men, one of them wearing boots too good for a market town and a coat cut in London. One of them was reading a folded sheet of typescript with the particular concentration of a man checking a description against faces.

Oscar did not turn his head. He lifted his glass and drank and watched the mirror-image of the man lower his paper and look — casually, professionally — at the two travellers by the window.

"We're known," he murmured into his beer. "Or we're suspected. Don't look."

"I'm not looking."

"The coat. By the bar. London tailoring, northern boots."

"Then we finish our tea slowly," Nell said, "and we leave separately in the morning, and tonight you sleep in the stable loft and I sleep in my room with the door wedged, and if anyone knocks I am a tired governess whose brother snores in the hay." She said it the way she recited the letters — level, precise, terrible in its calm. "Panic is a luxury, Oscar. We gave ours away at the second checkpoint."

They played draughts after that, badly and loudly, performing the boredom of ordinary travellers, while the London coat finished his pint and left without hurry. Oscar heard the yard gate, then a bicycle, then nothing.

He did not sleep in the loft. He slept in the car, wrapped in a horse blanket, with the bonnet cold against his shoulder blade and the whole dark sky for a ceiling, and he found — this astonished him — that he slept well. Better than he had in Belgravia, in the narrow bed above the garage, in all his careful invisible years. There was something about being empty-handed. Something about having nothing left to guard but a voice and a road and a woman asleep thirty feet away who contained, whole and ordered and indestructible, the ruin of Sir Roderick Vane.

* * *

Morning came hard and bright after the rain, the road steaming, hedges glittering. They took breakfast in shifts as agreed — Nell first, Oscar twenty minutes later — and met at the car as strangers meeting by chance, which was its own small theatre and which Nell entered into with unsettling relish, addressing him as "sir" until he threatened to make her walk.

By eleven the Pennines were shouldering up ahead of them, blue-grey and cloud-wrapped, and Manchester lay beyond like a furnace behind a curtain. Oscar drove with the windows down because the day demanded it, and the wind filled the car with the smell of wet stone and coal smoke drifting from distant towns, and for a few miles neither of them spoke, and it was not an uncomfortable silence. It was the silence of people who have said the unsayable and found the world still turns.

"There's a part I've never recited to you," Nell said at last, quietly, eyes on the hills.

Oscar glanced sideways. "A part of the letters?"

"A part of the fourth letter. The one they took." Her hands were folded again, church-hands, but her knuckles were white. "There was a paragraph Edith wrote small, cramped, at the bottom of the last page — as though she'd hidden it even from herself. I memorised it with the rest, but I've never said it aloud, because it isn't evidence. It doesn't name Vane. It doesn't convict anyone." The wind pulled a strand of hair across her face and she let it stay. "It says: *Tell whoever carries this that Tobias did not abandon me and I did not abandon her, and that the price was paid by both, and that if I live I will spend every year of my life making the ledger balance.*"

The car hummed. The hills came on.

"*Her*," Oscar said. "Not *him*. *Her.*"

"Yes," Nell said. "Whoever helped Crane get her out — the person who died for it, the person Crane won't speak of even in fever — Edith knew. She knew two years ago." She turned and looked at him fully, and behind the calm he saw the other thing, the thing that had driven her through Holloway and out the far side: not hardness, but a refusal, absolute and permanent, to look away. "Tomorrow we reach Manchester. Tomorrow the words start moving faster than any warrant. But someone should carry that paragraph too. Someone besides me." She put her hand briefly, firmly, on his forearm, on the wheel-side arm, the driving arm. "You've carried everyone else's lives for years without acting on them. Carry this one. It's time you had one that's yours."

The sign rose out of the haze then, whitewashed brick against the moor-edge: MANCHESTER 12.

And below it, smaller, freshly painted, official:

RAILWAY AND ROAD INSPECTION POINT — BY ORDER.

Two constables. A sergeant with a clipboard. And behind them, half-hidden by the signal box, a telegraph office with its window lit, and a woman's silhouette bent over the key.

Nell saw it at the same moment he did.

"Checkpoint three," she said. "Forewarned, just like we feared." She rolled her sleeve and checked the time and there was that place-where-a-smile-goes again, arriving at last. "Good," she said. "I'd hate to arrive unrehearsed."
