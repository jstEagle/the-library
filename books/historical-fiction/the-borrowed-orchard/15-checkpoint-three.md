## 15. Checkpoint Three

The rain had followed them from Newark like a debt collector, patient and certain, and by the time the train drew into Manchester London Road the windows were running silver and the platform lamps had bloomed into halos of yellow fog. Oscar stood in the corridor with his hat pulled low, watching the crowd resolve out of the steam — porters, soldiers on leave, women with parcels — and felt the particular dread of a man arriving at a place where he is already expected.

They had known since Sheffield. A boy selling papers on the platform there had shouted something about the Home Office inquiry, about irregularities in convict records, about a manhunt — and Nell had gone very still beside him, her gloved hands folded in her lap as though she were sitting for a portrait. Then she had said, in the flat voice she used when the fear was largest: "They'll cut the wires first. Before they close the roads. That's how Roderick Vane thinks — paper first, then people."

"So we're too late," Oscar said.

"We're exactly on time," she answered. "We just have to be faster than the scissors."

Now, on the Manchester platform, Oscar counted constables the way other men counted money. Two at the barrier, checking papers with a thoroughness that had not been there on his last journey through this city. A sergeant speaking to the stationmaster, who nodded and pointed toward the telegraph office. And above it all, strung along the wall near the parcel office, a notice board he could not read from here but whose shape he knew already: a rectangle of official paper, freshly pasted, its corners still wet.

"They have my face up there," he said quietly. "Or Crane's. Possibly both."

"Then don't look at it," Nell said. "Looking is how men get caught. They turn toward their own name like sunflowers." She took his arm — a sister's gesture, rehearsed in a hundred mirrors between London and here, but her grip was real enough to leave marks. "The telegraph office. Second floor, end of the corridor. You said the clerk is a woman?"

"Alice Fairburn. Verity's letter said—" He stopped. The letter was ash now, burned unread somewhere south of Grantham inside a hymnal that had cost a decoy courier three days of liberty and perhaps more. Everything they carried now lived behind Nell's eyes, forty pages of testimony recited nightly in dark bedrooms until she could say it forward, backward, and inside out, the way other people said grace.

"Verity's letter *said*," Nell finished for him. "I know. Trust the dead letter and the living woman." She exhaled. "All right, Oscar Delacroix. Last performance of the season."

* * *

The corridor smelled of coal gas and wet wool. At the foot of the stairs Oscar made himself walk slowly — a man with no urgent business, a chauffeur seeing his sister onto her train home after a family illness, nothing in his pockets but a return ticket and a prayer. The two constables at the barrier did not look at him twice; one of them was young and bored and kept glancing at the clock, and Oscar blessed every dull hour of that young man's life.

But halfway up the stairs a voice came down the well of the staircase, official and carrying: "—the woman especially. Dark hair, speaks quick, northern vowels. She'll try the wires. Cut the office if you have to."

Oscar did not turn. He climbed.

The telegraph office occupied a narrow room lit by green-shaded lamps, and it was nearly empty at half past six in the evening — which meant either luck or trap, and Oscar had learned in nine days on the road that the two wore identical coats. Behind the counter stood a woman of perhaps forty, her sleeves pinned back from forearms built by twenty years of keys and brass, her hair escaping its knot in the way of someone who had stopped negotiating with it long ago. Her name plate read **A. FAIRBURN**, and beneath the plate, taped to the counter's edge where only a standing customer would see it, a small printed card: *VOTES FOR WOMEN* — and below that, in ink, someone had added *and everything else.*

Nell walked past him to the counter as though he were luggage.

"Miss Fairburn? My name is Eleanor Whitlock. Lady Ashcombe wrote to you in March. About a printer named Garroway, and a friend of hers who—"

"I remember what she wrote," Fairburn said. Her eyes went past Nell's shoulder to Oscar, to the door, to the clock on the wall — reading the room the way Oscar read letters, all at once and completely. Then she reached under the counter and turned something. The sound of the outer door's lock engaging was very small and very loud. "You've got perhaps ten minutes before the next shift comes up those stairs, and I'd wager my pension the men downstairs aren't here for the timetable. So." She folded her arms. "What is it you need sent, and how fast can you say it?"

Nell set both hands flat on the counter. Oscar saw her draw the breath she drew every night in their lodgings before beginning — the breath of a woman about to carry something heavier than her body was built for.

"It's long," she said. "It's forty pages long, and I have it all."

Fairburn's eyebrows rose a fraction. "In your head?"

"In my head. It was written by Edith Marchmont, who the Home Office says has been dead for two years, and who is not dead, and what happened to her in Holloway was ordered, covered over, and signed for by Sir Roderick Vane. The certificate that says she died of pneumonia is false. The doctor who signed it has confessed it in writing. There are four witnesses living, and their names are in the testimony, and every hour those names stay unwritten is an hour they can be gotten to." Nell's voice did not rise; it grew denser, like metal under compression. "I can give you the opening testimony, the certificate passage, the doctor's confession verbatim, and the four names. That's the spine of it. The rest can follow in print."

For a moment Fairburn said nothing. Then she pulled the blank telegram forms across the counter and uncapped the inkwell, and her hands — Oscar noticed this, would remember it all his life — were perfectly steady.

"Say it once slowly," she said. "I'll take it down in longhand first. Wire second. If they come through that door before the wire goes, the longhand goes into the fire and you go out the window, and there's a roof over the parcels shed that will hold a woman if not a gentleman." She glanced at Oscar without warmth. "No offense."

"None taken," Oscar said. "I fall badly."

* * *

She began.

"'On the ninth of February, nineteen eleven, I, Edith Mary Marchmont, entered Holloway as convict number such-and-such—'" Nell's voice filled the little room the way water fills a lock, level and inexorable, and Fairburn's pen chased it, page after page torn from the pad and laid in a growing stack weighted with the inkwell. Oscar stood at the door with his ear against the wood, keeping watch, and found that he could hear both things at once: the stairwell below, where boots came and went without climbing, and Nell's voice behind him, carrying Edith's words through the evening air of Manchester for the first time.

He had heard the testimony every night for a week. He knew its geography the way he knew the streets of Bloomsbury. But something happened to it in this room, spoken aloud to a stranger's moving pen. On the road it had been cargo. Here it became what it had always been: a woman's voice, dictating across two years of official silence, refusing to be dead.

"—they call it administering food. I call it being held down by four people while a tube is forced into me until I bleed. I call it happening again the next morning, and the morning after that, for nineteen days. On the twentieth day I did not wake properly. What was done to me while I did not wake was decided upon in an office, and the name on that decision—"

"The name," Fairburn repeated, writing it whole: *Sir Roderick Vane, Under-Secretary, Home Office.* She looked up. "That's Parliament, that is. That's not some matron losing her temper. That's the machine itself, signing its work."

"That's why it's never been printed," Nell said. "And why it must be tonight."

Boots on the stairs.

Oscar's hand found Nell's shoulder and pressed once — their signal, invented in a Sheffield lodging house, meaning *faster* — and Nell did not stumble, did not pause, only leaned harder into the words as Fairburn tore free the finished pages and began transferring them to wire forms with a speed that blurred the pencil. Oscar watched the stairwell door handle begin to turn.

"Miss Fairburn," he said calmly, "your window."

"Third form from the end," she muttered. "*Certificate falsified at direction of the undersigned. Cause of death recorded as pneumonia. Actual cause—*" The handle turned. The lock held it — one second, two, while the man outside tried the door and found it stubborn — "*Actual cause of collapse, force-feeding, per instruction of S.R.V., who was present on the ward the morning prior—*"

The handle rattled again, harder. A fist followed it. "Telegraph office! Open this door! Police!"

"Names," Fairburn said, pencil flying. "Give me the four names."

Nell gave them — four ordinary names, women's names, the kind of names that appear nowhere in history books and everywhere in the small print of courage — and Fairburn wrote them, sealed the final form, and ripped the whole stack free in one motion.

The key turned from outside; a second officer had evidently been fetched with a duplicate. Fairburn swept the longhand pages off the counter and thrust them at Nell. "Window. Now. Both of you."

"What about the wire?" Oscar asked.

Fairburn was already at the key, her fingers dropping into position over the brass with the unconscious certainty of a pianist. Through the frosted glass of the door, torchlight swung and doubled. "The wire," she said, "is my job, same as yours was saying it. Get on that roof."

"But they'll arrest you."

"Yes," Alice Fairburn said pleasantly, and began to transmit.

* * *

The window was narrower than hope. Nell went through it first, folding herself over the sill with a economy that spoke of practice — and later Oscar would learn what Holloway taught a body about exits — and dropped the four feet to the parcels-shed roof, landing soft as a cat in the rain. Oscar followed less gracefully, catching his coat on the latch and tearing it, hanging a full terrible second with his legs kicking over the yard before the fabric gave and he fell the rest of the way, rolling, coming up on his elbows in a puddle with the taste of copper in his mouth.

Above them, through the lit window, they could see Fairburn at the key, unhurried, transmitting. Below, in the yard, boots. Somewhere beyond the wall, the great trunk lines ran north toward Garroway's print works — and along them, dot by dash by dot, Edith Marchmont's testimony was leaving Manchester at the speed of lightning, addressed to a cellar that did not yet know it was about to become the most important room in England.

A whistle blew. Doors slammed below. The light in the telegraph window went dark — but not, Oscar swore, before the rhythm of the key had changed character: slower now, almost leisurely, a clerk finishing her correspondence before closing time.

Then the window banged open and a bundle came out into the rain — the longhand originals, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with string. Fairburn's voice, pitched to carry no farther than the roof: "Garroway will want the originals! Proof against denial! Now *run*, Mr. Chauffeur, and tell your lady the wires may be cut, but they cut slow!"

Oscar caught the bundle against his chest. Beside him Nell was already moving along the rooftop ridge, low and fast, and he scrambled after her — a stolen orchard's worth of paper hugged to his heart, pursued across the dark city by everything the paper could burn down.

Behind them, in the darkened office, he heard — or dreamed he heard — one last burst of transmission, quick and defiant as a signature.

Ahead, through the rain, the print works waited. And between them and it, filling the street at the end of the lane, the blue lanterns of police were already ringing the building like a noose drawing closed.
