## 16. The Printer's Devil

Manchester announced itself by smell before it appeared at all — coal smoke and wet wool and the river's industrial sourness — and Oscar drove into it with the windows down because Nell had gone quiet in a way that made him want the noise of the world to fill the car instead of conversation.

They had come off the last stretch of road with the hymnal gone, the decoy courier somewhere behind them in police hands or worse, and nothing between them and Manchester but a memorized testimony and a name on a scrap of paper: *J. Whitlock, Anchor Street, ask for the cellar.*

The city swallowed them whole. Tram bells, cart horses slipping on cobbles slicked with drizzle, women with shawls over their heads hurrying between mill gates. Oscar drove slowly, reading doorways, reading faces. He had learned to read faces the way he had once read letters — patiently, hungrily, looking for the sentence underneath.

"There," Nell said, pointing left past a shuttered ironmonger's. "Anchor Street."

Anchor Street was narrow and mean and ended in a wall of soot-black brick where three printing works stood shoulder to shoulder like old men leaning on each other. The middle one had no sign. Its windows were high and grimy, its door unmarked except for a brass bell-pull worn smooth as bone.

Oscar pulled up in the shadow of a gasworks wall and killed the engine. For a moment they sat listening to the tick of the cooling motor.

"You wait here," he said.

"No," Nell said.

"Nell—"

"She knows me." It came out flat and final. "Or she knew me. Holloway, before they moved me. If Edith trusted her enough to put her name in the letters, then I'm going up that step with you, and you can argue about it in the rain if you like."

He didn't like it, but he had learned something on the long road north about which battles were his. They crossed the street together, two figures in a grey afternoon, and Oscar pulled the bell.

It was a long time before anything happened. Long enough that he heard footsteps pass twice inside, unhurried, deliberate. Then the door opened six inches on a chain, and an eye regarded them through the gap — a woman's eye, sharp, sixty-odd, ringed by skin the colour of tea left standing.

"We're closed," the eye said.

"Edith Marchmont sent us," Oscar said.

The chain did not move. The eye flicked from his face to Nell's, and something happened in it — not softening exactly; more like a lock recognizing a key it had hoped was destroyed.

"Say the other thing," the woman said quietly.

Oscar glanced at Nell. Nell stepped forward. "Ask for the cellar," she said. "And tell her the words are safe even when the paper isn't."

The chain slid. The door opened onto darkness that smelled of ink and hot metal and forty years of other people's arguments.

* * *

Her name was Judith Whitlock, though everyone called her Joss, and she had been setting type since she was eleven years old and could read a galley upside-down and backwards faster than most men could read it forwards. She was small and square and moved through the dim works like someone who had memorized every obstacle years ago and resented none of them.

She led them down a stair behind the press room, down again past a coal store, into a low cellar where a hand press sat under sacking and a lamp burned behind a tin reflector. There was a cot in the corner, a kettle, a crate of type cases, and a smell of candle wax and lead.

"They took the front shop last month," she said, hanging the sacking back over the press. "Official complaint — 'obscene literature.' My literature is many things but never obscene. They're coming back for the rest of it. Everyone knows they're coming back. So we print fast and we print small and we get it out in bread carts." She looked at them both over the lamp flame. "Now say it plain. What have you got?"

"The Marchmont testimony," Oscar said. "Complete. In her own words, copied out under her direction, verified twice."

Joss went very still. Then she sat down heavily on the type case, as if her legs had made a decision without consulting her.

"Alive," she said. Not a question. A word being tested against the tongue after two years.

"Alive," Nell said. "Hiding. And hunted harder now than when they thought her dead, because what she has to say proves they lied."

Joss pressed both hands flat on her knees. When she spoke again her voice had steadied into something workmanlike. "How many words?"

Nell told him later she had never seen anyone do it before and hoped never to see anyone do it again: Nell Fairweather, standing in a lamplit cellar in Manchester, reciting eleven thousand words of testimony from memory — not performing it, not rushing it, but delivering it in the level voice of a woman dictating a laundry list, while Joss wrote it down in shorthand as fast as the pencil could travel, correcting her only once, at a date, and Nell confirming it without a pause.

It took four hours. Nobody ate. Twice Joss stopped and put her head in her hands, and once she swore with great fluency and precision at Sir Roderick Vane's name, in language that would have shocked Lady Verity Ashcombe and would not have surprised Edith Marchmont at all.

When it was done, Joss read the shorthand back line by line and Nell confirmed every page, and then Joss looked up and said, "I can set this in nine hours if I don't stop. I'll need the press lit by midnight and quiet above stairs."

"And you'll have it," Oscar said.

* * *

The trouble arrived at half past ten, in the form of a boy.

He came down the coal chute — a chimney-sweep's apprentice, twelve years old maybe, black to the elbows — and delivered his message in the bored tone of a child repeating a lesson: "Two bobbies at the corner and a plain-clothes man talking to the night watchman at the ironworks. Plain-clothes man's got a list. Watchman keeps shaking his head, but the man's writing things down anyway."

Joss cursed again, economically. "Bread carts won't be round till five. If they come before—"

"How long to set the rest?" Oscar asked.

"Four hours, if the world leaves me alone. Six if it doesn't."

Oscar stood in the middle of the cellar and felt the old familiar vertigo rise in him — the spectator's vertigo, the itch to stand aside, to let events arrange themselves around a safer man. He had spent thirty-one years believing that history was a theatre and he had bought a ticket to the gallery. But somewhere between Hertfordshire and Newark and a burned hymnal, the gallery had burned down too, and here he was on the stage whether he'd auditioned or not.

"The car," he said. "How wide is your yard?"

* * *

The yard was a narrow stone-floored court behind the works, walled on three sides, open to Anchor Street through an arch just wide enough for a motorcar driven by a man who didn't mind scraping paint. There was a stack of empty barrels, a rusting boiler plate, and one ancient delivery cart whose shafts pointed at the wall like accusations.

"If I drive loudly through it," Oscar said, "they'll hear an engine, see lights, and think the printers are loading stock for a midnight run. Every policeman in Anchor Street will come to the arch. That's four hours' worth of attention bought for the price of one noisy lap."

"And when they catch you?" Joss asked.

"I'm a chauffeur," Oscar said. "I've been lost in strange cities my whole life. I'll be looking for a garage that doesn't exist, arguing with a map, complaining about my employer. I am, as it happens, extremely good at being nobody." He heard himself say it and understood, with a small cold shock, how much he had changed to be able to say it — and how much it still cost. "Besides. They're looking for a convict and a fugitive and stolen papers. Nobody is looking for a lost chauffeur."

"Nobody was looking for me either," Nell said, "until they were." She caught his arm. Her grip was stronger than her size suggested; everything about her was stronger than suggested. "You drive one lap, you draw them, and then you park behind the gasworks and walk back through the alley. You promise me that. One lap."

"One lap," Oscar said. "Two at the outside."

"That's what men say," Nell said, "right before the outside."

* * *

At quarter past eleven, Joss lit the press furnace, and the cellar filled with a glow like a held breath. She began to set type — picking letter by letter from the case with a speed that blurred her hands into something insect-like and holy — while Nell sat beside her reading aloud from the fresh copy in a low, steady voice, and Oscar went up the coal-store steps into the yard and stood in the drizzle beside his mud-splattered car and listened to the city.

He heard them before he saw them: boots on cobble, several pairs, out of rhythm with any workingman's walk. He counted by sound — four, perhaps five, moving toward the arch from the Anchor Street end, unhurried the way men are unhurried when they believe the building cannot leave.

He got in the car. His hands were shaking, and he watched them shake with a kind of distant interest, and then he started the engine, which roared in the stone court like something waking angry.

He turned on both lamps.

He put the car into gear with a grinding clang that carried down Anchor Street like a dropped church bell, and he drove — straight across the yard, missing the boiler plate by a coat of paint, through the arch with a shriek of metal along the wing, out into Anchor Street with the throttle open and the engine screaming in second gear, lamps swinging wildly across wet brick, exhaust cracking like rifle fire between the walls.

It worked better than he had planned, and worse.

Boots broke into a run. A whistle shrieked — the rising two-note cry of a Manchester constable who has seen something worth whistling about. Oscar took the corner at the end of Anchor Street on two wheels, genuinely uncertain for one long sliding moment whether the car or he would finish upright, and came out onto the wider road by the canal with five shapes pelting after him and a sixth already running from a side street to cut him off ahead.

So much for one lap.

He swung the car around a stationary coal cart — the driver's curses following him like thrown stones — and drove *away* from the print works as hard as the machine would go, because the arithmetic was simple and brutal: every yard they chased him was a yard they weren't searching the yard behind him. He ran the length of the canal road with the pursuit strung out behind him like beads on a wire, took a left at random, a right at random, and found himself, absurdly, in a respectable crescent of tall dark houses with brass numbers and drawn curtains, where a speeding motorcar sounded like a crime in itself.

A constable stepped out from under a porch lamp and raised his arm.

Oscar stopped. He stopped so smoothly, so obediently, so instantly, that the officer actually hesitated — men expect flight or bluster, and obedience disarms them.

"Evening, officer." Oscar leaned out, letting exhaustion and grievance flood into his voice, and discovered he had a talent for it, which frightened him more than the whistle had. "Thank God. Is there a garage anywhere near here? Anywhere at all? I've been driving in circles for an hour and I've a vapour leak and my employer will have my head if the car isn't under cover before morning."

The constable peered in. Behind him, at the mouth of the crescent, two running men slowed to a walk, then another — plain-clothes, collars up, breathing hard.

"Vapour leak, is it?" the constable said.

"Smells like a distillery back seat. You're welcome to check, but stand upwind." This was a lie built on a truth, which he was learning was the strongest kind: the car did reek of petrol, because everything did.

One of the plain-clothes men reached them, bent to the window, and shone a torch across Oscar's face. Oscar held still and let himself be examined, and thought, with a clarity that felt almost peaceful: *this is the moment*. The notice with the car's plates might be in this man's pocket right now. Newark had nearly been the end of them; Manchester could be worse.

But the torch moved on — to the mud, to the torn wing, to the empty seats.

"Alone, are you?" the plain-clothes man said.

"Since Sheffield, more or less. My sister got the train home. Said she'd rather ride third class than third gear." He produced the smile Verity had once described as *that of a well-brought-up horse*, and heard the man grunt.

"Seen anyone loitering round Anchor Street way? Printers' quarter?"

"Not tonight, sir — I've been lost, which is different from loitering, though I grant they look similar from the pavement."

Something in the plain-clothes man's face twitched — annoyance, or the ghost of amusement, or simply the fatigue of a long shift hunting ghosts. "Move along, then. And find that garage."

Oscar found it. Or found a street dark enough to serve, three turns away, where he parked beneath a railway arch among costers' barrows and sat with his forehead on the wheel until his heart agreed to rejoin the century.

Then he walked back through the alleys, counting turns the way Nell had taught him — *left at the wall with the painted star, right where the cobbles give way to flags* — and let himself in through the coal chute, black to the knees, into the smell of hot lead and candle wax and safety.

* * *

The press was running.

That was the first thing he understood, before the light or the faces: the sound, a rhythmic iron heartbeat filling the cellar, and Joss Whitlock standing over it with ink to her wrists and sweat cutting channels through the grime on her face, pulling the lever, feeding sheets, pulling the lever, feeding sheets, while pages accumulated in a wooden tray like leaves after a storm.

Above them, faintly, boots crossed the floor of the dead print shop. A torch beam swept the high grimy windows of the press room and slid away. Somewhere upstairs a door opened and stayed open a long time. Nobody in the cellar stopped working.

"They came in the front while you were playing charades with the constabulary," Joss said, without pausing her rhythm. "Three of them. Poked about for twenty minutes. Found nothing but dust and a very rude note I leave out for occasions like this." Pull. Feed. Pull. "Nell kept reading to me the entire time. Never raised her voice. I've known hardened compositors weep at less."

Oscar turned. Nell sat on the cot with the copy on her knees, and she looked up at him, and for the first time since Hertfordshire she smiled — small, tired, entirely real.

"You came back," she said. "Through the coal chute. Covered in soot." She said it as though cataloguing evidence.

"It seemed the discreet entrance."

"It was the ridiculous entrance," she said. "It was perfect."

He sat down on the type case beside her, and his hands, which had been steady all evening in exactly the ways that mattered and shaking in all the others, finally went quiet.

The press beat on. Page by page the tray filled: *THE TESTIMONY OF EDITH MARCHMONT, formerly of Holloway Gaol, certified alive, concerning her death, its falsification, and those responsible.* At the foot of the first column, in type Joss had chosen herself, large enough to be read across a kitchen table by candlelight:

*Printed and published this Friday, in defiance.*

Near dawn the last sheet came off the press. Joss wiped her hands, counted the pile twice, wrapped bundles in brown paper and string, and stacked them into a bread basket, a fish crate, and a pram, all three of which, she explained, had standing engagements at first light with people who had done this before.

"Sixty copies tonight," she said. "Two hundred more by Friday noon. It'll be in London by Saturday if the trains behave, and Vane's name will be in every breakfast room in England by Sunday, and there is nothing on God's earth he can burn fast enough to stop it." She looked at the two of them, and her voice roughened for just a moment before she mastered it. "Edith always said the truth was a slow train. Well. Tonight it's an express."

Oscar slept two hours on the cot in the ink-smelling dark, and dreamed of an orchard he did not yet understand, and woke to Nell shaking his shoulder and a sound above them that did not belong to dawn: boots, many, descending the outer stair — and Joss Whitlock's voice, calm as type metal, saying from the bottom step:

"They're at the door. Both of you — chute, now, and don't look back. And take this." She thrust a single printed sheet into his hands, folded, still warm from the press. "Proof copy. Whatever happens to me, that leaves this city."

Oscar climbed into the dark of the coal chute with the warm paper against his chest — and froze, because below him, muffled by brick, he heard a voice he knew, a voice from a London drawing room and a circulated notice and a Newark checkpoint, saying pleasantly:

"Judith Whitlock? Inspector Hale. We'd like a word about your recent order of newsprint."
