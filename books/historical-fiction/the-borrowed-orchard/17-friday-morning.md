## 17. Friday Morning

The presses of the *Northern Clarion* began to turn at four minutes past midnight, and Oscar Delacroix, standing in the ink-and-lampblack dark of the cellar on Tib Street, felt the floor shudder under his boots like the deck of a ship leaving harbour.

He had imagined this moment a hundred times since Newark — in the driver's seat with Nell asleep beside him, in the doorway of cheap lodgings, once, absurdly, in a dream where the letters themselves grew wings. In every imagining there had been trumpets, or at least a cheer. There was no cheer now. There was only Mr. Aldous Wren, compositor and printer's devil and — as it turned out — an old comrade of Edith Marchmont's from her days speaking on Hammersmith Green, hunched over the stone setting type by gaslight, his fingers moving with the blind certainty of a man who had been doing this since he was nine years old.

"Hold that lamp steady," Wren said, not looking up.

Oscar held it.

The type was set. The forme was locked. Somewhere above them, the police who had surrounded the print works earlier that evening had withdrawn — called away, Wren said, by a report of suffragists smashing windows on Deansgate, which report Nell had invented out of whole cloth from the telephone box at the corner while Oscar sat in the car outside with his heart hammering against his ribs like a bird in a crate. A diversion within a diversion. The girl memorized testimonies; apparently she also memorized how men in authority liked their crises arranged.

"Stand back," Wren said.

The press caught. Paper fed through with a sound like a long exhale, and then the first copy came off the drum, warm and slightly damp, and Wren took it in both hands the way one takes a newborn or a wounded bird.

He read it standing. All of it. The cellar was loud with the clatter of the machine but Oscar watched the old man's lips move through the passages — the hunger strike, the force-feeding, the name of Sir Roderick Vane set in black and white and ten thousand copies, the falsified certificate, the words *I am not dead, whatever the Home Office may prefer* — and when Wren reached the end his eyes were wet and his hands were shaking, and he pressed the paper flat against his chest as though it might fly away.

"Two years," he said. "Two years we printed her obituary in our hearts, lad. And here she is." He thrust the first copy into Oscar's hands. "You take it. First fruits go to the ones who carried the harvest."

Oscar took it. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

* * *

By six o'clock the bundles were gone — wrapped in brown paper marked as calendar stock, loaded onto three different carts going three different directions, because Wren had learned long ago never to trust all his eggs to one road. By seven, newsboys in Piccadilly Gardens were selling copies openly, the testimony splashed across page two under a headline Wren himself had composed: *THE WOMAN THE HOME OFFICE KILLED ON PAPER.*

By eight, it was in London.

Oscar learned this at half past eight, standing in the yard behind the print works with a mug of tea he couldn't drink, listening to the wireless-less silence of a city waking up to itself. Nell came out to him with the morning's second edition folded under her arm — Wren had run another five thousand before dawn, and would run more — and she didn't say anything at first. She just stood beside him, shoulder nearly touching his shoulder, and let him look at the thing they had done.

A man from the *Manchester Guardian* had come at seven, hat in hand, asking questions so fast they tripped over each other. A woman from a wire service had asked if Miss Marchmont could be reached for comment. Wren had said, with magnificent blandness, that the lady's whereabouts were known to God and to no one else.

"They'll reprint it," Nell said. "The London papers. Even the respectable ones. They can't help themselves — a dead woman writing letters is better than any murder."

"You sound pleased."

"I am pleased." She looked up at him. In the grey light her face was thinner than it had been a week ago, and harder, and younger all at once. "I'm also frightened, Oscar. Both things can be true at once. I learned that in Holloway."

He wanted to say something worthy of the moment. What came out was: "Do you think she knows? Edith. Wherever she is. Do you think she knows it's done?"

Nell considered this with the seriousness she gave everything.

"She'll know," she said finally. "Women like us have a way of knowing. It's the only newspaper we've ever been able to rely on."

* * *

In London, at that same hour, Lady Verity Ashcombe sat in the morning room of her house in Belgravia with the *Clarion* open on her knees and her hands trembling so badly she could barely hold the pages flat.

She had sent Mrs. Prewitt out at seven for every copy the corner shop possessed. The shopkeeper, a small ferret-faced man who had sold Verity her newspapers for eleven years without once meeting her eye, had leaned across the counter and said, quietly, "There's a copy put by for you, my lady. No charge. My sister were in Holloway, year before last." And Mrs. Prewitt, who cried perhaps twice a decade, had cried in the cab coming home.

Now Verity read aloud.

Crane lay on the daybed by the window, propped on pillows, still gaunt as famine but with colour in his face at last and the fever burned down to embers. He had insisted on being moved downstairs for this. He had shaved, badly, with a shaking hand. He wore a collar. She understood what the effort cost him and loved him for it, in the fierce impersonal way one loves a soldier home from a war one did not fight.

"Read it again," he said. "The part about the certificate."

"*'I was declared dead on the fourteenth of March, 1911,'*" Verity read, "*'by order of Sir Roderick Vane, Under-Secretary at the Home Office, whose signature appears on a death certificate naming a cause of death I did not suffer and a date on which I was, by the grace of God and the courage of Tobias Crane, alive in a hay cart outside Wakefield. I have seen the document. I am told it is filed among the records of the Prison Commission as neatly as you please. I died of cardiac exhaustion following forcible feeding, says the paper. I say I was beaten, starved, tube-fed until I vomited blood, and when my body began to fail they found it more convenient to bury me than to answer for me.'*"

Crane had closed his eyes. Tears ran down into the hollows of his cheeks and he did not wipe them away.

"The hay cart," he whispered. "She remembered the hay cart."

"She remembers everything," Verity said. Her voice cracked on the word and she let it. "That is rather the point of her, Tobias."

Outside, Belgravia went about its morning — carriage wheels, a milk cart, the clipped voices of servants — utterly unaware, or pretending to be, that the ground had shifted beneath its paving stones. But Verity could feel it shifting. She had spent thirty years in the company of powerful men and she knew the particular weather they made when a scandal broke: the sudden courtesy, the urgent telegrams, the knives coming out in drawing rooms and club smoking rooms, everyone scrambling to establish that they had known nothing, suspected nothing, approved of nothing — and above all, that someone else must be blamed.

Vane would fall. That much was certain now; the *Clarion* had been picked up by the London dailies — she'd had a telephone call from a journalist at the *Daily Chronicle* before nine — and a falsified death certificate was not an embarrassment a government could survive quietly. Questions would be asked in the House. Vane would resign, or be pushed, and the machinery would grind on.

But Vane was not alone. Men like Roderick Vane never travelled alone, and men like him, when cornered, did not die cleanly. They lashed. They reached.

"Verity." Crane's eyes were open again, fixed on the ceiling. "When they come — and they will come — you must be ready to give them me."

"Don't talk nonsense."

"I'm serious." He struggled upright against the pillows, and the effort left him breathing hard, but his gaze was level. "They'll trace the letters. They'll trace Crane the smuggler. I've been running two years, my lady; I know the arithmetic. If they find me here, in your house, then your name goes into their files alongside mine, and your work dies with your reputation. Better they find me at a lodging house in Camden with a fever and no friends."

"You are not leaving this bed," Verity said, "and you are certainly not leaving this house, and if Roderick Vane's people want you they will have to come through Mrs. Prewitt, who has already threatened one of them with a poker and meant it." She refolded the newspaper with unnecessary crispness. "We did not carry this truth across half of England so that I could begin surrendering people now."

Crane looked at her for a long moment. Then, slowly, he smiled — the first real smile she had seen on him, and it transformed his ruined face into something almost boyish.

"Edith used to say that," he said. "Almost word for word. 'We do not begin surrendering people.'"

"No?" Verity raised an eyebrow, though her throat was tight. "Then perhaps, Mr. Crane, you should introduce us properly when this is over. It seems she and I have a great deal in common."

* * *

The news spread the way fire spreads through dry grass — too fast to map, too fast to stop.

By noon, the story led three London papers. By two, a junior minister had been dispatched to the House to make "preliminary remarks" so wooden that even his supporters winced. By three, the word *VANE* was chalked on walls in Whitechapel and shouted by newsboys from Charing Cross to Kensington. By four, Sir Roderick Vane had issued a statement through the Home Office describing the published testimony as "a fabrication assembled by criminal elements for propagandist purposes" — a statement so transparently desperate that it was quoted everywhere precisely because no one believed a syllable of it.

And by five, in a quiet room above a milliner's shop in a town whose name appeared on no itinerary, Edith Marchmont read her own words in someone else's newspaper, and laughed, and wept, and asked the milliner's astonished daughter to fetch her coat, because she had letters to write and a country to face, and she intended to do both standing up.

* * *

Oscar heard none of this until evening. What he got instead was a telephone call routed through three exchanges and delivered in a whisper by a woman with a Manchester accent, passing along a message from London: *Tell the driver and the girl: well done, come home, and come carefully.*

He stood in the passage of Wren's house with the receiver at his ear and Nell watching him, and after he hung up neither of them spoke for a moment.

"Well done," Nell repeated softly. "Come home. Come carefully." She smiled without much humour. "Three instructions, and the last one cancels the comfort of the other two."

"He means the roads," Oscar said. "Checkpoints. They'll want to catch us coming back — easier to make us disappear on a road between towns than in a city full of witnesses."

"Then we don't go back the way we came."

"No," Oscar agreed. "We don't."

But even as he said it, some older instinct — the instinct of a man who had spent years reading dangerous mail and learning exactly how power moved when it was frightened — whispered to him that roads were the least of it. Vane's statement that afternoon had named no names. That was the frightening part. A man certain of his position blusters and threatens. A man preparing a trap stays silent, and lets you come to him.

* * *

Friday night, London emptied its pockets of the story and found it was not finished with them.

At the Gaiety Theatre, an actress stopped mid-scene and announced to a stunned audience that the woman the government had buried alive on paper was alive in fact, and got a five-minute ovation for the news. At a meeting in Hackney, three thousand people passed a resolution demanding Vane's resignation before the chairman had finished proposing it. At the Home Office, lights burned late in offices that preferred darkness, and clerks who had spent two years filing their consciences in locked drawers began, very quietly, to remember things — dates, signatures, the smell of the incinerator on the night the Marchmont file was thinned.

And in Belgravia, Verity Ashcombe stood at her window past midnight, watching the street, with the newspaper folded on the table behind her and Crane asleep upstairs, and counted the cost the way she had learned to count it: not in money, but in names.

Tomorrow, or the day after, the reprisal would begin. Not against Vane — Vane was finished, and the vultures knew it. Against the carriers. Against the little radical weekly that had dared to print; against the journalists who had amplified it; against the women who had marched and the men who had helped; and against, somewhere down the list, a chauffeur and a governess and a dying smuggler and an old lady who should have known better and was glad she hadn't.

Let them come, she thought. Let them bring their warrants and their plainclothes men and their careful legal knives. The words were out. You cannot arrest ten thousand copies. You cannot subpoena a memory. You cannot re-dead a woman the whole nation has watched stand up out of her grave.

She drew the curtain, and in the same hour, forty miles north of Manchester, a police superintendent received a telegram marked *URGENT AND CONFIDENTIAL*, read it twice, and gave orders for the Manchester railway station to be watched from first light — not for a woman, and not for a smuggler, but for a motor car, registration noted in a neat clerk's hand, last seen heading north.

The net was already closing. It just hadn't said so yet.
