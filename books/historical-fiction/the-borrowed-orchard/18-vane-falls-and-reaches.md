## 18. Vane Falls, and Reaches

The news came to them the way weather comes — first a change in the light, then the wind, then the rain itself.

Oscar heard it from the bootboy at the Manchester print works, who had run three streets through the drizzle to find him where he stood outside a tobacconist's window, pretending to read the headlines while he watched the door of the *Northern Clarion*'s office across the road. The Friday edition had been out for six days now. Six days in which the words Nell had carried in her skull like contraband had passed from one pair of hands to another — printer to newsagent to railway bookstall to drawing room — until they were no longer property at all but air, and no warrant on earth could recall them.

"Sir Roderick Vane's resigned," the boy panted. "This morning. There's a statement in Parliament. They're saying the death certificate was forged, saying there'll be an inquiry." He caught his breath, and his face did something complicated — triumph and fear sharing the same small space. "And sir — they're saying he's issued warrants."

Oscar paid him a shilling and did not move for a long moment after the boy's footsteps faded.

He had imagined this hour so many times during the drive north that it should have felt familiar when it arrived, the way a room feels familiar when you finally enter it after years of picturing it. He had pictured Vane's fall as a kind of sunrise. He had not pictured the shadow that comes attached to every sunrise, stretching long and pointing straight back at whoever stands in its path.

*Warrants.*

* * *

Nell was at Mrs. Kettering's boarding house, third floor, the room with the view over the canal, and she was mending a stocking when he came through the door without knocking. She looked up. She had learned, in the weeks since Newark, to read his face the way he had once read other people's letters — quickly, completely, and without permission.

"How bad?" she said.

"He's resigned. The certificate is public. There's talk of an inquiry."

She set down the stocking and the needle both, carefully, as if they might be needed later as evidence. "And?"

"And he's reached. Warrants. I don't know yet whose names are on them."

"You know whose names are on them."

Yes. He knew. He had known since the moment the boy said the word *warrants*, because Oscar Delacroix had spent eleven years reading the correspondence of powerful people and had learned the grammar of their vengeance: they never pursued the famous first. Lady Verity Ashcombe was untouchable — too visible, too well-connected, too useful as a martyr-in-waiting. Edith Marchmont was now too famous to touch quietly; arresting her would only feed the scandal. But a chauffeur? A chauffeur was a loose thread, and men like Roderick Vane did not leave loose threads. They pulled them, or they cut them, and either way the thread did not get a say in the matter.

"We leave tonight," Nell said. It was not a question and not quite a suggestion. It was the voice she used when she had already decided something and was informing the world of it.

"No," Oscar said.

Her chin came up. In the grey light from the window her face looked carved, all planes and decision. "Don't you dare tell me—"

"Not we," he said. "You."

* * *

They argued for two hours, and it was the best argument of his life, because it was the first argument he had ever had that mattered, and because she fought him not out of stubbornness — though there was plenty of that — but out of arithmetic. She counted what each of them carried. He was a man; he could travel with less suspicion. She was known to the Holloway authorities by sight, by name, by file; if they took her, the force-feeding would resume or worse, and no newspaper scandal would stop it this time, because scandals were perishable things and this one would be a week old by then. He counted back. His name was on the motor car's registration. His face had been seen at two checkpoints by officers who now had every reason to remember it. The car itself was a signature written in petrol and steel, and signatures could be traced.

"The car," she said suddenly, and stopped, and he watched the thought arrive behind her eyes like a train pulling into a station. "They'll trace the plates. That's how they'll do it. Not witnesses — plates. A man reported a Daimler behaving strangely at the Hertfordshire checkpoint, and someone wrote down a number, and that number is sitting on Sir Roderick Vane's desk right now with your name underneath it."

"Yes."

"So you can't drive anywhere. You can't keep the car. You can't—" She stopped again. He saw her arrive at the rest of it, the part he had been carrying since the tobacconist's window, and her face went very still. "You're not planning to run at all."

"I'm planning to stand still," Oscar said. "There's a difference. It took me thirty-four years to learn it."

* * *

He spent the afternoon preparing, and found that preparation, done properly, was its own strange comfort — the same comfort he had once taken in polishing the Daimler's bonnet until it held the sky. Money first: he drew out everything he had, forty-one pounds saved across eleven years of wages and tips and the small economies of a man who wanted nothing, plus the two hundred Verity had wired north the day the testimony printed, earmarked for exactly this contingency. He wrapped the notes in oilcloth and sewed them into the lining of Nell's coat with a sailmaker's stitch Crane had taught him before the fever took the old smuggler's hands steady — one of the few lessons Crane had ever given that required no confession attached.

Then the letter.

Edith's final letter had come to them through the telegraph clerk at Manchester Piccadilly, forwarded by the printer, written in a hand that shook now but was unmistakably alive. Verity had sent it north with a note: *She asked that this go to the two of you together. She says you will understand why when you read it.* Oscar had read it once, alone, standing in the boarding house corridor, and had put it away unread since, because some documents are not meant to be read twice until the moment demands it.

Now the moment demanded it. He laid it on the table between himself and Nell, and they read it side by side, her finger under the lines because her eyes still tired easily since Holloway, and the letter said:

*I am told there will be reprisals, and I am told they will fall on the small people before the large ones, because that is how power has always shot — low first, like a hunter clearing brush. So let me say this plainly, to whichever of you reads it. If any of you is taken, you are not evidence against the others. You are the reason the others are free. There is no shame in being caught carrying truth; the shame belongs entirely to those who made truth contraband. And to the chauffeur — Verity tells me you believed history happened to other people. Tell her she is wrong about you, even if you are not yet wrong about yourself. History does not happen to spectators. It happens through them.*

Nell read it twice. Then she folded it along its existing creases and pushed it back across the table toward him.

"She means it for you," she said.

"It says *whichever of you*."

"It says *to the chauffeur*. Don't split hairs with a dying woman's handwriting, Oscar, it's beneath you." She stood, walked to the window, stood with her back to him looking down at the canal where a barge was sliding through the evening drizzle with its lamp already lit. When she spoke again her voice had changed register — lower, flatter, the voice she used for the stories that cost her most. "You know what I did for two years after Holloway? Nothing. That was the sentence, though they called it release. I sat in my sister's front room in Leeds and I knitted, and every knock at the door was the police, and every silence was the police, and I learned that a person can be invisible and imprisoned at the same time. Invisible isn't safe. Invisible is just a smaller cell." She turned around. "I got out of that cell because other people stood up and were counted — loudly, stupidly, wonderfully. You drove me two hundred miles past three checkpoints. You don't get to hand me your share of the danger now like a gentleman handing down from a carriage. We are not gentry, either of us. We carry our own bags."

"I know," Oscar said. "That's why you take the money and the coat and the morning train to Edinburgh, where Crane's cousin keeps a room, and you stay there until Verity's lawyers say the water's calm. And I stay here, because here is where they will come, and if they come and find the house empty they will widen the search, and widened searches catch governesses." He met her eyes. "You taught me something on the road through Hertfordshire, Nell. You said complicity isn't only doing wrong. It's standing comfortably aside while wrong is done. Well. I'm done standing aside. This is what standing *in* looks like. It doesn't look like much. It looks like a man waiting in a mews. But it's the whole distance I've travelled, and I'll not have you talk me out of arriving."

She looked at him for a long time. Outside, the barge hooted once, mournful, and slid out of sight under the bridge.

"You memorized nothing," she said at last. "You have nothing on you. No letters, no papers, nothing they can use to build a conspiracy charge."

"Nothing," he agreed. "There is nothing left to find. Let them search. Let them turn out my pockets and read my notebook and question me until Thursday. Everything they want is already in ten thousand hands. I'm just the man who carried it, and carrying isn't a crime yet, whatever Vane wishes."

"And if it becomes one?"

"Then Verity's lawyers earn their fees."

It was not courage, quite, what he felt saying it. It was closer to relief — the relief of a man setting down a weight he had carried so long he had mistaken it for his own body. For eleven years he had kept himself light, unencumbered, portable, ready to slip away from any scene before it became his scene. Now he was choosing heaviness. Choosing to be findable. It felt, absurdly, like coming home.

* * *

They parted at half past five in the morning, on the platform at Manchester London Road, in the smell of coal smoke and wet wool. He had bought her a third-class ticket to Edinburgh and a paper of sandwiches and, on impulse, a single white chrysanthemum from the flower stall, because it was the sort of foolish thing he suspected people in found families did, and he was done denying himself membership.

"For the record," she said, taking the flower and tucking it into her buttonhole with great dignity, "I think this is a mistake."

"For the record," he said, "so do I. Do it anyway."

She laughed — a short, cracked laugh, the first he'd heard from her since Newark — and then, abruptly, she hugged him. It lasted four seconds. He counted them, because he intended to keep them.

"If they hurt you," she said into his shoulder, "I will testify. I will stand up in whatever court they invent and I will tell them everything, and I am very good at telling things now. You made sure of that."

"That," said Oscar, "is the nicest threat anyone has ever made me."

The whistle blew. She stepped back, adjusted her hat, became in three seconds an ordinary woman travelling alone, unremarkable, invisible by choice rather than by sentence — and then, just before she turned, she looked at him once more, and her eyes said what her mouth wouldn't in a public place: *come back to us*. He nodded. The nod said: *I'll try*. Both of them knew that nods were not contracts. She boarded. The train pulled out. He stood on the platform until the last carriage cleared the bridge, and then he turned and walked back into the city to wait for the future, which he now understood always arrived on foot, wearing plain clothes, asking questions.

* * *

London received him two days later with fog and indifference. He left the Daimler — he could not bring himself to sell it, and selling it would only delay discovery by a week — parked in the Ashcombe mews under its canvas cover, and took a room above a chandler's shop in Lambeth where the landlady asked no questions because he overpaid her not to. He wrote to Verity. He wrote to Edith, care of the orchard address, a letter that said very little and meant a great deal. He waited.

They came on the fourth evening, at dusk, when the fog was thick enough to soften the gaslamps into haloes and the chandler's shop below had gone dark. Two men. He heard them on the stairs — heavy, unhurried, the tread of men doing routine work, which was somehow worse than urgency. He did not reach for the window. He did not blow out the candle. He sat down in the chair by the cold fireplace, folded his hands, and waited for the knock with the peculiar calm of a man who has rehearsed a scene so long that its arrival feels like a cue.

The knock came. Three blows, official, patient.

"Mr. Oscar Delacroix?" Through the door, a voice like wet gravel.

"Yes," said Oscar, to the closed door, to the two men behind it, to the whole apparatus of the state that had spent two years declaring a living woman dead. "Come in. I've been expecting you."

He rose and opened the door himself, standing squarely in the frame, letting them see him clearly — his face, his name, his whole unhidden self — and thought, with a clarity that felt almost like joy: *Let the record show I was home.*

The taller of the two men produced a paper. "Oscar Delacroix, I have a warrant for your arrest."

"May I see it?" And when the man handed it over, Oscar read it slowly, thoroughly, the way he had once read other people's letters — and found, in the final line, the name signed at the bottom, and smiled at it without warmth. "Sir Roderick Vane," he said. "Resigned on Tuesday. Arresting people on Saturday. Some men don't know how to leave a room."

"Are you going to come quietly, or—"

"I've been quiet my entire life," Oscar said, reaching for his coat. "You're a little late for quiet. But yes. I'll come."

* * *

They did not handcuff him, which he chose to take as a compliment. They walked him down the fog-bound street to a black motor car of their own — a Wolseley, government issue, he noted professionally, decent engine, poor suspension — and as they helped him in he looked back once at the chandler's darkened window, at the ordinary world going about its extraordinary business, at the country that had woken on Friday morning to read a dead woman's testimony in its tea leaves and could never fully close its eyes again.

The car pulled away into the fog. Somewhere ahead of him was a cell, a trial, an uncertain number of years. Somewhere behind him was a kitchen table in Belgravia where a housekeeper had once poured tea for a feverish smuggler and a fugitive governess and a chauffeur who was learning, late and imperfectly, to count himself among the living. Somewhere north, a woman with a white chrysanthemum dried between the pages of a hymnal was waiting for news.

He settled back against the seat. The fog swallowed the lamps one by one.

Whatever came next, he would not be watching it from the wings.
