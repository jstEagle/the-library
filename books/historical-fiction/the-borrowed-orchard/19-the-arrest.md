## 19. The Arrest

They came for him on a Tuesday, in the grey hour when the mews still smelled of last night's coal and the horses next door were stamping the frost from their hooves.

Oscar had known they would come since Friday morning, when the *Northern Voice* hit the streets and the testimony of Edith Marchmont — dead woman, living witness — spread through London like ink through water. He had known it with the particular clarity of a man who has stopped trying to be elsewhere. For years he had prided himself on reading a room from the doorway, on knowing which conversations to be near and which to drift away from, on the fine art of being present and unremarked. Now he sat at the kitchen table in Belgravia with his hands wrapped around a cup of tea going cold, and he waited the way other men waited for a train: patiently, with his bag already packed.

Mrs. Prewitt moved about the kitchen behind him, doing things that did not need doing. She had polished a counter that was already clean. She had straightened the chairs. Twice she had opened her mouth to speak and closed it again, and Oscar understood that she was fighting the same argument he had already won inside himself.

"You could take the motor," she said at last. "You could be in Liverpool by dark. Mr. Crane knows people in—"

"Mr. Crane knows people everywhere," Oscar said gently, "and half of them are watched by now. And if I run, then I'm a fugitive, and everything Verity says on my behalf becomes a lie." He turned the cup a quarter-turn on its saucer. "They'll want someone. If it isn't me, it's her. Or you."

"Me." Mrs. Prewitt made the word sound like an insult.

"You let a dying man sleep in your spare room for a week. You boiled bandages. You lied to two plainclothes officers about a fever you called rheumatism." He almost smiled. "You're as guilty as any of us, Mrs. Prewitt. The difference is that nobody will ever look twice at a housekeeper. They'll look at me. So let them."

She set down her cloth with more force than the task required. "Your tea's gone cold," she said, and poured him a fresh one anyway, and that — he thought later — was as close to a benediction as either of them would ever come out and say aloud.

The knock came at nine minutes past seven. Not the service entrance; the front door, the one used by Members of Parliament and bishops and the occasional duke. Three measured raps, brass knocker, no haste. Men who are certain of their business do not hurry.

Oscar set down the cup, buttoned his coat to the throat, and went to answer it himself, because it seemed important — it seemed like the whole point, really — that no one should open this door for him but him.

* * *

There were four of them. Two in uniform, caps dripping from the fog, and two in the grey civilian suits that fooled no one in Belgravia and were not meant to. The tall one held a folded paper. Oscar recognized the typeface before he recognized the words; it was the same notice that had circulated to every constabulary between here and Manchester, describing a Wolseley motorcar, registration and all. He had driven those plates through three checkpoints under that description. It seemed only fitting that it should follow him home like a dog.

"Oscar Delacroix?" the tall one said. Though he must have known. They always knew. Knowing was the whole of their profession; the arrest was merely the paperwork afterward.

"Yes."

"Oscar Delacroix, chauffeur, in the employ of Lady Verity Ashcombe, of this address?"

"For six years," Oscar said. "Though I suppose that's ended now."

The officer blinked. He had prepared, clearly, for flight or denial or some combination of the two — the bolt for the back stairs, the sudden illness, the desperate invention. He had not prepared for a man answering questions like a man confirming a dinner reservation.

"You are detained on suspicion of conveying seditious material in violation of the—"

"The Defence of the Realm Act," Oscar said. "I know the one. Shall we do this properly? You'll want to search me, and then the mews, and then the house, though the house is Lady Ashcombe's and I'd advise you to wait for her lawyer unless you enjoy explaining yourselves to Sir Somebody-or-other over sherry."

Something flickered across the tall officer's face — irritation, mostly, but beneath it something that might have been respect, grudging and unwelcome. He gestured, and the two uniforms came up the steps.

"I have nothing on me," Oscar said, spreading his arms slightly, palms out. "Search whatever you like. There's nothing left to find. Everything we carried north is either printed or burned, and what's printed cannot be unprinted, gentlemen, no matter how many chauffeurs you collect." He heard his own voice, level and clear, and marveled at it. Somewhere in Hertfordshire a bored sergeant had waved them through; somewhere outside Newark a sharp-eyed inspector had bent over forged papers while Oscar's heart hammered like a fist on a door. That man — the man who had sweated through checkpoints, who had invented a dying aunt with Nell in a single breath — that man had been terrified. This one was not. It occurred to him, standing on the step with his arms half-raised, that fear had been the price of invisibility, and that having paid it so long, he had simply stopped owing it.

The uniforms patted him down with brisk, apologetic thoroughness. They found his watch, his wallet, three shillings and sixpence, and a ticket stub from Euston that he had kept without meaning to — the decoy courier's platform ticket, a relic of the hymnal and its burning. One of them turned it over, frowned, handed it to the tall one.

"What's this?"

"A train ticket," Oscar said. "From October. I keep my pockets tidier than you keep your files, evidently, or you'd know I've been to Manchester and back twice since then."

The tall one's jaw tightened. "You're very free with your tongue, Mr. Delacroix."

"I've been told it's my best feature." He stepped back into the hall to fetch his hat and gloves from the peg — deliberately, unhurriedly, making them wait — and it was there, in the narrow dimness of the hallway that smelled of beeswax and Verity's lilies, that he allowed himself one breath with his eyes closed. Just one. Six years of other people's letters, other people's lives, other people's secrets carried like contraband behind his own quiet face — and now the bill had arrived, itemized and stamped, and he found, pressing his thumb once against the cool marble of the hall table, that he was glad. Not happy. Glad. There was a difference, and the difference was everything.

He put on his hat and went out to meet his history.

* * *

They walked him down the steps and along the pavement toward a black motorvan idling at the corner, and it was on that stretch of perhaps forty yards that Belgravia came out to watch.

Fog or no fog, the arrest of anyone connected to the Marchmont affair was news by Tuesday morning; the papers had been shrieking about Vane's falsified certificate for four days, and Parliament had spent the weekend in a state of undignified uproar. So there were faces at windows, and a knot of shop-girls by the corner, and — because Mrs. Prewitt had slipped out twenty minutes earlier with instructions Oscar had never given but somehow known she would follow — a reporter from the *Daily Citizen* loitering with admirable fake casualness beside a lamppost, notebook already open.

And there was Lady Verity Ashcombe on her own front steps, in a grey walking dress and no hat, which for Verity was the equivalent of arriving in battle armor.

She descended the steps at a pace that made the tall officer instinctively straighten — rank recognizes rank even when it disapproves of it — and planted herself directly in Oscar's path, so that the procession had to stop.

"Lady Ashcombe—" the officer began.

"Mr. Delacroix," Verity said, ignoring him entirely, taking both of Oscar's hands in hers, "has been in my employment for six years, during which time he drove me to my office, drove me home from my office, and asked no questions beyond the price of petrol. Whatever papers were conveyed north last week were conveyed at my instruction, under my authority, and for my purposes. I composed the arrangement, I engaged the persons involved, and I accept the entirety of the responsibility for it." Her voice carried the way a bell carries — not loud, but impossible to talk over. "If any man in this city imagines that a domestic employee can be prosecuted for doing what I ordered done while I stand here untouched, he is welcome to try, and I promise him a trial that will be remembered longer than the war we're all pretending isn't coming."

"Lady Ashcombe, this is a police matter—"

"Everything is a police matter now, Inspector. That is rather the point of the articles your masters are so anxious about." She squeezed Oscar's hands, hard, once — a message in a code only the two of them spoke, six years of silent exchanges compressed into a grip — and released him. "Go with them, Oscar. Stand up straight. Say as little as possible and mean less. And when they ask you who wrote the letters—" her chin lifted, and for one unguarded moment he saw not the patroness, not the suffragist grandee, but the woman who had sat white-faced at her own breakfast table confirming a dead friend's handwriting — "tell them Edith Marchmont wrote them. Tell them a dead woman wrote them, and that it took the whole machinery of His Majesty's Government to silence her, and that it failed."

Then she turned on her heel and went back up the steps without looking at the officers again, and the door closed behind her with the soft, final click of a well-made thing.

The reporter by the lamppost was writing so fast his pencil nearly tore the page.

* * *

The van smelled of wet wool and old rope. They put him on a bench facing backward, which he supposed was standard procedure, and the tall officer sat opposite with the file on his knees, studying Oscar with the expression of a chess player confronting a move in a game he thought he understood.

"You don't seem frightened, Mr. Delacroix."

Oscar considered the question honestly, which surprised him. Outside the small barred window, London slid by in ribbons of fog — a milk cart, a lamplighter snuffing the night's last flame, a boy hauling a handcart of cabbages who would read about this arrest in tomorrow's edition and never know he'd been passed on the road by its subject. History, happening to everyone, all the time, whether or not anyone deigned to watch.

"I was frightened for weeks," Oscar said. "On the road, especially. At Newark I couldn't feel my hands. But fright is for things that might go wrong, and this—" he gestured at the cell-like interior of the van, the rope hooks, the file, the officer himself — "this has gone wrong already. Or right. I haven't decided which. Either way, it's finished happening, and there's a strange peace in that."

"They'll want you to name names. The governess. The printer. The woman."

"There is no governess," Oscar said. "There is no printer. And the woman is famous now, Inspector, which makes naming her the least dangerous thing I could possibly do. Ask me who wrote the letters and I'll tell you gladly: Edith Marchmont wrote the letters. She wrote them from a place where you people said she was dead. Everything after that is your problem, not mine."

The officer looked at him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he closed the file.

"My sister's in the movement," he said, to the window rather than to Oscar. "My wife thinks she's at a typing course in Brighton." He said nothing else for the rest of the journey, and neither did Oscar, and the fog scrolled past the little window like a page being slowly turned.

* * *

They processed him at the station with the usual rituals — the desk sergeant, the ledger, the surrender of shoelaces and belt and watch, the small indignities administered with institutional politeness. His cell was three paces by seven, whitewashed, with a cot, a stool, and a window set too high to see anything but sky. The sky, that first evening, was the color of pewter going to rose, and a line of starlings crossed it in a single fluid ribbon, thousands of them turning as one body, and Oscar lay on the cot with his hands behind his head and watched them until the light failed.

He thought he would be afraid in the night. He had budgeted for it — the dark, the lock, the enormous weight of doors closing on schedules not his own. But what came instead, around two in the morning, was memory, arriving not as torment but as inventory: Tobias Crane's rain-soaked face at the service entrance, asking for *the woman who keeps Edith's letters*. Mrs. Prewitt boiling bandages and pretending not to weep. Nell, in the lamplight of the moving car, reciting another woman's suffering from memory because paper could be burned and flesh, it turned out, could not — not the part of her that mattered. Verity's hands around his. The telegraph clerk in Manchester, transmitting testimony down a wire with shaking fingers and a steady voice. The compositor setting type by candlelight in a cellar while Oscar's engine roared above like a dragon bought and paid for.

None of them alone. That was the discovery he kept making, over and over, each time fresh. He had spent six years believing that safety was solitude, that the spectator's seat was the only one that didn't get splintered. And it had taken a dead woman's handwriting and a gaunt stranger's fever and a governess with a steel spine and a housekeeper's cold tea to teach him the opposite: that the seat that doesn't get splintered is also the seat where nothing happens, and that everything worth having — the testimony, the truth, the terrible bright fact of Edith Marchmont alive — had been carried the whole way by people holding pieces of it for each other.

He slept, eventually, better than he had slept in years.

In the morning, the warder brought the tray and, with it, tucked beneath the bread in defiance of several regulations, a newspaper folded small. The *Daily Citizen*. Page three. OSCAR DELACROIX, CHAUFFEUR, TAKEN IN BELGRAVIA ARREST — LADY ASHCOMBE CLAIMS SOLE RESPONSIBILITY: "PROSECUTE ME," SAYS PEER'S WIDOW.

And below that, in smaller type, a paragraph he read four times, his tea cooling untouched:

*Sources close to the inquiry report that Miss Edith Marchmont — whose survival has scandalized the Home Office since Friday — may appear publicly this week. A mass rally is reported to be assembling on Saturday.*

Saturday. Two days. He pressed the paper flat against his knee and read the paragraph a fifth time, and somewhere above his head, beyond the high window, the starlings were surely turning, all together, all at once.

Edith was coming out of the dark. And Oscar Delacroix, prisoner number 4471, began — quietly, carefully, so as not to be overheard through three feet of whitewashed wall — to laugh.
