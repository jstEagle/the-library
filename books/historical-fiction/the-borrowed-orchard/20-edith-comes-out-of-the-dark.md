## 20. Edith Comes Out of the Dark

The cell had taught Oscar the grammar of sound.

In nine days he had learned to read Brixton Prison through its noises alone: the iron tread of the night warder at half past eleven, the cough of the man in the next cell — a forger, they said, though Oscar suspected him of nothing worse than arithmetic — the groan of pipes that woke before the men did, as if even the plumbing resented the hour. He had learned which silences meant danger and which meant mercy. The silence of a governor deciding your fate was different from the silence of a warder who had decided to look away.

He was learning still. He expected to have time to become fluent.

They had taken his collar stud, his bootlaces, his watch — Verity's watch, engraved with a foxglove on the case — but they could not take the habit of his mind, which insisted on ordering things. So each morning he made a ledger of what he knew. *The testimony printed Friday.* That was the first entry, written in invisible ink on the inside of his skull. *Vane resigned Saturday.* The second. *Warrants issued Sunday; my name third on the list, after Crane and the compositor.* He did not know where Crane was. He did not know whether Nell had reached Manchester safely, or whether she had been followed past Sheffield, or whether the money he'd pressed into her hand at the edge of the city had bought her a room or only a longer road. The not-knowing sat in his chest like a stone swallowed whole.

But the ledger's final entry never changed, because it could not be amended by any warder or magistrate: *Edith Marchmont is alive, and the country knows it.*

He was pacing the four steps of his cell — three and a half, truly; he had measured them with his heel out of the same compulsive bookkeeping — when the key turned in the door without warning. That was itself information. Warders came on schedule here; unscheduled keys meant either trouble or gift.

The warder was young, with a face like uncooked dough and eyes that flicked once down the corridor before he came in. He held a folded newspaper under his arm the way a man carries something hot.

"Turn around," he said.

Oscar turned. There was a rustle, the soft thump of newsprint against straw, and then the door closed again and the key turned and the corridor swallowed the young man's footsteps going away too quickly, like a boy fleeing the scene of a kindness.

Oscar stood facing the wall for a full minute, letting his heart slow. Then he crouched and drew the newspaper from beneath the cot's canvas sling, where it had been shoved in one practiced motion.

*The Clarion*. Not the radical weekly that had run the testimony — this was bigger, national, its masthead bent slightly from being carried inside a coat. Someone had marked the page with a thumbnail crease. Tuesday's date. Four days since the printing. Four days since two plainclothes officers had walked into the mews at Ashcombe House and Oscar had stood among the horses with his hands visible and his heart astonishingly quiet and said, *I am the one you want.*

He unfolded the paper beneath the high barred window, where the light fell in a grey column thick enough to read by.

The headline ran across five columns:

**DEAD SUFFRAGIST ALIVE: MARCHMONT APPEARS AT HYDE PARK RALLY**

Below it, a photograph so grainy it might have been taken through rain: a platform, banners, and at the centre a slight figure in a dark coat, one arm raised, her face a smudge of resolve. But Oscar knew that face. He had studied it for years in the photographs Verity kept locked in her writing desk, in the newspaper clippings Mrs. Prewitt smuggled up with the tea, in the margins of letters he had steamed open over a kettle and read with the guilty tenderness of a man reading scripture belonging to someone else. Two years of official death had not blurred her. If anything, prison had carved her finer.

He sat down on the cot, because his legs told him to, and began to read.

* * *

The rally had begun, according to the report, as an ordinary demonstration — the word *ordinary* doing quiet work there, since an ordinary suffrage demonstration in the autumn of 1913 meant twenty thousand women and a police cordon and mounted officers at the gates. The speakers were the usual roster. The resolutions were the usual resolutions. And then, the correspondent wrote, in prose that kept slipping its leash despite every editorial effort,

*a woman ascended the platform whom several of our reporters recognized instantly, though they confess they doubted their own eyes. For two years the name of Edith Marchmont has appeared on no roll but the dead. Her death certificate, signed and filed by officials of the Home Office, records her passing in custody. This newspaper has now seen documents — published last week in full — suggesting that certificate to be a fabrication erected over a scandal of force-feeding, injury, and concealment reaching into the highest offices of the land. Yesterday those documents found their living rebuttal.*

Mrs. Pankhurst yielded the platform. Mrs. Marchmont spoke for eleven minutes.

Oscar read the account of those eleven minutes four times, and each time his throat tightened at a different sentence.

She had begun quietly, he imagined — the report said her voice *did not carry at first, and she did not strain after volume*, which sounded exactly like her, the woman who had written *they may take my body's account of me, but they shall not write it* in a hand steady as engraving. She had begun quietly and let the crowd lean toward her, twenty thousand people bending like grass, until the constables at the perimeter stopped pretending to be bored.

She told them about Holloway. Not in general terms — the report was specific, and specificity was the whole weapon. She named the cell. She named the dates. She named the doctor who had held her head and the tube and the method by which fifty-three days of hunger strike had been answered with machinery, and she named Sir Roderick Vane, formerly Under-Secretary at the Home Office, who had signed the order continuing the feeding after the second injury, and who — when the third went wrong, when her heart stuttered and stopped for ninety seconds on a Tuesday afternoon while two wardens stood by having been instructed that *no irregularity would be recorded* — had chosen, rather than a martyr's inquest, a clerk's solution.

*A death certificate,* she had said, and the reporter set it in italics as though the words themselves required emphasis. *It cost them eight shillings and sixpence to kill me. I have come back to collect the debt.*

The roar that followed, the correspondent wrote, was heard in Kensington.

Oscar lowered the paper. His cell was very small and very grey and smelled of carbolic and other men's despair, and none of it mattered. None of it could touch him. Somewhere across the river a woman the government had erased had walked back onto the earth and spoken, and the speaking had weight, actual physical weight, pressing outward against walls and warrants and the whole apparatus of respectable lying, and he was inside the pressure wave of it, sitting on a plank cot in Brixton with a stolen newspaper warming in his hands.

*History is a spectator sport.* He had believed that the way other men believed in weather — as a fact of nature, requiring no argument. You watched. You kept the engine running and the lamps clean and you read other people's mail because watching was the closest a sensible man came to living. And then a gaunt stranger had come dripping into the mews with a bundle of letters sealed in a dead woman's hand, and the spectator seat had turned out to be onstage all along, had perhaps always been onstage, and the play did not pause while you decided whether to learn your lines.

He thought of Nell reciting testimony in a telegraph office, her voice level while the line went dead behind her. He thought of the decoy courier — a printer's apprentice named Wilcox, nineteen, freckled, fearless — who had boarded the train with a hymnal in his bag and been detained at Crewe, and whose hymnal had been burned unread in a yard while he spent a night in a cell that probably looked a great deal like this one. He thought of Verity reading the morning edition aloud to a feverish Tobias Crane, her voice breaking only once, on the phrase *alive and in her own words*, and how Crane had wept without noise, the way men weep who have been hunted too long to waste the sound.

None of them had been spectators either. That was the thing the orchard had tried to teach him all along, if an orchard can be said to teach: fruit picked from a neighbour's tree is still sweet in your mouth. Borrowed lives, borrowed courage, borrowed words — you carried them, and carrying changed you.

He read on.

* * *

The report gave the aftermath in the flat tone newspapers use when events outrun their column inches.

The platform had been stormed — or *attended by officers*, in the Commissioner's later phrasing — within minutes of Mrs. Marchmont finishing. But the crowd had done something the report described with visible wonder: it had closed around her. Twenty thousand people, most of them women, most of them strangers to one another, had formed a corridor of bodies from the platform to the park gate, and Edith Marchmont had walked through it and out of it and gone to ground somewhere the police had not yet found, though the report promised, in the ominous future tense of authority, that the search continued.

And Vane — Vane, who had resigned on Saturday with a statement about *the pressures of public life* and *a desire to attend to private affairs*, as though forgery were a hobby one laid down for the season — Vane had issued, through solicitors, a denial so carefully worded it confessed everything. He did not deny the certificate was false. He denied *knowledge of the circumstances surrounding its preparation*. He had not known. He had merely signed. A man could sign a woman out of existence and plead ignorance of the pen.

But the papers were not letting go. That was the miracle Oscar kept turning over like a coin he suspected might vanish. The *Clarion* had run the testimony's worst passages beside Vane's signature on the certificate, side by side, in matching type, so that no reader could pretend the documents disagreed politely. The *Manchester Guardian* had sent a correspondent to Holloway's records. Even the *Times* — the *Times*, dry as communion wafer — had published a leader demanding inquiry, using the phrase *a grave administrative irregularity*, which in that paper's dialect meant *someone must hang for this*.

Parliament was to be asked questions on Thursday. Questions, in that season, were the opening move of ruin.

Oscar folded the newspaper along its original creases, precisely, because precision was the one form of prayer available to him. Then he unfolded it again and read the photograph caption: *Mrs. Edith Marchmont addressing the Hyde Park demonstration yesterday — her first public appearance since her reported death in 1911.*

Reported death. The passive voice doing its work at last for the right side.

He lay back on the cot with the paper spread on his chest, rising and falling with his breath, and permitted himself — for exactly the length of time it took the light through the window to travel one flagstone's width — to feel it. Not triumph; he was too well acquainted now with the price list for that. But something adjacent. Something like standing in a cold garden and seeing, on a branch everyone had written off as winter-killed, one green shoot, absurd and undeniable.

*Late,* he murmured to the ceiling. *But arriving.*

* * *

The days that followed established a rhythm he distrusted even as he accepted it.

The dough-faced warder — his name, learned through the wall, was Tilling — brought no more newspapers, but he had opened a channel and channels widen. On Wednesday a pencil stub appeared wrapped in the newspaper's replacement: a Bible, standard issue, with a note in a schoolboy hand tucked at Second Corinthians. *Gov says no post yet. Thought you'd want to know she spoke again. Bristol. Ten thousand. T.*

Oscar read the note twice, then ate it — tore it small and swallowed it with water from the jug, because he had read enough prisoners' memoirs in other men's houses to know the protocol. The pencil he hid in the seam of the mattress, feeling like a conspirator of twelve.

Bristol. Ten thousand. She was moving, then, protected by the scandal's sheer size — a woman the government had certified dead could hardly be arrested without producing the corpse, and the corpse was giving interviews. Every day she stayed free was another day the story grew roots. Verity's lawyers had told him as much through the solicitor's one permitted visit, in the careful language lawyers use when the client is listening and the warder is closer than he appears: *Your position improves daily, Mr. Delacroix. Public sentiment is a curious instrument. It cannot cross-examine, but it can make cross-examination dangerous.*

His position improved daily. He turned the phrase over during exercise hour, walking the circle of the yard with his hands clasped behind him like a vicar taking air. Other prisoners gave him space; word travelled even here that the quiet chauffeur was *political*, which in the yard's taxonomy placed him somewhere between madman and saint. The sky above the walls was the colour of dishwater. His breath hung in front of him. November was coming down the weeks like a slow tide.

He should have been afraid. He catalogued the reasons with his habitual thoroughness: conspiracy charges, obstruction, the forged papers Crane had prepared, the diversion he had driven through the print works' yard with the horn open and the engine screaming, a one-man cavalry charge that had bought the compositor forty minutes at the case. Any of it could be assembled into years. The trial was set for the fortnight after next. The magistrate had looked at him over the spectacles with the expression of a man reading a menu.

And yet the fear would not properly take hold, and he examined why, walking his circle, and arrived at the answer with the small surprise of a man finding a letter already answered in his own hand: because whatever they did to him now, the ledger balanced. The testimony was out. The certificate was a scandal. Edith Marchmont had stood on a platform in front of ten thousand people in Bristol and said *I am here* in a voice that carried, and no order signed by any Vane, present or future, could unsay it. They could lock him in this grey box until his hair went white and the arithmetic of it would not change. He had spent forty-one years believing that safety was invisibility, and invisibility had turned out to be just another word for complicity with better manners. He was visible now. It cost. Let it cost.

On Thursday evening, Tilling's key turned at the unscheduled hour again.

The young warder came in holding not a newspaper this time but a single sheet of cheap paper, folded once, and he was pale, and he did not say *turn around* this time. He stood there holding it out almost at arm's length, the way one holds a summons.

"It came to the governor's office," Tilling said. "Marked personal. There's been an argument about it for two hours." He wet his lips. "Governor says you're to have it. Solicitor's privilege, apparently. Your lady's barrister pulled strings." He hesitated, and then, in a rush, as if the words had been waiting behind his teeth all day: "It's from her. From Mrs. Marchmont. I saw the envelope."

Oscar took the sheet. His hands, he noted distantly, were entirely steady, which seemed a dereliction.

Tilling withdrew. The key turned. The corridor resumed its grammar.

Oscar unfolded the paper under the window's grey column of light. The handwriting hit him before the sense of it — that unmistakable hand, upright and unhurried, the hand he had known for years through steam and secrecy, the hand of a dead woman, except that now it was simply the hand of a living one, and the difference between those two things was the whole width of the world.

*Mr. Delacroix,*

*I am told you are in Brixton, and I am told why. I have spent two years learning what it is to be written out of the record by men who never troubled to learn my middle name. You read my letters for years and did nothing — yes, I know; Verity told me, and I confess it made me angry for the length of one cup of tea. Then I remembered that anger is cheap and knowledge is not, and that when the hour came, the man who knew everything acted at last, and acting is the only sacrament I recognize.*

*You are in there because you chose to stand where the light falls. I know what that costs. I paid it for two years in a smaller room than yours.*

*Hold fast. The trial will be a farce, and farces collapse. Verity's lawyers are better than their reputation and Vane's are worse than their fees. And whatever they decide to do with you, know that twenty thousand people heard my voice on Sunday because a chauffeur drove through the night with a dead woman's words under his spare tyre. History is not a spectator sport, Mr. Delacroix. I suspect you have learned that. I am writing to tell you the rest of us noticed.*

*Yours in the common cause,*
*Edith Marchmont*

Oscar read it three times. Then he sat on the cot and laughed — a short, cracked sound, startling in the bare cell, the first laugh he had managed since the mews — because of course she knew about the letters. Of course Verity had told her. Of course the woman he had spied upon for years, in his hollow, comfortable way, had weighed his sin over a single cup of tea and filed it under *human*.

*Anger is cheap and knowledge is not.*

He folded her letter along its crease and slid it into the Bible, at Second Corinthians, where Tilling's notes lived, building a small archive in the one library no governor could confiscate. Then he returned to the newspaper, to the photograph, to the raised arm and the smudged, indomitable face, and read the article once more from the top, slowly, the way one reads a psalm.

Outside, beyond the walls, beyond the wire and the warders and the whole grey machinery of consequence, the story was still moving. Parliament on Thursday. Questions. Inquiries. Vane's solicitors issuing statements that confessed by their caution. And somewhere north, a woman called Nell — alive, he had to believe it, the ledger demanded it — walking free under a sky that owed her nothing and owing her everything.

The light through the window thinned toward evening. Somewhere below, the prison counted itself, doors answering doors.

Oscar set the paper down, laced his fingers behind his head, and waited — calm, counted, and for the first time in his life entirely unhidden — for whatever history intended to do with him next.
