## 21. The Cell and the Orchard

They gave him a cell on the third floor of Holloway's men's wing — a misprint of geography, since the women's prison stood a mile away across the road, and Oscar liked to imagine that if he pressed his ear to the wall hard enough he might hear, faint as a hymn through plaster, the sound of Nell's voice among a thousand others. He did not press his ear to the wall. He was thirty-four years old and he had learned, at last and late, the difference between listening and longing.

The trial was set for the second week of May. Verity's lawyers had written — letters delivered by a warder who pretended not to know whose handwriting it was, which was its own small comedy, given everything — that the technicality was being assembled like a watch: mainspring here, escapement there, a case built around the whole so no one could see the gears. A warrant signed under a superseded statute. An arrest made outside jurisdictional hours. Paperwork, in short, done hastily by angry men, which was the one kind of paperwork that always forgave itself eventually.

Oscar read the letters twice and put them under his mattress, where he had once hidden nothing at all.

The days had a shape now, and he had discovered that he loved their shape, which surprised him. Wake at the bell. The slop bucket, the tin mug, the grey light climbing the wall opposite his window like a slow green tide. Breakfast — bread, tea the colour of regret. Then the long morning, which belonged to him entirely, and which he spent, mostly, remembering an orchard.

* * *

It had belonged to a farmer named Hobday, outside Kettering, three fields from the cottage where Oscar grew up with his mother and whatever man she was married to that year. The Hobdays' orchard ran along the southern wall of their garden: forty apple trees, some of them older than the church, their bark split into maps of themselves. In September the windfalls came down overnight like weather, and in the mornings the grass beneath the trees was littered with fruit going soft and sweet-smelling, wasps drunk in the bruised flesh.

The Hobdays picked their trees clean for market. But the windfalls they left. And every boy within a mile knew what that meant, or believed he knew: the windfalls were fair game. Borrowed fruit. You climbed the wall — the wall was old and its coping stones were worn smooth by decades of boys' palms — you dropped into the long grass, you filled your pockets and your shirt-front, and you climbed back out before anyone saw you. Nobody ever saw anybody. It was understood, the way rain is understood, without contract or conversation.

Oscar had been seven the first time he went over that wall, sent by older boys as their scout, terrified, certain he would be shot or hanged or worse, shamed before the village forever. He had come back over the wall with six apples in his jersey and his heart going like a drum, and the sweetness of the first bite — a russet, half-bruised, warm from the grass — was the most violent pleasure he had ever known. He remembered thinking, with a seven-year-old's terrible clarity: *this is not mine, and it is keeping me alive.*

His mother stewed the apples. That was the thing of it; that was the whole confession of it. They were poor in the way that doesn't make a story — not picturesque poverty but the grinding, unphotographed kind — and the borrowed apples went into the pot with a spoonful of sugar if they had it, and became supper, and kept a boy alive through another winter. Sweetness that wasn't yours. Nourishment you stole or were given, depending on how you needed to tell it afterward, depending on whether you were talking to a priest or to yourself.

He had thought about that orchard maybe four times in twenty-five years. Now he thought about it daily, the way a man dying of thirst thinks about a well. Not because he missed the apples. Because he had finally worked out what the orchard had been trying to tell him all along, and it had taken a cell, a trial, a burned hymnal, a detained decoy, and the face of a girl reciting a dead woman's testimony from memory across a telegraph counter — it had taken all of that — for him to hear it.

* * *

Here is what he understood now, lying on his bunk in the hour after lights-out when the mind goes honest:

History is an orchard. Everyone lives along its wall.

He had spent his whole adult life believing otherwise. Believing history was a parade — something that passed your window on fixed days, banners and brass bands, and your job was to stand at the glass and watch, and perhaps note down what you saw, and feel superior to those who watched less attentively. He had been the finest spectator in London. He could have written a history of the suffrage movement from the inside of Lady Ashcombe's letter rack alone: every appeal, every arrest notice, every desperate plea for bail money, steamed open, read, resealed, filed away in his memory like a collector's specimens. He had known everything and acted on nothing, and he had called this wisdom. Caution. Keeping one's head down. Knowing which way the wind sat.

And the truth — the truth that arrived in him slowly, in the dark, in the third week of April, with the patience of all true things — was that he had never been standing outside the orchard at all. There was no outside. He had been in the long grass his entire life, eating fruit off other people's trees. Edith Marchmont's suffering had fed his curiosity the way the windfalls had fed his body: something grown and ripened at tremendous cost to someone else, fallen within reach, taken up without leave, swallowed. The letters he had read for years — women describing hunger strikes, describing force-feeding, describing the taste of their own blood — those had been his russets, warm from the grass, and he had bitten into them nightly and called it knowledge.

The difference between himself and the boy at the wall was only this: the boy took the fruit because he was starving, and said thank-you to the tree in the only language he had, which was to plant the seeds. Oscar had taken the fruit and planted nothing.

Until Crane came to the mews. Until the bundle in the oilcloth, sealed with a dead woman's hand. Until Friday became a deadline and a spectator became a courier, a liar, a smuggler, an accomplice — until he drove north with a force-fed girl beside him and a dead woman's testimony in a spare-tyre casing, and learned in eleven days what twenty years at the window had never taught him: that history does not happen to spectators because there are no spectators. It happens to everyone. To the sergeant at the first checkpoint who waved them through because his daughter had marched last spring. To the telegraph clerk who broke regulation with her steady hands. To the compositor setting type in a cellar while the yard filled with police. To the decoy courier who lost his liberty so a hymnal could be burned unread. To Mrs. Prewitt, who had stood at the kitchen table with flour on her knuckles and said, "Well then, we'd best get it right," as though history were a soufflé and she had made a thousand of them.

Everyone lives along the wall. The only question — the only question he had ever been asked, though it had taken him thirty-four years to hear it asked — was whether you stayed on your side of the coping stones counting other people's apples, or went over and got the fruit to the people starving.

* * *

On the fourth of May, a warder brought him a newspaper, folded to conceal nothing, because there was nothing left in the world to conceal from Oscar Delacroix. The scandal had gone national. Sir Roderick Vane's resignation had been announced with a photograph in which he looked, Oscar thought uncharitably and then charitably, like a man who had finally read his own mail. And on page five, a smaller item: a rally on Kennington Common, forty thousand strong, addressed by — and here the typesetters had clearly been instructed to take care — *Mrs. Edith Marchmont, whose survival, falsely reported two years ago, has become a national cause.*

Alive. Speaking. Her voice carrying over forty thousand heads, and none of them fainting, and none of them looking away.

Oscar read the paragraph standing at his window with the paper shaking very slightly in his hands, and he found that he was not proud, exactly, and not glad, exactly. What he felt was closer to the feeling of the first bite of the russet: sweetness arriving exactly when the body had stopped believing sweetness was possible. Vindication, but late. Late the way spring is late — not less real for the delay, only more astonishing when it comes.

He folded the paper and sat on the bunk and let himself, for one full minute, remember the mews. The smell of petrol and horse and rain. Tobias Crane dripping on the cobbles, saying *the woman who keeps Edith's letters*, and the vertigo of it, the floor of the world tilting. He had hated that moment. He had understood even then that it was the end of his life as he had designed it, and he had mourned the design even while reaching for the bundle.

Now, sitting in the consequences, he found he could not mourn it at all.

* * *

There was a window in the cell, barred, high, facing south. Through it, between the chimney pots, he could see a strip of sky and — on clear evenings, when the light went amber — the tops of plane trees in some square he couldn't name, moving the way trees move when the wind finds the upper branches first.

He watched them the way he had once watched the Hobdays' orchard from his bedroom window in the last week of August: knowing what was coming, wanting it, waiting for the windfall season. Except that now he was not waiting for fruit. He was waiting for trial, and beyond the trial, whatever the lawyers' watchwork machinery produced — release, probably; release with a stain on it, certainly; a life afterward that would never again be quiet in the old way, since quiet, he understood now, had been his word for complicity — and beyond all that, a spring day outside Manchester, an orchard he had never seen, four people and possibly a fifth planting a tree in ground that belonged to no one in particular.

Nell would be there. He thought of her often, not with the ache he might once have expected but with a steadier warmth, like a stove banked for the night. She had not forgiven him on the road through Hertfordshire, and she need not forgive him ever; forgiveness was not the currency between them. What was between them was better and harder: she knew exactly what he had been, and she had driven eight hours beside him anyway, and she had trusted him with the only copy of Edith Marchmont that existed in the world, which was her memory. You do not trust a spectator with that. You trust a man who has gone over the wall.

He wondered if the tree would take. Apple trees want patience; they want you to dig past the rubble of yourself into soil that will hold water. He was no gardener. But Crane would know, and Verity would pretend to know, and Mrs. Prewitt would actually know, and between the four of them and the ground, something green would happen. That seemed to him now the whole of politics, reduced to a kitchen-garden scale: people who should have nothing in common deciding together that something living ought to go into the earth.

* * *

The trial came the way weather comes to Kettering — discussed for weeks, arriving suddenly, over in an afternoon.

He dressed in the suit Verity's lawyers had procured, which fit him well enough to make him suspect Verity had supplied his measurements from memory, down to the collar size, and this undid him more than anything else that month: that she had noticed, all those years, the exact dimensions of the man driving her carriage, the man she had caught reading her mail and never once mentioned. Perhaps she had read him too. Perhaps everyone in the house had been reading everyone, all along, and the only crime had been pretending otherwise.

In the courtroom there was Vane's solicitor, sleek and defeated in advance. There was a bench of magistrates with faces arranged into neutrality. And there, in the public gallery, in a hat he recognised because he had driven her to the milliner's to buy it — Verity Ashcombe, upright as a mast, flanked by Mrs. Prewitt in her Sunday best and, at the end of the row, thin and recovered and wearing a new coat, Tobias Crane.

Crane met his eyes. Oscar nodded, once, the nod of one man at a wall acknowledging another.

The proceedings went as the lawyers had promised. A warrant issued under a statute repealed in 1911. An arrest executed, technically, outside the jurisdiction in which the warrant held force. A clerk's error, magnanimously admitted by a Home Office anxious to demonstrate, post-Vane, its scrupulousness. The magistrates conferred for eleven minutes. The senior of them looked at Oscar over his spectacles with an expression that Oscar chose to read as follows: *I know precisely what has happened here, young man, I know what you carried and to whom, and the law permits me only to say* —

"Release him. The warrant is void."

Eleven minutes. Twenty years of silence, eleven days of terror, eleven minutes of clerical mercy. History, Oscar thought, walking down the courthouse steps into a May morning so bright it seemed rehearsed, keeps its own accounts, and pays in its own coin, and you may wait a lifetime at the window for change and receive it all at once, on the steps, in the sunlight, with the taste of apples in your mouth.

Verity was waiting at the bottom. She did not embrace him. She took his hand in both of hers, the patron taking the hand of the chauffeur, the reader taking the hand of the letter, and said only:

"The train to Manchester is at noon. Mrs. Prewitt has packed sandwiches. And Mr. Hobday's apples, Mr. Delacroix, are not the only ones that ripen by falling."

Behind her, at the railings, holding a paper-wrapped bundle he could smell before he could see it — russets, someone had found russets in London in May, which should have been impossible and was therefore entirely in character — stood Nell, and beside her Crane, and above them all the sky did what the sky over Kettering had always done in windfall season: turned the colour of the inside of an apple, and promised everything.

He went over the wall one more time, toward them, empty-handed, and it was the richest he had ever been.
