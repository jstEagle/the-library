## 22. What We Carried Forward

The train from London came into Manchester through a country already green, and Oscar Delacroix watched it pass with the particular attention of a man who had spent eight months learning what a window was worth.

He had not been outside since November. That was the arithmetic he carried now — not the sentence, which had been two years hard labour, commuted on appeal; not the charge, which Verity's lawyers had dismantled like children taking apart a clock; but the days themselves, counted one by one against the whitewash of his cell. A hundred and ninety-odd of them. He had learned to measure time in light on a wall instead of miles on a road, and when the train emerged from a cutting and the fields opened out — hawthorn in bloom, lambs stupid and joyful in the wet grass — he gripped his knee and made himself look until his eyes stung.

A porter at the station recognised him. Of course they would; his face had been in every paper for a week after the arrest, *CHAUFFEUR MARTYR*, the Manchester papers had called him, and then again for a week after Vane's fall, and once more when the appeal succeeded. The porter said nothing, only touched his cap with something that might have been respect or might have been the memory of contempt, and Oscar decided — as he had decided so often lately — that it did not matter which.

Nell was waiting under the station clock.

She looked different and exactly the same. The governess's grey was gone; she wore a plain blue coat and her hair was pinned up without apology, and she stood the way she had stood at the third checkpoint, when she recited Edith Marchmont's testimony aloud to a telegraph clerk while inspectors closed the line around them: upright, unhurried, as though courage were simply good posture maintained past the point of reason.

"You're late," she said.

"The train was late."

"I know. I wanted to see if you'd lie about it." She smiled, and it reached all the way up, which was still new enough that it startled him. "You didn't. You're improving."

"Eight months of nothing but truth," Oscar said. "It wears a man down."

She took his arm without ceremony, the way family does, and led him out into the noise and coal-smell of Manchester, and neither of them mentioned the last time they had stood together in this city, empty-handed, watching a stranger carry their hymnal toward a fire.

* * *

The orchard was forty minutes out by trap, beyond a village whose name Oscar forgot twice before he remembered it, up a lane where the hedges had gone mad with May. Nell drove. She drove the way she did everything — competently, without commentary — and Oscar sat back and let the country come at him: the smell of cut grass and cow parsley, a church tower, a field of oilseed rape so yellow it looked invented.

"It's Mrs. Prewitt's cousin's land," Nell said. "Leased. Nobody official knows, and nobody local will say, because Edith helped deliver half the babies in the parish before anyone knew who she was." She glanced at him. "She goes by Mrs. Marsh now. You'll have to remember that."

"Mrs. Marsh," Oscar repeated. "And I'm?"

"Nobody yet. We'll see what you grow into."

The orchard announced itself before they saw it — blossom first, a white haze above the hedge like smoke that had decided to stay — and then the gate, and then the trees themselves, old apples mostly, planted by someone dead a century, their trunks lichened silver, their branches heavy with a spring so extravagant it seemed almost reckless given everything the world had done lately.

Edith Marchmont was standing among them.

Oscar had imagined this meeting perhaps four hundred times in his cell, and in none of those imaginings had she been wearing a man's gardening coat and arguing with a goat. But there she was, thinner than the photographs, grey coming into her dark hair, holding a sack of prunings and telling the goat in no uncertain terms that the bark of the Cox was not its personal property. The goat disagreed. The goat was winning.

"Mrs. Marsh," Nell called. "I've brought you a criminal."

Edith turned. For a moment she went very still, the way Oscar had seen her go still in Verity's drawing room two years ago, listening to a letter being read — the stillness of a woman measuring whether the world could be trusted with the next thing. Then she dropped the sack, crossed the grass at a pace entirely unbefitting a convalescent, and took both his hands in hers.

"So you're the one who read my letters," she said.

Oscar felt the old shame rise, hot and familiar, the shame he had confessed to Nell on the Hertfordshire road and to the court and to himself in the dark. "Every one I could steam open, ma'am. For years. And did nothing about any of them."

"Yes," Edith said. "I know. I wrote knowing you'd read them. Half of us wrote hoping someone would." She squeezed his hands hard, harder than a woman just out of prison ought to be able to squeeze. "The other half hoped you wouldn't get caught. Look at you. Caught, tried, imprisoned, released, and here standing in my orchard. Mr. Delacroix, you are the most useful spectator I ever had."

He laughed — actually laughed, the sound strange in his own throat after so long — and she let go of his hands and turned to the goat and said, "Oh, leave her alone, she's earned the bark," and the ordinary morning resumed around them as though extraordinary men arrived in orchards every day.

* * *

Verity and Crane came up from the village at noon, walking slowly, because Crane walked slowly now and always would; the fever and the flight had taken something from his lungs that never came back. He had filled out since London, though. His coat fit. His eyes were clear. When he saw Oscar he stopped, and the two men regarded each other across twenty feet of orchard grass with the awkwardness of people who have been through a great deal together and discussed almost none of it.

"Well," Crane said.

"Well," Oscar said.

Crane nodded once, satisfied, as though some transaction between them had been formally concluded, and limped forward and shook his hand with a grip that cracked knuckles. "You look terrible," he said. "Prison food?"

"Prison everything."

"Better than prison nothing." He fished in his pocket and produced, of all things, an apple — small, hard, green, obviously stolen from one of these very trees within the last hour. "For the journey home. Don't eat it till you're on the train. Tradition."

"There is no tradition," Nell said. "You made that up this morning."

"A tradition has to start somewhere," Crane said with dignity, "and it may as well start with me, as I've started most things badly and one or two things well."

Verity Ashcombe came last, because Verity always came last now, letting the others arrive ahead of her, having learned — as she told the tribunal, as she told the newspapers, as she told anyone who would listen — that arriving last meant you could watch everyone else be safe first. She wore black, as she nearly always did, though the mourning was old now and had softened into mere habit, and when she reached Oscar she did not take his hands or shake them. She looked at him for a long moment, the way she had looked at him across the hall of her house the morning he left with the letters hidden in the spare tyre, and then she said:

"Lady Verity Ashcombe claims sole responsibility. That is what I told the police, the magistrates, the Home Office, and three newspapers. It is also, incidentally, true. I was your employer. Everything you did, you did on my mail."

"On other people's mail," Oscar corrected. "It was rather the point."

"Don't be clever, Delacroix, it doesn't suit you." But her voice caught on the last word, and she turned away quickly and busied herself with the hamper Mrs. Prewitt had sent — cold chicken, bread, a jar of pickle, and, wrapped separately in a cloth with the reverence of relics, six seedling trees, roots balled in sacking.

They ate lunch on the grass under the oldest tree, and the talk was small on purpose: the price of coal, the state of the lane, Crane's ongoing war with the goat, which had a name now (Duchess) and an unassailable position in the household. Only once did the conversation drift near the centre of things — Verity mentioning that Vane had gone to the Continent, that he would not be prosecuted, that the resignation had been the whole of the justice the law intended to serve — and Edith said, quietly, "The law served what it could. We served the rest," and passed the bread, and that was all.

But Oscar watched her as she spoke, and thought about death certificates, and about how a woman could be declared dead by a man in an office and have to live two years inside that lie, hiding in attics, moving by night, while her own name became a thing written in a dead hand. He thought about the cell, and the newspaper slipped through the bars, and the roar of the crowd at the mass rally coming off the page in ink — *MARCHMONT LIVES* — and how he had pressed that page flat against the whitewash and read it until the words blurred.

"Mr. Delacroix." Edith was looking at him. "You've gone somewhere."

"Just counting," he said. "An old habit. I used to count everything I knew and did nothing about. I'm trying to count differently now."

"And what does the new arithmetic say?"

He looked around the orchard: Verity unwrapping the seedlings with the concentration she usually gave to parliamentary strategy; Crane defending a chicken bone from Duchess by sheer force of personality; Nell sitting close beside him, her shoulder just touching his, not looking at him, which was how she said the things she never said.

"It says," Oscar said, "that history isn't a spectator sport. It says I was wrong for thirty-one years, and right for about eleven days, and that the eleven days cost me eight months and were worth every one of them." He paused. "It says the fruit was never mine. I picked it from other people's lives my whole life. And it kept me alive anyway. So now I suppose I owe a tree."

Edith smiled — a real smile, unguarded, the first he had seen from her that wasn't armour — and stood, and brushed the grass from her coat.

"Then let's plant one," she said.

* * *

They chose the spot together, which took longer than planting it. Verity wanted it visible from the lane, so that anyone passing would see it flourish and wonder. Crane wanted it near the wall, sheltered from the wind, practical to the last. Nell wanted it where the morning sun came first over the hedge, and when asked why, she said only, "Because it should get the best of whatever comes," and nobody argued with Nell about anything anymore; she had memorised a testimony in her head and carried it through three checkpoints and burned it into the public record, and her judgement had acquired a certain weight.

In the end they planted it in the middle of the row, between a russet older than the parish and a young pippin Crane had put in himself in March, and the hole went down through good dark soil that smelled of rain and worms and everything patient.

It was a Bramley. Edith chose it. "Cooking apple," she said. "Not much to look at raw. Sour on its own. But give it time and heat and it becomes the making of everything else." She crouched at the edge of the hole, holding the sapling upright while Oscar shovelled, and said, "That's the whole movement in one tree, if you ask me. Sour on its own. The making of everything else, given time."

Oscar tamped the earth down with the flat of the spade, working around the trunk carefully, and Verity knelt and firmed the soil with her gloved hands, and Crane fetched water from the rain butt, complaining, and Nell — Nell stood a little apart with her arms folded, watching them all, and when Oscar glanced up at her she said:

"You do it. Say something. You're the one who owes it."

So Oscar straightened, spade in hand, five people and a goat gathered round a stick of a tree in an orchard in Cheshire, and found — to his surprise — that he had something to say after all.

"When I was a boy," he began, and stopped, and started again. "When I was a boy we lived next to an orchard. Belonged to a solicitor named Hargreaves who never once picked his own fruit. Every autumn the wind took what it liked over our wall, and my mother sent me out to gather it, and we ate apples all winter that weren't ours. Borrowed fruit. I thought for years that was a story about theft." He looked at the sapling, pale and improbable in the fresh-turned earth. "It wasn't. It was a story about how sweetness gets around. How it crosses walls. Somebody plants, somebody else eats, somebody else plants again, and nobody owns any of it really — we just carry it forward. That's what we did last year. All of us. Edith carried the testimony out of a cell. Crane carried it across England. Nell carried it in her head. Verity carried it into print. I carried it through checkpoints, and then I carried a number on a cell door, and then you all carried me out." He set the spade down. "So. Here's a tree. It'll outlive every person standing here. Let it borrow from whoever needs it."

There was a silence, the good kind, the kind with weather in it. Then Crane said, "Well, that's the finest speech ever delivered over a Bramley," and Verity said, "Hush, Tobias," and Edith watered the roots from the can, slowly, thoroughly, the way she evidently did everything, and Duchess ate the sacking.

They stayed until the light went long and gold across the rows. Verity and Crane walked down to the village first, arm in arm, arguing amiably about whether traditions could be founded retroactively. Nell lingered by the new tree, and Oscar came to stand beside her, and for a while they said nothing at all, which between the two of them had long since stopped meaning avoidance and started meaning rest.

"They'll come for the vote eventually," Nell said at last. "Not this year. Maybe not the next. But it's coming. You can feel it under everything, like water under a field."

"I believe you."

"And when it comes, there'll be more work. Worse than last year, maybe. They don't forgive people who beat them in print." She turned and looked at him directly. "I'm asking, Oscar. Not telling. Are you in?"

He considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty and he had, at last, learned to afford it. He thought of the cell and the wall and the light moving across it. He thought of the hymnal burning unread in a railway grate, and the decoy courier's face as the police took him — a face Oscar still saw some nights, and probably always would. He thought of a boy gathering borrowed apples in the wet grass, certain the sweetness belonged to someone else.

"I spent my whole life believing survival meant staying invisible," he said. "I was wrong. It turns out survival means being part of something that keeps going after you stop." He held out his hand, palm up, the old chauffeur's gesture, waiting for keys or letters or orders. "I'm in. Whatever it costs. Put me anywhere on the route."

Nell took his hand — not shook it, held it — and together they looked down the row of trees, old and young, the ancient russets and Crane's March pippin and the thin new Bramley with its single fist of leaves, all of them leaning the same way in the evening wind, all of them green.

From the house, Edith called them in to supper, and the bell of the little parish church rang seven across the valley, and the future — cautiously, stubbornly, like blossom after a hard frost — came on.
