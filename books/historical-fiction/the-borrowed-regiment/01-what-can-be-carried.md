## 1. What Can Be Carried

There is an art to packing a woman's life into a trunk, and I had mastered it by my third week in Lady Verity Ashcombe's service, which was fortunate, because she required mastery of it roughly twice a month.

The trick is not to ask what a person owns. The trick is to ask what a person cannot bear to be without when the train lurches at Ashford and the porter drops the case and the whole expedition threatens to come apart on a cold platform. Everything else—everything lovely and useless—is negotiable.

So: two dresses for speaking, one for mourning-adjacent occasions (Lady Verity attended funerals the way other women attended garden parties, on the principle that grief made people receptive to arguments about justice), one serviceable walking costume. Pamphlets, always pamphlets, packed spine-up so they could be counted by touch. A writing case with ink that wouldn't freeze. Her mother's photograph, wrapped in a shawl because sentiment must be padded like glassware or it travels badly. And in the innermost pocket, sewn there by a previous generation of some housekeeper or other, the little revolver nobody ever mentioned, which I loaded and checked the way other women checked for moths.

What did I own? That was easier to answer, because the answer fit inside a hatbox.

A second-best skirt. A photograph of my brother Thomas, taken before he went out to India and stopped writing as often as a sister might wish. A copy of *Jane Eyre* with the cover held on by string. My gloves, darned so many times they were less gloves than a philosophy about gloves. And a small tin of needles and thread, because a person who can mend anything is a person who can be taken anywhere, and I had built my entire existence on that principle.

I was, you understand, portable. That was my great accomplishment. Twenty-six years old and I could be folded up and carried from county to county without leaving so much as a crease behind. Lady Verity said once—I was arranging her notes at the time, so perhaps she'd forgotten I was in the room—"Zephyrine has the gift of not being noticed, which is rarer than beauty and considerably more useful." She meant it as praise. It was praise. I have never been entirely sure why it sat in my chest afterward like a stone someone had swallowed for me.

* * *

The morning of the Maidstone rally began, as all our mornings began, with me kneeling in front of the trunks in the blue morning room while Lady Verity stood at the window dictating to me the way weather dictates to a landscape.

"The hall will be hostile," she said. "Half those men have never heard a woman finish a sentence."

"Then we shall pack shorter sentences," I said, folding a petticoat along its seams.

She laughed—one short syllable, quickly rationed—and turned back to the window. "You'll read the correspondence on the drive down. Anything from London goes straight in the dispatch case. Anything from—" a pause, delicate as a scalpel, "—abroad comes to me unopened. I'll want to see the postmarks myself."

"Yes, my lady."

This was the arrangement. It had been the arrangement since 1911, when the letters first started requiring what the government politely called supervision. Lady Verity corresponded with half of Europe—suffragists, socialists, one Norwegian countess who wrote exclusively about her chickens—and since August, every envelope bearing a foreign stamp arrived at Ashcombe Manor already opened by some clerk in London who read it with a pencil in his hand, then resealed it with a gummed strip and the smugness of a man who believes himself invisible.

You will notice I say *clerk*. You will notice how precisely I understood his position.

I drove her to the station at nine. I saw her onto the train with the dispatch case and the speech folder and the umbrella she would forget somewhere by four o'clock. Then I drove back alone through the wet green lanes of Kent, past orchards with their trees trained flat against walls like servants pressed to attention, and I did what I always did on the return journey.

I stopped at the top of Hollingbourne Hill, put on the brake, and read the mail.

Not all of it. I was not a monster. I read the way a starving woman eats—carefully, guiltily, taking only what would keep me until supper. Bills went unread. Letters from her sister were unread; I knew too much about her sister already. But the foreign post—the censored, pencil-marked, resealed foreign post—that I read on the hilltop with the engine ticking below me and the Weald spread out grey-green to the horizon, and I told myself each time that this was diligence. This was preparation. If Lady Verity needed to know something urgent, surely it was better that her driver should already know it.

It was a lie, of course. I knew it was a lie. It was the most comfortable lie I owned, and I kept it in good repair, like the gloves.

* * *

There was one correspondent whose letters I read differently. Greta Lindqvist, of Leipzig. Her envelopes came now with the censor's strip across the flap and sometimes with whole passages struck through in that clerk's neat pencil, sentences amputated mid-thought so that a letter about a suffrage meeting might arrive reading only:

*My dear Verity — The meeting was well attended despite — [omitted] — and my husband says that if the war lasts past spring we shall all be eating [omitted] for Christmas, so you see even here we keep our sense of humour. Give my love to England, which I — [omitted] — very much.*

Reading her was like listening to a violinist play a piece with three bars missing. Your mind supplied the gaps, and what your mind supplied was always worse than the truth.

I had begun, sometime in October, to answer her. In the margins. On scraps. Never anything posted—never, never posted—but in a cheap notebook I kept under the seat of the motor, I wrote back to her across the gaps: *The meeting sounds brave. What are you eating for Christmas? Tell me about your husband. Tell me about anything the censor didn't take.*

And once—and this is the part I confess with difficulty, the part I have never spoken aloud until now, and I am telling it to you because you, whoever you are, reading this years hence, cannot arrest me for it—once I wrote, on the flyleaf of that notebook, a letter addressed to no postal system on earth:

*Dear Greta. I read your letters before Lady Verity does. I am the invisible one. I drive the motor and carry the trunks and I know your handwriting better than my own now. If you are afraid, write it between the lines and I will find it. I am very good at finding things between lines. It is the only country I have ever lived in.*

That was November the ninth. I remember the date because it was the day Mrs. Pargeter burned the toast and declared the war responsible, and I laughed for the first time in weeks, and thought: how strange, that a woman can laugh in a warm kitchen while she builds herself a prison out of other people's secrets, and call the prison home.

* * *

By half past five I was back at the manor, unpacking. This was the liturgy: trunk open, contents inventoried, everything restored to its proper drawer so that the house might pretend no one had ever left it. Lady Verity arrived on the seven-twelve, hoarse from speaking, triumphant, slightly hoarser than triumph strictly required.

"Well?" she said, handing me her coat before I could reach for it. "Did the world end while I was away?"

"Only the toast, my lady."

"Mrs. Pargeter blames the war?"

"Mrs. Pargeter blames the war."

"Good woman. Keep her." She stood a moment in the hall, letting the fire find her face, and I watched the tiredness move over her like cloud-shadow over a field. Then, briskly: "There was a letter in the case. German stamps. Did you see it?"

My hands did not stop moving. I am proud of that, even now. "On the hilltop, my lady. As arranged."

"And?"

"Greta Lindqvist," I said. "Leipzig. Mostly struck through."

Something crossed her face—not worry exactly, more the look of a chess player watching an opponent's hand drift toward a piece. "She writes less every month," Lady Verity said, mostly to herself. "Or the clerk in London writes it for her." She unpinned her hat and gave it to me, and I took it, because taking things was my profession. "Zephyrine. Do you ever wonder what we'd do—all of us, all these careful women—if the war came to the door instead of arriving in the post?"

"I imagine we'd pack," I said. "We're very good at packing."

She looked at me for a moment longer than the joke deserved. Whatever she found in my face, she filed away; she filed everything. "Supper," she said. "And then bed. Tomorrow we start again."

I carried the hatbox upstairs. My own hatbox. My whole life in a box, light enough to lift with one hand, and I set it on the shelf and looked at it for a long moment, the way you look at a thing you suddenly suspect is too small.

Below me, in the hall, the telephone rang—a violence of sound in that quiet house—and I heard Mrs. Pargeter answer it, and then I heard Mrs. Pargeter say, in a voice I had never heard her use, "At the door? A child? At this hour?"

I was down the stairs before the hatbox finished settling on its shelf.
