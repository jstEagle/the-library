## 2. The Letters She Shouldn't Answer

I have a theory about post. It is that no one truly reads it—not the sender, not the recipient, and certainly not the person paid to carry it from the hall table to the pillar box at the end of the drive. A letter is a small, sealed agreement between strangers: *pretend I am only paper.* In eleven years of service to Lady Verity Ashcombe, I had carried some four thousand of these agreements, and I had broken my side of the bargain exactly twice.

The first time was an accident. The second was Greta Lindqvist.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, which is a habit of mine, and one Lady Verity has thrice corrected in my shorthand dictation. "You bury the lede, Miss Fairweather," she says, in the tone of a woman who considers buried ledes a form of moral weakness. So: the letters.

It began, as most of my sins do, with efficiency.

* * *

The war had changed the post the way it changed everything—slowly, then all at once, then with paperwork. By October 1914 there were rules. Letters to and from persons resident in enemy territory were to be read. Censorship, they called it, though the men who performed it were not censors so much as readers with stamps, and stamps, I have found, lend any man an air of authority he has done nothing to earn.

Lady Verity's foreign correspondence now came to her already opened, edges ragged where the official envelope had been slit, sometimes with a slip enclosed: *Examined by Censor.* She received perhaps six such letters a month, and she read them standing up, by the window, her jaw set in the particular way that meant someone she loved was on the wrong side of a line on a map.

My duties regarding this correspondence were simple. I drove to the village post office. I collected what could not be left at the gate. I carried the rest out—her replies, sealed, innocent as eggs. And because Lady Verity trusted me the way one trusts a well-made lock, nobody, ever, counted the letters in my basket.

That was the whole architecture of my life, really. Trust, load-bearing and unexamined. I stood inside it every day and called it safety.

The Lindqvist letters arrived on a Tuesday, three of them together, forwarded from London with the censor's mark like a bruise across each envelope. Lady Verity read them at the window. Then she set them down on the hall table, face-down, in the way she set down things she intended to deal with later and did not intend to feel about in the meantime, and said, "Zephyrine. The motor at two. Mrs. Fennick's committee first, then the post."

"Yes, my lady."

I should say here that Lady Verity and Greta Lindqvist had been corresponding for twenty-two years. I knew this without having been told, the way one knows things in a great house—not through confession but through sediment. There had been a photograph once, in a drawer I was dusting: two young women on a shingle beach, arms linked, laughing at something outside the frame. One of them was unmistakably my employer, thirty years younger and already formidable; the other was tall and fair and squinting into the sun with her whole face. On the back, in pencil: *G. & V., Ostend, 1892.*

So when the censor-stamped envelopes began arriving, addressed in that same slanting, confident hand, I did not need to be told who wrote them. I needed only to be told nothing, which is what everyone told me, always, as a matter of policy.

At two o'clock I drove Lady Verity to Mrs. Fennick's committee, waited ninety minutes in the cold while resolutions were passed, drove her home, took her dictated reply to Frau Lindqvist in shorthand, typed it, sealed it, and placed it in the basket with the rest.

And then I sat down in the boot room with the three incoming letters, and I broke my agreement with the post for the second time in my life.

Not the first letter. Let me be clear about that—I want the record accurate, since accuracy was, until recently, the only thing I owned outright. The first letter I left alone. It sat in my basket overnight and I felt its weight the way one feels an unexploded shell described in the morning papers: distant, theoretical, someone else's weather.

The second letter was the one that got me. Because at the bottom of the second page, beneath the censor's stamp and above the signature, Greta Lindqvist had written a single line that the censor had evidently decided required no comment:

*Tell the little grey one I am still counting her spoons.*

I sat in the boot room with the lamp turned low and understood, with the peculiar vertigo of the secretly known, that this sentence was about me.

Twenty-two years of letters, and somewhere in them, evidently, there was a story involving me, spoons, and a number I did not know. I had been in this house eleven years. Before that I had been elsewhere—positions in Bath, in Tunbridge Wells, a bad year in Brighton—and before *that*, a childhood in Kent with a brother who died at nineteen of the pneumonia, or of the poverty, depending on how you kept your accounts. I remembered no Germans. I remembered no spoons.

Which meant the story was not mine. Which meant Greta Lindqvist had heard it from Lady Verity, who had apparently mentioned me—a driver, a reader, a woman whose entire professional achievement was being forgettable—to her friend across the sea. Often enough, apparently, that *the little grey one* had become a character in their correspondence. A recurring role. Performed nightly, by me, without my knowledge or consent, in a theatre I would never see.

I ought to have felt violated. What I actually felt, sitting there in the smell of boot-black and linseed oil, was something far more dangerous.

I felt *included.*

* * *

Here is what you must understand about invisibility: it is comfortable the way a well-worn shoe is comfortable, and it pinches in exactly the same places, and after enough years you stop noticing either fact. I had made myself unremarkable on purpose. Small salary, smaller opinions, handwriting deliberately smoothed into anonymity, a face people described afterward as "the secretary" or "that Fairweather woman" and never with any detail attached. When Lady Verity traveled, I packed the trunks; when she spoke, I took the dictation; when she was photographed for the papers—which she was, often, furiously—I stood behind the camera. I could have been lifted out of any room in England and replaced within the week, and part of me had always found this restful. Portable. Safe. Nobody shoots at the furniture.

And all the while, apparently, across the North Sea, in a country we were now bombing with newspapers and indignation, a woman I had never met had given me a nickname.

*Still counting her spoons.*

That night I did not sleep. That night I lay in my narrow room under the eaves and constructed, item by item, the inventory of my own life, and discovered it came to very little: one good coat, two bad ones, a typewriter ribbon not yet opened, a photograph of a brother, a tin of shorthand notes I ought to burn and didn't. If I vanished tomorrow, the loss to the world would be administrative.

Somewhere in Germany there was a woman counting spoons on my behalf, and I did not even know how many there were.

* * *

I answered in the margins. That's the part I keep returning to—the margins, the coward's country of a page. Not a separate sheet, nothing that could be intercepted and *traced*. Just small pencil annotations in the white space beside her text, in handwriting slightly disguised from my own, the way one disguises oneself in a crowd by changing one's walk.

Her third letter, the last of the three, had contained a passage about rationing beginning in Munich, and her husband Karl's regiment having moved east, and her sister-in-law's family—who wanted, the letter implied with a delicacy that fooled neither Lady Verity nor me, to be rid of her—"asking again about the house." And at the end, the usual closing paragraph, warm and weary, asking after the cause, asking whether the English still believed women would vote in her lifetime, asking, in the last line, *whether the little grey one still drives too fast on the lane by the mill.*

She did not know I would ever read that line. That is the thing I want to make plain, to whatever tribunal eventually convenes over my conscience: I was not invited. I was simply the only person in Kent who both had access to the letter and knew what the question meant. Lady Verity had never once driven anything in her life. The little grey one drove too fast on the lane by the mill because the little grey one was me, and had been me, in ink, for years.

So I took my pencil, and I wrote in the margin, small enough to hide in the gutter of the page:

*I don't drive too fast. The lane is too narrow. — Z.F.*

Then I sat for a long time feeling like a burglar who has left a calling card.

Because here is the arithmetic of the crime, and I did it that night, honestly, methodically, the way I balance the household accounts: the letter would go back to Lady Verity, who might or might not notice the annotation; if she noticed, she would know my handwriting anywhere, disguise or no disguise; if she knew, she would either dismiss it as an eccentricity or sack me for tampering with the post of a woman under censorship regulations during wartime, which is, I am given to understand, the sort of thing that gets one talked about at magistrate meetings. Mr. Halloway's name was already going round the county attached to phrases like *enemy aliens* and *proper order,* the way weather goes round a church spire.

Against this: one woman, alone in a hostile country, writing to her oldest friend across a wall of censors, would find, in the margin of the next letter sent, proof that somebody on the far side of that wall had read her words all the way down. Not skimmed. Not stamped. *Read.*

I told myself it was harmless. I told myself it was charity. I told myself, in the small hours, that a person can be starved of being seen just as surely as of bread, and that Greta Lindqvist was starving in public, performing calm for the censor, and that my pencil-line was a loaf passed under a door.

I did not tell myself the truth, which was simpler and worse: I wanted to be known. I had spent eleven years making myself unseeable, and it had worked so thoroughly that the only person who had ever seen me lived nine hundred miles away and had learned of my existence secondhand, through spoons.

* * *

The reply came back eleven days later, opened, stamped, and containing—folded separately, as if the writer feared the censor's eye even for this—a loose slip of paper on which was written, in that slanting confident hand:

*To the little grey one. Karl's family means to take the boy when Karl falls. They have said so to my face. I have a plan and one address in England and it is this house. Tell V. nothing yet. Count the spoons yourself and you will understand.*

I read it four times. The boy was never named. The plan was never explained. *When Karl falls* was never qualified—it was not *if*, and the certainty of it sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

There are moments when a life turns, and they almost never announce themselves. There is no music. The clock in the hall keeps its idiot time. You are sitting in a boot room with a slip of paper, and somewhere ahead of you a child is walking toward a door you will one day answer, and you do not know it yet, and the paper does not tell you, and the only warning you receive is the sound of your own heart changing its mind about something.

I folded the slip along its existing creases and put it in the tin with the shorthand notes I ought to burn.

And then, because the household accounts would not balance themselves, and because Lady Verity would ring for the car at half past ten whether the world was ending or not, I went and checked my hair in the glass, arranged my face into its usual forgettable arrangement, and went to work.

Behind me in the tin, folded small, a woman I had never met was trusting me with the only address she had.
