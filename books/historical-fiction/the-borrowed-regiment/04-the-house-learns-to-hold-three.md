## 4. The House Learns to Hold Three

### Zephyrine

The story was invented over cold mutton on the second evening, the way all good lies are invented in England: reluctantly, at table, and with someone insisting it won't do.

"He cannot be German," Mrs. Pargeter said, as though the boy might argue the point. He sat at the kitchen's far end with a tin soldier standing sentry beside his bread and butter, watching us the way cats watch weather.

"Then what is he?" Lady Verity asked. She had a way of asking questions that made them sound like other people's problems.

"Belgian," I said. "Refugee. Half of Kent has taken one in; the vicar's been preaching sermons about it. Nobody will look twice."

"They'll look twice if they ask him his name and he says *Emil*," said Mrs. Pargeter.

So we gave him a second name, the way you give a coat to a shivering man. *Emile*, we decided — Emile Verhoeven, orphaned at Antwerp, kin to nobody, passed along by a fleeing aunt until he arrived at our door like a parcel with no return address. It was close enough to the truth to be easy to remember, which is the whole art of lying. A lie should fit the mouth comfortably or it will be spat out at the worst moment.

Emil took the new name without comment, but that night when I tucked the blankets around the cot Mrs. Pargeter had dragged from the attic, I heard him whisper it under his breath — *Emile* — twice, carefully, like a boy rehearsing a part he hadn't auditioned for. The tin soldier lay at attention on the pillow beside his head.

"You don't have to answer to it," I told him. "Not in here."

He looked at me with those grey eyes that took in everything and surrendered nothing, and after a long moment he said, "In here, what am I?"

It was the first full sentence he'd offered anyone in this house, and he'd spent it on me, and I found I had no good answer. "Ours," I said finally, and turned out the lamp before he could examine the word for cracks.

I had been portable my whole life. One trunk, one valise, a hatbox of letters I ought not to have. I knew precisely how little space a person needed to occupy in a household before she could be carried out with the linens and forgotten by Thursday. Children are not portable. Children take up room — in the house, in the day, in the chest, somewhere behind the sternum where I had previously kept nothing but patience. Emil had been with us four days and already there was a chair at the table that was *his* chair, and a chipped blue cup no one else dared touch, and a groove worn into the flagstones by the back door where he stood each morning to watch the lane, waiting — I understood, though he never said it — for a woman who had told him to forget her face.

She had asked him to forget hers. He stood in that groove every morning anyway, remembering.

### Mrs. Pargeter

Forty years I've cooked in great houses, and I'll tell you what I know: a household is a body. It digests what comes into it or it sickens. This one had been two mouths and a mistress who ate opinions for breakfast, and now there were four of us, and the body had to learn.

The learning went like this.

First, the eating. The lad pushed food round his plate like it owed him money, so I stopped serving him portions and started serving him *choices*. "Bread and dripping, or bread and jam?" A small thing. You'd be surprised how a child straightens up when the world offers him two things and lets him pick. By Friday he was asking for seconds of the rabbit pie, and by Saturday he'd informed me, gravely, that his mother put nutmeg in it. Whether that was true I couldn't say, but I grated nutmeg in thereafter, and if the ghost of a Belgian woman is going to haunt any kitchen in England, let it be mine, where there's always something on the stove.

Second, the flinching. Miss Fairweather warned me quiet, first night, hand on my arm in the passage: *Don't say German. Don't say Hun. Not even in temper.* I've a temper like a kettle, everyone knows it, but you can boil a kettle with the lid held down if your arm's strong enough. When the butcher's boy came Monday shouting about the latest atrocity story from the papers, I paid him and shut the door on his noise. When Mr. Tibbs the gardener grumbled about spies under every bed, I told him spies wouldn't last a week on his wages.

Third — and this is the thing I keep turning over like a coin — third, the lady herself. Lady Ashcombe came down Tuesday morning, looked at the boy sitting in her hall mending nothing in particular with that tin soldier, and said, "Pargeter, he needs boots." As if she'd noticed. As if noticing were beneath her and providing weren't.

"He's wearing boots, my lady."

"He's wearing *coffins*," she said. "Order some from Tredwell's in the village. Brown ones. Boys ruin brown ones slower."

Well. I've served her eleven years and I know her rations: she gives in units of practicality because kindness embarrasses her worse than scandal does. Boots instead of embraces. A tutor's timetable instead of a hand held. But I watched her watch that boy across the hall, and I watched her face while she thought she wasn't being watched, and I thought: *here's a woman building a wall around something brick by brick and calling each brick an inconvenience.*

I've burned things in my stove before — mistakes, letters, a soufflé once that still grieves me. I've a feeling I'll burn something again before this business ends. I find I don't mind. Some doughs need a hot oven.

### Zephyrine

The reading lessons began by accident, which is how most permanent things begin.

Emil had taken to following me on my rounds about the grounds, at a distance of exactly six feet, silent as my own shadow given legs. On Thursday I caught him crouched over a pamphlet the wind had pinned against the greenhouse glass — one of Lady Verity's, *WHY HALF THE NATION IS CHAINED*, bold as a shout. He was studying it upside down, lips moving.

"That's the wrong way up," I said.

He turned it right way up without embarrassment, which I admired. Most children pretend. "I know some," he admitted. "Mama taught. English words are — backwards. The sounds hide."

"The sounds hide," I repeated. "That's the best description of English spelling I've ever heard, and I went to a school where they charged for it."

So we sat on the greenhouse step in the October sun, and I taught him from the only reading matter this house produced in quantity, which was suffrage literature. He learned his *the*s off a pamphlet demanding votes for women. He sounded out *JUSTICE* letter by letter, then *BALLOT*, then frowned at *FRANCHISE* like it had personally insulted him.

"What is franchise?"

"A promise the country makes and forgets to keep."

He considered this. "Like Mama's address," he said. "She wrote it and then it wasn't true anymore."

I kept my voice level through sheer professional discipline. "Where did she write it?"

"On the soldier." He dug in his pocket, produced the tin figure, and showed me its base, where someone had scratched a name of a street and a city with a pin — careful little letters, half worn already by a thumb. Antwerp. I memorized it in the way I memorize everything, which is completely and against my will, and then I handed it back and said, "Your mama sounds clever."

"She reads fast," he said. "Faster than you."

"Faster than me," I agreed solemnly. "Nobody reads faster than me."

"I will," he said, "when I'm big," and bent back over his pamphlet, and I sat very still on that step and understood that I had just been promised a future by a seven-year-old, and that I had, apparently, accepted.

Lady Verity found us there an hour later. She stood at the end of the path with her hands behind her back, watching the boy spell out *DEMAND* with his finger tracing each letter, and something crossed her face too quick to catch — grief, maybe, or arithmetic. She had buried a son at Ypres in '14's opening weeks; the whole county knew and none of them mentioned it, least of all to her.

"Miss Fairweather," she said, "you're teaching him sedition."

"Spelling, my lady. Sedition comes next term."

"Hm." She regarded Emil, who had looked up and gone wary — boys learn quickly which adults are safe and which are weather. "Can you read numbers?"

"Yes, lady."

"Good. Then you shall help me with the campaign accounts. Every household needs an auditor who can't be bribed." And she walked on toward the stables, having, in the space of nine words, assigned the child a role, a dignity, and a place at her own desk — and having acknowledged to absolutely no one, including herself, that she had done anything of the kind.

Mrs. Pargeter, passing with a basket, caught my eye and raised one eyebrow a full inch. Cooks and drivers, we see everything. Between us we could have run the Empire, and between us, quietly, we were running something smaller and harder: a lie shaped like a home.

### Lady Verity

I have been accused of many failings, most of them accurately, but sentimentality is not among them. Let the record show, therefore, that what follows was strategy.

A household containing an unexplained child is a household with a wound. Wounds attract inquiry, and inquiry attracts Mr. Halloway, whose nose for other people's business is the finest instrument of its kind since bloodhounds went out of fashion. The correct treatment for such a wound is to make it boring. Refugees are boring; Kent is *full* of them. So: Emile Verhoeven of Antwerp, war orphan, distant connection of a Belgian acquaintance of mine, deposited here by the chaos of nations. Dull. Unimpeachable. The kind of fact a magistrate writes down and never thinks of again.

I instructed Zephyrine accordingly, and she absorbed the fiction the way she absorbs everything — silently, thoroughly, and with an efficiency that occasionally unsettles me. There is a quality to that young woman I have never quite named. She arranges herself to be overlooked; she succeeds; and yet one has the recurring suspicion of being *observed* by someone officially invisible. I have employed forty years of difficult people and I know a locked room when I dust around one.

But I digress. The accounts. The boy came to my study Thursday, boots already scuffed as prophesied, and checked my column sums with the grave intensity of a customs inspector. He found an error — my error, a transposed six — and pointed it out with the tin soldier, using the soldier as a pointer, which I permitted because correcting him would have required admitting I minded.

"You write angry," he said suddenly, tapping the margin of my draft speech.

"I write *forcibly*."

"Mama says angry writing means scared heart."

There was a silence of perhaps four seconds during which I reviewed thirty years of public life and found nothing whatsoever to say. Then I said, "Your mother sounds wise," and gave him a barley sugar from the drawer where I keep them for the vicar's dreadful children, and dismissed him, and sat alone in my study feeling as though a small grey-eyed mirror had been held up to me and had shown more than I'd authorized.

Zephyrine came for the post at six. I watched her sort it — bills, circulars, the *Times*, and one envelope in a foreign hand that she set apart with a hesitation I clocked and filed away. She thinks she is unreadable. She is readable to anyone who has spent a lifetime reading rooms.

"Anything of note?" I asked mildly.

"Nothing requiring your signature, my lady."

"Mm." I let it go. One does not pry open a locked room without knowing what's inside, and lately I have begun to suspect the contents of hers concern a handwriting I have seen before on pre-war correspondence from Berlin, and a debt I have never spoken aloud.

The house is holding four now, whatever the census says. It holds crookedly, like furniture assembled without instructions — a cook burning things on purpose, a driver teaching revolution to a refugee, a magistrate's shadow lengthening weekly across the lane, and myself, supposedly the mind of the establishment, discovering at sixty-one that I have acquired a grandson-shaped weakness and no lawful way to declare it.

Mr. Halloway called at the vicarage on Sunday. He asked questions. The vicar, poor man, answered them.

By Monday morning there was a note on my desk from Zephyrine, written in that neat portable hand: *My lady — the butcher's boy says Halloway has been asking at Tredwell's about the boots.*
