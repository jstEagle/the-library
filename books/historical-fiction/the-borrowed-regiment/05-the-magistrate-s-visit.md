## 5. The Magistrate's Visit

I had always believed that a lie, properly told, was like a badly packed trunk—it would hold until the first corner, and then everything would spill out onto the pavement for the whole street to see. I had built my life on never having to find out. Twenty-six years of being the woman who could be carried out with the luggage: no debts, no scandals, no opinions strong enough to be quoted back at me in a courtroom. When you are nobody in particular, nobody comes looking for you.

Mr. Halloway came looking on a Tuesday.

I knew his motorcar before I knew it was his—a black Daimler that took the gravel drive at a pace suggesting the driver had been instructed to impress rather than arrive. By the time it crunched to a halt before the steps, I had already done three things without thinking about any of them: set down the silver I was polishing, glanced through the morning room window to confirm Emil was safely in the kitchen garden with Mrs. Pargeter's cat for company, and straightened my collar.

The last of these surprised me most. One does not tidy oneself for the police unless some part of one expects to be examined.

"Miss Fairweather." He stood on the step where Emil had stood five weeks earlier, though he filled it differently—a broad man in a good grey suit, with the particular stillness of someone who has spent years watching other people fidget. "Magistrate Halloway. I wonder if Lady Ashcombe is at home."

"Lady Ashcombe is in Ashford today," I said, which was true. "She's expected back by six." Also true. I had driven her there myself that morning.

"Then perhaps you'd be good enough to answer a few questions." He produced a small notebook from his breast pocket with the unhurried air of a man drawing a pistol at a dinner party. "Nothing formal. Merely routine."

There is no such thing as merely routine when a magistrate arrives unannounced asking questions. I showed him into the morning room—the public room, the room with nothing in it but furniture—and asked if he would take tea.

"I shan't keep you long enough."

*You'll keep yourself exactly as long as you please,* I thought, and poured anyway, because Mrs. Pargeter had taught me early that a cup of tea in a visitor's hands keeps those hands from doing anything worse.

He did not drink it. He turned instead to the window, surveying the lawn as though appraising it for seizure, and said, pleasantly: "There's been a report. From one of your neighbours. A child seen on the grounds here who doesn't match any of the local registers. A boy, they say. Foreign-looking."

"Foreign-looking." I let the words sit between us like an unpaid bill. "In Kent?"

"In Kent." His smile was thin and patient. "These days, Miss Fairweather, foreignness has a way of announcing itself. Belgians mostly, since the invasion—poor souls, and God knows we owe them shelter—but a magistrate must ask. Is there a refugee child in this house?"

And here is what I can tell you now, writing this afterward with steady hands: there is a moment before a lie, a half-second of perfect clarity, when every version of the truth arranges itself before you like hats on a stand. The truth—that Emil Lindqvist was at this moment chasing a cat among the bean rows, that his mother's letters were folded inside my copy of *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*, that I had promised a woman across the North Sea I would forget her face and had failed within the hour—stood there plainly, waiting to be chosen, and would have ruined us all.

I chose the hat beside it.

"There is," I said.

His pen paused above the notebook. "There is?"

"A Belgian boy. War orphan. He arrived in August—you may verify the date against the parish register; Father Anselm baptized him into the Church of England the week after, poor mite, having lost everything including his name. We call him Emil because—" and here I produced something I did not know I possessed, a catch in the voice, a small wet shine, timed precisely, "—because it was the only word he answered to for the first fortnight. We thought changing even that would be cruel."

Every word of it was false. Every word of it was flawless. I watched him write *Belgian — baptized — parish register* and understood, with a sensation not unlike vertigo, that I could have kept going. That the lie had rooms in it. Corridors. I could have walked him through the whole house of it and shown him the view.

"You'll forgive me," Halloway said, not looking up, "but the neighbour's account mentioned the child spoke German."

"He speaks Flemish." I refilled his untouched tea, a hostess's rebuke so gentle it qualified as affection. "Which sounds sufficiently like German to frighten a lady who has read the papers. Mrs. Colton, I expect? At Hollyhocks? She heard him counting—one, two, three—and decided the Kaiser himself was billeted in the orchard. I'd have been happy to clear the matter up in August if anyone had asked me then, Mr. Halloway. It seems a shame it required a magistrate to make a seven-year-old's arithmetic a matter of state."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not belief—I was under no illusions about that—but recalculation. I was not the person he had been told to expect. He had come prepared for the flustered servant or the defiant suffragette, and found instead a woman who poured tea and quoted baptismal dates, and his notes were suddenly insufficient.

"The father," he said. "Where is he?"

"Ypres," I said, "or wherever they're burying Belgians this month. We don't press the boy on it."

"And how did he come to be here? Lady Ashcombe is a busy woman. A *prominent* woman." He let the word carry its cargo: *suffragist, troublemaker, the woman who chained herself to the railings in '08.* "Refugees generally go through official channels."

"Official channels," I said, "were somewhat occupied in August, as I recall. There was a priest at Calais doing what arranging he could, and Lady Verity took two children off his list because no one else would. The girl was placed with the vicarage at Wye. You may check that too." I hadn't invented the girl; I had simply promoted her from a rumour to a fact. A lie with load-bearing walls of truth stands longer than any other kind.

He closed the notebook. That frightened me more than the open one had.

"Well," he said, rising, "you've been very helpful, Miss Fairweather. More helpful, I suspect, than you realize." He paused at the door, and gave me a smile that was almost warm, which is the most dangerous temperature a magistrate's smile can reach. "Give Lady Ashcombe my regards. And do tell her—no, I'll tell her myself. These things go better face to face."

I saw him to his car. I stood on the step and waved as it pulled away, because I had learned in the past twenty minutes that I was capable of anything.

* * *

Mrs. Pargeter found me in the scullery twenty minutes later, standing very still over a basin of water I had no memory of running.

"Well?" she said.

"He believed me." I turned round. My voice came out strange, level, unfamiliar—as though someone wearing my clothes had spoken. "Or he didn't, and he's going to pretend he did until it's useful not to. Either way, Pargeter—he wrote down the parish register. He wrote down Ypres. I gave him dates. I gave him a *priest at Calais*."

"You gave him what for?" she demanded. "We never arranged any priest."

"No," I agreed. "We didn't."

She looked at me the way she looked at a soufflé that has risen when every law of her kitchen said it shouldn't—with pride and suspicion in equal measure. "Where did you learn to do that, then? Lie like a duchess?"

I opened my mouth to say *nowhere*, which would have been the last honest thing available to me, and stopped. Because the answer was there, and it had been there for months: I had learned it in the margins of Greta Lindqvist's letters. In learning to say one thing to the censor and mean another underneath. In reading a woman's true meaning through the gaps she was forced to leave. Every letter I had ever smuggled sense through was practice. Every careful sentence I had written around the black line of censorship had been a rehearsal for a grey-suited man in a morning room.

"I learned it from the war," I said, which was nearly true, and getting truer by the day.

Emil came in then with the cat under one arm and a fistful of runner beans he had harvested with more enthusiasm than judgment, and announced—in English, our English, the English I had taught him from pamphlets, *Deeds*, he could say, and *Not Words*, and counted to ten in Flemish only when he wanted to make Mrs. Pargeter laugh—"Zephyrine, the cat has caught nothing AGAIN."

Halloway had asked if there was a foreign child in this house, and I had lied, and the lie had worked, and the child whose existence depended on it was holding beans and demanding justice for a cat. Something in my chest that had spent five weeks clenched like a fist went very quiet and very hard.

Fear, I discovered, is a fuel. It burns cleaner than courage, lasts longer, and leaves no ash to confess to.

That evening I drove to Ashford to fetch Lady Verity home, and somewhere on the dark road between Lenham and the crossroads I made a decision I did not yet say aloud, even to myself: that the next time Halloway came—and he would come; men like him always come back for the second look—I would not be standing on the step alone with my wits. I would have the letters. All of them. Greta's letters, with their censored margins and their buried meanings, the proof of whatever it was Greta had done and whatever it was she feared, stacked and ordered and ready.

Because a lie holds until the first corner. But the truth, properly packed—

Properly packed, the truth will hold anything.

When I reached Ashford, Lady Verity was waiting on the platform with her campaign satchel and an expression I had never seen her wear before. She handed me a telegram without a word.

It was from London. The Home Office. Regarding *enemy persons of interest in the county of Kent.*

It was addressed not to her.

It was addressed to *the household at Ashcombe Manor, attention: the driver, Miss Z. Fairweather.*

They had my name.
