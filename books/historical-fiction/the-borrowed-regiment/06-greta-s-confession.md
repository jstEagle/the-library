## 6. Greta's Confession

I had told myself the letters were finished with me. The boy was fed, the lie about Belgium was stitched into place, and the house had learned his footsteps. But a promise made in the margins of someone else's correspondence is a strange kind of debt—it sits in you like a stone in a boot, unnoticed until you walk, and then felt at every step.

So on the third night after the boy arrived, I took the letters down from behind the loose brick in my room and read them again, all of them, from the beginning.

* * *

The first letter had come in March, before the war had teeth, when it was merely growling. Greta Lindqvist wrote to Lady Verity about suffrage in Hamburg, about her husband Karl's family—Lindqvists of some standing in Lübeck, shipowners or something of that sort—and about a son she called "our little storm," which I now understood to mean Emil. She wrote beautifully. That was the trouble with beautiful writers: they made you feel you knew them, and knowing people is how you come to owe them.

The later letters changed. By August they were shorter, careful as a person walking on river ice. By October they were being opened by someone else first—I could tell by the way the envelopes had been resealed, by the censor's pencil strokes like little grey scars through sentences. And by November, Greta had begun writing *around* the censor rather than through him, saying things sideways, in the spaces between what was permitted.

It was one of these that stopped me tonight. Dated the fourteenth of November:

*My dear Verity — You will forgive me if I do not speak plainly; there is a gentleman whose whole employment consists in reading this before you do, and I would not wish to take the bread from his mouth.*

*Things here are becoming simple, which is another word for terrible. A woman may be a citizen of nowhere very quickly. My husband's family has decided what I am. They have a list, and I am on it, though the list has no name except their faces at dinner.*

*Karl writes that he cannot help me. He writes this in the voice of a man describing weather. He is where they have put him, and where they have put him is not a place from which one argues.*

*I have a question for you, old friend, and I ask it as one asks a question across a border: if a thing were sent to your door, would your door open? Do not answer. Doors should not announce themselves. But know that I remember a conversation we once had, in 1911, about whether courage is a possession or an expenditure. You said expenditure. I am spending mine.*

*Tell me nothing. Show me everything. — G.L.*

Show me everything. At the time, reading it in November by candlelight, I had thought it a figure of speech. Now I understood it was an instruction, sent ahead of a child like a lantern hung out for a ship.

There was more. There was a letter from December I had read three times and each time stopped at the same place, the way one's tongue returns to a sore tooth. I found it now without searching.

*Verity — I will say this once and then never again, because even sideways words can be caught.*

*You had a brother. You spoke of him once, in 1910, on the terrace at Bad Homburg, and you said he was the only man you trusted, and then you laughed and said that was a low bar. Thomas. Thomas Fairweather's sister. He was taken in Kiel in '09 — you know this, you spent a year and a small fortune trying to learn where. What you do not know is who answered.*

*I was young and newly married and I had access to a dinner table where men discussed such things between the fish and the meat. I asked a question. It cost me nothing then. It has cost me a great deal since, but that is arithmetic, and I have never been afraid of numbers.*

*He went home. That was me. I have never told anyone, because a favour unpaid is a leash, and I did not want to hold a leash on a woman I admired. I wanted her to owe me nothing, so that anything she did for me afterward would be freely given.*

*But now I am asking. Not for me. For a boy who calls every loud noise "thunder" because he has been taught to call it that.*

*Forget the face of the woman who leaves him. Remember only that she was once kind to a stranger's brother.*

*G.*

I sat with that letter for a long while. The candle burned down a thumb's width. Somewhere below, Mrs. Pargeter's banked stove ticked as it cooled, the sound of a house settling around its secrets.

Thomas. My Thomas. Taken in Kiel the year I turned nineteen, vanished for eleven months while Verity tore through consulates and shipping offices and finally, humiliatingly, the offices of men who enjoyed being petitioned. He came home thin and silent and would never say where, only that "someone with a long spoon" had got him out, and he'd laughed—Thomas laughed at everything—and said the soup in Prussia was an argument against the empire lasting out the decade.

Eleven months. And somewhere between the fish and the meat, a German woman I had never met had put down her glass and asked a question.

I had spent my whole life believing I owed no one. That was the architecture of me—the portable life, the forgettable face, the woman who could be carried out with the trunks. Indebtedness is heavy; it does not pack well. I had arranged myself carefully into a creature with no debts and therefore no anchors.

And it had all been built on a lie of omission. I had been carried through 1909 on the strength of a stranger's dinner-table courage, and never known it, and gone on calling myself self-sufficient the way a man calls himself self-made when his mother paid the fees.

* * *

In the morning I drove Lady Verity into Ashford, and she talked the whole way about the inquiry Halloway was manoeuvring for—she had heard it from her cousin on the bench, she always heard things, the county was a colander and gossip poured through it—and I gave the correct murmurs at the correct intervals while my mind kept returning to a dinner table in Lübeck.

"Zephyrine." She had that tone. The one that meant my murmurs had drifted out of rhythm. "You have said 'indeed' four times and meant it none of the four."

"I'm sorry, my lady."

"Don't apologise. Tell me."

I watched the road. A cart loaded with hay, two boys walking, a church spire doing its usual impersonation of permanence. "Do you ever regret it," I said, "the things you don't know about people?"

"Constantly. It's why I make a point of finding out." She looked at me sidelong. Her eyes are grey and miss nothing smaller than a pin, and I am approximately pin-sized. "Is this about the boy?"

"Everything is about the boy now."

"Good. Things should be about him." She settled back. "Halloway will move within the fortnight. When he does, we will want to know precisely what we claim and what we withhold. I suggest you spend the intervening time deciding which you are."

"Which what?"

"Which you are. Claimed or withheld." She said it lightly, the way she says the unforgivable things, and looked out at the hay cart as though she had commented on the weather.

* * *

That afternoon Emil sat on the kitchen step learning his letters off a pamphlet headed *WHAT WOMEN WANT AND WHY*, which was not the primer Mrs. Pargeter would have chosen but was the paper we had.

"'F-f-fair,'" he sounded out. "Fair. Like your name."

"That's right. Fairweather."

"Fair weather means good weather." He frowned up at me, seven years old and grave as a magistrate. "Are you good weather?"

"Occasionally," I said. "Mostly I'm overcast with occasional bright intervals."

He considered this, decided it was acceptable, and returned to his pamphlet. Then, without looking up, in the tone children use for the questions adults have been dodging: "Did my mother write to you? Before?"

I kept my hands steady on the mending in my lap. "What makes you ask that?"

"She reads letters at night. She used to read them out loud to me, the funny ones." His chin trembled once and was firmly suppressed, a small jaw doing the work of a garrison. "She said if I was ever scared I should look for the lady who reads letters. Because readers of letters are brave."

"Did she say that?"

"She said—" he screwed up his face, retrieving it exactly, a scholar with his text, "—she said, *a person who reads other people's letters and doesn't turn them in is braver than a soldier, because a soldier only risks his own skin.*"

The wind moved the pamphlet page. I let the silence sit until it became an answer.

"Your mother," I said, "is the bravest woman I have never met."

"You've met her letters."

"Yes," I said. "I have met her letters."

* * *

That night I did something I had been circling for days the way a moth circles a lamp, telling itself it is admiring it. I took fresh paper and wrote—not in a margin this time, not sideways, not deniable—but directly, plainly, in my own name, which is to say in no name at all, since Zephyrine Fairweather has always been more a function than a person.

*Dear Greta Lindqvist,* I wrote. *You do not know me. I am the one who read your letters and answered them in the margins, and I am the one who opened the door.*

*Your son eats like a stevedore and learns his letters off suffrage pamphlets. He has named the cook's cat after Bismarck, which the cat resents. He sleeps with a tin soldier and flinches at thunder, and we are teaching him, slowly, that English thunder is an honest thunder that means only what it says.*

*You asked once whether our door would open. It opened. I want you to understand that it was not Lady Verity's door that opened first. It was mine. Whatever happens next—whatever inquiries, whatever magistrates—I opened it, and I do not unsay it.*

*You saved a man from a Prussian prison in 1909. He was my brother. I learned it four nights ago and I am still learning it. I have spent thirty-one years owing no one, and it turns out I have been in your debt since I was nineteen years old, and I find—this is the strange part—that I am glad. Debt means connection. Connection means I am not portable anymore.*

*I promised to keep him. I am keeping him. Keep yourself alive to hear it someday.*

*— The reader of your letters*

I read it over twice. Then I did the reckless thing, the thing that would have horrified the woman I had been in September: I signed a name. Not Zephyrine Fairweather, driver and reader-of-letters, unremarkable and indispensable. I wrote *Zephyrine*, alone, as though it belonged to somebody.

I sealed it and addressed it care of the Red Cross bureau in Geneva, the route Greta herself had suggested in her last letter, the one neutral thread left running between the countries.

Then I sat holding it and understood what I had done. A letter can be intercepted. A letter can be produced. Mr. Halloway's clerk could hold this envelope up at an inquiry and say, *and who, madam, is the reader of enemy correspondence?* Everything I had built—the smooth lies, the careful invisibility, the woman who could vanish with the trunks—all of it now had a return address.

Mrs. Pargeter would have burned it. The old Zephyrine would have burned it.

I posted it in Ashford the next morning, and stood a moment watching the box, feeling lighter and more exposed than I had ever felt in my life, the way a house feels with its shutters thrown open—every room visible, and the morning coming in anyway.

Two days later, Lady Verity sent for me, and her face told me before she said a word that Halloway's men had been busy too.
