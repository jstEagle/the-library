## 7. What Mrs. Pargeter Saw

I have been a cook for thirty-one years, and in that time I have learned that a kitchen is the confessional of any great house. People think the priest's box holds the secrets. It doesn't. Secrets come down to the kitchen on the soles of shoes and in the bottoms of teacups, and they sit themselves by my range like tired travellers, and I feed them whether I mean to or not.

I fed this one before I even knew its name.

It arrived in October, on a Thursday, in the shape of a boy with a tin soldier in his fist and a face like a dropped plate. Mrs. Fairweather brought him through the back door at dawn — she who never used the back door, because back doors are for people with nothing to hide — and she said, "Mrs. Pargeter, Lady Verity has taken in a war orphan," in a voice that had been rehearsed on the drive, and I looked at the boy and I thought: Belgian, is he? With those eyes? I've baked for Belgians at the fêtes. I know what Belgian children look like when they're frightened, poor souls, and it isn't *that*. That was a different fright. That was a fright with a border in it.

But I am not a woman who says such things aloud. A cook who says everything she sees lasts one season. A cook who says nothing lasts until her knees go, and mine have years left in them yet.

So I said, "He'll want breakfast," and I fried him an egg soft, because children who arrive at dawn are always hungry and always ashamed of it.

* * *

Over the following fortnight I collected the rest, the way one collects drippings — patiently, without comment, saving it all for something.

I saw that the boy flinched when the paper said *German* — which it did most days, being 1914 — and that he flinched hardest when nobody was looking at him, which told me he'd learned to be afraid quietly, and that somebody had taught him quiet fear, and that somebody loved him enough to teach it.

I saw Mrs. Fairweather teaching him his letters from suffrage pamphlets, of all things, so that the child's first written word was *VOTE*, which will either ruin him or make him, and I confess I hope for the second.

I saw Lady Verity look at the boy over her spectacles one evening as though doing arithmetic — the kind where you keep getting an answer you don't like — and then say, "More bread, Emil?" in a voice pitched exactly like a woman deciding something.

And I saw the letters. Well. I saw the *shape* of the letters, which is nearly as good.

Mrs. Fairweather reads Lady Verity's post; everyone knows that; it's why she was hired, or part of why. But there were certain envelopes she carried up to her own room instead of down to the study, and certain nights the light under her door burned past midnight, and once — only once — I heard her reading aloud in there, low, in a language that wasn't English, and the voice answering her was hers but younger, the way a voice goes when it remembers someone.

A cook files these things. We file them between the recipes and the grudges.

* * *

The clerk came on a Tuesday, because clerks always come on Tuesdays — it's their Sabbath, I believe, the day the great machinery of officialdom grinds slowest and a man can be spared to poke about in other people's butteries.

Mr. Halloway's clerk was called Tibbs, or Tibbetts, or something equally made of blotting-paper. He arrived in a coat too good for the weather and a manner too pleasant for the errand, and he presented Lady Verity's card to me across my own scullery table as though it were a warrant, which in spirit it was.

"Lady Verity kindly agreed I might take some refreshment while I inspect the estate accounts with Mr. Naylor," he said. "The magistrate's office likes to be thorough."

"The magistrate's office likes to be nosy," I said, "but thorough has a better ring to it, I'll grant."

He laughed. Clerks always laugh. Then he sat himself at my table, drank my tea, praised my seed cake in the tones of a man laying a trail of crumbs toward somewhere he shouldn't go, and began.

"A sad business, the war," he offered. "So many displaced persons coming through Kent now. The magistrate takes a particular interest in their welfare."

"Very Christian of him," I said, kneading.

"And of households like this one. Charitable houses." He turned his cup. "We hear the Ashcombe household took in a foundling. Belgian, I'm told?"

"War orphan," I corrected, because the distinction mattered to whoever had invented it, and I wasn't going to be the first to let it slip. "Belgian. Poor mite came to the door with nothing but a soldier made of tin."

"Tin," he repeated softly, as though writing it down inside his hat. "And how does the household find him? Settled in well?"

"He eats," I said. "Children who eat settle wherever they are. That's the whole secret of childhood, sir, and of most of adulthood besides."

Another laugh, another crumb laid down. Then, casually as a man asking after my mother's health:

"You'll have seen correspondence, of course, in a house like this. Foreign letters. One can't be too careful these days — half the post from the Continent is suspect. If anything unusual ever crossed your kitchen, Mrs. Pargeter, the magistrate would look on it very favourably. Very favourably indeed." And here he set down a coin — a shilling, no less, the extravagant devil — beside his cup, as if buying my discretion outright at market price.

Now. I have taken tips in my time. Every cook has; we'd starve otherwise, and prettier staff than me have taken worse than shillings. But there is a difference between a tip and a bribe, and the difference is that a tip thanks you and a bribe tests you, and I have never yet passed a test I didn't choose myself.

"That coin," I said, "will buy you the rest of the seed cake and my opinion of your employer, which is free and unflattering. Anything further costs more than you're carrying."

He coloured, apologised, gathered his hat — and paused at the door with the air of a man remembering an afterthought he'd rehearsed all morning.

"One small thing," he said. "The young person who drives for Lady Verity — Mrs. Fairweather. How long has she been with the household?"

"Long enough," I said, "to know which questions get answered in this kitchen and which get washed up with the rest."

And off he went, trailing politeness behind him like a smell.

* * *

Here is the thing I have not yet said, and it is the whole of the matter.

That same morning — an hour before Tibbs arrived — I had gone up the back stair to fetch Mrs. Fairweather's mending basket, because her ankle was bad and she'd left it in the linen room, and I am the only person in that house who thinks of mending before anyone asks. And tucked inside the basket, folded into a spare apron where no one folds letters, was a page in foreign handwriting.

I didn't read it. I want that understood. I am a cook, not a spy; reading is Lady Verity's department and Mrs. Fairweather's, and my department is knowing when *not* to look, which is the rarer skill and pays worse.

But I saw enough. I saw the date — September, before the boy came — and I saw the name at the foot of it, or the shape of a name ending in *-qvist*, Swedish-shaped, German-adjacent, and I saw, in the margin, in Mrs. Fairweather's neat hand, three lines of English that I could not help catching the way one catches a fall:

*I will keep him. You have my word. Whatever comes.*

Well.

I stood in that linen room holding an apron full of somebody else's dangerous promise, and I thought about my late husband, Bert, who spent forty years saying nothing worth saying and died comfortable, and I thought about the boy eating his egg soft at dawn, and I thought about what happens to promises left lying in mending baskets when men like Tibbs come sniffing with their hats full of notes.

Then I did something I had never done in thirty-one years of kitchens.

I put the letter in my pocket, carried it downstairs, and burned it in the range.

* * *

You will ask — Bert asked me similar questions his whole life, mostly with his eyebrows — why burn it? Why not return it? Why not lock it away?

Because returned things get found, and locked things get unlocked, and burnt things get believed in. That letter was a rope thrown across a gap, and ropes don't survive inspection; they survive being *used*. Mrs. Fairweather had made her promise twice — once in ink, once in fire — and fire is the copy that can't be seized by a magistrate. Let the original live in her memory, where it belonged, and let the ashes live in my grate, where nobody living would ever sift them but me.

Besides — and I admit this freely, sitting here with my knees aching and my conscience entirely at ease — I did not do it for strategy. I did it because that boy calls me "Mrs. Peg" in an accent he's trying to flatten into Kentish, because he carries wood for me without being asked and counts the logs wrong every time and looks so proud of the wrong number that I haven't the heart to correct him, because last week he stood on a stool and stirred batter until his arm gave out and declared it the finest work of his life.

I have buried a husband and no children. I am sixty-two years old. I had assumed the account was closed, that nothing more would be added to the ledger, that I would go out the way cooks go out — remembered fondly at two households, forgotten everywhere else.

And then the ledger opened again, on a Thursday, at dawn, with a tin soldier.

Let Halloway send his clerks. Let them count my tins and weigh my flour and ask their little questions with their little shillings. They will find a cook of unimpeachable temper, a war orphan who eats, and a grate full of ash that means nothing at all.

They will not find what I saw, because what I saw is no longer evidence. It is allegiance.

* * *

That evening Mrs. Fairweather came down for the mending basket and stopped dead when she saw it waiting, and I watched her check it — quick, professional, the flick of a woman counting her secrets — and watched the colour leave her face when she counted one short.

Our eyes met across the kitchen. Thirty-one years of practice went into keeping my face at baking temperature.

"The draft up there," I said, stirring the pot, "plays havoc with loose papers. Best keep important things where the wind can't reach them. Or where only friends can."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then, very quietly: "Mrs. Pargeter—"

"Eat your supper," I said, and set a bowl in front of her, and that was the whole of the oath, sworn in gravy as all the best oaths are.

She ate. The boy came clattering in with his wrong number of logs, and Lady Verity's bell rang for her tray, and the house closed around us like a hand making a fist — gently, but around something it meant to keep.

It was near midnight when the knock came at the kitchen door — hard, official, far too late for anything innocent. I wiped my hands, looked at Mrs. Fairweather, who had gone white as flour, and went to answer it myself, because whatever the world wanted from this house next, it would have to come through the kitchen first, and I intended to be standing in front of the stove when it did.
