## 9. The Inquiry

I had spent eleven years perfecting the art of not being noticed, and on the morning of the inquiry I discovered I had no talent at all for the opposite.

The county hall at Maidstone smelled of wet wool, floor polish, and fear—the last of it mine, though I flattered myself it was well concealed. Lady Verity had dressed me herself, which was its own kind of alarm. She had stood in my narrow room holding up a grey dress as though inspecting a recruit.

"You will look respectable," she said. "Respectability is armour. Wear it and then say exactly what you mean. The combination confuses men like Halloway more than any argument."

"And if it doesn't confuse him?"

"Then we shall be confused together." She smoothed my collar with two brisk tugs, an oddly maternal gesture from a woman who had once told a heckler in Tonbridge that she'd rather be tarred than patronised. "Zephyrine. Whatever happens in that room, you are to remember that you chose this. Nobody dragged you. That is the whole point."

I had chosen it. That was the strange part. For weeks I had rehearsed retreats—trains to London, a cousin in Norfolk who owed me nothing, the old portable life folded up like campaign luggage. And every rehearsal ended the same way: with Emil's tin soldier on the hall table where he always left it, standing sentry over nothing, waiting for him to come back from the garden and pick it up again. You cannot carry a boy out with the trunks. You can only stay.

* * *

The inquiry was held in the council chamber, under a ceiling painted with county worthies who gazed down with the serene disapproval of men who had never had to queue for anything. Mr. Halloway sat at the far end of the long table in his best aldermanic gravity, flanked by his clerk and a solicitor whose expression suggested he had already billed us for the afternoon. Lady Verity was given a chair at the opposite end, which she occupied like a throne she had merely deigned to borrow.

Emil was not present. Mrs. Pargeter had him in the kitchen corridor with a jam tart and strict instructions to count the flagstones if anyone spoke to him. He had counted them twice before we left and reported the total with the solemnity of a general giving troop numbers: forty-one.

The chairman called the room to order. There were perhaps thirty people present—neighbours, a reporter or two, the woman from the vicarage who had first reported "foreign persons" at Ashcombe Manor and who now wore the particular glow of a gossip vindicated by officialdom.

Halloway opened as I expected him to open: reasonably. That was always his weapon. Reasonableness, deployed against a woman famous for being unreasonable on principle.

"This is not," he began, "a tribunal against Lady Ashcombe, whom we all know and—ah—respect. It is a question of procedure. An alien child, found upon her doorstep, of documented German origin. Under the circumstances, the proper course is clear. The child should be registered, and placed with—" he consulted a paper he did not need to consult— "the appropriate authorities."

"Which authorities," said Lady Verity, "have been so overwhelmed by Belgian refugee children that they have lost track of forty of them since October?"

"The Belgian children, madam, are a separate matter."

"Because they are not German."

"Because," said Halloway, with the patience of a man feeding a slow fire, "they are not *enemy*."

There it was. The word landed in the room like a dropped glass. I watched faces turn away from each other, watchful, embarrassed. Emil flinched at that word even when he didn't understand the sentences around it; he understood this one the way dogs understand thunder.

Lady Verity answered him—I could not afterwards recall a word of it, only the sound, that magnificent organ-stop voice filling the chamber until the painted worthies seemed to lean in—and while she held the room I did what I had done for eleven years. I waited in the useful quiet. I checked my bag. The letters were there, wrapped in oilcloth because I am thorough even about treason. Greta Lindqvist's correspondence, three years of it: the pre-war friendship letters, the wartime ones with their margins full of my handwriting, and the last one, the confession, which I had read so many times I could recite it against the ceiling in the dark.

*I send you my son because you are the only Englishwoman I know who has never once lied to me. Forget my face, forget the face of the woman who brings him. If they ask you nothing, they can take nothing.*

And beneath it, in Greta's smaller hand, the part I had carried like a stone in my boot for a month:

*Ask your quiet friend with the beautiful margins whether she remembers a letter of 1911, from a man named Brandt, regarding a prisoner in Wesel. Tell her Theo Fairweather sends his regards, and that he is alive because a stranger chose to be seen.*

Theo. My brother, missing since 1911, presumed dead by everyone including, most days, me. Alive. Saved from a Prussian prison by a German correspondent I had never met, whose son I had promised—promised, with a note slipped into the margin of a letter that might never reach her—to keep.

Halloway's clerk read out the witness statement then. The woman detained at the alien camp had identified Ashcombe Manor; the child had been conveyed to England under false pretences; the household had represented him as Belgian. Facts, laid out flat and damning as specimens pinned to a board.

"Can the household," Halloway concluded, folding his hands, "produce any evidence whatsoever that this child was not placed here as part of some design? Any evidence at all beyond the word of a woman"—and here he turned, slowly, deliberately, and looked directly at me— "whose entire employment consists of handling Lady Ashcombe's private correspondence?"

The room turned with him. Thirty faces. Eleven years of being furniture, and now furniture with a name and a case number.

"Miss Fairweather," said the chairman. "You are the lady's driver and secretary. Do you wish to add anything?"

What I wished was to be sick into my hat. What I said was:

"Yes."

It came out steady. I have no idea how. I stood, and my knees behaved, and I unwrapped the oilcloth as though I were unpacking Lady Verity's trunks, which is to say competently, which is to say invisibly—one last time.

"I have handled this correspondence for three years," I said. "All of it. Including the letters from Germany, including those portions which ought by rights to have gone to the censor. I read them because nobody watches a secretary reading; that is rather the point of a secretary. And I answered some of them. In the margins. Which was foolish, and private, and none of this committee's business—but it is how I came to know Greta Lindqvist, and how I know that every fact Mr. Halloway has just presented is true, and every conclusion he has drawn from them is a lie."

The chairman made a sound like a man swallowing his own gavel. Somewhere behind me the reporter's pencil stopped, then started again very fast.

"Miss Fairweather," said Halloway, silkily, "are you confessing to intercepting and answering enemy correspondence?"

"I am confessing to corresponding with a German woman about her child, her husband, and the war, in the margins of letters neither of us was authorised to write. If that is a crime, sir, arrest me after I've finished, because what I have to say next will take ten minutes and you'll want it for your report."

Someone laughed. It broke something open in the room—the held breath of it—and I heard Lady Verity, without turning her head, murmur, "*Now* you're learning."

I laid the letters out on the table before the committee, dated, ordered, the way I order everything. The last letter I placed face-up.

"Greta Lindqvist is the wife of Karl Lindqvist, conscripted in August 1914 into a regiment she has no reason to love. Her husband's family—his brother in particular—is a member of the Pan-German League and has offered, in Greta's own words, to 'take charge of the boy's education,' which in her letters means military school and the erasure of everything she is. She did not send her son here as a design, Mr. Halloway. She sent him here the way people throw things out of burning buildings. Read her own hand. She asks Lady Ashcombe to forget her face precisely so that no one could ever prove a connection—not because there was something to hide, but because there was someone to protect."

"And the identification by the detained woman?" said Halloway. "That is your answer to that, is it? A woman who walked twenty miles to a port with a seven-year-old and handed him to strangers—"

"Is a mother, sir, who did the arithmetic all of Europe is doing. She calculated that a country at war with hers would still keep a child fed. She was right. He has gained four pounds and learned to read. From pamphlets, admittedly, but one takes one's tutors where one finds them."

Another laugh, wider this time. Even the chairman's mouth twitched before discipline reclaimed it.

"And you assert," Halloway pressed, leaning forward, "that you can vouch for all this. You. A secretary."

Here it was, then. The place I had been walking towards since dawn on the step with a note in Greta's hand.

"No, sir. Vouching is for character references. What I assert is a promise. In March I wrote to Frau Lindqvist, in the margin of a letter, that if her son ever reached England I would see him safe. I did not make that promise as Lady Ashcombe's secretary. I made it as Zephyrine Fairweather, whose brother Lieutenant Theodore Fairweather entered Germany in 1911 on family business and disappeared, and who learned four weeks ago—from these letters—that he is alive in Hamburg because Greta Lindqvist bribed a prison clerk to lose his paperwork. I have been repaying a debt this whole time. I simply didn't know the account existed." I set my hands flat on the table, on her letters, on his. "So you may take the boy from this house if you wish, Mr. Halloway. But you will do it over the testimony of the one person in this county who has read every document relevant to his case, including several your office hasn't. And you will explain to this committee why an English officer's sister, acting on an Englishwoman's written request, constitutes an enemy design."

Silence. The real kind, the kind with weight. Halloway looked at the letters, and I watched him do the arithmetic I had done a month ago—that the documents were genuine, that they were damning to *him* now, that a magistrate who removed a rescued child from the sister of a serving British officer, on the strength of a neighbour's curtain-twitching, would be explaining himself to rather larger committees than this one.

His solicitor leaned over and whispered. The clerk wrote something and crossed it out.

The chairman cleared his throat. "In light of... the evidence presented, the committee finds insufficient grounds to classify the child Lindqvist as a prohibited alien under guardianship restrictions. Recommendation to the Board: registration under the protection of Lady V. Ashcombe, subject to standard review." A pause, and then, almost gently, the gavel came down. "This session is concluded."

The room emptied the way rooms do, in eddies and murmurs. The woman from the vicarage avoided my eyes. The reporter did not; she caught my arm at the door and said, "Miss Fairweather. Might I get your name spelled correctly?"

And I gave it to her. All of it. Spelled out loud, in front of God and Kent and the painted worthies on the ceiling, like a woman who had never once in her life been portable.

* * *

Mrs. Pargeter had the kettle on before the motorcar had fully stopped, and Emil came barrelling down the corridor with a jam-bright grin, tin soldier raised in salute. Lady Verity went past us both to her study without a word, which from her meant triumph.

I crouched to the boy's level. Forty-one flagstones, a tart, a soldier, a promise kept. He studied my face with those grave grey eyes—Greta's eyes—and asked, in his careful new English, "Did they say yes?"

"They said yes."

He nodded once, satisfied, and ran back to the kitchen, and I stayed crouched a moment longer than I needed to, because my legs had only just remembered their job.

That was when Lady Verity appeared in the study doorway with an envelope in her hand—a telegram, by the cut of it, and by her face, not from the Board.

"Zephyrine," she said. "Before you pour that tea. This came for you. Marked urgent. It's from Hamburg."

My name was on it. Not Miss Fairweather, secretary. *Zephyrine.* And inside, in a hand I had only ever known through margins, five words that undid me entirely:

*THEO IS COMING HOME. —G.*
