## 10. Everything Worth Keeping

Spring came to Kent the way forgiveness comes—slowly, and with a great deal of mud.

I had lived at Ashcombe Manor for six years before that winter, and I could have told you, in the old days, exactly how much of me belonged to it. Two trunks. A hatbox. A sewing kit my mother left me and a fountain pen I'd bought secondhand from a stationer in Maidstone who swore it had belonged to a colonel, though it wrote like it had belonged to a drunkard. Everything worth keeping, I used to say to myself on the nights when being portable felt less like freedom and more like erasure—everything worth keeping fits in two trunks.

It was a lie, of course. It had always been a lie. But it was a lie I'd built my whole life inside of, the way a snail builds its shell, and shells are comfortable right up until someone points out that you're carrying your house on your back because you don't believe anywhere will hold you.

The inquiry was four months behind us now. Four months since I stood up in a room that smelled of beeswax and civic self-importance and said, into a silence you could have bottled and sold as medicine: *My name is Zephyrine Fairweather, and I read Lady Ashcombe's letters.*

Four months since Mr. Halloway's face did something complicated, like a clock deciding whether to be a clock.

He had expected the boy to be the scandal. He had expected Lady Verity to be the scandal—he had been *hunting* for her to be the scandal for years, the way some men hunt foxes and some men hunt grievances. What he had not expected, what no one in that room had expected, was a driver and letter-reader nobody could put a face to standing up and putting a face on, and it was that, more than Greta's letters, more than the testimony of Mrs. Pargeter (who arrived at the inquiry in her good shawl and her terrible temper and dismantled his clerk's case like she was plucking a chicken), that broke the thing open. Halloway had built his entire case on the assumption that the household of Ashcombe Manor was exactly what it appeared to be.

We never were. That was rather the point.

Emil was declared a ward under Lady Verity's guardianship by the third week of January, after paperwork so extensive I began to suspect the War Office simply wanted paper. He was seven going on eight, and he had learned to read—not from the suffrage pamphlets anymore, though we still kept *The Suffragette* folded into his lesson books out of a kind of family piety, but from *Treasure Island*, which Mrs. Pargeter produced from a box in the attic and which Emil pronounced, with the gravity of a magistrate himself, "better than pamphlets."

"He's not wrong," Verity said, when I reported this.

"You could defend the cause."

"I could," said Lady Verity Ashcombe, who had once addressed a crowd of two thousand in a rainstorm without notes, "but I have been eight, and Stevenson wins."

* * *

I kept my post. I want to write that plainly, because there was a season—I can admit this now, to myself, in the privacy of my own head where I keep all my best admissions—when I believed the opposite would happen. That visibility, once achieved, was a door that swung shut behind you. That having stood up in front of the county and said *this is who I am*, I would somehow no longer be needed. The invisible woman is useful precisely because she is invisible; make her visible and what is she but another mouth at the table?

Lady Verity disabused me of this in her usual fashion, which is to say completely, and without appearing to notice she had done it.

"Fairweather," she said one morning in February, not looking up from her correspondence, "you'll drive me to Tunbridge Wells on Thursday, and you'll take minutes at the branch meeting, and after that I should like your opinion on the petition wording, because Cook's opinion runs heavily toward *burn the lot of them*, which is bracing but not actionable."

"Yes, my lady."

"And Fairweather." Now she looked up, and there was something in her face I had learned to read over six years the way sailors read weather. "You are not here because you were useful when you were small. You are here because you are not small. Do try to keep up."

I drove to Tunbridge Wells with my eyes stinging and the wind through the gap in the window doing its best to convince both of us it hadn't happened.

That was the thing about belonging, I was discovering. It wasn't a shell you grew or a place you were granted. It was a decision other people made about you, made aloud, in plain sentences, and then kept making, day after day, until you stopped waiting for them to unmake it.

* * *

Emil changed with the season the way gardens do—all at once, and mostly underground first.

By March the flinch was nearly gone. He still went quiet at the word *German*, but it was a different quiet now: not the silence of a boy hiding, but of a boy thinking. In April, walking back from the village with a basket of seed potatoes between us, he asked me, apropos of nothing, "Is Mama's English good? In letters?"

"Very good. Better than mine, and mine's excellent."

"She writes like—" he searched for it, kicking a stone along the lane with the concentration of a boy defusing something, "—like she is giving you a present even when it is only words."

I thought about that all the way home. *She writes like she is giving you a present.* Out of the mouths of babes, etc., etc., and also: out of the mouths of boys who had been handed across a continent in a coat too thin for the crossing, with nothing but a tin soldier and a note that said *forget the face*, and who had come out the far side of it able to describe love accurately.

The tin soldier still lived on his windowsill, one leg gone at the knee, standing his eternal watch over the chickens in the yard. Emil had named him in February. He called him Papa, which broke my heart weekly and which I never once let show, because there are some things you carry quietly, and I had, after all, been training for that my whole life.

* * *

The letter came on the last Tuesday of April.

I knew before I touched it. Six years of sorting correspondence teaches you things no one can teach you on purpose—the weight of bad news, the shape of official envelopes, the particular soft slouch of an envelope that has traveled a long way through a war. This one had come by way of Switzerland, via Amsterdam, via three forwarding addresses and a censor's stamp that had read it and, miracle of miracles, let it pass. And the handwriting on it—

Well. You know whose handwriting it was. I knew it the way you know your own name.

Greta Lindqvist, alive. Alive, in Hamburg, writing in that hand that leaned slightly leftward like a woman walking against wind, alive and thanking God in three languages and thanking *me* in a fourth, and I sat down on the hall chair right there with the post still in my lap because my legs had filed their resignation without consulting me.

I didn't open it until evening. I don't know why. Some part of me, I think, wanted the day to be ordinary right up until the moment it wasn't—to give the ordinary its full due, after everything.

* * *

Supper at the manor in spring 1915 was not the supper of 1913. There was rationing, and a war kitchen, and Mrs. Pargeter's ongoing personal vendetta against the potato, which she considered "a coward's vegetable." But there was bread, and there was stew, and there were three of us at the long table because Verity had long ago abolished the distinction between upstairs and downstairs eating with a wave of her hand and a sentence I cherished: *"I have eaten alone with my own consequence for forty years; I find it indigestible."*

Emil had homework spread at his elbow. Verity had the *Times*. I had the letter in my apron pocket, and it was burning a hole through the linen, and I held out approximately four minutes.

"Fairweather," Verity said, without lowering the paper, "you are radiating like a grate. Either tell us or go stand in the yard."

So I took it out. And then—and this is the part I want to get right, because it is the part I will remember when I am old, if I am old, if any of us are—so then I found that my hands were not quite steady, and I did the thing I had spent thirty-one years learning how never to do.

I asked.

"May I read it aloud?"

Verity lowered the paper. Emil looked up from his sums. And the silence that followed was not the silence of the inquiry—that courtroom hush, that held breath—it was the silence of a table waiting, of people who wanted to hear, of a room leaning in.

"It's from Germany," I said. "It's from Emil's mother."

Emil put down his pencil very carefully, the way he did everything, as though the world might bruise.

I unfolded the page. Greta's voice came off it whole, undiminished by ocean and censorship and the eleven months since a woman had stood on our step at dawn and become a face we were instructed to forget.

*"Dear Zephyrine,"* I read. My voice behaved itself, mostly. *"The censor has read this, so I must write carefully, but I trust you will read between the lines as you always have—you always read between the lines, even before I knew you were reading at all. I know now what you did. Herr Brandt's cousin in Amsterdam writes that the papers carried the story of the inquiry. A lady novelist. A cook who frightened a government clerk. And a driver who stood up in front of the whole county and said, this child is mine to keep.*

*I have read that account perhaps two hundred times. I have it folded in my dress.*

*You do not know what you saved, so let me tell you. Karl's family had arranged to send Emil to his uncle's regimental school in Prussia—a school where they make soldiers the way tanneries make leather. I could not stop it. I could not protect him. I had failed once already at protecting someone, years ago, a young Englishman in a prison cell in Königsberg whom I helped walk out of the country because his sister's letters begged me, and I promised myself then that if I ever again had the chance to pull one person out of a machine, I would not hesitate.*

*You never knew that was me. Your brother never knew. Perhaps now you do.*

*Tell Emil his mother loves him past language. Tell him I am well. Tell him the cherry tree on our street survived the winter, which I choose to believe means something.*

*And tell Miss Fairweather—no. Forgive me. Tell Zephyrine that I spent my life thinking a person must be loud to matter, and she taught me otherwise in eleven months of marginalia. Forget the face, I wrote. I have never written anything more wrong. Keep the face. Keep it always.*

*Your friend,
Greta"*

I finished. The kitchen clock ticked. Outside, late light lay along the garden like something spilled and unhurried.

Emil slid off his chair, came around the table, and stood beside me, looking at the letter upside down with the fierce literacy of a boy who could now, thanks to Stevenson, read every word of it himself.

"She says keep the face," he said.

"That she does."

"Good." He nodded once, decisively, a magistrate settling a matter. "Because I'm keeping yours too."

Mrs. Pargeter, who had entered at some point with a rice pudding and stayed for reasons she would deny to her grave, blew her nose with the report of a starting pistol and said, "Right. Pudding."

Lady Verity reached across the table, took the letter from my hands with a gentleness she reserved for documents and almost never for people, read it through, and gave it back.

"Well, Fairweather," she said. "Everything worth keeping."

I looked down at the page in my hands—Greta's leftward-leaning hand, the censor's stamp, the creases where it had been folded and refolded on its long road to this table—and I understood that she was not quoting me. She was not even talking about the letter.

She was talking about the three of us, and the pudding, and the boy, and the empty chair that was not empty because a woman in Hamburg was alive in it, and the tin soldier on the windowsill keeping his one-legged watch, and the trunks in my room that I had finally, sometime over the winter, without ceremony, unpacked all the way to the bottom.

"I know," I said. And for once, being seen saying it felt exactly like being safe.
