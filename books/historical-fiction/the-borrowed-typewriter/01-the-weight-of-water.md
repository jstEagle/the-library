## 1. The Weight of Water

There were three silences Yara Nakamura had mastered before her fifteenth birthday, and she was thirty-four now, which meant she had been practicing them for nearly twenty years — long enough that they came to her the way breathing came to other people.

The first silence was for the boats: when a diver came up too slow, or not at all, and the men on the deck began to shout numbers instead of names. You said nothing. You looked at your hands. The sea had already spoken; anything you added would only be an argument with it.

The second silence was for the office: when Mr. Okabe recalculated a family's debt after a poor season, adding interest like salt to a wound, and the diver's wife stood in front of the desk trying not to cry. You copied the new figures into the ledger in your small, even hand, and you did not look up, because looking up was the same as taking a side, and there was no side to take that did not drown somebody.

The third silence was for home: when her mother asked, in the dark, whether the company had really owed her father anything after eleven years of diving, or whether the debt had simply grown the way debts grew, like barnacles on a hull, until the thing underneath could no longer be seen. You said, *I only keep the books, Mother.* You said it gently. You said it as if it were true.

It was almost true. That was the trick of it. A silence built on a lie cracked; a silence built on something almost true could hold a whole life together, the way a hairline crack in a pearl shell holds if nobody presses on it.

* * *

On the last ordinary evening — though of course neither Yara nor anyone else in the town knew it was the last ordinary evening, history being famously bad about sending invitations — Yara sat in the back room of the Okabe Trading Company doing what she did every evening: closing the day's accounts by lamplight, and then, after the lamp was out, opening the other account, the one kept nowhere but inside her.

The real ledger lay locked in the iron cabinet behind Mr. Okabe's desk. It was a fat, ugly thing, bound in cracked black leather, its corners worn round from twenty years of being carried between the office and whatever room served as the counting room that season. In its pages were the true figures: what each diver had earned, what each diver had been advanced against those earnings, what percentage the company took for the boat, the gear, the salt, the light, the privilege of breathing their air on Okabe's dock. And beside the figures, in the margins, in Mr. Okabe's cramped hand — dates. Dates a clerk was never supposed to notice. The dates divers stopped appearing in the earnings column and started appearing in the debt column, their balances transferred, without ceremony, to their wives, their sons, their nine-year-old daughters.

Her father's page was forty-one. She didn't need the ledger to know it anymore. She hadn't needed it for years. Kenji Nakamura, entered the water on the fourth of March, 1931, and did not come up. Balance outstanding: one hundred and twelve yen, forty sen, transferred to the household of. She used to think the sentence ended there. *The household of.* As if they were a place and not people. As if the debt had simply changed addresses.

Yara sat in the dark and recited names to herself the way other people recited prayers. Not all of them — there were over two hundred divers in the ledger, and even her memory, trained by years of copying columns until the numbers swam behind her eyelids, could not hold two hundred lives entire. But she held the ones from her father's boat crew. She held the mothers who had come to the office window. She held old Tome, who dove past fifty and whose debt had somehow *increased* the year she couldn't dive at all, and Yara had copied that figure with a hand so steady it frightened her afterward, how steady it had been, how easily the silence had come, how it fit her like a glove made to measure.

She was practicing, she realized now, without quite admitting she was practicing. Reciting. Testing herself. *If someone ever asked, could I say it? Could I say any of it out loud?*

She always arrived at the same answer: *No one will ever ask.* The powerful did not ask the powerless for the truth; they told them which version of it to carry.

She blew out the lamp a second time, out of habit, and went home through streets that smelled of low tide and woodsmoke.

* * *

Her mother was awake, of course. Her mother was always awake at the hours when Yara's face was least prepared for inspection.

"You've been counting again," her mother said from the dark. "Your eyes do it. They go somewhere else and count."

"There's nothing wrong with counting."

"No," her mother agreed. "Only with what gets counted." She shifted on her mat, and the floorboard beneath her gave its familiar complaint, a sound Yara had heard ten thousand times and would have recognized blindfolded in a strange country. "Mr. Okabe sent word. Soldiers tomorrow. American soldiers, from the transport ships. They're requisitioning the town — the warehouses, the docks, the big houses. The office too."

Yara stood very still in the doorway. Outside, the tide was coming in, patient as everything else in this town. "Requisition," she repeated. It was a word from the radio, a word with clean edges, a word that belonged to other people's wars.

"They'll want an interpreter. Someone who keeps books." Her mother's voice was flat in the way that Yara knew meant fear wearing its work clothes. "They'll want *you*, Yara."

"Then I'll interpret."

"And the cabinet? The iron cabinet with the—" Her mother stopped. In their house, the ledger was never named. It was *the cabinet*, it was *the papers*, it was *that thing*, as if naming it might summon it, the way certain words summoned storms.

"It stays where it is," Yara said. "Locked. What can soldiers want with a trading ledger? They want rice, fuel, boats. They don't want arithmetic."

Her mother was quiet for a long moment, and then she said the thing she had been building toward all evening, the way the sea builds toward a wave. "Let it go. Whatever happens tomorrow — let it go. You have spent your whole life holding things that were never yours to hold. Your father's debt. Tome-san's debt. Every name in that book. You are not the keeper of the dead, my daughter. You are the keeper of yourself, and I would like, just once, before I die, to see what you look like unburdened."

Yara knelt beside her mother's mat. In the dark she could make out the shape of her hands, folded, still, waiting — the same hands that had once pulled pearl shells from thirty feet of cold water and never once complained about the cold.

"The names are theirs, Mother," Yara said. "Not mine. I only borrowed them."

"Then borrow something lighter."

But Yara was already somewhere else, already counting — two hundred and six divers, forty-one pages, one hundred and twelve yen and forty sen — and her mother sighed the sigh of a woman who has lost this particular argument every night for twenty years and expected to lose it tomorrow as well.

* * *

The soldiers came at mid-morning, which everyone agreed afterward was unsporting of them, since mid-morning was when the fish market was at its loudest and no announcement could be heard over it anyway.

They were younger than Yara had imagined. That was her first thought, standing in the doorway of the Okabe office with her interpreter's armband pinned crooked: *they're children.* Boys in oversized uniforms, their rifles held the way boys hold things they've been told are important — carefully, self-consciously, with too much attention. An officer with a clipboard walked the length of the warehouse row, pointing. Pointing was apparently the entire occupation, in miniature. He pointed, and men with crates followed.

He pointed at the Okabe office.

Yara translated the officer's instructions to Mr. Okabe, whose face performed grief while his eyes performed arithmetic — she could see him calculating which losses were recoverable and which were not, and she watched him arrive, with visible relief, at the conclusion that the ledger's loss was acceptable, since the ledger's contents were, in his view, negotiable anyway. Debts to dead men. Paper. The victors would tear up the paper, and the paper's truths would go with it, and Kenji Nakamura's balance would dissolve into the general fog of history like breath off warm water.

The officer ordered the office sealed. Wax and wire on the door. The iron cabinet — too heavy to move today — would be crated with the rest and shipped to occupation headquarters within the week, catalogued, stored, forgotten. A corporal pressed a strip of paper across the doorframe and signed it with a flourish, as though he were autographing the building.

Yara stood on the step and watched the seal go on, and felt something crack — not loudly, nothing so dramatic, but distinctly, the way ice cracks on a pond in early morning: a single line running out from under your boot before you understand that the whole surface is about to give.

Two hundred and six names. Behind wax and wire. Going into a crate, going onto a ship, going into a warehouse in some city she'd never see, filed under *miscellaneous*, which was where powerless people's histories always went.

"Miss Nakamura." The officer was speaking to her. "You'll report to the administration office tomorrow. Eight o'clock. You're assigned to us now."

"Yes," Yara said, in both languages, meaning neither.

That night she did not recite the names. For the first time in twenty years, she sat in the dark and looked at them instead — really looked, one by one, the way you look at faces on a departing boat. And at the bottom of the list, where she had never dared to look before, she found a question waiting, patient as the tide:

*If the ledger leaves, and the truth leaves with it, then what exactly have all these silences been keeping safe?*

Somewhere down the hill, in the requisitioned schoolhouse, under dust and canvas, an army had left something behind. She didn't know what yet. But she would find out tomorrow, at eight o'clock sharp, when she reported for duty — carrying, as always, everything she was supposed to forget.
