## 2. Requisition

The soldiers arrived on a Tuesday, which Yara thought was fitting. Tuesdays were the company's ledger days — the day Okabe Trading posted the divers' debts, recalculated interest, and quietly erased whatever it wished to erase. If history had to change hands, let it do so on a Tuesday.

They came in trucks that chewed the coral road to gravel, and by noon the American flag flew over the cannery where her father's body had once been weighed, wet and dripping, like so much cargo. By evening they had nailed a notice to the company office door: SEALED BY ORDER OF THE OCCUPATION ADMINISTRATION.

Yara stood before that notice for a long time. Behind the door, in the iron cabinet with the warped second drawer, sat the ledger — the true one, not the pretty one shown to inspectors. Twenty years of names in her own careful hand: how much each diver owed, what interest had bloomed on those debts like algae, and — entered in the narrow columns Mr. Okabe believed no one read — the dates the boats went out and the dates the boats came back short.

She had copied figures into that book since she was eleven years old, when her mother traded her silence to the company in exchange for the debt being "adjusted." She knew every page the way other people knew their prayers.

"Come away," her mother said at her shoulder. "It's paper."

"It's twenty-three widows," Yara said, "and forty-one children who don't know why there was never enough rice."

Her mother looked at the notice a while longer. Then: "Paper drowns easier than people. Come home."

Yara came home. But she did not sleep, and in the morning she walked back past the sealed office as though it were nothing, the way she had walked past everything for twenty years, and hated herself for the ease of it.

* * *

The interpreter's position found her rather than the reverse. A lieutenant named Calloway — young, sunburned, earnest in the way of men who had been told they were liberators and half believed it — was handed a list of bilingual townspeople and told to find one who could read a commercial ledger.

"You worked for the company?" he asked, in Japanese that made her wince gently.

"For nineteen years. Bookkeeping."

"And you're not... bitter?"

Yara considered the question with genuine interest. Bitterness, she had learned, was a luxury item, sold only to people who could afford consequences. "I'm accurate," she said. "You'll find that more useful."

He laughed, delighted, and hired her on the spot. She filed this away as evidence of how much he still had to learn about her island, and about her.

Her work was tedious and therefore perfect. She translated requisition lists, supply manifests, complaints about the water. And twice a week she watched crates go out of the company office under military seal, bound for headquarters on the main island. On the third Thursday she saw the crate she had been waiting for — long, shallow, stenciled FINANCIAL RECORDS, OKABE TRADING CO. — lifted onto a truck between a dead man's piano and forty cases of tinned peaches.

She stood very still on the dock and performed her finest silence: the one that said *I see nothing, I want nothing, I am furniture.* Inside her chest something small and furious turned over, like a diver rolling in the current.

The truck drove away. The proof of twenty-three drowned men and their arithmetic of debt would sit in some headquarters archive until it yellowed, misfiled, forgotten — or was burned in some clerk's efficient spring cleaning. Either way, gone.

That night Yara sat in her room and did something she had not done since childhood. She spoke aloud, to an empty room, the first line of the ledger.

*"Abe, Kenji. Entered service 1921. Debt at death, 340 yen, 12 sen."*

Her voice cracked in the middle like ice on a pond. But the words stayed spoken. They did not un-speak themselves.

* * *

The schoolhouse had been requisitioned as a records annex, which meant the occupation had inherited four hundred textbooks, a broken piano, and a great deal of dust. Yara was sent there to sort captured documents, alone in the afternoons because the soldiers considered document sorting beneath them and Elena — the clerk from headquarters who came Tuesdays — considered it above everyone else's competence but hers.

It was Elena who found the typewriter, actually, or pretended to. It sat in the principal's old office under a tarp: an Army Special, field-gray, its keys stiff with salt air. Someone had abandoned it during the landing, along with a half-finished letter home that began *Dear Ma, the fruit here is strange.*

"Surplus," Elena said, dragging it into the light. "Technically. Nobody inventories typewriters. Nobody has ever once cared about a typewriter." She said this with the flat confidence of a woman who had spent two years watching armies lose track of things. She was perhaps twenty-two, with ink permanently on the side of her right hand and a way of looking at Yara as if they were both in on a joke no one else had been told yet.

"Are you suggesting I steal government property?" Yara asked.

"I'm suggesting nobody will ever miss it." Elena blew dust off the ribbon spool. "There's a moral distinction. I'm very big on distinctions."

Yara should have refused. Instead she heard herself say, "I'll need ribbon. And paper."

Elena didn't ask why. That was the first kindness, and Yara felt it land somewhere she had boarded up long ago.

That night, after her mother slept, Yara carried the typewriter home wrapped in a fishing net, like contraband eel. She set it on the low table, fed in a sheet of the good rag paper Elena had liberated from a destroyed shipment, and placed her fingers on the keys.

She had typed ten thousand pages in her life. She had never typed anything that mattered.

The first key stuck. The second struck double. Her hands, steady on abacus beads and ledgers for two decades, shook like a girl's. She breathed the way her father had taught her to breathe before a dive — slow, down into the belly, accept the pressure, work within it.

Then she began.

*Abe, Kenji. Entered 1921. Debt at death: 340 yen 12 sen. Last dive: March 4, 1929. Boat returned short.*

The clatter sounded enormous in the dark house. She stopped twice, heart hammering, certain the neighbors would report her, certain the whole occupying army would descend. But the town slept, and the soldiers slept, and the machine kept its own counsel.

One page took her two hours. She managed three lines of the second before her eyes failed her. She hid the typescript inside the tatami edge, wrapped the typewriter in its net, and lay awake listening to the sea count its endless silent inventory.

Forty-one names to go. Forty-one men the official record called "lost at sea," as though the sea had simply developed an appetite.

* * *

For six nights she typed. Each afternoon at the annex she sorted documents with perfect dullness while Elena stamped things two desks away, and each evening Elena lingered over the tea she'd begun bringing — terrible army coffee, actually, which she defended with religious fervor — and said nothing about the faint ink stains now appearing on Yara's fingers.

On the seventh night, Yara's luck ran out, or changed; she would spend years deciding which.

She had just finished the page for Mori, Tatsu — *debt at death, 512 yen, boat returned short, April 1933* — when the door slid open and there stood Elena, holding her coffee tin, frozen in the doorway like a heron surprised in shallow water.

The typescript lay in plain view. The typewriter sat behind it, unmistakable, guilty, gray.

Neither of them moved. Outside, a patrol passed singing something cheerful and off-key. The sea kept counting.

"He owed five hundred and twelve yen," Yara said finally, her voice steadier than she felt, "when the company's rope failed. His widow repaid four hundred of it out of the pearl money. The rest was forgiven — meaning, erased — meaning, it never happened. There are forty-two more pages like this in a crate on a ship right now."

Elena set down her coffee tin with exaggerated care. She crossed the room. Yara braced for the word *thief*, for the report, for the end of everything.

Instead Elena picked up the top sheet, read it, and read it again. When she looked up, her eyes were bright and wet in a way that had nothing to do with fear.

"My brother signed a dive contract in thirty-four," she said. "We got his effects. We never got his numbers."

She pulled the second chair to the low table, sat, and rolled a fresh sheet into the platen with the crisp authority of a woman who had found her life's work at last.

"Dictate," she said. "You remember, I'll type. At your speed we'll finish sometime around the heat death of the universe."

And Yara — who had practiced silences for twenty years, who had built her whole life out of the things she did not say — opened her mouth and began, for the first time, to pour.
