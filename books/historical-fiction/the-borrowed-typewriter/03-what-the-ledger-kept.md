## 3. What the Ledger Kept

Three days. The number sat in Yara's chest like a swallowed stone.

She had counted the pages twice by lamplight — forty-one typed, and the ledger in her memory held sixty-three. Twenty-two names remained unaccounted for: divers whose debts she had never been asked to record, only to witness. Old Tome's second contract, renewed when the first drowned him. The Higa sisters, who had shared one air line between them like a ribbon at a festival. Her father's final entry, which she had copied into her hands a thousand times but never onto paper.

"You're doing arithmetic with your face," her mother said that morning, setting down rice and pickled radish. "Whatever it is, eat first. Arithmetic is worse hungry."

Yara ate. It was easier than explaining.

* * *

Elena found her in the schoolhouse at dusk, as she always did now — a habit neither of them had named aloud. The typewriter waited under its dust cloth like a patient animal. Elena carried two cups of the administration's terrible coffee, which she had learned to sweeten with condensed milk stolen from her own supply room.

"I did your routing stamps," Elena said, setting the cups on a desk still bearing a child's carved initials. "Fourteen documents marked *filed — annex B*. Nobody reads Annex B. I don't even read Annex B."

"Then why does it exist?"

"Because Lieutenant Marsh likes the sound of it." Elena smiled, then didn't. She looked at the stack of typescript, squared and tied with string. "How many left?"

"Twenty-two names. Maybe more if my memory is honest, less if it isn't."

"And you can't reach them."

"Some of them I was too young to see written down. I saw the men carry them out of the water. I saw what the company paid after. But the numbers themselves—" Yara pressed her palm flat against the ledger-shaped ache behind her ribs. "A silence can hold a face. It cannot always hold figures."

Elena drank her coffee. Outside, gulls argued over the fish market leavings.

"My brother," Elena said.

Yara went very still.

"Tomás. He signed with a pearling outfit off our coast when I was nine. Same kind of contract as yours, I think — same handwriting, honestly. Companies are all written by the same tired man." She turned her cup slowly on the desk. "He came back with his ears ruined and his lungs full of salt, and he died two years later, slow, in our kitchen. My mother kept his contract framed above the stove. She said it was the only paper that ever admitted he existed."

The lamp hissed softly between them.

"So when I saw you typing names," Elena said, "I thought: there's a woman writing the paper Tomás never got." She set down the cup. "Give me the ones you can't remember. Tell me everything around the hole — the faces, the funerals, who wept loudest, who didn't weep at all. I'm good at numbers. It's a boring talent, but someone has to have it."

"Elena—"

"If you say *it's dangerous*, I will remind you that I already forge military routing stamps before breakfast, which is either dangerous or deranged, possibly both." She pulled a chair close. "Now. The Higa sisters. Start with their mother's funeral, because I can hear from your face that it was memorable."

Yara laughed — a small, cracked sound, like ice shifting on a winter pond — and began to speak. And where her memory failed, Elena's pencil filled the gaps, and by midnight they had eleven names between them.

* * *

The soldiers came asking questions on the second day.

They moved through town in pairs, polite the way weather is polite before it turns. *Stolen property*, they said. *Company materials, mislaid during transfer.* They knocked at doors and smiled and wrote things in notebooks, and every householder bowed and said no, no, nothing here, nothing at all.

At the Nakamura house, a corporal with a boyish chin asked Yara's mother whether she'd seen ledgers or documents belonging to the Okabe Trading Company.

"Ledgers?" her mother said, in the voice she used for talking about weather to strangers. "We are divers' family. We keep pearls, not papers."

The corporal nodded and wrote something anyway, and after he left Yara's mother stood at the window a long time.

"They know something is missing," she said quietly. "A missing thing makes more noise than a present one."

"It isn't missing," Yara said. "It's crated. It ships tomorrow night."

"Then whatever else is missing — whatever a daughter might be hiding —" her mother turned, and her eyes were the gray-green of deep water, unreadable, "— it should learn to swim quietly."

It was not a blessing. It was not a warning. It was both, which was how love sounded in their family.

* * *

That evening, Elena arrived breathless, coffee forgotten.

"Sergeant Doyle is doing a room-by-room sweep of requisitioned buildings tomorrow. Starting with the schoolhouse. He has a list." She dropped onto the chair. "Yara, he has *our building* circled."

Yara looked at the typewriter, the growing stack beneath the floorboard, the forty-one plus eleven plus the six they'd finished just last night — fifty-eight names, five short, and no time.

"We finish tonight," she said. "All of it. Then we hide it where Doyle's list won't think to look."

"Where? Under the floor?"

"He'll lift floors. That's what lists are for." Yara ran her hand along the typewriter's case — leather, brass corners, a lock long broken so the lid sat crooked. "But nobody searches inside a machine they intend to confiscate. A confiscated typewriter goes to the depot with all its parts. Even the paper in its case."

They worked until the lamp oil thinned. Elena recited sums; Yara checked each against the shape of the debt in her mind, the way a diver checks a knot by feel alone. At some hour past midnight the last name went down — old Tome, his second contract, the figure that had bought his widow one black kimono and half a sack of rice — and Yara read the page over twice and then, because Elena insisted, once more aloud, so that the room itself had heard it.

Then they gathered the typescript — sixty-three pages, thin as a bird's bones — and folded each sheet in thirds, and packed them into the hollow beneath the carriage assembly, flat against the machine's cold iron spine. The lid closed over them with a click that sounded, in the silent schoolhouse, like a door locking.

"There," Elena whispered. "Fifty-eight kilograms of army property containing sixty-three lives."

"Doyle weighs things?"

"Doyle believes whatever the manifest says. Manifests are the true scripture of the occupation." She paused at the doorway, lamplight catching her face. "Tomorrow, when they open this building — whatever happens, you let me do the talking. I've been lying to lieutenants since I was nineteen. You've been telling the truth to no one for twenty years. We're both professionals."

* * *

Doyle came at eight with a clipboard and a private, and Elena met him on the schoolhouse steps radiating bureaucratic boredom.

"Typewriter's on your list, Sergeant. Model '38, seized property, scheduled for depot transfer. Manifest's stamped." She handed him the paper with the forged Annex B routing, and Yara, standing behind her with her heart beating somewhere near her throat, watched a man read a lie and find it well-made.

Doyle glanced at the typewriter case. Tapped it once with his knuckle, as one knocks on a melon.

"Works?"

"Ribbon's dry," Elena said. "Everything else is army-tough."

He shrugged and waved the private forward, and together they carried the case out to the truck — out into the daylight, down the steps, past children playing jacks on the road, carrying sixty-three names through the open street like pallbearers who didn't know they were pallbearers.

Yara did not breathe until the truck turned the corner toward the depot.

"It worked," she whispered.

"It worked," Elena said. Then her smile faded, and she gripped Yara's arm. "Yara. The crate. The original ledger — it ships tonight, doesn't it? From the dock?"

"Yes. The *Kiso Maru*, evening tide."

Elena's face had gone pale in a way Yara recognized from the water's edge — the color of someone looking down into depths.

"Then we have until evening tide," Elena said, "to decide whether those pages stay in a warehouse — or get read aloud where it counts. Because I heard something this morning, from the inquiry office. They're taking testimony. Public testimony. And the hearing closes Friday."

Yara looked toward the harbor, where the cranes stood waiting like patient herons, and felt twenty years of silence shift inside her like a tide beginning to turn.
