## 4. Read Aloud

The morning the crate was to leave, the harbor wore its Sunday face. Gulls wheeled over the dock in their white argument, the tide came in smelling of salt and diesel, and soldiers in rumpled khaki stacked crates with the cheerful incompetence of men who had never once been trusted with the family china. Yara stood at the edge of the pier with the typescript strapped against her ribs beneath her coat, forty-one pages, and felt them the way she had once felt the weight of a diving belt before slipping beneath the water: heavy, and necessary, and the only thing between her and drowning.

Her mother had pressed a rice ball into her hand at the door that morning and said nothing, which was its own kind of blessing. Twenty years of silences between them, and this morning her mother had looked at Yara's coat, at the square shape beneath it, and simply nodded. *Go,* the nod said. *Finish it.*

Elena found her by the customs shed, walking too fast, her clipboard held like a shield.

"They're loading the crate at eight," she whispered. "I checked the manifest twice. It's marked *commercial records — low priority*, which means it goes to the bottom of a warehouse in headquarters and no human being opens it before 1955, if ever." She glanced over her shoulder. "The inquiry office opens at nine. Lieutenant Okafor. He's the one who actually listens — the others just want the shipping figures and their lunch."

"And the routing stamp?"

Elena's ears went pink. "I've been practicing on scrap paper all week. I'm either a forger of genius or the stamp is just very forgiving." She pressed a manila envelope into Yara's hands, already stamped and addressed in her careful clerk's hand: *Office of the Commission of Inquiry — Attn: Lt. Okafor — Re: Labor Records, Okabe Trading Co.* "Once it has that stamp on it, it's official correspondence. Nobody questions official correspondence. That's the entire tragedy of bureaucracy, and today it's going to work for us."

Yara looked at the envelope. It was such a small thing. A rectangle of paper. Inside it, forty-one pages she had typed one finger at a time in a cold schoolroom while the town slept, her father's debts and her neighbors' deaths marching out of her memory and onto the page, line by line, name by name. She had typed *Nakamura, Kenji — drowned 14 October 1931 — debt outstanding at death: 340 yen, transferred to widow* and had to stop for an hour because her hands would not behave.

"Elena," she said.

"Yes."

"Thank you for not reporting me."

"You're welcome. Thank you for not being a spy." A pause. "Also, your typing is terrible. Two fingers, Yara. Truly heroic. Like watching a woodpecker fight a piano."

"I learned on a machine missing the letter E."

"That explains so much about your correspondence."

They laughed, quietly, the way people laugh at funerals and dockyards — small, sideways, holding something.

* * *

At five past nine, Yara walked into the inquiry office with the envelope under her arm and her heart going like a diver's pulse in deep water.

The room smelled of paper and cold coffee. Lieutenant Okafor sat behind a desk too large for him, a young man with a tired, kind face and ink on his fingers, and beside him sat the historians — two of them, round and neat, with fountain pens and the particular serenity of men who believed history was something that happened to other people's paperwork.

Yara had rehearsed this. She had rehearsed it in the bath, on the walk, in the schoolhouse at midnight. *I have documents. I request to testify. I am the daughter of a diver and I kept the company's books for twenty years.*

What came out was: "I have documents."

Okafor looked up. "You have documents."

"Concerning the Okabe Trading Company. The diving contracts. I request to testify." Her voice held. She was almost proud of it. "I am Yara Nakamura. My father was Kenji Nakamura. He died in their water in 1931, and his debt was transferred to my mother, who paid it for eleven more years with her hands."

The historians' pens had stopped moving. One of them raised an eyebrow at the other, the way men do when a woman says *died in their water* as though it were a legal phrase.

Okafor took the envelope. He read the routing stamp, and Yara watched him read it, and watched him decide — she would learn later that Elena's forgery was, in fact, flawless — that this was ordinary mail. He slit it open and began, out of politeness, to skim.

He stopped skimming on the second page.

The room changed. Yara had spent her life reading silences — her mother's, the company clerks', her own — and this silence was different. This was the silence of a man realizing the paper in his hands was heavier than it looked.

"Mrs. Nakamura," he said. "Where did these come from?"

"From my memory, Lieutenant. The original ledger is in that crate on your dock, marked low priority. These are the divers' names, their debts, their dive dates, and their deaths. Every one I could recall. Forty-one pages. There is a second set of pages —" she did not look at the historians — "compiled with the assistance of a colleague, covering the contracts I never saw. Together they are complete."

One of the historians cleared his throat. "Madam, the Commission's mandate concerns shipping tonnage and port logistics. Individual labor accounts of this nature are, frankly, outside —"

"Then open the ledger," Yara said.

The words came out of her with twenty years behind them, and she felt the room flinch.

"You have the original in a crate marked *commercial records*. If my memory is wrong — if I have invented these debts, these deaths, these dates — then the ledger will prove it, and you may dismiss me as a hysterical woman with a typewriter. But if the ledger agrees with me, gentlemen, then your historians have been about to ship the truest record in this town to a warehouse where no one will ever read it. And you will have chosen the warehouse." She folded her hands, the way her mother folded hers at the shrine. "I am asking you to choose the record."

The silence went on long enough for a gull to cross the window twice.

Okafor set down the pages, very carefully, as if they might bruise. "Sergeant," he called to the doorway, "have the crate marked *commercial records, Okabe* brought up from the dock. Unopened. And get the stenographer."

* * *

They opened the crate in the inquiry office at half past ten, with the stenographer's keys already clacking, and the historians pried the lid with the reluctance of men opening a trunk they suspected was full of their own mistakes.

Yara watched them read. She watched the younger one go pale at the columns of transferred debts, watched the older one begin, very quietly, to check her typed figures against the ledger's — page after page, name after name — and she watched his fountain pen slow and stop, because her memory had not been wrong. Not once in forty-one pages.

"Read them," she said. "Into the record. All of them."

"Madam," the older historian began, "the transcript is a summary document, we cannot possibly —"

"The names," Okafor said, "go in. Every one." And there was something in his voice that suggested a man who had grown up listening to his grandmother tell stories no official had ever bothered to write down.

So they read them.

The stenographer's keys struck like rain on a roof. *Nakamura, Kenji. Yamane, Toshi. Okada, the brothers Hideo and Masaru. Fujimoto, Setsu.* Name after name, debt after debt, date after date, and outside the open window the town heard it — because the window was open, and the office was on the square, and the square filled the way it fills for a festival, quietly at first and then all at once. Fishermen stopped mending nets. The fishwives came up from the stalls with their hands still wet. Old Mrs. Yamane stood in the crowd with her shawl pulled tight, and when the stenographer read her husband's name she did not weep; she stood very straight, like a woman being paid a debt at last.

Yara's mother was there. Yara saw her at the edge of the crowd, hands folded, and her mother looked at her across the heads of the town — across the whole twenty years of silence — and nodded once, the way she had nodded that morning. *Finish it,* the nod said. *You finished it.*

When it was done — when the last name, little Emi Okada, seventeen years old, had been spoken aloud and struck into the official transcript of the Commission of Inquiry — Lieutenant Okafor looked up at Yara over the pages.

"Mrs. Nakamura. For the record: do you swear these accounts are true?"

Yara thought of her father coming up out of the green water with the light on his shoulders. She thought of the schoolroom, the woodpecker typing, Elena's forged stamp, her mother's rice ball. She thought of every silence she had ever kept, and how they had all been leading here, like rivers to the sea.

"I swear it," she said, "on the water."

The stenographer typed it. It went into the record, and the record, this time, was going nowhere but into the world.
