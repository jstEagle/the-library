## 1. The Weight of Lead

The type had opinions that morning.

Lior Crane—or Lio, as the town knew her, as her father knew her, as God Himself had perhaps agreed to know her for the sake of convenience—stood at the sorting frame with a stick of fourteen-point Caslon and tried to convince an e to stop masquerading as a c. It wasn't the type's fault. Seven years in the case had worn the serifs soft as an old man's teeth, and a worn e will lie to you if you let it. Her father said you could tell a printer's whole history from his type: how it wore, where it was greasy, which letters he'd reached for so often they'd gone bald.

By that measure, their shop told the story of a family that loved the word *the* above all others.

She set the errant e aside in the repair box—a wooden tray Aldous called the Hospital, where damaged letters went to be mended or mourned—and pulled a fresh one from the upper case. Outside, past the fogged windows of Crane & Son, Printers, the drums were at it again in Vellmere Square. Not the festival kind. The festival kind stumbled and drank between beats. These were imperial drums, which never stumbled, never drank, and never once forgot what they were for.

*Da-dum. Da-dum. Da-dum-da-da-dum.*

"You'll ruin your eyes squinting like that," Aldous called from the back room, where he was doing something to the press that involved swearing in three languages, two of them dead.

"I'm not squinting. I'm listening."

"To drums? Waste of good ears." He emerged wiping his hands on a rag that had been gray since before she was born, though everyone politely called it white. He was a long man made short by stooping over cases for thirty years, with ink in the creases of his knuckles that no lye would ever shift. He glanced at the window without really seeing it, the way you glance at weather you've already decided not to discuss. "They've been drumming since dawn."

"Since before dawn," Lior said. "I heard them when I banked the stove."

"Ah." He considered this. "Well. Bread still needs baking. Type still needs sorting. The world can drum all it likes; Mrs. Halloway's funeral bills won't print themselves."

This was Aldous Crane's entire philosophy of empire, war, and apocalypse, delivered in one sentence, and Lior loved him for it even as some quiet animal part of her wanted to shake him and say, *Father, the drums are getting closer.*

She went back to her sorting. That was the first silence she kept—the one about the drums. There was a whole shelf of such silences by now, seven years deep, and she knew them the way she knew her typecases: by touch, in the dark, without looking.

There was the silence about her hands, which were smaller than an apprentice's ought to be, and softer, and which she kept roughened deliberately with pumice and lampblack so they'd look like a boy's hands looked after honest work. There was the silence about her voice, pitched down into a register that made her throat ache by evening, every day, seven years of evenings. There was the silence about the binding she wore, about the way she'd taught herself to walk with her weight forward, to laugh low instead of high, to spit—God help her, she'd learned to *spit*, and rather well now—because Tam Crane had spat like a camel with grievances and the town remembered.

And underneath all those, floor of the cellar, foundation under everything: the silence about Tam himself.

Her brother had died eleven days after his fourteenth birthday, of the lung fever, in the bed directly above the shop, while their father sat up with him and their mother sat in the kitchen not sitting up with anyone. Lior had been twelve. She had listened through the floorboards to the sound of a boy breathing less and less, and she had thought—with the terrible clarity of children—that someone in that house was going to have to become Tam Crane, because the apprenticeship papers bore his name, and the papers had cost their grandfather two hundred crowns and a favor from a bishop, and Aldous had built the future on the assumption of a son.

Their mother had solved the problem differently. She'd left in the spring, with the good shawl and the silver spoons and a man who drove the mail coach to Correnne, and she'd left a note that said, in full: *I am not brave enough for this house.* Which was, Lior had come to appreciate years later, the most honest sentence anyone in her family had ever written.

Aldous hadn't cried. He'd stood in the doorway of the shop the morning after the burial and looked at his remaining child—thin, furious, grief-struck Lior, wearing Tam's spare shirt because her own clothes had been packed or given away—and something had passed over his face like weather. Then he'd said, "The galleys won't set themselves," and handed her the composing stick, and that had been the second-most-honest sentence in the family, because what he meant was: *Yes. Do it. Become him. I cannot bear to lose the shop too.*

So she had. And here was the strange thing, the thing she kept even deeper than the rest, in a locked drawer of herself: she was *good* at it. Better than Tam had ever been, if she was honest. Tam had hated the smell of ink and fidgeted at the stone and dreamed of boats. Lior loved the work the way other people loved music. She could read mirrored type fluently, upside down and backward, faster than most folk read plain. She could tell a font's foundry by the curve of its question mark. At fifteen she'd cut her first woodblock ornament, a small leaping fish for a chandler's invoice, and Aldous had held it up to the light and said nothing at all for a long moment, and then said, "Your grandfather couldn't have done that," which in Aldous Crane's language was a parade with brass bands.

Being good at a borrowed life is its own particular trouble. A bad disguise is a prison you're always trying to escape. A good one is a prison you keep improving.

"Lio."

She surfaced. Aldous was looking at her with the expression he wore when he suspected she'd gone somewhere inside her head and he didn't want to ask where, because asking might lead to answers. "Mrs. Quill's broadside. Did you finish the lockup?"

"Last night. It's on the stone waiting for you." She hesitated, then added, because honesty is a habit that dies hard: "You've got the 'wedding' spelled with one d again."

"I did not."

"It says *weding*. On forty copies. Mrs. Quill will hang us with the bunting."

Aldous strode to the stone, bent, peered, and straightened with the dignity of a man who has decided the error is typographically interesting. "A single letter," he declared, "is beneath correction. It adds humility to the document."

"A wedding with one d adds humility to the bride, maybe. She'll think we mean it's half a wedding. A trial run."

He snorted—which was as close as he came to admitting defeat—and began pulling the forme apart, and Lior turned back to her case and let herself smile where he couldn't see it. This was the currency of their days: corrections and counter-corrections, jokes with type in them, the easy shorthand of two people who had spent seven years agreeing not to discuss the largest fact of their shared life. It worked. It almost worked. Some mornings, sorting type with the stove ticking and the smell of oil and paper rising around her, she could nearly forget that the whole arrangement rested on a silence the size of a person.

Nearly.

* * *

At midday the bells rang wrong.

Vellmere had five church bells and each had its own voice and its own schedule, and Lior knew them the way she knew everything about the town: sideways, quietly, by heart. Saint Ferren's rang the hours. Saint Odile's rang funerals, nine strokes for a man, six for a woman, three for a child, and the town pretended not to count but everybody counted. The harbor chapel rang only for storms and drownings. So when a bell began striking at noon and did not stop at twelve, did not stop at thirteen, but kept on, flat and urgent, and then a second bell took it up out of rhythm with the first—

Lior put down her composing stick.

Aldous was already at the door. He didn't open it; he stood with his hand flat against the wood, head tilted, listening the way a man listens to a floor when he thinks there's water where water shouldn't be. The drums in the square had stopped. That was worse than the bells. Drums stopping meant the drummers were waiting for something.

"People coming back from market are walking fast," Aldous reported, watching through the gap in the shutters. "Not running. Walking fast. That's townsfolk for *we don't know yet whether to panic*."

"What do you think it is?"

"Requisition, most likely. Or a levy announcement. Or the Governor's come to dedicate a fountain." He said it lightly, but his hand stayed flat on the door a moment longer than lightness required. "Whatever it is, it'll want printing eventually. Everything does. Proclamations, notices, schedules of execution—" He caught himself. Cleared his throat. "Bills of fair, I mean. Fairs. Very popular, fairs."

Lior looked at her father's back and filed that stumble away in the shelf of silences, next to the others. It rattled around in there and refused to sit still.

They ate bread and hard cheese standing up, as always, and it was while she was cutting the cheese—badly; she'd inherited Tam's knife skills along with his name—that the knock came at the street door. Three knocks, spaced like punctuation. Official.

Aldous wiped his hands, squared his apron, and opened the door with the face he used for customers and magistrates: pleasant, blank, faintly apologetic, a face that said *whatever you want, it's terribly inconvenient, but I suppose we'll manage.*

Two soldiers stood outside. Behind them, at the corner, more soldiers, and beyond them the square, where a cordon of blue-gray coats had grown up around the courthouse like frost. An officer stepped forward from the shadows of the doorway—a young officer, polished boots, gloves so white they seemed lit from within, and a voice that had been trained in rooms with high ceilings.

"Aldous Crane. Proprietor?"

"The same."

"Captain Verel, Imperial Fourth. By order of the Provisional Authority, Vellmere is placed under requisition effective immediately. Billeting will be assigned. Curfew begins at dusk. All civic records"—the officer produced a folded paper, but didn't offer it, merely held it the way a priest holds a text he knows by heart—"are to be surrendered to the records office by nightfall. Ledgers, registers, rolls of debt and tax, parish copies. Failure to comply is treated as concealment."

His eyes moved past Aldous into the shop, unhurried, taking inventory: the press, the cases, the drying lines hung with posters of a lost dog (reward: one crown; the dog had been lost for two years now and was, Lior suspected, thoroughly found elsewhere). The gaze crossed hers for half a heartbeat. It registered nothing—or appeared to register nothing—and moved on. She'd spent seven years learning to be furniture. It held.

"Ledgers," Aldous repeated mildly. "We're printers, Captain. We print ledgers. We don't keep them."

"The master ledger of Vellmere is kept in your vault. The town clerk has used your strongroom for storage these nineteen years. You'll forgive me for knowing my business, sir."

Something happened in Aldous's face. It was very small—it was nothing, really, a flicker around the eyes, a half-beat too long before the shrug—but Lior saw it, because she was her father's daughter and had been trained her whole life to read the space between words. And what she saw frightened her more than the bayonets on the corner, because it wasn't fear.

It was relief.

"Of course," Aldous said. "Come in. Mind the wet ink on the third table. The ledger's in the vault; I'll fetch the key."

He went back into the shadowed interior, moving quickly, too quickly for a stooped old printer asked to surrender his town's memory to an occupying army. Lior stood by the case, hands dirty, heart doing something complicated, and smiled the apprentice smile at Captain Verel, who was reading the spines of their sample books with what looked—absurdly, horribly—like genuine interest.

"You set all of these?" he asked suddenly, tapping a spine.

"My master does," Lior said. "I clean up after him."

Verel looked at her a moment. His eyes were pale and patient and missed nothing he chose to notice, and she felt, absurdly, like a piece of type being weighed in a compositor's hand—checked for wear, for cracks, for truth.

"Someone in this shop sets beautiful work," he said, and turned to follow Aldous into the dark.

* * *

From the mouth of the stairway, Lior watched them bring the ledger out.

It came up wrapped in oilcloth, carried between the two soldiers like something wounded or sacred—it was hard to say which, and she suspected it had been both, alternately, for ninety years. Her great-grandfather had begun it, they said, back when Vellmere's books were kept in chalk and memory. Every birth registered late. Every debt forgiven and unforgiven. Every bribe paid to the harbor master, every dowry, every feud, every child entered under the wrong father's name for reasons of mercy or malice. Ninety years of the town's real opinion of itself, in one book.

Aldous walked beside it to the door. He didn't watch it go the way a man watches a treasure leave. He watched it go the way a man watches a heavy thing finally get carried downstairs for him.

"Sign here," Verel said, holding out the folded paper and a pencil.

Aldous signed. Verel checked the signature against something, nodded, and paused at the threshold. For a moment he seemed about to speak—to Aldous, to the room, to the shop itself. Then he settled for: "Curfew at dusk. Keep your shutters closed and your lamps shaded."

"And if we need to work?" Aldous asked pleasantly. "Ink doesn't keep office hours."

"Then work in the dark, Mr. Crane. It's a trade suited to it."

The door closed. Boots receded. The bells had stopped at last, and in the ringing quiet afterward Lior became aware of her own pulse and of her father, standing with his back to her, still holding the pencil they'd made him sign with.

"Well," she said. "That's that. The whole town's secrets, off to the courthouse in an oilcloth bag."

"Mm," said Aldous.

"That was quick. I'd have thought you'd argue. You argued with the assessor over a half-crown paper duty for three weeks. You wrote him *poems*, Father. Insulting ones."

"Poetry achieves nothing with assessors," Aldous said. He set the pencil down on the stone, precisely, parallel to the chase. "Whereas handing over a ledger costs nothing at all."

"Costs nothing—"

"There's nothing in that book we need," he said, and turned, and gave her the pleasant blank face, the customer face, the magistrate face. "Nothing that matters. Come. Mrs. Quill's broadside won't correct its own spelling, and neither, apparently, will I."

He went to the stone and began setting type, humming—actually humming, a little tuneless hymn from his chapel-going childhood—and Lior stood in the middle of the shop that smelled of her whole life, and understood two things with perfect clarity.

The first was that her father was lying to her, smoothly, in the family tradition, and had been for some time.

The second was that whatever he was hiding, it lived somewhere in this shop—in the vault that had just been emptied, or the cases, or the formes, or the locked drawer of the back room she'd never once seen opened—and that tonight, after dusk, after curfew, with the imperial drums silent and the lamps shaded as ordered, she was going to find it.

She picked up her composing stick. She sorted type. Outside, the soldiers' boots passed and repassed in the street like a tide deciding where to come ashore, and the worn old e lay in the Hospital tray, pretending to be a c, waiting for someone to set it right.
