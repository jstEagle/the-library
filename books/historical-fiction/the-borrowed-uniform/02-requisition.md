## 2. Requisition

The drums started at dawn, which was rude of them. Even the river gave a town an hour's grace before it began its noise, but imperial drums had no such manners.

Lior heard them from her cot in the loft above the press room and lay still for a moment, counting. Not the quick rattle of a patrol changing watch—that she could sleep through, and often did—but the slow, deliberate beat of a full company on the march, each stroke landing like a fist deciding where to fall.

*Boots in the square by midmorning,* she thought. *Proclamations by noon.* She had learned the grammar of soldiers the way other apprentices learned their letters.

She dressed as she always did: binder first, pulled tight enough to flatten what needed flattening, then the shirt with the frayed collar that Tam had worn soft before he ever wore it at all, then the waistcoat with the ink stains at the cuff that no amount of scrubbing would lift and which, frankly, she had stopped trying to lift, because an apprentice with clean cuffs invited questions, and questions were the one commodity Vellmere could not afford this season.

In the glass—such as it was; the shop's mirror had been cracked since before she was born and showed a person in three slivers, which suited her fine—she checked the result. A boy looked back at her. Narrow, sharp-elbowed, fourteen if you were generous, sixteen if you were honest about the height. *Lio Crane*, apprentice compositor, son of Aldous Crane, master printer, a young man of no consequence whatsoever.

Seven years of practice, and the lie fit like Tam's shirt: worn soft, slightly too small, and hers now whether she liked it or not.

Downstairs, her father was already at the stone, sorting type by candlelight, which meant he'd been up all night or hadn't slept at all, and with Aldous Crane the two conditions were indistinguishable.

"You hear them?" Lior said.

"I hear them." He didn't look up. His fingers walked the case with the blind ease of forty years, lifting sorts two at a time. "Company strength. Two drums, so at least sixty men."

"That's a lot of boots for a town that mostly smells of fish."

"A lot of boots is exactly what they send when they intend to stay." He set the sorts down and finally regarded her over his spectacles, which sat low on his nose in the way of a man who used them less for seeing than for looking over. "Lio. Whatever happens today—and something will happen today—I want your hands busy and your mouth shut. Can you manage both?"

"I've managed both for seven years, Da."

Something crossed his face then, quick as a misprint, there and corrected before she could read it. "So you have," he said, and went back to his type, and the silence between them settled into its usual shape, comfortable as an old chair with one broken rung nobody mentioned.

* * *

The boots arrived at half past nine, precisely as forecast, and they were polished to a shine that seemed almost personal, given the mud of the Vellmere streets. Captain Verel rode at their head on a grey horse too fine for the cobbles, and behind him came sixty men in imperial blue and a wagon with a locked strongbox, and behind *that* came every soul in Vellmere who had nothing better to do, which was everyone, because a requisition was the finest free entertainment the river offered outside of floods.

Lior watched from the doorway of the shop, sweeping a step that did not need it. Sweeping was excellent cover. Nobody interrogated a boy with a broom.

Aldous stood beside her, arms folded, wearing the expression he reserved for customers who wanted gold leaf on a broadside and expected to pay in onions.

An officer dismounted in front of the town hall—the same hall where the mayor kept the tax rolls, the parish kept its registers, and, two doors further along Crane's Lane, in a vault under the print shop floor, the master ledger of Vellmere kept everything else.

"By order of the Provisional Governor," the herald read, in the flat voice of a man reading his ninth such proclamation that month, "the town of Vellmere is placed under military administration. All able-bodied persons shall present themselves for labor registration. Curfew shall commence at the evening bell and end at dawn. All civic records—" here Lior felt her father go very still beside her, a stillness she felt more than saw, the way you feel a press begin to take weight— "shall be surrendered to the office of the captain commanding, for inventory and safekeeping. Noncompliance will be treated as sedition."

"Sedition," muttered Old Pem the fishmonger, two steps away. "Half the town can't write. What are we going to be seditious *with*? Herring?"

"Hush," said his wife, who hushed him professionally.

Lior kept sweeping. But her mind had gone ahead of the proclamation, down through the floorboards, past the coal cellar, to the iron-banded door of the vault, and to the ledger that slept inside it—a book as long as her forearm, bound in oxblood leather gone soft as a dog's ear, holding every debt, every payment, every birth registered late and death registered never, every favor called in and grudge banked, going back four generations to some ancestor named Crane who had apparently decided that if a thing happened in Vellmere, it ought to be written down in his hand.

Her father's father's book. Her father's book. The spine of the whole town, and the soldiers wanted it.

"They'll come here," Aldous said quietly, without moving his lips much, in the manner of a man commenting on weather. "Records means us. We're records, Lio. Half the town's history went through that press."

"Will you give it to them?"

He didn't answer immediately, and in that non-answer lived a whole conversation they were not going to have. "Go put the kettle on," he said instead. "If we must be requisitioned, we'll be civil about it. Civility confuses them."

It was true, and it was also the closest thing to strategy Aldous Crane possessed, and Lior had inherited it entire.

* * *

They came at eleven: Captain Verel himself, two soldiers, and a clerk with ink-stained fingers whom Lior eyed with immediate professional sympathy and immediate professional suspicion, because a fellow ink-stained clerk was either an ally or a rival and there was no third category.

The bell over the shop door had been silent since the occupation began—it had been removed, actually, melted for shot in the last war, and the hole where it hung let in drafts—but Verel entered as though a bell had rung anyway. He was younger than Lior expected. Thirty, perhaps. A face built for official portraits: straight nose, level eyes, a mouth that looked as though smiling had been trained out of it at some expense. His boots gleamed. His gloves did not match the rest of his kit, being older, softer, mended at one knuckle—a detail Lior filed away without knowing yet why she filed things about this man, only that some instinct told her she would want them later.

"Master Crane." He pronounced it correctly, which put him ahead of most visitors. "Captain Verel. Imperial Third Riverine. I'm told you keep the civic ledger."

"I keep a ledger," Aldous said. "Whether it's the civic one depends on who's asking."

"Don't fence with me, printer. It wastes both our mornings." Verel's gaze moved over the shop with an appraiser's eye—the cases, the stone, the press itself, old Maren's iron heart that had beaten out a million pages. "You have a fine establishment."

"For a town that smells of fish."

"For any town." Something flickered at the corner of that trained mouth—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, court-martialed before it could form. "The ledger. And any papers of record. Birth lists, debt rolls, parish copies, anything bearing names. You'll surrender them for inventory."

"And if I say some of those papers belong to private parties?"

"Then private parties may petition me for their return after the war." Verel said it mildly, the way a man says *the tide comes in*, and Lior understood suddenly why the herald's voice had been flat: it wasn't the words that carried the threat, it was the arithmetic underneath them.

Aldous nodded slowly. Then, to Lior's astonishment—he saw it coming and could do nothing, standing there with his broom like a prop in someone else's play—her father turned, unlocked the drawer beneath the counter, lifted the false bottom, and set the master ledger on the counter between them. Oxblood leather. Four generations of Vellmere's secrets, sleeping soundly.

No argument. No delay. Not even the token grumbling that would have been expected, that any man in Vellmere would have performed as naturally as breathing.

"There," Aldous said. "Inventory it."

Verel looked at the ledger. Then he looked at Aldous. Then—Lior felt it happen—he looked at *her*, briefly, a glance weighing perhaps two seconds, taking in the boy with the broom and the ink at the cuff, and finding him unremarkable, which was the finest compliment the world ever paid her.

"Your father surrenders easily," Verel said to her. It was the first time he'd addressed her directly, and his voice was different when aimed at her—lighter, almost conversational, a man testing ice with one foot.

"He taught me not to argue with the tide," Lior said. "Sir."

That ghost of a smile again, surfacing and drowning. "Sensible town, Vellmere." He drew on one glove, finger by finger. "There'll be billeting notices posted within the hour. Six men to this street. They'll expect bread, water, and to be left alone. In return, they'll leave your press alone. I trust we understand each other, Master Crane."

"Perfectly," said Aldous.

But Lior, watching, understood that they did not understand each other at all, because her father had handed over the book the way a card sharp folds a losing hand—smoothly, gratefully, glad to be rid of it—and Captain Verel, whatever else he was, did not strike her as a man who missed a fold. He had looked at the ledger the way she herself looked at a page set in unfamiliar type: reading it, even closed. Reading the absence of hesitation where hesitation should live.

The soldiers took the ledger to the wagon. The clerk logged it. Verel paused at the door, one hand on the frame, and glanced back at the press.

"What face do you use?" he asked abruptly.

Lior blinked. "Sir?"

"The typeface. For your broadsides and bills." He nodded at a sample pinned to the wall, a handbill for a boat race three summers dead that nobody had taken down. "That's not a foundry cut I know. Local work?"

"My grandfather cut it," Lior said, before she could stop herself, because pride in the craft was the one reflex faster than caution. "Every punch by hand. We call it the Vell."

"*Vellmere*'s own." Verel considered this with what appeared to be genuine interest, which alarmed Lior more than any threat had. "My mother was a scrivener. I grew up under a writing desk the way you seem to have grown up under a press." He said it lightly, and left before anyone could ask what a soldier's childhood had to do with anything, which was clearly the point.

The door closed. The boots receded up Crane's Lane, polished and unhurried.

* * *

"Well," said Lior, when the last blue coat had turned the corner. "That went well."

"It went *fast*," Aldous said.

He was still standing where he'd stood, and there was something wrong with the picture that took Lior a moment to place, and when she placed it her stomach dropped through the floorboards toward the empty vault. Her father's hands were shaking. Aldous Crane's hands, steady through typesetting marathons and press breakdowns and the day they'd buried Tam—those hands trembled now, faintly, as he wiped them on his apron as though the ledger had left residue.

"Da. You gave it up like it was nothing."

"It *was* nothing. An old book full of other people's quarrels."

"Four generations of—"

"Ledgers are paper, Lio." He said it too quickly, too smoothly, and then caught himself doing it and stopped, and for a moment father and child regarded each other across the counter with perfect mutual understanding that neither would act upon. "Paper burns. Paper gets rewritten. The important things aren't written anywhere." He turned to the case and picked up his stick as if the morning's interrupted composition still mattered, as if the world were a job that simply ran late. "Kettle's cold. Put it on."

Lior put the kettle on. It was what she was for, apparently: hands busy, mouth shut.

But upstairs, alone in the loft while the water heated and the drums faded to memory, she sat on her cot and turned the morning over like a proof sheet held to the light, hunting flaws.

Her father had given up the ledger without a fight. Her father, who argued with fishmongers about credit terms. Who had once spent three days composing a scathing letter to the Guild over a misdelivered font, and mailed it, and framed the reply. That man had folded at the first polished boot.

Unless—

Unless the ledger on the counter had been exactly what he'd said it was. Paper. A book full of other people's quarrels.

And unless the real one—the one with the debts that didn't add up and the entries in margins she'd glimpsed and never asked about—was somewhere else entirely, somewhere her father's shaking hands and smooth answers made sudden terrible sense.

The kettle began to sing downstairs. Through the window, across the rooftops, Lior could see the records wagon lurching away toward the town hall, carrying somebody's secrets into the keeping of the empire.

The question that had kept seven years of silences comfortable was suddenly, uncomfortably loud:

*Which book did we just lose?*

And its twin, quieter, worse: *What has my father been printing?*

Below her, the press room door opened and closed. She heard her father cross to the vault, and she heard—distinctly, unmistakably—the scrape of the iron-banded door opening onto a darkness that should have held a book and did not.

Lior did not go down. Not yet. But she marked the hour, the way her father marked every entry in every book, in the hand she was learning to read whether he knew it or not.
