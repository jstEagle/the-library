## 3. What the Ledger Keeps

The house above the shop went quiet in a particular way at night. Not the silence of the empty street—Vellmere's streets never truly emptied, what with the gulls arguing over the fish market refuse and the harbor bells counting out the watch—but the silence of a building settling into its bones. Lior knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat: the tick of the cooling press, the sigh of damp paper drying on the lines, and beneath it all, her father's tread overhead as he made his last circuit of the rooms.

Except tonight there was no tread. Tonight Aldous Crane had gone to bed early, complaining of a chill in his chest that he refused to call anything so dramatic as an illness. "I'm fifty-four years old, not a wet apprentice," he'd said, coughing into his sleeve. "Go to sleep."

Which was precisely why she was going down the stairs instead.

The ledger sat where Captain Verel's men had returned it—or rather, where they had never taken it. That was the thing that had been sitting in Lior's chest for three days like a swallowed stone. When Verel requisitioned the town, he had demanded every civic record in Vellmere: parish rolls, tax books, guild registries, the lot. And Aldous had handed over the master ledger—the book that held every debt, every name, every transaction worth hiding in a river town—without a word of protest. Too readily. Too smoothly, like a card player folding a hand he'd already lost on purpose.

Then Verel's clerk had flipped through it, frowned at something, and left it behind with the words *"We'll send for it."* They never had. Three days of imperial occupation, and the most dangerous book in Vellmere sat in its drawer under the counter, guarded by nothing but a brass lock older than the empire itself.

Lior stood at the bottom of the stairs in her stocking feet and listened to the house breathe. Her father coughed once, twice, and then nothing.

*You're only going to look,* she told herself. *You're going to look because if you don't know what's in it when they come back for it, you can't protect him.*

It was a good lie. She almost believed it herself, which she supposed was a skill of sorts.

The lock gave to the thin blade of her type gauge—her father would have been horrified to learn what else that tool could do, or perhaps not; perhaps he'd have simply raised one eyebrow and asked why she hadn't thought of it sooner. The ledger came out heavy as a paving stone, bound in oxblood leather gone soft at the corners, its spine stamped in faded gilt: *CRANE & CRANE, PRINTERS AND STATIONERS, EST. BY GODFREY CRANE.*

Three generations of Cranes in one book. Her grandfather Godfrey had started it; her father had kept it; and Tam—

She stopped that thought where it always stopped, at the edge of the drop.

Lior carried the ledger to the workbench nearest the stove, banked coals still breathing their faint orange warmth into the dark, and lit the lamp she'd hidden earlier behind the stack of blank folios. She turned to the first page.

* * *

Reading her grandfather's handwriting was like being scolded by a ghost. His script was severe, upright, economical—a hand that clearly resented having to write anything down twice. *Bought of Merren & Sons, 40 lb. rag paper, 3s 6d.* *Paid Wexley for chimney sweeping, grudgingly.* (That last word was actually written in the margin. Lior felt an unexpected flicker of affection for a man dead twenty years.)

But the ledger was more than accounts. It was Vellmere's whole secret heart, set down in ink by three generations of men who understood that a print shop hears everything.

There were debts, of course—half the town owed the other half, and the Cranes' ledger kept the score. Old Dannel Ovey owed for funeral broadsheets he'd never paid for, three years running, and each year the entry appeared again with the note *waived — his grief is payment enough.* There were bribes, though no one called them that. *To Harbor Warden Pell, "gift," 2s* appeared every quarter for eleven years, and Lior suddenly understood why the warden had never once inspected the shop's imports of Dutch type metal despite the empire's tariffs. There were entries that made her snort despite everything—her father's dry marginalia was a genre unto itself. Beside a payment of sixpence to the widow Hask for mending the shop curtains: *she charged me for thread I supplied. A saint, apparently.*

And then there were the hidden births.

Lior found the first one by accident, in a year before she could remember. An entry for christening invitations, paid by a name she didn't recognize, followed in her grandfather's cramped hand: *child registered as nephew. It is not a nephew.* Nothing more. No judgment, no elaboration. Just the fact, preserved, the way a printer preserves everything—because paper remembers even when people are forbidden to.

She began hunting them deliberately after that. A girl born to the ferryman's wife and entered in the parish roll as a cousin from upriver. Twins born to a couple married four months prior, dates quietly adjusted in the ledger though never in the church book—Godfrey Crane had apparently held strong opinions about which records deserved honesty. Page after page, decade after decade: the births, the adoptions, the marriages that happened somewhere other than where the paperwork said. Whole invisible genealogies of Vellmere, threaded through the columns between invoices for lamp oil and binding twine.

*Everyone has something in here,* Lior realized, her finger resting on a note about the harbormaster's actual parentage. *Half the town is someone the parish says they aren't.*

The thought should have comforted her. Instead it made her skin prickle, because she was someone the parish said she wasn't too, and if the ledger knew everyone's secrets, whose eyes had read hers?

She checked. Of course she checked. Her own name—her real one—did not appear anywhere in the family sections. There was an entry from seven years ago, in her father's hand: *Apprentice Lio Crane, son, indentured. Fee waived.* The ink was slightly blurred, as if the pen had rested on the page a moment too long. She stared at those words for a long time. *Son.* One letter doing seven years of work.

She was still looking at it when she turned the page and found the fist marks.

* * *

They started eight years back, just after Tam died, in her father's hand but different—quicker, less careful, the writing of a man working late with his mind already three steps ahead. At first glance the entries looked ordinary. Orders for paper. Payments received. But each one carried a small symbol pressed into the margin beside it: a printer's fist, the little pointing hand that compositors used to mark directions in job copy. *Look here,* it meant. *Pay attention.*

Her father had marked forty-one entries.

Lior counted them twice, her lips moving silently, because forty-one was such an oddly specific number and specificity meant intention. Then she looked closer at what the marked entries actually recorded, and the warmth went out of her slowly, like a stove dying overnight.

An order for two hundred sheets of fine vellum stock, paid by Maret Suden, the laundress who did the shop's linens—and who had no conceivable use for two hundred sheets of anything. A payment received from the cooper Tovan Bray, whose cooperage had burned down the winter before and who should have had nothing to give anyone. A run of *private stationery* printed for the widow Corvel, billed as calling cards, except the quantity—three hundred cards for a woman who received perhaps ten visitors a year—was absurd on its face.

And then, near the bottom of the marked pages, an entry that was not disguised at all. In fact it was almost careless, which frightened her more than any cipher would have:

*Exemption papers, 12 sets. Consignment fee waived. — Tell no one. Burn the proofs.*

Twelve sets of exemption papers. Imperial documents, the kind that bought a young man freedom from conscription—the kind that were supposed to come from the provincial capital, sealed and serialized and utterly beyond the reach of a river-town printer. Except that a printer with a skilled hand and a patient eye could cut a facsimile plate. Except that her father owned the finest engraving burin in Vellmere, and had taught her to use it since she was nine years old, telling her that precision was a form of love.

Lior sat very still in the lamplight and let the arithmetic finish itself.

Forty-one entries over eight years. Paper orders that weren't paper orders. Payments from people with no money and no reason. Twelve sets of forged exemptions, and a father who wrote *tell no one* in a book he must have known was the least private object in the household.

He had been printing forgeries under her nose. For years. In the same shop where she sorted type fourteen hours a day, where she prided herself on knowing every stick of furniture and every hidden drawer, where she had built her entire life on the principle that she noticed things—that noticing was her one talent, her armor, her craft. And she had missed this. Or worse—had she missed it? Had some part of her seen the odd late hours, the locked cabinet in the back room, the nights her father burned papers in the stove and told her the damp had ruined the proofs? Had she chosen not to see, the way she chose not to see so many things, because seeing meant acting and acting meant risk?

No. She tested the memory honestly, the way you test a forme for level. No. This was genuine ignorance. He had kept it from her specifically. Seven years of teaching her everything—every trick of the trade, every subtlety of spacing, how to kern letters so tightly they seemed to lean against each other whispering—and he had kept this one door shut.

Why?

Because she wasn't trustworthy? The thought stung, then dissolved. No—because she *was*. Because she passed. Because a girl living as a boy had spent seven years learning exactly how dangerous knowledge could be, and her father, whatever else he was, had loved her enough to keep her ignorant of anything that could hang her.

Or had loved himself enough. Or loved the work enough. The motives tangled like snarled lead type, impossible to separate without breaking something.

Lior copied the fist-marked entries onto a scrap of proofing paper, her own handwriting disguised into a round clerk's hand without her even deciding to disguise it—an old reflex, automatic as breathing. Forty-one names. She folded the paper small and slipped it into her shirt, against her skin, where the borrowed identity lived.

Then she heard the floorboard creak overhead.

* * *

She had the ledger back in its drawer and the lock turned before the second creak sounded. The lamp she pinched out, and she stood in the sudden dark letting her eyes adjust, cataloguing sounds the way her father had taught her to catalogue type: by weight, by position, by feel.

Footsteps crossed the room above. Paused at the top of the stairs. Long enough for a man to decide whether to descend. Then they retreated, and a bed frame groaned, and the house resumed its breathing.

He'd gotten up for water, probably. Or to check the stove damper. Or—

Or he slept badly these days, the way he'd slept badly before the requisition, before Verel's polished boots came clicking across their threshold, and part of him lay awake listening for exactly what she'd just done.

Lior climbed the stairs with her heart going hard and quiet. In her narrow bed, in the dark, she took out the folded proofing paper and held it without unfolding it. Forty-one names. Twelve forged exemption sets. A father who was either a criminal or a rescuer, and in Vellmere tonight, under imperial law, those were the same thing—which was somehow the worst part, because it meant the question wasn't whether he'd done wrong. The question was what she was going to do about knowing.

Report him, and hang him. Stay silent, and share the rope when it came. And somewhere in between, a third door she couldn't quite see yet, standing open in the dark of the shop below.

Through the window, across the roofs, the imperial barracks lanterns burned in the square where the drums had marched all week. Somewhere out there, Captain Verel's clerk was presumably doing exactly what she had done tonight—reading, cross-checking, looking for the discrepancy that unraveled everything. They'll send for it, the clerk had said.

They would. And when they did, they would find the fist marks too. Unless—

Lior lay staring at the ceiling beams until the harbor bells rang three, and the shape of what she might have to do grew no clearer, only heavier. Below her, in the dark, the press waited like a sleeping animal that had finally shown its teeth.

In the morning, she decided, she would ask her father nothing. She would simply stand in front of him, and set a line of type, and watch his face when she reached for the locked cabinet in the back room.

And if he flinched—if he flinched even a little—then she would finally understand what her family had really been printing all these years, and whose name would be next in the ledger's long, patient memory of secrets.
