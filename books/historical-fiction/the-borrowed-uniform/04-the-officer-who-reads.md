## 4. The Officer Who Reads

He came back on a Thursday, alone, without his boots polished to the point of insult.

Lior knew it was Thursday because Thursdays were when Maret's laundry cart rattled past the shop window at half-past nine, and because her father had taught her that a week was just a forme of days locked tight together, and if one went missing the whole page collapsed. She had been sorting type — the fourteen-point Caslon that Aldous hoarded like a miser hoards coins he never intends to spend — when the bell above the door gave its small brass cough and Captain Verel stepped in out of the drizzle.

No escort. No sergeant. No drummers in the square. Just the captain, a folded paper under his arm, and rain beading on the shoulders of his grey uniform like the town itself had tried to make him less official and failed.

"Apprentice," he said.

"Sir." Lior set down her composing stick with the deliberate slowness of someone who had nothing in her hands worth hiding. This was not entirely true. Her hands always had something worth hiding. They were simply very good at looking empty.

"You're the son."

It was not a question, which was fortunate, because Lior had answered it so many times in seven years that the word *yes* came out of her the way ink comes off a well-inked roller — smooth, even, without thought. "Yes, sir. Lio Crane."

Verel looked around the shop the way she imagined a man might look around a church whose denomination he didn't share — respectful, wary, and privately counting the candlesticks. His gaze traveled over the cases of type, the two presses (the old Albion that groaned like her father's knees, and the newer hand-press Aldous kept under oilcloth for fine work), the hanging sheets of last month's proclamations, tax notices, a wedding announcement for the Harrow family done up in swashes so ornate that even Lior had thought them excessive.

"You did this?" He nodded at the Harrow announcement.

"The master set the design. I imposed and pulled the proofs." *The master* was a lie of convenience; her father had been abed with fever since Tuesday, sweating through three shirts, and Lior had imposed, proofed, corrected, and re-imposed the whole job herself. But *the master* was also armor, and she wore it the way Verel wore his coat.

"He trusts you with imposition." Verel said it as though she'd confessed to handling gunpowder.

"He trusts me with everything, sir. There's only the two of us."

"Mm." He unfolded his paper. It was the requisition order — his own signature on it, she noticed, small and precise, like handwriting that had been beaten into obedience by a schoolmaster with a ruler. "I have questions about your recent orders. Commercial ones. You keep records of commissions?"

"In the day book, sir."

"Show me."

Here was the thing about the day book: it was honest, because honesty in a day book is cheap. Every job for six months sat there in her father's crabbed hand or her own careful one — playbills, invoices, funeral cards for old Widow Sennet printed four times because the woman kept not-quite dying, forty hymnals for the Chapel of the River Stair. Nothing seditious. Nothing interesting. The interesting work, if it existed — and Lior had begun, in the small hours, to suspect it existed — lived elsewhere, behind other doors, in the vault where the ledger slept.

She fetched the book and laid it open on the stone counter between them, and Verel began, unbelievably, to read it.

Not skim. Not flip pages with the bored authority of a man confirming paperwork exists. He *read*, the way her father read — lips slightly moving, forefinger tracing, pausing at the margins. At one point he turned back two pages to check something against what came later, which no soldier in the history of armies had ever done to a printer's day book, and Lior felt something dangerous loosen in her chest, some knot she hadn't known was tied.

"You're left-handed," he said suddenly.

"Yes, sir."

"But you write right-handed in the book."

The silence lasted perhaps two seconds. Two seconds is nothing; two seconds is the time it takes a press to close and open. In those two seconds Lior watched her whole borrowed life flicker like a guttering lamp — *he sees, he knows, it's over* — and then she heard herself say, mildly:

"My master says a compositor's hands belong to the case. Left hand pulls, right hand sets. Writing's just another kind of setting. Habit spreads."

It was a beautiful lie. It was even mostly true. Aldous *had* said something like it once, though about whistling, not writing. Verel studied her a moment longer — long enough that she catalogued, uselessly, the exact shade of tired under his eyes, the way his collar chafed him, the ink stain on his own right forefinger that he clearly didn't know was there — and then he let it go.

"Hm," he said, which from him seemed to constitute surrender.

* * *

What followed was the strangest hour Lior had spent since the drums came to Vellmere, because Captain Verel, imperial officer, requisitioner of ledgers, terror of the paper quarter, wanted to talk about *typefaces.*

"I've been reviewing the civic documents we seized," he said, wandering along the type wall with his hands clasped behind him like a man touring a garden. "Proclamations, tax rolls, parish notices going back sixty years. All printed here, I assume."

"Everything official comes through this shop, sir. Has for three generations."

"And the type changes." He stopped before the case of long-bodied letters Aldous called *the widow-maker* because its serifs were so thin they snapped if you breathed on them. "Sixty years ago the numerals had flat tops. Forty years ago, curved. Twenty years ago, someone recut them smaller." He glanced at her. "Why?"

Lior blinked. Of all the interrogations she had rehearsed in her head since the requisition — *where do you print at night, who brings you paper after dark, why does your father sweat when soldiers are mentioned* — none of them had included a question about numeral tops.

"Punches wear, sir. And each generation of Cranes recuts what wears out. My grandfather believed a town should be able to date its own documents by its type, the way you date a tree by its rings."

"A town should be able to date its own documents," Verel repeated. Something moved across his face — not suspicion; recognition, almost, like a man hearing his own thought spoken in an unfamiliar accent. "That's exactly what I told the quartermaster in Corvane. He looked at me as though I'd suggested the army adopt poetry."

"What did the quartermaster in Corvane suggest instead?"

"Bigger stamps. Redder ink. The belief that authority is proportional to volume." The corner of his mouth twitched — upward, briefly, before discipline caught it. "I was overruled."

Against every instinct drilled into her over seven years of silences, Lior heard herself laugh. A small laugh, quickly strangled, but a laugh. Verel turned and looked at her with mild surprise, as though a piece of furniture had spoken.

"Forgive me, sir. I only — my master says the same thing. About volume. He says a proclamation nobody reads is just expensive wallpaper."

"Your master sounds like a man I might have liked," said Verel, "if he were not currently confined to quarters under municipal audit."

"He has a fever, sir. That part isn't politics."

"No," Verel agreed. "Fevers rarely are." He drifted back toward the counter and the day book, and Lior relaxed by precisely one degree, which was one degree too many.

* * *

Because then he asked the question.

It arrived without ceremony, tucked between remarks about paper stock, the way a splinter hides in a plank of wood — invisible until your hand finds it.

"The tribunal schedule," he said, examining the grain of the counter as though it were a document in itself. "Forty households summoned for review. Their exemption papers will need verifying against original records. Your shop printed exemptions, historically?"

"Sometimes, sir. When the governor's office sent the forms."

"When the governor's office sent the forms." He nodded slowly. "And in the last year? How many exemption orders came through here?"

Lior kept her eyes on the day book. Somewhere in the room a drop of water fell from the eaves outside, patient as a clock. She could feel the shape of the true answer sitting in her chest like swallowed lead — *none, sir, not one in eleven months* — and beside it the shape of the answer her father would want, and between them the narrow gap she had to walk.

"Orders come and go, sir. I impose what's put on the stone. The commercial side of the shop is below my station to track."

"Below your station." He looked at her, and there it was again — that literate, unhurried attention, the attention of a man reading a difficult page. "You impose type for a living, apprentice. Impression is the entire trade. Nothing gets printed in this building that doesn't pass under your eye first. So let's not insult each other with 'below your station.'"

The room tilted, very slightly. Not fear exactly — or not only fear. There was something else in it, warm and appalling, that Lior refused to name: he saw her. He saw how good she was. He saw that the boy standing at the counter could read a man's questions the way she read a galley proof, catching the errors before they were struck. And some starved, buried corner of her — the corner that had been fed on seven years of *don't look too closely, don't stand too straight, don't laugh too loud, be Tam, be Tam, be Tam* — leaned toward that seeing like a plant toward a window.

*Be careful*, said the rest of her, coldly. *Windows are also how they shoot you.*

"You're right, sir," she said. "Nothing prints here without passing my eye. Which is why I can tell you honestly that I pull proofs of whatever's composed. What's composed, and by whom, and why — that conversation happens upstairs, at the desk, with the man whose name goes in the book. Mine doesn't."

Verel considered this. Rain ticked against the window. Down the street, Maret's cart rattled past, late today, and Lior tracked the sound with the edge of her hearing while keeping her face perfectly, professionally bored.

"That," Verel said at last, "is the most honest evasion I've heard since I took this posting."

"I learned from watching the army, sir."

This time the twitch at the corner of his mouth got all the way to his eyes before he killed it. He closed the day book. "I'll want the vault opened tomorrow. The ledger, and anything stored with it. Ten o'clock."

"Of course, sir."

"And apprentice—" He paused at the door, one hand on the frame, and did not turn fully around. "The Harrow wedding announcement. The swash on the capital H. It's cut wrong. The tail crosses the bowl where it shouldn't. Someone modified that letter recently — added the flourish by grinding down an ordinary H, unless I miss my guess, which I don't."

Lior's heart performed a slow, deliberate somersault.

"You're right, sir," she said. "We couldn't afford a new ornament sort, so I altered one. My master hasn't seen it yet. He'll have opinions."

"He should. It's good work." Verel said it flatly, the way a man reports the weather, and then he ruined it entirely by adding: "Better than the master's, maybe. Don't tell him I said so."

And then he was gone, into the drizzle, boots striking the cobbles in that measured cadence that the whole street had learned to flinch to, leaving Lior alone in her father's shop with the smell of ink and rain and the ringing silence of a sentence she would turn over for the rest of the day like a coin she suspected was counterfeit:

*Better than the master's, maybe.*

* * *

She did not tell Aldous. Aldous was asleep, feverish, muttering figures in his sleep — sums, she thought, columns of sums, though once he said a name twice, low and urgent, and it was neither hers nor Tam's nor her mother's, and she decided not to hear it.

But she stood at the type wall for a long time after, in the grey light, looking at the case where the altered H lived among its unmodified brothers. One letter, ground down and reshaped by lamplight, hiding in plain sight among forty others, doing work it was never cast for, indistinguishable unless you knew — unless you *looked*, really looked, the way almost no one ever bothered to look.

Verel had looked.

He had looked at a swash on a wedding announcement and seen the file-marks, the grind of metal, the hours of somebody's secret labor, and called it *good work*. And Lior stood there with her heart going too fast and understood that she was in more danger than the drums had ever put her in — not because the captain suspected her, but because some reckless, starving part of her wanted him to.

That night, after she banked the forge and checked the lock on the vault door twice, she found the note.

It had been slipped under the door sometime during the day, folded small, unsigned. She recognized the hand immediately — the same blunt, soap-scented script that signed the laundry receipts. Four words only:

*He's counting the type.*

Lior stood holding it over the banked coals for a long moment, watching the firelight move through the paper. Then she fed it to the embers and watched it curl, and went upstairs to sit with her father, and did not sleep.

Counting the type. Counting the rings of the tree.

Tomorrow at ten o'clock, Captain Verel would stand in front of the vault door, and Lior would have to unlock it, and somewhere inside — beneath the ledger with its final page bearing a name she had not spoken aloud in seven years — whatever her father had been printing in the dark was waiting to be counted too.
