## 5. Seven Years of Silences

The first silence Lior ever kept was at her brother's funeral.

She was nine years old and did not yet know it was a craft. She thought it was simply that she had nothing to say. Tam lay in the coffin looking like a boy who had been asked a difficult question and was still deciding how to answer, and everyone around the grave wept in their various ways—her father silently, in the manner of men who believe grief is a leak they can personally patch; old Mistress Quennel loudly, into a handkerchief that smelled of camphor; and her mother not at all.

Her mother stood with her hands folded, dry-eyed, watching the coffin as if it were a crate she was calculating freight on. Later Lior would understand that look. It was not coldness. It was a woman doing arithmetic she could not say aloud: one child gone, one husband drowning, one daughter left over—and what does this town do with daughters?

The answer came within the month. Her mother left on the river barge with a single trunk and a kiss pressed to the top of Lior's head, which even at nine Lior recognized as a payment rather than an embrace. "Mind your father," she said. "Mind the shop." And then, quieter, so quietly that only a girl already practicing silences could have caught it: "And mind yourself, little one. You're more than you're being made into."

Lior did not know then what she meant. She spent seven years learning.

* * *

The trouble began with the apprenticeship itself—or rather, with what Tam's death did to it.

Aldous Crane held the only press license in Vellmere, and a press license required a registered apprentice, and a registered apprentice required a bond paid to the Guild of Ink & Impression in Corvane, and the bond was paid for one Tammas Crane, son of Aldous Crane, aged twelve years and four months when he went through the ice on the Mere with two other boys, both of whom were fished out breathing and neither of whom ever quite forgave themselves or the river.

The guild did not refund bonds for dead apprentices. The guild did not transfer bonds to daughters, because the guild did not recognize that such a thing could exist. Aldous sat up three nights with the license papers spread before him like a patient bleeding on a table, and on the fourth morning he called Lior down to the shop early, before light, and set her on the stool at the stone sink where Tam used to wash the rollers.

"From today," he said, in the voice he used for setting type—flat, precise, no letter wasted—"your name is Lio."

She looked at him. He looked back with an expression she would spend years decoding: part apology, part plea, part the terrible practical calm of a man who has decided that a living lie is kinder than a dying truth.

"The guild man comes at midwinter," he said. "He will ask for my apprentice. I have one apprentice. His name is Lio Crane. He is small for his age, which happens. He lost his voice to a fever last spring, which also happens." A pause. The ink-smell of the shop hung between them, linseed and lampblack. "Can you be quiet, Lior?"

She could. Being quiet was the one thing she had never once failed at.

"I can be quiet, Papa."

"Then be quiet beautifully," he said, and something in his face cracked just slightly, like a forme under too much pressure, and he turned away and began locking up a chase as though the world were a normal place where fathers did not ask daughters to disappear into their dead brothers' clothes.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about becoming someone else: it is mostly laundry.

Tam's shirts needed taking in at the shoulders. His boots needed stuffing at the toe until Lior's feet stopped sloshing in them like eggs in a bucket. His breeches needed—well. There were arrangements, learned by trial and error and one mortifying incident involving the privy behind the Wheelwright's Arms that Lior resolved never to think about again and thought about constantly. Her hair went under a cap and stayed there. When it grew too thick to hide, Aldous cut it himself with the shears they used for trimming paper, standing her on a beer crate in the yard, snipping in silence while snow fell into the ink barrels.

"You'd think," he said at last, holding up a hand-mirror so she could see the result—a sharp-faced boy with ink-stained fingers and enormous watchful eyes—"you'd think after thirty years at the case I could make a straighter line than that."

"It looks fine," said Lio.

"It looks like a hedge attacked in the dark." But his mouth twitched, and that twitch was worth more to her than she could say, because in those first months the twitches were rare. Grief had moved into the shop the way damp moves into plaster: invisibly, everywhere, and showing itself only when the weather changed.

They developed rituals. Every evening he taught her the trade properly—not as play, not as disguise, but truly, because (he said) a lie held up badly is worse than no lie at all; if you are going to be an apprentice, be the best damned apprentice in Vellmere. He taught her the names of the types the way other fathers taught sons the names of fish: Garamond, that's a proud face, wants room to breathe; the pica body, workhorse of the trade; never, ever mix foundries in the same forme unless you want your lines walking off at different heights like a militia that's never drilled together. He taught her to feel a piece of type wrong-side-up in the dark and know its letter by the nick. He taught her that the press was not a machine but a temperamental animal, and that it hated cold, haste, and flattery, in roughly that order.

And every night, before banked the lamps, he said the same thing: "Good work today, lad." Not Lior. Lad. It was a key turned in a lock each night, and she felt it turn, and she told herself the small ache afterward was nothing, was habit, was the price of bread.

* * *

The guild man came at midwinter, as promised: a narrow, sour individual named Petch with spectacles thick as press-glass and the unshakeable conviction that all boys were lying about something.

"Apprentice Crane," he said, squinting at Lio across the shop. "You're small."

"Growth comes unevenly, sir," said Aldous smoothly. "Like impression from a worn platen. We compensate."

"Hm. And I hear the boy had a fever. Lost his voice?"

"Partially. Comes and goes. Doctor Voss has it in writing."

Doctor Voss did indeed have it in writing, because Aldous had paid Doctor Voss to have it in writing, which was the first time Lior understood that her father's silences had infrastructure—that behind each one stood documents, arrangements, small payments, a whole architecture of quiet built as carefully as any cathedral. She had thought lies were simple things, spoken and done. She learned they could be load-bearing.

Petch peered at her. Lior looked back with what she hoped was sullen adolescent boredom, a performance she found so easy it alarmed her slightly. Say nothing. Boys who talk get caught; boys who sulk get believed.

"He'll do," Petch pronounced at last, making his mark in the ledger—the ledger, Lior noted, the great master book that lived in the vault beneath the shop floor and held every debt, birth, bribe, and bargain in Vellmere. "Though I'll say it plain, Crane: that boy's got a look about him. Too watchful. Watchful boys grow up to be trouble."

"Not in my shop," said Aldous.

But that night, after Petch had gone, Aldous sat down heavily on the composing stone's edge and laughed—a short, cracked, astonished laugh, the first since the funeral.

"Too watchful," he repeated. "If only he knew, Lior. If only the man knew what he certified today."

"What did he certify?" Lior asked.

Her father looked at her for a long moment. "An apprentice," he said finally. "He certified an apprentice. That's all any of them will ever see. That's the trick of it, little one. People see what the page says. Always remember that. People see what the page says."

She remembered. She built her life on it.

* * *

By her thirteenth year, Lior had become genuinely good at being Lio—which was, she was beginning to suspect, a different thing entirely from being good at anything else.

It happened in increments so small she didn't mark them until they were done. She learned to walk with her heels down and her shoulders rounded, to laugh in a lower register, to spit (an accomplishment Aldous claimed not to have taught and visibly took pride in). She learned the geography of the boys' world: which corners belonged to which gangs, how to decline a wrestling match without inviting one, the exact shrug that said *I don't fight, I print* in a way that earned respect instead of fists. She learned that a boy could put his arm around another boy's shoulders, and she learned the specific loneliness of receiving that gesture while knowing it was addressed to a ghost.

And she learned—this was the strangest part, the part she kept folded away like a proof pulled too early—that some of it she liked. Not the deception. The competence. When she walked into the market square as Lio, nobody looked at her twice, and that not-looking was a kind of freedom she hadn't known existed. Girls in Vellmere were watched—assessed, priced, discussed—as relentlessly as goods on a scale. Boys were merely *there*, like lampposts, like rain barrels. To be unseen was to be unburdened. She understood why her mother had watched the barge leave with dry eyes, maybe. Perhaps invisibility had seemed to her too a fair price.

But there were nights—many nights—when Lior climbed the ladder to the loft above the shop, lay in Tam's old bed under Tam's old blanket, and felt the price come due with interest.

Because here was the arithmetic nobody had written down: in exchange for keeping her father's shop alive and her own belly full and her father's heart from stopping entirely, she had agreed to stop existing. Not die—nothing so clean. Just... pause. Be shelved, like a font out of season, wrapped in paper and labeled with somebody else's name, waiting for conditions that might permit her return. And the longer she waited, the more she suspected the conditions would never come. The guild man would return next midwinter, and the one after. The fever excuse would hold forever because it cost Aldous a coin a year to keep it holding. There was no exit from the story except the one she couldn't take, because taking it would kill him faster than the river had killed Tam.

So she practiced. That was the word for it, she decided one night, staring at the rafters: she was *practicing* silence the way Tam had practiced his letters, diligently, daily, in hopes of mastery. She practiced not answering when customers called her "young man" with particular warmth. She practiced not flinching when the miller's wife said, "Your mother, now, she had hair like yours under that cap, didn't she?"—because the miller's wife had known, or half-known, or wanted to know, and Lior had given her a blank apprentice stare until the woman's own doubt swallowed the question. She practiced being a closed book.

The irony was not lost on her. She lived above ten thousand open ones.

* * *

There was gentleness in it too. She would be dishonest with herself not to say so.

On winter evenings, when the presses were locked up and the coal glowed in the little stove, Aldous would sometimes forget himself—forget the performance, or perhaps stop performing it—and talk. Really talk, the way he never had when she'd been a daughter, because daughters in Vellmere were talked *at*, instructed, chaperoned in conversation as in everything else. Sons got confided in.

"That's your grandfather's frame," he said once, patting the great wooden skeleton of the press. "Hauled it from Corvane on an ox-cart when I was younger than you. Man told me it wouldn't survive the hills. Everything worth having gets told that." He warmed his hands at the stove. "One day it's yours, lad. Whatever else happens. The trade doesn't care what you are underneath. Ink's the most honest thing in the world—it goes exactly where you tell it and nowhere else. I've trusted it more than most people."

"Including Mama?" Lior asked, before her practice could catch her tongue.

The stove ticked. Aldous looked into the coals for a long time.

"People leave," he said at last. "It's not treachery. It's just... some people need a bigger page than this town offers." He glanced at her then, and there was something in his face she couldn't read—regret, maybe, or guilt, or the strange tenderness of a man looking at the child whose life he had rearranged to save his own. "You're not like her, though. You stayed."

*I wasn't asked,* Lior thought. But she said nothing, because saying nothing was the family trade, and she was its finest apprentice.

* * *

Seven years. Seven midwinters, seven guild inspections survived, seven springs of cutting her hair on the beer crate, seven autumns of watching girls her age walk to the well in pairs with their sleeves rolled and their laughter carrying, belonging to a country she could see but not enter.

She got so good at it that sometimes she forgot herself entirely—which was its own kind of horror, waking in Tam's bed with Tam's name in her mouth, reaching for the person under the costume and finding the seams had healed over. Who was she? A girl pretending to be a boy? Or a boy who occasionally dreamed of a girl? The distinction mattered less every year, and its mattering less frightened her more.

And then, in her sixteenth year, the imperial drums came to the square, and polished boots came to the shop door, and Captain Verel requisitioned the town and demanded the master ledger—the ledger, the great book in the vault, the book with every name in Vellmere written down including, somewhere in it, whatever pages recorded the true history of the Crane family, including the baptismal record of one Lior Crane, daughter of Aldous, born in the year the ice took her brother.

Her father handed it over too readily. She noticed that. Filed it away among the silences.

And later—much later, past midnight, unable to sleep, telling herself she was only checking the inventory of what the empire now knew about them—she lifted the false floor of the vault where a second copy lived, her father's working copy, the one he consulted for debts and dates, and she carried it up to the lamp, and she opened it.

Most of it was what she expected: accounts, arrears, the petty economy of a small town's grudges. But near the back, in her father's careful hand, was a list of names she didn't understand. Forty-odd of them, each marked in the margin with a small printed symbol she knew intimately from the type cases—a printer's fist, the little pointing hand that marked a note of importance.

She stared at it for a long time. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, in the street, an imperial patrol went by, boots striking cobbles in perfect time.

Forty names. Her father's hand. A mark meaning *look here, this matters.*

Lior closed the ledger softly, the way one closes a door on a room where something is breathing.

For seven years she had believed the silences in this house were hers alone—hers and her father's, a shared architecture, load-bearing, safe. Now she understood there had been another silence all along, running beneath theirs like a second cellar, and it was full of names.

In the morning, she decided, she would ask him what the fist marks meant.

In the morning, she discovered, she would finally break the one rule that had kept them both alive: she would speak first.
