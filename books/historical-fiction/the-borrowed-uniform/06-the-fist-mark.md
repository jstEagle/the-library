## 6. The Fist Mark

Lior had learned to read her father's silences the way other people learned to read weather. There was the silence of concentration, which hummed like a well-tuned press. There was the silence of grief, which arrived every year on the tenth of Thawmonth and sat in the shop like an unpaid debt. And there was a third kind—the one she had heard only twice before in seven years—which was the silence of a man deciding whether to lie.

She heard it on Thursday morning, while she was distributing type into the case and Aldous was standing at the vault door holding the ledger he was supposed to have surrendered three days ago.

He hadn't surrendered it. That was the first thing. Captain Verel had requisitioned all civic records; Aldous had handed over a book. Lior had watched him do it, watched the officer tuck it under his arm with the satisfaction of a man collecting taxes, and she had thought nothing of it—until this morning, when she went down into the vault for a spare quoined frame and found a second ledger lying where the first should have been. Same binding. Same water stain shaped like a leaping fish on the spine. A different book entirely.

The forgery was good. It wasn't perfect. The fish was facing the wrong way.

Now Aldous stood at the top of the vault stairs with the real ledger under his arm, wearing the third silence, and Lior set down her composing stick very carefully, as if it might go off.

"Papa," she said. "Where did you get that?"

"Got what?" said Aldous, who had once convinced an imperial tax assessor that the shop had no second floor.

"The ledger," Lior said. "The one you gave to Verel. The one I've been dusting around my entire life. The one with the fish."

"The fish is a decorative flourish."

"The fish faces left, Papa. The fish has always faced left. I used to think it was swimming toward the river." She came up the stairs slowly. "You gave him a copy. You bound a copy in three days, during curfew, in a locked shop, without waking me—and I sleep directly above your workbench."

Aldous looked at her for a long moment. Then he did something worse than lying or confessing: he smiled, faintly and with genuine pride, the way he did when she caught a kerning error nobody else could see.

"You checked the direction of the fish," he said. "Seven years old, sitting on that stool, and you told me the watermark swam the wrong way. I knew then you'd be the one to catch me someday."

"That is not comforting, Papa."

"No," he agreed. "I don't imagine it is."

* * *

Aldous Crane had rehearsed this conversation for six years, and in every rehearsal it went badly.

In the first rehearsal, from the early years, he confessed everything at once and Lior wept and forgave him, and they burned the ledger together and fled to the coast. In later rehearsals she didn't weep. In the most recent ones she simply looked at him with Tam's eyes—which were also her eyes, which was the whole unbearable trick of it—and asked questions he couldn't answer, because the true answers were shameful in ways forgery was not.

The truth was this: he had never planned any of it. He had inherited the ledger from his own father, who had inherited it from his, and the Crane family business had always been two businesses. There was the print shop, honest as bread. And there was the book—a record of everything Vellmere preferred not to say aloud. Debts forgiven and debts held over heads. Babies born nine months after certain weddings, entered under the wrong father's name so that the right one could be blackmailed quietly for a generation. Payments made to magistrates. Payments made by magistrates. His grandfather had kept it; his father had kept it; and Aldous had kept it, telling himself each year that this would be the year he burned it, and each year finding a reason—a debt to settle, a secret that still protected someone—that let him keep it one more winter.

And then, four years ago, the resistance had come to him through Maret, and discovered that the town's most dangerous book was kept by the town's most invisible man, and asked him a question no rehearsal had prepared him for: *Master Crane, if a family needed papers saying their son was exempt—too ill, too essential, already dead—could you make such papers?*

He had said yes. He had told himself it was because forty families would hang otherwise. That was true. But underneath the truth was another truth, smaller and uglier: after Tam died, after his wife walked out of the shop and out of Vellmere both, Aldous Crane had discovered that being trusted with a great secret was the only thing keeping him upright. He had lost a son and a wife. He had kept a daughter by turning her into someone else. And the ledger, the lies, the hidden work—it was the last room in his life where nothing had been taken from him yet.

He saw all of this clearly now, standing at the top of the stairs with his daughter looking at him like an examiner, and he understood that whatever he said next would be the least honest sentence of his life unless he was very careful.

"Sit down, Lio," he said, and then stopped. "Lior. Sit down, Lior."

It was the first time in seven years he had said her name aloud inside the shop. He watched it land on her face like a dropped case of type.

* * *

Her name. Her actual name, in her father's voice, in the workshop where names went to die.

Lior sat down on the distribution stool because her legs suggested it. Aldous set the real ledger on the bench between them, open to the middle, where a page bore a small symbol stamped in red ink beside eleven names: a closed hand, knuckles forward. A printer's fist. The mark typesetters used to flag corrections.

Except these weren't corrections.

"These families," Aldous said. "Eleven names. Eleven households. Each one marked with the fist because each one has a son the tribunal will call for when the conscription rolls are verified. And each one has received, from this shop, a document stating that son is exempt—unfit, indispensable, deceased, whichever lie fit best. I printed those documents. I cut the plates myself. And I paid for the materials"—he tapped the ledger—"out of accounts in this book that don't belong to anyone anymore."

"What accounts?"

"Debts owed to dead men. Bribes recorded against magistrates who died before they could collect. Money that exists nowhere but here." He turned pages. "Your grandfather called them ghost sums. They can't be spent in the ordinary way—but paper can be bought, ink can be bought, and a laundress named Maret can carry coin to a chandler in Lowmarsh who asks no questions. This book has been paying for the lives of eleven families for four years, and no one alive knows how except you, now, and Maret."

Lior looked at the fist marks. Eleven little red hands, raised like the fists on resistance broadsides, except these had been stamped by her father, in her shop, in the hours she thought he was rebinding hymnals.

"Why didn't you tell me?"

The question came out steadier than she felt. Aldous winced anyway.

"Because telling you made it yours," he said. "Because you have spent seven years carrying one secret large enough to crush a person, and I swore—I swore over Tam's grave—that you would not carry two."

"And instead I carried one secret and a lie about the secret." She stood up. Her voice was doing something she didn't authorize; it had gone high and hard. "Papa, do you know what I thought when I found that fake ledger? I thought Verel had gotten to you. I thought they'd threatened you and you'd folded. I lay awake half the night wondering if I'd need to run, or if I'd need to—to fix it somehow, forge my way out, because that's apparently what this family does now—"

"Lior—"

"I built a whole plan! At three in the morning! I decided I would steal into Verel's quarters and swap the ledgers back, and I got as far as working out that his window latch is broken on the left side because I *watched him leave it unlatched*, Papa, I cased a captain of the imperial army based on a *fish*, and meanwhile you're downstairs running a forgery ring like it's a normal Tuesday!"

There was a silence. Then, horribly, Aldous laughed—not much, just once, quickly, before pressing his lips shut—but enough. Enough that Lior felt the strange double heat of fury and laughter fighting in her own chest, and lost.

"You cased the captain," Aldous managed.

"He reads typefaces, Papa. Nobody reads typefaces. He asked me whether our body text was cut locally and I nearly died."

"He asked you that?" All the humor went out of Aldous's face at once. He turned a page of the ledger, quickly, hunting. "When?"

"Last week. Why?"

"Because there's a name missing from this book," Aldous said. "One entry, torn out at the gutter—cut, not ripped, cut with a knife—and the last person I showed this ledger to before I bound the copy was Captain Verel."

* * *

Verel had cut the page. Aldous was almost sure of it.

Almost sure was a new condition for Aldous Crane, who had spent fifty years being sure of things—sure of his type, sure of his silences, sure that invisibility was a craft and he was its master craftsman. He watched his daughter across the bench, her jaw still set from shouting, her hands already moving toward the ledger with a compositor's instinct for taking hold of things properly, and he felt the argument arrive that he had been dreading longer than the confession itself.

Because she was going to say it. He could see it forming in her like ink soaking into unsized paper.

"You should burn it," Lior said.

"There it is," Aldous murmured.

"After the tribunal. After the forty—eleven, whoever—all of them are safe. Burn it. Every ghost sum, every secret birth, every magistrate's bribe. If Verel finds it—if the empire finds it—it becomes a weapon against everyone in it. And if we keep it, it becomes a weapon forever. It's already a weapon, Papa. It's been a weapon since Grandfather's day. You just never had to be the one it pointed at."

"It points at everyone," Aldous said quietly. "That's what a ledger is."

"Then why keep it?"

And here was the thing he could not say, the thing that had no honorable version. So he said the next-best thing, which was at least true.

"Because my father kept it. And his father. Because there are entries in this book that are the only proof certain people ever existed—servants, bastards, the drowned, girls whose mothers paid to have their births written down wrong so they could inherit. You want me to burn the weapon. I hear you. But this book is also the only place some of those people are still *in*. You understand that better than most." He met her eyes. "I'm not only keeping the secrets, Lior. I'm keeping the people."

Lior was silent for a long moment. Outside, imperial drums sounded in the square—the noon muster, flat and regular as a press beating out its impression. When she spoke again her voice had changed, gone careful.

"Tam's name is in there," she said. "His apprenticeship. The day you registered me in his place—you wrote it in the ledger, didn't you. 'Tam Crane, apprentice, present and accounted.' Seven years of accounting for a boy who's dead."

"Yes."

"So part of what you're keeping," Lior said, "is me."

"Part of what I'm keeping," Aldous said, "is the only way I know how to keep anything."

* * *

Lior looked down at the ledger and understood, suddenly, that she and her father had been having this argument for years without either of them saying it aloud. Every time she'd caught him glancing at the vault door. Every time he'd touched the book's spine like a man touching a scar. Loyalty and truth had been living in this shop together the whole time, sharing the same lamp, and neither of them had ever asked which one slept on the floor.

She wanted to hate the ledger. She tried. But her hands knew its weight the way they knew the press, and somewhere under her anger was a terrible, treacherous pride: her father, the invisible man, had been cutting forgery plates in the dark for four years, alone, for strangers. It was the bravest and stupidest thing she had ever learned about him, and she had once learned he'd smuggled banned pamphlets inside catechism covers.

"Show me how the plates are done," she said at last.

Aldous blinked. "That's not—"

"You cut them yourself, at your age, with your eyes, alone. And now Verel's asking questions about typefaces and tearing pages out of books, and you've got a fever coming on—I've watched you shiver twice since I came downstairs and you keep standing near the stove like it owes you money. If something happens to you, the work doesn't stop, because eleven families don't stop hanging. So show me."

"Lio—"

"Lior," she corrected. "If we're using real names down here, use mine."

Something moved across her father's face—grief and pride braided tight together, exactly as hers were. He reached beneath the false bottom of the type cabinet, past the wedding invitation formes, and lifted out a wrapped plate, and unwrapped it, and set it in the lamplight: exemption papers, cut fine as lace, good enough to fool a tribunal.

And that was when they both heard the knock at the street door—heavy, official, gloved—three blows, evenly spaced, the knock of a man who expected doors to apologize.

Aldous's hand closed over the plate. Lior blew out the lamp.

From the darkness of the workshop, they listened to boots cross the threshold, and Captain Verel's voice, mild as milk, say: "Master Crane. Don't get up. I've only come about a page that seems to be missing from your book."
