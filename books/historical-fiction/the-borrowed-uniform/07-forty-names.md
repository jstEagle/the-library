## 7. Forty Names

Maret Solenne had been doing the Crane family's laundry for eleven years, and in that time she had learned three things about them: that Aldous paid promptly, that he was hopeless about ink stains and would deny their existence to her face, and that the child—*Lio*, the quiet one, the one who folded like paper and watched everything through his lashes—was the sharpest blade in a drawer of dull knives.

She had learned a fourth thing only recently, and it was the reason she now stood in the wash-yard behind the print shop at half past five on a morning cold enough to crack a bucket, holding a basket of shirts she had no intention of washing.

The fourth thing was this: Aldous Crane was a forger.

Well. That wasn't quite fair. Aldous Crane was a printer, which in Maret's experience amounted to nearly the same thing but with better posture.

"Your shirts," she called toward the open back door, where lamplight spilled yellow onto the frost. "Three of them. The grey one's beyond saving, so don't look at me like that when you see it."

Aldous appeared in the doorway, wrapped in a coat that had seen better decades. "Maret. It's early."

"It's laundry." She lifted the basket onto her hip. "Laundry keeps its own hours. Are you going to invite me in out of the cold or are we going to conduct our business shouting across your yard like two geese?"

He hesitated. She counted it—one heartbeat, two—and then he stepped aside. That hesitation told her more than anything he might have said. Eleven years ago he'd have waved her straight to the kitchen without looking up from his proofs.

The shop smelled as it always did: ink and oil and hot metal, the smell Maret privately thought of as *money being made honestly*, which was ironic now, given everything. Lamps burned low over the composing stone. And there, curled on a stool by the type cases with a stick in hand and a frown like a small thundercloud, sat Lio.

"Maret," the child said—not looking up, because looking up cost something; Maret had noticed years ago how carefully the boy rationed his glances, as if eyes were coins.

"Lio." She set the basket down with a thump. "You've grown again. Another inch and you'll be taller than me, and then who will I scold from above?"

"You could still try."

"There's spirit. Your father had none at your age, I'll tell you that for free." She said it lightly, watching both of them. Aldous flinched at nothing. Lio flinched at *everything*, very slightly, very fast, the way a deer does—and yet never once, in all these years, had the flinch been about what people thought it was about. Maret kept that observation folded up tight in the pocket where she kept dangerous things.

"Aldous," she said. "I need a word. In private."

"We're mid-sort—"

"It'll keep until after my word, it will." She smiled at Lio. "Go fetch me water from your pump, love. My arms are old and my basket is heavy and I'll not apologize for using children as pack mules, it's tradition."

Lio went. Maret listened to the footsteps fade, waited until she heard the pump handle groan, and turned to Aldous with the smile gone off her face the way frost goes off a window.

"Forty," she said.

He went pale in the particular way printers go pale—all at once, like a sheet pulled off the press. "I don't know what you mean."

"Course you don't. And I don't do your wash, and the moon is made of cheese." She stepped closer. "Forty names, Aldous. Forty families on Verel's tribunal list. I've seen the copy—I've *carried* the copy, sewn into a mattress, which my back will be complaining about till spring. Forty households marked for the hanging tree because someone in each of them signed a petition, or sheltered a cousin, or looked at an officer sideways in the fish market. And every exemption paper you've printed so far has been for the *easy* names. The ones already half-hidden. You've done eleven."

"Twelve," Aldous said quietly.

"Twelve. Lovely. Twenty-eight to go, and the tribunal sits in nine days, and here you are sorting loose type at dawn like a man with nothing on his conscience but ink stains."

"How—" He stopped. Started again. His hands, she noticed, were shaking, and he pressed them flat against the stone to stop them, the way a man steadies a page. "How do you know the count? Those lists were—"

"Copied, Aldous. Everything gets copied. That's rather the whole point of your trade, isn't it?" She let him sit with that a moment, then softened, because softening him was a skill too, and she'd had practice. "Sit down before you fall down. No—make tea first, you're useless without tea, you always were. Your wife used to say so."

Something crossed his face at the mention of the wife—the mother, gone these seven years, gone the way some people leave, which is entirely and without forwarding address. Maret hadn't liked the woman much, truth told; a laundress notices who treats other women as furniture. But grief isn't tidy and doesn't consult taste.

Aldous made the tea. His hands stopped shaking somewhere around the second cup poured, and Maret took hers and warmed her fingers and got down to it.

"The committee sent me," she said. "Not because I'm clever, though I am. Because I'm nobody. A laundress walks into any house in Vellmere and no one looks twice—that's my uniform, same as a soldier's got his. And they sent me because the last man they sent was Tam Crane's friend, and Tam's dead, and they thought—" she paused—"they thought the name might open your door faster than mine would."

"It did open the door," Aldous said. "It just also closed my throat for a week."

"I know. I'm sorry. Using a dead boy's name is ugly work." She drank. "But here's where we stand, and I'll say it plain because plainness is the one luxury I allow myself. Twenty-eight exemptions remain unprinted. The papers must be finished, verified, and distributed before the tribunal convenes—before, mind, not during, because once those officers start reading names aloud there's no unpicking it. And there's a further problem, which is the reason I've come at dawn instead of midnight like a sensible smuggler."

"The ledger," Aldous said.

"The ledger." She set down her cup. "Verel has it locked in the records office, and Verel, God rot his careful soul, is *reading it*. Page by page. Cross-checking. The man reads like a priest does scripture—slowly, and looking for himself in it. When he finds the accounts you've been drawing on—the hidden entries, the fist-marked names—it won't take him long to trace the money to the paper. Paper to press. Press to printer. Printer to—" she waved her cup at the room, at the type cases and the stone and the low lamps—"all of this."

"So the run must finish before he finishes reading."

"So the run must finish before he finishes reading. Yes. And there's the last bit, the bit I've been circling like a cat round a hot stove." She looked at him steadily. "The master plate can't be cut by you."

Silence. From the yard, faintly, the pump handle groaned again—Lio was taking his time, bless him or curse him.

"My hands," Aldous began, and she saw it plainly now, up close, what she'd been pretending not to see for weeks: the tremor that wasn't fear, the flush along the jaw, the sweat at the hairline despite the cold. "My hands are steady enough."

"Your hands are the hands of a man burning up with fever, and we both know it, and you've known it since the equinox and said nothing because saying it would mean stopping." She reached over and took his wrist—not gently—and held it between her own two hands until the trembling was undeniable to them both. "That's not fear, Aldous. That's sickness. How long?"

"...Since summer," he admitted, in the voice of a man confessing to a crime worse than forgery.

"*Summer.*" Maret released him and stood, pacing, because sitting still was going to make her throw something. "Summer. You've been cutting plates and setting type with a fever in your blood since *summer*, and telling no one, because heaven forbid a Crane admit to being merely human."

"If I stop—"

"If you stop, you might live. If you don't stop, you'll die at that stone with twenty-eight names unfinished, and I'll bury you, and then I'll be very cross with you at your funeral, and everyone will think I'm grieving, when really I'm just annoyed."

That startled a laugh out of him—a cracked, rusty thing, but real. Maret counted it a victory and pressed on.

"So. The committee met last night, and I argued until they gave me what I wanted, which they mostly do because I'm loud and right, the two most persuasive qualities a person can have." She crouched to bring herself level with his seated form, the way you do with frightened horses and proud men. "Someone must cut the remaining plates. Someone young enough to work two days without sleep, steady enough to cut fine, and unknown—unknown, Aldous—because Verel knows your face and knows your shop and sooner or later will know your hand. It cannot be you."

"And you propose... whom, exactly? Yourself? Can you even read a proof, Maret?"

"I can read enough to know when a man's lying on paper, which is most of what reading is. But no. Not me." She drew a breath. Here was the part she'd rehearsed on the walk over, and rehearsing hadn't helped, because some things stay heavy however you carry them. "The child."

The silence that followed had weight and texture. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked. The pump handle outside had gone quiet—had gone quiet some minutes ago, Maret realized, and the cold in her stomach told her why, though she refused to turn and look at the door just yet.

"No," Aldous said.

"You haven't heard—"

"I don't need to hear. No. Absolutely not. Lio doesn't know the scope of it, doesn't know the committee exists, doesn't know anything except that I've been tired lately. I've kept—" his voice caught and steadied—"I have spent seven years keeping that child safe by making him invisible, and I will not now make him *visible*. Not to the resistance, not to Verel, not to anyone. Cutting a plate is not sorting type. It's a signature. It's a confession in copper. If it's ever traced, it traces to the hand that held the graver, and Lio is—"

"Is what?" Maret asked softly. "Say what the child is, Aldous. You raised him. Say it."

He didn't say it. He looked at the door instead, and Maret finally followed his gaze, and there, framed in it, stood Lio—with the water jug full and untouched in both hands and frost melting off his shoulders and an expression on his face that Maret had last seen on a woman listening at a sickroom door.

"How long," Aldous said hoarsely.

"Since you started whispering," Lio said. "Which was the moment she arrived, because you only whisper around Maret, and you only pour the good tea for Maret, and you've been pouring the good tea for six months." The child set the jug down with exaggerated care, the way Lio did everything, as if the world were made of wet paper. "Forty names. Twenty-eight left. Nine days."

"You shouldn't have—"

"I sort your type, Papa. I've sorted every letter of every lie you've printed since spring. Did you think I couldn't count?"

Maret watched father and child face each other across the warm room, and understood suddenly, completely, the shape of the thing between them: two people keeping the same secret for opposite reasons, each convinced the other was the fragile one. She'd seen marriages like it. She'd seen wars like it.

"Lio," she said, and the child's eyes came to her, quick and guarded and—she searched for the word and found it—*hungry*. Hungry the way a person is hungry who has been fed exactly enough to survive and never one crumb more. "Come here. Sit. Both of you, sit, because I'm only explaining this once and then I'm going home to actually do some washing, or my husband will divorce me for a woman with drier hands."

They sat. She laid it out: the forty, the twelve done, the tribunal, Verel and his patient terrible reading, the fever, the plates. She watched Lio listen the way some people pray—total, silent, frightening.

"Why me?" Lio said at last. "There are other engravers in Vellmere."

"There are two other engravers in Vellmere, and one drinks and one talks in his sleep, and both are known to Verel's clerks. And neither of them, if you'll forgive the vanity of an old washerwoman, is as good as you're going to be. I've seen your practice strokes in the wastepaper bin, love. You cut a swash tail on that *g* last month that made me wish I could write, just so I could be worthy of it."

"That was—you went through our rubbish?"

"Honey, I go through everyone's rubbish. It's how I know things." She leaned forward. "Here's the truth of it, and then you decide—both of you, separately, because I'll not be party to a father bullying a child nor a child shaming a father. Twenty-eight families. The Vasseurs and their four girls. Old Piotr who taught half the town its letters. The Ferrow widow and her sister who isn't even *in* the petition, they just share a roof. Forty names, and I've washed clothes for thirty-one of those households, and I can tell you what they smell like when they're frightened, because I've been smelling it for a month." Her voice did something she hadn't authorized; she let it. "Somebody cuts those plates. Somebody whose hands Verel has never imagined. Or somebody hangs. That's the whole arithmetic. There's no version where nobody pays."

Lio looked down at those hands—long-fingered, ink-stained at the cuticle, a printer's hands already though the world thought them an apprentice's—and Maret watched something settle in the child's face, something that had perhaps been waiting a long time for permission.

"I'll need to see the exemption format," Lio said. "All twelve finished ones. And the seal impression. And whatever Verel's office uses for registry numbers, because if the numbers don't follow their sequence he'll catch it inside a day—he counts things, I've watched him count things."

Aldous made a sound that was half protest and half surrender and entirely a father's heart breaking politely.

"And," Lio went on, in that flat, careful voice, "I'll need the good lamp moved to the back bench. And no one to speak to me while I'm working. And—" a pause, the smallest hitch, quickly ironed flat— "no one to touch the plate but me. Not even you, Papa. A plate carries the hand that cut it. If yours slips near mine, it becomes ours, and ours is easier to trace than mine alone."

Maret blinked. *Mine alone.* The child spoke of it like an heirloom. Like a name.

"Well," she said, standing, gathering her empty basket and her scattered composure. "That's settled, then, and more sensibly than the committee managed, which is humiliating but I'll survive it. I'll come back tomorrow with the reference copies sewn in the mattress again—my back sends its regrets. Cut well, Lio. Sleep when you can. And eat something, both of you; revolution runs on bread, whatever the poets say."

At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back at the two of them—the feverish father and the quiet child already turning toward the type cases with a graver's focus coming into those watchful eyes—and felt the peculiar ache of a woman who spends her life handling other people's secrets and occasionally has to hand one back changed.

"One more thing," she said. "The committee asked me to ask. Afterward—when the papers are placed, when the town is safe or it isn't—what do we do about the ledger?"

Aldous's face closed like a book. Lio's face did something stranger: opened, flickered, closed again, all in the space of a breath, like a door with wind behind it.

"Tomorrow," Aldous said.

"Mm," said Maret, who did not believe him, and walked out into the frost carrying twenty-eight names and the growing, unwelcome suspicion that the heaviest secret in the Crane household was not the forgery at all—and that the child who had just agreed to counterfeit an empire's mercy knew precisely what it was.
