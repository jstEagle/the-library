## 8. Verel's Ledger

Captain Edran Verel had been in the imperial service long enough to know that the truth of any town did not live in its church or its garrison or the speeches of its notables. It lived in paper. In ledgers, in tax rolls, in the grubby margin-notes of men who recorded what people actually did rather than what they claimed. Which was why, on his third evening in Vellmere, he sent his dinner back untouched and asked instead for the master ledger to be brought to his quarters at the customs house.

The clerk who carried it — a nervous boy named Pell who smelled of tallow and terror — set it down as though it might bite.

"Careful," Verel said. "It's a book, Pell, not a viper."

"Yes, Captain. Sorry, Captain." Pell hesitated at the door. "Only — the printer's daughter asked after it. The apprentice, I mean. Lio. Asked if it would be kept dry."

Verel looked up from unbuckling his sword belt. "Did he now."

"The Crane boy. He keeps the shop's accounts, sir. Very particular about damp. Paper being — well." Pell gestured vaguely, as though paper were an exotic animal whose habits he could only guess at.

"Paper is hygroscopic," Verel said. "Tell him — tell the boy — that it will be kept dry. There's a stove in this room and I intend to sit near it all winter like a cat."

Pell left, visibly relieved to have escaped without being shot, which Verel found mildly insulting. He was not, whatever the garrison believed, in the habit of shooting clerks. He had shot precisely one man in nine years of service, and the man had deserved it, and Verel still dreamed about him twice a month. But reputation was a uniform you couldn't take off at night, so he let them think what they liked.

He pulled a lamp close and opened the ledger.

* * *

The first hour was honest work. Debts, mostly. Vellmere was a river town, and river towns ran on credit the way mills ran on water: constantly, precariously, with everyone downstream of everyone else. The ferryman owed the cooper. The cooper owed the tanner. The tanner owed the church, which apparently forgave debts at Easter but charged interest the rest of the year, which Verel noted with the small private satisfaction of a man collecting ammunition against the clergy.

The hand changed about a third of the way through. The early entries were cramped, fussy, the writing of a man who pressed too hard — Aldous Crane, presumably, or his father before him. Then the script loosened into something confident, economical, almost beautiful. A different generation. And then, scattered through the later pages, entries in a third hand: lighter, quicker, the letters leaning forward like runners at a start line.

A child's hand grown up, Verel thought. An apprentice learning the trade one entry at a time. There was something touching in it — you could watch the letters steady themselves across five years of ink, could see the moment the boy stopped copying his master and started becoming himself.

He made himself stop finding things touching. That was how officers got killed, or worse, promoted.

The discrepancy appeared at midnight, and like most discrepancies, it arrived politely, dressed as nothing at all.

An entry for flour — two sacks, sold to the widow Hest, priced at the autumn rate. Except the autumn rate for flour in Vellmere, per the requisition schedules he'd spent two days memorizing, was four coppers lower than the figure in the book. Not fraud. Fraud was loud. This was four coppers, quietly, entered wrong, or entered right and paid wrong, month after month after month, across a dozen names.

Four coppers would buy a candle. Or a lie, if you were buying in bulk.

Verel sat back. Outside, the curfew drums sounded the second hour, and somewhere a dog answered them with more conviction than the entire Vellish militia had shown during the annexation. He turned the page. Another entry, same pattern. And another. And then, near the bottom of the sheet, a small mark he'd almost missed: a tiny printed fist, the pointing-hand glyph every print shop kept in its case, inked neatly beside three names.

Not beside the flour. Beside the names of the people who'd bought it.

He went through the ledger again with a pencil, marking each fist. Eleven of them. All commoners. All, when he cross-checked against the billeting roster in his head — he had a memory for lists that his instructors had called a gift and his mother had called suspicious — all families with sons of conscription age.

Well. That could mean anything. It could mean nothing. It could mean the printer marked his best customers with a fist because he liked them, the way Verel's father had chalked a star above the stalls of farmers who haggled honestly.

His father had also, Verel reminded himself, been ruined by exactly this kind of small quiet arithmetic.

He poured the last of the wine and kept reading until the candle drowned in its own wax.

* * *

The parish records lived in the vestry of Saint Aldhelm's, under the custody of Father Ambrose, who received Verel the next morning with the wariness of a man greeting a tax assessor and the hospitality of a man hoping to convert him.

"You'll want tea," Ambrose decided, before Verel could say otherwise. "You have the look of a man raised on army chicory, which is to say, no look at all, because chicory kills the soul first and the palate after."

"I'm on duty, Father."

"Duty improves with tea. Everything does. That's practically theology." Ambrose was already moving through the vestry, a big untidy bear of a man, disarranging things in search of cups. "You want the baptismal registers. Everyone wants the baptismal registers eventually. Births are the one thing nobody can forge convincingly, because God witnesses them and God has terrible handwriting."

Verel blinked. "That's remarkably cynical for a priest."

"That's remarkably accurate for a priest." Ambrose surfaced from a cupboard with two chipped cups and a teapot shaped like a cow. "My predecessor kept the records beautifully and trusted everyone absolutely, and in '39 somebody sold him a forged marriage certificate that cost a widow her house. Since then I read everything twice and believe half of it. Sit down, Captain. You're standing like the room owes you money."

Verel sat. The tea, he admitted privately, was excellent, and the cow pot was absurd enough to loosen something in his chest that had been clenched since the border.

He worked through the morning while Ambrose drifted in and out, cataloguing vestments, offering commentary. The registers were old, foxed, written in the terrible handwriting of six generations of curates, and Verel matched name after name against the fist-marked list. Widow Hest: baptized, confirmed, married, widowed, all present and correct. The ferryman Brannoc: present. The tanner Ossel's family: four children, one died young, all accounted for.

Nothing. For two hours, nothing — and Verel began to feel the specific disappointment of a man who has built a case out of candle prices and knows how thin it sounds spoken aloud.

Then he came to the Cranes.

The Crane family ran back through the registers like a thread through cloth. Printers, every one, back to a Josiah Crane baptized in the reign of the old king, and Verel felt the familiar tightening behind his ribs — dynasty, inheritance, a name passed hand to hand like type itself. His own family name had lasted one generation. It hadn't even lasted that.

Tamuel Crane, son of Aldous. Baptized such-and-such year. Dead at nineteen — fever, per the burial register, seven years past.

And below Tamuel's entry, in the same year's baptisms:

*Lior Crane, daughter of Aldous Crane and Sefa Crane.*

Born the same season. Twins, then, or near it. A sister.

Verel read the line four times. A daughter. So Aldous Crane had a living daughter, born and baptized in this parish, inked in God's terrible handwriting, and yet the shop employed an apprentice named Lio Crane — the boy Pell described, the boy who worried about damp — and no daughter appeared anywhere in the household records Verel had reviewed upon taking command of the town.

Households misfiled daughters constantly. Daughters married, moved, died in childbirth, became someone else's surname. It meant nothing.

Except that Lior and Lio were the same mouth-shape. Except that Tamuel dead at nineteen would leave an apprenticeship — a guild placement, a livelihood — hanging empty. Except that the third hand in the ledger, the light quick forward-leaning hand, belonged to an apprentice who had appeared in the shop's accounts seven years ago, fully formed, with no earlier entries, no childhood scrawl, no clumsy first attempts.

Apprentices started clumsy. Verel had watched boys learn to write; it took years. This hand had arrived fluent.

He closed the register carefully, as if it were sleeping.

"More tea?" Ambrose offered.

"No. Thank you." Verel stood. "Father, do you know the printer's family?"

"Aldous? Sad story. Wife left him years ago — went south, some say. And lost his boy to fever, poor devil, the same summer. The daughter—" Ambrose paused, teapot in hand, and something careful crossed his broad face. "The daughter lives with an aunt downriver, I understood. Grief does strange things to families. Splits them like timber."

"Of course," Verel said.

Ambrose looked at him a moment longer than the conversation required. "You'll bring the cup back when you're done with it. It was my mother's."

* * *

He should have written it in his report that afternoon. *Discrepancies in civic ledger consistent with systematic falsification; recommend audit of Crane printing concern.* Fourteen words, and by week's end there would be inspectors in the shop, and the fourteen words would become forty files, and files became tribunals, and tribunals became the firing range outside the walls where the snow stayed red until spring.

He knew this because he had seen it. Because once — years ago, in another town, wearing a junior officer's uniform and believing absolutely in the cleanliness of procedure — he had filed a report just like it, accurate in every particular, and had learned afterward what the report fed. What grew from it. Who was taken away in it.

And he had learned, in the same season, what a lie in a ledger could do, because there had been one in his family's ledger too. One line, one false entry, his sister's name struck through and re-entered under another man's household — a clerk's small correction, a debt settled in flesh — and by the time anyone thought to check, she was gone, and the record said she had never been his sister at all, and the paper was right, and the paper was everything, and the paper had eaten her.

So he did not write the report.

Instead he sat in his quarters by the stove with the master ledger open on his knees, and he told himself he was being methodical. Careful. A good investigator confirmed before he reported; that was procedure; that was diligence. He copied the eleven fist-marked names onto a slip of paper. He copied the flour figures. He wrote *Lior Crane, daughter* on a separate slip, and looked at it for a long time, and then — he would not be able to explain this later, and never tried to — he folded that slip in half, and in half again, and put it inside his glove, which was not filing and not reporting and not anything a career officer did with evidence.

Outside, the drums sounded the third hour. The stove ticked. Somewhere across the frozen town, in a shop that smelled of ink and lamp oil, a boy who might be a girl was probably awake, worrying about damp.

"Whoever you are," Verel said quietly to the ledger, "you're good. The hand is beautiful. Whoever taught you taught you well."

The ledger said nothing, which was the nature of ledgers, and the trouble with them, and the reason he could never put them down.

In the morning, he decided, he would go back to the parish and pull the Crane baptismal page itself — hold it, verify the ink, check the curate's marginalia for corrections. Procedure. Diligence. Confirmation before report.

In the morning, he told himself, he would decide what kind of man he was going to be about it.

The slip of paper stayed in his glove all night, warm as a coal.
