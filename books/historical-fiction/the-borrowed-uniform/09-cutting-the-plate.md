## 9. Cutting the Plate

The coal scuttle told the truth before anyone else in the house would.

Lior tipped it over the stove at dusk and watched perhaps a dozen lumps rattle out, small and mean as knucklebones. Enough for one night of heat, if she was stingy about it. Not enough for two days of presswork with a fevered man in the next room who kept insisting, between shivers, that he was perfectly well and merely resting his eyes.

"Resting his eyes," she muttered, banking the fire low. "In December. Under three blankets."

From behind the curtain that divided the workshop from the sleeping alcove came a sound that was either agreement or delirium. With her father it had become difficult to tell the difference.

She had hung the curtain herself, years ago, when Tam was alive — good linen, dyed indigo because Tam had insisted the shop needed color, "something to look at besides your own gloomy face, Aldous." The dye had faded unevenly so that now, by lamplight, it looked like a strip of evening sky caught mid-collapse. Lior had spent seven years not looking directly at it. Tonight she looked, because tonight everything in the shop seemed to be asking her what it had witnessed, and she had no honest answers for any of it.

On the workbench before her lay the copper plate, blank and gleaming, no bigger than a hymnal. Beside it: the burin, the scraper, the burnisher, the lamp turned up high enough to hurt her eyes if she looked at it straight. Maret had left an hour ago with the sample impression folded into her bodice like a knife carried flat against the ribs. *Forty families,* she'd said at the door, not dramatically, which somehow made it worse. *Tribunal sits on Thursday. If the papers aren't in their hands by Wednesday night, forty names get read out in the square, and none of them will be read kindly.*

Wednesday. Two days. One plate to cut, one press to run, one father to keep breathing, and perhaps eleven lumps of coal to do all of it.

Lior sat down, flexed her fingers the way Aldous had taught her — spread, curl, shake loose, as if shaking water off — and picked up the burin.

* * *

Cutting a forgery, it turned out, was nothing like cutting a wedding invitation.

She had learned engraving the way she'd learned everything else in that shop: sideways, quietly, by doing it wrong until doing it right became the only interesting thing left. Wedding invitations were her specialty, though she could never say so aloud. Flourishes, doves, interlocking initials — work that wanted confidence and forgave nothing. A wobble in a dove's wing and some bride's mother would squint at it forever, and brides' mothers squinted professionally.

But a wedding invitation only had to be beautiful. This had to be *true*. Or rather, it had to be false in exactly the way the imperial chancery's documents were true — same weight of line, same slightly cramped official hand, same little quirks of the chancery engraver whose work Lior had studied for three nights now under this same lamp: the way he crossed his t's with a slight downward flick, as if impatient; the way his serifs thickened on the left foot, a habit no writing master would have permitted but which ten thousand stamped documents had made into law.

Imitation, Aldous always said, is not flattery. It is autopsy. You must know a thing down to its bones before you can wear its skin.

Her father had taught her that about type. He had never imagined she'd apply it to treason.

She cut for an hour before she let herself think, and then, because thinking arrived anyway, she thought about how *good* she was getting.

That was the horror of it. Not the danger — she'd made her peace with danger somewhere around the second night, or told herself she had. The horror was the pleasure. Her burin moved through the copper like a skate over black ice, and every stroke came away clean, and there was a moment — she hated the moment, she would remember hating it later — when she finished the imperial seal's outer ring and held the plate to the light and felt *pride*, bright and shameless as a struck bell.

*I am good at this,* she thought. *I am good at lying in metal.*

It was not so different from the other lie, really. Seven years of walking like Tam, shrugging like Tam, answering to a name that wasn't hers with a casualness she'd practiced in front of the polished press bed until it stopped costing anything visible. She had gotten good at that too. Good enough that Maret had known her two years as Lio Crane and never once looked twice. Good enough that Captain Verel — and here her burin paused above the copper — had stood three feet from her yesterday discussing typefaces and seen nothing but a clever apprentice boy.

And Verel had *liked* the apprentice boy. That was the part that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. He had looked at her work with real respect, asked real questions, listened to her answers as if answers from a print-shop boy were worth having. For ten minutes she had been, in however small a way, *seen* — and the person seeing her had been looking at someone who did not exist.

If she cut this plate well, forty families lived. If she cut it badly, they died. And underneath both of those truths ran a third one, quieter and colder: that she was becoming, stroke by stroke, a person whose finest work was deception.

"You're muttering," said her father's voice through the curtain, cracked and thin. "Apprentices who mutter at their plates grow up to mutter at their customers."

"You're supposed to be asleep."

"I am asleep. I'm doing it with my eyes open. It's an advanced technique." A cough followed, long and wet, and Lior was on her feet and through the curtain before the last of it died.

Aldous lay gray against the pillows, three blankets and his coat on top, his forehead slick. She pressed the back of her hand to it and his skin burned like a press bed after a long run.

"You're feverish."

"I'm warm-blooded. It's a family trait."

"Papa."

"Aldous," he corrected, eyes half-closing. "In the shop I'm Aldous. In the shop you've never once called me Papa, and I confess I've missed it these seven years, so say it again and then bring me something to drink that isn't well water, because well water tastes like the well."

There was no cider left. There was barely anything left; Maret took most of the food money now, and rightly, since forty families ate more than two Cranes. Lior heated water, stirred in the last spoonful of honey, and held the cup while he drank, his hands trembling against hers — hands that had set type for thirty years and could no longer hold a cup steady.

"The plate," he said, when the cup was empty. "How far?"

"Seal's done. Names go slowly. I want them perfect."

"Mm." His eyes found hers, and even fever couldn't quite dim the look in them — that terrible, complicated pride she'd seen when she confronted him, pride braided through with guilt like wire through wax. "Perfect is right. Don't rush it for me. I've set type through worse fevers than this."

"You set type through a fever in 1809 and misprinted an entire parish newsletter, and Mrs. Halloway's banns came out reading that she was marrying a *goat*."

"And Vellmere laughed for a month," Aldous said, with the ghost of his old smile. "Best day the shop ever had. People bought copies just to burn them." The smile faded. He looked at the ceiling. "Lior. If I don't—"

"Don't."

"If I don't see Thursday—"

"You'll see Thursday. You'll see Friday too, complaining about the state of the gutters." She tucked the blanket higher, briskly, because if she wasn't brisk her hands would do something embarrassing. "Sleep. Real sleep. Eyes closed. I have work."

He slept. Or did the advanced technique. Either way, the curtain fell still, and Lior went back to the lamp.

* * *

The names were the hardest part, and not because they were difficult to cut.

Maret had brought the list written in her laundry-book cipher — numbers and marks that meant nothing unless you knew that the Halloways were a washboard symbol and the Perrins a mangle. Forty names. Lior copied each onto scrap paper first, in the chancery hand, practicing the flick of the t's, the heavy left serifs, until her wrist ached and the practice sheets littered the bench like snow.

And that was when the trouble started. Because the names weren't strangers.

Old Widow Sarel, who paid for her printing in eggs and once gave Lio a whole basket because "you've got honest hands, boy." The Perrin brothers, who'd bought their exemption paper already — the earlier, cruder run — and would hang if the new tribunal spotted the flaws Lior could now see plainly: the seal ring a hair too round, the ink too eager. The Ferro family, all six of them, whose youngest girl used to sit on the step outside the shop eating plums and spitting the pits into the gutter with tremendous accuracy.

Lior cut each name into copper knowing that the depth of her lines was the distance between life and the firing squad, and her hands did not shake, and that frightened her more than shaking would have. A craftsman's hands should tremble at this. Hers didn't. They simply worked, cool and certain, and she watched them work as if they belonged to someone else — someone competent and ruthless, someone who could wear any uniform, answer to any name, forge any document — and she understood, sitting in the lamplight with copper dust on her fingers, that this stranger was not someone else at all.

This stranger was what seven years of silences had built, the way water builds a channel. She hadn't chosen it, exactly. She'd just never once, in seven years, said no to it.

The lamp guttered. She trimmed the wick and checked the coal stove and found it breathing its last, one orange eye closing. Eleven lumps had become four, and four had become embers, and outside the wind had come around to the north and was finding every gap in the old window frames with a whistler's precision.

Cold was the enemy of the plate. Cold made the copper brittle at the edges, stiffened her fingers, thinned the wax ground. She fed the stove two of the remaining lumps — stealing, in effect, from tomorrow's presswork — and wrapped her free hand around a mug of hot water just to have something warm to return to between strokes.

Somewhere past midnight, her father began to talk in his sleep.

Not words, mostly. Fragments. *Set it tighter... the forme's loose...* and then, once, clearly, a name that was not Lio and not Lior but *Tam*, said in the mild instructive tone he'd used for twenty years, *Tam, mind your quoins,* and Lior sat very still with the burin in her hand and let the name pass through her like weather.

She had worn that name for seven years. She had gotten so good at wearing it that even her father's sleeping mind reached for it first. And she found — this was the strange thing, the thing she turned over and over like a piece of type examined by lamplight — that hearing it didn't wound the way it used to. It just sounded like a coat left on a hook. Still shaped like somebody. No longer anybody.

*Who am I, then?* she asked the plate. The plate, being copper, declined to answer. But the seal stared up at her, imperial and intricate, forged so finely that Verel himself — Verel, who read ledgers for sport and hunted discrepancies like a man hunting wolves — would need a lens and an hour and a grudge to find the flaw she'd built into it. Because she had built one in. A single hairline break in the inner scrollwork, invisible without magnification, placed where only someone who knew exactly how the original was cut would ever think to look.

Aldous didn't know about it. Maret didn't know about it. It was hers alone: a signature inside the forgery, a truth hidden inside the lie, put there for no reason she could defend except that some part of her refused, absolutely refused, to make anything — even a crime — without leaving herself in it.

*You're good at deception,* she told herself, resuming the cut. *Fine. Be good at it. But be yourself somewhere inside it, or the forty names won't be the only things lost.*

* * *

Dawn came gray and coal-less. She'd used the last lump at three, feeding the stove like a nurse feeding medicine, and now the workshop air had gone sharp enough to bite. Her breath fogged over the plate. She warmed her hands at the dying embers, flexed the cramp out of her neck, and surveyed the work.

Done. Nearly done. Every name cut, the seal complete, the border rules laid in with a steadiness that would have won a prize at any guild exhibition she could never enter. She cleaned the plate with spirits, breathed on it, wiped it again, and held it up to the window where the gray light could rake across the surface.

It was beautiful. That was the word, and she let herself have it, because denying it would have been one more lie and she was, she decided, temporarily out of stock.

Then she inked it, laid the dampened proof paper, and pulled the first impression on the hand press — gently, gently, the pressure of a confession rather than a printing.

The sheet came away and she held it at arm's length and her chest did something complicated.

It was perfect. It was an imperial exemption certificate, flawless, official, bearing the names of forty people Vellmere had decided to forget, and it would save their lives, and it was a lie from margin to margin, and she had made it with love. That was the sentence she couldn't get around, standing in the cold with the proof drying in her hands: *with love.* The same love she'd put into wedding invitations and funeral broadsides and the town's one almanac. The work didn't know the difference. Her hands didn't know the difference. Only she did, and the knowing sat in her like a swallowed hook.

Behind the curtain, her father's breathing had changed.

She heard it the way printers hear a press fall out of register — not loudly, but wrongly. She was through the curtain in three steps.

Aldous had thrown off the blankets. His eyes were open, fixed on the ceiling beams, and he was shivering so hard the cot frame knocked the wall, and when she touched him he looked at her without a flicker of recognition and said, in a voice like paper tearing:

"It has to be tonight. They'll come for the press. They always come for the press."

"Papa. Papa, it's Lio. Lie back."

"They'll come for the press," he repeated, and then, softer, with terrible clarity: "And the ledger. Whatever happens, the ledger doesn't burn. Your grandfather's book. It doesn't burn, do you hear me—"

The words dropped into Lior like stones into a well — she thought of Maret's flat voice, *the whole book goes into the river after the run, or we're all sold out forever* — and she pushed the collision away for later, because later was a luxury and the present was a fever. She got the blankets back on him, got hot water into him, got the last of the willow bark tea steeping, and stood a moment with her hand on his burning forehead while outside the window the sky went from gray to the dirty white of a Vellmere morning.

Two days to the tribunal. One finished plate. A sick pressman, an empty coal scuttle, and a father who talked in his sleep about things he shouldn't know were coming.

Lior went back to the bench, hung the proof sheet to dry among the wedding invitations where it hid in plain sight, and was reaching for the ink slab to begin setting the lockup when boots sounded in the street outside — not one pair, not two, but the measured, unhurried cadence of soldiers, growing louder, and stopping.

Directly outside the door of Crane & Son, Printers.
