## 10. The Search

There were three knocks, and then there were no knocks at all — the door simply opened, because doors in Vellmere had stopped belonging to anyone the day the drums came.

Lior had perhaps four seconds. Four seconds was nothing; four seconds was also, she would learn that morning, a very long time indeed.

She looked at the forme on the stone bench: the half-set lockup of wedding invitations for the Halloway girl, all cream paper and flourished initials, waiting only for its quoins to be tightened. And beside it, wrapped in the oilcloth where she'd left it at dawn, the forgery plate — forty names' worth of salvation, thin as a hymn book and twice as damning.

Four seconds. She did not think. Thinking was a luxury of people whose fathers were not feverish upstairs, whose coal scuttles were not empty, whose towns were not two days from tribunal. Her hands did what her hands knew how to do, which was what they always did when the truth needed somewhere to hide: they put it inside something beautiful.

The plate slid into the invitation forme, face-down beneath the standing type, snug as a coin under a mattress. She dropped the chase's top bar over it, tightened the quoins with two hard turns of the key, and was blowing ink dust off her fingers when the first soldier filled the doorway like weather coming indoors.

* * *

There were six of them, and then Captain Verel, who entered last and removed his gloves, which Lior had learned was worse than if he'd drawn a sword. A man who took off his gloves intended to stay.

"Crane & Son," Verel said, reading the sign above the press with the mild interest of a man reading a menu. "Which is it? Crane, or son?"

"Both," said Lior. Her voice sat where it always sat, low and unremarkable, a boy's voice worn smooth as a shop counter by seven years of use. "Father and apprentice."

"Your father is ill, I understand." He said it pleasantly, and the pleasantness had teeth in it somewhere, the way a fisherman's smile has hooks. "Feverish men talk. Feverish men have been known to talk *a great deal*."

Lior kept her eyes on the middle button of the nearest soldier's coat. "Printers talk about ink, sir."

"Printers talk about everything. It's why empires keep an eye on them." Verel drifted along the wall of type cases, trailing one finger an inch above the little wooden drawers, never quite touching. He had done this before, on his earlier visit — the visit when he'd asked about Bodoni's serifs and whether Aldous cut his own ornaments, and Lior had spent the whole conversation wanting, horribly, to answer him honestly. She had decided since then that Captain Verel was the most dangerous kind of man: not cruel, but *curious*, and curious men found things out without meaning to.

"Sergeant Doss," he said, without turning, "begin with the cellar. Corporal, the loft. I want every drawer opened, every forme broken down, and any press that isn't printing prayer books or price lists reported to me personally."

"Yes, sir."

"And gently." He glanced back at them, almost apologetic. "Type is fragile. I'm told."

* * *

What followed was two hours of organized vandalism, and Lior stood in the middle of it learning exactly how much of herself lived in this room.

She had thought, before, that she knew. She'd thought the shop was her father, and the trade, and Tam's name she wore like borrowed boots. But watching a corporal named Pell — young, freckled, apologetic in the way of boys made to do things their mothers wouldn't recognize — pull open the case of twelve-point Garamond and run his thumb through the little steel forest, she felt something go hot behind her ribs that was not fear. It was closer to watching someone handle a sleeping child roughly.

"Careful," she heard herself say. "That's the display roman. It's older than the empire."

Pell looked up, startled. Verel looked up too.

"Older than the empire," he repeated. "You're certain?"

"My grandfather bought it secondhand from a house in Corvane that was closing." True. All of it true, which was the best kind of lie-adjacent material there was. "Corvane houses were closing before there was an empire to close them."

Something moved at the corner of Verel's mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment, maybe — one professional recognizing another's inventory. He turned away, and Lior breathed again through her nose, slow, counting the breaths the way Maret had taught her for the nights the walls felt close.

They worked through the shop with terrible thoroughness. They found the smuggler's false bottom in the paper cabinet (empty three weeks now, thank whatever governed such things). They found Aldous's flask of pearlash and argued briefly about whether pearlash was seditious. They found, in the loft, a box of Tam's old clothes that should have been burned years ago, and Lior watched a sergeant hold up a boy's shirt far too small for any living person and felt the floor tilt very slightly under her feet before reason arrived: nobody knew Tam was dead but family, and family wasn't talking.

Upstairs, faintly, her father coughed. Then — worse — went quiet, which meant sleep, which meant talking.

Verel tilted his head toward the ceiling. "Your father sleeps loudly?"

"He talks in his sleep," Lior said. "Mostly about prices. Paper's gone up forty percent since the requisition." Let him chew on commerce. Commerce was harmless. Commerce was the thickest blanket you could throw over a sleeping man.

"Mm," said Verel.

* * *

It was going well. That was the horror of it — it was actually going well. The cellar produced nothing but damp and mouse droppings. The loft produced grief and old shirts. Sergeant Doss had broken down three formes and found wedding invitations, funeral bills, an announcement for the wool fair, and a poem somebody had paid good coin to print for a woman named Bess, which the soldiers read aloud and enjoyed more than they should have.

And then Corporal Pell, moving down the stone bench with his orders and his careful hands, stopped in front of the Halloway forme.

Lior's heart performed a maneuver she felt in her teeth.

"That's set work," she said. "Paid for. Due Thursday."

"I've orders to break down every forme."

"It's *wedding invitations*."

"So was that lot in the loft." Pell reached for the quoins.

"There are two hundred guests," Lior said, hearing her own voice go crisp with a desperation she prayed read as tradesman's irritation. "Miss Halloway has waited three years for this wedding because of the war. If you break that lockup I can reset it, yes, but not before Thursday, and not well, and her father will come here and shout at me, which is fine, I deserve it, but he'll also write to the garrison commandant, who is his cousin."

That last part was invented. The Halloways knew no one. But Pell was nineteen and terrified of officers, and the sentence worked on him like cold water.

He hesitated. His hand hovered over the chase.

And Verel crossed the room.

Lior had prepared herself for many versions of this morning. She had not prepared for the version where the captain leaned over the forme himself, unhurried, and studied it the way he'd studied the type cases — with that literate, hungry attention that made her want to both impress him and never be perceived by him again.

"Halloway–Vetch," he read aloud, tilting his head. "With a fleuron between the names. That's a nice touch. Most village printers would use a comma."

"A comma's what you put between things that aren't happy about each other," Lior said.

This time the smile did arrive, brief and real, and she hated how much of her noticed it. "May I?" He didn't wait. His fingers found the edge of the chase, and —

— and Lior discovered that a human being can hear their own pulse.

Because here was the thing she could do nothing about, the flaw in the beautiful hiding place: the plate was *in* the lockup. Face-down under the standing type, wrapped in oilcloth, but present. Heavy. If Verel lifted the forme — printers lifted formes constantly, checked them from below against the light, held them up to see the impression reversed and gleaming — the weight would be wrong. A wedding-invitation forme weighed like paper and lead. This one weighed like paper, lead, and a secret the size of forty lives. In his hands, it would feel like holding a book with a brick inside it.

Verel was not a printer. But Verel was a man who noticed weights. She had watched him notice everything else for a week.

He lifted the chase an inch off the stone.

Set it down.

Lifted it again, and turned it slightly, the way a man turns a page he means to read properly.

"The spacing's generous," he said. "Airy. Confident setting."

"Thank you," said Lior, in the voice of someone discussing kerning while drowning politely.

He turned the forme once more in his hands — and Lior saw it happen, saw the exact moment the arithmetic reached him. A small pause. His brow did something microscopic. His grip shifted, testing the balance of the thing, and Lior understood with total clarity that in approximately two seconds he was going to look at the underside, or weigh it against his palm and frown, or simply ask, in that pleasant hooked voice, *what else is locked into this forme, apprentice?*

The coal scuttle was empty. Her father was asleep and talking. Two days to the tribunal. And the entire enterprise, forty families, seven years of silence, one stolen apprenticeship — all of it balanced on a captain's wrists.

Then, from the stairwell, Sergeant Doss called down: "Captain! Upstairs — the sick man's calling for someone. Says a name."

Verel set the forme down.

Not quickly. Not guiltily. He set it down the way a man sets down a book he intends to finish later, aligned neatly on the bench, and straightened, and pulled his gloves out of his belt.

"Which name?" he asked the ceiling.

"He keeps saying 'the boy.' Wants 'the boy.'"

"Ah." Verel looked at Lior, and for one suspended moment she was certain — certain — that he saw all the way through her, through the binding and the flat chest and the seven careful years, down to the name in the ledger and the daughter in the parish book and whatever else he carried around behind those literate gray eyes. The look lasted long enough for her to compose a eulogy.

"You heard him," Verel said. "Go up. Sick men heal faster with someone to shout at." He was already turning toward the door, gesturing his men ahead of him with two fingers. "We're finished here anyway. Nothing but weddings and wool fairs." At the threshold he paused, hand on the frame, and glanced back at the bench — at the invitation forme sitting innocent in the lamplight.

"Congratulations to Miss Halloway," he said. "I hope the fleuron prints clean."

Then the boots receded down the street, unhurried as ever, and the door stood open on the gray morning, and Lior discovered she had been holding the edge of the stone bench hard enough to leave marks in her palms.

* * *

She locked the door. She put the bar across it, which she had not done since the requisition began, because bars invited questions. Then she stood with her forehead against the cool wood and let her knees inform her, at length, what they had been through.

Upstairs, her father was still calling. "*The boy.* Boy! Where's the boy got to—"

"Coming, Father," she called, and her voice cracked in the middle of it, and she stood there another moment letting the crack exist, alone, in the one room in Vellmere where no one could see her be anything.

Then she went to the bench, and lifted the forme, and felt the wrongness of its weight in her own hands — the plate beneath the type, patient as a seed — and understood that they had been given a morning's grace and nothing more.

Because Verel had set the forme down *aligned*. Neatly squared to the bench's edge. A man planning to forget a thing does not square it up. A man planning to come back does.

Two days to the tribunal. One plate hidden inside a wedding. And a captain who knew, now — she was sure of it, sure in her marrow — that something in this shop weighed more than it should.

She climbed the stairs to her father, already composing in her head the message she'd carry to Maret after dark: *he'll return, and next time he'll bring the light.*
