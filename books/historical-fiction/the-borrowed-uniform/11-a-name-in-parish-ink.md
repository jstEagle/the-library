## 11. A Name in Parish Ink

Captain Elias Verel had been in the army long enough to know that the most dangerous documents were never the loud ones. Sedition shouted from rooftops got itself arrested within the week. It was the quiet papers that killed you—the ledger entry that didn't balance, the name that appeared where it had no business appearing, the small arithmetic of a lie that some careful person had hoped nobody would check.

He checked. That was rather the whole trouble with him, his superiors had decided years ago. They had promoted him out of the way for it and then, when the Vellish situation curdled, promoted him back in again, on the theory that a man who could not stop checking was exactly what a town like Vellmere required.

So on the fourth morning after the requisition, Verel sat in the commandeered records office with the master ledger open before him, a pot of tea gone cold at his elbow, and forty-one parish registers stacked around him like the ramparts of a small paper fortress.

The ledger itself was beautiful. He resented it for that. Someone—Aldous Crane, presumably—had kept it in a hand so even and unshowy that reading it felt like being spoken to by a reasonable person. Every debt recorded. Every payment. Every birth entered into the town's book with the same care as every coin. Verel had audited garrison accounts in three provinces and had never once seen a ledger kept this well, and he found himself distrusting it precisely because of that.

*No one is this honest,* he thought. *Honesty this thorough is a kind of architecture. Somebody built it.*

He turned back to the discrepancies.

There were eleven of them. Not errors—errors scattered themselves randomly, the way water stains spread. These clustered. Eleven families whose debts, over the past seven years, had been quietly reduced. Never erased; never forgiven in any way that would show in a column. Just... trimmed. A debt of sixty crowns becoming fifty-four, then forty-eight, then thirty, in increments so gentle that only a man comparing year against year against year would ever notice the slope of it.

And each of those eleven families had something else in common: a son or daughter who had reached conscription age and was no longer, according to any record Verel could find, resident in Vellmere. Gone to an uncle in Harrow Fen. Gone to work the river barges. Gone, in one memorable case, "to pursue ecclesiastical studies," which Verel strongly suspected was a fiction, since the boy in question had once thrown a turnip at Verel's own horse during the last census tour and shown no discernible piety whatsoever.

Eleven families, gently relieved of debt, their children gently removed from the rolls.

Someone in Vellmere was buying sons out of the empire's grasp, and doing it through the pages of this immaculate book.

Verel drank the cold tea, made a face, and pulled the parish register of births toward him.

* * *

The priest who kept the registers was a small, damp man named Father Osk, who smelled of incense and boiled cabbage and who had greeted the imperial occupation with the serene indifference of a man who had outlived four governments and expected to outlive a fifth. He had surrendered the registers without protest two days after the requisition, saying only, "Mind the third volume, the binding's held together with prayer since the flood," and had returned to whatever priests did all day in towns under martial law. Verel suspected it involved the cabbage.

Now Verel worked through volume three with a pencil, cross-checking names. The Crane family first, because Aldous Crane was the pivot on which everything in this town seemed to turn: the printer, the ledger-keeper, the man who had handed over his most precious possession with an ease that still bothered Verel like a pebble in a boot.

Crane, Tam. Born the ninth of Frostmonth, year such-and-such. Mother: Sera Crane. Father: Aldous Crane. Verel copied the entry neatly into his working notes.

Then he turned the page, because he was a man who turned pages, and there it was.

**Crane, Lior. Daughter. Born the twenty-second of Thawmonth, seven years after her brother.**

Verel sat quite still for a moment.

He knew the name. Of course he knew the name. The apprentice—Lio Crane, the son, quiet as spilled flour, hands stained to the wrist with ink, who had stood in the print shop doorway during the requisition with the particular stillness of a person counting exits. Verel had noticed him. One noticed people who watched that carefully.

But the register said *daughter.* Said it plainly, in faded iron-gall ink, witnessed by a midwife's mark and Father Osk's own signature, dated seven-and-twenty years ago.

Verel did what he always did with an anomaly: he looked for the boring explanation first. Clerical error. A twin who died. A cousin raised as kin. Towns did these things; he had seen a household in Corvane list a goat among its dependents for eleven consecutive years because nobody wanted to reopen the argument about it.

Except.

Except that if Lior Crane had been born a daughter, then someone had spent years making sure the town's books said otherwise. And the town's books—the ledgers, the apprenticeship rolls, the militia lists—were kept, maintained, and corrected in one hand. The same even, reasonable, architectural hand that had written *Lior Crane, apprentice* into the master ledger while the parish ink said *daughter* seven feet away in the same locked room.

Verel set down his pencil.

The fire ticked in the grate. Somewhere outside, a sentry called the half-hour, and a dog answered it with more conviction than the sentry deserved.

Here was the thing Verel understood about himself, the thing his sister would have told him if his sister had existed to tell it: he was not built for this part. He was built for the finding. Give him a discrepancy and a stack of registers and he was happy as a ferret in a grain sack. But the finding always ended at a door, and behind the door was a person, and the person was never just a discrepancy.

He looked at the page again. *Lior Crane, daughter.*

An apprentice who did not exist. A son who was not a son. Which meant the boy in the print shop doorway—the one with the careful eyes and the ink-stained fingers and the silence that had a shape to it, the silence of somebody holding very still inside a borrowed coat—

Which meant that everything Verel thought he knew about the forgery investigation had just acquired a second, deeper floor beneath it, and he was standing on the trapdoor between them.

* * *

He went walking. It was a habit from garrison life; a mind that wouldn't stop turning could sometimes be walked quiet, like a restive horse. Vellmere in the rain was gray stone and brown river and the smell of coal smoke, and the soldiers he passed saluted him with the flat efficiency of men who had decided the occupation would be boring and were determined to be right.

He found himself, without entirely meaning to, at the corner across from the print shop.

This was, he told himself, reconnaissance. Perfectly legitimate. He was observing the establishment under suspicion of seditious printing, which was his duty, and duty was a comfortable coat that he wore well.

Through the diamond panes of the workshop window, lamplit, he could see the boy at the composing stick. Working late. Of course working late; the shop's orders had tripled since the requisition—wedding invitations, funeral notices, broadsheets, all the ordinary traffic of a town trying to pretend nothing was happening—and the old man had been ill, coughing in a way that Verel had heard through the open door on his last visit and had pretended not to hear, because pretending was apparently contagious now.

Lio Crane set type the way Verel imagined other people prayed. There was a rhythm to it, an absorption, the small brass pieces going into the stick with little clicks like beads on a string. Once, the boy paused, lifted his head as though hearing something no one else could, and for a heartbeat his face was open—unguarded, young, tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep—and Verel, watching from the wet street, felt something in his chest turn over with the specific dread of a man who has just realized the discrepancy has a face.

*You're not a clerk's error,* he thought. *You're a person. That's the whole problem with you.*

He had been here before. Not here, exactly—but here. Twelve years ago, in his own home province, another officer had matched another name to another register, and what followed had eaten his family whole. His father's lands, his mother's standing, and finally his sister—struck from the family record so thoroughly that when she died, two provinces away, no official document anywhere acknowledged she had ever been born. All because one careful man with one pencil had decided a discrepancy was a crime.

Verel had enlisted partly to get away from that memory and partly, he suspected, because he had never stopped arguing with it.

*A name in the wrong column isn't a treason,* he thought, watching the lamp in the print shop window. *It's just a name. The treason, if there is one, is elsewhere—in the debts, in the missing sons. Follow that. Leave the girl—leave whoever—to her own account.*

But duty, that comfortable coat, had begun to chafe at the shoulders. Because the debts and the missing sons pointed at Aldous Crane, and Aldous Crane was dying by inches in a room above the shop, and the only person keeping the press running—the only person who could be running those eleven gentle reductions, or covering for whoever was—was the apprentice who did not exist.

Arrest the boy and the truth came out anyway, eventually, in the tribunal's cold arithmetic. Protect the boy and the forgery ring—if ring it was—kept printing, and forty names' worth of imperial authority dissolved one trimmed debt at a time.

Mercy or duty. The war began quietly, the way they always did. No drums. Just a man standing in the rain, getting soaked, unable to make his feet carry him either toward the shop door or away from it.

* * *

In the end he did neither. He went back to the records office, dried off, and sat before his notes until midnight.

There was a protocol for this. Report the anomaly up the chain; let the tribunal sort it; let it become someone else's door to knock on. He took out a fresh report sheet. Dipped his pen. Wrote the date, the reference numbers, *Re: Crane, L., apprentice, age approximately nineteen—*

And stopped.

Because to write the next line was to finish it. *Sex registered female at birth; identity presumed assumed.* Six words, and the machinery started—questions, searches, a tribunal hearing, the tribunal already scheduled, its appetite sharpened by the missing conscripts. Six words, and the boy in the lamplit window became a case file.

Verel looked at the six words for a long time.

He thought about his sister, whose name existed now only in his own head, because every official record of her had been fed into exactly this machine. He thought about how the machine had never once been satisfied—it had taken the name, ruined the family, ruined the province, and learned nothing, because the machine did not learn; it only processed. He thought about Father Osk's damp voice: *mind the third volume, the binding's held together with prayer.*

He thought, most inconveniently, about the way the apprentice had looked at the wedding invitation proofs during the requisition—not at Verel, at the type—with an expression of pure professional heartbreak, as though the kerning were a moral failing, and how Verel had almost laughed, alone in an enemy's shop, and how he had not laughed at anything since.

The pen hovered.

Then Captain Elias Verel did the thing that would have astonished his superiors, had they ever been permitted to know of it, and which he himself would spend many sleepless nights refusing to call mercy, because mercy implied a decision, and he was not ready to decide.

He folded the report sheet in quarters, blank but for the date, and slid it into the bottom of his dispatch case, beneath the requisition orders, beneath everything.

He left the parish register open on the desk, though. Open to the page. Where anyone might see it.

That was the compromise his conscience brokered, and both sides of the war in him accepted it with bad grace: he would not spring the trap himself. But he would leave it set. If the tribunal's clerks found the page, they found it. If the boy came to the records office—as the boy might, as boys investigating missing files often did—and saw the register lying open, then perhaps the boy would understand that the game was known, and choose, freely, what to do next.

*I'm not deciding,* Verel told himself, banking the fire and taking up his candle. *I'm merely... declining to interfere.*

The candle guttered as he opened the door, and in the draft the parish register stirred, its pages breathing, the name *Lior Crane, daughter* lying in the lamplight like a coin left on a table for whoever needed it next.

Outside, the rain had stopped. Across the dark town, one window still burned in the paper quarter, and Verel stood a moment on the steps watching it—that stubborn square of gold—before turning away toward the barracks, carrying his unsigned report and the growing, unwelcome suspicion that somewhere between the ledger and the lamplight, he had already chosen a side.

The tribunal convened in nine days.

And in the morning, a courier would arrive at the records office with a requisition order that would pull every file on forty families into a single review—an order signed, as Verel would discover too late to matter, in handwriting far too fine for any imperial clerk.
