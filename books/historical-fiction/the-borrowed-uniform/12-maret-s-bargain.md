## 12. Maret's Bargain

Maret Sorrel had washed other people's linens for thirty-one years, and she had learned something in that time that no magistrate or captain would ever put in a report: you could tell everything about a household by what they were ashamed of.

The Cranes, she thought, scrubbing at a collar stain that morning with more force than the collar deserved, were ashamed of all the wrong things.

The boy—Lio, everyone called him, and she was one of perhaps three people in Vellmere who knew better—had come by her washhouse two days ago with the proofs folded inside his coat like contraband scripture, which in a sense they were. He'd stood there in the steam with his cap pulled low and asked, very quietly, whether the papers would pass inspection. And Maret had looked at the fine engraved border, the seal so perfect it made her teeth ache, the handwriting that wasn't handwriting but a small miracle of cut brass—and she had said, "They'll pass inspection in the Emperor's own parlor," and watched the boy go pale with relief, which told her he understood exactly what he'd made.

A thing good enough to save forty lives. Also a thing good enough to hang every one of them if it were found half-finished.

Which was why she was climbing Crane's Hill at dusk with wet hems and a hard knot of purpose in her chest, past the shuttered chandlery, past the house where the Pell family had nailed their shutters closed since the requisition, up to the print shop with its crooked sign—*CRANE & SON, PRINTERS*, the paint flaking off *SON* in a way that had always struck Maret as the universe having a private joke.

She knocked the pattern: two, pause, three. The door opened on Aldous Crane himself, which surprised her. He should have been abed. The fever had him by the throat for four days now, and anyone could see it—the gray in his cheeks, the way he braced his hand on the doorframe as though the floor might argue with him.

"Maret." He attempted a smile. It came out at roughly half strength. "Come to gloat? You told me the coal prices would ruin me, and here I am ruined."

"I told you coal prices would ruin you in *March*. This is February. You're ahead of schedule." She stepped past him into the warm ink-and-paper smell of the shop. "Where's your apprentice?"

"Upstairs. Eating. Or pretending to—I've stopped being able to tell with that child." He closed the door and shot the bolt, and his hand shook on the iron, and he pretended it didn't, and Maret let him pretend because that was a kindness she could afford. "You've heard about the tribunal, then."

"The tribunal's moved up." She unwound her scarf. "Thursday. Not next week. Thursday, Aldous. Which means we have tonight and tomorrow night to finish the run, and I've come to settle something before we spend them."

"Settle what?"

"The ledger."

The word dropped between them like a typecase turned upside down. Aldous went still in a way Maret recognized from thirty years of watching this man—he went still the way a press goes still, all that stored weight suddenly waiting on a decision.

"Sit down, Aldous."

"I'm standing fine."

"You're standing the way a fence post stands. Sit down before you fall down and crack your head on the composing stone, and then I'll have to explain to your apprentice why I let you."

He sat. That alone told her how sick he was; Aldous Crane argued with weather, with magistrates, with the quality of imported ink, but he did not sit when told unless his body had overruled him.

Maret took the chair across from him. Upstairs, faintly, she could hear the creak of someone moving—a board, a chair, the small honest noises of a person trying not to be overheard. Good. She'd have preferred to say what she had to say to both of them at once, but the boy would hear soon enough, and some arguments were kinder begun with the elder.

"After the run is finished," she said, "the ledger burns."

"No."

"You haven't heard the whole—"

"There's nothing to hear, Maret. No."

She let the silence sit. Silence was a tool of her trade too; she'd got more confessions out of customers with a paused scrub-brush than any priest had got from a pulpit. But Aldous Crane had been raised in this shop the way an oak is raised in wind, and silences slid off him.

"It's my father's book," he said at last, when she wouldn't speak first. "His hand started it. Forty years of entries in his fist, and then mine under it. Every debt forgiven, every bribe paid, every child born above the bakery whose father paid to keep it out of the parish rolls—it's all in there, and it's *ours*, Maret. It's the only true history this town has. You want me to feed four decades of my family into a stove."

"Yes."

"One word. You don't even hesitate."

"I hesitated," Maret said. "For eleven years. While I carried names to you and you carried papers back, I hesitated, and I told myself the book was safe because it was hidden, because it was ours, because the people in it trusted us." She leaned forward. "And then Captain Verel walked into this town with polished boots and a list, and the first thing his list wanted was the ledger. Do you remember handing it to him?"

"That was strategy—"

"That was you giving a soldier the master key to every soul in Vellmere because you couldn't bear the thought of parting with it honestly!" Her voice cracked against the rafters, and upstairs the creaking stopped. She lowered it. "I know why you handed it over. You thought: better they take the real one than search and find the false bottom where we keep the accounts that fund the exemptions. Clever. It bought us time. I grant it. But Aldous—that book is sitting in the records office right now, and Verel is *reading it*. A man who reads. Do you understand what that means? We are not hiding a treasure anymore. We are keeping a hostage."

He stared at his hands. They were stained to the second knuckle, the way they always were, ink worked so deep into the creases that no washing would ever get it out—not even hers, and she had tried once, years ago, when his wife left and he'd sat in this chair for three days unshaven and she'd taken his hands and scrubbed like she could scrub the grief off too.

"My father kept it through the last uprising," he said quietly. "Soldiers came then too. He hid it in the flour barrel and lied to a sergeant's face with a pistol against his jaw, and the book survived, and when it was over, every name in it was still safe. That's the covenant, Maret. That's what a keeper does. The book outlasts the trouble. That's the whole point of a book."

"And how many people did your father's generation lose to that trouble?" she asked. "Because their names were written down somewhere. Somewhere tidy. Somewhere *kept*."

"That was parish registers and tax rolls. Different thing entirely."

"Is it?" She stood, because staying seated was making her feel like a petitioner, and Maret Sorrel had never petitioned anything in her life. She crossed to the window instead, where the last light was going copper over the rooftops, and the smoke of occupied Vellmere rose straight and thin in the cold air. "You know what I do all day, Aldous? I handle what people can't hide. Sheets, collars, the stains nobody wants named. And do you know what I've never once found in thirty-one years of laundry? A single piece of cloth that made a person safer for having been recorded."

He made a sound that was almost a laugh. "That's not an argument, that's a sermon."

"Good. I've been saving it." She turned back to face him. "Forty families. Forty. The Delacroix cousins, the widow Anse, the Perrin brothers who can't read well enough to know what's been forged on their behalf—which, incidentally, makes your beautiful papers a death sentence twice over if they're traced, because those men will swear blind they never saw a document in their lives, and they'll be telling the truth, and it won't matter. All of it hangs together, Aldous, on one condition: that after Thursday, there is nothing left to find. No plate. No proofs. No ledger. The tribunal comes, asks its questions, finds ash and a respectable print shop, and forty families live."

"The plate I'll break myself," he said. "Gladly. I'll melt it down and cast it into candlesticks. But the ledger—" His jaw set, and for a moment she saw the young man he'd been, the one who'd argued the guild council into accepting a printer's daughter-apprentice without asking inconvenient questions, the one who'd buried a son and said almost nothing and gone back to work the same week because work was the only prayer he knew. "No. Ask me anything else."

"Aldous—"

"*Anything else.*"

There were footsteps on the stair. Of course there were. Maret had a theory that the Crane child could hear an argument through two floors and forty feet of joists, the way other people heard dinner bells.

Lio came down with a candle, moving carefully, the way the boy always moved—like someone perpetually apologizing for taking up space. The disguise was good. Maret had to give it that. Seven years of it, and the shoulders had learned their lie so thoroughly that even now, knowing the truth, she sometimes had to look twice. What she saw tonight, though, under the cap and the borrowed name, was a face doing arithmetic: father gray-faced in the chair, laundress standing like a drawn bow, and somewhere in between, the shape of a word—*ledger*—that had already traveled.

"We weren't shouting," Aldous said.

"No, sir."

"We were discussing."

"Yes, sir." Lio set the candle on the composing stone, and the flame stood straight up in the still air, and for a moment none of them spoke, and Maret watched the boy watch the both of them, and thought: *this one knows already. This one has known since the proofs went out the door.*

"Your apprentice has a stake in this," Maret said.

"My apprentice is tired and should be abed."

"With respect," Lio said—voice pitched carefully, carefully neutral, a voice worn smooth like a river stone—"I've been cutting paper for the run for six nights. If there's a question about what happens after, I'd rather hear it now than at the stove."

Aldous closed his eyes briefly, a man asking heaven for patience heaven had stopped providing. "Fine. Your laundress wants to burn my father's ledger."

"I want," Maret said, "to burn the only document in Vellmere that lists every secret worth killing for—including yours, boy, if you think Verel can't read 'Crane' and count apprentices. I want it burned while we still choose the hour, instead of waiting for a soldier to choose it for us. And I want your father to explain to me, plainly, why a book matters more than the forty people whose lives depend on nobody ever reading it again."

The silence stretched. The candle flame leaned, recovered.

"It's not about the book," Lio said at last.

"Lior—" Aldous began, sharp, and caught himself too late, the true name escaping like a bird from a too-quickly opened cage, and Maret watched the boy freeze, and watched Aldous go white around the mouth, and thought: *well. There's the whole story, isn't it. Sitting right there in the open like spilled type.*

But Lio recovered the way the child always did—smoothed it flat, swallowed it down, became Lio again in the space of a breath. Only the hands, hanging at the sides, curled slightly, and uncurled.

"It's not about the book," the boy repeated, evenly. "It's about what burning it says. That the names in it were only safe as long as they were hidden. That the best we can build is a bigger lock." A pause. "Isn't that right? Isn't that what you actually think, Master Sorrel? Or do you just want it gone?"

Maret studied the child—this girl wearing a dead boy's name and a dead boy's apron, seven years deep in a silence that would drown most adults—and felt the argument shift under her feet.

"No," she admitted. "Not just gone. Here's what I believe, and you can laugh at a washerwoman's philosophy if you like. A record that can sell you is worth more to your enemies than to you. Always. Every ledger, every register, every neat column of names—it's a weapon lying on the ground waiting to be picked up, and the only question is whose hand gets there first. I've spent my life watching powerful men treat other people's names as inventory. Burn the inventory, and you starve the trade."

"But the debts forgiven are in there," Lio said. "The bribes that kept families fed in the famine year. The births. The marriages. Forty years of Vellmere taking care of itself, off the books, because the books belonged to the Empire." The candlelight moved on the young face, and Maret saw, not for the first time, that there was nothing boyish in the intelligence behind it—only in the costume. "If we burn it, we agree the official version is the only version. We burn our own history to spite them. Isn't that letting them win too?"

"Oh, splendid," Aldous murmured. "Now I'm outnumbered by my own side."

"You're not outnumbered," Maret said. "You're *outvoted*, which is different, and worse for you." She turned back to him, and gentled her voice, because the point needed landing softly or it would bounce. "Aldous. Listen to me. Your father built something brave. I know it. Nobody in this town knows it better than I do—half the entries in that ledger are names I carried to him myself, in bundles, marked with soap symbols only he could read. I loved that old man. I loved what he kept. And if the world were fair, the book would sit in that vault until your granddaughter dusted it, and every name in it would be a happy one." She knelt—knelt, which cost her knees and her pride in equal measure—to be level with him in the chair. "But the world sent us Verel. And Verel reads. And Thursday is coming whether we finish the run or not, and if we finish it and leave that book intact, then every life we save this week we spend the rest of ours holding hostage. Is that the inheritance? A sword over forty families' heads, forever, because it was your father's?"

Aldous looked at her for a long moment. Then at the dark doorway of the vault stairs, where the ledger lived behind its false wall. Then at his own stained hands, spread on his knees like something surrendered.

"When my father died," he said, "the last thing he said to me was *keep it*. Not keep it safe. Just—keep it. As if the book were a heartbeat and stopping meant he'd truly died." His voice frayed. "Do you know what it costs a man, Maret, to be asked to kill his father twice?"

"I know what it costs a town," she said, "to bury forty families because one man couldn't."

Nobody moved. Upstairs, a floorboard settled. The candle had burned a thumb's width lower, and somewhere outside, far off toward the square, a drum sounded—one beat, correction, two—and stopped, and the silence after it was worse than the noise.

It was Lio who broke it.

"There's a third option," the boy said quietly.

Both of them turned.

"Not burn it. Not keep it. Not yet." Lio picked up the candle and held it so the light fell across all three faces at once, a little conspirator's tableau, and Maret thought, with a strange lurch: *whoever taught this child to be invisible taught her to command a room too, and doesn't seem to have noticed.* "Finish the run first. All of it, both nights, whatever it takes. Then decide—together, all three of us, with clear heads—what happens to the book. Because if we tear each other apart over it now, we'll be too tired and too angry to pull clean sheets Thursday, and then it won't matter what's in the vault, because we'll all be in the ground with it."

"That's not a solution," Aldous said. "That's a delay."

"Everything is a delay," Lio said. "Until it isn't."

Maret laughed—short, unwilling, surprised out of her. "God help me, the child negotiates like a fishwife. Fine. Fine! Truce until the run is done. But hear me, both of you, and mark it well." She stood, knees cracking, and pointed one finger at Aldous, then at the boy, then back. "When the last sheet comes off that press, we settle it, and I will not be out-argued twice. And if you die of that fever before Thursday, Aldous Crane, I promise you I'll burn the book out of pure spite at your funeral, so consider that your incentive to stay alive."

"Your tenderness is legendary," Aldous said dryly—but he was smiling, faintly, really smiling for the first time since the drums had come to Vellmere, and Maret counted it a victory of a kind.

She gathered her scarf. At the door, with the bolt half-drawn, she paused.

"One more thing," she said. "The courier's uniform arrives tomorrow, hidden in the paper shipment from Marrow & Sons. Wool, imperial gray, lieutenant's braid." She looked from the father to the child and saw the color leave both their faces in matching degrees, and felt no satisfaction in it at all. "Someone will need to wear it into the records office before the tribunal verifies names. Someone small. Someone quick. Someone, ideally, whom nobody in this town has ever once thought to look at twice."

The candle in Lio's hand trembled, very slightly, and steadied.

"Goodnight, Master Sorrel," the boy said.

"Goodnight, Lior," Maret said, deliberately, watching the name land like a stone in still water, watching the father flinch and the daughter not, and let herself out into the cold.

Behind her, through the closing door, she heard Aldous begin—low, urgent, frightened—*"We need to talk about that,"* and the boy answer, calm as setting type:

*"No, Papa. We need to talk about the press. Two nights, you said. We'd best begin."*

Maret pulled her scarf up against the wind rolling off the river and started down Crane's Hill, and did not look back, because she had wash to see to, and names to carry, and two nights left in which to teach a girl in a borrowed name how to walk like an officer of the Empire.
