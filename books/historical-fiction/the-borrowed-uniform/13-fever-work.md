## 13. Fever Work

The first night began with a lie of kindness.

"You should sleep," Aldous said from the back room, propped against his rolled-up coat, and then ruined the sentence entirely by trying to stand and listing sideways into the shelf of quarto chases like a ship finding its mooring wrong.

Lior caught him under the arm—her father had always been heavier than he looked, as if he stored his stubbornness in his bones—and walked him back down onto the cot Maret's people had smuggled in two days before, along with willow bark tea, a crock of rendered fat, and one entire boiled mutton that nobody in the shop had the appetite to face.

"I'm fine," Aldous said, which was the second lie of kindness, and by morning it would be the third and fourth and fifth, because the fever did not break. It climbed. By the time Lior had inked the first forme of the night, he was arguing with someone who was not there—a man named Pell, dead eleven years, about the price of rags—his voice rising and falling through the thin partition like weather.

She pulled the bar. The platen came down with its familiar breath, that soft iron kiss she had known since she was tall enough to see over the stone. *Thump.* One sheet. *Thump.* Another.

Forty exemptions. Forty families whose names would hang or not hang depending on whether her hands held steady through two nights and however many hours the tribunal's clerks needed to make their lists official. Maret had said it plainly, the way laundresses say everything: *They verify names Thursday. If your papers reach the district office before Thursday noon, forty households become exempt households on imperial stationery, and nobody questions imperial stationery.*

So: two nights. Roughly three hundred impressions, allowing for spoilage, misfeeds, the temperamental left corner of the press that had to be coaxed like an old dog afraid of stairs.

Lior set the candle where it always went, rolled her sleeves, tied back her hair under the cap she'd worn so long it felt less like disguise than scalp, and began.

* * *

The shop talked to her at night. It always had. Every workshop has its own language—the tick of cooling metal, the sigh of the dampened paper stack settling, the way the type seemed almost to murmur when you brushed your fingers across a set line, reading by touch the way other people read by lamplight. Her father claimed a press could hear a lie in the compositor's hands. Lior had never been sure she believed him until tonight.

Because here was the thing she discovered somewhere around hour six, pulling proofs by a candle burned down to a thumb: the press did not care what the words said.

It cared only that they were true to themselves. A forged exemption printed cleanly was still, typographically, an honest object. The letters sat where letters belonged. The spacing breathed. The imperial crest she had cut—God forgive her, she had cut it *beautifully*, the eagle's wing feathers finer than the original, because Verel's own copies had been worn plates and she could not bring herself to reproduce shoddy work even in treason—sat centered and proud on every sheet.

"You're a hypocrite," she told the press.

*Thump,* said the press.

"No, I mean it. You'll print truth or forgery, you don't distinguish. Father says a press is a conscience. He's wrong. A press is a mouth."

*Thump.*

"Fine. Keep your opinions."

This was how the conversation went. She was aware it was not a conversation. She was aware, also, that it was the most honest talking she had done in seven years, which said something about the rest of her life that she chose not to examine while holding a brayer full of incriminating ink.

Sometime past midnight the fever-talk behind the partition changed. Aldous had stopped arguing with Pell and started calling a name. Not hers. Not "Lio," the apprentice name, the borrowed one.

He called *Tam.*

"Tamsin. Tamsin, come away from the window." A pause, thick with sleep-sweat and delirium. "The drums. Don't listen to the drums."

Lior stood very still with a wet proof dripping slightly in her hand.

Tamsin. Tam. The brother whose name she wore like a coat cut for someone else's shoulders. Dead of the winter fever at fourteen, and their mother gone by spring—not dead, just *gone*, which in some ways was worse, because a grave stays where you put it—and Aldous standing in the doorway of the shop with the apprenticeship ledger open in his hands saying, in the flat voice he used for prices, *The guild bond is in Tam's name. If the bond ends, the shop loses its license. If the license goes, we all go.*

And twelve-year-old Lior had understood, with the terrible clarity children have, exactly what was being asked, before anyone asked it aloud.

She hadn't even cried. That was the detail that came back now, thumping up out of the dark with every fall of the platen. She hadn't cried. She had asked only one question: *What do I have to learn first?*

And Aldous had looked at her—at this small girl offering to erase herself like a smudged galley—and something in his face had broken quietly, the way type breaks, one piece at a time, invisible unless you know the line is wrong.

"Tamsin," the fever said again. "Come inside."

"She can't," Lior said softly, to the partition. "She's me."

She hung the proof on the drying line and pulled the next sheet.

* * *

Dawn came gray through the north windows, the color of unprinted paper. Lior had finished the first forme—all twenty-five sheets of the first half-run, plus eleven spoiled ones she fed to the stove, watching exemption papers curl into flame and feeling nothing she could name—and she went in to change her father's poultice.

His forehead was a bread oven. His eyes found her and focused, briefly, with terrifying lucidity.

"The kerning on the eagle's left wing," he rasped. "Did you—"

"It's fine, Papa."

"Don't tell me it's fine. Your grandfather lost a commission over a wing feather. Vellmere does not do *approximate* eagles."

"The eagle is perfect. Sleep."

"Is the coal—" He tried to sit up. She pressed him back down with one hand on his chest, gently, the way you hold down a page that wants to curl.

"Coal's low," she admitted. "I'll manage."

"There's a sack behind the—behind the..." His eyes drifted. The lucidity went out like a snuffed lamp. "...Maret knows the rag man. Rag man owes me. Everyone owes me. That's what the book is, Lio, that's all the book *is*..."

Lior froze with the poultice half-wrung.

He'd never called her Lio in his sleep before. In his sleep he was honest, and honesty, apparently, meant the borrowed name too—or maybe the fever simply reached for whatever was nearest, the way a hand reaches for a familiar tool in the dark. She couldn't decide which possibility hurt more, so she shelved both and got back to work, because the tribunal did not wait for feelings, and neither did the press.

* * *

The second night, the coal ran out at ten o'clock.

This was a problem of a kind outsiders never think about. Print shops live on heat—dampening water must be the right temperature, ink must flow, and more importantly, in February, the *compositor's fingers* must work, because setting type is a craft of fingertips and cold turns fingertips into borrowed tools from someone clumsier.

Lior burned the spoiled proofs first. There is a special category of warmth reserved for burning your own treason, and she stood over the stove warming her hands and thought, *somewhere in the capital there is a clerk whose file copy of these exact documents is keeping him warm too, and neither of us knows we're colleagues.*

Then she burned the old wedding invitation samples from the Hollis job, three years stale. Then, wincing, a crate of unsold ballads about a highwayman named Ferro Wild, which the town had decisively declined to purchase, possibly because Ferro Wild was obviously Aldous himself, right down to the limp and the hatred of toll bridges.

At two in the morning she was down to the packing papers when someone knocked.

Three knocks. Then two. Then one. Maret's rhythm, the one that meant *open slowly, it's only me, probably.*

She let in a wall of cold air shaped like a laundress. Maret carried a coal scuttle in each arm and an expression that dared Lior to comment on either.

"Rag man owed your father," she said. "Also everyone else in this town owes somebody. That's what makes us a town." She set the scuttles by the stove and looked around the shop with the efficient eye of a woman accustomed to assessing other people's messes professionally. "How many?"

"Nineteen of the second forme. Twenty-two spoiled. The eagle keeps catching on the left corner of the chase—I've shimmed it twice."

"Shimmed it with what?"

"A folded strip of the Ferro Wild ballads."

Maret considered this. "Best use those ballads ever had." She crossed to the press and watched Lior feed the next sheet, arms crossed, head tilted. "Your father pulls the bar smoother."

"My father has thirty years on me and doesn't have to worry his sleeves are rolling up wrong."

"Nobody's looking at your sleeves."

"Somebody might be." The words came out sharper than intended. Maret heard them, being a woman who heard everything, and mercifully let them go.

She stayed an hour, sorting dried type for the next formes without being asked, fast and accurate, a woman who had clearly handled other people's small sharp objects before. As she wrapped up again against the cold, she paused at the door.

"He's asking for Tamsin," Lior said. She didn't know why she said it. Maybe because the press had heard everything else; it seemed unfair that Maret should get less truth than a machine.

Maret was quiet a moment. "Fever picks the oldest wound," she said finally. "My mother, at the end, spent three days apologizing to a mule." And she stepped out into the dark, leaving Lior with two scuttles of coal and the strange comfort of knowing the whole town was carrying wounds it apologized to nightly.

* * *

Hour nineteen. Hour twenty-one. The candle stubs multiplied like a congregation of tiny martyrs. Lior's world narrowed to the width of a composing stick.

There is a place you go, on the second night, when the body stops negotiating and simply obeys. She had been there once before—after Tam died, the week of the funeral, setting and resetting the memorial broadside until the letters swam. It is a quiet place. The hands know the work better than the head does. The eyes read the composed lines upside down and backward without translating, because translation is a luxury of the rested.

Somewhere in that quiet, the press stopped being a machine and became the only creature in the world who knew everything about her and had never once flinched.

*You know,* she thought, feeding sheet after sheet into its iron mouth. *You've known since I was twelve. You've held my secret under every impression. Seven years of my hands, and you've never once said a word to anyone.*

*Thump.*

"That's loyalty," she murmured. "Or you're just iron."

*Thump.*

"Yes, all right. Iron. But you're *my* iron."

She laughed at herself—actually laughed, alone at three in the morning over forged documents with her father raving ten feet away—and the laugh cracked something loose, and for a minute she stood with her forehead against the press frame and let the tears come, the ones from age twelve, arriving seven years late like a badly scheduled delivery. The press took the weight of her. It was built to take weight. That was practically its whole character.

Then she wiped her face on her sleeve, because there were nine sheets left in the forme and crying smears ink, and Vellmere does not do approximate eagles.

* * *

The last sheet of the last forme came off the press as the bells rang six on the second morning. Forty exemptions, complete, drying in ranks on the lines like laundry—Maret would appreciate that—each one bearing the imperial crest with its immaculately kerned eagle, each one a lie so well-made it had become a kind of truth: *these families are exempt,* the papers would say, and by Thursday noon, if the papers worked, the saying would make it so.

Lior cleaned the press. This was ritual, not choice—Aldous had drilled it into her since childhood: *the press is cleaned after every run, no exceptions, no excuses, because a neglected press neglects you back.* She washed the ink plate, oiled the joints, broke down the forme and distributed the type back into its cases, letter by letter, each one going home.

Her father slept without raving for the first time in two days. She checked his poultice, replaced it, and stood a moment watching his chest rise and fall, thinking about Tamsin, and about how someday—soon, soon, sooner than she was ready for—she was going to have to tell him things the fever already knew.

She was reaching for the kettle when she heard the knock.

Not Maret's rhythm. Three knocks, evenly spaced, unhurried, official-sounding—and Lior's whole body went rigid, because at dawn on Wednesday, with forged exemptions drying overhead and her father delirious ten feet away, nobody knocked like that except trouble.

She opened the door a hand's width.

No soldier. No officer. Just the paper merchant's boy, red-nosed with cold, staggering under a shipment crate nearly his own size. "Delivery for Crane," he wheezed. "Special order. Paid in advance." He dumped it across the threshold, accepted a coin, and fled toward warmth.

Lior stared at the crate. They hadn't ordered stock—she was sure of it, sure down to the ledgers in her memory—and paid-in-advance special orders did not arrive at dawn unannounced in occupied towns.

She pried up the lid.

Paper on top, yes—good rag stock, the kind used for official documents, which made her stomach drop before she'd even touched it. But beneath the paper, wrapped in sacking, was something with buttons.

She unwrapped it, and the color drained out of the room.

An imperial courier's uniform. Dark blue, good wool, sized for someone slight. A courier's dispatch satchel. And pinned to the coat's inner lining, a paper tag in a hurried hand:

*Thursday. Records office. Pull files 1–40. Wear this. Burn this note. — M.*

Lior looked at the uniform. The uniform, being wool, said nothing, but she heard it anyway, the way she'd learned to hear the press.

*Borrowed lives fit you,* it seemed to say. *One more won't kill you.*

*That,* she thought grimly, folding the coat closed, *is exactly what worries me.*
