## 14. The Uniform

The paper shipment came on a Tuesday, which was already strange, because nothing honest ever arrived on a Tuesday. Deliveries came Thursdays, from the mill upriver, in bales wrapped in oilcloth and smelling of chalk and river weed. This one came at dusk, on a handcart pushed by a boy who wouldn't meet anyone's eyes, and it smelled of tallow and horse.

Lior signed for it with her father's name—she signed everything with her father's name now; Aldous had been three days abed with the fever that rattled in his chest like loose type—and carried the first bale inside while Maret stood in the doorway pretending to count coins.

"Mill shipment," Lior said.

"Mm," said Maret.

"He said Thursday."

"Mm," said Maret again, in the tone of a woman watching a pot she knew was about to boil over.

It was the third bale that told them. The first two were good rag paper, heavy stock, exactly what the shop needed for the tribunal work. The third weighed wrong. Lior hoisted it onto the stone bench and the rope slipped, and instead of the soft slump of paper it landed with the clank of something dense folded into the middle of it, like a stone sewn into a loaf.

She looked at Maret. Maret looked at the ceiling, whistling two notes of a laundress's song, badly.

"You know what this is," Lior said.

"I know I'm not supposed to say."

"Maret."

"And I know your father would want me to let you open it yourself, and I know you'll open it whether I let you or not, because you've got his hands—you can't leave a knot alone." She sighed, sat down on the delivery stool, and folded her arms. "Open the knot."

The oilcloth was stitched shut with waxed thread, and whoever had done it had done fine work, but they had not done print-shop work, and Lior's knife found the seam in under a minute. She peeled the layers back the way she'd peel an onion, expecting—she didn't know what she expected. Guns. Letters. A head, in her worst imaginings, though she kept that imagining firmly in its box.

What she got was a coat.

Not just a coat. It lay there in the lamplight, folded with military precision by someone who had cared about creases even at the end: a courier's uniform, imperial gray-blue, brass buttons gone dull at the edges, the shoulder insignia of the Third Vellish Courier Corps—the corps that no longer existed, because two months ago the empire had stopped trusting local couriers and started shooting them. Under the coat, a shirt. Under the shirt, trousers, boots, a belt with a buckle stamped with the double eagle, and a peaked cap with a sweat-stain inside the band shaped like a small dark moon.

And pinned to the lapel, a wooden dispatch tag, numbered 114.

Nobody said anything for a long moment. From the back room, through the wall, came the sound of Aldous coughing, deep and wet, and then the sound of him stopping himself, because he'd learned to stop himself, because coughing loudly meant someone would come check on him and he hated being checked on more than he hated dying, probably.

"He was called Fen," Maret said quietly. "The courier. Younger than you. They took him off the north road." She touched the coat's collar without quite touching it, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you weren't sure of. "His mother washed it herself. Boiled it twice. She wanted—" Her voice did something complicated and then reassembled itself. "She wanted it used properly. That's what she said. *Used properly.* Not hung in a cupboard going gray."

Lior looked at the uniform and felt the floor tilt very slightly, the way it always did lately when the world asked her for one more thing.

"It's too big," she said, which was a stupid thing to notice first, but noticing stupid things first was how she survived. "I'd drown in it."

"It's not too big," Maret said. "Try it."

* * *

The plan, as Maret laid it out over the next hour, sitting at the shop's workbench with the ledger's ghost between them—because the ledger itself was locked in Verel's strongroom now, but Lior had copied enough of it in seven nights of stolen candlelight that the copy lived in her head like a second skeleton—

The plan was simple, which is how Lior knew it would be terrible.

"The tribunal convenes Monday," Maret said. "Before the tribunal convenes, the records office has to verify the forty names against the files they pulled from your vault. Verification takes a day. If the files are missing Monday morning—if they're simply *gone*, misfiled, vanished—the tribunal can't confirm the sentences. And an unconfirmed sentence, in this army, gets kicked upstairs. Upstairs is slow. Slow is time. Time is forty families still breathing."

"So we steal files."

"We don't steal files. Stealing leaves a hole. You walk in wearing that—" she nodded at the uniform hanging from the beam where they'd pegged it, and it swung gently, emptily, like a man waiting to be filled "—you carry a dispatch order, real-looking, official, telling the records clerk that all forty case files are recalled to division headquarters for pre-tribunal audit. He signs them out to you. You sign the register. You walk out."

"And then the files are gone, which is a hole."

"Not if the register says they went to headquarters. Then it's not theft, it's *procedure*. Nobody checks procedure. Procedure is the best hiding place in the world." Maret almost smiled. It was a grim little smile, the kind that lived mostly in the corners. "You'd be amazed what I've walked out of houses carrying, just by looking bored and holding a clipboard."

Lior turned her cup on the bench, around and around. "Who writes the dispatch order?"

"You do."

Of course. Of course. She thought of the forgery plate she'd cut by lamplight two weeks ago, the finest work of her life, and how she'd hated how much she'd loved doing it. "They'll have real dispatch orders. Real ones. With the right watermark, the right hand, the right—"

"You've seen their orders?"

"I've seen everything that comes through this town on paper. It's my job. It's been my job since I was nine years old." She hadn't meant to sound bitter. Or maybe she had. "Verel's own field orders crossed this bench in October. I set a receipt for his quartermaster. I know his adjutant's abbreviations. I know the division stamp has a chipped eagle—the left wing, third feather."

Maret stared at her. "How do you know that?"

"Because I fixed the receipt he sent back complaining about it. He wrote *defective seal* in the margin and I had to typeset the replacement, so I looked at the seal very carefully for a long time, because that is what careful people do before they are hanged for carelessness." She pressed her palms flat against the bench. "Maret. I can make that order so true that Verel himself would sign it."

"That's the idea."

"No, you don't understand. That's the *problem*." She heard her own voice go thin and was embarrassed by it. "Every time I do this, it gets easier. Do you understand? Cutting the plate was easy. Forging Aldous's signature on the requisition receipts was easy—I've been forging his signature since I was eleven, because he made me practice it, because a printer's apprentice should be able to reproduce any hand, he said, it's a craft skill, like kerning." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong. "I have spent seven years being good at being someone else, and every year it costs less effort, and I keep thinking, at some point, shouldn't it start costing *more*?"

Maret was quiet a while. Outside, the curfew drum sounded, four beats and a rest, four beats and a rest, the heartbeat of the occupation.

"When I was fifteen," Maret said finally, "I borrowed my sister's washing contract. She was sick—truly sick, not faking—and the family needed her route covered or we lost the house. So I went door to door with her book, wearing her shawl, answering to her name, and nobody knew, because nobody looks. And I remember standing on Mrs. Halloway's step—Mrs. Halloway, who'd known us both since we were born—and she handed me the wash money and said, 'Tell your sister I hope she's better,' and I said 'I will,' in my sister's voice, and I walked away, and I felt *fine*. That's what frightened me. Not getting caught. Feeling fine." She reached across and put her rough hand over Lior's ink-stained one. "So feel bad about it. Feel bad all the way through. That's not weakness, girl. That's the part of you they haven't borrowed yet."

From the back room, faintly, deliriously, Aldous began reciting the alphabet backwards—a fever habit of printers, some old mnemonic for sorting type cases—and got to R before dissolving into coughing.

Lior stood up. "I should see to him."

"See to him," Maret agreed. "Then try on the coat."

* * *

Her father was asleep when she checked—actually asleep, not performing sleep, his breath whistling through the rattle in his chest, his hands curled loosely on the blanket like two resting animals. In the candle stub's light she could see how the fever had hollowed him. Seven years ago he'd seemed enormous, a man made of forearms and opinions. Now the opinions were still there, muttered into his dreams—he said *beware the widow asterisk* at one point, which meant nothing to anyone but a compositor—but the forearms had gone thin as a boy's.

She stood there longer than she needed to. She was aware, distantly, that she was memorizing him, the way you memorize a page you might not get to proofread twice.

Then she went back out and tried on the coat.

Maret had left, leaving only a note in laundry-book shorthand that Lior couldn't read and a stick of charcoal with instructions implied. The workshop was empty except for her and the dead boy's uniform and the press, and the press watched her, the way it always did, with the patience of a machine that had seen every foolish thing humans do and intended to outlast all of it.

The coat fit.

That was the horror of it. She'd braced for drowning in it, for comedy, for the excuse to laugh and hang it back on its peg. Instead the shoulders sat where her shoulders were, and the sleeves broke at the wrist, and when she buttoned it up—it took both hands, and she made herself look at each brass button as she closed it, *used properly*, Fen, all right, I'm trying—the coat closed over her chest and simply made her flat, made her straight, made her into a shape the world already knew how to read.

She caught her reflection in the darkened window glass, overlaid on the street outside, and for a moment she couldn't find herself in it at all. There was a young man in a courier's uniform standing in a print shop. Number 114. Some nameless, faceless functionary whose whole biography was a tag on a lapel.

"Hello," she said to him, experimentally.

He had her voice, but lower. She'd done it before, of course—seven years of *Lio* was a performance held together by pitch and posture and the strategic slouch—but this was different. Lio was a role built slowly, lovingly, out of her own materials, like setting type letter by letter. This was a full cast. This was borrowing a whole life, buttons to boots, a dead boy's sweat-stain in the cap band.

She put on the cap. The moon-shaped stain settled above her brow like a blessing or a brand.

In the window-glass, the young man stood very straight, and behind his shoulder, superimposed on the street, a figure was walking past in a long officer's coat—just a citizen out after curfew bell, hurrying home, tall, unhurried, familiar in the way of nightmares.

Verel.

Her heart performed an undignified maneuver. But he passed on, unaware, a shadow among shadows, and Lior stood in her borrowed skin and realized, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that in four days she would stand in front of him wearing this coat, and he would look at her—at number 114, at a courier, at a *boy*—and see nothing.

And she didn't know why that thought landed like grief instead of relief.

She took off the cap. Held it. The sweat-stain was still damp-feeling, or she imagined it was.

"One more time," she said to the window, to Fen, to Tam, to every name she'd ever worn. "One more borrow, and then I'm done."

The window said nothing. Windows never did. But somewhere in the type cases behind her, seven thousand pieces of lead waited in their compartments, each one a letter, each letter patient, and Lior Crane—who had spent seven years being nobody's daughter and everybody's apprentice—went to fetch the good paper, because if she was going to forge the empire, she was going to forge it beautifully.

* * *

She worked until the fourth hour of morning. The dispatch order grew under her hands: the correct stock, salvaged from a requisitioned consignment; the correct chipped-eagle stamp, carved into a new block by candlelight with a graver finer than her smallest type; the adjutant's cramped abbreviation style, *rec'd div. hqdrs., att. tribunal audit, prio.* She even reproduced the tea-ring stain that Major Ostrev's orders always bore, using the actual ring from the actual cup on the actual bench, because detail was truth and truth, tonight, was a tool she refused to feel guilty about.

Tomorrow she would walk into the records office. Tomorrow a clerk with bad eyesight would hand forty lives across a counter to a dead boy's coat.

At dawn she hung the uniform back on its beam, and it swayed there in the gray light, empty and waiting, and from the back room her father's fever broke all at once with a great clearing cough, and his voice—hoarse, alarmed, instantly himself—called out:

"Lio! What's hanging on my beam?"

Lior looked at the coat. Looked at the door.

"Nothing, Papa," she called back, in her brother's voice. "Just paper."

But even as she said it, her hand was already closing the vault door on the dispatch order, and on the wooden tag numbered 114, and on the list she'd written of forty names that were, as of dawn, officially her responsibility—and the lie sat in her mouth lighter than any lie she'd ever told, which frightened her more than the soldiers ever had.
