## 15. Two Mirrors

The uniform arrived folded inside a ream of rag paper, wrapped in oilcloth that smelled of tallow and river mud, and Lior stood over it for a long moment before she touched it.

It was an officer's coat. Imperial blue, the collar faced with the dull silver braid of a records clerk—second rank, unglamorous, the kind of posting given to men who were good at filing and not much else. The courier who had carried it into Vellmere would never carry anything again; Maret had said this plainly, without apology, and Lior had learned by now which silences kept people safe. She said nothing either.

She locked the workshop door. She banked the stove so the room went dim and orange. And then, because there was no one to see and because she had promised herself she would not be a fool about this, she undressed to her shift and lifted the coat off the paper with both hands, the way Aldous lifted a finished sheet off the press—as though it might still be wet, as though it might change if handled carelessly.

It was heavier than she expected. Wool and brass and authority, all of it sewn together into something a person could put on.

* * *

The trousers fit. That was the first miracle, and she laughed at herself for thinking of it as one—a short, startled sound in the empty shop, quickly smothered. Seven years of binding and belting and choosing coats two sizes too large had taught her exactly how a body could be persuaded into a shape it didn't own. But these trousers had been cut for a slim young man, and Tam's ghost, or whatever it was that lived in her shoulders and stride, filled them out without argument.

The coat was another matter. She got one arm in, then the other, and stood before the cracked mirror in the corner—the one Aldous used to check his plates for flaws—and looked at what the glass gave back.

A soldier looked back. Young, pale, tired around the eyes. The blue made his skin look sallow, which was useful; sallow men were forgettable. He stood with his weight slightly on his left heel, the way she'd seen Verel stand, and when she noticed herself doing it she stopped, appalled, and then did it again on purpose, because if she was going to borrow a life she might as well borrow it well.

"Second Clerk Hessen," she said to the mirror, trying the name Maret had given her. It sat strangely in her mouth. All names did, except one, and that one she hadn't said aloud in seven years.

She buttoned the coat to the throat. The silver braid caught the stove-light. In the glass, the apprentice Lio vanished—not hidden, exactly, but folded away somewhere beneath the wool, the way a forme of type is locked into its chase: all the letters still there, just held tight and turned toward the paper.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her most. She had expected them to shake.

What she felt instead was worse than fear. It was competence. She was *good* at this. She had been practicing since she was eleven years old, and every year of silence had been a stroke of the burnisher, smoothing her into someone else's shape until the seams hardly showed. Looking at the soldier in the mirror, she understood that the uniform was not really a disguise at all. It was just the newest coat over the oldest lie.

And underneath it, faint as watermark, Lior Crane waited to be read.

She took the coat off carefully, hung it on the press frame where the ink fumes would help age it, and told herself the trembling started only after she'd finished.

* * *

Captain Verel owned three shirts, one good pair of boots, and a habit of walking the town alone in the hour before curfew, which his adjutant considered eccentric and his superiors would have considered insubordinate if they'd known. He walked because offices made him feel like a document himself—filed, stamped, archived while still breathing. And he walked because Vellmere at dusk was honest in a way paperwork never was. You could smell what people had cooked. You could hear what they'd stopped saying when you passed.

He was on Tanner's Row, past the shuttered dyeshops, when he saw the soldier.

That alone was nothing. Soldiers were everywhere now, requisitioning, loitering, kicking stones. But this one was walking wrong. Not badly—correctly, even precisely—but wrong the way a proof is wrong when a single letter has been set upside down. Everything reads. One glyph nags.

Verel slowed.

The soldier wore a records clerk's coat, second rank, and wore it like a man who had been told once how to wear it and had practiced in front of a mirror until the telling was gone. His boots were right. His cap was right. His pace was a half-beat too measured, the walk of someone counting steps, and no real second clerk counted steps, because real second clerks had long ago stopped believing anyone was watching.

Verel had spent eleven years learning to read documents, and the last six months learning to read people the same way—for erasures, for watermarks, for the telltale weight of paper that claimed to be older than it was. This soldier was a forgery. A very good one.

He should have called out. He should have demanded papers, and the soldier would have produced them, and the papers would have been flawless, because whoever had made this forgery made everything flawless, and then Verel would have had him—or her, some part of his mind said quietly, *her*, and he told that part to wait its turn—by the wrist.

Instead he said, "You're a long way from the records office, Clerk."

* * *

Lior's heart performed a single enormous beat and then, traitorously, settled.

Of all the streets in Vellmere. Of all the hours. She had put the coat on tonight for exactly ten minutes of practice—walking the length of the workshop, sitting and rising, learning where the braid pulled—and then, in a fit of stupidity she would examine later, she had stepped out the back into the alley to try the stride on cobbles instead of floorboards, and the alley had become Tanner's Row, and Tanner's Row had produced Captain Verel out of the dusk like a type-high piece of lead falling out of a case onto her foot.

*Don't stop,* she told herself. *Stopping is confession.*

"Clerk Hessen," she said, and heard her own voice come out lower than usual, flatter, a voice with the ink still wet on it. "Evening walk, Captain."

"Evening walks." Verel came closer. Up close he was thinner than he'd seemed in the print shop, and there was ink on his fingers—actual ink, the cheap iron-gall kind the office used, stained deep in the whorls. She stared at it a moment too long. "Curfew's in an hour."

"I know the regulations, sir."

"Do you." He wasn't smiling. But something in his face had shifted, the way light shifts on a plate when you tilt it. "Funny thing about regulations, Clerk. Everyone in this town claims to know them, and no one can recite Article Fourteen."

Lior had read Article Fourteen twice, by lamplight, three nights ago. *All civic records seized under requisition shall be inventoried within fourteen days and verified against parish registers within thirty.* She let the silence stretch exactly as long as a man annoyed at being tested would let it stretch, and then said, "Records are to be verified against parish registers within thirty days, sir."

Something flickered across Verel's face. Recognition, maybe. Or its cousin.

"You've been reading," he said.

"It's my work, sir."

"Yes," Verel said softly. "I imagine it is."

* * *

They fell into walking side by side, which neither of them decided and neither of them prevented. This was the thing Verel would think about later, lying awake in his billet with the parish register open on his chest like a cat that wouldn't move: that he had recognized a forgery and responded by taking a walk with it.

He asked questions the way he always did—sideways, unhurried, the questions themselves innocuous. How long posted in Vellmere. Whether the quarters were adequate. Whether the clerks had enough lamp-oil, because he'd had a complaint.

"Enough," said the clerk.

"And the files? Forty of them came over from the tribunal docket. Are they in order?"

"They're in order." A pause, expertly placed. "Mostly."

"*Mostly?*" Verel turned. Here it was, then. Here was the hook he'd been setting without quite admitting it. "Which ones?"

But the clerk only shrugged, a small shrug, a shrug that said *I'm second rank, sir, I notice things and say nothing, it's how I've survived*—and Verel felt the strange vertigo of looking into a posture he knew from the inside. Because that was his own shrug. That was the shrug he'd worn through six years of a ministry that had erased his sister from the family record with one administrative stroke and expected him to initial the page.

They passed the mouth of Cooper's Lane. A dog trotted out, inspected them both, and followed at heel for twenty paces before deciding they were boring.

"You're not from Vellmere," Verel said.

"No, sir."

"But you know the town."

"I know towns," the clerk said. "They're mostly the same. There's a street where the money lives and a street where the work happens, and everyone pretends the second street doesn't feed the first."

Verel laughed before he could stop himself—a short, rusty sound that surprised them both. "You'll go far in the service, Clerk."

"That's what I'm afraid of, sir."

* * *

Ten minutes. Lior counted them the way she counted press-pulls, automatically, a rhythm under everything. Ten minutes of walking beside the man whose signature ordered firing squads, and the extraordinary thing—the thing she would take home and turn over all night like a plate examined under raking light—was that it didn't feel like walking beside an enemy. It felt like walking beside a mirror that had learned to talk.

He was lonely. That was the first thing she'd seen, and she hated how fast she'd seen it, because noticing was her gift and her curse. Lonely men in borrowed authority, walking streets that feared them, asking questions about lamp-oil because nobody asked them questions about anything else. She knew that architecture from the inside. She had lived in it for seven years under a different name.

And he was reading her. She could feel it happening, patient and thorough, the way he'd run his thumb along the type specimens in the shop and asked which faces were her father's and which were older. He read the way she set type: completely, and with his whole attention, and looking for the flaw.

So she gave him a flaw. Small, deliberate, the way a forger leaves one deliberate error to satisfy the eye of the examiner. When he asked where she'd trained, she hesitated a half-second too long before saying "Vellport," and watched him file the hesitation away, satisfied, and understood that men like Verel trusted their own discoveries more than any perfection.

It was the cruelest thing she knew how to do, and she did it gently.

* * *

At the corner where Tanner's Row met the square, the drums were changing for evening muster, and the clerk stopped.

"This is me, sir," he said.

"Is it." Verel looked at him—at the correct cap, the correct coat, the eyes that were doing far too much work behind a deliberately bored expression. Every instinct he owned, honed on forged deeds and doctored ledgers, stood at attention and screamed. *This is your man. This is your forger, or the forger's hand, or the forger's heart; pull the thread.*

And underneath the screaming, quieter, a memory: a parish page with a name struck through in careful bureaucratic ink, a sister reduced to *deceased female child, entry void*, and the clerk who had done it, who had probably gone home that night to his supper feeling he'd done accurate work.

"Article Fourteen," Verel said. "Thirty days for verification. Do you know what happens to records that fail verification, Clerk?"

The clerk's jaw tightened, just barely. Just humanly. "They're destroyed, sir."

"They're destroyed," Verel agreed. "Or corrected. Depending on what the verifier decides is true." He straightened the man's collar with two fingers—an absurd gesture, paternal, nothing a captain does to a clerk—and felt the small flinch under the wool, quick as a heartbeat. "Mind your lamp-oil, Hessen."

"Sir."

The clerk walked away toward the square, and his stride was perfect now, and that was how Verel knew. Forgeries relaxed when the examination ended. Only guilty men and innocent ones stayed stiff.

He stood a long time in the darkening street, turning something over. Not the question of whether the clerk was false—he'd answered that. The other question, the one with teeth:

*Why am I standing here hoping he gets wherever he's going safely?*

* * *

In the workshop, Lior locked the door, leaned against it, and laughed until it turned into something else, and then back into laughing again, quietly, with her hand over her mouth like a girl at a funeral.

He'd fixed her collar. The enemy captain had reached out and straightened her collar with two fingers, gentle as Aldous checking a sheet for set-off, and she had nearly—wept, right there on Tanner's Row, in a dead man's coat.

Because here was the terrible arithmetic of the evening: she had spent ten minutes being seen. Not Lio, not the apprentice, not the silent girl folded into her brother's name—*seen*, argued with, tested, laughed at, touched. Verel had looked straight at the forgery and spoken to whatever was underneath it, the way a reader can feel a printer's hand through the worst disguise of a typeface. He thought he was talking to a frightened clerk named Hessen.

He was talking to Lior Crane. He simply didn't have the page yet.

Tomorrow she would put this coat on and walk into the records office under his actual supervision and pull forty files out from under his nose, and he would either catch her or he wouldn't, and she found—to her horror—that some small treacherous part of her did not want him to be humiliated when it happened. She wanted him to understand.

On the press frame, the uniform hung in the dark, silver braid catching the last of the stove-light.

Maret was due at dawn. The tribunal moved its date again—sooner now, everyone said, sooner—and forty names sat in the records office like type waiting to be distributed back into the case, each letter returned to anonymity, each family dissolved into the general stock.

Lior banked the stove, checked the lock twice, and went up to bed wearing her own name in silence, practicing it like a stride.

Tomorrow, she would borrow one more life. And this time, the man whose life it endangered would be watching her do it.
