## 16. The Records Office

The uniform fit better than it had any right to.

Lior had expected the courier's coat to hang off her shoulders like a borrowed future—too broad at the chest, too long at the cuff—but whoever Corporal Denn had been, he had been a small man with a stoop from years of saddle work, and the coat sat on Lior like it had been waiting for her. She told herself not to think about that. She told herself a great many things that morning, standing before the cracked mirror above the washbasin while Maret pinned her hair flat and tugged the collar straight with hands that smelled of lye and lavender soap.

"You look," Maret said, stepping back, "like a boy who has never once been asked to carry anything heavier than his own opinion."

"That's the idea."

"It's a good idea." Maret's eyes were red-rimmed. She hadn't slept; none of them had. Aldous was still feverish upstairs, murmuring type sizes in his sleep, and the tribunal bell would ring at noon. "Lio. If it goes wrong—if he stops you—you run. Don't be clever. Cleverness is for people who have time."

"I'm always clever."

"That's what frightens me."

* * *

The records office occupied the old customs house on Rope Walk, a building designed by someone who believed that fear worked best in high ceilings and stone floors. Lior had passed it ten thousand times without once going in. Now she went up its steps wearing Denn's coat and Denn's cap pulled low, carrying Denn's dispatch satchel with forty blank requisition forms inside it, and her heart did not hammer so much as knock politely, over and over, like a customer at the shop door asking after a job that could not possibly be finished by Friday.

There were two sentries outside. One was younger than she was. He looked at her papers—forged that morning, her finest work, the Verel signature rendered so faithfully she'd had to look away from it twice—and waved her through with the bored contempt soldiers reserve for clerks.

Inside: rows of shelving, a long counter, an iron-banded cabinet marked TRIBUNAL — PENDING CASES, and behind the counter, sorting papers into piles with the grim economy of a man who had done nothing else for three weeks—

Captain Verel.

He did not look up immediately. That was the first mercy. The second was that when he did look up, he looked at her exactly the way he had looked at every other courier who'd come through that door since the occupation began: with mild, tired recognition and no interest whatsoever.

"Dispatch?"

"For the tribunal files, sir." Her voice came out lower than she'd practiced. She set the requisition order on the counter between them—the one bearing his own forged signature—and watched him read it.

Here was the thing she had not let herself think about on the walk over, because thinking it would have stopped her feet: Verel knew handwriting. He had spent three evenings in the print shop turning type specimens over in his fingers, asking which faces wore best, which ink bit deepest into cheap paper. He had held her proofs. He had complimented her kerning.

He read the signature. The silence stretched long enough for Lior to count the ceiling beams (fourteen) and reconsider her entire life (twice).

"Forty cases," he said at last.

"Yes, sir."

"The tribunal moved them up. Everything gets verified by noon." He said it flatly, a fact among facts. Then he stood, took a ring of keys from the drawer—heavy, official, the keys themselves making the only sound in the whole stone room—and walked around the counter to the tribunal cabinet.

And unlocked it.

"Take them," he said. "You'll want the folder ties from the second shelf. They'll scatter otherwise."

Lior's hands, when she reached past him for the ties, were perfectly steady. This surprised her more than anything else that morning. Seven years of silences had taught her body things her mind refused to learn: how to be calm inside a lie, how to move through danger the way water moves through a lock, filling the shape given to it, asking no questions.

She pulled files. One, two, five, ten. Names she knew on the tabs—the Harrow family, old Sennet the ropewalk foreman, the twins from the dye house whose mother had once paid her father in eels. Each folder was thin. A name, a parish record reference, an accusation, a verdict pending. Forty lives reduced to paper so flimsy she could have printed all of them on one sheet.

Verel stood beside her the entire time. Not watching, exactly. Present. He re-sorted his own piles. He checked a ledger against a list. Once, when a file slipped from her grip and fanned across the floor—a page showing the accusation against Widow Quill, *harboring seditionists, son fled north*—he crouched and gathered the pages himself and handed them back to her in order, squared corners aligned, their fingers not quite touching.

"Careful," he said quietly. "Paper remembers being dropped."

"So do people, Captain."

Something flickered across his face then—something that was almost a smile and almost grief—and Lior felt the ground tilt beneath the uniform. Because he knew. She saw it now, or believed she saw it, the way you believe you see a face in firelight: the too-long pause before he'd unlocked the cabinet, the way he had not asked for her escort chit, the way his hand had rested a half-second on the drawer where the verification lists lay finished and signed.

He knew. And he was handing her the files anyway.

Neither of them said it. Neither of them could say it, because saying it would make it true, and true things required decisions, and decisions in Vellmere this winter got people shot against the granary wall. So they performed instead, both of them, with the fluency of two people raised in the same theater: she the courier doing tedious duty, he the officer enduring tedious paperwork, and between the lines of the performance ran the other thing, unspoken and enormous, like a river under ice.

"There's a discrepancy," Verel said suddenly, and Lior's blood went to lead.

"Sir?"

"In the file count. Requisition says forty." He turned a page on his clipboard. "I count thirty-nine folders in your satchel."

She counted. Frantically, inwardly, with the part of her mind that had once found a dropped em-quad in a locked forme at midnight. Thirty-nine. One file—the dye house twins' mother, Marta Ellery—sat on the counter where she'd set it down to retie the bundle.

"No, Captain." She picked it up and slotted it home. "Forty. I set that one aside while tying."

He looked at the satchel. He looked at her. And then Captain Verel, imperial officer, hunter of discrepancies, the man who had spent three weeks cross-checking names against parish ink with the patience of a typesetter himself—closed his clipboard and said:

"So you did."

* * *

The last file was the hardest, though not for the reason she expected. It bore a name she didn't know—Tomas Wren, cooper, aged nineteen—and a parish reference in Verel's own small precise hand. As she lifted it, she saw that beneath the accusation line, someone had written a note in pencil, faint, recent: *verify against baptismal register, St. Alder's.*

St. Alder's was Tam's parish. St. Alder's was where the record lived—or had lived—that said *Lior Crane, daughter.*

Had he written that note before or after? Did it matter? She slid the file into the satchel with the others, and if her hand shook, Verel was looking at the window, at the gray light coming down Rope Walk, at nothing.

"That's all of them, sir."

"Mm." He did not turn around. "Tell the quartermaster the escort chits are unnecessary for internal transfers. Saves everyone the paperwork."

"Yes, Captain."

"And—" He stopped. His back was very straight, the way her father's back went straight when he was about to say something true and couldn't afford to. When he finally spoke, his voice was quiet enough that the sentries outside could never have heard it through the door.

"When you return these—" a pause, precise as a hairline space, "—if you return them. Make certain they're kept somewhere dry. Paper rots faster than people expect. Faster than reputations. Faster than most kinds of memory." He turned then, and his eyes above the counter were tired and young and asking for something he could not name. "Do you understand me, courier?"

Every instinct Lior owned screamed at her to keep the performance seamless. But there was a silence in the room, and she had spent seven years learning which silences keep people safe, and this one—this one, she understood suddenly, was meant to keep *him* safe. He needed her not to answer honestly, so that he could go on not knowing what he had done. He needed plausible deniability the way a drowning man needs driftwood, and she could give him that, at least. It cost her nothing. It might have been the first kind thing she'd ever done with a lie.

"I understand, Captain," she said. "Dry storage. I'll see to it personally."

He nodded once, briskly, an officer dismissing a clerk. Then, as she reached the door with forty lives in a leather satchel against her hip:

"Courier."

She stopped. Did not turn. Her heart knocked its polite knock.

"The coat you're wearing belonged to a man who carried dispatches through the Marish winter. Nobody ever questioned his papers. Not once." A breath. "People see what the uniform tells them to see. It's not their fault. It's hardly even a lie."

"No, sir," Lior said. "It's printing."

She heard him inhale—sharp, startled, almost a laugh—and she opened the door and walked out into the cold with her spine straight and Denn's cap low, down the steps, past the young sentry who touched his helmet to her, along Rope Walk toward the print shop, and she did not run until she rounded the tannery corner and could no longer see the customs house windows.

Then she ran, and laughed, and wanted to weep, and did a little of both, alone in an alley that smelled of oak bark, holding forty names against her ribs like a heartbeat that wasn't hers yet.

* * *

Maret met her at the back door of the shop and took the satchel with the reverence other people reserved for relics.

"All forty?"

"All forty."

"And Verel?"

Lior untied the cap and pulled it off, and her hair sprang loose, and she was Lior again, apprentice, daughter, girl, herself—all of it at once, all of it hers. "He unlocked the cabinet," she said slowly, working it through as she said it. "He counted my files and miscounted on purpose. He told me to store them somewhere dry. Maret—he *knew*. I don't know how much, and I don't know why, but he knew, and he let me walk out."

Maret was silent a long moment, hugging the satchel. Outside, the town bells began to ring—not the tribunal peal, not yet, just the ordinary eleven o'clock, but it sounded different today, or she heard it differently.

"People aren't what the uniform says," Maret said at last. "That's the whole trouble with this war, isn't it? Everybody keeps discovering the enemy is a person, and nobody knows what to do about it."

"He burned something once," Lior said. "In his office. I saw the ash. I never asked."

"Don't ask. Some mercies work best unsigned." Maret shouldered the satchel. "Come on. We've got till noon to get these families moving, and your father's asking for you—fever's broken, and the first thing he said when he woke was that your plate cutting was sloppy on the descenders."

Despite everything, Lior smiled. "He's going to live, then."

"He's going to *criticize*," Maret corrected. "Which is better."

They went inside together, and behind them, up Rope Walk, the tribunal bell began to ring early—and wrong, fast and ragged, the alarm pattern, the one that meant the soldiers had discovered the files were gone.
