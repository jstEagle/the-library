## 17. What Verel Knows

Captain Edran Verel had written reports the way other men said prayers: nightly, kneeling at a small table, in a hand so precise it could have been set in type.

He was not kneeling now. He was sitting in the requisitioned parlor of the Harrow house on Cordwainer's Row, which smelled faintly of lavender and old arguments, with three candles burning and the parish records spread before him like an accusation he had invited to dinner. Outside, Vellmere slept under curfew, or pretended to. Somewhere a dog was conducting its own private rebellion against the drums, and losing.

He dipped his pen. He wrote:

*Report to the Provincial Commissioner, Ninth District. Re: civic records of Vellmere, audit of same.*

Then he stopped, because honesty stopped him, the way it always did, at the worst possible moment.

The report he *ought* to write was simple. Three lines, even. *Discrepancies detected between master ledger and parish registers. Forged exemption papers suspected; press located; principal actors identified.* He could name the print shop. He could name the old man Crane, fevered and half-dead in his bed above a workshop that smelled of ink and coal smoke. He could, if he wished—and this was the line his pen kept circling like a hawk—name one more.

*Lior Crane, daughter of Aldous Crane, born the ninth of Frostmonth, entered in the baptismal register of Saint Ferrel's, where no daughter should be, because everyone in Vellmere believes Aldous Crane has only a son.*

He had copied that page out twice already. The copy sat in his dispatch case with the seal unbroken, waiting for the morning courier. It would take one signature from him to end everything: the tribunal would convene early, the forty families on Maret's list would hang by week's end, the press would come apart board by board, and Captain Verel would be commended for thoroughness in a province where thoroughness usually meant cruelty performed with good posture.

His pen hovered.

The dog outside gave up on the drums and started on the moon instead.

"Quiet," Verel told it, without heat.

He had been in Vellmere eleven days. Long enough to learn that the bread at the garrison was terrible and the bread from the woman on Tanners' Lane was excellent and that accepting the second while officially enforcing the embargo on the first constituted a moral failure he was willing to live with. Long enough to know that the town's silences were not empty but full—packed tight as a forme of type, every letter in place, waiting for someone to pull the lever. And long enough to understand that he had been sent here not to govern a town but to interrogate it, and that interrogation was a thing he was, inconveniently, rather good at.

Because he knew lies. He had grown up inside one.

---

Verel's mother had died when he was six, and his father had remarried within the year—a brisk, kind woman named Sera who raised him without ever quite pretending he was hers, which he had always respected her for. There had been another child, though. A sister. Two years younger than Edran, quick-footed, loud in a household that prized quiet, with his father's dark eyes and none of his father's caution.

Her name had been Ilse.

He remembered her the way you remember weather: a summer of her laughing at the well, a winter of her coughing, a spring—he was nine—when she simply wasn't at breakfast anymore, and then wasn't anywhere, and no one spoke of it, and by autumn there was no photograph of her on the mantel, and by the following year there was no *Ilse* anywhere at all, not in the family Bible, not in the parish book his father kept copies of, not in his father's mouth.

His father had never explained. His father had gone to his grave without explaining, and Verel had spent thirty years assembling explanations out of scraps: a fever season, perhaps, or something worse, some shame the family had decided to survive by amputation. What he knew for certain was the method. That a life could be removed from the record cleanly, without violence, without scandal, with nothing but silence applied patiently, the way you'd rub out a pencil line. That the record was not a mirror. That the record was a weapon that anyone with a steady hand could point in any direction they liked.

That was why the Commission had hired him, in the end. He was famous—not widely, but in the right narrow circles—for finding the lie in a ledger the way a hound finds blood under snow. Other men audited numbers. Verel audited silences. He looked for the page that had been too clean, the entry that had been too convenient, the name that appeared everywhere until the day it appeared nowhere.

And in Vellmere, he had found exactly what he was looking for, and it had turned around and looked back at him with grey eyes and ink-stained fingers and a face he had been thinking about at entirely improper hours.

---

He set down the pen and rubbed his eyes.

The problem—the actual, practical, career-destroying problem—was not sentiment. He wanted to be very clear with himself about that, sitting alone with his candles like a man taking his own confession. Sentiment was cheap. He had watched sentimental officers die of it in two provinces. No: the problem was that his report contained a conclusion he could not verify, and Verel did not sign conclusions he could not verify. It was the only rule he had never broken.

The conclusion was this: *the apprentice Lio is the daughter Lior.*

Everything supported it. The baptismal record, in Aldous Crane's careful clerk-hand, naming a daughter. The absence of any birth record for a son Tam beyond a single entry that matched no burial. The boy's hands—too fine for a typesetter's hands, no, that was nonsense, they were magnificent typesetter's hands, that was not evidence of anything except excellence. The way the boy had stood in the shop when asked about recent orders and answered each question correctly and completely and with the particular stillness of a person reciting from a memorized text. The way the boy had looked at him on Cordwainer's Row last Tuesday, wearing a courier's uniform two sizes wrong in the shoulders, and had spoken to him for ten minutes about type foundries with a voice pitched just slightly too low, and had known things about Verel's own questions—had *anticipated* them—that no honest apprentice should have been able to anticipate.

And then there was the records office. Four days ago. The borrowed officer standing at his own counter pulling files Verel himself had catalogued, and Verel standing three feet away knowing—*knowing*, the way you know rain is coming, in the teeth and the old scar on his knee—and saying nothing, and signing nothing, and walking the young officer to the door like a courteous host.

He had told himself afterward that he'd been gathering proof. Building the case properly. Letting the fish run with the line.

He had never once believed himself.

* * *

There was a knock at the parlor door—one soft double-tap, apologetic even in knuckle-language. Sergeant Brannic put his head in. Brannic was a large, gentle man built along the lines of a grain barn, with the soul of a sheepdog and the political instincts of one as well.

"Begging pardon, sir. Courier came early. Wants to know if there's dispatch for the morning run." He squinted at the table, the registers, the untouched report. "You've been at that since supper."

"I'm aware."

"Only—" Brannic shifted his weight, floorboards complaining beneath him like a ship. "Only the men are saying things, sir. In the barracks. About the tribunal lists. Forty names, they're saying. And Kessel's boys are taking bets on which families, which I don't care for, sir, if I'm honest, and neither does half the barracks, and—" He caught himself, straightened. "Is there dispatch or isn't there?"

Verel looked at the dispatch case. The seal sat on it, unbroken, patient as a trap.

"No dispatch tonight," he said. "Tell the courier he leaves at dawn whether I've written or not."

Brannic's eyebrows climbed a little way toward his cap and then thought better of the journey. "Sir." He paused at the door. "If it's not my place—which it isn't—but the baker's wife says the Crane girl—that is, the Crane *lad*, the apprentice—brought the old man's fever medicine round to three houses this week off the garrison allotment, and nobody ordered that done, sir. Nobody official." He cleared his throat. "Good night, Captain."

The door closed. Verel sat looking at it for a long moment.

*Nobody ordered that done.* Which meant the apprentice had stolen from imperial stores to dose imperial subjects, and the sergeant knew, and had reported it anyway, in the tone of a man reporting good news.

His whole command was going soft. Or—more troubling—going honest.

* * *

He took out the copied page again. Not the dispatch case; the loose copy, the working draft, the one with his annotations in the margins like a scholar's sins.

*Baptismal register, Saint Ferrel's, ninth of Frostmonth, fourteen years past:* **Lior Crane, daughter of Aldous Crane and Maren Crane (née Vasch).** Sponsors listed. Date certain. Ink period-correct—he had held it to the candle and checked, because he checked everything, it was the curse of his life. The entry was real. The girl had existed. She had been baptized, named, welcomed into the town's memory in front of God and the parish clerk.

And then she had vanished from every subsequent record as thoroughly as his sister Ilse had vanished from his father's Bible.

Except she hadn't vanished. That was the elegant, maddening, beautiful thing about it. She had been *transposed*. One name swapped for another, one life continued under a dead boy's name, and the whole town had agreed—must have agreed, must have *known*, towns always know—to keep the secret. Seven years of a whole community holding one silence between them like a plate of glass.

He understood the mechanics perfectly. He had to admit that part of him admired the mechanics.

What he could not stop examining was the *why*, and the why had teeth. Because there were only two reasons to erase a girl and install a son in a print shop, and one of them was inheritance—an apprenticeship, a trade, a livelihood that would die with a daughter—and the other was conscription, and both of those reasons were the empire's fault, were *his* fault in the sense that he wore its uniform, and he found he could not pick up his pen to finish the sentence *principal actor identified* without hearing his father's voice saying, in that mild terrible tone, *some things are kinder left out of the book, Edran,* on the morning Ilse's name went out of the Bible.

Some things are kinder left out of the book.

It had taken him twenty years to understand what a monstrous sentence that was. His father had not been protecting Ilse. He had been protecting *himself*—his comfort, his tidy grief, his right to never explain anything to his son. The silence had not been mercy. It had been ownership. His father had owned the family story the way a man owns a field, and had fenced off whatever portion he didn't wish to look at, and called the fence love.

And now here was Verel, holding a page that could erase a living person as efficiently as his father had erased a dead one. Here he sat with the power to write *Lior Crane, daughter* into an imperial report and thereby convert a seven-year act of survival into a crime, a hanging offense, a footnote in somebody's tribunal summary. Truth-as-weapon. His father's truth. The Commission's truth. The truth that wins commendations.

Or.

* * *

He got up and walked to the window. The moon over Vellmere was nearly full and stupidly beautiful, laying silver across the river roofs, and somewhere out there—on Tanner's Lane, or the paper quarter, or wherever she was sleeping tonight, if she was sleeping—was a person who had looked at him across a counter in a stolen uniform and let him see everything except the one thing, and had done it with such craft, such *artistry*, that he had walked home feeling less like a fooled man than like a man shown a masterpiece he wasn't supposed to touch.

He thought about the ten minutes on Cordwainer's Row. She had talked about typefaces—the difference between a cut made for beauty and a cut made for endurance—with her whole face lit up like a shop window, and for those ten minutes neither of them had been lying, and he had felt more honestly conversed-with than he had in a year of mess dinners. Then the moment had closed and the lies had come back on both sides, politely, like shutters.

*Each hiding something,* he had thought that night. *Each recognizing something.*

He had recognized, in fact, exactly one thing, and he had not let himself name it until now, standing at the window with the copied page in his hand. He had recognized a person keeping a silence that was not theirs to keep alone—a silence being kept *for* someone else, at cost, daily, without applause. He knew the weight of that. He had carried his father's silence for thirty years, and it had bent him into precisely the shape of man the Commission prizes: a man who hunts other people's secrets because he was never allowed to ask about his own.

The dog had finally given up on the moon. Good.

Verel went back to the table. He took the dispatch case, opened it, and read his own draft one final time, all three lines of it, thorough and damning and true.

Then he took the parish page—the original, the one with the baptism in Aldous Crane's clerk-hand, *Lior Crane, daughter*, dated fourteen years past—and he carried it to the hearth, where the fire had burned down to a bed of orange patience, and he held it a moment longer than he needed to.

"Ilse," he said quietly, to the room, to the fire, to whichever of them was listening. "I don't know if you'd want this. I never got to ask you anything."

He let it go.

The page caught, curled, brightened, and was gone in four seconds—a girl's whole recorded existence, ash in a grate. He watched it the way you watch a funeral: not because you believe the person is in the coffin, but because someone ought to stand there.

Truth-as-mercy. It was a phrase he invented on the spot, standing there, because he needed something to call what he'd just done and *cowardice* didn't fit and *kindness* fit too well. Let the philosophers sort it out. He had a war to lose gracefully.

He returned to the desk, took a fresh sheet, and began the report he could actually sign:

*Audit of Vellmere civic records continues. Discrepancies noted in ledger pagination suggest clerical error during prior administration; further verification against tribunal schedules required before findings may be finalized. Recommend extension of audit period.*

Clerical error. It was the flimsiest document he had ever produced, and he wrote it in his most beautiful hand, and he hated it, and he signed it.

* * *

He was sealing it when the first shout went up—from the direction of the river, thin and wrong, the kind of shout that has fire behind it. Then a bell began, not the chapel bell but the paper quarter's alarm, ragged and urgent, and boots were running in the street below, and Brannic's fist hit the door hard enough to crack the frame.

"Captain! The quarter's burning—they're firing the warehouses—retreating column, torches, it's coming up Cordwainer's Row—sir, the *print shop*—"

Verel was already moving, coat half-on, pistol belt in his hand, the sealed lie abandoned on the desk behind him glowing faintly in the candlelight.

And one thought rode ahead of him down the stairs, faster than any order he shouted after it:

*Aldous Crane will go back for the ledger. He'll go back into the burning shop for his father's book, and the apprentice will follow him, and God help us all, so will I.*
