## 18. Fire in the Paper Quarter

The smoke came before the bells.

Lior smelled it first as a sweetness in the wind off the river — not woodsmoke, which she knew the way she knew her own hands, but something richer, resinous, wrong. Paper smoke. A quarter's worth of paper smoke.

She was standing at the press with ink to her elbows, halfway through pulling the thirty-first sheet of forty, when the sweetness drifted through the open loading door and settled over everything like a shroud laid gently on a sleeping face.

"Maret," she said quietly.

Maret looked up from the stack of finished exemptions she was counting for the third time, lips moving under her breath with each name. She'd been counting all evening. Forty names, forty families, and Maret counted them the way other people prayed.

"I smell it," Maret said. "Been smelling it ten minutes. Told myself it was the tannery."

"The tannery doesn't smell like that."

"No," Maret said. "It doesn't." She set down the stack with terrible care, as though the papers might bruise, and went to the window overlooking Cordwainer Row.

What she saw made her go very still, and Lior had learned — seven years of learning — that stillness in Maret Sable was worse than screaming.

"They're firing the chandlers' shops first," Maret said. "Retreating. The column broke at the bridge an hour ago — I had it from the fishwives — and now they're burning what they can't carry." She turned. "They'll come down this way. Paper Quarter burns fastest. It always has."

Lior looked at the press. Her father's press, her grandfather's press, hauled up three flights of stairs by six men and a team of mules before either of them was born, its iron frame worn smooth at every place four generations of hands had gripped it. The forme was locked in the chase. Thirty-one sheets hung drying on the lines overhead, forty names apiece, exemption papers good enough to fool a tribunal because she had cut the plate herself, by lamplight, better than anything she would ever make again.

Thirty-one of forty.

"Nine more," she said.

"Lior—"

"Nine more pulls. Ten minutes. Maybe less if my arms hold."

Maret crossed the room and took Lior by the shoulders, and there was ash already in the air between them, fine and gray, drifting in like snow that had lost its way. "Listen to me. Your father—"

"Is delirious upstairs and cannot walk downstairs without falling."

"—is upstairs, and the fire will be here before you finish your nine pulls, and I am asking you, girl, as one woman who owes her life to another: what is worth more? Him or it?"

The question landed in Lior's chest like a dropped type case — all the letters scattering at once, none of them legible.

She did not answer. There was no answer yet. She only said, "Get him ready. Bundle him however you have to. If he fights you, tell him—" and stopped, because there was nothing to tell him. Aldous Crane could not be argued out of the ledger. He had slept beside it for thirty years the way other men slept beside their wives.

"He won't leave without it," Maret said flatly.

"I know."

"And you won't leave without the run."

"I know that too."

Maret held her gaze a moment longer, then released her shoulders and went up the stairs two at a time, calling Aldous's name in the bright, commanding voice she used on stubborn customers, and Lior turned back to the press and pulled sheet thirty-two.

* * *

There is a strange calm that comes over a person when the world ends on a schedule. Lior felt it settle over her like her father's old apron — heavy, familiar, absurdly comforting. The smoke thickened. Somewhere on Tanner's Lane glass burst in a bright scatter. The bells began, all of them at once, the alarm peal Vellmere hadn't rung since the flood of '09, and beneath it the drums of the retreating column beat their indifferent retreat toward the bridge.

Lior pulled sheets. Thirty-three. Thirty-four. Her forearms burned; she had been printing for two days on stolen sleep and cold tea, and the platen seemed heavier with every pull, as though the press itself were reluctant, as though it knew.

"You're doing fine," she told it. "We both are."

Talking to the press was an old habit, one of the honest ones. The press never lied back. It only answered in the language of pressure and impression: *too much, too little, just right.* No other conversation in her life had ever been so simple.

Thirty-five.

The light changed. That was how she knew it had jumped — not crept but *jumped*, the way fire does once it finds a gap between buildings and decides it likes the view. The amber glow beyond the window went orange, then a white-edged gold that threw the hanging sheets into sharp relief, forty little ghosts swaying on their lines.

Thirty-six. She hung it. Thirty-seven —

The stair door banged open and heat came in ahead of Maret like a herald.

"It's in the dye-house," Maret shouted over the bells. "Two doors down. We have minutes, Lior. Minutes." Behind her, half-carried, half-dragged, came Aldous Crane in his nightshirt with a blanket around his shoulders, his face gray and slick, his eyes unfocused — and then, horribly, focusing, finding the ledger shelf across the room the way a compass needle finds north even in a fever.

"No," he said. It was barely a voice. "No, no, the book —"

"Papa—" Lior started, and he was moving, actually moving, shrugging off Maret's grip with the sudden terrible strength of the dying and the devoted, crossing the floor in his bare feet toward the vault where the master ledger sat in its cradle of oilcloth, half-burned already in spirit, whole in fact, every name and debt and secret in Vellmere bound in calfskin.

Aldous reached the shelf and got his arms around the book and could not lift it. His strength left him all at once, like water through a cracked jug, and he slid down the shelving with the ledger in his lap, clutching it, breathing in wet rattles.

"Aldous." Maret knelt by him. "Aldous, we must go *now*."

"My father kept it through the last war," he whispered. His eyes found Lior, and for one moment the fever cleared and she saw her father entire — the man who had taught her invisibility as a craft and forgery as mercy and silence as love, all of it braided together so tight she might never untangle which strand was which. "Through the occupation. Through the burning of the customs house. It isn't paper, Lior. It's everyone we ever —"

"It's a weapon," Lior said. "You know it's a weapon. We argued this. Maret's right."

His hand stroked the scorched calfskin. "It's also the only proof of who owed whom. When the tribunal comes, when they ask who collaborated — this book answers honestly. It always has. That's all I ever wanted anything in this shop to do." He coughed, long and awful. "Answer honestly."

The window glass cracked. Not burst — cracked, a slow spiderwebbing, and the sound of the fire changed key, became a roar instead of a rush, and hot wind shoved through the broken pane carrying cinders like furious fireflies.

"Maret." Lior heard her own voice come out level and strange, someone else's voice, an officer's voice, a printer's voice. "Take him out the loading door, down the alley, to the cooperage. Don't wait for me."

"Absolutely not—"

"That's not a request, Maret. You've spent three weeks telling me what courage costs. Here's mine." She pointed at the press. "I have three sheets left. Three. I am not letting forty families hang because I ran out of stubbornness nine pulls early."

Maret looked at her — really looked, the way Verel had once looked at her type work, seeing the skill and not the secret — and something passed over her face that was grief and pride and fury all pressed into one impression.

"Two minutes," Maret said. "Then I'm sending the cooper's sons in after you whether you're done or not." She hauled Aldous upright, got her shoulder under his arm. He twisted toward the ledger, fingers hooked on its edge, and for one suspended moment the whole scene hung like a forme in a chase: father and book and fire and daughter and press, every element locked, waiting for the pull.

"Leave it, Papa," Lior said. "Please. Leave it. I'll carry what matters."

He stared at her. And then — she would think about this moment for the rest of her life, turning it over like a piece of misprinted type — he let go. His fingers uncurled from the calfskin one by one, and Maret dragged him toward the loading door, and the last thing he said before the smoke took him was:

"*Both*, then! Both things matter!" — and he was gone into the alley, coughing, arguing, alive.

* * *

Thirty-eight.

Her hands shook so badly she nearly spoiled the pull. She braced, breathed the way Aldous had taught her — *the press rewards patience, Lio, it punishes panic, it is the most honest teacher you will ever have* — and drew the bar smooth and even. The impression came up clean. Forty names. Another family's morning saved.

She hung it. Took the next sheet from the stack. The heat at her back was a living thing now, breathing on her neck, and the drying sheets above her head had begun to curl at their edges like leaves in autumn, and somewhere very close a beam groaned the deep wooden groan of a thing deciding to die.

Thirty-nine.

In the middle of the pull the thought arrived, unbidden and ice-cold amid all that heat: *you are going to die in this room, and for what? Forged papers. Lies, beautifully printed.*

And the second thought, arriving with the bar, with the impression, with the clean bite of type into damp paper: *no. For mercy, beautifully printed. There's a difference. Papa taught me the difference. The difference is everything.*

She hung thirty-nine. One left.

The ceiling over the type racks caught — a lick of flame running along the joists like ink wicked along a blotter — and Lior did the arithmetic she had been refusing to do all night. The press could not be saved. Four hundred pounds of iron, three flights of narrow stairs, a firestorm behind her. Her grandfather's press, her father's press, the great honest heart of the shop that had raised her: it was going to burn, tonight, with everything else.

But the plate was small. Copper, eight by eleven inches, her finest cutting, wrapped in oilcloth in the flat drawer. And the forme — no, the forme was locked in the chase, too heavy, too late.

Plate. Ledger. Father already out. Nine pulls done — thirty-nine done.

One left.

She pulled forty.

It was the best pull of her life. She would swear it later with a straight face and mean it: the pressure perfect, the register true, all forty names crisp as coin-strikes, the printer's fist mark at the bottom small and proud and hers. She hung it with hands that no longer shook at all, and stood one second looking up at the little row of ghosts — forty families, forty futures, swinging gently in the furnace wind — and then she opened the flat drawer, took the plate, and turned for the ledger.

Which was when she saw the beam come down.

It fell across the far corner, where the vault shelf stood, and the shelf went over with a crash, and the master ledger — her grandfather's ledger, her father's whole heart, the book of every name in Vellmere — slid free of its cradle and landed open on the burning floor.

Lior ran.

Later she would remember almost nothing of those four steps: only heat like a wall, only the floor breathing fire, only the weight of the copper plate against her ribs and the book lying open in the flames, pages lifting and curling, names going up in bright birds of ember. She snatched it up — it was hot, the cover searing her palms through the thin cloth she'd wrapped them in, and she did not care, would carry the blisters for weeks and count them fair price — and she crushed it against her chest with the plate, book and plate and girl, and spun for the loading door.

The alley was a canyon of orange. Maret stood at the far end silhouetted against the cooperage lights, shouting her name, and the cooper's sons were already coming back for her with a wet blanket and no fear worth mentioning, and Lior ran toward them through the falling sparks of everything her family had built over sixty years, and she was laughing — actually laughing, breathless, wild — because the run was complete.

Forty of forty.

Behind her the press gave a great dying groan, and she did not look back. Some things you let burn so that other things survive. She was only beginning, holding her father's inheritance in one arm and her own finest lie in the other, to understand which was which.

* * *

The cooperage yard was chaos organized by Maret into something merely desperate. Families from the Row, wet blankets, buckets passed hand to hand, and in the corner on a pile of sacking sat Aldous Crane, wrapped in every spare coat the coopers owned, gray as ash himself but breathing — breathing, and watching the fire with the expression of a man attending his own funeral and finding the eulogy inaccurate.

"You went back," he rasped when Lior knelt beside him.

"For you?" She set the ledger in his lap, gently, though her palms screamed. "You walked out on your own feet, Papa. Barely."

"Not for me." His cracked lips moved toward something like a smile. "For the book. You went back for the book." His hand trembled on the scorched cover. "After everything — after Maret and me arguing, after what it cost us —"

"I went back for the run first," Lior said. "The book was an afterthought."

It wasn't true, quite, and by the flicker in his eyes he knew it wasn't true, and she let him keep the flattery anyway. There were silences that kept people safe, and there were small lies that kept them warm, and she was, after all, her father's daughter.

Maret dropped down beside them, soot-striped, wheezing. "Forty?"

"Hung and drying until the roof went. Then carried out under my coat." Lior patted the oilcloth bundle inside her jacket, where the plate rode over her heart like a second, harder heartbeat. "Every name."

Maret closed her eyes. For a moment the laundress, the resistance contact, the woman who counted names like prayers simply *breathed* — and then her eyes snapped open and fixed on the ledger in Aldous's lap, half-blackened, one corner charred to a curl, and her expression went from relief to resolve in the space of a heartbeat.

"The tribunal convenes at dawn," Maret said quietly. "And Verel is still in town. Half the column retreated, but not him — the fishwives saw his quarters lit an hour ago. Which means the man who reads everything is about to read a town full of ashes, and the first thing he'll ask anyone standing in this yard is *what did you save?*" She held out her hand. Not grabbing. Offering. "Give it to me, Aldous. Tonight, while there's still a river to drown it in. Please. Before it becomes the thing that kills us all."

Aldous's arms tightened around the book.

And Lior — soot-blackened, blistered, forty sheets safe and her father alive and her family's press a glowing skeleton two streets away — looked from Maret's open hand to her father's white knuckles to the ruined calfskin binding, and felt the question of her whole life finally arrive at the press bar of her own chest, locked in the chase, waiting for the pull.

"Papa," she said. "Let me see it first. Before anyone decides anything — let me see what the fire left."

Aldous looked at her. Slowly, wincing, he loosened his arms and tipped the ledger open to its middle, and the wind turned a page for them — and there, half-eaten by flame but legible, in her father's own careful hand, was the final page.

Her name. *Lior Crane, daughter.* Marked with the fist.

And coming up Cordwainer Row through the smoke, unhurried, alone, lantern in hand, came Captain Verel.
