## 19. The Last Impression

The smoke came before the bells.

Lior smelled it first as a sweetness in the coal smoke—paper burning somewhere it shouldn't be, ink and rag stock going up like a sacrifice—and then the bells began, all of them at once, which meant not a fire but a surrender. A town that had been occupied for a season knew the difference between one bell and every bell. Every bell meant the soldiers were leaving, and soldiers who left Vellmere in a hurry did not leave it tidy.

She was at the press with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and forty-one sheets of wet exemption paper hanging on the drying lines above her head like strange pale laundry, when the first window across the lane glowed orange.

"Father," she said, though Aldous was three rooms away and delirious, propped on pallets in the back office where Maret's people had carried him an hour ago, still muttering ledger entries to nobody. "Father, get up."

The press didn't care about fires. The press never cared about anything; that was what Lior loved about it. It wanted its platen closed, its forme locked, its paper fed true, and if she gave it those things it would give her back the only honest sentence in Vellmere: *This man is exempt from conscription by lawful authority,* over and over, forty times, each copy bearing a name that would keep a neck out of a noose. Thirty-eight sheets hung drying. Two more pulls remained.

Two more pulls, and beams falling in the street.

She could hear the soldiers now—not marching but running, boots scattering like dropped coins—and the crackle from the paper quarter was climbing from a whisper into a conversation. The granary was burning. The chandlery was burning. And the wind, the traitor wind off the river, was pushing it all toward Crane & Son, Printers, which had been Crane & Son for thirty years and Crane & Daughter for exactly none of them officially.

Lior set the next sheet.

Her hands were steady. She noticed this with a kind of distant pride, the way you notice weather in someone else's country. Seven years of learning to keep her breathing even while imperial officers read over her shoulder had apparently left her with hands that refused to panic even when the roof might come down. *Good,* said some cold little clerk in her chest. *Useful.* And underneath that clerk, warmer and much less useful: *You are pulling proofs in a burning building because your father taught you that a job left half-done is a lie told twice.*

The platen closed. The screw turned. The impression kissed the paper, and she lifted the thirty-ninth sheet of Maret Sarn's exemption, and it was perfect—the plate she'd cut by lamplight while her father burned with fever, the finest work of her life, every hairline of the seal crisp as frost. She hung it on the line. Her fingers shook only afterward, which she decided counted as dignity.

Then the door burst open and Maret came through it sideways, soot-striped, hauling a bucket in each hand like a woman carrying groceries through a mild rainstorm instead of the end of the world.

"The granary's gone," Maret announced, as though reporting on a neighbor's hedge. "Chandlery's going. They fired the whole row on their way out—petty little devils, they couldn't take it so they'd rather we eat ash." She took in the scene: the drying lines, the press, the girl standing over it with ink to her wrists. "How many?"

"Thirty-nine done. Two pulls left."

"Then pull them."

"There's a fire," Lior said, gesturing at the window with some emphasis, "the size of a cathedral."

"And there are two names on that plate who'll hang Thursday if their papers don't exist. Pull them. I've brought four men and a tide of neighbors behind me; this shop will be defended like a fortress or buried like a king. Your choice is just whether you're printing when it happens." Maret set down her buckets, seized a wet blanket, and started beating embers off the shutters with the enthusiasm of a woman who had spent thirty years fighting laundries and considered fire merely a larger stain. "Go!"

Lior went back to the press.

* * *

The fortieth sheet came off clean. She hung it, and stood there a moment with her hand flat on the last blank page of the stack, feeling the paper drink the damp air, and thought: *one more*. One more pull and the run was done and forty families were saved and everything after that was somebody else's problem, ideally somebody with buckets.

That was when the ceiling beam over the drying rack cracked like a pistol shot.

It didn't fall. It groaned, and leaned, and shed a snowfall of burning lath onto the far corner where the finished stock was stored, and the fire, which until now had been a rumor at the windows, became a tenant. Lior grabbed the drying lines' frame—light, portable, forty sheets of salvation swaying like washing in a gale—and hauled it toward the yard door, and it was heavier than it looked and she was smaller than she pretended, and she got perhaps six feet before her shoulder met something immovable and furious.

Aldous.

He should have been unconscious. He was mostly unconscious, in the way of fevered men who rise for exactly one purpose and will accept no substitutes. His eyes were open and unfocused, fixed on the middle distance where, presumably, the vault stood, and his voice came out cracked as old type.

"The ledger," he said. "Lio. The ledger's still in the vault."

"I know."

"It's my father's book." He gripped her arm with a strength that had no business living in him anymore. "Every name. Every entry since my grandfather cut his first fount. You can't let it burn, Lio, it's—it's *us*—"

Behind him, the beam chose that moment to finish its deliberations and come down through the roof of the front room, and the sound it made was the sound of the argument ending. Not because he conceded. Because there was suddenly no time for anyone to concede anything.

Maret's people came through the yard door like a rescue performed by a very determined tide. Two of them took Aldous under the arms—he fought them, actually fought them, a sixty-year-old printer with a fever landing a blow on a blacksmith—and Maret herself planted herself in front of Lior, soot-faced, and shouted directly into her ear over the roar:

"Your father goes with us NOW. The book is WOOD AND RAG, girl, it's dead men's ink—"

"It's forty names," Lior shouted back, "including ones that aren't on the exemptions, including *mine*—" and then stopped, because that was a sentence for another chapter of her life, and Maret's eyes went sharp as a knife being sharpened on the moment, and there was no time for that either.

"Choose," Maret said, quieter, in the terrible pause between crashes. "Him or the book. You can't carry both and the building won't wait for your arithmetic."

And that was the moment Lior Crane had been rehearsing for without knowing it, seven years of silences compressing into one second of speech.

She looked at her father being dragged into the yard, still mouthing *my father's book*, and loved him so hard it felt like injury. Then she looked at the vault door, glowing at the edges.

"Take him," she said. "I'm faster than all of you and I know where everything is."

* * *

The vault was a stone room built by a grandfather who had been, in Lior's professional opinion, paranoid in a way the family had never sufficiently appreciated. The door was iron. Inside, the air was hot but breathable, lit orange through the vent slit, and the master ledger lay on its shelf where it had lain for eighty years, fat as a Bible, patient as one.

The heat came off the walls in waves. Above her, the shop floor was a drum of flame, and she could hear the press—her press, the old Columbian, her one true conversation partner these two nights—groan as something collapsed near it, and she made herself not think about that yet. Grief could be scheduled. Paper could not.

She wrapped the ledger in the leather apron off the hook by the door—Aldous's good apron, the one he wore for customers, which seemed fitting—and hugged it to her chest, and it weighed what it had always weighed, maybe twelve pounds, and it weighed everything, which was different.

On the way out she passed the forme of wedding invitations that had hidden her forgery plate through the soldier's search, sitting on the stone bench where it had waited through all of this like a loyal dog. The plate was inside it, locked in the chase. She hesitated—one heartbeat, two—with fire raining through the floor above, and then she picked it up too, because the plate was her own work and she found she could burn her father's inheritance but not her own hands' proof that she existed.

The yard door was twenty feet away. The floor between her and it was beginning to glow in patches, like a face coming down with fever.

Lior ran.

* * *

Outside, the street was full of the town, and the town was full of shouting, and the sky over the paper quarter was the color of a forge gone wrong. Somebody threw water on her. Several somebodies. She stood in the middle of the lane dripping, clutching a leather bundle to her chest like the world's least glamorous Madonna, and watched Crane & Son, Printers, breathe its last.

It died well, as shops go. The sign held on longer than expected—*CRANE & SON* in gilt letters her grandfather had paid too much for—and then it swung loose and fell and landed in the mud face-down, which Lior chose to interpret as modesty rather than omen. The windows went. The roof went, in stages, each stage producing a fresh chorus of gasps from the crowd and a fresh volley of curses from Maret, who had organized a bucket line with military precision and was losing to physics.

They got Aldous clear. That was the arithmetic that mattered, and she checked it over and over like a compositor checking a line: father, alive, coughing, furious, alive. Forty sheets, safe, hung on the drying frame that two teenagers were holding upright in the churchyard like a banner. Forgery plate, under her arm. Ledger—

The ledger was against her chest, and it was warm. Too warm.

She unwrapped the apron right there in the mud and found the damage. Fire had gotten into the vault somehow—through the vent, through the floor—and it had taken the ledger's edges. The bottom corner of the whole book was charred to a black crust, pages fused into a single coal, and when she touched it gently, flakes came away like burnt moth wings. How many entries lived in that corner? Births? Debts? Some poor family's entire recorded existence, gone to carbon?

But the spine held. The greater part of the book held. She opened it with hands that trembled properly now that there was nothing to operate, and the names marched on, page after page, scorched at the borders but legible, the whole stubborn census of Vellmere surviving like a town that had taken fire on one side and kept its records anyway.

"Well," said Aldous.

She hadn't heard him come up. He was upright, barely, propped between two of Maret's men, gray as unprinted paper, his nightshirt scorched at the hem and his dignity entirely intact. He was looking at the shop. Then at the ledger. Then at her.

"You went back for it," he said.

"You went back for it first. I just did it better."

Something crossed his face—grief for the shop, pride in her, the fever still burning underneath all of it like a banked coal—and settled into a look she'd never seen him wear: a man seeing clearly, briefly, the cost of the thing he'd asked for. "Half of it's gone," he said hoarsely. "My grandfather's entries. My father's. The first twenty years of us."

"No." Lior turned the book so he could see the spine, the bulk, the mass of it. "Half a corner's gone. Most of it's here. Enough to know everybody's real name." She heard herself say it and felt the ground tilt pleasantly under her feet. "All of our real names."

Aldous looked at her for a long moment. In the light of his own burning workshop, fever-bright, he almost smiled. "That," he said, "sounds like a confession."

"It's a broadside," Lior said. "I haven't set it yet."

* * *

They regrouped in the churchyard, which was the only stone-heavy ground left standing, and the town gathered around them the way water gathers around a dropped stone—slowly, then all at once. Maret counted heads. The forty exemption sheets fluttered on their line between two gravestones, drying in the heat-shimmer, looking absurd and holy, like flags of a country that consisted of forty families and one liar-turned-printer.

Lior sat on a tombstone with the ledger in her lap and the plate under her arm and let herself, for exactly one minute, feel it all. The shop was ash. The press was ash—she'd seen the Columbian's iron frame glowing through the doorway as she ran, and she would mourn it properly later, alone, the way you mourn a person you talked to more than most people. Her father was alive and sick. Forty names were saved. Her own name was still a secret, folded up small in a book that half the town was eyeing like bread in a famine year.

Because they were eyeing it. She could feel it. The freed town of Vellmere had spent an evening watching its occupiers burn its property, and now it stood in the churchyard smelling smoke and wanting justice, and justice, as everyone knew, was kept in ledgers.

A man near the gate—Tobin the cooper, whose debts she'd read in that very book—was saying loudly to his neighbor, "That's the master ledger, that is. That's every collaborator's name in Vellmere. We should have it. It belongs to the town now."

"It belongs to whoever reads it," said his neighbor, in a tone that made several people move slightly apart.

Maret appeared at Lior's elbow, arms crossed, soot to the elbows, watching the crowd watch the book. "Tomorrow," she said quietly, "that mob decides whether that book is a sword or a bonfire, and either way it decides without asking you. Tonight you should decide what you want it to be."

Lior looked down at the ledger. Half-burned. Half-whole. Her grandfather's hand in the early pages, her father's in the later ones, and hers—in a manner of speaking—on the final leaf, where *Lior Crane, daughter* waited in her father's careful italic like a fuse laid years ago and finally, finally reached.

She had pulled forty perfect impressions tonight. She knew exactly how many remained in her.

"A broadside," she said. "I'm going to read every name in it aloud. Right here. Starting tomorrow at dawn, or tonight if the crowd doesn't disperse." She stood, hugging the book, and found the churchyard had gone quiet around her, the way rooms go quiet when a press starts. "Every debt. Every birth. Every bribe my father ever recorded and every name he ever saved—including the one he wrote last. If the town wants its truth, it can have all of it, out loud, where nobody can forge it, hide it, or burn the corners off."

Maret studied her for a long moment, and then, slowly, began to grin like a woman watching a very good print come off a very bad day.

"Dawn," Maret said. "I'll bring the whole square. And Crane—" her eyes flicked, deliberate, to the ledger's last page, then back up, and she said nothing else, which from Maret was a signed declaration.

Above the smoking ruin of the paper quarter, the sun came up on the ashes, and Lior opened the book to the first page, and began.
