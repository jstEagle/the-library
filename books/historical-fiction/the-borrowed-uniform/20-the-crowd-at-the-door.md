## 20. The Crowd at the Door

The smoke had not finished deciding whether it was going to kill anyone before the town decided it was free.

Lior stood in the square with the half-burned ledger in her arms and watched Vellmere remember itself. It happened in pieces, the way a press gathers speed: first a shutter thrown open above the chandler's, then a voice—Maret's voice, cracked and enormous—crying that the tribunal was broken, that the lists were burned, that no one would hang tomorrow or ever. Then the bells. Someone had reached the bell tower, someone with scorched hands and a fool's courage, and the old Vellish peal came down over the rooftops like rain after a drought, so familiar that Lior's body answered it before her mind did: seven years of Sundays, of funerals, of market mornings when she had been Tam Crane and nobody had looked twice.

People came out of doorways as if the doorways had been holding them shut for a month. The cooper's family. The widow Ansel with her two girls. Old Pem who had not left his cellar since the requisition. They came blinking into the ash-fall light and looked at one another, and then, all together, as if pulled by the same rope, they looked at her—at the print shop girl standing in the square holding the master ledger against her chest like a shield that had already failed once.

The book was heavier than lead type. It was heavier than it had ever been, and it was half char, its spine cracked down the middle where Aldous had wrenched it from the burning shelf, its last third swollen with water Maret's people had thrown over him and the floor and everything else. It smelled of smoke and river and ink, and Lior could feel through the cover the faint irregular heartbeat of pages gone wavy with heat.

"Lior." Maret appeared at her elbow, laundry-red hands, a burn blistered along one forearm that she had not yet noticed she had. Her eyes went to the ledger and something moved across her face—not hunger exactly, but the look of a woman measuring a load she intended to carry herself. "You should give that to me. Before they decide what they want."

"Before who decides?"

"Before everyone does," Maret said. "Because everyone is about to."

* * *

They decided quickly.

It began with the cooper, Bren Ostrow, a mountain of a man with sawdust still in his hair, who had spent four weeks polishing boots for a sergeant because the alternative had been his son on a tribunal list. He crossed the square with his family behind him like a tide behind a boat, and he stopped an arm's length away, and he said, in the reasonable voice of a reasonable man pushed past reason:

"That's the book."

"It's the book," Lior said.

"It has every name in it. Every debt. Every man who signed the billeting rolls, every woman who took imperial coin to wash their shirts, every—" He stopped, and his jaw worked. "Every collaborator. Every traitor. My name's in there, girl. What I did to keep my boy alive. You know what's in there about me."

"I know what's in there about everyone," Lior said. "That's rather the problem."

Bren looked at her as if she had spoken in tongues. Behind him, the crowd had thickened—the whole street now, and the lane beyond it, faces she had set type beside for seven years without ever quite seeing them. She knew them by their debts instead. That was the ledger's trick, she understood suddenly; that was why her grandfather had kept every entry in the same fair hand, why Aldous had guarded it like a second heart. A town that knows itself by its accounts will do anything to be the one holding the account book.

"They'll want it for the trials," said a voice—Tamsin Reeve, the ferryman's wife, whose brother had been the first name read at the tribunal that never happened. "When the province sends judges back. Proof. Evidence of who served them and who didn't."

"And who decides 'who didn't'?" Lior asked. "The book? Or the people reading it?"

"The book doesn't lie," Tamsin said. "That's the point of it."

"No," Lior agreed. "It doesn't lie. It just only tells one kind of truth, and it tells it in my grandfather's handwriting, and it has never once asked anyone's permission." She shifted her grip on the ledger. Somewhere under her ribs, her father's voice was saying *invisibility is a craft, Lio*, and Maret's voice was saying *burn it, no one can ever be sold out again*, and both of them were wrong, and both of them were right, and neither of them was here, because Aldous was lying on a pallet in Maret's back room coughing smoke while three women argued over whether to bleed him.

"Give it here," Bren said, and held out his hands.

And the crowd, which had been a crowd of neighbors, became a crowd.

She felt it happen. It was a thing she could feel the way she could feel a forme go loose in the chase—a small slippage, a shift of weight, forty people becoming one animal with one want. Not cruelty. She wanted to be clear about that, even in the moment, even with her heart going hard against the scorched cover. It wasn't cruelty pressing toward her across the cobbles. It was fear wearing justice's coat, which was the most dangerous clothing there was. They had spent a month being names on lists. Now they had a chance to hold the pen, and God forgive them, they wanted to hold the pen.

"You don't understand what you're asking," she said.

"We understand fine," said Ostrow's wife, Hesta, and her voice was shaking. "We're asking for the truth."

"You're asking for *your* truth," Lior said. "The page with your husband's name. And that man wants the page with his neighbor's. And she wants the one with the miller's. Nobody is asking for the book. Everybody is asking for a knife with a handle shaped like a name."

"Careful," someone said, low.

"No," Lior said, and heard her own voice come out in the register she used for customers who tried to haggle over funeral broadsides. "I've been careful for seven years. I have been the most careful person in this town, and I am finished with it. You want the truth? Here is some truth. Your husband polished boots, Mistress Ostrow, and so did eleven other men, and the ledger records it because my grandfather recorded everything, including the bribes he took himself—including the money he moved through hidden accounts to buy forged papers for forty families, yours among them, paid for out of fines levied against men whose names are also in this book. So tell me which page is the truth. Tell me which line makes your husband innocent. Pick it. Go on. I'll cut the plate myself."

Silence. The ash drifted. Somewhere a child was crying and being hushed, hushed, hushed—the sound of seven years compressed into one mother's palm.

"She's mad," Tamsin Reeve said. "The smoke's got into her."

"The smoke's got into all of us," Lior said. "The question is what we build out of the clearing." She lifted the ledger higher, and her arms were trembling, and she let them tremble where everyone could see. "My father nearly died coming back into a burning building for this. Do you know why? Not because it's precious. Because he couldn't bear for it to end. It's his inheritance. It's how his father owned this town without ever lifting anything heavier than a pen. And I am telling you—if you take it from me now, if you make it a weapon against your neighbors, then the empire leaves and the book stays, and in twenty years some upright committee of honest Vellish men will be reading names off these pages and calling it justice, and it will be the same list with better handwriting. I have seen what happens to names on lists. I have *been* a name on a list."

She had not meant to say that last part. It came out of her like a type falling from a loose stick, and she felt the crowd feel it—the small collective intake, the sense of a shape almost glimpsed.

"What are you talking about?" Hesta Ostrow said slowly. "What list? Who are you?"

* * *

Later—much later—she would try to remember what made her answer, and she never entirely could. Partly it was exhaustion. Partly it was the bell still ringing, that honest Vellish peal that had counted her whole life and deserved better than to count a lie. But mostly, she thought, it was the book itself, hot and ruined against her chest, full of every true name in Vellmere except the one that mattered, the one her father had entered on the final page in a hand that shook slightly—*Lior Crane, daughter*—as if writing it might summon her into existence. Seven years she had kept that silence. Seven years of silences keeping people safe, and where had it gotten any of them? Her father burned. Tam dead and erased. Forty families saved by lies printed beautifully.

A town rebuilt on a silence was just a fire waiting for weather.

"My name," Lior said, and her voice did not shake, which surprised her more than anything else that day, "is Lior Crane. Daughter of Aldous Crane, printer. Sister of Tam Crane, apprentice, who died seven years ago of the fever, and whose place I took, and whose name I wore, and whom I have missed every single day of every single one of those years. I am the girl who set your wedding invitations, Mistress Reeve. I am the boy who bound your account books, Master Ostrow. I am the one who printed the papers that saved your families, and I am the one holding the book, and I am telling you the truth of it because somebody in this square has to start, and it may as well be the person with the most to lose."

The crowd made a sound—not a gasp, nothing so neat. A low murmur, like a press taking up its rhythm.

"You're the printer's *daughter*?" Old Pem, from his doorway, incredulous. "But—you carried my coal up three flights—"

"With a bad back," Lior said. "Yes. You tipped me in onions. It's in the ledger, actually. Page sixty-one. 'Crane, apprentice: one sack coal, payment rendered in alliums, noted with affection.' My father wrote that. He wrote *with affection*. There are forty entries in that book like it, next to the debts and the bribes and the shameful things. That's what you'd be burning or wielding. All of it. Together. Unsorted."

Hesta Ostrow was staring at her with an expression Lior had seen once before, years ago, on Verel's face in the shop—the expression of a person watching skill and secrets occupy the same pair of hands. "Then what do we do with it?" Hesta asked, and it was almost gentle. "If it's not a weapon and it can't be burned—Maret's been saying burn it—then what?"

And that was when the crowd parted, because Captain Verel had come into the square.

* * *

He was out of uniform. That was the first thing, and it landed strangely: a spare gray coat, no braid, a man-shaped absence where the polished officer had been. His face was smoke-streaked. He walked through the parting townsfolk the way he had once walked through her shop—carefully, reading everything—and he stopped at the edge of the ring around her, and he looked at the ledger, and then at her, and Lior braced for whatever came: arrest, accusation, the parish page produced like a knife.

Instead he said, quietly, "The records office is empty. The files are gone. Somebody very thorough got there before me." A pause. "I've been trying to work out whether to report that."

"Have you," Lior said.

"For about an hour." He glanced around at the crowd—at Bren Ostrow's fists, at Tamsin Reeve's wet eyes, at old Pem in his doorway—and something tired moved across his face. "Your town is about to do exactly what my superiors did. I've seen it before. In Kesselvane, after the garrison fell. They opened the collaboration registers in the square and read names until the mob ran out of enemies and started on strangers. Three hundred people. Most of them guilty of nothing worse than arithmetic." He looked back at Lior. "You were saying something about unsorted pages."

"I was saying," Lior said, "that the book is true, and that's precisely why it can't be trusted to speak alone."

Verel nodded slowly, and Lior realized—with a jolt, like a misaligned sheet—that he had known. Had known for days, perhaps. Had watched her walk out of his own records office in a borrowed uniform and had said nothing, and had burned something, she'd wager, because his cuffs smelled faintly of paper ash and there was a specific kind of quietness in a man carrying an unsaid mercy.

"The tribunal lists are ash," he said to the crowd. "The provincial judges won't come for weeks, and when they come, they'll bring their own questions. You have that long to decide what Vellmere is." He turned back to Lior. "Printer. What do you propose?"

And the whole square waited, forty faces plus the ones arriving every minute, the bell finally stilled above them, the smoke thinning to show a hard clean evening sky. Lior looked down at the ledger in her arms—her grandfather's hand, her father's hand, the fist-marks, the onions, the debts, the daughter—and felt the weight settle into something she could carry, because she finally understood what it was for.

Not a weapon. Not an inheritance. Paper was neither of those things until someone stood up and read it.

"Tomorrow," Lior said, "at noon, I'm going to stand where the tribunal scaffold was built, and I'm going to read every name in this book aloud. All of them. The collaborators and the bribers and the brave ones and the liars, the entries marked *with affection* and the ones marked with shame, straight through, in my grandfather's hand and my father's hand and mine. No one gets to pull out their own page. No one gets to hide behind someone else's. And when I get to the last page—" her throat tightened, and she let it, "—you'll hear my name, and my father's, because we're in it too, and we don't get out of it either. And then this town can decide what it is with the whole truth in its ears, instead of the useful part in its fists."

Silence, long enough to hurt.

Then old Pem, from his doorway, in the voice of a man settling a debt: "Page sixty-one had better be read properly. I want my onions on the record."

And someone laughed—a wet, ragged laugh, and then another—and the animal in the crowd lay back down, and the neighbors came forward again, one by one, to look at the book they had wanted to seize, seeing it perhaps for the first time.

Above them, on the steps of the shuttered customs house, unnoticed until now, a figure detached itself from the shadows—broad-shouldered, laundry-sleeved, arm bound in scorched linen. Maret. She had heard all of it. Her face was unreadable as wet stone, and she was looking at Lior with an expression that contained, somewhere deep beneath the anger, something that might have been grief, or pride, or the exact spot where the two of them met.

"You're going to read the hidden accounts," Maret said. "Out loud. The resistance's money. The men who funded us. Every name we protected by keeping it dark."

"Yes," Lior said.

"Half those families will be furious. Some of them will be *afraid*."

"I know."

Maret came down the steps slowly, and the crowd watched her come the way it had watched the soldiers come a month ago, and when she reached Lior she stood close enough that only the two of them—and perhaps, at the edge, the former captain in his plain gray coat—could hear what she said next.

"Then you'd best sleep," Maret said, "because starting at dawn, I'm teaching you which of those names you're reading means death to say wrong—and there are forty of them, and I only know thirty-eight."

Lior looked at her. "Who are the other two?"

Maret glanced once, briefly, at Verel—and then, more slowly, at the scorched ledger in Lior's arms—and said nothing at all.
