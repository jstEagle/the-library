## 21. Names Read Aloud

The ledger was heavier than it had any right to be.

That was Lior's first coherent thought as she stood in the square of Vellmere, half-burned book in her arms, smoke still rising from the paper quarter behind her like a flag nobody had agreed upon. Seven years she had carried things in that shop—chases and formes and buckets of type heavy enough to bend a grown man's spine—and none of them had ever felt like this. The ledger weighed what a town weighs. Every debt, every bribe, every hidden birth and unpaid fine and whispered transaction going back four generations, bound in calfskin that smelled now of char and river water.

The crowd wanted it.

She could feel the wanting the way you feel weather coming—pressure behind the ears, a hush that wasn't peace. They had come out of their houses when the drums stopped, when the soldiers' column broke and scattered up the north road, and they had found the square full of ash-flakes drifting like filthy snow and one girl holding a book that knew everything about them.

"Lio." Maret's hand closed on her elbow, laundry-rough and steadying. "Give it here. We'll put it somewhere safe until—" She stopped herself, because they both knew there was no *until* that made that sentence honest. Somewhere safe meant somewhere Maret's committee controlled it. Somewhere safe meant leverage.

Aldous stood three feet away, propped between two of Maret's cousins, his face gray as unprinted paper, his eyebrows gone entirely—he had singed them going back into the shop for this book, and without them he looked startled by the entire world. "Maret," he rasped, "I have told you twice tonight. It is my father's book."

"It is forty families' noose," Maret said, not loudly, which somehow made it worse. The crowd leaned in. Even the fire seemed to listen.

And at the edge of it all, apart, in an officer's coat with one sleeve scorched and no hat, stood Captain Verel, watching Lior with an expression she had seen once before across the width of her father's workbench—the look of a literate man standing in front of a page he cannot yet read.

Someone in the crowd shouted, "Burn it!" and someone else shouted, "No—read it!" and for one absurd moment Vellmere's first free public debate was a shouting match over a ledger, conducted by people who mostly could not read, about whether truth should be a bonfire or a weapon.

Lior looked down at the book.

The last time she had opened it, three nights ago, by candle stub, hunting the page with the printer's fist, she had done it the way she did everything: silently, invisibly, a girl wearing her brother's name reading a town wearing its secrets. She had learned every silence this place kept. She had kept most of them herself.

Her arms ached. Her throat was raw from smoke. And underneath all of it, small and stubborn as a seed under snow, was a thought she had never once permitted herself in seven years:

*I am tired of silence.*

"Quiet," she said.

It came out cracked and smoky and far too quiet. Nobody heard. Maret squeezed her arm warningly; Aldous shook his head, a slow, exhausted *don't*; and Verel took one step forward and then, deliberately, stopped, hands loose at his sides, as if stepping back from a door he had decided not to open.

Lior set the ledger down on the fountain's dry rim. The stone was warm from the fires. She climbed onto the rim beside it, found her balance the way she'd found her balance on the press stool since she was nine—knees soft, weight centered—and raised her voice the way Aldous had taught her to raise it for customers, clear and carrying, a voice built for rooms louder than this one.

"I said *quiet*."

The square went quiet. Two hundred people, maybe more, ash settling on their shoulders, looking up at a soot-streaked apprentice on a fountain, and Lior thought, with a wild bubble of laughter she swallowed whole: *Tam would have loved this. Tam would have been down there in the front row, starting the arguments.*

"My name," she said, "is not Lio Crane."

* * *

The silence afterward had a texture. She would remember its texture for the rest of her life—like the moment after a forme is locked, when everything holds together because you made it hold.

She saw it land on faces in waves. Old Pem Halloway, who had sold her pipe tobacco for six years and winked and called her "young master," blinking as if the light had changed. The Widow Sarn, whose son had been conscripted in '09, whose exemption paper Lior had printed at midnight, mouth open. A boy of perhaps ten, standing near the front, who looked up at her with pure uncomplicated delight, as if she had just announced she was secretly a heron.

Only three faces did not show surprise. Maret's, because Maret had guessed weeks ago and had the decency never to say. Verel's, because Verel had known—had known since the parish record, since the records office, since some moment in the middle of all this when duty and mercy had fought and mercy had won without either of them discussing it. And her father's, because Aldous Delacroix—no. Aldous Crane. Because Aldous had known since the beginning, since the worst day of both their lives, and had carried that knowledge in the same silence he had taught her, and watching him now she understood that the silence had been killing him slowly for seven years, the way damp kills a press.

"Seven years ago," Lior said, "my brother Tam died of the fever. He was fourteen. He was my father's apprentice, and the guild law says an apprenticeship lapses if there is no heir, and the guild law also says—" her voice caught, and she let it catch, and let them hear it catch, which was the whole point, wasn't it, the whole terrible new thing— "*also* says a daughter can't inherit. So I became my brother. I cut my hair. I lowered my voice. I answered to a dead boy's name for seven years, and every one of you helped me do it without knowing, because you were kind to the boy you thought I was, and I want to tell you—" she breathed— "that boy was real. He was me. He just had the wrong name."

Old Pem Halloway said, thickly, "Well. You were always better with the kerning than Tam ever was, God rest him," and somebody laughed wetly, and the terrible glass tension in the square cracked just enough for breathing.

Then Lior put her hands on the ledger.

"This book knows everything about everyone in this town. Debts. Bribes. Who paid whom to keep which birth off the rolls. Which magistrate took how much. Which families bought exemptions and which couldn't afford them." She looked at Maret. "Which is why the resistance wants it locked away, so no collaborator can ever be named. And which is why the Empire wanted it seized, so every one of us could be sold." She looked at the crowd. "Both of them are right that it's dangerous. Both of them are wrong about what to do with danger."

"Girl," said a voice—a merchant, Corvel the grain factor, whose name she happened to know appeared on eleven separate pages— "you don't know what's in that book. Your father does. There are things in there that will ruin—"

"Yes," Lior said. "That's rather the idea."

She opened it. The burned corner crumbled softly; the early pages were intact, the ink steady, her grandfather's hand giving way to her father's. She found the first leaf.

"Here's how this works," she said. "I'm going to read every name in this book. Out loud. All of them. The debts and the bribes and the births and the fines. Mine too—you'll get mine again in a minute. Nobody gets skipped. Not the heroes. Not the collaborators. Not the people who paid to have inconvenient children left off parish rolls, and not the people who paid the bribes, and not the magistrates." She looked straight at Corvel. "Not even the ones who are standing in this square hoping I'll lose my nerve."

Corvel went the color of his own flour.

"Because here's what I've figured out," Lior said, and her voice was steadier now, finding its footing the way her feet had found the fountain rim. "A secret isn't a stone you carry. It's a debt. And my family has spent seven years learning what debts do when nobody reads the ledger—they grow interest. They compound. Silence isn't safety. Silence is just a loan against a worse day." She touched the page. "So today the account comes due, and everybody pays the same rate, which is: being known."

From the edge of the crowd, quietly, Captain Verel said, "Read."

Not an order. Almost a blessing. Lior met his eyes—one heartbeat, ten minutes on a street in a borrowed uniform compressed into it, each of them recognizing something in the other that neither had words for—and then she bent her head to the page.

* * *

It took four hours.

She read until her voice went hoarse and Maret climbed up to spell her, and then Aldous insisted on taking a turn despite his burns, and read his own grandfather's shadier entries in a voice that trembled and then, remarkably, didn't. The crowd stayed. That was the miracle of it—that they *stayed*. People laughed at odd moments (the entry recording that the town council in 1788 had spent eleven crowns on "wine, official purposes" that everyone knew had been wine, entirely unofficial purposes). People wept at others. When Lior read the name of the woman who had informed on the Halloway boy in '09—a name everyone knew and no one had ever said aloud—an awful ripple went through the square, and old Pem stood up and said, in a voice like gravel, "Let her be judged properly, by the tribunal we're actually going to have, with witnesses, in daylight, like civilized people, instead of the way the Empire did it," and the ripple settled into something harder and better: resolve.

Twice, Lior paused over entries involving her father—the forged exemptions, the hidden accounts that had funded them, the fist-marked pages—and read them exactly as written, neither softening nor inflating, and Aldous stood beside the fountain through both passages with his chin up and his eyebrowless face open to the town, and when she finished the second one he said clearly, "Every word true. I'll answer questions at the tribunal," and sat back down to scattered, disbelieving applause.

And near dusk, when the last pages turned and only one entry remained, Lior looked up at the crowd—at Maret with her sleeves rolled, at Aldous gray but upright, at Verel standing apart with his ruined sleeve and his unreadable, readable face—and read:

"Final page, in the hand of Aldous Crane: *Lior Crane, daughter of Aldous and Sefine Crane, born the third of Thaw-month, 1796. Apprentice to this shop since 1810, in the place of her brother Tamas, deceased. Best compositor I have ever trained. Kept in secret against guild law, at her mother's insistence, for her safety. My shame and my pride.*"

She closed the book.

"That's me," she said. Her voice cracked clean through and she let it. "Lior Crane, daughter. Born 1796. Compositor." She held up her hands, black with ink and soot. "These are my father's hands and my grandfather's hands and they're mine, and I am done borrowing anybody's uniform—not Tam's, not an officer's, not silence's. This town has spent four generations keeping a ledger because we didn't trust each other with the truth. Well. Here's the truth. All of it. Free of charge. Now we find out if we can build something on it."

For a moment, nothing.

Then the Widow Sarn began to clap—slowly, deliberately, the way you applaud something that matters more than entertainment—and the sound spread through the square like ink through water, uneven, blotchy, real, until it was a roar and the ash came down on all of them and nobody minded.

* * *

Afterward, in the blue hour when the fires had burned down to sullen orange teeth and the crowd had drained away toward homes and candles and the strange giddy labor of beginning, three people remained by the fountain.

Maret picked up the ledger, weighed it in her capable hands, and looked at Lior for a long moment. Then she held it back out.

"You're mad," she said. "You know that. Half those names will come for you. Collaborators, creditors, husbands whose second families are now a matter of civic record—"

"Then they can come argue with the broadside," Lior said. "I'm printing the whole thing. Annotated."

"*Annotated.*" Maret pressed the book into her arms and something in her fierce laundress's face loosened into something almost young. "God help Vellmere. You know your father said you'd be the death of him? He said it last week, while feverish, and I believe he meant it fondly." She pulled her shawl tighter. "Tribunal convenes Thursday. Pem Halloway's organizing witnesses. Try to sleep sometime before then, daughter-of-nobody's-business." And she strode off into the dark, already shouting instructions at someone.

Verel lingered. He had removed his officer's coat at some point during the reading—it lay folded over the fountain rim, stripped of its insignia—and beneath it he wore a plain soldier's shirt, and without the coat he looked, for the first time, like a man rather than an office.

"The parish page," Lior said quietly. "You never filed it."

"No."

"Why?"

He considered the question with the seriousness he brought to typefaces. "My sister," he said finally, "was struck from our family record in 1801. A dispute over inheritance. I grew up visiting a house that officially contained no one where I remembered someone." He looked at the ledger in her arms. "I've spent twenty years believing records were the enemy. Tonight I watched you make one the opposite. It seemed—" he searched, and found— "it seemed worth protecting. The possibility, I mean. Not the page. I burned the page."

"I know," Lior said. "It smelled like it."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth that on another man might have been called a smile. He picked up his folded coat, hesitated, and held it out to her.

"I won't need it where I'm going. There's a tribunal in my future too—I imagine. And after that, whatever comes after empires." His eyes flicked to the print shop's scorched skeleton up the lane, then back to her. "You'll need good paper stock. And a press. Yours is—"

"Crispy," Lior said. "Yes."

"There's a requisitioned shipment in the north warehouse. Fine rag paper, imperial seal, several hundred sheets. It appears to have been mislabeled." He handed her the coat as well, formally, absurdly, like a man returning borrowed property. "Someone should see to it. Someone local. With authority over inventory."

"Captain," Lior said, taking the coat, "are you committing fraud?"

"I'm told," Verel said, "that around here it's called printing." And he inclined his head, and walked away down the ash-strewn lane, unhatted, unranked, lighter by one uniform.

Aldous limped over and stood beside her, following Verel's retreating figure with his eyes. "That," he said, "is either the most decent man in the Empire or the most dangerous, and I genuinely cannot determine which."

"He reads colophons, Papa. He's decent. It's terminal."

Aldous laughed—a rusty, painful, wonderful sound—and put his arm around her shoulders, and they stood together in the ruins of the day, the half-burned ledger between them, the town breathing around them like something waking up.

"Your mother," Aldous said at last, very quietly. "When this settles. When the tribunal's done. She should know your name is yours now. Publicly." He swallowed. "I should write to her. Or—" his eyes slid sideways to her, hopeful and terrified in equal measure, an expression she recognized from every customer who had ever asked for something delicate in gold leaf— "you could. If you wanted."

Lior looked at the darkening sky, at the first stars over the paper quarter's smoking bones, and felt seven years of silences settle into their final shape—not gone, never gone, but finished, like an edition closed and counted.

"Not a letter," she said. "Papa. Give me the type case. I'm setting it myself."

Aldous grinned—eyebrows or no eyebrows, it was unmistakably Tam's grin, which had always been hers—and said, "Then we'd best start casting. You'll want a face of your own for it, and Lord knows we haven't got one."

And that was how, in the ashes of the paper quarter, the conversation about Lior Crane's first original typeface began.
