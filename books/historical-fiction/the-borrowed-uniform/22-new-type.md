## 22. New Type

The mold was hot before dawn.

Lior had learned that much from her father in the weeks since the fire: the mold must be hot before you pour, or the metal sulks and gives you a letter with a chipped foot, a crooked stem, something you'd spend years apologizing for every time it touched paper. Aldous said it the way he said everything now—slower, with pauses where the fever had burned gaps—and she wrote it down anyway, because some lessons deserved to survive even if the man saying them got tired of repeating them.

She stood at the casting bench with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, watching the crucible's orange eye, and she thought about names.

Her own, mostly. Lior Crane. She had said it aloud in the square with half of Vellmere listening, and the world had failed to end, which was either a miracle or proof of how little the world had ever been paying attention. Since then she had said it perhaps four hundred times. To Maret, who now answered to *Madame Speaker* and pretended to hate it. To the carpenters rebuilding the shop's roof, who called her *Miss Crane* until she corrected them—not because she minded being missed, but because she had decided that being known was going to be her full-time occupation, and she intended to be thorough about it.

"Crane," Aldous said from the doorway, testing it. "That's still my name too, I suppose."

"It's on the broadside draft," she said. "Twice. You can't take it back now."

"I wouldn't dream of it." He came in leaning on the new cane Maret's nephew had carved—a fat, ridiculous thing shaped like a goose, because Vellmere's sense of humor had survived the tribunal intact even when its windows hadn't. He lowered himself onto the stool by the stone sink, which was his stool now, the one he supervised from, and watched her work with the particular silence she had spent seven years learning to read.

This silence meant pride. She could tell by the way he kept almost ruining it with commentary.

The rebuilt shop smelled different than the old one. That had taken her longest to accept. The old shop had smelled of coal smoke and hide glue and the ghost of her brother Tam's hair oil, sunk so deep into the beams that no scrubbing would ever get it out—which was fortunate, because there were no beams anymore. The fire had taken those, and the loft where Tam had slept, and the press itself, poor old Bastian, whose iron bones they had dragged out of the ashes like relatives from a shipwreck. What remained of Bastian hung on the wall now above the new press, polished and named, a memorial to a machine rather than a man. Lior felt this was fair. Machines never lied to anyone on purpose.

The new press was called nothing yet. She was letting it earn a name.

"You're pouring at the wrong angle," Aldous said.

"I am pouring at exactly the right angle."

"The metal wants to come off the lip's edge, not the—"

"Papa." She turned, ladle in hand, molten type-metal trembling gold in its bowl. "Seven years I did everything wrong-angle and you never once corrected me. You let me believe my whole apprenticeship was a lie. Now the truth is out and suddenly you have opinions?"

Aldous smiled. It was still strange on him, the fever having rearranged his face slightly, the way weather rearranges a coastline—but it fit better now, she thought, than anything had fit in the old days. "I didn't correct you," he said, "because you were doing it right and calling it wrong, and I couldn't work out how to explain that without explaining everything else."

Lior considered this. It was the closest he had come, in all their long fireside conversations about the ledger, to an apology for the silences he'd asked of her—the borrowed name, the flattened voice, the careful boyhood she had worn like ill-fitting type, every letter slightly the wrong height. She decided not to make him say more. Some apologies were like type-metal: best poured once, quickly, while hot.

She poured.

The metal ran into the matrix clean and bright, and when she cracked the mold a minute later and knocked free the little block, there it was: a letter that existed nowhere else on earth. Her letter. Her design, drawn forty times in charcoal before she'd committed, cut into the punch over eleven evenings with gravers borrowed from the engraver Oskan up the hill, struck into copper, justified, fitted. A lowercase *l*, as it happened—the first letter of the first word of the first thing she intended to print under her own name. She held it up to the window light and turned it.

"Well?" she said.

Aldous took it with hands that no longer shook, or shook less, and examined it the way he had once examined Tam's schoolwork and her own disguised proofs—with total attention, total seriousness, as though a child's small achievement might contain the whole future of the craft. Which, Lior reflected, it occasionally did.

"The serif," he said. "It flares outward instead of stopping flat. Like it's about to move."

"That's the idea."

"Like it's mid-stride. Like the letter is walking somewhere." He handed it back. "Whose letters taught you that?"

"Yours," she said. "And Verel's handwriting, if you want the honest answer. He writes like a man trying to arrive somewhere before his doubts do."

Aldous snorted. "Still writing to him, are you."

"We correspond," Lior said with dignity, "about typography."

They did, in fact, correspond about typography. Captain—Illias, now, stripped of rank along with the rest of the occupation garrison, though nobody in Vellmere had quite settled on what to call him—had stayed on after the retreat, ostensibly to catalog the seized records for return to their owners and actually, everyone understood, because he had nowhere to go and no great desire anywhere to go to. He lived in a room above the cooper's yard and spent his days matching burned ledger fragments to parish books, restoring to each family what was theirs, and his evenings writing Lior long, careful letters about sixteenth-century Venetian faces and whether a counter could be said to have moral character. He signed them *I.V.* He had offered once, in the third letter, a paragraph that began *Regarding your name, which I learned in the worst possible way and have never once regretted knowing*—and then abandoned the sentence, crossed it out with two firm lines, and moved on to kerning. Lior had appreciated that. There were people who needed to say things and people who needed to set them properly, and she suspected Verel was discovering he was the second kind.

* * *

By midmorning the light through the new south windows was good enough to set type by, and Lior carried her tray of fresh-cast letters to the composing stone with the care of a woman transporting eggs she had personally convinced the hens to lay.

The broadside had been three weeks in the drafting. It was one page—one page only, she had told herself firmly, after the third draft threatened to become a book, and the fifth a career—and it told the true history of Vellmere's autumn in plain language: the requisition, the ledger, the forged exemptions, the forty families, the courier's uniform, the records office, the fire, the reading of the names in the square. All of it. Including the parts that made her wince. Especially those.

Maret had read the seventh draft standing in the doorway with flour on her hands, because Maret always had flour on her hands now; running the town's provisioning committee turned out to involve an amount of baking that no one had warned her about. "You've left out the part where you lied to me for a week about who cut the plate," Maret said.

"No, I haven't. It's line fourteen."

Maret found it and read it aloud. *"The plate was cut by the apprentice Lio, who was not who she said she was, and who has since apologized to everyone except herself, which the author is working on."* Maret put the draft down. "Good," she said. "But change *apologized* to *made amends*. An apology is words. You gave Mirelle Ostrow her husband's exemption back with your own hands. That's amends."

So line fourteen became *made amends*, and Lior kept the distinction in her chest like a coin in a pocket.

The hardest line had been the last one. Not the history—the signature. For twenty-one drafts she had ended the page with *Printed in Vellmere*, which was safe, which was how broadsides ended, which required no one to stand behind the ink. And then one night she had sat with the pen hovering and thought about her father's ledger, and what a ledger really was: a book that said *someone was here, someone counted, someone is answerable*. And she had written, beneath the colophon, in her own plain hand as a guide for the compositor:

*Set, printed, and signed by Lior Crane.*

No *daughter of*. No *apprentice to*. Just the name, standing alone on the page like a letter walking somewhere.

* * *

Composing went slowly, which was right. Every word of the history had to be earned twice—once in memory, once in metal—and she stopped often, holding a line up to the window, checking spacing the way you check a stranger's face against a remembered one. Around noon the bell over the door rang and Oskan's granddaughter Pell came in with the delivery of paper: two reams of good stock bought from the mill at Halfway Bridge with money from the shop's first honest orders since the uprising—wedding invitations, a butcher's price list, funeral notices for three men who had died of ordinary causes, which the town had begun to treat as a kind of luxury.

"How's the goose?" Pell asked, nodding at Aldous's cane.

"He's molted twice and bitten the mail carrier once."

"A healthy bird, then." Pell stacked the paper, lingered, and pointed at the composed galley. "Is that the true history? The real one? My gran says the version going around the taverns has you fighting three soldiers on the stairs of the records office."

"There were no stairs. There was a corridor, and I fought no one, and a captain looked at my boots and decided not to see me."

Pell considered this with the frank disappointment of youth. "Can't you put the fight in anyway?"

"No," Lior said, and then, because the girl deserved a real answer: "Because in ten years somebody will believe it, and the man who actually opened those files for me will have become a monster in the story, and he isn't one. That's how lies start—not big, just convenient." She tapped the galley. "This page is the opposite of convenient. That's the entire point of it."

Pell shrugged the shrug of the young, who have their own histories to misplace, and left. But at the door she turned around. "My gran also says you read every name in the square. Even the collaborator ones. Even the ones that got people hurt."

"Every name."

"She says she cried. She'll deny it." The bell rang again, and she was gone.

Lior stood a moment with her hand on the galley. That was the thing about the square, about that hour with the half-burned ledger open on her arms while the crowd pressed close and Verel stood at the edge of it with his cap in his hands like a man at a funeral not his own—that was the thing she had never managed to explain to Aldous, who had been recovering upstairs and heard it only secondhand. Reading the names had not felt brave. It had felt like setting type: each name a piece of metal, each piece placed, no skipping, no convenient blanks. Forty families. Six collaborators. Two dead soldiers whose mothers' addresses she tracked down afterward and wrote to, and received one answer back, from a woman in the capital who wrote simply: *Thank you. No one had told me where he ended.* The ledger could have been a weapon. In the crowd's first hunger it very nearly was. But a weapon only aims one direction, and the truth aims everywhere, and Lior had stood in the middle of it and read until her voice cracked and Maret's arm came around her shoulders and finished the last twelve names with her.

Afterward the crowd had wanted the book—what was left of it, its scorched cover, its water-swollen pages—and Lior had stood between them and it, exhausted beyond fear, and said: "It's yours already. You heard it. A book you've all heard isn't a secret anymore. Let me keep the paper, so we remember what paper costs."

They had let her. Vellmere was learning mercy the same slow way she was: one decision at a time, most of them awkward.

* * *

The afternoon passed in lock-up and quoins, in furniture and frisket, in the thousand small negotiations between a compositor and a page. Aldous dozed in his chair and woke to offer advice and dozed again, and Lior let the rhythm of the work carry her the way the press rhythm used to carry her back in the silent years—except that now the work talked back. Now when her hands moved, they moved for a woman with a name, and the silence around her was chosen quiet, not purchased invisibility.

At the bottom of the forme, below the last line of the history, she set the signature. She set it slowly, breathing, using her own new letters wherever the text allowed—her walking *l*, her flaring serifs, a face that had no name yet either but was already answering to the one she'd been privately calling it: *Vellmere*, because a town ought to have a type of its own and no one had ever bothered to give it one.

Then she locked the forme, carried it to the press—the new press, still nameless—and inked the plate.

The first proof came off grey and shy. The second, with the pressure eased and the ink thinned, came off like morning. She pulled three, then five, then a dozen, hanging them on the drying lines strung across the shop like festival washing, and stood back among them at dusk with ink to her wrists and looked at forty identical truths, and felt something she had no letter for yet.

Aldous woke, looked at the lines of hanging broadsides, and said nothing for a long time.

"You should sign the press," he said finally. "Give it a name. It worked all day without complaint; that's more than I managed."

"I'm thinking."

"Don't think too long. A press without a name starts making decisions on its own." He levered himself up with the goose cane, crossed to the drying lines, and read the broadside top to bottom, lips moving slightly at the hard parts. When he reached the signature he stopped, and his thumb hovered over the ink as if to touch it, and didn't. "Lior Crane," he read aloud. "Set, printed, and signed."

"Yes."

He nodded, once, and she saw him swallow whatever the next sentence was—the apology, or the blessing, or both—and file it away in the place where he kept the things he couldn't pour while hot. "Your mother," he said instead, "would have bought every copy."

Lior blinked. In twenty-two chapters of their family's history her mother had occupied roughly the space of a watermark—present in the paper, invisible unless held to the light. "You never talk about her."

"I'm practicing." He looked at the drying broadsides again. "There's a letter. From her. Came the week before the requisition—I hid it in the ledger vault because I didn't know what to do with it, and then the army took the vault, and then the fire—" He stopped. Started again, slower. "Verel returned it to me last month, out of the recovered records. I haven't opened it. I keep telling myself I'm waiting for the right moment, but I think the truth is I'm a coward with good taste in timing."

Lior looked at her father—this man who had taught her that invisibility was a craft, who had spent a lifetime demonstrating that the safest sentence is the unsaid one—and felt the old familiar ache, and something newer underneath it, steadier. "Papa," she said gently, "you raised a printer. We don't wait for the right moment. We make the impression and find out what it says."

Aldous laughed—a real laugh, rusty but whole—and reached into his coat, and brought out the letter, soft-cornered from weeks of handling, unopened.

Outside, the evening bell rang across the rebuilt roofs of Vellmere, and inside, under lamplight, her father held out her mother's sealed words, and Lior took them, and the paper was warm from his pocket, and the whole unread future of her family weighed less than a forme of type and waited, like everything worth printing, for a steady hand.
