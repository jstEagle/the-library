## 1. The Weight of Quiet Letters

The type spoke to Bram Ishikawa in the dark before dawn.

Not aloud — lead and antimony had no voices — but in the way she'd learned to read them over twenty years: by weight, by wear, by the small imperfections a letter carried like scars. This *e* had been cast the year her father died. This *n* had a hairline crack along its stem that would show as a faint white line on paper if you knew where to look, and she always knew where to look. She could set a line of eight-point Baskerville blindfolded, could tell a ligature from a plain f-i by touch alone in a composing room lit only by the banked fire's last coals. The letters were quieter than people. They never asked what you were. They only asked that you hold them right-side up and in their proper order, which was more courtesy than Manchester ever showed her.

She sat at the stone with the broadsheet copy propped before her on a slanted board, its ink still smelling of the clerk's cheap vinegar-based formula. *HARROW & SONS CELEBRATE TWENTY YEARS OF PROSPERITY AND PROGRESS*, it said, and beneath, in the florid hand of some hired pen, a catalogue of virtues: employment given to hundreds, cloth sent to markets across the seas, harmony between master and hand unbroken since the mill's founding. There would be a paragraph about the anniversary dinner at the Corn Exchange, tickets half a guinea, and a smaller notice thanking the district's tradesmen for their faithful service. The Ishikawa Printing Office was named among them. Her name — her brother's name — was set in the list like any other: *B. ISHIKAWA, PRINTER, ANCOATS LANE*.

*Bram Ishikawa*. It had been her father's joke once, or perhaps her mother's — a girl named for a raven in an old story her mother half-remembered from an island Japan, a bird that carried messages and kept secrets. Now the name belonged to a dead man. She had worn it eleven years, since the winter her brother coughed his lungs out into a basin of blood and her father followed him inside the year, and the creditors came sniffing round the shop like rats after grain, and the trade protection societies made it clear that a print shop run by a woman — a foreign woman, a half-caste woman, a woman at all — would be ground into paste inside six months. So Bram had bound her chest, cut her hair with her father's own shears, deepened her voice by an act of will that left her throat raw for a fortnight, and become the son who inherited. *Mr. Bram Ishikawa.* The neighborhood believed it because believing was easier than looking. Men saw the cap, the ink-stained waistcoat, the flat way she held her shoulders, and their eyes slid off. Women were harder; they watched hands and hips and throats. But Bram had learned to keep her hands busy, her stride short, her voice pitched low and bored. Silence was her trade as much as printing. She had gotten so good at not being seen that sometimes she surprised herself in the shaving mirror — a lean face, sharp-jawed, eyes her mother's black and her father's wary — and thought: *who is that?*

The answer came back the same every morning: *the man who keeps the shop.*

She worked the stick and compositor's rule now, filling the galley line by line, the brass rule clicking against the type as she justified each measure. The composing room smelled of ink and cold metal and lamp oil, and beneath those, faintly, of the river muck that seeped through the cellar walls in wet weather. Outside, Ancoats Lane lay under a fog so thick the gas lamps burned as smears of orange, and the first carts were already grinding toward the mills, and somewhere a factory bell was tolling five o'clock like a slow heartbeat under the skin of the city.

Twenty years of prosperity and progress. She set the words without expression. Type did not care what it said. That was the whole secret of the trade, and maybe of survival: lead took any shape you pressed it to, and held it, and asked nothing.

But her hands knew things her mouth would never say. As she set the word *harmony*, her thumb found the worn shoulder of the *h* and paused there a fraction too long. Harmony. Twenty years. She was setting celebratory type for Harrow Mill in the same month — though no one else seemed to have marked it — as another anniversary. One nobody would be printing notices for.

She shut that door in her mind the way she always did, quick and quiet, and reached for the next piece of type.

* * *

By seven the shop was awake around her in its small ways. Tam Olleridge, the pressman, came in stamping snow-melt off his boots and grumbling about the price of coal, and Nell — no, Ned, she corrected herself, *Ned Quill's lad*, though the child was barely fourteen and all elbows and ink — arrived to sweep and fetch and stand too long watching the press as if it might confess something. The apprentice. Her one weakness, if she was honest, though she tried not to be honest even in the privacy of her own skull. A printer needed an apprentice the way a ship needed ballast, and the boy was quick and hungry to learn, and he looked at her sometimes with a naked admiration that made her feel like a fraud twice over: a woman pretending to be a man, teaching a boy a trade by pretending not to love it openly herself.

"Morning, Mr. Ishikawa." Tam hung his coat by the stove. "Fog's bad enough to chew. Reckon half the weavers'll be late and the overlookers docking 'em for it."

"They're always late in February," Bram said. Low, bored, flat. "Set the forme for the Harrow job when you've warmed your hands. I want a proof by nine."

"Anniversary rubbish." Tam spat into the sand bucket, a habit that would have earned him a hiding anywhere but here. "Half a guinea a ticket while the spinners are eating boiled nettles. You want my opinion—"

"I don't," Bram said, mildly, and Tam grinned and let it go, because that was the arrangement between them: he vented, she declined to hear it, and neither of them risked anything that mattered. She had built her whole life on such arrangements. Don't ask, don't say, don't look too long. The district ran on them the way the mills ran on steam.

Ned swept past her with the broom, close enough that she caught woodsmoke and hunger on him. "There's men talking at the corner again," the boy said, too low for Tam to hear. "About the Charter. About a strike. Big'un this time, they're saying. General."

"Men talk," Bram said. "Sweep the stairs."

He went, but she felt his eyes on her back, bright with questions she wouldn't answer. Questions were expensive in Ancoats. She'd learned the price list young: a question answered wrong cost a shop, a name, a life. Her father had taught her the arithmetic at his knee — not in words, never in words, but in the way he'd burn certain proofs unread and turn away certain customers and once, memorably, throw a drunken soldier out bodily rather than print him a ballad about a mill fire, though the coin offered had been good. She'd thought, as a child, that her father was simply careful. It had taken her years to understand he'd been afraid, and longer to learn what of.

She didn't think about that either. She set type.

* * *

The proof came off the stone at half nine, grey and crisp in the cold light. Bram read it standing at the window, where the fog had thinned to a dirty gauze through which the district showed itself: the sooted brick of the mills ranged along the canal like a jury, their windows thousands of dark eyes; women trudging to the spinning rooms with shawls over their heads; two dragoons on the canal bridge, unhurried, bored, present. The town had that feeling it got before weather or trouble — a hush with pressure behind it, like a boiler gauge trembling at the red line. She'd seen it in '39, the year of the great Chartist meeting on Kettledrum Fields, when the crowd had been so vast and so quiet and then so loud, and the yeomanry had ridden in after, and the shops had shuttered for three days. This felt like that. Worse, maybe. The petitions had failed again. Hunger was older and deeper now. And everywhere in the public houses and the chapels and the union rooms, men were saying *strike* the way other men said *fire*.

She checked the proof mechanically. *Harmony between master and hand unbroken since the mill's founding.* Her eye snagged on it, the way a sleeve snags on a nail. Unbroken since the founding. She knew — the whole district old enough to remember knew — that this was a lie with a specific date attached. Twenty-one years ago there had been a fire at Harrow Mill. Everyone knew there'd been a fire; fires were common enough in cotton towns, timber stairs and oil lamps and lint in the air. What everyone did *not* seem to know was how many had died, or who they'd been, or why no memorial stone stood anywhere in any churchyard, or why the parish registers recorded nothing that month but baptisms. When she was a girl she'd asked her mother about it once, and her mother had gone still in the way that meant the conversation had ended before it began, and her father had come home that night and drunk more than usual and said nothing at all, and Bram had learned the lesson that governed her life: *some questions are doors you don't open, because behind them are rooms you can't leave.*

She'd been twelve. She had not asked since.

The proof trembled very slightly in her hand, and she told herself it was the cold from the window glass.

"Proof's clean?" Tam asked.

"It's clean." She handed it back. "Pull the edition this afternoon. Two thousand."

Tam took it, hesitated. "You all right, Mr. Ishikawa? You've gone a bit — " he gestured at his own face vaguely — "grey."

"I'm always grey. It's the ink." She turned back to the case. "Get out of my light."

He laughed and went, and the composing room closed around her again, warm metal and lamplight and the tick of her own breathing, and Bram set type for a lie about harmony and told herself the familiar story: *keep your head down, keep the shop alive, keep the secret, survive.* It had worked for eleven years. It had kept a roof over Ned's head and coal in the stove and her father's presses turning, and if it had cost her — her name, her face, her future, whatever soft unlived life waited in the locked room behind the door she never opened — well, the dead don't pay rent, and the living must.

That was the creed. She recited it to herself the way other people recited scripture, and usually it held.

Usually.

It was nearly noon, the anniversary broadsheet locked in its chase and waiting for the press, when the knock came at the shop door — not the brisk double-rap of a customer with money, but something slower. Measured. Almost courteous.

Through the front glass, distorted by old panes and fog, Bram saw a figure on the step: a tall man in a good coat gone shabby at the cuffs, holding a leather satchel and a long wooden case such as surveyors carried. He stood quite still in the murk, and even through the warped glass she had the sense of being looked at — measured, weighed, entered into some ledger she couldn't see.

"We're closed for the job," she called through the door, pitching her voice down into its working register. "Back tomorrow."

The man didn't move. His breath made a small ghost in the cold. When he spoke, his voice carried through the door with unsettling clarity, mild and precise as a schoolmaster's.

"Mr. Ishikawa? My name is Vane. Elias Vane. I'm the census taker for this district." A pause, the length of a held breath. "I wonder — might I trouble you for a few minutes? Only there's a matter of the records. Of 1821."

Bram's hand was already on the bolt — habit, courtesy, the reflexes of a man who kept customers — before the year landed.

1821.

Her fingers stopped on the iron.

Outside, the fog shifted, and the man's face came clear through the glass for half a second: pale, patient, watchful, with eyes the colour of slate water that did not slide off her the way the neighbourhood's eyes did, but stayed.

"I know the shop is busy," Elias Vane said quietly. "Everyone in Ancoats is busy this week. But I've been asking after the fire at Harrow Mill, Mr. Ishikawa. And yours is the only house on the lane that printed nothing at all."

The boiler gauge in Bram's chest trembled at the red line.

"I wonder why that is," he said.
