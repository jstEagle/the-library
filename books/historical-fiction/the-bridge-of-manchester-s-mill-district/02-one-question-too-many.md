## 2. One Question Too Many

The census man came on a Tuesday, when the fog off the Rochdale Canal sat so thick in the streets that the gas-lamps burned at noon like drowned coins. Bram saw him first from the composing room window — a tall figure picking his way along the gutter-splash of Foundry Street, umbrella furled though the drizzle had not stopped for three days, a leather satchel held close to his chest the way other men carried Bibles or babies.

She knew what he was before he reached the door. The district had been full of them since spring: enumerators with their printed schedules and their blue-black ink, going house to house, name to name, counting Manchester the way a butcher counts stock. They were harmless, mostly. Tired clerks with chilblained hands who wanted their forms filled and their evenings back.

But this one did not walk like a tired clerk. He walked like a man reading.

Bram set down her bodkin and looked once at herself in the small mirror she kept by the stone — a vanity her father had mocked and then, without ever saying why, never removed. Cap straight. Collar high. The stubble she painted onto her jaw each morning with a mixture of lamp-black and tallow had faded at the hinge; she renewed it in four quick strokes, checked that the binding under her shirt had not loosened, and went down to open the door before he could knock twice.

Two knocks brought questions from neighbours. One knock meant the man already believed someone home was listening for him.

"Good morning." He removed his hat, and rain dripped from its brim between them like punctuation. "Vane. Elias Vane. Census enumeration — I'm verifying schedules for this ward." He produced a folio stamped with a crown and something official-looking beneath it, held just long enough to be seen, not long enough to be read.

"Shop's closed to callers till one," Bram said. Her voice sat where she kept it, low and flat as a planed board. Twenty-one years old when her father died, and six years since, and still every sentence came out of her mouth pre-cut, like type locked in a chase.

"I'm not here for the shop, Mr.—"

"Ishikawa. Bram Ishikawa."

"—Ishikawa. Though I'll want the premises recorded too, eventually. Dwelling-house above commercial property, is it? I'm here for the household." He opened the satchel against his knee, out of the wet, and took out a schedule sheet already half-filled in a small, precise hand. "Your father. Kenji Ishikawa, printer. Deceased, I see — my condolences. Six years gone?"

"Seven in the autumn."

"And your mother?"

"Gone before him."

He wrote. She watched his pen move — not the scrawl most enumerators used, all haste and blotted tails, but letters set upright and even, like a compositor's hand. It put her teeth slightly on edge. Men who wrote like that had been taught by someone patient, or had taught themselves, and either kind noticed things.

"Siblings?" he said.

"One brother. Deceased."

"Name?"

"Bram Ishikawa."

His pen paused — a hair's width, no more — above the paper. "The same name."

"He died as an infant. Our father gave the name to me after." She had told this lie so many times it had worn smooth as river stone; she could say it asleep, drunk, weeping, and had done all three. "Old custom. His family's. From before they left Nagasaki."

Actually it was a custom she had invented at nineteen, standing over her brother's grave with her father too broken to argue, but customs invented are still customs if you hold to them hard enough.

Mr. Vane wrote *Bram*, then stopped again. "You were given a woman's— given *his* name entire."

"It was his wish that the name continue."

Something moved behind his eyes — not suspicion, exactly. Interest. Suspicion she could have handled; interest was worse. Interest lingered.

"Well," he said, and made a small tick. "Household of one. Occupation, printer and letterpress proprietor. That's the household done, Mr. Ishikawa, and I thank you for your patience." He did not close the satchel. He stood on the step with the rain threading past his shoulders, and looked past her into the dim of the shop, at the great iron press standing in its shadow like a sleeping animal, at the racks of cases glinting dull brass and wood. "Though while I'm here — and it's no part of the schedule, so tell me if I trespass — I couldn't help noticing your window."

Bram turned her head a fraction. In the shop window, framed and glazed against the damp, lay the current job: two hundred copies of a celebratory broadsheet for Renwick's new spinning block, all laurel borders and a woodcut of the mill crowned in smoke like a halo. *PROSPERITY OF MANCHESTER*, the headline said, in the fat Didone capitals Bram herself had cut and kerned until they sang.

"A fine piece of work," Vane said. "You did the setting yourself?"

"The shop does its own work."

"Of course." He smiled — briefly, politely, and not with his eyes. "It's only that I've been walking this ward for eleven days now, Mr. Ishikawa, and I've seen a great deal of printing in windows. Weddings. Prize-fights. Sermons. A good deal about the prosperity of Manchester." He tilted his head toward the broadsheet. "And I wondered — purely as a matter of local record — whether any shop on this street ever printed death notices. For Harrow Mill."

The word went through Bram like a bodkin through a quire.

She had been braced, without knowing it, since he paused at the infant's name. But not for that. Not for that.

"There was a fire at Harrow Mill," Vane said mildly, to the silence. "1821. February. I understand it was considerable."

"Fires were considerable," Bram said, "in 1821. Building codes being what they were."

"Mm." He consulted nothing; the date had been waiting in him. "Thirty years ago this winter, nearly. And yet I can find no notice. No memorial card, no funeral bill, no black-bordered anything from any printer in Ancoats or Angel Meadow or here. I've asked the vestry clerk, the newspapers' morgues, three stationers." He said it lightly, the way a man describes a hobby. "For a fire that killed — well. That killed people. Someone always prints something. Grief wants paper, Mr. Ishikawa. In my experience grief is the best customer a printer has."

"You'd know better than me. Was your family in the trade?"

"No." A pause, small and deliberate as a space inserted between words. "My father worked. At Harrow Mill."

The canal fog seemed to come into the doorway then, cold and coal-tasted, wrapping them both. Down the street a cart rattled past loaded with wet cotton waste, and somewhere a child was beating a hoop with a stick, monotonous as a pulse.

"I'm sorry," Bram said, because that was the safe thing, the correct thing, the thing a neighbour says.

"It was before my time. He was young." Vane adjusted his grip on the satchel. "I mention it only because I'm compiling more than the census, privately. Local history. Deaths of the district — the ones that got written down and the ones that didn't. And Harrow Mill is a gap in the record, Mr. Ishikawa. A gap shaped exactly like thirty-one people."

*Thirty-one.* The number landed in her chest and stayed there, hot as a fresh-cast slug.

"That's a precise figure for a gap," she said.

"Censuses make precision men of us all." He smiled again, and this time there was nothing polite in it at all — only a long, patient hunger, banked and careful. "Thank you for your time. I'll leave my card — if the household remembers anything further, anything at all, I'm lodging at the Turk's Head until the enumeration's finished. I pay for recollections. Fair rates. Some of the older residents remember a great deal, given the encouragement."

He set the card on the doorframe's ledge rather than handing it to her — a courtesy, or a test, or simply a man who had learned not to put things directly into other people's hands. Then the hat went back on, and he stepped down into the fog, and Bram watched through the glass as he walked not away up Foundry Street but slowly along the front of the shops, reading every window the way he had read hers, pausing at the stationer's, at the chandler's, at the shuttered office of the *Ancoats Advertiser*, his head tipped like a bird's.

* * *

She gave him ten minutes to get clear of the street. Then she bolted the door, turned the sign, climbed to the composing room, and pulled the job books for 1821 down from the shelf where the dead years lived.

Her father's ledgers were beautiful. That was the thing nobody who hadn't known him would understand — that Kenji Ishikawa, who had crossed the world and buried a wife and a son and finally himself, kept accounts like a monk illuminating scripture. Every entry ruled true. Every figure in a hand so controlled it might have been type itself. Bram had learned her letters tracing those columns before she learned them anywhere else, sitting on a stool by the stove while he set type overhead, the smell of ink and whale-oil and the particular sweetness of molten metal.

February 1821. She turned the pages with hands that were steady, she noted with detached approval, entirely steady.

The winter jobs scrolled past: funeral bills for the Kershaw infant, five shillings. Memorial verses for old Nab Wainwright, seven and six, paid by his lodge. Black borders for the Ashworths, whole family taken by typhus, the largest order of the season — grief wanting paper, just as Vane had said, grief always wanting paper.

Then February, third week, and the entry for the twelfth:

*Harrow Mill — enquiry re: broadsheet, memorial, names of the deceased —*

And that was all.

No price quoted. No sum received. No *declined*. No *refused*. The line simply stopped, mid-phrase, the way a sentence stops when the man writing it looks up and sees something in the doorway. The next entry began a week later with a job for a grocer's labels, brisk and ordinary, as though nothing had intervened — but Bram laid her fingertip below the unfinished line and felt, in the paper's faint ripple, that the pen had pressed hard there, hard enough to nearly score the page, and had lifted.

Someone had come to the shop in February 1821 and asked her father to print the names of the dead.

And her father had written the enquiry down — which he never did for work he intended to refuse; refusals went unwritten, unrecorded, unremembered — and then had not refused it. Had not accepted it either. The entry hung open across twenty years like a door left ajar.

Below it, in the column where payment should be, someone had later drawn a single line through the blank space. Not her father's ruling-hand. The stroke was English, hurried, heavy — a different pen, a different pressure, a different decade even, the iron-gall gone brown while her father's entries around it stayed black. It had been struck through by someone else, some other day, deliberately, the way one strikes through a debt one means never to acknowledge.

Bram sat very still in the grey light with the ledger open on her knees.

She thought: *the census man said thirty-one.*

She thought: *I was born in 1815. I was six years old. I was alive, in this city, when those thirty-one names existed, and I never heard one of them spoken.*

She thought of her father in the last years of his life, how he had grown strange about certain jobs — would not print mourning cards for mills, would take funerals for churches and lodges but went quiet and grim if the deceased had died in any works in the district; how he had once, uncharacteristically, refused a lucrative contract from the Harrow company itself and let Bram, aged twenty, believe it was pride, or politics, or simple stubbornness. How he had burned papers in the yard the week before he died — she had smelled it, had watched from the window, had said nothing, because knowing which questions not to ask was the first thing he had ever taught her.

The stove ticked. The fog pressed at the windows. Below in the street, life resumed its noise — carts, children, a drunk singing about some girl in Salford.

Bram looked down at the struck-through blank where a payment should have been, and understood, with the slow cold certainty of metal cooling in a mould, that the silence around Harrow Mill had not happened on its own. Silence was a product. Like type. Like broadsheets. Someone had composed it, set it, distributed it — and her father had been made, somehow, someway, to hold a forme of it for twenty years.

She closed the ledger gently, as if it were sleeping.

On the ledge downstairs, the enumerator's card lay where he had left it. *E. VANE,* it said, in that upright compositor's hand. *ENUMERATOR & COMPILER OF LOCAL RECORDS.* No address beyond the Turk's Head, no flourish, nothing wasted.

Grief wanted paper. So, apparently, did the truth — and it had sent a man with a satchel and thirty-one missing names to stand in the rain outside her door.

Bram took up her apron and went back to the case, to the Renwick broadsheet, to the fat triumphant capitals spelling out the prosperity of Manchester. But her hands, for the first time in six careful years, were not quite steady on the stick; and when she reached the word *PROGRESS* she found she had set, without meaning to, one wrong letter — a silent, impossible *q* where a *p* should stand — and she sat looking at it for a long moment before she decided whether it was an error at all.
