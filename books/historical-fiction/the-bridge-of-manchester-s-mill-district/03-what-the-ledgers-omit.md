## 3. What the Ledgers Omit

The rain came down on Ancoats like a verdict nobody had the courage to read aloud.

Bram Ishikawa stood in the back room of the print shop with a candle burning low and her father's job books open before her, and she told herself — as she had been telling herself for two days — that there was nothing to find. That Elias Vane's questions had lodged in her ear like a splinter and her mind was doing what minds did: worrying at it until the flesh around it swelled.

The shop smelled of ink and lamp oil and the ghost of tallow candles long since replaced by better ones. Rain drummed the skylight above the composing stone. Somewhere beyond the wall, a cart rattled through standing water on Great Ancoats Street, and a woman called out something about chestnuts or matches or salvation — in this district the three were often sold from the same tray.

Bram pulled the 1821 ledger toward her across the scarred oak of the counting desk.

It was a thick book, bound in marbled boards gone soft at the corners, its spine cracked in the way of books that had lived honest working lives. Her father's hand filled it: *Kenyon & Ishikawa, Printers*, though by 1821 the Kenyon of that partnership was already dead and the name remained only because customers trusted it more than they trusted an Ishikawa alone. The entries marched down each page in her father's small, upright script — a hand that had learned its letters in Nagasaki and then had them beaten into English order by a writing master in Liverpool who charged sixpence a lesson and called him "Jap" to his face for eleven years.

*5000 bills, Harrow Mill, cotton twist, 4d per 100.*
*1000 circulars, Manchester & Salford Equitable Co-op, 7s 6d.*
*2000 posters, Theatre Royal, blood-red ink, 12s.*

Ordinary work. Twenty years of ordinary work pressed flat between boards. She knew this book the way a priest knows his breviary; she had copied entries from it as a girl, seated on a stool too tall for her, while her father cut type at the stone behind her and her brother Kenji practiced his serifs on scraps of spoil paper.

She had not opened it since his death.

That was the thing she would not say even to herself: that she could have looked at these pages any time in the four years since he died, and had chosen not to, because his handwriting was a kind of voice, and hearing a dead man's voice unasked-for, in a dark room, with no one to see you flinch —

The candle guttered. She trimmed the wick and made herself begin.

*January, 1821.* Bills for the Bridgewater Arms. A funeral card for a child named Ogden — *small lot, black border, paid half, balance forgiven*, her father noting the mercy without comment, because mercy between tradesmen was never spoken aloud. February brought election squibs and a price list for a corn factor. March —

March was when the strike began. Everyone in Ancoats remembered March of '21 the way you remembered the year of a fever. The mills had cut the piece-rates, and the hands had come out, first at Harrow and then along the canal like sparks jumping a gap, and for six weeks the district had held its breath and the masters had held their keys.

Bram turned the pages slowly. April. May.

And there — her finger stopped on the page as though the paper had grown hot.

The entry began in her father's usual hand, neat and level:

*Broadside, memorial, Harrow Mill deceased,*

And stopped.

Stopped mid-word, mid-line, the pen lifted so abruptly that the final stroke of *deceased* trailed off into nothing — not a period, not a dash, just the letter's last hairline thinning away into the paper as if the hand itself had been snatched backward. Below it, blank space. Then, three lines down, a different entry entirely, dated five days later:

*2000 bills, Renwick & Sons, new steam looms, 9s.*

Bram sat very still.

She read the broken line again. And again. The candle flame leaned in the draught from the door and righted itself. Outside, the rain went on stitching the district shut.

A broadside, memorial, Harrow Mill deceased. Thirty-one names — thirty-one, if Vane's arithmetic was to be believed, and why else would a census man count the same households twice? — set down on paper, printed, pasted to walls and posted to parishes, read aloud in chapels from Ancoats to Ardwick. That was what should have followed that line. That was what any decent shop in Manchester would have run off within the fortnight, grief being grief and trade being trade.

There was no such broadsheet. She knew it now the way she knew the contents of her own type cases. She had spent twenty years in this trade and she had never once seen a death notice for Harrow Mill. Not a bill, not a card, not a mourning border in any window. The mill had burned with thirty-one souls inside it and the printing houses of Manchester had printed — what? Ballads about the weather. Advertisements for Renwick's new steam looms.

Her father had taken the commission. Had begun to enter it, pen to paper, in his own book, in his own hand.

And someone had made certain it was never finished.

* * *

She did not sleep that night. Sleep was for people whose fathers' ledgers behaved themselves.

In the morning she opened the shop late, sent the apprentice Jem home with a sovereign's worth of excuses about damp type-metal, and went back to the books. There were other volumes — she fetched them all, hauling them down from the shelf above the stone where they had sat undisturbed like urns: 1819, 1820, 1822, 1823. She laid them open across the composing stone in a row, weighting the pages with quoins, and worked through them the way a surgeon works, methodically, refusing to look at the whole wound until she understood its edges.

The 1821 volume had gaps. Not torn pages — nothing so crude. But pages that had been removed cleanly at the binding, four of them between May and June, the stubs still visible if you tilted the book to the light and pressed the spine apart with your thumbs. She found the knife-marks where a razor had slipped once, a fine nick in the board beneath.

Someone had cut the leaves out with care, in this room, at this desk.

She thought: *my father did it himself.* And the thought arrived complete, dressed in its own logic — he was careful, he kept his accounts exact, he would not suffer loose leaves — and she rejected it just as completely, because she had watched that man keep accounts through a fire scare, through cholera summer, through the week Kenji died. He entered everything. He entered the sixpence he forgave a grieving mother. He would sooner have cut off his own hand than cut out a page.

But someone had.

She turned to the day-book — the rougher book, the one where orders came in before they were entered fair — and found May of 1821 nearly empty. Two entries only. One for posters for a public house. And one, dated the ninth of May, nine days after the fire, in her father's hand but pressed hard enough to tear the paper:

*Mr. T. Clegg calls. Says the memorial is not wanted. Says it is wanted NOT AT ALL. Paid what was promised and twice over. Would not leave his name in the book.*

Below that, after a space, smaller, almost hidden against the margin, written sideways as though the pen had been turning the thought over even as it wrote:

*What sort of man pays a printer to forget?*

Bram put her palm flat over the words. The paper was cold and slightly furred with age, and under her hand it felt like the flank of some animal that had lain down in these pages twenty years ago and never got up again.

Thomas Clegg. She knew the name the way everyone in Ancoats knew it — Clegg had been Harrow Mill's overlooker, the man who walked the floors with the wage list rolled in his fist like a truncheon. He had left Manchester after the fire, gone south somewhere, and good riddance said every weaver who'd ever drawn short pay under him. But here was his name in her father's book, attached not to cruelty on the mill floor but to a quieter crime: the crime of paying for silence, and paying double, and knowing exactly which door to knock on.

Because her father's shop was where the memorial would have been printed. Of course it was. Half the district's hands had been taught their letters from primers the Ishikawa press had run off; the Japanese man who cut beautiful type and asked no questions about your politics had been trusted by strikers and chapel-folk alike. If anyone in Manchester was going to print those thirty-one names, it was Kenzō Ishikawa.

And Thomas Clegg had known it, and had come here, and had bought the forgetting wholesale.

*Paid what was promised and twice over.*

Bram closed the day-book gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child.

Then she sat in the grey morning light with her hands in her lap and let herself feel what she had been holding off since the census man's visit: not suspicion, which was a tool, but grief, which was a country. Because she could see it now — could reconstruct it whole, the way she could look at a forme of type and see the page it would become. Her father taking the commission from some widow or chapel committee, entering it in his book, beginning to plan the setting — and then the knock at the door, and Clegg's purse, and the choice.

He had chosen silence. He had cut the page out — no, wait. He had not cut the page out, because the razor-nick and the missing leaves argued for a later hand, a tidier hand. He had simply stopped. Stopped mid-word. Left the broken entry standing in his own book like a tooth knocked out, because some part of him had refused to make the record clean. *What sort of man pays a printer to forget?* He had written his shame sideways in the margin and then he had lived with it for nineteen years, and he had never once said a word of it to her.

Never a word. All those evenings at the stone, teaching her to kern and justify, telling her stories about Nagasaki, warning her — always warning her, *Bram, be quiet, be careful, a girl who passes must pass perfectly* — and not once had he said: I was paid to bury thirty-one names and I took the money, and I have been dying of it ever since.

Or had he tried to say it, near the end? She cast back, hunting through the last conversations the way she was hunting through the ledgers. He had been ill eight months. In the final weeks he had grown strange, had gripped her wrist once with terrible strength and begun, *"When I am gone, you must know—" *and she had hushed him, God help her, she had hushed him, said *nothing matters but you getting well, Father,* because she could not bear deathbed confessions, because she had needed him steady, needed the shop steady, needed everything steady —

She had hushed him. And he had let himself be hushed, because protecting her from hard truths had been his life's second trade.

The candle had burned to a puddle. Daylight stood in the skylight, pale and indifferent. Down the street the mills were sounding their bells, calling the shift on, and the sound rolled over Ancoats like it had rolled for forty years, unbothered, certain of itself.

Bram rose and went to the case marked *Long Primer* and stood before it with her fingers resting on the frame, not taking type, just touching the wood her father's hands had worn glossy. Twenty years of careful silence. That was what she had inherited — not the shop only, but the silence, maintained like a machine, oiled daily with small refusals. Don't answer the question. Don't meet the eye. Don't ask what became of the men who left Harrow Mill.

Vane had asked. Twice now. Once about the ledgers, once about the workers who *left*. And she had given him the smooth non-answers she gave everyone, and he had nodded and thanked her and gone, and she had told herself the splinter would work itself out.

It had not worked itself out. It had festered, and tonight it had burst, and underneath it was this: a broken line in her father's hand, four razored pages, a name — Clegg — and thirty-one people whom no bell in Manchester had ever tolled.

She thought of Martha Quill, who had come out of Harrow Mill alive and never spoke of it, selling her matches in the alley by the canal with her shawl pinned like armour. Martha had been there. Martha had seen whatever there was to see.

And she thought of Elias Vane, who counted households twice and looked at her with eyes that were doing their own kind of arithmetic, and who had said, in his mild, unhurried way, *Curious, isn't it, how many folk a fire can make invisible.*

Bram blew out the candle although daylight made it pointless, an old habit, a closing-the-shop gesture, and stood in the sudden dimness listening to the mill bells die away.

Tomorrow, she decided. Tomorrow she would go to the parish records, and to the newspaper files at the Exchange newsroom, and find out whether any notice of those deaths had ever appeared anywhere in the kingdom of print — proof, if proof existed, of how wide the forgetting had been bought. And after that, if the trail held, she would go and stand in front of Martha Quill's match tray and ask the question that cost something to ask.

But tomorrow was not yet here. Tonight there was one thing more.

She took up the 1821 ledger one last time and carried it to the window, tilting it against the light, pressing the spine apart where the pages had been cut away. Four leaves, gone. But paper leaves a shadow of itself in the binding glue, and old printers' ink sometimes transfers — and there, faint as breath on glass, offset against the stub of the last remaining leaf, she could make out the ghost of what had been written on the page now missing.

Not an account entry. A list. Columns of names — she could see the regular downward rhythm of them, dozens of strokes, each one a word ending in what looked like a date.

Thirty-one times over, perhaps.

Someone had written the names of the Harrow Mill dead into her father's ledger, and someone else had cut them out — and her father, who entered everything, who forgave sixpences and noted mercies, had let the cutting stand.

Which meant the person with the razor had not been Clegg. Clegg's visit was nine days after the fire; the offset ink was older, deeper. Someone closer than Clegg. Someone with access to this room, this shelf, this book.

Bram lowered the ledger and stood very still in the grey light, the shop silent around her except for the tick of the cooling stove and the far-off roar of the district going about its evening, and she understood that she had opened a door in her own house and found another house behind it.

Three days later, a knock came at the street door, and through the glass panel she saw the narrow silhouette of Elias Vane, hatless in the drizzle, holding a document case — and this time he did not ask about households at all.
