## 4. The Taker of Names

Elias Vane had counted names for eleven years, and he had learned that every name was a small door. You knocked politely. Sometimes the door opened onto a family of six crowded above a dye-house, all of them alive and hungry and ordinary. Sometimes it opened onto nothing at all — a room where a wife had died in winter and no one had thought to amend the schedule, a household of ghosts that persisted on paper long after the flesh had gone to the churchyard. The census did not care which it found. It only asked him to write what stood before him, in his round clerk's hand, in the columns provided: name, age, occupation, place of birth.

It was the place of birth column that had brought him back to Ashgrove's print shop.

He told himself, on the walk from Ancoats, that it was the signatures he wanted. The head of household had failed to sign the enumerator's book on his first visit, and the Registrar's office was strict about signatures; a schedule without one came back marked deficient, and deficiencies came out of an enumerator's pay. That was the official reason, and Elias repeated it to himself twice as he crossed the canal bridge, and a third time as he turned into the narrow street where Ishikawa's shop sat wedged between a chandler's and a house that had been empty so long its windows had gone grey with dust.

But he was not a man who lied well, even to himself. He had never been able to manage it. His father had been the same — Josiah Vane, who could not hold his tongue at Harrow Mill, who said once too often what the overlookers deserved, and who had gone into the mill on the morning of the fire with four hundred other hands and come out of it as a line in a ledger Elias had spent eleven years pretending not to read.

The shop smelled of ink and lamp oil and the particular metallic sweetness of type metal, a smell that reached into Elias's chest and turned something over there. His father had worked a press, once, before the mill took him — a hand-press in a cellar in Salford, printing ballads and funeral verses and once, memorably, a pamphlet against the Corn Laws that had cost him two days' wages in fines. Elias had been seven. He still remembered the smell of his father's collar on those nights, ink ground so deep into the cloth that no washing ever lifted it.

So when he pushed open the shop door and the bell above it gave its cracked little ring, and the printer looked up from his composing stone, Elias felt the lie about signatures dissolve like sugar in water.

"Mr. Ishikawa." He touched his hat brim. "I've come for your signature. The schedule wasn't countersigned."

The printer — Bram Ishikawa, though Elias had already begun, privately, to doubt the name — set down his composing stick with the deliberate slowness of a man putting down something that might be mistaken for a weapon. He was younger than Elias had first taken him for, now that he looked properly. Smooth-cheeked under the lamplight, though the shadow of the room and the ink stains helped. There was something in the way he held his shoulders, drawn up and slightly forward, like a man perpetually about to step through a doorway too low for him.

"Of course," Ishikawa said. His voice was quiet, pitched low. "You'll forgive the state of the place. We're rushing a job."

"I can see that." Broad sheets lay stacked by the door — the Renwick commemorative order, if Elias guessed right, the celebration of Harrow Mill's twenty years of prosperity. He had passed a copy pasted to a wall on Deansgate that morning. TWENTY YEARS OF INDUSTRY, it said. A GOLDEN RECORD. He had stood looking at it for longer than a census taker ought to look at anything.

Ishikawa fetched the enumerator's book and signed where indicated — *Bram Ishikawa*, the letters formed with the same precision the man brought to everything, each stroke considered, nothing wasted. Elias watched the hand rather than the name. A printer's hand. Steady. Careful. And yet the grip on the pen was white at the knuckles.

"You keep late hours," Elias said. It was not a census question. He knew it was not a census question even as it left his mouth.

"The work keeps them." Ishikawa closed the book and handed it back. "Is there anything else? Only I have three hundred of these to set before Friday."

*Three hundred broadsheets celebrating a golden record.* Elias turned his hat in his hands. He should go. Every instinct trained into him across eleven years of doors and ledgers said: take the signature, thank the man, move to the next house. The census did not ask him to notice things. The census did not want noticing. It wanted columns filled and schedules filed and a quiet district counted without incident.

But his father had not been able to hold his tongue, and whatever inheritance Elias had received from Josiah Vane, it apparently lived below the tongue, in the chest, in the thing that turned over whenever he smelled ink.

"There is one matter," Elias said. "Not strictly census business."

Ishikawa went very still. It was remarkable, really, how completely still — a stillness Elias had seen before only in men who had served time, or soldiers, or animals that had learned that movement draws the eye.

"Oh?"

"On my first visit," Elias said, "I asked you whether the shop printed death notices after the fire at Harrow Mill. In 1821. You said you didn't recall."

"I don't." Flat. Immediate. Too immediate — a door shut before the knock finished.

"No," Elias agreed mildly. "Twenty years is a long time. A man forgets whole years of his trade." He let the silence sit a moment, then nodded at the broadsheet stack. "May I? While I'm here."

Ishikawa hesitated, then gestured assent. Elias lifted the top sheet from the stack and read it standing there in the lamplight, though he had read its twin on Deansgate. Twenty years of industry. The golden record. Sir Aldous Renwick's name set in letters an inch high, and beneath it, smaller, the roll of the mill's achievements: output, expansion, dividends. Not one word anywhere on the sheet about the hands who had made that record. Not one word about any year but the triumphant ones.

"It's a fine piece of work," Elias said. "Whoever cut this type understood his letters. Look how the R sits on its tail. Most shops would have used a stock face." He set the sheet down carefully, aligning its edge with the ones beneath, because he could not help himself; disorder bothered him the way lies bothered him. "My father was a compositor. Before he went into the mills. I grew up with type metal under my fingernails. So I notice these things."

"Your father was a compositor," Ishikawa repeated. Something moved behind his eyes — not suspicion exactly. Recognition, maybe. The wariness of a man hearing another man speak a language he'd been taught to pretend he didn't know.

"And then he was a weaver at Harrow Mill," Elias said. "For nine years. Until the fire."

The lamp flame bent slightly in a draught from the ill-fitting window. Neither man moved to shield it.

"That was a terrible night," Ishikawa said at last. "The whole district mourned."

"Did it?" Elias kept his voice level, almost pleasant. He had practised this tone in front of mirrors, in front of windows, walking alone along the canal. A tone that asked nothing, accused nothing, demanded nothing. "I was eight years old. My mother took me to the gates the next morning. There was a crowd, but nobody was mourning. They were waiting. Waiting for the owners to publish the list of the dead. And the list never came. Do you remember that, Mr. Ishikawa? Did your shop print the list?"

"I told you. I don't—"

"Your father ran this shop then. Samuel Ishikawa. He was the best letter-cutter in Manchester, they say — the only man in the district who could cut a memorial face worth the name. When a child died, families came here for the funeral cards. When the Reform Union lost its men at Peterloo's anniversary meetings, they printed the names here. Everyone knows this. Everyone remembers that when the dead needed counting, it was Ishikawa who counted them." Elias paused. "Except once. Except 1821. No card. No list. No broadsheet. Nothing. As if thirty-one men and women simply walked out of the world and no one thought to write their names down."

The silence stretched so long that somewhere in the street a cart rattled past, and its noise seemed to belong to another country.

"How do you know the number?" Ishikawa said finally. His voice had dropped further, until it was barely above the sound of the lamp burning. "Thirty-one. Who told you thirty-one?"

*There it is,* Elias thought. *There's the crack.*

"The census," he said.

Ishikawa frowned. "The census doesn't begin until tonight. You're verifying schedules ahead of the count. How could—"

"Not this census." Elias reached inside his coat and drew out a folded sheaf of papers, soft-worn at the edges, carried so long they had taken the shape of his body. "The one before it. 1831. I wasn't the enumerator then — an old man named Croft was, and he drank, and he hurried, and he wrote down what people told him without asking twice. But numbers are stubborn things, Mr. Ishikawa. You can hurry past them, but they wait for you. I've spent eleven years comparing schedules. Households. Parishes. The burial registers at St. Philip's — incomplete, of course, half the poor buried without fee or record. But between them all, a pattern emerges." He laid the papers on the composing stone, between the type cases, and unfolded them with the reverence of a priest handling relics. "In 1819, Harrow Mill employed four hundred and twelve hands whose households appear in the district schedules. In 1821, after the fire, the same households account for three hundred and eighty-one. Thirty-one souls unaccounted for. Not emigrated — no ships' manifests match. Not relocated — no schedules in Stockport, Bolton, Salford. Not buried — the register has entries for only nine, all of them attributed to 'fever.' Thirty-one people, Mr. Ishikawa. Grown men, grown women, some of them children half-time workers. They were there in 1819 and they were nowhere at all in 1821. As if someone reached into the record with a wet thumb and smudged them out."

Ishikawa had not moved. He stood behind his stone with his hands flat on either side of the type cases, and his eyes were fixed on the papers, and his breathing had changed — Elias could hear it, shallow and quick, the breathing of a man reading something he cannot stop reading.

"My father," Elias said quietly, "was one of the thirty-one. Josiah Vane. Schedule forty-seven, household of three. In 1821 the household of three became a household of two. My mother never spoke of him again. Not his name, not the manner of it. She went quiet the way this whole district went quiet, and I grew up inside that quiet thinking it was just how things were. That grief was simply silent here, and that was all." He looked down at the papers. "It took me twenty years to understand that silence isn't grief's natural voice. Someone taught it to us."

"You should take these away," Ishikawa said. His voice was strange, strained. "You shouldn't have shown me. If anyone knew—"

"Knew what? That an enumerator counts? That's all I am, Mr. Ishikawa. A taker of names. I knock on doors and I write down what lives behind them." Elias began, slowly, to fold the papers again. "But a count cuts both ways. The census will show what the census shows. And when it's done, when the schedules are sealed and sent to London, there'll be a number in a column somewhere that says how many souls dwell in Ancoats. And someone in London may compare that number to the number from ten years ago, and the one from ten years before that. Numbers remember, Mr. Ishikawa. Even when people are paid to forget."

"Stop." The word came out sharp, and Ishikawa seemed startled by his own voice. He glanced toward the street door, then the inner door that led to the dwelling rooms, then back. "You don't know what you're asking. What you're implying. This district — you don't pull a thread here unless you know where it's anchored. Renwick holds half the mortgages on this street. His men drink in every public house. One word of this, one, and—"

"And what happened in 1821 happens again," Elias said. "I know. I've had eleven years to imagine what happened in 1821. Locked doors. Chained exits. A strike broken not by negotiation but by fire. And then a verdict of accident purchased from a doctor whose coach and horses Renwick paid for. I don't *know* any of it, Mr. Ishikawa. I only have numbers. Numbers are circumstantial. Numbers can be explained away. What numbers cannot do—" and here his own composure slipped, just for a moment, just enough that he heard the crack in his voice and hated it, "—what numbers cannot do is tell my mother why she spent thirty years setting a place at the table for a man who was never coming home, and taking it away again, and never once saying his name aloud."

The lamp burned. The type metal cooled in its trays. Somewhere overhead a floorboard creaked — the apprentice, perhaps, or the settling of an old building — and both men looked up at the sound like guilty creatures.

When Ishikawa spoke again, his voice had changed. The flatness was gone from it. What remained was quieter and far more dangerous.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because your shop printed nothing," Elias said. "And I need to know why."

"Perhaps my father was paid not to."

"Then someone paid him. And I need to know who."

"Perhaps my father was afraid."

"Everyone was afraid. Fear explains silence, Mr. Ishikawa. It doesn't explain a gap in a job book." Elias had done his homework; he had spent three nights in the Registrar's archive and two in a public house buying drinks for retired mill clerks, and one of those clerks, three gins deep, had talked about a memorial broadsheet commissioned in 1821 and never seen. "A commission comes into a print shop, it goes into the job book. Ink costs money. Paper costs money. Even a job refused gets written down — *declined*, *cancelled*, something, in somebody's hand. But there's a space in the world where that entry should be, and the space is clean. Clean spaces frighten me more than dirty ones. Dirty spaces show struggle. Clean spaces show money."

Ishikawa's hand had drifted, without the man seeming to notice it, to the shelf behind him — to the row of old job books, leather-spined, dusty, ranged in chronological order like a line of sleeping animals. His fingers rested on the spine of one near the end of the older group. He did not pull it out. But he touched it, and Elias saw him touch it, and neither of them acknowledged it, and that was somehow the most honest exchange they had made all day.

"I can't help you," Ishikawa said.

"No," Elias agreed. "Not yet."

"Then why—"

"Because in a week, or a month, or however long it takes Sir Aldous Renwick's historians to finish their golden record, the official story will harden. Twenty years of industry. A golden record. And underneath it, sealed forever, thirty-one names that no one living will be able to find. Except I've found them, Mr. Ishikawa. Or most of them. Household by household, schedule by schedule — I have thirty-one gaps, and I have probable names for twenty-six of them. Probable. Reconstructed from widows' remarriage records and apprenticeship papers and a burial register that lies." He tucked the papers back inside his coat, and his hands were steady now, steadier than they had been all morning. "Probable names are not enough. My mother went to her grave not knowing which of the stories was true — that her husband ran, that he was trapped, that he never existed at all. I won't hand the district probable names. I want certain ones. Witnesses. Documents. The truth with its boots on."

"And you came to a printer," Ishikawa said slowly, "because a printer is the man who makes truth wear boots."

"I came to a printer," Elias said, "because your shop printed nothing in 1821, and in twenty years of knocking on doors, that silence is the loudest thing I've ever heard."

He put on his hat. At the door he stopped, hand on the latch, and did not turn around.

"For what it's worth, Mr. Ishikawa — the schedule you signed today. Occupation: printer. Place of birth: Manchester." He paused. "The registrar's index cross-references births against parish baptisms. It's how I verified half my thirty-one. It's remarkable, the things a man can find in the records, when he goes looking. Things people believed were safely forgotten."

He heard, behind him, the small sharp intake of breath — the sound of a locked door realizing it had been seen.

"Good day," Elias said, and stepped out into the street, where the light was going coppery over the mill chimneys, and somewhere beyond them the great wheels of Harrow Mill were turning, turning, grinding the hours down into wages.

Behind him, through the glass of the shop door, darkened with grime though it was, he could see the printer standing motionless among his cases of type. And then — slowly, as if the movement itself were a confession — Bram Ishikawa reached up and took down the oldest of the job books, and opened it beneath the lamp, and began to turn the pages.

Elias Vane walked away without looking back again. He had knocked on thousands of doors. He knew better than most that what matters is never the answer you get at the threshold. It is what the person inside does after the door closes.
