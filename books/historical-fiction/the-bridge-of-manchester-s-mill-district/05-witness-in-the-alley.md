## 5. Witness in the Alley

The name came to me the way most dangerous things did in Ancoats: folded twice and passed without looking.

I had spent two evenings after closing going through my father's old delivery book — the one with the grease-stained corners that he'd kept even when the shop was too poor to keep a fire lit. Elias Vane's question about the death notices had lodged in me like a splinter under a nail. I told myself I was only satisfying curiosity, tidying an old account, nothing more. But I sat up past midnight with a candle burning low, tracing the faded entries of 1821, and there it was, on the third-to-last page of that year's book:

*Quill, M. — weaver, Harrow Mill — copy of petition, hand-delivered.*

No surname beyond the initial. No address. But my father had written *hand-delivered* beside it, which meant he had walked to her door himself, and men like my father did not walk far from Bridge Street unless the work mattered.

That was all I had. A letter M, a trade, and twenty-one years of Manchester doing what Manchester does to people, which is grind them fine and scatter them.

I went looking anyway.

* * *

The district wore its Sunday best even on a working Tuesday, which is to say it wore its poverty carefully, buttons mended, collars turned, everything arranged so that shame would have to look hard to find its mark. The general strike was coming. You could feel it gathering the way you feel weather behind hills — not in anything you could point to, but in the quality of the quiet. Men stood in knots at the corner of Great Ancoats Street speaking low and fast. Boys ran messages between public houses with the particular urgency of boys carrying words too large for them. At the mill gates the overlookers counted hands twice over, as if counting might hold the days together.

I kept my collar high and my hat brim low and let the streets read me as what I appeared to be: a young man about printing business, unhurried, unremarkable. Twenty years of practice. Bram Ishikawa had been buried under a boy's name so long that sometimes I caught myself thinking of her as someone I had known once, a sister I was keeping alive by forgetting her.

The match sellers worked the same ground as always — the mouths of alleys, the churchyard wall, the stretch outside the gin palace where pity and thirst arrived together. I bought matches from three of them before I found her.

She was at the mouth of a cul-de-sac off Redfern Street, a narrow throat of brick where the sun reached perhaps an hour a day. She stood with her tray hung from a strap around her neck, and she did not call out her wares the way the others did. She simply stood, still as furniture, and let the world come to her.

Martha Quill had been beautiful once; you could see the architecture of it beneath the ruin, the good bones, the level brow. Now she was maybe forty and looked sixty, her hands cracked red from phosphor and washing, her eyes doing the constant arithmetic of mothers — counting children, counting coppers, counting hours until dark. She had a girl with her, six or seven, sitting on a doorstep behind her sorting broken matches into bundles with the solemn efficiency of very small workers everywhere.

"Matches, sir," Martha said, without inflection. "Penny the dozen. Two for tuppence."

I bought a box I didn't need. Then another. She watched me do it with flat patience, the way women like her watch all men who linger — waiting for the real errand to show itself.

"Mrs. Quill," I said quietly.

Something moved behind her face. Not fear exactly. The place fear keeps its coat while it decides whether to come out.

"That's not my name."

"It's the name in my father's book." I kept my voice down. The alley was empty but alleys in Ancoats had ears the way mills had windows. "Bram Ishikawa. My father kept the print shop off Bridge Street."

"I don't know any print shop."

"You brought him a petition in 1821. About Harrow Mill. He delivered the copies back to your door himself."

For a long moment she said nothing. Behind her, the little girl held up a finished bundle, and Martha took it without looking, her eyes never leaving mine, and put it in her apron pocket. That small economy of motion told me more than her face did. Here was a woman who had learned to do two things at once so that neither could be taken from her.

"You've the wrong woman," she said. "And you're spending money you want back. Take your matches and go."

"Mrs. Quill—"

"Don't." The word came out sharp enough that the little girl flinched. Martha softened instantly, turning her head, touching the child's hair without breaking eye contact with me — again the double work. When she spoke again it was barely above breath. "Don't say that name in this street. There's people that ask after it. Have done, these weeks past. A gentleman. Well-dressed. Asks at the public houses, asks at the mission hall, asks has anyone seen Martha Quill that was at Harrow Mill. Offers money." Her lip did something that was not quite a sneer. "Offers money. To a match seller. Like offering a crown to a drowning woman instead of a rope."

My stomach tightened. "What gentleman? Did he give a name?"

"He gave money to them that answered. That's his name." She shifted the tray higher on her shoulder. "Now go away, sir. Whatever you're selling, I'm not buying."

"I'm not selling anything. I want to know what you saw. In '21. The night of the fire."

The silence that followed had weight to it, physical, almost audible — the silence of a door being barred from inside. Somewhere beyond the alley mouth a dray rumbled past; a man shouted a price for herring; the ordinary city went on with its noise, and inside the alley none of it touched us.

"I saw nothing," Martha said. "I was not there. I left the mill months before. Ask anyone."

"Your name is in the petition."

"Then your father wrote wrong." But her hands had begun to shake, small tremors she hid by gripping the tray strap until her knuckles whitened. "Or maybe he wrote right and it's the only true thing anyone ever wrote down about that night, and that's why they burned every other paper of it. Is that what you want to hear? Is that why you've come, twenty-one years after, to stand in a gutter and make me say it?"

I hadn't expected her to break open like that. I had prepared for denial, for stonewalling, for the weary theater of the poor performing ignorance for the respectable. I had not prepared for fury with grief underneath it, worn smooth as river stone.

"The doors were chained," I said. It wasn't a question. Elias Vane's file — the coroner's verdict, accidental, the doctor's signature — had shown me the shape of the lie, and lies have shapes, and once you know a shape you can see it through cloth. "Thirty-one hands locked in during the strike, and the fire came, and the owners paid to make sure no one ever printed otherwise. Didn't they."

Martha Quill looked at me for a long time. And then she did something I will remember until I die, whatever else becomes of me: she laughed. One dry syllable, no humor in it whatsoever, the laugh of a woman hearing a child recite the family curse accurately for the first time.

"Come here," she said. "Not into the alley. Round the corner, by the pump. Where folk can see us talking about nothing."

* * *

We stood at the street pump while she filled a chipped jug, our voices pitched under the traffic noise, two acquaintances discussing the price of bread. It was, I thought, its own kind of typesetting — arranging dangerous words in plain sight, choosing which faces to show the street.

"There were thirty-nine of us locked in that weaving shed," she said, staring straight ahead at the traffic. "Strikebreakers' shift, they called us — which was a joke, because half of us weren't breaking anything, we just couldn't afford the strike. Rent doesn't strike. Hunger doesn't strike." She pumped the handle, water drumming into the jug, covering her voice. "Renwick's men chained the outer doors at dusk. Both leaves. Padlocked, and a chain besides. I saw Tom Maddox do it with my own eyes, and Tom Maddox now drives a cart for Renwick and wears a good coat, so you may judge what seeing is worth."

"Why chain the doors?"

"So we couldn't walk out and join the pickets. So the mill could be said to be working full. It wasn't meant—" Her jaw worked. "Everyone says it wasn't meant. The lamp, the oil room, the dust in the air. Maybe it wasn't meant. But a door that opens only inward, and eight women and children at the far end of the shed, and smoke moving faster than any man can run — you can mean nothing and kill thirty-one souls all the same. Meaning is a luxury of the living."

The little girl appeared at the alley mouth, watching us with wide, serious eyes, and Martha's whole body changed — a subtle gathering, a mother drawing her borders tight.

"Eight died in the shed itself," I said. "The rest at the inner stair?"

"At the stair, and against the yard gate, which was locked from outside because of the pickets." She handed the girl the jug to carry and made her voice light, ordinary, the voice of a woman discussing weather. "You'll want me to swear to it. Write it down. Put my name on paper next to those names."

"Yes."

"No." She said it fast and flat, before hope could get a grip. "You think I haven't been asked before? Twice since the fire. Once by a kind newspaper man who vanished off the face of the earth, and once by a lawyer who wanted money first and courage second. Both times I said no. Both times my children ate that winter." She looked at me then, fully, and I understood that the flatness was the mercy — she was sparing me the sight of what it cost her. "I've got four. The eldest is twelve and works the card room at Angel Meadow, God help her, same trade, same masters, different chimney. You'll write my name on paper and that paper will find its way to a magistrate, or worse than a magistrate — to a foreman with a list, and my Nellie will be turned off without a character, and the younger ones will learn what their mam's honesty costs at dinner time. Truth is a rich man's meat, Mr. Ishikawa. Us poor eat silence, and it fills the belly better than you'd think."

"Martha—"

"Mrs. Quill," she corrected, gently, and it was worse somehow. "There's no Mrs. Quill anymore either. He drank himself into the canal four years back, which was the one kindness of his life, leaving me clear to be nobody. Nobody is safe. Nobody has a name worth putting on paper." She adjusted the tray on her hip, the matchboxes rattling softly like small dry bones. "You seem a decent lad. Your father was a decent man — he printed that petition fair and charged me nothing, and when I offered to pay him in weaving he said his loom was letters. He was the only soul in this town who ever treated what I said like it was worth ink."

The words went through me sideways, unexpected, aimed at a grief I hadn't invited to this meeting. My father. Dead eleven years, and still handing me gifts through strangers.

"He refused other work too," I heard myself say. "In '21. After. There's a gap in his job book where a memorial should be."

Martha stopped dead. Even the matchboxes went quiet.

"He told me once," she said slowly, "that the hardest job he ever took was the one he didn't take." Her eyes searched my face, looking for something — resemblance, maybe, some trace of the man in the daughter wearing a son's collar. "They offered him the commemorative broadsheet, after. Renwick's agent. Gold border, official text, *deplorable accident*, the whole liturgy of it. And your father — who couldn't afford coal that month, I knew his wife, I carried soup to that house — your father said no. Said his press wouldn't set a lie in display type. They found someone cheaper by Friday, and your father lost the mill accounts for ten years after, and never told anyone why, and went poorer and poorer and said nothing."

The alley seemed to tilt. All my life I had read that ledger gap as failure — a lost commission, an old man's mistake or weakness, one more entry in the long arithmetic of our decline. And it had been refusal. Deliberate, costly, silent refusal. He had starved rather than set those names in gold around the word *accident*. He had kept the gap in the book like a man keeps a wound clean.

He had also, I realized, kept me ignorant of it. Twenty years of silence, purchased at that price, and he'd never once spent the coin of it to make himself understood. Even his courage he had hidden from me. I didn't know whether to grieve him or argue with him, and he was eleven years past both.

"Mr. Ishikawa." Martha's voice pulled me back. "Go home. Print your bills and your ballads and your wedding notices. Let the dead of Harrow Mill rest however the masters let them rest. Because I'll tell you what happens to the ones who don't let well alone." She glanced down the street, toward the mill district, toward the smoke. "There's a man comes asking for me, like I said. Well-dressed. Polite as Sunday. And last week he found Danny Kershaw, that was a bobbin-boy in '21 and remembers everything, and Danny talked, because Danny's soft and the money was real. Danny's lodging house burned on Thursday. No one hurt — everyone happened to be out — but Danny sleeps in a hayloft now and won't say a word to anybody about anything, not even the weather." She picked up her daughter's hand. "That's the shape of the game, and I've got four lives in my keeping, and I fold. Do you understand me? I fold."

"I understand," I said. And I did — that was the terrible thing, I understood completely, I had folded myself for twenty years, I had built an entire life on the principle she was defending. "Thank you for telling me what you can't tell me."

She almost smiled. "That's printer's talk, that is. Your father's talk." Then, as I turned to go: "Wait."

I waited.

She reached into her apron pocket — the pocket where she'd stowed the bundle of broken matches her daughter had sorted — and pressed something into my palm, folding my fingers over it fast, so fast that anyone watching would have seen only a transaction concluded, change returned.

A scrap of paper, torn from the flyleaf of a prayer book, old and soft with handling. On it, in a laborer's careful hand, thirty-one names in three columns, each one spelled out with the anxious precision of someone who feared forgetting a single one. Some were marked with a cross. Two had dates beside them — birthdays, I realized. Mothers' records. The private census of the uncounted.

"Danny Kershaw wrote that list the week after the fire," Martha said. "Before they got to him. Before he learned to forget. I've kept it in my pocket eleven years — since my husband went in the canal, since I decided somebody ought to carry it who wasn't too frightened or too drunk." Her voice stayed level, but her thumb brushed the edge of the paper where it vanished into my fist, one last touch, like letting go of a child's hand at a school gate. "I can't swear to what I saw. I've told you why, and you'll not argue me out of my children, so don't try. But paper remembers what women daren't. Take it before I come to my senses."

"Martha—"

"Go." She hoisted the tray. "Matches! Penny the dozen!" — loud now, the seller's cry restored, the woman vanishing back inside the vendor like a lamp behind shut glass. "And Mr. Ishikawa — that gentleman asking after me. He'll come asking after you next. He's already asked at every print shop in the district." She looked at me over her daughter's head, and for one instant the match seller fell away and I saw the weaver of 1821, young and furious and standing in front of a chained door. "He calls himself Sir Aldous Renwick's historiographer. Come to write the mill's history, proper and grand, everything in its place. And everything in its place means some things nowhere at all."

She turned into the alley, took up her daughter's hand again, and was gone into the brick shadow, and I stood at the pump with a box of matches I didn't need and a list of the dead warming in my closed fist, understanding that I had gone out to buy a witness and come home carrying a target.
