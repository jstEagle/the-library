## 6. The Owners' Historian

The letter came on a Tuesday, carried by a boy in livery too fine for Ancoats and too nervous for his errand. He stood in the doorway of Ishikawa & Son, Printers, holding the envelope the way one holds something that might bite, and when I took it from him he touched his cap twice and fled as though the shop itself might pursue him.

I set down my composing stick and turned the envelope over. Thick paper. Cream, not white — the color of money that has never had to apologize for itself. The seal was pressed deep, wax the shade of old blood, and stamped with a crest: a mill wheel quartered with a sheaf of wheat.

Harrow Mill.

My hands did what twenty years had taught them to do, which was nothing at all. They held the letter steady while my heart climbed into my throat and settled there like a stone swallowed whole.

*Mr. B. Ishikawa.* The direction was written in a clerk's copperplate, flawless and impersonal. Inside, on a second sheet nearly as heavy as the first:

*Sir Aldous Renwick requests the pleasure of Mr. Ishikawa's attendance at Harrow House, Thursday next, at two o'clock, on matters concerning a commission of printing.*

I read it three times. Then I folded it along its original creases, so precisely that no eye could tell it had been opened, and put it in the drawer where Father had kept his important papers — the drawer that still smelled faintly of his pipe tobacco, though he had been dead six years, though I had never once allowed myself to smoke a pipe in this room.

Through the window, over the rooftops toward the east, I could just see the stump of Harrow's chimney against a sky the color of dishwater. Twenty-one years since the fire. Twenty years since the district agreed, all together and without anyone saying so aloud, to stop speaking of it.

And now its owner wished to speak to me about printing.

* * *

Thursday came grey and windless, the sort of day Manchester manufactured by the dozen. I dressed with more care than I had dressed for my father's funeral: good black coat, brushed; collar clean and high enough to hide the line of my throat; hair cropped short again, though it had been growing shaggy through the spring and Elias Vane had looked at me twice, the second time too long, before I'd taken the scissors to it.

Elias. I had not seen him in nine days. Nine days since he'd sat across this very desk and told me, in the flat careful voice of a man handling live coals, that thirty-one names appeared on no census, no parish roll, no coroner's list. Thirty-one workers who had gone into Harrow Mill on the night of the strike and had not come out, and whom the record insisted had never existed at all.

Nine days, and I had done nothing but set type for other people's lies and turn the ledger's blank page over in my mind until I could see the ghost of the memorial broadsheet that should have been there — *In Memory of the Hands Lost* — and was not, because someone had paid for silence, and I suspected now whose hand had signed the payment, and it made me sick to suspect it.

I left word with Jem, the apprentice, that I'd be out on business, and walked east.

Harrow House sat above the district like a man watching a fight from an upstairs window. It had been built new within my lifetime, all pale stone and tall windows, set back from the road behind iron gates and lawns that must have cost more to keep green than a weaver earned in a decade. The gates stood open. That was its own message: *you may come in. Whether you leave is another matter.*

A footman took my card — I had cards printed, *B. Ishikawa*, the single initial doing its silent work — and showed me through a hall hung with portraits of Renwicks going back three generations, each face wearing the same expression of patient appetite, and into a study lined with books that had clearly been bought by the yard.

Sir Aldous Renwick stood at the window with his back to me, and for a moment I simply looked at him. He was past sixty, broad still rather than stooped, with a head of white hair cut short and a posture that suggested a man who had spent forty years being obeyed and saw no reason to alter the habit. When he turned, his face was ruddy and open, almost jovial, the face of a grandfather at Christmas.

"Mr. Ishikawa." He crossed the room with his hand extended. "Sit down, sit down. Tea? My housekeeper makes tea that would shame a Chinese emperor — no offense intended to your family name, I trust."

"None taken," I said, and sat, and declined the tea, and watched him pour himself a cup with the unhurried movements of a man who owned every clock in the room.

"You'll wonder why I've asked you here." He said it lightly, settling into the chair opposite mine, and I noticed then the third person in the room, whom I had somehow failed to mark: a thin gentleman in spectacles seated at a side table with a writing case open before him, who now glanced up with the quick, cataloguing look of a man memorizing my face.

"My secretary," Renwick said, following my glance. "Mr. Pell. He forgets nothing, which is either a virtue or a disease. Now." He leaned back. "You know Harrow Mill, of course."

"I know it," I said carefully, "as every printer in this district knows it. Your works have kept half the shops in Ancoats fed for thirty years."

"And your shop fed us in return, eh? Your father — the elder Ishikawa — did work for us. Wage bills, notices, that sort of thing. Good hand, they said. Meticulous." Something moved behind the joviality, quick and cold as a fish under ice. "Meticulous men are rare."

"He was," I said.

"And you've inherited his habits, I'm told. And his press." He sipped his tea. "Which brings me to my purpose. Next summer marks twenty-five years since the founding of the new Harrow works — and twenty-one years since the fire, which I intend to address in the same volume, frankly and finally, so that the matter is settled in print for all time and no sensible person need raise it again."

There it was. Delivered between one sip and the next, smooth as a bill of exchange.

"A history," I said.

"A *history*." He savored the word. "Not a pamphlet, Mr. Ishikawa — a proper book. Two hundred pages at least. The rise of the firm, the growth of the district, the benefactions — the schools, the chapel roof, the widow's fund. And the fire treated honestly: a terrible accident, a tragedy of the age, machinery and carelessness and locked doors that were locked against agitators, not against the hands within. Every fact documented. Every question answered." He smiled. "So that afterward, when some hothead stands on a platform and shouts about Harrow, any reasonable man can point to the book and say: *it is all there. It was investigated. It is finished.*"

Mr. Pell's pen scratched softly. Writing down my face, or perhaps writing down the words *locked doors* so they would exist in draft before they existed in type.

"The commission includes the printing, naturally," Renwick went on. "Fine work — rag paper, a commissioned frontispiece, the lot. Payment in advance. A hundred pounds for the setting and presswork alone, and I'm given to understand a hundred pounds is not a sum your shop sees often these days."

It was not. A hundred pounds was the shop's rent for a year and a half. A hundred pounds was new rollers, a repaired platen, coal through the winter, wages for Jem, food in my own mouth. A hundred pounds was survival, wrapped in cream paper and tied with a crest.

"I see," I said, because I could think of no safe alternative, and my voice came out level, and I hated how level it was.

"Do you?" He set down his cup and studied me, and for the first time the grandfather's face went away entirely, and what remained was older and harder and much more honest. "Then let me be plainer, because I respect plainness in trade. There are printers in this city who'd take my commission and set my book beautifully and say nothing to anyone, and there are others—" he paused, delicately— "who might let their politics interfere with their business. Chartists, some call them. I don't ask a man's politics when I buy his type. I ask only that the type do what it's paid for. Can you give me that assurance, Mr. Ishikawa?"

The study was very quiet. Somewhere below, the gates clanked; a carriage coming in, or going out. On the wall, the painted Renwicks watched with their patient appetite.

I thought of the ledger's blank page. Of my father stopping mid-entry, his pen lifting from the paper, and the silence he had bought and paid for and died inside of. I thought of Elias Vane's voice: *thirty-one names, and the record insists they were never born.* And I thought of Martha Quill in her alley with her matches and her children, and the way she had said *I have little ones, mister,* as if that were the whole of the argument, and perhaps it was.

And beneath all of it, cold and clear as water under ice, I thought: *he is telling me the price of the muzzle before he fits it. He wants me to nod. That is all. One nod, and the shop lives, and the book gets printed, and the last door on the truth closes forever with my own hands turning the lock.*

"Yes," I said.

Renwick's eyebrows rose pleasantly.

"Yes, I can assure you the type will do what it's paid for," I said. "That is what type does. It is ink and lead, sir. It has no opinions." I met his eyes. "But I'll want the terms in writing, and I'll want to see Mr. Pell's manuscript before I commit press time. A job of this size — I must schedule around it."

Something flickered across his face — surprise, perhaps, that the mouse had negotiated — and then the joviality returned like a tide. "Sensible. Pell will call on you Monday with the outline and a draft chapter or two. You'll find the account of the fire scrupulous. Scrupulous," he repeated, tasting it. "That's the whole art of it, Mr. Ishikawa. Scrupulousness. Say everything, document everything, and choose what everything *is*."

He stood, and I stood, and he shook my hand with a grip like a vise wrapped in kid leather.

"You'll go far," he said. "Your father went far too. Quietly. It's the quiet ones who last in this town." At the door he paused, and without turning added, lightly, almost kindly: "Oh — and Mr. Ishikawa. There was a census man making a nuisance of himself round the district lately. Asking after old fires. If he troubles your shop, you needn't stand on ceremony. The Census Office takes a poor view of its clerks pursuing private grievances on public time. He could lose his post over it." A small pause. "Among other things."

"I haven't seen him," I said.

"No," Renwick agreed. "I imagine you haven't."

* * *

The walk home took longer than the walk out. I found myself stopping at corners, standing in doorways, letting carts pass, and it was some minutes before I understood that I was not avoiding anything — I was thinking, and my body had always thought better in motion, pacing the length of the composing room at midnight when a forme wouldn't lock.

*A hundred pounds.* Jem would eat. The roof would hold. And every summer henceforth, when the anniversary came round, the district would have its handsome book to point to — *scrupulous*, documented, *finished* — and the thirty-one would sink another fathom into the dark, and this time the stone over them would bear my imprint at the foot of the title page. *Printed by B. Ishikawa & Son.* Father's shop name. Father's lie. My signature under it, in ink that would outlast us both.

Unless—

Unless a book, once commissioned, belongs partly to its printer. Unless a manuscript passes through a compositor's hands, and a compositor reads every word — must read every word, to set it — and a manuscript that claims to answer *every question* about the fire must contain the questions themselves. What the owners chose to print would tell me exactly what they feared. Their omissions would be a map. Their careful scrupulousness would show me, page by page, the shape of the hole where thirty-one people used to be.

And Pell would deliver the drafts to my shop. To my press. Into my hands.

I stopped at the mouth of Angel Meadow, where the light was failing and the first gas lamps were being lit by a boy on a ladder, and I made myself say it plainly, the way I never said things plainly even to myself.

*I can take his hundred pounds and set his lies, and survive. Or I can take his hundred pounds and read his lies closer than any man alive, and learn where every body is buried — and decide afterward what my type will do.*

It was not yet refusal. It was not yet betrayal. It was a bridge, and I meant to walk to the middle of it and see both banks before I chose.

When I reached the shop the lamp was lit in the front window, which I had not left lit, and through the glass I could see a figure standing at my desk with his hat in his hands, reading the note I'd left for Jem — reading it upside down, from across the desk, which told me he had learned to read inverted type the way printers do.

Elias Vane looked up as I came in. His face was grey.

"Bram," he said quietly. "The watchman from Harrow Mill — the one who held the keys. Someone's been asking after him today. Not me." He swallowed. "Renwick's men. And I don't think they mean to ask him questions."
