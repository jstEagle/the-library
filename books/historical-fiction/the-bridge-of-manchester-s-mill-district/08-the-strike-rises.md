## 8. The Strike Rises

The first sign was not the drums. It was the ink.

Bram had mixed a fresh batch of lampblack and varnish at dawn — the same recipe her father had used, the same slow spiral of the pestle she could perform half-asleep — and when she lifted the lid off the stone jar, the surface trembled. Not from her hands. From the floor. A fine, continuous shiver running up through the flagstones of the shop, through the legs of the composing table, into the very type cases, so that the loose sorts in their compartments chattered like teeth.

She stood with the pestle dripping and listened.

Boots. Hundreds of boots, thousands, striking cobbles somewhere to the east, where the mills stood. Not marching — walking, a great unorganized tide of them, and beneath it voices, not shouting yet but humming, the way a crowd hums when it has decided something together and has not yet found the words.

She set the pestle down and went to the window.

The street below was empty in that particular way Manchester streets were never empty: no carts, no mill girls hurrying with shawls over their heads against the soot, no dray horses. The knocker-up who came down the row each morning banging doors had passed the shop without knocking on any of them. Every window along Ishikawa's row held the same pale face she saw reflected beside her own — printers, chandlers, the widow who kept the pie shop — all looking east, all silent.

The silence lasted until eight o'clock.

Then it broke like a dam.

It began as a sound she felt before she understood it — a low roar rolling across the rooftops from the direction of Ancoats, swelling as it came, and then the head of the procession swung into the square below the shop and Bram forgot how to breathe for a moment. She had seen processions. She had seen funerals, Orange marches, the great Chartist meetings on Kersal Moor that drew crowds like weather. This was none of those things. This was the district itself walking. Mill hands still in their aprons. Colliers with white eyes in black faces. Women carrying hammers wrapped in red flannel. Boys on the railings. A man hauling a handcart piled with loaves, guarded by four women like something sacred. And above them, on poles and pike-shafts and broken broom handles, hundreds of papers fluttering — the People's Charter, printed and reprinted in a hundred cellars, and Bram knew at least six of those cellars by name.

*Peaceably if we may,* said a banner in letters three feet high. *Forceably if we must.*

"Mr. Ishikawa!"

Jem Toller, the apprentice, stood in the doorway behind her, his cap crushed in both fists. He was sixteen and terrified and trying to look excited instead. "They're calling at the shops. Asking printers to shut. The Union's sent round a circular — no work till the Charter's won."

"I heard." She did not turn around. Below, the crowd had thickened until the cobbles disappeared, a river of caps and shawls flowing toward the town center, and along its edges moved other figures — not marchers. Men in good coats, walking quickly, heads bent together. And further back, where the street bent toward the tollgate, the glint of something that caught the light in a way cloth and wood did not.

Dragoons.

"They're saying the mills are stopping one after another," Jem went on. "Harrow first."

That turned her. "Harrow?"

"Aye — first thing this morning. They say the new master wouldn't even open the gates. Just stood on the wall and told them the strike was illegal and they'd be prosecuted for assembling." Jem swallowed. "There was some pushing. Some stones, they're saying. And then—" he hesitated, and Bram watched the boy decide how much truth he wanted to carry into her shop, "—then the dragoons came, sir. Sabres drawn. It's bad out there. It's going to be worse."

Bram looked back at the window. At the press under its dust sheet in the corner. At the forme standing locked on the stone — the one holding Elias Vane's careful copies, the watchman's confession still warm in her memory of setting it, thirty-one names she had not yet dared set because names in metal were a promise and promises could be seized.

Twenty years of quiet letters, and now the whole district had decided to shout at once.

"Shut the door," she said. "Bar it. And Jem — nothing goes out of this shop today. Nothing comes in that you don't see first."

The boy barred the door and stood with his ear against it, listening to the century turn over outside.

* * *

By noon the shop had become an island in noise.

The roar never fully stopped now; it changed shape, gathered and thinned, swelled again, and between its movements came sounds Bram catalogued the way she catalogued typefaces — automatically, precisely, because precision kept fear in its box. Glass breaking, two streets over. A whistle. Hoofbeats, many, at a trot. A woman screaming not in pain but in fury. Once, unmistakable even through brick and shutter, a single pistol shot, followed by a silence so complete that Jem looked up from the case he was pretending to sort and Bram's own hand stopped on the tympan lever.

"We should go home, sir," Jem said. His voice cracked on *sir*, which it did sometimes lately, and Bram pretended not to notice, as she always did. "My mum—"

"Go, then. I'll bar up after you." She softened it. "Mind the back lanes. Don't cross the square."

He went gratefully, and she was alone with the press and the formes and the shivering windows, and she allowed herself, for exactly one minute, to stand in the middle of the floor and feel what she felt. That the world she had built — the small, walled garden of routine and anonymity she had tended since she was seventeen years old and put on her dead brother's coat — was being trampled by something enormous that had no idea it was doing it. History did not knock. History kicked the door off its hinges and asked questions later, and every question it asked was the kind she had spent twenty years learning not to answer.

Elias would be out in it somewhere. Census men did not stop for riots; she suspected Elias Vane stopped for nothing except ledgers and graves. She thought of him with his notebook, walking into a sabre charge the way he walked into her shop — politely, relentlessly, writing everything down — and the thought frightened her more than the hooves did, which told her something about herself she filed away to examine never.

At half past two, someone hammered on the door.

Not the crowd-hammer, the flat desperate drumming of a mob. Three blows, spaced, deliberate, weakening. Then a sound like a sack of meal sliding down a wall.

Bram went to the peephole her father had drilled in 1819, after Peterloo, because her father had learned the same lesson every printer in Manchester learned eventually: keep one eye free. Through the brass tube she saw the step, and on the step a shape.

A man, crumpled against the doorpost. Young. One arm pressed to his side, and even in the grey light through the fanlight she could see the wet shine on his fingers. Behind him the street ran empty — the crowd had rolled on toward the town center and left its wreckage behind — but emptiness, today, meant only that whatever came next had not yet arrived.

Every instinct she owned said leave him. Twenty years of instincts said it in chorus: *not your trouble, not your name, not your blood on your step when the magistrates come asking.* Her father had survived two decades of Manchester by keeping his press clean and his opinions in the type of other people's invoices.

But she had already set thirty-one names in her mind. She had already read a dying watchman's confession aloud to make sure she'd transcribed it true. Somewhere between the census taker's first question and last night's candle-end, the arithmetic of her life had quietly inverted, and the old sum no longer balanced.

She shot the bolt.

Getting him inside took everything she had. He was taller than her by a head and dead weight on his right side, and she got her shoulder under his good arm and walked him backward step by step into the shop while he tried to help and mostly failed, gasping apologies that smelled of gin and blood. She got him onto the bench by the cold stove, barred the door, and only then let herself look at the damage.

A slash across the ribs, left side, long and shallow but ugly — sabre work, or a trooper's edge laid on carelessly rather than aimed to kill. He'd bled plenty but not fatally; she had seen enough of both kinds of wound in this district to know the difference. Under the blood and coal dust he was very young, younger than Jem, with the weaver's calluses and the flattened chest of a man who had leaned into a loom since childhood.

"Hold still." She fetched the spirit bottle, the linen, her mother's needle case — her mother, who had stitched silk in Nagasaki and would have laughed at what became of her needles here. "Name?"

"Tom." A hiss as the spirit went in. "Tom Larkin. Harrow's second room." He managed a ruined grin. "You'll be the printer, then. They said Ishikawa's was a safe house."

"Who said that?"

"The lads." His grin faded as fast as it came. "Said you print fair and ask nowt."

*I print fair and ask nowt.* There was her epitaph, delivered by a bleeding boy on her bench. She tied off the bandage and did not argue with it.

"How many hurt out there?" she asked.

"Dunno, sir. Fifty? Hundred?" His eyes had gone glassy with pain and exhaustion. "They rode into us on the toll road. No warning. Mr. O'Connor says it's war now, proper war. They're calling everyone to the fields Sunday — Kettledrum Fields — biggest meeting ever seen, they say. Half a million." He blinked slowly. "We shut Harrow first thing. All of us together. Nobody scabbed." Pride surfaced through the pain like a fish. "First time in my life I've been part of summat."

Bram sat back on her heels.

Harrow. Shut by its own hands, first of all the mills, this morning — and this morning she had felt the strike coming through a jar of ink. The mill whose fire Elias Vane could not stop circling. The mill whose owners had buried thirty-one workers under a verdict of *accidental* and were now, this very month, commissioning a commemorative history to pour concrete over the grave forever. Those hands' sons and daughters had walked out together at dawn, singing or silent, not knowing they were also walking out of history's most successfully suppressed chapter.

"You know much about Harrow's old days?" she asked, too casually, wiping the needle. "Before your time?"

Something flickered in Tom Larkin's face. Something that closed.

"My gran worked there," he said carefully. "Till '21."

"And after?"

"After what, sir?"

"After the fire."

The boy looked at her for a long moment, and Bram recognized the look, because she had worn it in mirrors for twenty years: the look of a person deciding which question is safe. He had learned it young. They taught it early in this district, the curriculum of silence.

"People don't talk about that," he said finally. "My gran never did. Only once, when she was near her end, she said —" he stopped himself, winced, whether from the wound or the memory Bram couldn't tell — "she said the count was wrong. Said it always bothered her, the count. Thirty-one should have been thirty-two, she said, and then she made me swear never to repeat it, and I haven't, not to anyone, and I don't know why I'm saying it now except you've a kind face when you think nobody's watching, sir."

The shop was very quiet. Outside, far off, the crowd roared and subsided like a sea that had forgotten the shore.

"Thirty-one," Bram repeated. "Should have been thirty-two."

"That's what she said. Never said who." His eyes were closing despite himself, pain and gin dragging him under. "Sorry, sir. Can't hardly—"

"Sleep," she said. "You're safe till dark, and I'll find you better than that after."

He slept almost instantly, breathing shallow but steady, one hand curled loosely around the red flannel scrap from a banner that someone had bound round his wrist, and Bram sat looking at him and feeling the ground move under her life the way it had moved under the ink that morning.

Thirty-two. A grandmother's deathbed arithmetic, matching the census taker's ledger-ghosts. Another thread, and every thread she pulled now seemed to lead into the same buried room.

* * *

Dusk came down brown and smoky over the district, and with it came the soldiers.

She heard them first — the change in the hoofbeats from trot to canter, the jingle of harness gone from background to foreground — and went to the peephole. Down the length of the street rode a column of dragoons, torches lit though it wasn't full dark, moving house to house. At each door an officer dismounted, hammered, spoke briefly to whoever answered, and moved on. At the pie shop the widow stood in her doorway shaking her head. At the chandler's, the same. Reading houses, she realized. They were searching reading rooms and printing offices — the Chartist circulars had named the printers they trusted, and the magistrates had read the same list.

The officer reached the shop next door to hers, then crossed the pavement.

Three hammer-blows on her door. Bram's heart went once, hard, and settled.

Through the peephole: a young lieutenant, tired-eyed, torchlight jumping on his chinstrap. On her step where Tom Larkin had lain, the blood had dried to a black fan on the stone, and she watched the lieutenant's gaze travel directly to it and stop.

Behind her, on the bench, the wounded striker breathed in his sleep. Slowly, Bram raised one hand and motioned Jem — who had crept back at dusk, white-faced, unable to stay away — to get the boy down the cellar stairs. Now. The trapdoor. The blanket over the coal.

She opened the door on the chain, six inches of lamplight falling out into the torchlit street.

"Print shop?" the lieutenant said. It was not quite a question. His eyes went past her shoulder, cataloguing: the press under its sheet, the type cases, the forme on the stone. Everything a magistrate's list cared about. "Ishikawa's?"

"It is."

"You've not produced seditious matter, I trust. No Charter copies. No strike circulars." He recited it flatly, a boy performing a script written by older, harder men, and his gaze dropped again to the blood on his own boot-toe, on her step. "There's been violence at the tollgate. A printer in Angel Street was taken this afternoon with three hundred Chartist addresses drying on his stone. He'll be transported, likely." He said it without relish, without appetite, the way a clerk reads a sentence. Then, quieter, almost off the record: "There's blood on your step, Mr. Ishikawa."

"A customer fell," Bram said. "Cut himself. He's gone home."

The lieutenant looked at her for a long moment across the six inches of chain. Torchlight moved on his face. Somewhere behind him a woman was shouting from an upstairs window, and the column waited, horses stamping, patient as winter.

"A lot of people falling down today," he said at last. "Keep your door barred, sir. Curfew at nine. And if any of your trade friends come round with propositions—" he glanced, deliberately, at the covered press, "—remember that presses can be confiscated faster than they can be rebuilt. Good evening."

He touched his cap and moved on down the row, and Bram closed the door and stood with her forehead against the cool wood and counted ten breaths.

When she turned, Jem was at the top of the cellar stairs, ashen. "Sir — there's a man down there asking for you. Says his name's Vane. Says—" the boy swallowed, "—says he's sorry to come at such a time, but he's found another name for the list. Says it couldn't wait for morning."

Bram looked at the barred door, at the sleeping boy below, at the forme of type on the stone holding a confession that was now worth a transportation sentence or worse to anyone who seized it.

Outside, the curfew bell began to ring.

"Let him in," she said. "And Jem — bar it behind him quick."
