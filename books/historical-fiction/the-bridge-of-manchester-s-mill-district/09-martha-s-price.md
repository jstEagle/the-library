## 9. Martha's Price

The match-seller's stall stood empty when Bram came back to the alley off Angel Meadow, and for a long moment she thought Martha Quill had fled — packed her children and her tray of tallow sticks and vanished into the rookeries where even the census could not follow. The morning was raw, a thin rain sifting down through the smoke of a thousand chimneys, and the alley smelled of wet ash and horse dung and the sweetish rot that clung to Manchester in every season. Bram stood at the mouth of it with her collar up and her heart going like a treadle, and she waited.

She had learned waiting from her father. Josiah Ishikawa had been a man who could stand over a case of type for six hours without shifting his weight, coaxing a recalcitrant sort into line with a tap of his bodkin, patient as stone. *The letters come when they come,* he used to say, in his accented English that the apprentices had mocked until Bram, aged nine, had made them stop. *You cannot hurry metal.* She had thought he meant only the trade. She was beginning to understand he had meant everything.

A quarter hour past, a shape detached itself from the shadow of the gin shop opposite. Martha came down the alley with her tray on her hip and her shawl drawn so low that only her chin showed, and even then she did not stop walking until she had passed Bram by two full paces and turned, as if by accident, to face the same blank wall.

"You came back." Her voice was flat as a worn rule.

"I said I would."

"Men say things. Printers say things. I've no use for either."

"I'm not here to sell you anything," Bram said. "I'm here to buy."

That got her. Martha's chin lifted an inch above the shawl, and one eye — brown, quick, ringed with the red of someone who slept badly — found Bram's face and held it. "Buy what?"

"Your memory."

* * *

They walked. It was safer than standing; Martha insisted on it, moving at a match-woman's ambling pace through streets where nobody looked twice at two women, because women were the one commodity Manchester produced in such abundance that even its own people had stopped counting them. They went up past the tanneries, where the stink covered conversation, and Martha talked without looking at her, eyes fixed ahead, voice pitched under the noise of the carts.

"Thirty-one," she said. "You want me to say thirty-one names into your machine so some magistrate can read them out and call it sedition."

"So a coroner can read them out and call it murder."

"There's no coroner in this county'll touch Renwick. There's no doctor, no alderman, no vicar. His money's in all their pockets like a hand in a glove." Martha spat, delicately, into the gutter. "I saw what I saw. Doors chained. I told you that much already and I've regretted it every night since."

"Why regret it? You told no one but me."

"Because telling is the first half of a thing, missus—or mister, or whatever you are—and the second half is being believed, and there's no second half coming for the likes of me. A match-seller swears an oath against Sir Aldous Renwick and what happens?" She ticked it off on fingers stained yellow with sulphur. "I'm a liar. Then I'm a troublemaker. Then I'm a woman of bad character, and my Nell can't get place as a servant, and my Sam can't get work at any mill in the district, and we're turned out of our room because no landlord wants the family that spoke. That's what happens. I've watched it happen to others. I kept my mouth shut twenty years and fed my children on the keeping of it."

"And now?"

Martha stopped walking. They were in the mouth of St. George's churchyard, where the gravestones leaned like bad teeth, and she turned and looked at Bram full in the face for the first time since the alley — a hard, exhausted, unflinching look, the look of a woman doing arithmetic with her own life.

"Now you come asking. Now the census man comes asking. Now there's strike in the air and Chartist men on every corner and the whole city wound tight as a forme, and you think—both of you think—that maybe the world's cracked open just wide enough for the truth to fall out of it." Her mouth twisted. "Maybe it has. But cracks close, printer. Cracks always close. And when this one shuts, it'll shut on me and mine."

"Then name your price," Bram said. "Not for the truth. For the risk."

* * *

It took Martha less time than Bram expected. Perhaps she had carried the price around for days, turning it over, testing its edges, the way Bram herself tested a new letterform against the light. They sat in the churchyard on a fallen slab, rain spotting the stone between them, and Martha laid it out plainly, without drama, which somehow made it worse.

"My three. Nell's fourteen and strong, Sam's eleven, and the little one, Alice, she's seven and sickly — chest like a paper bag. If I speak, they can't stay in Manchester. Not after. Not ever. Renwick's reach isn't a thing you dodge, it's a thing you live inside, like weather." She pulled the shawl from her face entirely, and Bram saw that she was younger than her voice — thirty-five perhaps, with grey already threading her hair. "Get them out. Get them to my sister in Leeds, safe passage, papers in order, and I'll swear your statement before any justice you can find who'll take it. Sworn, signed, my mark if need be. I'll give you the doors, the chains, the foreman Hobbs with the key-ring at his belt, the lot of it. I'll give you the faces at the windows."

"The faces at the windows," Bram repeated quietly.

Martha's jaw worked. "They weren't all dead when the fire took them, you understand. Some were alive. At the windows. Second floor, the weaving shed. I was across the yard — I'd slipped out the side gate when the lock-in started, God forgive me, I'd got little Nell with me that day, she'd come to bring my dinner. And I saw them at the glass. I saw hands." She looked down at her own hands, the sulphur-yellowed fingers curled in her lap. "Twenty years I've seen those hands every time I shut my eyes. So yes, printer. I'll pay you in the worst coin I have. But my children go first. Before I sign anything. Before I say one word into your machine. They go, and they go safe, or the dead can keep their own counsel another twenty years."

* * *

Bram walked home through the gathering evening with the bargain sitting in her chest like a stone set in wet mortar — heavy, and beginning to set.

Papers. Martha wanted papers, and not the kind bought from some ship's runner at the docks, which any constable with a grudge could unpick in an afternoon. Real papers. A pass, a recommendation, the signature of a respectable person vouching for a widow and her three children travelling to Leeds for service and health. In ordinary times such a thing cost money and patience. These were not ordinary times. The general strike was coming — everyone could smell it, the way animals smell weather — and the magistrates had begun demanding documentation for anyone leaving the district, hunting the Chartist delegates who moved between cities like sparks along a fuse. Papers forged now would be examined by men looking for exactly the flaws a frightened amateur would leave.

But Bram Ishikawa — Bram who had been Ben Ishikawa these twenty years, Ben who cut letters finer than any man in Manchester — was not a frightened amateur.

Her father had taught her letterforms before he taught her letters. She had been cutting steel punches at eleven, grinding her own gravers, learning how a curve behaved differently in metal than it did on paper. And a signature, she had understood long ago, was nothing but a letterform written fast: a machine made of habit. Every hand had its habits — the pressure, the slant, the places where the pen hesitated and the places where it flew. Copy a man's habits and you had his soul on paper.

She had never done it. Twenty years of passing, twenty years of small deceptions worn smooth as river stones, and she had never once put a false name to paper. Passing was silence. Forgery was speech. It was the difference, she thought, standing in the dark doorway of the shop on Turner Street, between hiding and lying — and she had built her whole life on the conviction that the two were different, and that she had only ever done the first.

She lit the lamp in the back room and sat down at the sloped desk where her father had once drafted his specimen sheets, and she made herself say it aloud, to the empty room, in her father's borrowed English cadence: *"Once you cut this letter, you cannot uncut it."*

Then she began to plan.

* * *

The signature she needed belonged to a Mr. Edmund Prowse, attorney, of King Street — a man whose office had ordered printing from the Ishikawa shop for fifteen years, whose hand Bram knew intimately from hundreds of job tickets and instructions, and who was, as of Tuesday last, laid up with gout at his house in Buxton, a fact his clerk had lamented loudly enough for all Turner Street to hear. Prowse's hand was a lawyer's hand: upright, compressed, with a peculiar hooked terminal on the final letter of his surname that looked, Bram had always thought, like a man pulling a door shut behind him.

She practised it forty times on scrap before she touched good paper. She wrote it large, then small, then at speed, then slowly, learning where Prowse pressed and where he skimmed. She learned that his *d* leaned further than his other letters, that his ink thickened at the ends of strokes because he was a man who paused at the finish of things. Around midnight she took out a sheet of the shop's best wove stock — the same stock they used for Renwick's own broadsheets, which was its own bitter joke — and ruled it lightly in pencil lines that she later erased with bread crumbs, and she wrote.

*Alicia Quill, widow, of this parish, with three children, is known to me and travels to Leeds upon family business, being recommended to all whom it may concern.*

And beneath it, the hooked door pulled shut: *Edmund Prowse.*

She sat looking at it for a long time. The lamp burned steady. Somewhere outside, a patrol went by, boots ringing on the cobbles, and Bram did not move until the sound faded. Her hand, when she finally looked at it, was perfectly steady. That frightened her more than trembling would have.

This was the line. She had crossed smaller lines for years — wearing Ben's clothes, signing Ben's name, letting the world believe its comfortable lie about her — but those had been silences, omissions, the passive architecture of survival. This was active. This was a crime with a name, and if they caught her, it would not be *passing* they tried her for. It would be forgery, and forgery meant transportation, and transportation meant her father's shop sold from under her and every careful year of her life burned like waste paper.

And Martha's children would be on a coach to Leeds by Saturday.

She thought of the hands at the glass. She had not seen them; she never would see them; she carried instead a different image, one Elias Vane had given her without meaning to — the census ledger, the neat columns of names, thirty-one entries that simply stopped, as though the men and women of Harrow Mill had walked off the page of England itself. Her father had spent his life making letters so that names could be recorded. Someone had spent gold making sure these thirty-one were not.

Bram blotted the signature, folded the paper, and sealed it with plain wax. Then she did something she had not planned, something that rose out of her hands rather than her head: she set Martha Quill's name in type. Just her name, alone, in 12-point Caslon, and pulled a single proof on scrap paper, and looked at it.

*MARTHA QUILL.*

It looked like the beginning of a list.

* * *

Friday morning, fog thick as wool, Bram met Martha at the coach office in Oldham Road with the papers sewn into the lining of Alice's coat, because no searcher in England would strip-search a sickly child of seven while her mother wept at her. Martha had wept on cue beautifully; Bram suspected the tears were half rehearsal, half real, and respected her enormously for both.

"The 8:40 to Leeds, change at Huddersfield," Bram murmured, walking beside her through the crowd of shawls and crates and shouting porters. "Your sister's direction is on the reverse of the pass. Go today, don't wait for the Saturday coach — Saturday there'll be soldiers checking every soul that leaves."

"And you?" Martha's eyes flicked over Bram's face, reading her the way Bram read type. "When I swear this statement, when it prints, they'll come for whoever printed it. You know that."

"I know it."

"Then why—" Martha stopped, seized Bram's sleeve, and for a moment the match-woman's mask dropped entirely and underneath was something fierce and grieving and old. "Why are you doing this? You've a shop. A life. Whatever it is you're hiding — and I've sold matches long enough to know a hidden face when I serve one — you've kept it hid twenty years, by your own telling. One paper, one statement, and you burn it all. Why?"

Bram looked out past her, at the fog rolling down Oldham Road, at the mill chimneys standing in it like the masts of drowned ships. Thirty-one names. Her father's handwriting stopping mid-entry. A memorial broadsheet paid for and never printed, its absence a hole shaped exactly like a massacre.

"Because somebody paid to make sure they were never counted," she said. "And I make my living counting. Somebody has to set the names, Mrs. Quill. That's the whole of the trade. Somebody sets the names, or the names never existed."

Martha held her gaze a moment longer. Then she nodded once, sharply, and hoisted Alice onto her hip, and gathered Nell and Sam with a look apiece, and turned toward the coach.

At the steps she paused. "Monday," she said over her shoulder. "St. Thomas's, Stockport — there's a curate there owes me, and he's not Renwick's. Monday, ten o'clock. Bring your census man and your oath-book." A grim ghost of a smile. "I'll give them the hands at the windows, printer. God help us both."

Bram watched the coach pull away into the fog until the horses' hooves were only a sound, and then a suggestion, and then nothing. She stood in the emptying yard with the cold working through her coat, aware of what she had done — her first crime, committed cleanly, irreversibly, and without a single tremor in her writing hand — and aware also that it had felt less like falling than like setting a difficult line: terrifying while the steel was in the wood, and then, once pulled, simply true.

She was halfway back down Oldham Road when she saw the boy.

He was loitering by the chandler's shop opposite the coach office — the apprentice, Jem Cotter, thirteen years old and usually at his bench by seven — and he was not loitering. He was watching the coach yard with the studied idleness of someone paid to be idle, and as Bram drew level with him he turned, and for half a second their eyes met, and he smiled at her.

It was a smile she had seen before, on the faces of men leaving her father's shop with work they had no right to. It was the smile of a boy holding something worth more than his wages.

"Morning, Mr. Ishikawa," Jem said, touching his cap. "Fine fog for travelling."

Bram walked on, unhurried, her pulse a slow iron hammer. When she reached the corner she glanced back once. He was gone — down the alley toward King Street, she would have bet the shop on it. Toward Renwick.

Monday was three days away. Three days to move the statement, move the witnesses, move everything — before whatever Jem Cotter was carrying reached the hands that paid him.
