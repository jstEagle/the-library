## 10. The Second Witness

Elias Vane had learned, in twenty years of counting the living, that the dying were easier to find than anyone supposed. They did not run. They did not hide. They sat where they could be found — in the back rooms of charity wards, in the single rooms above the dye-works where the rent was cheap because the stairs were steep and the air was poison — and they waited with a patience that was not patience at all but surrender, dressed up as calm.

He had been looking for Joseph Tredgett for six days.

The census rolls of 1831 listed him as watchman, Harrow Mill, night shift, age fifty-one. Elias had copied that line out himself once, years ago, in another life when his work was only work. He remembered it now with the peculiar clarity of things seen before they are understood: *Tredgett, J., watchman.* A name like any other name in a ledger of ten thousand names. It had meant nothing to him then. It meant everything now, because thirty-one other names were missing from that same book, and a watchman holds keys.

Martha Quill would not say more than she had said, and Bram had not pressed her — Bram, who understood better than any man he had ever met the arithmetic of silence, what it cost and what it bought. But Martha had let one thing slip before she turned her face to the wall of her cellar room and ended the conversation: *"Ask old Tredgett, if you've the stomach for it. He's at his sister's on Ancoats Lane, dying slow and sorry."*

Dying slow and sorry. In Elias's experience those two conditions rarely arrived together. Men died slow and unrepentant, or fast and sorry, and almost always they died clutching their version of events the way a drowning man clutches driftwood. He did not expect confession. He expected a locked door, or worse, an open one and a man paid well enough by Renwick's people to lie convincingly even to God.

What he found instead was a room barely wider than a coffin, smelling of camphor and wet plaster, and a man propped against two chairs pushed together because there was no bed that would hold him, coughing in a rhythm so established it had become almost musical — three short, one long, a rest, and again.

"You're the census man."

Elias stopped in the doorway. The single window gave onto the lane and a slice of grey sky, and in that grey light the old watchman looked less like a man than like something the mill had finished with and set out for collection — a great frame of a body gone hollow, hands enormous and still, resting on the blanket as if they had forgotten their trade.

"I'm Vane," Elias said. "I called yesterday. Your sister told me—"

"She told me." Tredgett's voice was gravel poured slow. "Said there'd been a government man asking after Harrow Mill. I told her to send you up." He turned his head, and his eyes in the lamplight were pale and steady and terribly tired. "I've been waiting twenty years for somebody official to come ask me what happened that night. Twenty years, Mr. Vane. You're late, but I'll allow it. Come in before the damp does. Sit."

There was only one stool. Elias took it. Between them stood a small table bearing a candle, a jug of water, and a Bible so swollen with damp it had begun to eat itself.

"You knew who I was before your sister said my name," Elias said.

"I know every kind of man that comes to this door. Rent men. Priests. Doctors that aren't doctors." A cough folded him double for a moment; when he straightened, there was blood-bright colour high on his cheekbones. "Government men come in ones, quiet, with their questions wrote down neat beforehand. You've got questions wrote down neat. I can see the shape of your pocket where the book sits."

"Then you'll know why I've come."

"I'll know why you've *think* you've come." Tredgett reached for the water jug with both hands, as a child does, and drank. "But you don't know yet. Nobody knows. That's the whole trick of it, see. That's the beauty of what we done. There's nothing to know. Thirty-one men and women went into Harrow Mill on the ninth of August, 1821, to break the new engines' strike, and thirty-one people died in a fire that started nobody-knows-how, and the coroner said accidental, and the newspapers printed four lines between the cattle prices, and everybody went home to their suppers." He set down the jug. His hands were not quite steady. "Except everybody didn't have no home to go to, did they. Because they was dead. And nobody asked how the doors got chained."

Elias felt the words land in his chest like cold coins.

"You saw the chains."

"I *held* the chains." The old man began, very softly, to laugh, and the laugh was the worst sound Elias had heard in all his years of listening — worse than weeping, worse than the screaming he'd heard through asylum walls in his first year of service. It was a laugh with nothing behind it but a hole. "That's the joke, Mr. Vane. That's the beautiful, rotten joke. Everybody thinks a watchman watches. Nobody asks what a watchman locks. I held the keys to Harrow Mill for nineteen years before that night, and on the ninth of August I locked the yard gates at half past nine like it was any other night, and then I went round the side door — the weavers' entrance, the one by the canal — and I put the chain through and I shot the padlock, and I walked home, and I slept."

"Why?" The word came out of Elias harder than he intended. "In God's name, why?"

"Because Mr. Renwick's foreman told me to." Tredgett said it flatly, without drama, the way a man reports the weather. "Ned Carver. Foreman since 'nineteen. Big man, carried a stick with a silver band, wore his hair oiled like a chapel deacon. He come to my lodge at eight o'clock that evening with five pounds wrapped in paper and he says, *Joe, there's trouble coming tonight and the owners want the premises secured against intrusion. Chain the side door. No one in, no one out till morning.* And I says, *there's men sleeping in the weaving shed, Ned, them as wouldn't join the strike and them as couldn't afford to* — thirty-one of them, we counted, we always counted, it was my job, counting is a watchman's trade same as it is yours — and Carver looks at me with his little eyes and says, *Joe, you've got a wife and six children and this here's five pound, and tomorrow it'll be ten if you keep your mouth shut, and if you don't keep it shut it'll be your neck in a rope for interfering with lawful business.*"

The candle guttered. Somewhere below, in the lane, a cart passed, its wheels grinding the cobbles.

"And the fire?"

"The fire started past midnight. We never learned how — lamp knocked over, maybe, or maybe not an accident at all, I've had twenty years to wonder and I still don't know. What I know is what I heard after. The screaming carries over water, Mr. Vane. Did you know that? Sound travels along a canal like it's laid on rails. I was in bed and I heard them screaming from across the water, and I knew — I knew before I was properly awake — that the chain was mine. My chain. My lock. My hand on it." He looked down at his hands, those huge ruined hands, as though they belonged to a stranger he despised. "I ran. God forgive me, I ran, and I stood on the towpath with fifty other fools watching the windows go orange, and there were hands in them, Mr. Vane. Hands in the glass. And I had the key to the yard gate in my pocket the whole time, and I never moved. I never moved. Carver was standing three yards off in the crowd, dry as Sunday, watching me, and when our eyes met he touched his hat. Touched his hat! Like we was gentlemen meeting at church!"

Elias found he could not speak. His father's face rose before him — his father, who had gone into that building because a man with a family takes the night shift when it's offered, strike or no strike, and who had never come out of it except as a name on a compensation list so short it fit on half a page. Twenty-two years he had carried the question *why*, and here was the answer sitting in a chair in Ancoats Lane, wearing a body already halfway to judgment.

"The inquest," Elias managed. "You were called before the coroner."

"I was. I said I found the doors open when I came running." Tredgett's mouth twisted. "Carver sat behind the coroner's clerk with his arms crossed, and every time I opened my mouth he'd look at his watch. Five pound became ten pound became twenty pound became a cottage with a garden in Beswick and my boys apprenticed to good trades. Twenty years, Mr. Vane. Do you know what twenty pound buys a watchman with six children? It buys everything. It buys the silence so deep you forget you're making it. Until your wife dies, and your boys move off, and the cough comes, and you're lying awake nights in a room the size of a grave doing sums. Adding up. Five pound against thirty-one lives. And finding the sum don't come out right, no matter which way you set it down."

* * *

Elias walked to Ishikawa & Son through streets gone strange with the early dark of a fog that tasted of coal. He had left Tredgett alive and talking and terrified of stopping — the old man had talked for two hours, pouring out twenty years in a voice going hoarser by the minute, and had gripped Elias's sleeve at the door with a strength that seemed impossible in that ruined frame and said, *Bring your printer. Bring whoever sets type. I want it wrote down proper, before I go. Wrote down proper, with my name at the bottom, so there's a record somewhere that isn't lies.*

Bram was at the case when he came in, and even before he spoke she read him — he watched it happen, that quick flicker of assessment crossing her face, the small adjustment of her whole body toward the door as though expecting pursuers. She had been doing that for days, ever since the boy Jem Cotter and whatever he carried toward King Street. The shop felt besieged. It smelled of ink and fear.

"He talked?" she said.

"He confessed. All of it. The chain, the payment, Carver by name." Elias set his notebook on the composing stone. "And he wants a sworn statement, written and signed, while he still has breath. He asked for you specifically. He said—" Elias hesitated, because the phrasing had struck him strangely even at the time. "He said, *send the printer's hand, the one that passes for a son. I want a steady hand that knows what letters cost.*"

Bram went very still.

"He knows," she said at last. Not a question.

"He knows *something*. Dying men see sideways, Ishikawa. Whatever he saw, he kept it to himself. But he wants his truth in type, and he wants it in a hand that understands the weight of it." Elias met her eyes. "So do I. Will you come?"

She looked at the forme of type waiting on the stone — the statement pages, the witness testimony, everything they had built and everything Jem Cotter might already be selling — and Elias saw her weigh it, that swift silent arithmetic she performed better than any magistrate. Then she reached for her coat and her father's heavy stick, and said only:

"Fetch the paper and a spare pen. If we're writing a man's soul into the record, we'll do it properly."

* * *

Ancoats Lane after dark was a corridor of lit windows and unseen lives, gaslight failing against the fog so that each house floated alone like a ship. The sister let them up without a word; she had been told, evidently, and had made her peace with it, or perhaps simply had no fight left for anything beyond keeping her brother's last days clean.

Joseph Tredgett looked smaller than he had that morning, as though the day's talking had spent something vital. But his eyes were clear, and when Bram entered and set the writing board across his knees, he studied her face for a long moment with an expression Elias could not read.

"So you're the printer's hand," he said.

"I'm the printer's hand," Bram agreed.

"They tell me you cut letters yourself. Cut the actual steel." He shook his head slowly. "My father was a die-sinker in Sheffield. I know what it is to make a letter that lasts." He closed his eyes, opened them. "Right. Write what I say, and stop me if I wander, because I will wander, and I won't have lies getting in by accident any more than I'd suffer them on purpose."

Bram dipped the pen. "State your name and condition for the record."

"Joseph Tredgett, sometime night watchman of Harrow Mill, Ancoats, Manchester. Of sound mind, though poor body, being in the last stages of the white plague, and wishing to set down the truth concerning the fire of the ninth of August, eighteen hundred and twenty-one, before I answer for it to a higher authority than Sir Aldous Renwick."

And then he dictated. For nearly two hours, pausing only when the coughing took him, he laid it all down in the plain unlovely speech of a working man: the five pounds in the paper; Carver's exact words, repeated twice over until Bram's transcription matched his memory to the syllable; the names of the thirty-one, recited from the watchman's count-book he had kept in his head for twenty years because the physical book had been taken from him the morning after the fire and never returned — *"they said it was evidence, and I suppose it was, and I suppose it burned"*; the touch of the hat on the towpath; the inquest and the watch and the rising sum of hush money; and finally, his voice dropping to almost nothing:

"And I swear before God Almighty, who I shall stand before very shortly, that Edward Carver, foreman, did order the chaining of the weavers' door with full knowledge that workers remained within the premises, and that Sir Aldous Renwick, owner, either ordered the same or knowingly concealed it, and that the verdict of accidental death recorded at the inquest was purchased, and I, Joseph Tredgett, sold it to them, and I repent it, and may this paper weigh against my sins, for nothing else of mine is worth the weighing."

Bram read it back to him, line by line, and he corrected two words — changed *ordered* to *ordered or permitted*, and struck out a phrase where Elias had prompted a detail and Tredgett, honest to the marrow, refused it: *"No. I don't know that Renwick gave the direct order. I only know what Carver said and whose money it spent like. Put down what I know and no more. A dying man's oath should be clean."*

Then Bram guided his ruined hand to the signature — his fingers could not grip the pen alone — and pressed the nib into his grasp, and Joseph Tredgett signed his name beneath his confession in a straggling, iron-fortified scrawl, and Elias signed as witness, and Bram signed as scrivener, using the name she used in the shop, and Tredgett watched her write it and said quietly, not to either of them exactly:

"There. Now there's two records. Theirn and mine. Let the future choose."

It was past eleven when they came down into the lane. The fog had thickened; the gas lamps were mere haloes, drowned moons. Elias offered to walk Bram home but she shook her head, holding the folded papers inside her coat against her chest like something warm-blooded and fragile.

"Go straight to Ada Fenn's," she said. "Tell her to expect us tomorrow with a new page to set. The watchman's statement goes in the pamphlet — it changes everything, it turns rumour into sworn testimony. And Elias—" She hesitated, and in the yellow smudge of the nearest lamp her face looked suddenly young and exhausted and resolute all at once. "Thank you. For finding him. For sitting there two hours and letting an old man empty himself. Most men would have wanted only the facts. You let him have the whole of it."

"He gave me something too," Elias said. "A name for my grief. I've carried a question for twenty-two years. Tonight it got an answer, and the answer has a name and a silver-banded stick, and I find I want very much to see justice done to it."

They parted at the corner of the lane. Elias watched her go, a slight figure swallowed step by step by the fog, the most careful person he had ever known carrying the least safe document in Manchester against her heart.

He was three streets from his own lodging when he heard the running.

Footsteps behind him — more than one set, hard and urgent, the particular slap of nailed boots on wet stone that soldiers made and no one else. He turned into the mouth of an alley on instinct, pressing himself against the bricks, and saw them pass under the lamp: four men in civilian coats moving like military men, and between them, supported under the arms, dragged more than walking —

His stomach dropped through the cobbles.

The figure was tall and hollow and unmistakable, its head lolling, its great hands hanging loose. Even at that distance, even in that light, Elias knew the frame. Knew the cough that bent it.

They were carrying Joseph Tredgett somewhere, and Joseph Tredgett was not walking under his own power, and the sister's window above the lane had been dark when they left it, and Elias understood with terrible clarity that the confession they had just sealed in ink had cost the old man more than his breath.

It had cost him whatever hours remained — unless Elias and Bram could reach the sister's room before the men in civilian coats reached wherever they were going, and learn whether Tredgett was being taken to a doctor, a magistrate, or a canal.

Elias pulled his collar up and followed, low and quick, into the fog.
