## 11. Betrayal in the Composing Room

The trouble began with a cup of tea.

I should have known it then — should have read the omen in it the way my mother once read weather in the smell of a river. Josiah Crane had never once brought me tea in six years of apprenticeship. He was a sulking boy, seventeen and all elbows, who resented every hour of his indenture as though the shop had kidnapped him rather than fed him. He brought me tea on the morning of the ninth of August without being asked, set it down at my elbow among the quads and leads, and said, "You've not eaten since yesterday, Mr. Ishikawa."

"Ishikawa" — always the name, always said too carefully, the way a man repeats a password he has overheard.

"Thank you," I said, and did not drink it. That was no insult to him; I drank nothing in the composing room that had not come from my own kettle. But I watched the steam rise off it while he shuffled back to his stone, and something in the set of his shoulders — the particular hunch of a boy carrying a coin in his pocket he cannot stop touching — stayed with me like a splinter.

The statements lay locked in the iron cabinet under the stairs. Three documents now: Martha Quill's sworn testimony, dictated to Elias Vane and signed with her mark because she could not write; the watchman's confession, taken down in my own hand over two nights of coughing and weeping; and Elias's copy of the coroner's file, fair-copied so that the original might stay hidden where it lived. Thirty-one names between them. The watchman had given me twenty-nine from memory, weeping through most of them, and Elias had supplied the last two from parish registers of baptisms — children christened whose fathers never came home to see them walk.

We meant to print them. That was the whole of it, the terrible simplicity of it. Ada Fenn's press waited in Ancoats, and Renwick's commemorative history sat half-composed in my own shop, its gilded lies drying slowly in the galley trays like meat going off. We had perhaps ten days before Sir Aldous's book went out into the world and made its verdict permanent — *accidental*, *unavoidable*, *an act of God* — and after that, thirty-one dead mill hands would be nothing but an embarrassment someone had already apologised for.

So I was careful. God knows I was careful. The cabinet key hung against my skin on a cord beneath my shirt. The statements were copied in ciphered shorthand only Elias and I could read. The window of the composing room was shuttered even in daylight because the type itself would tell a story to anyone who knew how to read it — and I told myself, as I locked up each night, that care was a wall no bribe could climb.

But walls have doors, and doors are opened from inside.

* * *

It was the smell of tobacco that undid him, in the end. Not mine — I don't smoke — and not my father's, whose pipe had been cold these twenty years. On Thursday evening, closing the shop, I stood in the composing room doorway and caught it: a thread of cheap shag, the kind sold at the corner stall by the Bridgewater Arms, sweetened with liquorice. It clung to the air near Josiah's stone the way guilt clings to a man, faintly and everywhere.

And there were other small things, once I let myself look. A smear of graphite on the frame of the case where the memorial job had been set — the Harrow history, the one I'd been forced to accept. Graphite, when the boy worked in ink. A candle stub burnt to nothing on a night I'd been certain the shop had closed dark. And the galley holding the first sheet of the history — I checked it three times — was a quarter-inch out of register, as though someone had lifted it, set it down again in the dark, in a hurry.

I did not sleep. I sat in the back parlour with the lamp turned low and made myself consider it plainly, the way one considers a diagnosis. Someone had paid my apprentice. Renwick's agent — the smooth grey man named Pell who had delivered the contract with the smell of church incense about him — had found the softest board in my house and pried it loose. What had he asked for? Not the statements themselves; Josiah couldn't reach those. He'd asked what Josiah could give: what was set, what was planned, what the printer intended. Whether the shop was preparing anything besides Sir Aldous's book. Whether the printer kept company with a census taker.

Whether the printer was what he pretended to be.

That thought arrived with its usual cold hand around my throat, and I mastered it as I had mastered it for twenty years, and then I got up and went down to the composing room to move the statements out of the cabinet entirely — into the flour barrel in the cellar, wrapped in oilcloth, where no boy bribed with sovereigns would think to dig.

The cabinet was empty.

Not unlocked. Not forced. *Empty.* The lock sat shut and smug in its plate, and inside, where three documents should have lain, there was only the false bottom I had installed myself, and dust, and a single dried apple core that Josiah must have left months ago.

I stood there in the dark with the lamp shaking in my hand and understood, with a slowness that was its own kind of horror, that the theft had happened days ago. Perhaps the night I'd heard the floorboard creak and blamed the settling of the old building. Perhaps the night of the tea. Every careful thing I had done since — every lock, every check — had been a woman guarding an empty box.

They had everything. Names. Confessions. The sworn mark of Martha Quill. The dying watchman's account of chained doors and bought silence. Everything Elias and I had bled for, carried out of my shop folded inside a boy's waistcoat, priced at whatever Pell had judged a conscience cost.

I blew out the lamp and stood in the black, listening to my own heart, and made the coldest calculation of my life: Josiah must not know yet that I know. If the boy still believed himself unseen, he might be watched instead of warned — followed to whoever held his string. I would lose the papers either way; that was done. But I might still learn where they'd gone, and who had counted them, and whether they meant to print them or simply burn them.

Burn them, I thought. They'll burn them. Why would Renwick print the truth when burning is cheaper?

Because burning proves nothing, answered another voice, Elias's voice, in my head. Because a record destroyed leaves a hole shaped exactly like doubt. Because if they can produce the witnesses' words themselves, at the right moment, before the right magistrate, they can rewrite them.

I didn't yet know which fear was true. I would know by morning, and the knowing would be worse than both.

* * *

I confronted Josiah at dawn, but not as I'd planned to. Plans went out of me somewhere around the third hour of sitting motionless in the parlour, and what remained was simpler and more dangerous.

He came in at seven, yawning, smelling of the liquorice shag, and found me standing at his stone with the stolen evidence laid out between us: the graphite smear, the candle stub, the displaced galley. I said nothing. I only looked at him, the way my father used to look at misprinted sheets — not with anger, but with the arithmetic of loss.

The boy lasted four seconds.

"They said nobody'd get hurt." His voice cracked in the middle like green wood. "Mr. Pell — he said it was just paper, just copying, that a man's entitled to know what's wrote in his own — I never read it all, Mr. Ishikawa, I can't hardly read the shorthand, I just copied what was plain and took the rest whole—"

"Where is it now?"

"I don't know. I swear on my mother. I gave it to him Monday night, behind the corn factor's on Store Street, and he counted out five pounds and said there'd be five more when—" He stopped. The colour drained out of his face in a way that told me he had just remembered the rest of the sentence.

"When what, Josiah."

"When the old man's seen to." He was whispering now. "The watchman. He said the old man had been talking, and talking gets people talked about, and there was ways — Mr. Pell laughed when he said it. I thought he meant scare him. I thought—" He put his hand flat on the stone as though the floor were tilting. "Mr. Ishikawa, what have I done?"

What have you done. As though the question were his alone. I wanted to shake him; I wanted, worse, to comfort him, because he was a child who had sold strangers the lives of everyone who trusted him and did not yet understand the exchange rate. Five pounds. Ten more promised. Thirty-one souls and two living witnesses, weighed against a boy's back rent and a new coat.

"Go home," I heard myself say. "Not to work. Home. If Pell comes near you again, you will tell me within the hour — do you hear me? Within the hour, or so help me, I will make certain the magistrates hear your name before they hear mine."

He fled. I listened to his boots diminish down Chapel Lane, and then I went straight for Elias, walking fast through streets already thickening with strike crowds, past chalked slogans on the walls — *THE CHARTER AND NO SURRENDER* — past dragoons watering their horses with the unhurried menace of men waiting for permission. I did not run. A printer's master does not run in his own district. But inside, some part of me was already running ahead, down Store Street, behind the corn factor's, trying to arrive in time.

* * *

We did not arrive in time.

Elias met me at the corner of Miller Street, and one look at his face told me everything before either of us spoke. He had been to the lodging-house off Great Ancoats where the old watchman — Thomas Rennick, no relation to the baronet except in the way poverty is related to power — had been living out his last winter on a charity bed. Elias had gone at first light to bring the old man word that the printing was nearly ready, that his confession would soon be worth more than the silence he'd been paid for.

"He's dead." Elias said it flatly, the way men say things they refuse to feel yet. "Fell down the stairwell in the night. Broken neck. The landlady swears he'd taken to sleeping badly, wandering — drink, she says. There'll be no questions. Who asks questions about a drunk watchman falling downstairs?"

"The coroner Renwick keeps on retainer," I said, "will ask exactly none."

We stood in the crowd's edge, two figures among hundreds, and around us Manchester roared toward its reckoning — banners going up, fists going up, the whole district a kettle beginning to sing. And I felt the full shape of it close around me at last: the theft, the death, the timing. Not chaos. *Method.* They had taken our record and killed its living voice in the same breath, so that when the statements surfaced — and they would surface, at the moment of Renwick's choosing — there would be no one alive to confirm them, no hand to swear to them, no witness to point to and say *yes, that is my voice, that is my memory*. Dead men make convenient authors.

Unless the words were already beyond recall.

"Elias," I said. "Ada's press. Did you send her a copy?"

Something moved behind his eyes — hope and horror wrestling for the same chair. "No. You said — we agreed, no copies until the type was set. One document, one custody. Yours." He stopped. "Bram. Tell me you sent her something."

I had not. I had been careful. Careful as a vault, and the vault had been emptied by a boy with liquorice on his breath, and now the only existing testimony of thirty-one murdered human beings lay wherever Pell had chosen to keep it — in a strongbox, perhaps, or a lawyer's drawer, or already feeding a fire in a gentleman's grate while its author lay cooling on the boards of a lodging-house stairwell.

"No," I said. "There's nothing. It's gone."

Elias turned away from me. For a long moment he looked at the marching crowd as though it were water and he were deciding whether to drown. When he spoke again his voice had changed — flattened, stripped, the voice of a man reading his own verdict.

"Then we do it again," he said. "Martha still lives. Her statement exists in her memory, and memories can't be picked out of cabinets. The parish registers still hold the names of the fatherless. And I—" His jaw worked. "My father is in neither ledger nor grave-marker. Nobody can steal him twice. We start again, Bram. Tonight. Tomorrow. However long it takes, however many times they burn it. That is the whole difference between us and them: they can only destroy what exists, and we can always make more."

It was a fine speech, and I wanted with all my heart to believe it. But even as he spoke it, my eye was caught by movement across the street — a familiar figure, smooth and grey as wet slate, standing beneath the awning of the corn factor's office with his hands clasped behind him, watching us with the mild professional interest of a man confirming a delivery.

Pell saw that I saw him. He inclined his head — a small, courteous bow, almost respectful — touched the brim of his hat, and turned away into the crowd, unhurried, a man with nowhere in particular to be and all the time in the world to be there.

And I understood, finally and completely, that the theft had never been the end of his errand. It was the middle. He had the statements. He had a dead witness. He had a bribed boy who would confess anything under pressure, and a printer whose secret — *my* secret, the one that could hang me twice over, for forgery and for the life I had stolen to live — sat in his employer's pocket beside the five-pound note that had bought it.

He hadn't taken the record to destroy it.

He had taken it to trade with.

Elias was still talking — names, registers, tonight, tomorrow — and I gripped his arm to stop him, because across the street, nailed to the post outside the corn factor's where any passing hand could see it, a fresh bill had been pasted up not an hour ago. I could read it from where we stood, because I had taught myself long ago to read print at distances other men needed steps for, and because I knew the typeface the way you know your own handwriting.

It was a notice of reward. *Information respecting seditious printing within the township of Manchester.* Fifty pounds. And beneath it, smaller, almost an afterthought, the line that stopped my blood:

*Particular attention to be paid to persons of foreign descent residing under assumed names.*

Elias followed my gaze. I felt him go still beside me, felt the exact moment he understood what I had understood — that Renwick no longer needed to guess what I was, because my own apprentice had sold him that too, and the machinery of the law now had a shape drawn around me, waiting to be filled.

"Walk," I said quietly. "Don't look at it again. Walk toward the shop like a man with nothing on his mind."

We walked. Behind us the crowd surged, the drums began, the day of the great rally gathered itself like a wave — and somewhere in that roaring city, in a strongbox or a drawer or a lawyer's keeping, the voices of thirty-one dead waited to be spent, and I did not yet know for what price.
