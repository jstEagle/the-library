## 12. Fugitive Typesetters

The knock came at four in the morning, three blows soft as a knuckle on a coffin lid, and Bram knew before she opened the door that the world had turned against them in the night.

She had slept at the shop — of course she had slept at the shop; where else did a fugitive sleep but beside the thing that would betray her? The forme of type sat wrapped in oilcloth beneath the floorboards by the press, thirty-one names and two sworn statements locked fast in iron and lead, heavier than any coffin plate and harder to bury. She had checked it twice before lying down. She dreamed of it. In the dream her father stood over the chase with a mallet and asked her, gently, *whose name goes first, little one?* and she could not answer because all thirty-one spoke at once.

Now the knocking again, and through the shutters the street glowed wrong — lanterns moving in a pattern, not drifting. Soldiers moved in patterns. Neighbors moved like weather.

Bram dressed without light. Breeches, shirt, the waistcoat with the mended left pocket. Her brother's boots, still too big after eleven years, though her feet had grown into them at last as if the dead boy were slowly lending her his shape. She lifted the floorboard, took out the forme, and felt its weight settle against her chest when she strapped it under her coat — a slab of metal the size of a hymnal pressing against her heart, cold through the linen.

*They will search the presses first,* she thought. *Then the type cabinets. Then they will find nothing, because I am carrying it.*

The third round of knocking was harder. A voice: "Open in the name of the peace. Printers' premises are to be inspected."

She went out through the yard.

The alley behind Ishikawa's was black and smelled of coal smoke and rain that hadn't fallen yet. She moved along it the way her father had taught her to move through a crowd at a execution — not hurrying, never hurrying, because hurry was a signature. At the mouth of the alley a dragoon sat his horse like a statue of himself, lance upright, breath steaming. Bram turned the other way, toward Ancoats, and let the district swallow her.

Behind her, faint through walls, she heard the shop door give way. Then the sound she had imagined for two days and never once prepared herself for: men in her composing room, handling her letters. Her father's letters. She kept walking.

* * *

Elias Vane counted soldiers the way other men counted sheep, and he was not sleeping either.

He had taken a room above a chandler's off Great Ancoats Street under the name Mr. E. Vane, census officer, which was true enough to pass and false enough to hang him if anyone looked past it. On the table before him lay the coroner's file in its oilskin wrapper, and beside it the copy of the statements — no, not the copy. There was no copy now. The apprentice Jem had copied them for Renwick's man, and the copy had gone wherever bribed paper goes, and last night old Hobbs the watchman had gone out of a third-floor window of his own lodging house with his neck broken on the cobbles, and this morning the whole district knew what that meant even if the newspapers would print *misadventure.*

Which meant the original — the forme, locked and wrapped — was the only witness left alive in Manchester.

He heard the boots on the stair at half past four and knew they were not coming for him. Too many, too loud, too careless. They wanted him to hear them coming. He put the coroner's file into the stove, watched the corners catch and curl, and climbed out the back window onto the chandler's lean-to while the door came down below him. His hat blew off into the yard. He left it. A census taker without a hat was half invisible anyway; a census taker running was a target.

The streets were already filling. That was the thing no one outside the district understood — that the strike did not begin at an appointed hour but rose like water finding its level, weavers and spinners and piecers flowing out of every court and cellar, moving not with fury but with a terrible patience, ten thousand people walking in the same direction because there was nowhere else. Elias folded into the current and let it carry him toward the one place he could not go and could not stay away from: Ishikawa's.

He found the door standing open. Inside, four redcoats were turning the place inside out with the cheerful thoroughness of vandals who believed themselves policemen. Type drawers pulled and dumped, quoins scattered, the press itself — old Ashgrove iron, sixty years of Manchester in its frame — being pried at by a corporal with a crowbar.

"Where's the printer?" one of them asked, seeing Elias in the doorway.

"Dead," Elias said. "I've come for the estate papers."

It was almost true. It had the ring of paperwork about it, and paperwork was a password. They let him stand in the wreckage while he looked at what they could not read: the empty space beneath the floorboard by the press, the scrape marks fresh, the board replaced a half-inch proud of its neighbors. She had gotten out. She had taken it with her, which was brave, and foolish, and exactly like her.

A sergeant held up a galley of type, squinting at it upside down. "What's this say, then?"

"Nothing," Elias said. "Printers leave it reversed. You'd need a mirror."

The sergeant dropped it back in the drawer as though it might bite. Elias understood then something that steadied him all day: they were looking for a pamphlet, for paper, for a thing they could burn. Not one of them had thought to look for a woman carrying forty pounds of lead against her ribs.

He went out into the rising crowd to find her before someone cleverer did.

* * *

By seven the district had become a country of its own.

Bram moved through it with the forme under her coat and every step a negotiation with pain — the metal edge found her collarbone, her sternum, the soft place below her ribs, marking her like a brand with each stride. Around her the strike poured toward the squares: men in their Sunday coats because a march was church, women with shawls over their hair, boys beating tin pans, and over it all the Chartist cry rolling from street to street like a tide finding its channel — *the Charter, the Charter, vote yourself a farm* — and underneath, quieter, older, in the mouths of the mill hands who had been at Harrow twenty years ago or had mothers who were: nothing. Just looks. The look of people who remembered doors.

At the corner of Miller Street a hand closed on her sleeve and her heart stopped until she saw it was only a beggar woman with matches — no. Not a beggar woman.

"Martha." Bram drew her into the shadow of a shuttered warehouse. "You shouldn't be out."

"Nor should you." Martha Quill's face was thinner than it had been in the alley, her eyes red-rimmed. "They came to my room last night. Two gentlemen. Asked did I know anything about fires at mills. Offered me money to remember nothing and more money to tell them who else remembers." Her grip tightened. "I said nothing. But Bram — they knew my name. They knew my children's school. They're not asking anymore. They're counting."

"I know. The watchman—"

"I heard. Everyone's heard." Martha looked up and down the street, and when she spoke again her voice had changed, dropped its fear and picked up something flinty. "My Danny's twelve. He can carry things. He knows the courts between here and Angel Meadow better than any soldier born in Yorkshire. If you need to move that—" her eyes flicked to the bulge under Bram's coat, "—he's your boy today."

"Why?" Bram asked. "You told me you wouldn't speak. You have children. I understood that."

Martha's chin came up. "I have children," she said. "That's why. I won't testify — I can't, I'd be dead inside the month and them in a workhouse. But I'll spend what I can afford. A son running errands costs me nothing they can prove." She pressed a matchbox into Bram's hand, absurdly domestic. "For the light. Go to the Old Boar Yard, off Threadneedle Court, and ask for Danny Quill. And Bram—" for one moment her face was the face from the alley, the witness who had refused, "—don't let them win quietly. My mother died in that fire. I was six. Nobody ever told me her name was worth saying."

Bram stood a moment with the matchbox in her palm. Then she said, "Her name is in the forme. I set it myself. Sarah Quill, hand 214."

Martha turned away fast, wiping her face with the heel of her hand, and pointed Bram toward Threadneedle Court without looking back.

* * *

Danny Quill was a narrow, quick-eyed boy with soot on his knuckles and the unimpressed manner of a man of fifty compressed into four feet of height. He looked Bram up and down — breeches, coat, the unmistakable rectangular bulge — and said, "Mam said you'd be heavy. This way, and don't walk like you're carrying summat."

"What's it like, carrying summat?"

"Walk like you're late for dinner," Danny said. "Nobody stops a lad late for dinner."

He took her off the main roads into the district's secret anatomy: passage-ways between backs of houses, steps down into cellar yards, a tunnel under the Rochdale road where the mill lads had carried coal for a hundred years and smuggled everything else for fifty. Twice they heard boots ahead and Danny simply turned down another throat of brick without breaking stride, and Bram understood that the boy's map was drawn in generations of flight. Once a patrol passed close enough that she could hear a lieutenant complaining about the crowds, and Danny stood her in a doorway, tipped a stolen apple into his mouth, and chewed with his eyes half-closed until the danger passed like weather.

"You're the printer," he said around the apple core. It wasn't a question.

"I'm a typesetter."

"Mam says you're going to print the names." He kicked a stone ahead of them down the passage. "Of the fire people."

"Yes."

"My gran's name's in there, then." He walked a few steps. "Sarah Quill." He pronounced it carefully, like a word in a foreign language he intended to learn. "Mam never says it. I didn't know it till last week when she got strange." Another few steps. "Is it true the doors was chained?"

"It's true. A man confessed it before he died."

Danny nodded, absorbing this the way boys absorb terrible things, filing it somewhere it would calcify into either rage or purpose. "Good," he said finally. "Then it ought to be wrote down proper. Wrote-down things count. Unwrote things just happen and happen again." He glanced up at her with those old eyes. "That's what my mam says, anyways. She says it quiet."

*Old Hobbs*, Bram thought. *You held the keys twenty years and a child of the burned has just handed down your sentence.* "Your mam is wiser than most parliaments," she said aloud.

They came up out of the tunnels near Angel Meadow, that green-brown waste where the district buried what it couldn't afford to mourn, and Danny scanned the field with a fowler's eye. "There's your gentleman," he said. "By the fence. Been there an hour, standing like a post. Mam said look for a tall fellow who stands like he's reading an invisible ledger."

Elias Vane crossed the rough grass at a pace he was clearly restraining, and Bram saw in his face everything he was too disciplined to say — the relief arriving like a blow, the fear banked behind it.

"They emptied the shop," he said. "I watched them do it. Your press—"

"Presses are iron. Iron can be recast." She touched the forme beneath her coat. "This cannot. Where do we take it?"

"Not Ada Fenn's. Not yet." He lowered his voice though the nearest soul was fifty yards off among the gorse. "If Jem gave them the statements, they know who our friends are. Fenn's press will be the second place they look. We need a night and a day somewhere the owners' men can't buy their way into, and then we move the forme to Ada after dark, when the streets belong to the strike and not to the patrols."

"And where does one hide in a city that's burning?"

Elias almost smiled. It was a tired, crooked thing, but it reached his eyes. "In a census taker's lodging, apparently. No one believes a census exists. I've lived off that fact for years." He hesitated, and she watched him decide to say the next part plainly, because they had passed beyond indirection somewhere between the watchman's death and the empty shop. "Bram. Last night I burned the coroner's file rather than let them take it, and I stood in your shop while strangers broke your father's type, and I realized I have spent my life counting other people and keeping myself out of the count. Whatever happens when this is printed — I'm done being a man who verifies records. I mean to be in this one."

She looked at him for a long moment. The Meadow stretched gray-green around them; somewhere beyond it the drums of the great rally were already tuning, a sound like a heartbeat under the earth.

"Then keep close," she said. "Because tonight we carry the truth of thirty-one people across a city full of soldiers, and tomorrow—" she laid her hand flat against the forme, against the names, "—tomorrow we make it permanent."

Danny Quill, who had been ostentatiously studying the horizon, cleared his throat.

"If you two are done," he said, "the patrols change at noon, and there's a cellar off Thompson Street where nobody asks questions and the landlady owes my mam. Walk like you're late for dinner."

* * *

They waited out the afternoon in the landlady's cellar among hams and kegs, and it was there, in the smell of salt pork and lamp oil, with the drums growing louder overhead, that Elias saw her take the forme out from under her coat at last and hold it in both hands like a thing newborn.

"May I?" he asked.

She unwrapped the oilcloth. The type lay locked in its iron chase, mirror-reversed, unreadable to any eye but a printer's — and Bram read it aloud anyway, tracing with one finger, translating backward line by line into the daylight language: *Statement of Thomas Hobbs, watchman... the keys were given to me... the foreman Rakestraw chained the doors at the master's order...* and then the list. Thirty-one names in columns, set in fourteen-point, each letter chosen and placed by her hand over three nights while the city slept.

"Read them," she said suddenly. "Out loud. I've only ever heard them in my head."

So Elias read. *Sarah Quill. Abraham Nadin. Mary-Ann Blakely...* His voice was not made for public speaking — it was a clerk's voice, precise, unhurried — but in that cellar it became something else, a tolling. When he finished, neither of them spoke for a moment, and above them the whole district seemed to be marching in time.

"Twenty years," Bram said at last, rewrapping the forme with hands that were not quite steady. "Twenty years my father kept silent to keep me safe, and I kept silent to keep his shop, and between us we built a fortress out of nothing said." She tied the knot hard. "Renwick thinks he's hunting a pamphlet. Let him hunt. Paper burns. Lead doesn't. These names will outlast his mill, his title, and everyone currently breathing in Manchester — including us, if it comes to that."

"That," Elias said, "is the least comforting thing anyone has said to me all week."

"I'm a typesetter. Comfort isn't in my font."

The laugh escaped him before he could stop it — short, cracked, real — and in the lamplight of the cellar, with the drums rolling overhead and forty pounds of truth sitting between them like a third person, Bram thought: *this is what it is to not be alone in it.* She had forgotten. She had been forgetting since her brother died.

At dusk Danny came down the stairs two at a time, breathless, and the look on his face ended the hour's grace.

"Soldiers at Fenn's old print works," he said. "Lots. And a gentleman in a gray coat directing them — Mam's seen him before, the one what visits the big house. They ain't found her yet." He caught his breath. "But they're pulling in every printer in Ancoats, missus. Every single one. And the landlady says there's dragoons massing by Kettledrum Fields, and the rally's starting early, and—" he swallowed, "—if you're going to cross town tonight, you'd best go now, because by morning there won't be a road in Manchester that isn't watched."

Bram looked at Elias. Elias looked at the forme.

"To Ada's," she said, "the long way. Through the fields."

"Through ten thousand Chartists and a cavalry line," Elias said.

"Yes," Bram said, strapping the names of the dead against her heart and standing up. "Through the middle of everything. Where else would the truth feel at home?"
