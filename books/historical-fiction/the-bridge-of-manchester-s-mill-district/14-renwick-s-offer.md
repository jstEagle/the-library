## 14. Renwick's Offer

The summons came the way a summons from Sir Aldous Renwick came to everything in Ancoats — not as a request but as weather. It arrived on Tuesday morning, borne by a boy in livery too fine for the streets he walked, who handed Bram a card of heavy cream stock and waited, hat in hand, until she read it.

*Mr. Ishikawa is requested to attend upon Sir Aldous Renwick at his residence, Ardwick Green, Thursday next, two o'clock. A matter concerning the Harrow Mill commemorative volume.*

No question mark anywhere in it. No *if convenient*, no *should he be so good*. The card assumed Bram's compliance the way Renwick's mills assumed her labor, and she stood in the doorway holding it while the boy shifted his weight and looked past her shoulder into the shop, cataloguing, she supposed, the presses and the type cases and the narrow stairs that led up to rooms where no one named Ishikawa had legally slept in twenty years.

"Tell Sir Aldous I'll come," she said.

The boy touched his brim and went, and Bram turned the card over twice in her fingers before setting it down on the composing stone, face up, where she could see it while she worked. All that morning her hands knew their trade without her — distributing type, locking a chase, pulling proofs of a draper's handbill — but some cold, patient part of her mind kept returning to the card like a tongue to a broken tooth.

He knew. That was the first thing, and she made herself look at it plainly. He could not have summoned her with such confidence unless he knew about the statements — or enough of them. The apprentice, Jem, had copied pages out of the shop; the watchman had died within a day of his confession; those two facts sat together in Bram's mind like type locked tight in a forme, and between them they spelled betrayal. Renwick had the stolen copies. He had read them. And instead of sending constables or worse, he had sent an invitation.

Which meant he did not yet have what he actually wanted. Which meant there was still something left to lose, or to save.

* * *

She spent Wednesday preparing as though for a battle, because it was one, though the weapons were strange ones. She took Elias's advice and made no copy of the surviving statements to keep in the shop; instead she sewed the original inside the lining of her best coat, flat against her ribs, where it made a small stiff rectangle over her heart that she felt every time she moved. She wrote to Ada Fenn through the coal merchant who carried messages between radical printers, telling her only: *If I do not send word by Friday night, take what we discussed and consider our arrangement ended.* She ate a full meal, though she had no appetite, on the principle that a faint body makes a faint mind. She looked at herself in the cracked glass above the washstand for a long time — the cropped hair, the straight coat, the jaw she'd learned to hold still when men spoke to her — and practiced, absurdly, saying aloud: *I am Bram Ishikawa, and my father was Josiah Ishikawa, and this shop is mine.*

Her voice in the empty room sounded thin. But it held.

Elias wanted to come with her. He stood in the doorway at closing time on Wednesday with his hat turning in his hands, and said, "I could wait outside. On the Green. In case—"

"In case of what?" Bram asked, not unkindly. "In case they set on me in a baronet's own garden with half of Manchester watching? He wants something, Elias. Men who want something don't break their instruments."

"You're not an instrument."

"No," she said. "I'm a printer. And printers get used up all the time." She saw him flinch and softened it. "Wait three days. If you don't hear from me by Friday night, go to Ada. Do what we planned. Don't come looking for me — that's not sentiment, it's tactics. One of us has to stay free, and it can't be me if I walk into that house tomorrow."

He argued, briefly, in his quiet way — the way of a man who had spent years asking questions no one wished answered and had learned to make each one count. Then he took her hand, which he had never done before, held it a moment, and let go. "Friday night," he said. "I'll be counting."

* * *

Ardwick Green was another country. Bram had lived twenty years within earshot of mill engines, and the silence of the Green — its clipped lawns, its gravel drives raked smooth, its trees that were allowed to grow old — struck her almost physically, like stepping from noise into water. Renwick's house was not large by the standards of the new rich, but it was finished: stone dressed clean, windows washed to invisibility, a door knocker of polished brass that gave back her own distorted reflection as she raised her hand.

The servant who admitted her wore gloves. Everything here wore gloves, she thought. Even the air.

She was shown into a library lined with books whose spines had never been cracked — Renwick kept his reading elsewhere, she would learn, in pamphlets and ledgers and other men's letters — and left alone for exactly as long as it takes a man to teach you that your time is worth less than his. She counted four hundred seconds by the mantel clock before the door opened.

Sir Aldous Renwick was smaller than his reputation. That was her first thought, and her second was that this was precisely the effect he cultivated, because the man who came across the carpet toward her with his hand extended was compact, silvering, pleasant-faced, with the mild eyes of a parish treasurer. He might have been a physician, a dissenting minister, anyone's kind uncle.

"Mr. Ishikawa." His grip was dry and brief. "You'll take tea. My housekeeper makes it properly — none of your shop-chicory filth." It was not quite a joke, and it required no answer, which told her he already knew how she took her tea, how she took everything, how much the shop earned, what she owed, and whom she saw. "Sit, sit. You'll forgive an old manufacturer his armchair."

They sat. The tea came, and was good, and she drank it because refusing hospitality was a move in his game, not hers.

"I'll come directly to it," Renwick said, "because I know you tradesmen value directness, and because I've taken up enough of your morning." He smiled at his own condescension as though offering her a gift. "The commemorative volume. *A History of the Harrow Mills, 1790–1842.* Two hundred pages, fine paper, steel engravings, edition of five hundred for distribution to subscribers — the gentry, the clergy, the coroner, the magistrates. Every gentleman in Lancashire will have it on his shelf inside the year. And every clerk, every schoolmaster, every newspaper office will have the cheaper reprint." He let that settle. "It will be, Mr. Ishikawa, the last word on Harrow Mill. Books are very durable things. More durable than mills. Mills burn."

Bram kept her hands still around the cup. "I know something about durable printing," she said. "My father taught me."

"He did. Good man. Careful man." Something flickered behind the mild eyes — the first true thing she had seen in them. "Knew when to print and when not to. There's wisdom in that. Wisdom, Mr. Ishikawa, is mostly knowing what to leave in the case."

"And the commission?"

"The commission is yours. Three hundred pounds — I'll not insult you with less — paid half in advance. Your name on the colophon: *Printed by B. Ishikawa, Manchester.*" He pronounced the name carefully, correctly, and watched her as he did it. "Think what that does for a shop. Every manufacturer in the district will bring you his work after that. You'd never set another handbill in your life."

Three hundred pounds. Bram let herself feel the number honestly, because Renwick would smell a lie. Three hundred pounds was the roof mended, the rent arrears cleared, Jem apprenticed proper, coal through the winter. Three hundred pounds was the shop alive for ten more years, her father's name on a colophon, her own secret name safe behind it forever. She let the wanting sit in her chest where he could see it, because hiding it was impossible and pretending otherwise was weakness.

"It's generous," she said.

"It's fair," Renwick corrected mildly. "Fairness is the whole of my religion. Which brings me to the matter of the text." He rose, went to his desk, and returned with a manuscript bound in gray boards, thick as a Bible. He laid it on the table between them without ceremony, the way a man lays down a winning card. "The historian's draft. You'll find it thorough. Fires happen in mills, Mr. Ishikawa — always have, always will. Grease, lamps, cotton dust, carelessness. This one was careless times five hundred. Tragic. Thirty-one souls — the book names them, every one, in an appendix. Honors them, even. A memorial appendix." He tapped the cover once. "That is what history looks like when it's done properly. Accidents have causes; causes have remedies; remedies have been applied. Nothing here requires a gentleman to hang or a district to starve. It is a good book. It will do good. And you, Mr. Ishikawa, are going to print it."

"Not going," Bram said quietly. "Being offered to."

Renwick smiled as if she'd pleased him. "There's the printer's blood. Very well — being offered to. And here is the offer entire, so there's no confusion between us later." He folded his hands. "You print the book as written. No emendations, no marginalia, no clever errors that survive to the second impression — and I will tell you now, Mr. Ishikawa, I shall compare every sheet against this draft myself, for I am told the Ishikawas have clever hands. In exchange: three hundred pounds, the contract, my patronage, and—" he paused delicately, "—my continued failure to remember certain irregularities."

The clock ticked. Somewhere upstairs a door closed softly.

"Irregularities," Bram said.

"A census anomaly or two. A household entered under a name belonging to a young man who died of fever in 'twenty-one — God rest him — while a young woman of the same years lives on unentered, uncounted, invisible to His Majesty's enumerators. Forging a dead brother's identity for two decades." He said it without heat, almost tenderly, the way one recites a diagnosis. "And then there's the travel papers. Three of them. Forged, and rather well — the Quill woman's children, I understand, are already in Liverpool awaiting a boat." He shook his head with something like admiration. "Criminal offenses both, Mr. Ishikawa. Forgery carries transportation. Passing oneself off — well. That carries whatever the magistrate's imagination supplies. Neither offense requires proof so much as attention, and I am a man who commands a great deal of attention when he chooses to spend it."

The fire was lit against the chill though it was nearly summer, and Bram realized she had stopped feeling its warmth. Her ribs pressed against the hidden papers. *They cannot take what they cannot find,* she reminded herself, and found the thought insufficient, and hated that it was insufficient.

"And the statements?" she said. "The ones your agent stole out of my shop?"

Something crossed Renwick's face then — not guilt, nothing so useful as guilt, but the small annoyance of a man interrupted mid-performance. "Ah. Yes. The old watchman's ravings and the Quill woman's fears. I have copies. Copies are wind, Mr. Ishikawa. Paper is paper, ink is ink — you of all people know that a statement proves nothing. But sworn testimony before a magistrate, depositions, a reopened inquest — those are not wind. Those are storms. And I am given to understand—" he leaned forward slightly, and the mildness dropped away for just a moment, like a shutter slipping, showing her the machinery underneath, "—that somewhere in this city there exists an original. Perhaps several originals. Signed, witnessed, dated. Dangerous documents. Documents that could undo twenty years of a great many reputations, including, I should say, your late father's good name, which would suffer sadly if the world learned he'd trafficked with radicals and harbored sedition under his own roof. You wouldn't want that, Mr. Ishikawa. A son protecting his father's memory — I respect that. I count on it."

*He thinks I'm doing this for Josiah Ishikawa,* Bram thought, with a strange, cold clarity. *He has built his whole trap around a son's loyalty, and he doesn't know there's a daughter in the room.*

"There is one original," she said. "One. Signed by the watchman before witnesses, sworn and attested, lodged where neither fire nor theft nor Sir Aldous Renwick can reach it — lodged, in fact, with instructions that if anything happens to me, it goes to the *Northern Star* within the week, along with an account of this conversation." It was half bluff, half truth, and she delivered it flat and level, watching his eyes. "So you see, sir, killing me or ruining me buys you nothing but a scandal you cannot buy back. The statements protect themselves now. They're printed, in effect — distributed, duplicated, beyond recall. I'm a printer. That's what we do. We make things that outlive us."

For a long moment Renwick said nothing. The mantel clock counted. Then he laughed — softly, genuinely, and it was somehow worse than anger.

"Oh, you're your father's child after all. Very well. Very well." He refilled her cup himself, which was a performance of equality she understood perfectly. "Then hear the whole of it, printer, since you insist on dealing. Print my book, deliver the originals to me within the month, and all else passes unremembered — the census, the forgery, the papers, the lot. Refuse, and I print nothing through you, ruin you by Friday, and take the statements anyway, because I have men whose trade is finding paper, and yours is making it. You cannot win, Mr. Ishikawa. You can only choose how much it costs. That is what power is. That is all it has ever been." He settled back, mild again, kind again. "Take the manuscript home. Read it. I don't require your answer today."

Bram stood. Her legs held. She picked up the gray-bound manuscript — it weighed nothing and everything — and walked to the door, and paused there with her hand on the handle because there was one thing she needed to say, not for strategy but for herself.

"Thirty-one people," she said, without turning around. "You put their names in an appendix so gentlemen can run their fingers down the list over port and call it tragedy. I set type for a living, Sir Aldous. I know the difference between a name in an appendix and a name in a headline. One is a decoration. The other is an accusation. You're buying the first. What I have will give them the second."

She heard him set down his cup. "Good afternoon, Mr. Ishikawa," he said pleasantly. "Mind the step."

* * *

The walk back across Manchester took an hour and cost her more than the meeting had. She went the long way, through the strike-crowded streets where Chartist banners hung from upper windows and women chalked numbers on doors, and all the while the gray manuscript rode under her arm like a sleeping animal, and the offer rode inside her chest like a second heart.

Three hundred pounds. The shop. Safety. Her father's good name, undisturbed. Twenty more years of careful silences, comfortable ones this time, paid for rather than merely endured. She could print Renwick's beautiful lie and set her own name beneath it, and the world would call that success, and she would wake each morning knowing precisely what her hands had built.

At the shop, Elias was waiting in the alley despite his promise, hatless in the drizzle, and one look at her face told him everything.

"He offered you everything," Elias said.

"He offered me everything I've spent twenty years being afraid to want," Bram said. She held up the manuscript. "And this is the price tag."

She unlocked the door. Inside, the type cases waited in their long ranks, silent, patient, full of every letter she would ever need — including, she knew now, the letters of thirty-one names that deserved better than an appendix.

"Clear the composing stone," she said, and her voice did not shake. "We're setting the truth tonight."

But even as she said it, her eye fell on the shop's back door — standing open an inch, swinging gently, though she had locked it when she left.

Someone had been there first.
