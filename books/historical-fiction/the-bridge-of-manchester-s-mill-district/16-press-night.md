## 16. Press Night

The cellar under Ada Fenn's house had once held coal, and the smell of it still lived in the walls — a black, mineral breath that rose up through the floorboards and mingled with the sharper scents of ink and oil and lamp-black. Bram stood at the foot of the stairs and let the dark take her for a moment before she lit the first candle. It was better, she had learned long ago, to know a room in the dark before you knew it in the light. You learned where the truth of it was.

The press stood against the far wall like something raised from the dead.

Ada's old Albion — the one the constables had broken on their knees in '39, smashing the head with sledgehammers while Ada's husband bled in the doorway — was not that press. That press was splinters somewhere, or burned. This one was its sister, bought secondhand from a jobbing printer in Stockport who'd wanted rid of it, hauled through three cellars and two false walls over four years of patient Sundays, rebuilt bolt by bolt with Ada's hands and Elias's money and, in the last fortnight, Bram's own knowledge of what a frame needed to hold true.

It stood now, levelled on fresh oak sleepers, its platen gleaming where Elias had oiled it, its tympan patched with felt from an old coat. It looked, Bram thought, like a man who has been beaten within an inch of his life and has decided, quietly, to stand up anyway.

"You're staring at it again," Ada said behind her, coming down the stairs with a tray of candles. She was sixty if she was a day, small as a sparrow and hard as one too, her grey hair pinned back so severely it might have been set in type itself. "Staring won't pull sheets."

"I'm not staring. I'm listening."

"To what? It's not running yet."

"To whether anyone above us is listening to us." Bram tilted her head. The floor overhead creaked once — Ada's lodger, turning over in his bed, or the house settling on its joists. Then nothing. "All quiet."

"Quiet as a grave." Ada set the candles along a shelf cut into the brick. "Which is fitting, I suppose, given what we're about to wake up."

* * *

They worked by no schedule but the dragoons'.

That was the thing no one told you about printing sedition: the enemy kept hours, and you kept theirs. Every quarter hour, one of them climbed the cellar steps to the little trapdoor under the kitchen floor and put an ear to the gap. Outside, on the street above, the patrol passed at intervals that were almost regular enough to set your pulse by — boots on cobbles, the wet snort of horses, the creak of leather, sometimes a low word between soldiers, sometimes only silence, which was worse. Twice during the night a lantern's glow came down through the kitchen grating and laid bars of light across the flour sacks stacked against the wall, and all three of them froze where they stood, mid-motion, like figures in a tableau, until the light moved on.

Bram composed at a table wedged into the corner farthest from the trapdoor. The forme grew under her hands column by column — *The Truth of Harrow Mill* — and every piece of it she had argued over with herself for days. Too short and it would read like gossip; too long and they could not print three hundred copies before dawn. In the end she had pared it to a single sheet, dense as scripture, and the heart of it sat in the middle columns like a heart should:

Thirty-one names.

She had cut each one herself.

Not the names — those were only letters, and letters she could set in her sleep. But the face. *Truth*, she had called it, and the name was vanity and she knew it, but there are vanities that are also vows. For a week she had cut punches at night after the shop closed, grinding steel against steel in the small hours, filing matrices, casting type by candlelight when casting type by candlelight is a thing that can ruin your eyes and your fingers and your patience all at once. The face was plain — she had wanted it plain. No serifs fine enough to break on a bad press, no flourishes for the eye to slide past. Letters like doorposts. Letters that would stand up in a courtroom and say what they said and not apologize.

And now here they were, standing in the forme in rows, and Bram looked down at them and saw that they did not look like her work at all. They looked like the thirty-one themselves. Weavers and piecers and scavengers and doffers, ages thirteen to sixty-one, set line upon line the way they had been locked row upon row behind the chained doors of Harrow Mill, except that these rows were going out into the world instead of into the fire.

"Bram." Elias's voice, low, from the stairs.

She looked up. He stood with his ear still half-turned toward the ceiling, toward the street, and his face in the candlelight had gone the colour of tallow.

"Horses," he said. "More than the usual pair. They've stopped outside."

* * *

The candles went out. All of them, pinched between finger and thumb in the space of five seconds, because Ada Fenn had done this before and did not fumble. The dark in the cellar was total — the kind of dark that presses on the eyes until they invent their own light, little drifting sparks that are nothing but blood and nerves.

Above them, through the floorboards, the front door knocker sounded. Once. Twice.

Nobody answered. Ada's lodger had been paid to be elsewhere tonight, the front room made up to look unoccupied, the dust of a fortnight laid artfully over the parlor chairs.

A voice through the door, muffled: "Open in the King's name."

Silence. Bram's hand found the forme on the table and rested on it, lightly, the way you rest a hand on a sleeping child — as though touch alone could keep it safe. The type was locked tight in the chase, wedges driven home, but her palm pressed down anyway. Thirty-one names under her hand. Martha Quill's testimony beneath them in smaller text. The watchman's confession — God rest him, and God forgive the fall that killed him, though Bram no longer believed in the accident any more than she believed in the coroner's verdict of 1821 — set in full, every damning sentence of it.

Boots on the step. The letter-slot scraped open. Lantern light fell through it in a bright blade across the parlor floor — she could see it in her mind's eye, had rehearsed this room in her mind a dozen times — and then the light swept, lingered, and went out.

The slot closed. Boots descended. A murmur of voices outside, unhurried. Then the horses again, moving off, the sound thinning down Grafton Street until it was nothing.

They waited a full ten minutes longer, because Ada insisted, counting the time by the slow trickle of sand in a glass she kept for exactly this purpose. Then the candles relit, one by one, and nobody spoke about it, and the press began to run.

* * *

There is a sound a hand press makes when everything about it is right — the ink rolled thin and even, the frisket lifted true, the platen coming down square — a sound like a great slow heartbeat, *thump* and sigh, *thump* and sigh. Bram had heard it ten thousand times in her father's shop and had never once thought of it as sacred. Tonight it was sacred. Tonight it was the only prayer she trusted.

Ada pulled the lever. Elias fed the dampened paper. Bram stood at the stone and took each sheet as it came off, laying them in the pile with the reverence other people reserved for relics, because that was what they were. Three hundred copies. Three hundred chances for the dead to walk out of this cellar and into the mouths of Manchester.

The first impression was crooked. The second was perfect.

She held the second one up to the candle and read her own cutting by its light — *Truth*, standing crisp and black on cheap paper — and found that her eyes had blurred. She blinked the blur away impatiently. There was no time for weeping; weeping took minutes, and minutes took copies.

But she allowed herself one look. One long look at the middle columns, where the names ran in her own invented letters, each one spelled and spaced by her own hand: **HANNAH BREARLEY, aged 19.** **THOMAS OGDEN, aged 34.** **MARY ELLEN WHITTAKER, aged 13.** Down the column, thirty-one times, the age and the trade and the name, because Renwick's historians had recorded none of it, because the coroner had recorded none of it, because for twenty-one years the official account of Harrow Mill had contained exactly zero names and exactly zero ages and exactly one lie.

"They'll try to burn these," Elias said quietly. He was feeding paper with mechanical care, but his eyes were on her. "You know that. If they catch us, the first thing they'll do is fire."

"Then they'd best catch all three hundred," Bram said. "We'll not make it easy. Ada?"

Ada wiped her inky hands on her apron and grinned like a woman half her age. "Distribution's arranged. The Chartists move them at Kettledrum Fields tomorrow — hand to hand, before the rally, while the crowd's still gathering and the magistrates' men can't tell a pamphlet from a hymnal. By noon there'll be more of these in Manchester than there are copies of Renwick's precious history." Her grin faded a shade. "After noon, mind, it depends on whether the dragoons charge or not. So don't be late, and don't be proud, and for the love of God don't be seen carrying them."

* * *

Toward the small hours the rhythm settled and became almost peaceful, which Bram distrusted profoundly. Peace, in her experience, was what danger wore when it wanted to get close.

She worked the composing stick between pulls of the press, correcting as they printed — a turned letter here, a widow there, small sins of typography she could not abide even now, even tonight. Elias watched her do it and shook his head slowly.

"The fate of the city hangs on this sheet," he said, "and you're kerning."

"The fate of the city will hang better kerned." She slid the corrected line into place. "My father used to say a printer's conscience lives in the spacing. Loose spacing, loose morals."

"Did he?" Something moved across Elias's face — the shadow of the thing he never said aloud, the father who had died at Harrow Mill when Elias was nine years old, whose name Bram had set in that column an hour ago with hands that shook only slightly. "I wish I'd known him."

"He'd have liked you. He liked stubborn people who asked questions." She set another name — **JOSHUA FENN**, aged 27, no relation to Ada that either of them had ever traced, though Ada had gone very still when she'd seen it — and paused with the stick in her hand. "Elias. If tomorrow goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"If it does. If they take me, or the papers, or—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again, because twenty years of practice at not saying things was not undone in a night. "There's a copy in the forme. Hidden in the type. If everything else burns, the type survives. Type always survives. You melt it down and recast it and the words come back. Promise me you'll remember that. Whatever they smash — the words come back."

Elias set down the paper he was holding. He crossed the cellar and took both her hands, ink and all, and did not seem to care.

"Tomorrow," he said, "the whole district reads your father's conscience. And yours. And mine." His voice dropped lower. "And my father's. He's in that column, Bram. Third from the top. I checked the spelling four times."

"I know," Bram said softly. "**ELIAS VANE THE ELDER**. I set him myself. I wanted him to be the straightest line on the page."

For a moment neither of them moved, and the press thumped on behind them, Ada pulling the bar with her sparrow's strength, sheet after sheet after sheet, three hundred heartbeats of a machine that the King's men had once tried to kill and failed.

Then the sand in Ada's glass ran out for the final time, and Elias pulled the last damp copy from the stone and laid it atop the pile, and it was done.

Three hundred.

Bram stood over the stack in the guttering candlelight — a foot and a half of paper, weighing less than a loaf of bread, containing more truth than the entire archive of the Manchester boroughreeve's office — and felt the terror arrive all at once, now that there was no work left to hold it off. Tomorrow the dragoons. Tomorrow the magistrates' men. Tomorrow Renwick's agents with their lists and their lanterns. Tomorrow she would climb onto a platform at Kettledrum Fields with a price on her head and her brother's coat on her back and thirty-one names in her fist.

"Sleep," Ada ordered, snuffing candles. "Both of you. Two hours. You'll want your legs tomorrow."

Bram lay down on the pallet by the press without undressing, her cheek against the cold stone floor, and listened to the house breathe above her and the last of the ink dry below it. Sleep came slowly, and when it came, it brought the fire with it — it often did — doors chained, windows barred, thirty-one voices behind the glass.

But this time, in the dream, when the voices cried out, someone answered. Someone opened a door. And the sound that came through was the sound of a press, *thump* and sigh, *thump* and sigh, printing their names over and over until the whole sky was papered with them and no one could ever again pretend they had never lived.

She woke to Ada's hand on her shoulder, and to grey light seeping down the cellar stairs, and to the smell of rain on coal smoke — and to a knocking at the street door above, quick and urgent, and the sound of Ada's lodger's key turning in the lock an hour before he was due home.
