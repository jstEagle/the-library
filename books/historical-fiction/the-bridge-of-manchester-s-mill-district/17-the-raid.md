## 17. The Raid

The press was singing.

That was the only word Elias Vane had for it — the great iron voice of Ada Fenn's rebuilt Albion, shaking the cellar air in a rhythm like a hymn sung by something with no soul and perfect memory. Frisket down, tympan up, the platen falling with its deep bell-note, and each time it rose, another copy of *The Truth of Harrow Mill* lay damp and warm on the stone, thirty-one names inked black as wounds.

Three hundred copies. They were at two hundred and six.

Elias stood at the far end of the cellar with his coat off and his sleeves rolled, feeding sheets to Bram, who worked the press with her wrists and forearms and the whole lean architecture of her shoulders, her hair bound back under a cap that had slipped half loose. She had not stopped since midnight. Neither of them had spoken more than a handful of words in two hours; there was no need. The work spoke. The names spoke. Somewhere above them, Manchester slept badly under a sky the colour of wet ash, and the strike's third night crackled through the streets like a fire waiting for wind.

Ada Fenn sat by the candle on an upturned crate, folding. Her hands were sixty years old and moved like water. She folded each sheet once, twice, squared the edges against her knee, and laid them in a wicker basket that was already more than half full. She had been arrested twice before in her life, she'd told them earlier, and both times they had smashed her press, and both times she had built or bought or begged another within a year. "Iron is cheap," she'd said. "The trouble is always finding hands that remember how to make it speak."

Bram pulled the lever. The platen fell. Rose.

"Two hundred seven," Elias said.

"Two hundred eight," Bram corrected, pulling again before he could count the sheet. There was ink to the second knuckle of her right hand, worked so deep into the creases that it looked like shadow. She had not looked away from the forme in four hours except once, when the candle guttered, and even then only long enough to shield it with her body.

Elias thought: *we are going to finish this.* It was the first time in three weeks — since the watchman Cobbett had died, since the apprentice Wicks's face had gone strange and smooth when asked what he'd seen in the composing room — that he had allowed himself to believe it whole.

He should have known better. He was a census man. He knew better than anyone alive that the record is never finished until someone in power decides to close it, and closing is their favourite act in all the world.

* * *

It began, as catastrophes do, with a small wrongness.

A sound from above — not footsteps exactly, but the particular weighted silence of many men arranging themselves. Elias had spent eleven years listening at doors for a living; he heard the difference between a street emptying and a street filling, and Ancoats Lane was filling. He raised one hand without looking up from the stack of damp sheets, and Bram went still with her hand still on the lever.

They listened. Ten seconds. Fifteen.

Then boots on the pavement overhead — a dozen pairs, moving in step, and the flat official knock of a rifle butt on a door, and Ada Fenn was already on her feet saying, in a voice of terrible calm, "The coal hatch. Behind the paper store. I've kept it clear twenty years, God help me, I never thought I'd need it for myself."

"Bram—" Elias started.

She was already moving, sweeping the basket into his arms, killing the lamp over the stone, dragging the canvas cover off the press — no, leaving the cover, there was no time for dignity, no time to hide anything. The front door of the shop above splintered. Light bloomed down the stairwell, torchlight, and voices, and the voice that cut over all the others belonged to a man with a magistrate's authority in every syllable: *"In the King's name — nobody touches the machine till it's listed!"*

Listed. Inventoried. Seized as evidence. Elias understood, with a cold drop through his stomach, that this was not a raid to stop a printing. This was a raid to *record* one. Renwick wanted the sedition catalogued, the press impounded, the operators named in a report that would sit in a Home Office file longer than any of them would live. Renwick did not want them silenced. He wanted them *archived* — fixed forever in the record as criminals, while the thirty-one stayed uncounted.

"Go," Ada said. She put both her old hands flat on the basket's rim and pushed it toward him. "You carry it. You're the one they'll hang for papers; I'm only the woman they've hung for before." And when Elias hesitated — one heartbeat, two — she struck him across the chest, lightly, with the back of her hand, a printer's cuff, almost affectionate. "Boy, I've been in Strangeways twice. I know where they keep the tea. *Move.*"

Boots on the cellar stairs now. Torchlight swinging wild shapes across the low ceiling. Bram caught Elias's sleeve and hauled him behind the paper racks, into the reek of size and coal dust, where a wooden hatch lay flush in the floor beneath a pile of waste quires. She heaved it open on a breath of cold black air.

"In," she said. "Basket first."

He lowered the basket into the dark, felt it taken by nothing, heard it land somewhere below with a soft slap of paper — a drop of perhaps four feet onto coal. Then Bram went, feet-first, quick as water, and Elias followed, and above him the door at the top of the stairs gave way with a crash like a felled tree, and a voice shouted *"There! Below!"* and the hatch came down over his head as Bram dragged it shut from underneath.

Darkness. Total. The smell of coal, deep and mineral, a hundred years of it soaked into the wood. His own breathing, too loud. Bram's hand finding his wrist in the black and gripping hard — *this way* — and then her fingers leaving him, striking a lucifer.

The match flared. They were in a brick-lined channel barely wide enough for two men abreast, running out into darkness on both sides, the floor heaped with slack coal. The basket sat intact. Bram snuffed the match between finger and thumb.

"They'll find the hatch," she whispered.

"Not tonight." Her voice was steady but very fast. "It takes them ten minutes to smash a press. They'll enjoy it. We have ten minutes' start, maybe twelve." A pause. "Ada will give them nothing. She'll give them *lectures*. They'll be hours writing her down."

Elias lifted the basket. Two hundred and eight copies of the truth, weighing perhaps thirty pounds, and he carried them like something consecrated, because they were — the only surviving copy in the world of everything Cobbett had confessed with his dying breath, everything Martha Quill had signed her name to, every one of the thirty-one names set letter by letter by Bram Ishikawa's own hand.

They ran, bent double, into the dark.

* * *

The tunnels were older than the mills. Old workings, sealed galleries, drainage adits dug when Ancoats was fields and the river ran clean — nobody living knew them entire, but Ada had told them the shape of the thing that afternoon in her flat, matter-of-fact voice, drawing lines in spilled tea on the table: *the gallery runs north under the lane, then west along the old canal bed; there's a shaft by the tannery wall with an iron grate; the grate lifts if you know it lifts.* Bram knew it lifted. Bram knew everything about this district that a person could know without being born to it — and some things, Elias suspected, that she'd learned by being born to something else entirely, some other country of patience and precision that lived in her hands.

They went by touch and single matches, struck cupped, killed fast. The ceiling came down until Elias had to walk stooped, then nearly crawl. Water dripped somewhere with the patience of geology. Twice the passage forked and Bram chose without hesitation, left, then left again, and the second time Elias caught her arm in the dark and felt her pulse hammering under his fingers, faster than her voice had ever admitted to.

"You know where we are," he said. Not a question.

"I've delivered print to the tannery for nine years. There's a grating in their yard the apprentices use for— never mind. It opens onto Shottery Row." A beat. "Elias. If they've posted men on the row—"

"Then we come up into their arms, and the basket comes up with us, and we lose everything in one place instead of slowly." He shifted the weight on his shoulder. "I've read enough coroners' verdicts to prefer the one place."

Something that might have been a laugh escaped her, brief and dark. In the matchlight her eyes were enormous and absolutely calm, and Elias understood suddenly, viscerally, why she had survived twenty years passing as her dead brother in a city that ate the unwary: not hardness. *Attention.* She simply saw everything, all the time, and told no one what she saw.

They reached the shaft. Iron rungs set in the brick, green with old rust, rising perhaps fifteen feet to a square of grey pre-dawn light crossed by the shadow of the grate. Bram climbed, tested the grate with two fingers — it lifted — and eased it aside an inch, and lay listening against the opening for a full minute while Elias held his breath below her in the coal-smelling dark.

"Clear," she breathed. "Rain coming. Nobody stands sentry in rain if they can stand sentry in a doorway."

She went up. He handed the basket after her, climbed, and came out into the tannery yard at first light, into a thin needle-rain and the stench of lime pits, and pulled the grate back down over the hole, and stood for a moment among the hides and the dripping eaves feeling the astonishing, fragile miracle of open air.

Shottery Row was waking. A milk cart. A woman beating a rug. Smoke from a hundred chimneys standing straight up in the windless grey. Three-quarters of a mile south, Kettledrum Fields waited, and by noon it would hold a hundred thousand people, and the pamphlets had to be among them before the crowd formed ranks, because a crowd could be searched but a *movement* could not.

"We split them," Bram said, already counting the bundles by touch, wrapping them in oilcloth from her satchel, her hands doing three things at once. "Forty to the marchers coming down Every Street. Forty to the weavers' picket at the canal bridge. The rest to the field itself, to whoever has hands and a conscience. You take the bridge. I take Every Street."

"No." It came out of him harder than he intended. "Not split. Not today. Today they're hunting us by name, Bram — Wicks described you to them, do you understand? Your *face*, your build, the cap, the ink. A woman alone distributing seditious pamphlets on strike morning is a description they can work with. We stay together, we move together, and we get these into the field or we don't get them anywhere."

For a moment she looked at him with something flickering behind the calm — he couldn't name it, anger or fear or the thing that lived between those — and then she nodded once, and shouldered the basket, and they walked out of the tannery yard into the rain like two ordinary workers going to a rally, which was the most dangerous disguise either of them had ever worn.

* * *

Every Street was filling.

By the time they reached the corner of Foundry Lane the rain had thickened and the crowd had begun to arrive — not a mob yet, not a procession, just Manchester pouring downhill toward Kettledrum Fields in a thousand separate streams: mill girls in shawls two and three abreast, colliers with their faces still grey, Irish labourers, weavers carrying folded banners wrapped in oilcloth like infants, old men, boys running messages between groups, and everywhere the Chartist cry passed mouth to mouth in a low continuous murmur, *the Charter, the Charter, vote and be free,* under it all a deeper note that Elias had heard once before, years ago, at his father's graveside — the sound of people who have decided to stop being afraid.

He felt the basket's weight shift as Bram began, quietly, without breaking stride, to feed bundles to the crowd. Into the hands of a mill girl: a folded pamphlet, *read it, pass it on.* Into the pocket of a carter leaning on his cart: same words. A boy took six and vanished uphill with them. Within fifty yards of walking, forty copies were gone into the current of bodies, and no soldier on earth could ever retrieve them all.

And then Elias saw the boy again — the same boy, doubled back, cutting through an alley mouth — and saw whom he was running toward.

Three men stood outside the shuttered corn factor's office, trying not to look like what they were. Rain-dark coats, good boots, and the stillness of hired muscle. One held a slate. As the boy reached them and spoke, pointing back up Every Street, the man with the slate looked up — and Elias knew the face. Wicks. The apprentice. Standing in the rain beside Renwick's agents with his collar up and his chin tucked, and even at seventy yards Elias could see the terrible eagerness of him, the hunger of a weak man finally holding something worth selling.

"He's pointed," Elias said quietly. "Bram. He's pointed at the crowd. They'll work the line now, checking faces."

"Then we stop being faces." Her voice didn't rise at all. "Head down. Walk. The fields are two streets on — once we're inside a hundred thousand people, a description dies."

They walked. The rain came harder. Behind them, faint through the drumming on cobbles, Elias heard a shout — one of the agents, calling to another, and the sound of boots beginning to move — and he did not run, because Bram had taught him without ever saying it that running is a confession, and they walked, and turned, and the crowd took them.

Kettledrum Fields opened ahead like a sea deciding to rise. Tens of thousands already, and more streaming in from every road, banners unfurling wet and bright — *No Vote, No Work* — and somewhere a band playing, and dragoons drawn up in ranks along the far hedgerow, patient as winter, and magistrates' carriages gleaming at the field's edge like beetles.

Into that, Bram Ishikawa walked with the last hundred and twenty copies of the truth on her shoulder, head down, ink-stained, hunted, and Elias saw her lift her face at last to look at the platform at the field's centre — the rough plank rostrum where the speakers would stand — and he saw the thought arrive in her the way type arrives in a stick, letter by letter, inevitable.

"The platform," she said. "When the moment comes, I go up, and I read the names aloud myself. All thirty-one. Before the charge, if there's a charge — and there will be a charge." She looked at him, rain running off her jaw, and her eyes held no doubt whatsoever, which frightened him more than the soldiers did. "Everything else is lost. The shop, the press, my name, my life as it was. But the papers aren't lost, Elias. They're *here*—" she touched the basket "—and in forty minutes they'll be nowhere and everywhere at once. Inside ten thousand heads. You taught me that. Records can be burned. *Crowds* can't."

Down the field, among the wet thousands, a bugle sounded — not the Chartists'. The dragoons' pickets were mounting.

And behind them, unnoticed until now, working along the crowd's edge with a slate in his hand and Renwick's agents at his back, came Wicks — reading faces, moving closer, closing the distance with every step, a boy who had sold his master's secret and was about to be paid for it.
