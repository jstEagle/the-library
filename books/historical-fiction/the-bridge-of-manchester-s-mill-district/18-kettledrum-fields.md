## 18. Kettledrum Fields

The pamphlets weighed nothing and everything.

Three hundred copies of *The Truth of Harrow Mill*, wrapped in oilcloth beneath my coat, pressed against my ribs like a second heartbeat as Elias and I came up out of the coal tunnels into the grey morning light of Kettledrum Fields. My hands were still black to the wrist from the press — Ada's press, now a ruin of iron and broken stone behind us in that cellar — and I had not stopped to wash them. There had been no time. There was no time now.

The fields were already filling.

I had seen crowds before. I had stood at the shop window on Peter Street during the turnouts of '36 and watched ten thousand men move through the district like a slow brown river. But this was different. This was not a river. This was a tide coming in from every direction at once — from Ancoats and Bradford, from Salford across the water, from the villages beyond Gorton where the mills had swallowed whole generations. Men in their Sunday coats because they had no working coats left worth wearing. Women with shawls over their heads and children hoisted onto shoulders so they could see. A forest of banners — the cap of liberty, the loom, the printing press itself rendered in gold thread on green silk — dipping and swaying above the sea of hats like the masts of a fleet at anchor.

And everywhere, the sound. Not shouting yet. Something worse than shouting: a low continuous murmur, forty thousand voices all speaking at once but all leaning toward the same silence, waiting for it to be filled.

"They'll disperse by noon," Elias said beside me. He had his collar up and his census satchel slung across his chest, and he looked, absurdly, like a man come to take a count. In a way, I supposed, he was. "The magistrates have declared it unlawful. The dragoons are massed at the Infirmary. We have until they decide we've waited long enough."

"Then we don't wait."

I had spent twenty years learning how to be small. How to stand at the back of a room with my eyes down and my voice pitched low and my true name folded away like a letter never sent. Every instinct I owned was telling me what it had always told me: find the edge of the crowd, work outward, hand them out quietly, one to a man, and let the truth travel the way truth travels among the poor — mouth to mouth, whisper to whisper, untraceable.

But three hundred pamphlets passed hand to hand would reach perhaps three hundred people, and half of those would tuck them away unread, afraid of the sedition charge, afraid of the constable's truncheon, afraid of losing the day's wage or the day's bread.

Forty thousand people were standing in this field. And Renwick's agents were already among them.

I had seen two of them on the walk up from the tunnels — men too clean for the district, standing at the field's corners with their hands in their pockets and their eyes doing arithmetic on every bundle anyone carried. One wore a green baize coat I recognized. He had sat in my composing room eleven days ago, turning my father's job books with gloved fingers while the apprentice, Jem, hovered and lied for him. If he saw me, if he saw the shape of the parcel under my coat, it would end here, in the mud, before a single name was read.

"Bram." Elias caught my sleeve. "You're not going to do what I think you're going to do."

"The platform," I said.

* * *

The platform had been built overnight at the field's centre — rough planks on beer barrels, a roof of sailcloth strung between poles, meant for the speakers whose turn would come when the great men arrived: Feargus O'Connor's delegates, the men whose names were on every wall in Manchester. It stood empty now, guarded loosely by a knot of Chartist stewards with white wands, who were less a defence than a suggestion.

Getting to it meant crossing four hundred yards of crowd, and every yard of those four hundred yards was a chance for the green baize coat to find me.

We went together, which was either courage or foolishness, and I have never settled which. Elias walked on my left, between me and the corner where the agents stood, talking steadily about the weather, about the price of flour, about anything, his voice a screen behind which I moved. The crowd did the rest. Forty thousand bodies make a country of themselves; you pass through hamlets and parishes of strangers, and each one presses close and apologizes and presses close again, and no man can see another man's burden in such a crush.

Halfway across, a woman looked at me — really looked, the way women look when they have learned to read faces for danger — and saw whatever there was to see. The black on my hands. The terror under my hat brim.

"What is it you're carrying?" she asked. Not hostile. Curious. Her shawl was the grey of mill lint and her knuckles were the knuckles of thirty years at a loom.

I should have said nothing. I had said nothing for twenty years; saying nothing was the one craft I had mastered better than type.

"It's names," I heard myself say. "Thirty-one names."

Her face changed. Not understanding — recognition. As though I had spoken a word from a language she thought dead.

"Harrow," she said.

"Yes."

She turned without another word and began to clear a path. Not dramatically — she simply put her shoulder into the crowd ahead of us and shouted, "Make way for the printer! Make way!" and whether they believed her or merely obeyed, the sea opened. Word ran along the crowd faster than we walked, the way fire runs along a fuse: *the printer, the printer, she's brought the printer.* By the time we reached the platform steps I understood that I had not crossed a crowd at all. The crowd had carried me.

A steward with a white wand barred the steps. "Speakers only."

"I'm not a speaker." I pulled one pamphlet from the oilcloth and held it up between two fingers, and the wind took the top sheet and fluttered it open, and the steward read the first line upside down — *On the night of the ninth of March, 1821, the doors of Harrow Mill were chained from the outside* — and his face went the colour of the sailcloth above us.

"Christ's bones," he said. "Get up, then. Get up before I think better of it."

* * *

From the platform, the field was an ocean, and I was a single plank adrift on it.

The sailcloth roof snapped in the wind. Below me the stewards' white wands bobbed like buoys. And beyond the crowd, at the far edges of the fields, I could see them: the mounted police, drawn up in column, blue and motionless, with the dragoons' red coats visible behind them near the Infirmary road like a fire banked but not out. They were waiting for orders. Orders would come. Everyone in that field knew it, and everyone stayed anyway, and that — more than any banner, any speech — was the most frightening thing I had ever seen: forty thousand people who had decided that being dispersed was no longer the worst thing that could happen to them.

Elias stood at the foot of the platform, holding the oilcloth bundle, passing copies up to me two and three at a time.

"You don't have to read it aloud," he said quietly. "Hand them down. Let them read for themselves. That's enough. That's more than enough."

But I had set every one of those names myself. Letter by letter, in the dark of Ada's cellar, with the dragoons' hoofbeats audible through the floorboards overhead, I had chosen each piece of type for each dead hand — the tall f for Martha Quill's sister, the worn e for the watchman's own son, whose name the old man had dictated through his tears before Renwick's men dropped him down a stairwell. Those letters had been in my fingers for six hours. They were in my fingers now.

I raised the first copy over my head.

"Hear me!" My voice cracked on the second word. Twenty years of pitching it low, and now I needed it to cross an ocean, and it failed me like a rusted tool. I tried again, and the wind ate it, and the nearest hundred faces tilted up at me with polite, hopeless attention, the way one watches a madman on a barrel.

Then the woman in the grey shawl was beside the platform, and she cupped her hands around her mouth and roared — a sound that came up from somewhere below the lungs, a weaver's sound, the sound of commanding a shed full of screaming looms for thirty years:

"*QUIET! She's got the Harrow names!*"

The murmur died. It died the way a loom dies when the belt slips — not gradually, but all at once, a vast mechanical silence falling across Kettledrum Fields, forty thousand people holding their breath in the same instant. Somewhere a baby cried and was hushed. The wind snapped the sailcloth. And I stood on the planks with the paper shaking in my hands and understood that I would never again in my life have a silence like this one offered to me, and that what I did with the next minute would be the whole of what I was.

"On the night of the ninth of March, 1821," I read, and my voice held, "the doors of Harrow Mill were chained from the outside, by order of the foreman Josiah Crake, acting for Sir Aldous Renwick, while two hundred workers stood strike inside."

The silence broke — not into noise but into a kind of terrible attention, a forward lean of the entire field, forty thousand bodies tilting toward me like grass toward light.

"The fire began at half past nine. The chains were not struck off. The overseers' door was locked. The windows were barred against the unions. Thirty-one workers did not come out. Their names were never entered in the parish register. No inquest was truly held. No notice was printed. For twenty-one years, they have been counted as runaways, as debtors fled, as nobody at all."

I drew the first breath of the roll.

"Ann Barlow. Weaver. Nineteen years of age."

A sound went through the crowd — not a gasp; something lower, older, a groan that seemed to rise out of the ground itself.

"Thomas Barlow. Drawer. Seventeen years of age."

Brother and sister. I had set them side by side in the forme because the watchman had named them side by side, weeping, and I could not bear to separate them even in lead.

"Martha Quill's sister, Esther Quill. Weaver. Twenty-three years of age—"

I saw it happen then, the thing I will see for the rest of my life whenever I close my eyes: the names leaving me and becoming the crowd's. A woman near the front took up the reading after me, echoing each name back in a carrying shout so the far thousands could hear, and then men further out took it from her, name by name, relaying it outward across the field like buckets at a fire, until the whole of Kettledrum Fields was one distributed voice reciting its own dead. *Samuel Ogden. Mary Ashworth. Joseph and Lydia Fearnley, husband and wife.* Each name fell into the crowd and was caught, and held, and passed on, and I stood at the centre of it with my arms aching from holding papers high and tears cutting channels through the coal dust on my face, and I understood that this was what a press was for. Not the iron frame. Not the screw and the stone. This. A name is a piece of type, and a crowd is a press, and today the whole of Manchester was pulling the lever.

Below me, Elias was no longer passing copies up. He was standing on a barrel, hurling armfuls of them into the crowd, and the crowd was not keeping them — it was *reading them aloud*, strangers clustering around each copy, one man holding it up while a dozen others spelled the words over his shoulders, and those dozen turned and shouted the gist to the people behind them. The pamphlets were dissolving into the crowd like salt into water. You could not have gathered them back if you had bought every copy with gold.

Which is when the green baize coat started moving through the crowd toward us, and behind him, at the field's edge, the mounted police lowered their visors.

* * *

I heard the order given before I saw it given — a thin silver whistle from the Infirmary road, three notes, and the column of horsemen unfolded like a blade opening. They came on at a trot along the field's northern margin, not yet charging, herding, the way they always began, testing the crowd's temper with the sheer weight of horseflesh. The stewards' white wands waved frantically, directing people back, back, and the crowd gave ground sullenly, closing around the platform like a fist around a coal.

"Off," Elias shouted up at me, hauling at the planks. "Bram — off, now, they'll ride straight through this stage and everyone on it—"

"Not yet." My hands were empty. All three hundred gone — I had watched the last bundle vanish into the sea of upstretched arms not two minutes past. But the master copy, the forme itself, the actual lead — I had promised Ada. The type was wrapped in sacking inside my shirt, thirty-one lines of it, cold against my skin. "The type goes back to Ada. Whatever happens to me—"

"Nothing happens to you." His face was white and furious and terrified, and underneath all of it, unmistakably, was something else, something we had not had time to name in cellars and coal tunnels and I suddenly hated myself for leaving unnamed. "Get *down*."

The green baize coat reached the platform steps with two other men, and they came up onto the planks with the confidence of men who had a magistrate's warrant in a pocket somewhere and the law's whole weight behind their elbows. The crowd around the platform went very still, the way a street goes still before a fight.

"There she is," the green baize coat said, pleasantly, loudly, for the benefit of whoever might be listening. "Miss Ishikawa. Or should I say—" and he smiled, and I knew exactly what word was coming, the word he had held over me since the composing room, the word that had kept me small for twenty years, *master* Ishikawa, *mistress* Ishikawa, the word that would undo me in front of forty thousand people—

"—the *printer*," he finished, because forty thousand people were watching, and even Renwick's creatures understood that some words were dangerous to say in certain air.

He reached for my arm. The whistle sounded again, closer, urgent, and the drum of hooves changed — the trot became a canter became something worse — and the crowd screamed as the first ranks of mounted police drove into the field's edge, batons rising and falling, and the whole ocean of humanity surged toward the platform like water toward a breach.

What happened next happened in the space of three heartbeats, and I have never been able to arrange it into order since. The green baize coat seized my wrist. Elias struck it free. The sailcloth roof tore loose from one pole and came down over all of us like a wave, and under its smothering canvas I heard the crowd roar — not in fear, in fury, forty thousand voices finding one voice at last — and I felt hands, dozens of hands, anonymous and sure, lift me off the platform entirely and pass me backward over the heads of the mob, away from the police, away from Renwick's men, from grip to grip to grip, the way the names had been passed, the way the pamphlets had been passed, until I dropped into the press of bodies at the rear and found Elias's hand closing around mine in the smoke and the shouting.

Behind us the platform went down under the horses. Ahead of us the road to Ancoats lay open, and somewhere in the crowd a boy of maybe twelve was running beside us, waving a crumpled copy of the pamphlet over his head and screaming the names at the top of his voice, and every person he passed took the cry up and carried it further.

"They have it," Elias panted, dragging me through the gap in the crowd. "Bram — they *have* it. It's out of our hands now."

"No," I said, because I could feel the forme of type hard against my ribs, and because I had seen, in the last instant before the sailcloth fell, a phaeton at the field's far corner, and Sir Aldous Renwick sitting in it very upright and very pale, watching his history dissolve into forty thousand mouths. "It's only just begun. He knows every name that read them. And he knows where Ada's cellar is — and Jem has been missing since dawn."

The first dragoons came onto the field at the gallop, and the crowd broke like a dam, and we ran.
