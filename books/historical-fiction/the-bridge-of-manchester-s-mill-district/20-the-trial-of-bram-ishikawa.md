## 20. The Trial of Bram Ishikawa

They held the trial at the Sessions House on Hunt's Bank, in a court built to make small people feel smaller — a vaulted room all dark oak and darker glass, where the light came down filtered and gray like weather through a dirty window. Bram had set type for a hundred legal notices in her life. *Notice is hereby given.* She had never once considered that she would stand beneath words like them.

The cell below had been cold but quiet, and quiet was a country she knew how to live in. Twenty years of it. She had learned its dialects: the silence of a daughter becoming a son overnight, the silence of a printer who saw what crossed his shop and asked nothing, the silence of a whole district agreeing not to remember thirty-one names. She had thought silence was armor. Standing now before the bar, wrists chafed where the irons had been struck off, she understood it had only ever been a slow way of drowning.

The courtroom was full past sense. They had expected a curiosity — the seditionist printer, the forger — and got instead half of Kettledrum Fields squeezed into benches and standing along the walls, mill hands with their caps crushed in their fists, women with shawls over their hair, faces she knew from the crowd that had passed the pamphlet hand to hand while dragoons wheeled through the smoke. The magistrate had wanted a quiet trial. The district had declined to give him one.

Bram stood in borrowed clothes — they had taken her coat when they took her, and someone had returned a shapeless jacket that hung wrong on her shoulders — and she kept her eyes level and her breathing even, because those were the two disciplines her father had taught her first. *Steady hands, Bram. A trembling compositor ruins a page.* Her hands were steady. Her heart was not.

The prosecutor was a thin man named Garrowby with a voice like a filing knife, and he began as she knew he would: not with Harrow Mill, not with Renwick, but with papers. Travel warrants, forged. Names invented, dates falsified, an official seal imitated by a skilled hand. He held up Martha Quill's passage documents as though they were instruments of treason rather than tickets out of a city that had chained its doors on living women once already.

"Three warrants," Garrowby said, turning them so the gallery could see, "each bearing a false name, each procured under false pretenses by the prisoner, who presents herself to this court as—" he consulted his sheet with theatrical care— "*one* Bram Ishikawa, printer, of Ishikawa & Son, Ancoats."

*As one*, she noted. He could not resist beginning there. Not the forgery itself, not even the sedition — but the *as*. He meant to build the whole case on that foundation: that everything else followed from a woman who had lived twenty years as a lie.

She let him build. She had learned from Elias Vane, over cellar candles and formes of type, how evidence was assembled, how a record hardened into truth simply by being written down in the proper order. Let him write his order. She intended to amend it.

* * *

The morning went as prosecutions go — methodical, airless. Witnesses paraded: the apprentice, Jem Cotter, pale and unable to meet her eye, reciting how he had copied the witness statements for "a gentleman" whose name he did not know; the stationmaster who had examined the forged warrants; a constable describing the raid on Ada Fenn's cellar, the press broken, three hundred pamphlets seized or scattered. At each item the gallery stirred and settled again like water finding its level.

Then Garrowby reached the part he had saved, the part he clearly believed was the blade.

"I put it to the court," he said, "that this prisoner's entire existence is a forgery. That the person before you is not Bram Ishikawa at all, but his sister — Miss Bridget Ishikawa — who has usurped her dead brother's name, his business, his very identity, these twenty years. Every document she has touched is tainted by that original fraud. Can we trust the word of a woman who has deceived her neighbors, her creditors, her King's officers, for two decades?"

He turned to her then, and there it was — the look she had spent twenty years learning to read on customers' faces, on census takers' faces, on every face that paused too long over her hands or her voice or her jaw. The look that said *I know what you are, and I am deciding what it costs you.*

The gallery had gone very still. Somewhere near the back, a woman whispered, and the whisper moved outward like wind through wheat.

The magistrate leaned forward. "Prisoner. You will answer the charge."

And here was the moment — she felt it arrive the way she used to feel a press come to the top of its stroke, that instant of perfect balance before the impression bites. She could answer carefully. She could say *I am Bram Ishikawa* in the flat legal sense, claim the name as property, fight for it on technical ground, keep the door of herself shut. It was what she had done all her life. It might even work; courts respected technicalities the way mills respected steam.

But she looked up at the gallery — at Martha Quill's eldest daughter sitting with her mother's arm around her, wearing the coat Bram's forged papers had bought her passage toward — and she thought about what silence cost, and who paid it.

"My name," Bram said, and her voice carried further than she expected, the way voices do in rooms that have gone utterly silent, "is Bram Ishikawa."

Garrowby smiled thinly. "The court has heard—"

"I am not finished," she said, and something in her tone stopped even him. "You wish to tell the court who I am. Very well. I will tell them myself, since apparently no one else can be trusted with the telling."

She stepped forward to the rail, and the constable beside her did not stop her, because there was nothing in the rules against a prisoner speaking her own name.

"I was born in this city to Toma Ishikawa, a typeface cutter, and his wife Keiko, who came from Nagasaki by way of Amsterdam and never once complained of the cold. I had a brother called Bram, who was sick from birth and died at fourteen. When my father's hands failed him, someone had to run the shop. The trade guilds would not license a woman. The banks would not lend to one. The law of this land decided, without asking me, that my labor was worth nothing unless it wore a man's name." She let that stand a moment. "So I wore it. For twenty years I set your newspapers, your hymnals, your legal notices — the very forms Mr. Garrowby reads from were likely cast from matrices my father cut and I dressed. I printed this city while it slept, and it never once occurred to any of you to wonder at my hands."

A sound ran through the gallery — not laughter, not anger. Recognition.

"You call me a fraud. I say the fraud was committed *against* me, by every institution that made honesty impossible. But I will not defend myself on that ground alone, because there is a greater matter in this room than my name, and Mr. Garrowby has worked very hard to keep it out of his list of charges. So I will enter it myself."

She turned to face the magistrate directly.

"On the ninth of August, 1821, thirty-one workers were locked inside Harrow Mill during a strike. The doors were chained. The watchman held the keys and was paid afterward to forget. When fire took the mill, those thirty-one men and women burned, and the coroner ruled it accident, and the death notices were never printed because my father was paid to see they never were — and when he refused to print the memorial broadsheet, another printer did, and that printer's press was smashed for it. Sir Aldous Renwick has spent twenty-two years buying silence, and last month he tried to buy mine, and when I refused, he sent soldiers to destroy the testimony of the witnesses I protected. That, gentlemen, is the true indictment. I merely forged some travel papers. This city forged a massacre."

The gallery erupted. It was not a riot — not quite — but it was a roar, forty throats and then four hundred echoing *thirty-one, thirty-one*, and the magistrate's gavel came down again and again and could not find the floor of the noise. Garrowby was on his feet protesting, and the protest drowned, and Bram stood at the rail in her borrowed coat and felt something she had not felt since her father died: that she was exactly where she was supposed to be, saying exactly what needed saying, in her own voice, under her own name.

It took the bailiffs several minutes to restore order. She waited them out with steady hands.

* * *

They nearly adjourned. She heard the magistrate confer in low tones with Garrowby — *most irregular, the prisoner cannot be permitted to turn the trial into a platform* — but the gallery would not disperse, and dispersing it by force would have required troops, and troops in the streets after Kettledrum Fields was a thing even the magistrates feared more than sedition. So they pressed on, and the afternoon belonged, unexpectedly, to the defense.

Her advocate was a weary radical barrister named Croft whom Ada Fenn's friends had scraped together from somewhere, and he did what he could with little, until late in the day, when the courtroom doors opened and Elias Vane walked in.

He looked worse than when she had last seen him — thinner, a healing cut above his eyebrow from the coal tunnels, his coat scorched at one cuff — and he walked with a limp he tried to hide. In his arms he carried a document folder tied with string, and behind him came a clerk of the Home Office, which caused a visible stir along the bench of magistrates' officials, because a Home Office clerk meant London, and London meant the inquiry.

Elias did not look at her as he presented himself. He was disciplined that way; he knew how a glance between conspirators read in a courtroom. But when he reached the well of the court and turned, their eyes met for half a second, and in that half-second he told her everything: *it worked. London read the pamphlet. The names counted.*

"The prosecution," Elias said to the court, in the dry voice of a man accustomed to being believed, "has characterized the prisoner as a forger whose documents cannot be trusted. I am a census officer of eleven years' standing. I hold here the sworn statement of Samuel Oakes, watchman of Harrow Mill, dictated in the presence of two witnesses five days before his death, attested by me and countersigned. I also hold the coroner's inquest file of 1821, which I obtained through lawful inquiry, and which I invite the court to compare against the payroll ledgers of Harrow Mill for the same period."

Croft rose. "Does the witness assert that these materials are genuine?"

"I assert more," Elias said. "I assert that I have spent eleven years counting the people of this district, and that thirty-one of them have never been counted at all. My office exists to make every soul answerable to the record. Somebody made certain these thirty-one never entered it. The sworn statement names the foreman who chained the doors. The payroll shows wages continued to a dead man's number for six weeks after the fire. And the prisoner—" he paused, and now he did look at her, openly, before the whole room— "the prisoner risked her life to carry that statement out of a burning searchlight of persecution, and lost her shop, her liberty, and nearly her life doing it. If forging papers to save a widow's children from this city's justice is a crime, then I say the crime lies in the justice, not the papers."

He untied the string. He laid Oakes's statement on the bench of the examining officer, and the courtroom bent toward it as one body — mill hands craning, clerks forgetting themselves, even Garrowby leaning despite himself, a man watching his own case walk out the door.

The reading took time. They made Elias read portions aloud, his voice level, each sentence falling into the hush like type into a forme: *I held the keys. I was told the hands would be let out at six. At six I was told to wait. I smelled the smoke and I ran for the door and the chain was on it and I had the key to the chain in my pocket and I was not permitted—*

He stopped there. His voice had not broken, precisely, but it had changed weight, and everyone in the room understood why: his own father's name was among the thirty-one, and he had just read aloud, in effect, the manner of his father's death.

No one spoke. Even the magistrate lowered his eyes.

When it resumed, the trial was a different animal altogether. Garrowby tried twice more to raise the forgery charges and twice found the gallery humming with contempt. The question before the court was no longer whether Bram Ishikawa had falsified travel warrants — she had, and said so plainly when asked, because she would not lie in a courtroom after telling so much truth. The question, murmured in the aisles and the corridors and spilling out onto the steps by evening, was why a city that punished her for forging three papers had rewarded the men who forged thirty-one deaths.

* * *

They did not reach a verdict that day. The magistrate announced that in light of the new materials, and "the pending inquiry of the Home Office," sentencing would be deferred — a weasel's phrase, but Bram had learned to hear the current beneath official language. Deferral meant doubt. Doubt, in a courtroom full of the district's sympathy and London's clerks, meant she might live.

They took her back down through the stone corridors toward the cells, and at the turning of the stair a bailiff — a heavy, red-faced man she had watched all day maintain perfect neutrality — glanced left and right, then said quietly, without looking at her:

"My mother wove at Harrow Mill. She weren't on the night list." He said it the way a man drops a coin in a beggar's cup and hurries on. "Good luck to you, missus."

*Missus.* The first person outside a handful of souls to call her that to her face, and he chose a prison stair to do it, and she found she could have kissed him.

In the cell, the lamp guttered and the stone breathed cold, and Bram sat on the plank bed and did the thing she had not allowed herself since the raid: she thought about her father. *You chose silence to protect me,* she thought. *And I have chosen noise, and it may hang me, and I think you would understand anyway.* She could almost see him at the stone, sleeves rolled, setting a line of type he would never sign, teaching her that letters had weight — and that weight, borne long enough and honestly enough, becomes bearing.

Near midnight, boots in the corridor, keys, and the door opened on Elias Vane, holding a lantern and a piece of paper, his face doing something she had never seen it do — trying to decide between gravity and joy.

"They've reopened the inquest," he said. "Formally. And there's more — the historian. Renwick's historian. He was at Kettledrum Fields when the names were read. He's asked to testify."

Bram stood. "Renwick's own historian."

"Defected this evening," Elias said, and held out the paper — a deposition, freshly signed, the ink still smelling of the candle it had been written by. "Bram. It isn't over. But the record is moving. And it's moving *our* way."
