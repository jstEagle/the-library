## 21. The Record Amended

The Home Office letter arrived on a Tuesday, and Elias Vane read it four times before he trusted himself to understand it.

It was addressed to him — *Mr. E. Vane, Census Office, Manchester* — in a hand so official it might have been cut into stone rather than written with a pen. The seal had been broken by a clerk somewhere in Whitehall who would never know what the words inside meant to the man holding them. Elias stood at the counter of Ada Fenn's cellar workshop, where the wrecked press still sat under its sheet of sacking like a body awaiting burial, and read:

*In consequence of representations received respecting the circumstances attending the destruction by fire of Harrow Mill, Manchester, on 14th August 1821, Her Majesty's Commissioners have resolved that the matter of the coroner's verdict be reopened to full inquiry. Witnesses desiring to submit sworn testimony are instructed to apply to the Assistant Commissioner appointed for the Northern Districts.*

Below that, a name and an address in London, and below that, in smaller type, the sentence he kept returning to: *Particular attention will be paid to any evidence contradicting the findings recorded in the original inquest of September 1821.*

They knew. Somewhere above them, in rooms with high windows and long tables, men whose faces he had never seen had read the pamphlet — or heard it read aloud, which amounted to the same thing — and thirty-one dead weavers had become a question the government could no longer leave folded shut.

He carried the letter upstairs through the shop that smelled of ink and coal smoke and old paper, past Ada's daughter grinding type-cleaning oil into a rag, up the narrow stair to the room Bram had taken since the trial collapsed. He knocked twice, their signal, and heard her voice say come in.

She was at the window. She spent a great deal of time at windows now, as though she were learning, after twenty years of passing unseen, how it felt simply to be looked at. Her hair was loose — she wore it however she pleased these days, and the district had adjusted its gossip accordingly, the way water adjusts around a stone. When he laid the letter on the table between them without a word, she crossed the room and read it standing, one finger tracing the lines the way she traced the grooves of a new-cut letter.

"They're reopening the inquest," she said.

"They're reopening the inquest."

"Because of us." She said it flatly, testing the weight of it, the way she tested a newly cast piece of type between finger and thumb — checking for flaws.

"Because of you," Elias said. "I only carried things."

She almost smiled. It didn't fully arrive; it hadn't, much, in the three weeks since the trial. Justice, they had both learned, did not feel the way the crowd's cheering had made it seem it would feel. Justice felt like this: a letter, a reopened file, a man in London preparing to ask questions that should have been asked twenty-two years ago, while the people who could answer them counted their dead again one by one and found the counting cost more than the cheering ever promised.

"The Assistant Commissioner will want witnesses," Bram said. "Sworn testimony. In person, I imagine, or at least notarized." She turned from the window. "Elias. Martha cannot go to London. You know what Renwick is."

"I know what Renwick *was*," Elias said. "I'm less certain what he is this morning."

* * *

What Sir Aldous Renwick was, that morning, was a man watching his own history being unwritten.

Elias learned of it secondhand, then thirdhand, then from every mouth in the district at once, because Manchester in the spring of 1843 talked the way the Irwell flooded after rain — all at once and everywhere. The commemorative history of Harrow Mill, two years in the preparation, five hundred guineas of Renwick's money sunk into paper and engravings, was recalled from the bindery. Not postponed. Recalled. The printer who held the contract — not their shop; Renwick had never forgiven the shop — sent back the unbound sheets and refused further work, and when a reporter from the *Manchester Guardian* asked him why, the printer looked at the floor and said he had been given to understand certain chapters required revision, and no revision had been supplied, and he was not a man to print blank pages.

Then Mr. Callum Reeve resigned his commission.

That was the name the district had not known until now: Callum Reeve, historian, Fellow of some society or other, author of the commemorative history, compiler of the official record. For years he had been Renwick's quiet instrument — the man who turned thirty-one deaths into an accident, who smoothed the fire's rough edges into a narrative suitable for a marble plaque. Elias had seen him once, at the rally on Kettledrum Fields, a small precise man with a notebook, standing at the edge of the crowd while the names were read aloud. He had watched Elias watch him, and there had been something in his face that Elias had thought at the time was fear, and understood later was the beginning of arithmetic.

Reeve had gone home from Kettledrum Fields and done what historians do. He had checked his sources.

And his sources, it emerged over the following fortnight in a series of revelations that fell upon the district like slow thunder, did not agree with each other. The payroll records Renwick had given him showed forty-two hands employed in the carding and weaving sheds on the day of the strike. The coroner's list accounted for eleven bodies. Reeve had noted the discrepancy years ago — of course he had; he was a careful man — and had been told, in terms that left no ambiguity, that the discrepancy was a clerical error and would he please confine himself to the chapters assigned him. He had confined himself. For two years he had confined himself, and the confinement had eaten him from the inside the way silence eats anyone with a conscience, and on the fourteenth of April he walked into the Home Office's temporary commission offices in King Street, Manchester, carrying his private notebooks, and asked to see the Assistant Commissioner personally.

By evening everyone knew. By morning the newspapers knew, and the *Guardian* printed the essential fact on its front page beneath a headline set in letters large enough to read across a street: **HARROW MILL INQUEST REOPENED — HISTORIAN'S NOTEBOOKS SURRENDERED — "THIRTY-ONE NAMES OMITTED," COMMISSION TOLD**.

Elias bought three copies. He took one to Bram, one to Ada, and kept the third, and that night he sat up with it long after the candle had burned down, reading the quoted passages until the words blurred. Reeve had written, in a margin note dated the previous autumn: *The verdict of accidental death rests entirely upon Dr. Pellowe's report, and Dr. Pellowe has attended Sir Aldous Renwick's family since 1819. This is not evidence. This is bookkeeping.* And beneath it, in a different ink, added recently, the ink still sharp on the page: *I built a monument to a lie and called it history. God forgive me. Let my testimony stand where my silence has stood.*

Bram read that passage aloud in Ada's kitchen the next day, slowly, the way she read proofs. When she finished, nobody spoke for a while. Ada Fenn, whose hands had been smashed by soldiers' boots six years ago and who had rebuilt her press anyway, joint by joint, said at last: "A man who changes sides late is worth ten who never had a side at all. But he'll want watching. Men who defect carry the habits of the army they're leaving."

"He's terrified," Bram said.

"Aye," Ada said. "So were we."

* * *

The summons came for Elias on the last day of April.

Not for Bram — the commission, whatever else it wanted, was scrupulous about not appearing to reward sedition, and the woman who had published *The Truth of Harrow Mill* under her own name remained officially a person of interest, her pardon provisional, her forgery charge struck but not forgotten. They summoned Elias because he was respectable. A census taker. A man with a government appointment and a widowed mother's house in Ancoats and nothing in his history but diligence. He was, he realized with a strange inward laugh as he dressed in his good coat, exactly the witness he had spent months pretending not to be.

The commission offices had taken over a merchant's house on King Street, and the clerk who logged him in did so with the same bored efficiency Elias applied to household schedules, and there was a moment of vertigo in recognizing his own trade reflected back at him from the machinery of state. Names, addresses, occupations. The empire ran on lists. He had known that always, but he had never quite known it — never understood that the same dry power that numbered chimneys and looms could, if the numbers were forced to tell the truth, pull down a knight of the realm.

The Assistant Commissioner was a grey, unhurried man named Wexford who listened the way few officials listen: completely. Elias gave his statement in the morning session and returned for questioning in the afternoon, and between the two sessions he sat in the corridor on a bench meant for waiting tradesmen and watched the door open and close on the parade of witnesses the commission had summoned.

Martha Quill came at noon, escorted by a charity matron, her best shawl pinned at the throat with a brooch Bram had given her. She had fought going to London — the children, always the children — but the commission, in a concession that told Elias how seriously they had been made to take the case, had agreed to sit in Manchester. She came out after forty minutes, white-faced, and sat down beside Elias on the bench, and her hands shook until he covered them with his own.

"It's done," she said. "I said it all. The chains on the doors. The foreman with the keys. I said the names I remembered, twelve of them, God forgive me for forgetting the rest." She stared straight ahead at the wainscoting. "My Tom asked me where I was going in my good shawl and I told him to mind the little ones till I came back, and he looked at me like I'd grown another head." Her mouth trembled toward something that was nearly laughter. "Twenty-two years I kept my mouth shut and my children fed. And now a boy of nine minds the baby while I tell a commissioner about the worst day of my life."

"Will you be punished for it?" Elias asked quietly. "Renwick—"

"Renwick," said Martha Quill, with a bitterness so worn smooth it had become almost calm, "can find me whenever he likes. He's always known where I am. That was never the point of my silence. The point was that silence kept bread in the house." She looked at him then. "You want to know the truth of it, Mr. Vane? Silence doesn't keep bread either. Not really. Not in the end. It just makes the hunger quieter."

The old watchman came next. They brought him in a chair, for he could no longer manage the stairs — dying, the doctors said, of the growth in his chest, and past caring what Renwick or anyone else thought of his testimony. His name was Joseph Tredgar, and Elias had taken down his confession in a garret in December while the man coughed blood into a rag and named Renwick's foreman in a voice like gravel. Today he repeated it all, formally, for the record, and when he came out he was wheeled past the bench and stopped, and looked up at Elias with eyes sunk deep in a face gone the colour of tallow.

"They asked me why I waited," Tredgar said. "Why I let them pay me to forget." He worked his mouth. "I told them: because remembering wouldn't bring them back, and forgetting kept my family off the street. And then your pamphlet came out, young man, and I read those thirty-one names, and I found my brother's son among them, and I understood that forgetting hadn't saved anybody. It had only buried them deeper." His hand found Elias's sleeve and gripped it with a strength that surprised them both. "Tell the Ishikawa woman — the sister, the daughter, whoever she is, tell her all of her — that the names she set in type have done more than twenty years of my prayers. Tell her old Tredgar said so, and mean it."

He died eleven days later. His sworn statement survived him, entered in the record, witnessed, sealed. It was, Elias thought, standing bareheaded at the pauper's funeral while drizzle came down over Weaste, a kind of resurrection — the man outliving himself by way of paper and ink. Bram would have understood that better than anyone. Everything she loved lived in type.

* * *

Ada Fenn came home on the sixth of May.

Elias and Bram went together to the New Bailey to fetch her, and waited among the wives and mothers in the visitors' yard while the machinery of the prison produced her, blinking, thinner, her grey hair escaping its pins. The charges against her — conspiracy, sedition, harbouring fugitives — had been withdrawn in the general collapse, the magistrates evidently deciding that prosecuting the printer of a pamphlet whose contents a royal commission had validated would look less like justice than farce. But the decision had taken weeks, and weeks in New Bailey aged a woman like years.

She embraced Bram first, hard, and then Elias, and then stood back and looked at them both with her fierce ruined-hands pragmatism already reasserting itself.

"Well," she said. "Is my press a total loss?"

"Stone cracked," Bram said. "Frame sound. I've been working on it."

"You've been—" Ada's eyebrows rose. "You've been repairing my press in my own cellar without my leave?"

"Somebody had to," Bram said. "You weren't here."

For a moment Ada's face did something complicated — pride and grief and amusement fighting for possession of it — and then she laughed, a short barking laugh, and put her arm through Bram's, and they walked out of the yard together like two women who had known each other all their lives instead of one hard winter.

That evening, in the cellar, with candles lit and the press half-dressed in its new parts, Ada touched the repaired frame the way other people touched relics. Then she said, casually, in the tone of a woman mentioning weather: "There's a job coming, if the shop wants it. The commission will publish its interim findings. Public document. They need it printed fast and printed true, and they'd sooner give it to a Manchester house that knows the district than ship it up from London." She glanced at Bram. "They asked after you by name."

Bram went very still.

"They know what she is," Elias said carefully. "Her situation. It's no secret since the trial."

"They know," Ada agreed. "Wexford's clerk told my solicitor that the Commissioner considers it a point of principle. The document will bear the imprint of the woman who broke the story. If Renwick's friends object, the Commissioner says, they may address their objections to the evidence." She smiled thinly. "Which is rather a great deal of evidence."

Bram stood looking at the press for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was low and even, the compositor's voice, the voice she used when setting a difficult line.

"My father lost everything keeping silent. I lost nearly everything speaking. And now they want to pay the shop for the truth." She shook her head slowly. "It isn't fair, any of it. None of it comes round fair. But it comes round." She looked up. "We'll take the job. And we'll print it clean, every figure, every name — and we'll set it ourselves, no apprentice hands, no borrowed compositors. If this record is going to exist, it will exist in type I can vouch for with my life. I've paid enough to know the difference."

* * *

Renwick's fall, when it finally came, was not dramatic. That was the strangest part.

No mob at his gates, no arrest in the night. Just a Tuesday in late May when the mills he controlled announced shortened hours, and the Wednesday when his bank declined to extend his notes, and the Thursday when the *Guardian* reported that Sir Aldous Renwick had placed his affairs in the hands of trustees pending the outcome of the reopened inquest, and the Friday when the first civil action was filed by the family of a dead weaver whose name — Sarah Mott, seventeen years old — Bram had cut into type herself and could recite from memory.

The foreman who had held the chained doors' keys fled to Ireland before he could be served. Dr. Pellowe, the coroner's tame physician, did not flee; he simply produced, before the commission, a revised account of his original findings, hedged and qualified and drenched in passive constructions, that satisfied no one and convicted him of nothing except cowardice, which was not a chargeable offence. The law moved as law moves: partially, slowly, missing some and catching others, and never once doing the whole of what the grieving wanted.

But it moved.

Elias marked its movement in small ways. The memorial stone the parish had refused for twenty-two years was suddenly, grudgingly, discussed. A subscription opened for the families of the thirty-one and filled within a fortnight, working people putting in coppers along with merchants putting in pounds. Children in the streets chanted fragments of the pamphlet's closing line — *count them, count them, count the dead* — as a skipping rhyme, not knowing or caring where it came from. And one evening walking home through Ancoats, Elias passed the wall of Harrow Mill's ruin itself, the blackened gable that had stood like a warning all these years, and found chalked upon it, in a labourer's hand, fresh and simple: *31*. Nothing else. Just the number, standing where the silence used to be.

He stood before it a long time.

His father had died in there. Twenty-two years ago a man named Thomas Vane had gone through those doors to earn fourteen shillings a week, and the doors had been chained behind him, and the ledger of the world had recorded him as a discrepancy, a clerical error, a rounding-down. Elias had spent his adult life taking the census of other people's households — counting strangers' children, strangers' lodgers, strangers' dead — and had never once been able to count his own father anywhere, because nowhere in the official record did Thomas Vane exist.

But the record was amended now. The commission's interim report listed the thirty-one by name, age, and occupation, reconstructed from wage books and chapel registers and the memories of the poor, and Thomas Vane stood in it at last: *weaver, aged 34, of Ancoats.* Elias had read the entry perhaps a hundred times. He would read it a hundred more. Some hungers, he had learned from Martha Quill, were only quieted, never filled — but even quieted was a mercy beyond what he had expected of this world.

* * *

On the last night of May, the commission's final proof sheets came to the shop, and Bram locked the door and spread them across the composing-room table under lamplight, and she and Elias and Ada read the whole document aloud, taking turns, the way the strikers had once read the pamphlet across Kettledrum Fields.

It was dry prose, committee prose, full of *it having been represented* and *the evidence tends to indicate*, and yet Elias had never heard language do anything so beautiful. Every clause was a door swinging open. Every finding was a chain struck off. When they reached the passage recommending that the verdict of accidental death be quashed and replaced with a finding of unlawful killing by persons unknown, Ada read it twice, and then set the sheets down very carefully, and pressed both her ruined hands flat against the table, and said nothing at all for a full minute, and none of them needed her to.

Afterward, Bram stood at the case of memorial type she had been cutting — the monument letters, the thirty-one names, each one shaped by her own graver stroke for the stone that would rise where the mill had burned. She lifted out one piece and handed it to Elias without explanation.

He turned it in the light. It was a capital V.

"Vane," she said. "Thomas. Weaver, aged thirty-four. I cut him first, weeks ago, before any of this. I wanted him to be ready." Her eyes were bright and steady. "He'll be on the stone, Elias. Your father. Cut in metal that will stamp his name into granite, and granite outlasts everything except memory, and now the memory's written down too, so it can't be lost even if the stone cracks."

Elias held the piece of type and could not speak. Twenty-two years of census forms and doorsteps and other people's families, and here in his palm was his father, rendered in lead the size of a thumbnail, heavier than it looked, the way the dead are heavier than they look.

"There's one thing left," Bram said softly, when he could breathe again. "One loose thread, and it isn't Renwick's and it isn't the commission's. It's ours." She reached beneath the proof sheets and drew out a folded paper — old, creased soft with handling, the apprentice's stolen copy of the witness statements, recovered from Renwick's agent during the trial and returned to her by the magistrate with a caution she had accepted in silence. "This copy exists. The agent's deposition says there was a buyer. Someone paid for these pages before the watchman died — someone in this district who wanted the record silenced even after the pamphlet flew. Renwick's fallen, but money like that doesn't fall with him."

Ada frowned. "The magistrate closed that inquiry."

"The magistrate closed it," Bram agreed. "I didn't." She set the paper on the table between the three of them, in the lamplight, and her jaw set in the way Elias had learned meant a forme locked and ready. "Thirty-one names are counted. Now I want to know who tried to make it thirty-one plus three — me, Elias, and the truth. Because a record that's only half amended is a record still lying."

Outside, rain began on the cellar grate, and somewhere above in the waking district a bell rang the hour, and Bram Ishikawa reached for the lamp to turn it brighter.
