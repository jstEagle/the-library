## 22. Setting the Names

The burin sang.

Bram had cut type for twenty years — letters small enough to hide a lie inside, letters bold enough to sell a mill owner's triumphs — but she had never cut a face like this one. It had no name in the specimen books. She was calling it *Remembrance*, and it would exist for exactly one purpose: to carry the names of thirty-one people out of the dark and into stone, where no agent of Sir Aldous Renwick could ever buy them back into silence.

She worked at the long bench by the window of the new shop on Tib Street, where the morning light came in clean off the rooftops instead of filtering through the soot-haze of the mill district. The light mattered. Cutting a letter was an act of seeing, and Bram Ishikawa had spent half her life learning what she could afford not to see. Now she looked at everything.

The steel blank sat clamped before her, and under her hands the letter *H* emerged — Harrow's first initial, the first letter of the first name on the list: **HANNAH BREARLEY**, aged nineteen. Bram had chosen Hannah deliberately. The girl had been the youngest of the thirty-one, or nearly; the census records Elias had recovered gave ages for only twenty-six of them, and for five the paper simply said *unknown*. Bram had set those five as *UNKNOWN* in smaller type beneath their names, because the truth included its own gaps, and a monument that pretended otherwise would be just another kind of lie.

The burin lifted curls of steel like pale shavings of bone. Her father had taught her this — Josiah Ishikawa, who had come from Nagasaki by way of Amsterdam with a case of punches and a name nobody in Manchester could pronounce, who had taught his daughter that a lettercutter's first duty was to the letter itself, and never to the man paying for it.

He had broken that rule exactly once. She knew that now. Ada Fenn had told her, in the cellar on Turner Lane with the press still warm: how Josiah had stood in Ada's shop in the winter of 1821, the memorial broadsheet already composed, thirty-one names locked in a forme, and how he had stood there a long time and then begun to break it apart, letter by letter, saying only, *I have a child.* He had paid for the paper to be destroyed instead — that was the gap in the job books, the entry that stopped mid-line — and he had carried the knowledge of it like a stone in his chest until the day he died, and perhaps after.

Bram did not forgive him. Forgiveness was too simple a word for what she felt when she thought of her father now. But she understood him, and understanding had turned out to be heavier than forgiveness, the way lead was heavier than steel. She set her grief into the letters instead. It made a clean face. Grief, properly directed, always did.

"You've gone past the crossbar," said a voice behind her. "Twice."

Martha Quill leaned against the doorframe, arms folded, a basket of washing on her hip. A year ago Martha had sold matches in the alley behind Harrow Row and refused to speak for the sake of her children. Now she sold nothing and worked three days a week keeping the accounts of the Ishikawa print works, because Bram had decided that if a woman's testimony was going to cost her everything, someone ought to pay her for it.

"I'm leaving it proud," Bram said. "It'll stand better against weather."

"It'll stand," Martha agreed, "because you'll stand over it with a hammer if it doesn't." She set down the basket and came to look at the punch, tilting her head the way she did when she was about to say something she'd been carrying all morning. "There's a woman at the door. Says she's from the Home Office. Says her name is Miss Crowther, and she's come about the amended record."

Bram's hand stilled on the burin.

"Show her in," she said. "And put the kettle on. Government women drink tea whether they want it or not. It makes them slower to leave."

* * *

Miss Crowther was younger than Bram had expected, with ink-stained fingers and the harried look of someone sent north with insufficient instructions. She laid a leather folder on the counter of the shop — the new counter, oak, unscarred — and opened it with the reverence of a person handling something she half-disbelieved.

"The reopened inquest," she said. "Final findings. The Commissioner asked me to bring you the copy personally, since—" she hesitated, then went on, "—since your evidence formed the greater part of it. Thirty-one deaths by unlawful confinement. The verdict of 1821 is struck. There will be a formal amendment entered in the parish registers, and—" she turned a page, and Bram saw the list, the whole list, in government type, ugly government type, "—and the names are to be recorded as they appear here."

Bram read down the page. Hannah Brearley. Thomas Ogden. Mary-Anne Fitchett. Samuel and Ruth Clegg, husband and wife, found together by the east door. On and on, thirty-one lines, each one a life reduced to a name and an age and, for five of them, that terrible word. She had read the list a hundred times. She had set it a hundred times. But reading it in the Home Office's own hand, with the crown's seal at the bottom, was different. It was like watching a door open onto a room everyone had told her was bricked up.

"They've got the ages wrong on two of them," Bram heard herself say. "Ogden was fifty-three, not fifty-one. And Fitchett — Mary-Anne Fitchett was born in March of 1803; I have the baptism record. She was eighteen, not twenty-two."

Miss Crowther blinked. "You have the baptism record?"

"In the second drawer there, under the specimen books." Bram fetched it herself, a curling sheet of parish vellum, and laid it beside the folder. "If the record is to be amended, let it be amended correctly. The dead can't correct it themselves."

For a moment Miss Crowther said nothing. Then she took out a pencil and wrote in the margin, and Bram saw that her hands were not quite steady. "They said in London you'd be difficult," the young woman said. "They said you were — well. That you'd made trouble."

"I did make trouble," Bram said. "It's the family trade. We make letters, and letters are trouble waiting to happen."

When Miss Crowther had gone — pausing at the door to look back at the composing frames, the press, the racks of newly cast type, with the expression of someone memorizing a place she suspected she would dream about — Martha appeared with two cups of tea and drank them both.

"A year ago I'd have hidden in the cellar if a government woman knocked," Martha said. "Now you're correcting her arithmetic."

"A year ago we had something to lose by speaking," Bram said. "Now we've something to lose by silence. It changes the sums entirely."

* * *

In the afternoon the apprentices came up from the casting room, and Bram called them to order around the big table with the same gravity her father had once used, minus the fear. There were four of them now. Tom Pritchard, sixteen, all elbows, whose father was a striker from '42 and whose hands had finally learned patience. Nell Barrow, who had been thrown out of two other shops for asking questions and whom Bram had hired specifically for that reason. Old Sam Ollerenshaw, sixty-one, who had lost two fingers at Harrow Mill years before the fire and who had come to Bram's door the week the trial ended, cap in hand, saying he'd sooner die at a compositor's frame than live another day idle — and who was teaching the young ones the old speed-work faster than any master could.

And at the end of the bench, smallest and most watchful, sat Grace Quill.

Martha's eldest was fourteen and had her mother's stillness and none of her mother's fear. She had arrived the week after the monument commission was announced, standing in the doorway with her chin up and her mother's hand on her shoulder, and she had said, in the flat clear voice she used for recitations at the Sunday school, *"I want to learn the letters. Mam says yours are honest ones."*

Bram had taken her on without a word, and had regretted it for exactly one afternoon, when Grace had caught her first finger in the caster and hadn't cried — hadn't made a sound — and Bram had recognized in that silence her own twenty-year-old self, the girl who had learned that quiet was survival. She had sat the girl down then and there, wrapped the finger, and told her: *In this shop you may cry, shout, sing, swear, or ask questions. The one thing you may not do is disappear.*

Grace had looked at her with Martha's level grey eyes and said, "Can I still get on with the work?"

"Yes," Bram had said, laughing despite herself. "That above all."

Now Bram set the proof sheet on the table before them — the full monument inscription, pulled that morning, the ink still smelling of the good lampblack. Thirty-one names in the new face, each one cut by her own hand over eleven months, each letter drawn from the model of the old Roman capitals but widened at the waist, deepened in the counters, given weight — letters meant to be read by a person standing in rain, in wind, in grief. Below the names, the single line of text:

**THEY WERE LOCKED IN AND THE DOORS WERE CHAINED AND THEY DIED AT THEIR WORK. REMEMBER THEM BY NAME.**

No ornament. No apology. No passive construction. She had fought Renwick's committee for six weeks over that sentence — they had wanted *regrettable circumstances attending the dispute of 1821*, and she had wanted the truth in words plain enough to be shouted, and in the end the committee had blinked, because the alternative was no monument at all and the city wanted its monument.

"Read it through," Bram told the apprentices. "Letter by letter. A monument is forever, and forever does not accept errata slips."

They bent over the proof. Sam read aloud in his cracked mill-broken voice, and the names filled the shop — filled it the way the pamphlet had filled Kettledrum Fields a year ago, passed hand to hand, read aloud by strangers while the dragoons charged and Bram had stood on the platform with her heart hammering and her voice gone and the crowd's voices carrying the names further than any single throat could have done.

Grace reached the last line and stopped. "Miss Ishikawa. The word *chained*. The H looks different from the others."

Everyone looked. Bram looked longest.

"It's the second one I cut," she said. "The first cracked in the hardening. This one has a flaw at the left serif — see, a hairline. I kept it because—" she paused, and decided, as she had decided once before in her life, to tell the truth even when a smooth lie lay ready to hand. "Because my father cut a flawed letter once and it saved his life. A flaw means a human hand was here. Machines don't make mistakes. Only people do, and only people can mend them."

Grace wrote that down in her practice book. Bram watched her write it and thought: *there, that one will carry it on.*

* * *

Evening came early that time of year. Bram dismissed the apprentices at dusk, and when the last of them — Tom, who lingered always, hungry for the trade the way she had once been hungry for safety — had gone out into the lamplit street, she climbed the stairs to the flat above the shop.

Elias Vane was there, as he was most evenings now, sitting by the window with the last grey light and a book he wasn't reading. He had resigned from the census office in the spring — handed in his ledger, his portfolio, his little tin box of verification stamps, and walked out with nothing but his father's Bible and his notebooks, which were worth more than everything else combined. He taught writing now at the mechanics' institute three evenings a week, to men and women who had been told all their lives that letters belonged to other people. His students adored him. He remained faintly bewildered by this.

"The committee approved the inscription today," Bram said, hanging her coat. "All of it. Even the sentence."

"I heard. Martha told the whole institute between verses of the hymn." He set the book aside. "You're done, then. Eleven months of cutting, and tomorrow they hoist the first stones."

"Tomorrow they hoist the stones. The bronze comes next month." Bram sat across from him, and for a while neither spoke, because they had both earned the right to silences that meant nothing terrible. Outside, Manchester murmured its evening murmur — carts, gulls off the canal, a church bell, the distant hoot of a mill whistle marking hours for workers in mills that, since the strike, since the inquiry, since the Reform Act's slow grind, had begun — only begun — to post their doors unlocked.

"My father broke the forme," Bram said at last. "Ada told me. He broke it himself, letter by letter, because of me."

Elias waited. He was good at waiting; a census taker learned it, and a man hunting a massacre buried for twenty years had learned it better.

"I used to think silence was a thing done to us," she said. "Done to the dead, done to me, done to every woman who ever passed as something she wasn't. Something done *by* powerful men, and it was, it was that. But it was also done by frightened people, one letter at a time. My father. Me. Even Ada, for years — she printed the memorial and then hid it and said nothing, and hiding it was almost as bad as breaking it." She looked at her hands, scarred, steady, stained with ink that no amount of pumice would ever fully lift. "Twenty years, Elias. Twenty years I kept the shop alive by being my brother. I told myself it was survival, and it was. But every year I didn't speak, the dead got quieter. You can't keep a grave safe by standing on it."

"No," Elias said quietly. "You can't."

"And now?" She gestured vaguely downward, toward the shop, the type, the city beyond. "Tomorrow they raise the stone. Next month the bronze goes up with my face's letters on it, and my name in the corner — *cut by B. ISHIKAWA, MANCHESTER*, not Bram, not Benjamin, not brother to anyone. Just mine. And I find I don't know what to do with a life that isn't organized around hiding. I've been Bram-the-brother so long that Bram-myself feels like type that hasn't been proofed yet. I keep waiting for the correction."

Elias rose and came to sit beside her, and took her hand, the scarred one, and turned it over and looked at it the way he looked at documents — thoroughly, without flinching.

"When I verified households," he said, "there was a question on the form I hated. *Condition as to education.* As though a person were a ledger line. But I used to look at the answers people gave. Most lied, of course — everyone claimed more schooling than they had, or less, whichever seemed safer. Except once, an old woman in Ancoats, a washerwoman. She said: *I can read anything and I write my name and that is condition enough.*" He smiled. "I've thought of her every day since the fields. Your condition is written, Bram. It's in bronze coming next month. Nobody amends it now."

Bram laughed — a real laugh, short and surprised out of her — and leaned her head against his shoulder, and below them the city went on making its noise, and somewhere in the shop beneath the floorboards thirty-one names waited in steel and lead for the morning, patient as the dead, certain as print.

* * *

They hoisted the stones in a thin cold drizzle that nobody minded.

Half of Manchester seemed to have come anyway. The square before the old Harrow gate — the gate itself long since torn out, the mill a ruin awaiting demolition — was thick with people: millworkers with their caps off, Chartist men grown grey since Kettledrum Fields, women with children on their shoulders, the mayor looking uncomfortable beside the bishop looking uncomfortable beside Ada Fenn looking magnificent in a green dress and absolutely no discomfort at all. Sam Ollerenshaw stood near the front holding the framed first proof like a relic. Martha held Grace's hand very tightly.

Bram stood at the edge of the platform they'd built for the ceremony, in a plain black coat, and discovered that she could not remember a single word of the speech in her pocket. It hardly mattered. The rector read the lesson; the choir sang something old; the crane groaned; the first stone rose swaying against the grey sky, swung, settled, and met its bed with a sound like a held breath released.

Then the bronze letters went up over the following weeks, and Bram was there every day, directing the fixers, checking each name against her proof as it was bolted home. On the last afternoon of the work, alone in the fading light, she stood before the finished wall and read it top to bottom, slowly, touching nothing.

Thirty-one names. Five marked *age unknown*, honestly. And beneath them, in letters cut to be read in rain and wind and grief, the sentence the committee had fought and lost:

*They were locked in and the doors were chained and they died at their work. Remember them by name.*

A year ago these names existed nowhere. They had lived in the memory of a match-seller and a dying watchman and a file in a coroner's office marked *accidental*, and they had been one bad winter away from vanishing forever, smoothed over by a gentleman historian's commemorative volume, gone as completely as if they had never drawn breath. Now they were in bronze, in the parish register, in three hundred surviving copies of a seditious pamphlet that schoolteachers would one day assign and apologize for, and in the hands of a fourteen-year-old girl downstairs who was, at this very moment, probably setting her first independent line of type and getting the spacing wrong and not crying about it.

Bram took out her knife and, in the soft mortar at the base of the wall, where no inspector would ever look, cut one small private mark — a single letter, her own initial, the way cutters had signed work since Gutenberg. Not hidden. Not proclaimed. Simply present, the way a person is present.

Then she went down the hill toward the lights of Tib Street, where the windows of the Ishikawa Print Works glowed yellow against the dark, and the press waited, and tomorrow there would be more names to set — living ones, this time, names of apprentices and customers and a whole unruly city learning, letter by letter, to speak.
