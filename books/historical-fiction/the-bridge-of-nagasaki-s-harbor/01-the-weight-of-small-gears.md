## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

There are two ways to carry a trade. The first is the way the priests carry theirs — in the mouth, loudly, with much gesturing toward heaven. The second is the way my father taught me: in a leather roll that fits inside a coat pocket and weighs almost nothing at all.

I have always preferred the second. A man shouting about his own importance can be ignored. A woman with a jeweler's screwdriver and a steady hand cannot be dismissed so easily — though Nagasaki's harbor authorities were going to spend a remarkable amount of energy trying.

The morning I was summoned to the observatory, I carried eleven tools, one spare pair of tweezers, a loupe I had inherited from a dead man whose name I never learned, and a lunch of rice balls wrapped in bamboo leaf that my father had made without being asked. That last item should have been my first warning. Tomás Sandoval did not make lunches. Tomás Sandoval made excuses, ledgers, and lately — money. Lunches were beneath him now, or above him; it was difficult to tell which direction his new prosperity ran.

"You'll want to eat," he'd said, pressing the bundle into my hands at the door. "Observatory work is slow work."

"Slow work pays by the day," I said. "We've had few enough of those."

He smiled at me the way a man smiles when he is thinking about something else entirely, and I went down the hill toward the harbor carrying my tools and my father's strange good mood, both of them heavier than they looked.

* * *

The harbor observatory sits on its stone finger like an old man who has been listening so long he has forgotten how to speak. From its gallery you can see everything worth seeing in Nagasaki: the masts, the cranes, the gray warships that had begun appearing more often that year, sitting in the outer roads like guests who arrive early and say nothing. Below, the water does what water has always done here — carries things in, carries things out, remembers nothing officially.

The master clock lived three flights up, in a room that smelled of brass polish and cold stone. It was a French piece, older than the observatory itself, shipped around the Cape in some previous century by men who clearly believed a clock ought to be built like a cathedral: heavy, ornate, and impossible to move once installed. Its pendulum hung from a knife-edge suspension that had gone dull as a butter knife, and somewhere in its train a tooth was skipping, which meant the harbor's every other clock — the customs house, the ferry office, the warehouse gates — was drifting out of true by minutes a week.

Minutes matter in a harbor. I did not yet know how much.

The director met me at the door. He was a small round man named Okada who wore his authority the way some men wear a borrowed coat — correctly, but without conviction.

"Sandoval," he said, reading from a paper rather than looking at me. "You are the daughter of Tomás Sandoval."

"I am the apprentice of Tomás Sandoval," I said. "The daughter part came first, but it's less relevant to clocks."

Something flickered across his face — not quite a smile, not quite disapproval. "Your father speaks highly of your hands."

"My father speaks highly of anyone holding a wrench," I said. "Where is she?"

"She?"

"The clock. You people insist on calling ships and engines *she*. It seems rude to exclude the only thing in this building that actually works."

That earned me half a smile before he remembered himself and led me up the stairs. I noted, as we climbed, that he did not ask me questions about my father beyond the ones already written on his paper. Men who don't ask questions are usually men who already have answers, and men who already have answers are either satisfied customers or something else entirely. At nineteen I filed this observation under *probably nothing*, which is where I filed most of the observations that would later ruin me.

* * *

A master clock is honest in a way people are not. Open its case and everything it has ever done is written there: the wear patterns on the pivots, the dust in the oil, the places where someone's impatient thumb left a smear forty years ago. You cannot lie to a clockmaker standing in front of an open movement. You can only stand there while the mechanism testifies against you.

This one testified fluently.

"Someone's been in here," I said, after an hour. "Recently."

Okada, who had been pretending to read reports at a desk in the corner, looked up too quickly. "Maintenance staff."

"Maintenance staff wound it and let it be. Someone else opened the case." I tilted the lamp. "See here — fresh scratches on the case screws, wrong-sized driver, someone in a hurry. And this." I pointed to the count wheel, where a tiny wedge of paper had been jammed between two teeth, removed badly, leaving fibers behind. "Paper doesn't belong in a clock. Paper in a clock is either carelessness or intention, and the scratches say whoever did it wasn't careless."

"What kind of intention?" Okada asked. His voice had gone carefully flat, the way voices do when they are trying very hard to sound bored.

"That's what I intend to find out," I said. "It's why you're paying me by the day instead of by the guess."

He went back to his reports. I went back into the clock.

Here is what nobody tells you about repair work: it is mostly listening. The eye finds the obvious fault — the broken tooth, the dried oil — but the ear finds the truth underneath. So I stopped talking, stopped thinking about my father's rice balls and our rent and the particular quality of silence that had settled over our dinner table these past months, and I listened.

The escapement ticked. The train turned. And deep in the going train, where the third wheel should have meshed clean with the fourth, there was a whisper. Not a skip — a whisper. Something small, riding where nothing should ride.

I pulled the fourth wheel.

And there, wedged flat against the hub where no casual inspection would ever find it, sealed in wax and wrapped in oilcloth no bigger than a postage stamp, was a letter.

* * *

I want to tell you that I hesitated. That some instinct of self-preservation rose in me like a hand and said *Mirabel, put it back, close the case, bill the director for a cleaned escapement and go home.* But honesty compels me to admit the truth, which is that my hands were moving before my mind caught up. Curiosity in a clockmaker is not a virtue. It is the entire engine. We open things because they are closed, and we keep opening until either the thing works or we understand why it doesn't. There is no gear in us marked *stop*.

I unwrapped it at the bench, under the lamp, with Okada twenty feet away performing his imitation of a man reading.

The envelope was cheap paper, the kind sold in bundles at the stationer near the fish market. The seal was plain wax, pressed with nothing — no crest, no initial, which is its own kind of signature. People who press crests into wax want the seal to testify. People who press nothing want deniability.

Inside, in a cramped commercial hand:

*...the Sandoval accounts continue to clear through the third warehouse as arranged. T.S. has proven reliable, more than can be said for the brothers at Dejima, who talk. If the naval audit comes in spring, the manifest corrections must be complete by February. V. insists the timing mechanisms be kept in perfect order — he says, and I quote, "a harbor is only as honest as its clocks." Payment as before.*

*Burn this.*

I read it twice. Then a third time, because the words refused to arrange themselves into anything I could survive.

*T.S.* My father was Tomás Sandoval. There were perhaps four other families in Nagasaki with that surname, and none of them, as far as I knew, owned anything resembling a warehouse account. *V.* meant nothing to me yet. It would come to mean everything, but that morning it was just a letter wearing another man's initial like a mask.

What I understood, even then, even with my heart doing something arrhythmic and unprofessional against my ribs, was the last line. *A harbor is only as honest as its clocks.* I was kneeling inside the harbor's clock. I was the person paid to keep it in perfect order. Whoever wrote this letter had hidden it in the one place in Nagasaki where precision was sacred and interference was unthinkable — and I had found it precisely because I was good at my work.

My competence had just become the most dangerous thing about me.

* * *

"Is there a problem, Sandoval?"

I looked down at my hands. They had, without any instruction from me, closed around the letter and moved it — moved it *somewhere*, the somewhere being the hollow of my rolled sleeve, against my forearm, where the skin is warmest and the fabric hangs loosest. I had not decided to hide it. My body had decided for me, the way a body flinches from a spark.

"No problem," I said. "Escapement needs re-adjusting. The suspension's worn. I'll need tomorrow as well."

"The director's office authorized—"

"Two days," I said. "Or you can find someone faster, and they'll fix the symptom and leave the disease, and in six weeks you'll be paying by the day again anyway."

He considered this. Money, as always, argued my side better than I could. "Two days," he agreed, and gathered his papers, and left me alone with the clock and the letter burning a small rectangle of heat against my arm.

I finished the escapement properly. I want that on the record, wherever records are kept for people like me: whatever else happened afterward, whatever names got attached to mine, the escapement was adjusted to within a tenth of a millimeter and the master clock kept time to two seconds a week, which for its age was a kind of miracle. I did not steal the day's wage. I did not cut corners. Whatever else Mirabel Sandoval became that winter, she remained, first and always, a craftsman.

But when I packed my tools that evening, I did not mention the paper fibers to Okada. I did not report the tampering. I walked out of the observatory with a sealed letter naming my family hidden against my skin, and I descended the stone stairs past the gallery windows where the harbor lay below in the blue-gray light — the masts, the cranes, the warehouses ranked along the quay like patient animals — and for the first time in my life, Nagasaki's beautiful orderliness looked to me less like order and more like arrangement.

Everything in its place. Everything accounted for. Everything exactly where somebody wanted it.

* * *

My father was home early. That was the second warning, and this time I received it properly.

Tomás Sandoval sat at our kitchen table under the lamp with a cup of tea he wasn't drinking, and when I came through the door he looked up with an expression I had never seen on him before — or rather, an expression I had seen, but only on men waiting for news they dread. He smoothed it away so quickly that a slower observer might have missed it. I am not a slow observer. I had spent the afternoon learning that my whole life was an arrangement.

"You're late," he said.

"The clock was worse than advertised." I set my tool roll on the shelf, letter still against my arm, feeling it there like a coal. "French movement. Beautiful bones. Someone's been meddling in her."

"Meddling," he repeated. The word landed strangely, as if it had been dropped from a height.

"Tampered with. Hidden things." I watched his face across the lamplight, and I did the arithmetic I had been refusing all day: the new coat hanging by the door, wool, good weave, the first new coat in six years. The debt collector Mr. Ferreira who had stopped coming by in autumn. The mother's medical bills, paid off in installments that had suddenly, mysteriously, stopped arriving as demands and started arriving as receipts. My father's hands, which had begun to look rested.

"How was the director?" he asked. "Okada. Difficult man."

"He didn't ask many questions."

My father nodded slowly, and there it was again, that flicker — relief, I would call it later, though that night I only registered it as a wrongness, a note played off-key in a song I knew by heart. "They never do," he said, "when the work is good," and he smiled at me, and the smile was warm, and it was real, and it was the smile of a man who believed his daughter knew nothing, and I stood there in our kitchen holding a letter that proved otherwise against my own skin, and said nothing.

Emilio burst in then, fourteen years old and smelling of the wharf, full of a story about a freighter and a crane operator and a rope that nearly took someone's arm off, and the moment broke apart into ordinary noise, dinner, dishes, my brother's laughter, the lamp, the rain starting against the shutters. Ordinary life, which I had always thought of as the thing that happened underneath history. That night I understood for the first time that it can also be the lid placed over it.

I slept badly. In the morning I rose before my father, took the letter from my sleeve, and hid it in the one place I trusted absolutely: inside the movement of a customer's mantel clock awaiting repair, wedged where only a Sandoval would ever think to look.

Then I went back up the hill to finish the master clock, keeping time for a harbor that was lying to everyone.

I told myself I had three days to decide what to do.

The harbor gave me two.
