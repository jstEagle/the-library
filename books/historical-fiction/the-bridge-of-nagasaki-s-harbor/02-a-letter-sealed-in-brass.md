## 2. A Letter Sealed in Brass

The master clock of the Nagasaki harbor observatory had been running four minutes slow for eleven days, and everyone in authority had responded to this fact in the way people in authority respond to anything: by holding meetings about it.

I learned this from Director Ishikawa himself, who met me at the observatory door on the second morning with the expression of a man who had been asked too many questions at dinner parties. He was small and round and wore his uniform like an apology, and he looked at my toolbox the way other men looked at a woman's ankles.

"You are the Sandoval girl," he said.

"I'm the Sandoval clockmaker," I said. "The girl part is temporary."

He did not laugh. Directors of observatories, I would come to understand, are men who have been laughed at precisely enough in their lives and have decided against further installments. He led me up the spiral stair to the clock room, talking the whole way about the importance of the instrument — how the harbor pilots took their bearings from it, how the ferry schedules hung off its pendulum like laundry off a line, how the whole city's tidy agreement about what time it was began in this one room at the top of this one hill. He talked as though he were defending it. As though I had come to accuse it of something.

The clock itself stood taller than my father, a black walnut case with a brass face and a pendulum that swung with the patient arrogance of something that knows it is right even when it is wrong. Four minutes wrong, but right about it. There is a word for that kind of confidence. I have met many men who have it.

"Eleven days," Ishikawa said. "Two men have looked at it already."

"And they found?"

"Dust."

I set down my toolbox and opened it, and I confess I did it slowly, because there is a particular pleasure in letting your tools be seen by someone who does not understand them. My father taught me that a clockmaker's reputation lives or dies in the first thirty seconds, when the client watches you choose your instruments. Choose carelessly and they doubt you forever. Choose with ceremony and they will forgive you almost anything afterward — including, as it turned out, a great deal more than lateness.

"The escapement, most likely," I said. "Or the pendulum's lost weight. Or someone has been winding it with affection instead of technique." I glanced at him. "Who winds it?"

"I do. Every morning at six."

"Show me your hands."

He showed me his hands. They were director's hands, soft, and they told me nothing except that he had not been doing this long, and I decided the fault was probably in the movement itself and asked him to leave me alone with it.

He hesitated at the door. "You'll tell me if you find anything unusual."

"Directors always say that," I said. "And then they're disappointed by what counts as unusual. A dead spider in the barrel arbor. A coin someone dropped in 1909. Clocks are where cities keep their secrets, Director. Most of them are boring."

He gave me a look that suggested he hoped I was boring, and went away, and I turned to the great black case and got to work.

* * *

There is a silence inside a stopped clock that no other silence resembles. When the mechanism runs, even badly, the room has a pulse. When it stops, the quiet comes off the case like cold off stone. I opened the trunk door and put my ear against the walnut and listened to that deadness for a while the way you'd listen at the chest of a sick animal, and then I began.

Four minutes in eleven days is a strange failure. A worn pivot steals time gradually, a few seconds a week, so gently nobody notices until the ferry captains start swearing. But four minutes arriving all at once, or in lurches — that means something shifted. Something moved that shouldn't move. I checked the escapement first, because it's always the escapement, and it was clean. I checked the pendulum suspension and found it sound. I checked the hands, the dial work, the strike train. All healthy. All lying.

Which left the going train, and beneath the going train, the weight channels, and beneath those, the place where two earlier repairmen had evidently decided their curiosity ended.

I want to be honest about what I felt at that moment, because honesty is the one material I've never learned to work cleanly. I felt delight. Pure, professional, greedy delight. Three days into the most important commission of my young career, I had found the thing two men before me had missed, and I remember thinking — I remember this exactly — *Papa will be proud. This is worth telling at dinner.* I was twenty years old and I still measured my days by whether they contained something my father would raise his eyebrows at. It would be another week before I understood what his raised eyebrows were worth.

The false panel sat behind the third wheel's mounting plate, a rectangle of brass cut to fit flush against the frame, painted over in the same dull green as everything else. It was good work. Not clockmaker's work — clockmakers don't hide things, we display them, we sign them, we put our names in enamel on the dial for strangers to admire two hundred years later. No, this was carpenter's work, or clerk's work, the work of a man who needed a thing hidden rather than made. But whoever did it had known just enough about movements to pick a spot only another repairman would reach, and to cut it so the plate above it looked undisturbed.

I worked the panel loose with a case knife and a patience I did not feel, and it came away with a small sucking reluctance, and behind it, wedged upright in the cavity between the frame members, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp —

A letter.

Sealed. Red wax, unbroken, pressed with a seal I didn't recognize: some kind of anchor device, official-looking, the kind of stamp that gets borrowed from a desk drawer by men who want a document to seem more lawful than it is. The paper was good stock, heavier than the harbor office uses. Someone had wanted this letter to survive. Someone had wanted it found eventually — by the right person, at the right moment, in the right hands. Letters sealed into clock cases are never accidents. Clocks don't swallow things. People feed them.

I sat back on my heels with the oilcloth bundle in my lap and told myself reasonable things. It's correspondence between officials. It's maintenance records. It's money — harbors are full of bribes tucked into instruments, every apprentice hears the stories. It has nothing to do with you, Mirabel Sandoval, you are a girl with a screwdriver and four minutes to recover, and your job is the escapement, not the secrets.

But my hands already knew. Hands know before heads do; any craftsman will tell you that. And my hands were shaking in the particular way they shake before something irreversible, the same tremor I'd felt at fourteen the first time I broke a customer's balance spring and had to decide in one breath whether to confess or lie.

I opened it.

* * *

I won't reproduce the whole of it here. Some documents you carry so long they wear smooth in your memory like a river stone, and yet putting them down on paper feels like handing them to a stranger, and I have handed enough to strangers. But the parts that mattered, the parts that reached out of the page and took me by the throat, were these:

It was dated fourteen months earlier. It was written in a careful, educated hand, the hand of a man composing rather than corresponding — a draft, or a copy of a copy, meant to survive its original. It named a route. Small coastal freighters, running goods through Nagasaki harbor under manifests that did not match their holds, timed against patrol rotations with a precision the writer clearly admired, because he used the word *precision* twice. It named sums. It named three ships' names, one of which I knew — I'd seen it moored past the breakwater a hundred times, rust-orange, unremarkable, the kind of vessel your eye slides off like water off wax. And in the fourth paragraph, without flourish, without apparent malice, the way you'd list an ingredient:

*the timing arrangements handled through the shop of T. Sandoval, clockmaker, whose discretion may be relied upon.*

My father's initial. Our street. Our shop, with its crooked doorbell and its smell of oil and cedar, sitting in a stranger's sentence like a coin dropped in a collection plate.

I read the paragraph four times. Each time I looked for a loophole — a different Sandoval, a different city, a coincidence of initials so large it could shelter a family. Nagasaki has other Sandovals; there are six families of us in the district registry, mostly fishermen, none of whom fix clocks. The letter didn't say clockmaker the second time. It said *the shop on —— street,* and there is one shop on our street that repairs timepieces, and I have swept its floor since I was seven, and last autumn my father bought a new coat, and in winter my mother's debts were suddenly, quietly, impossibly paid down, and I remembered standing in the kitchen asking him how, and watching him look at the window instead of at me while he said *the business is good this year, little one,* and I had wanted so badly for it to be true that I had let myself believe a sentence with no load-bearing walls.

That's the thing about wanting to believe. You become a bad engineer of your own life. You approve the plans because you like the drawing.

I folded the letter along its original creases. My fingers were very steady now, steadier than they'd been all morning, the way they go steady in emergencies — my father calls it the Sandoval calm and claims it as an inheritance alongside the tools. Outside the clock room's window the harbor lay below in the morning light, ferries crossing on their exact schedules, cranes swinging on theirs, the whole glittering machine of the city keeping time off the pendulum ten feet from where I knelt with contraband in my hands. Everything orderly. Everything correct to within four minutes. And underneath the brass floor of it, this.

I thought about carrying it straight to Director Ishikawa. I honestly did. I rehearsed it standing there: *Director, I found this behind the third wheel, I opened it before thinking, I'm sorry, but you should see paragraph four.* And in my rehearsal he took it gravely and thanked me and the machinery of officialdom ground forward and justice happened to somebody else, somewhere else, at a comfortable distance.

Then I thought about who seals letters into clocks. Not honest men. Honest men file things; filing cabinets exist precisely so that honest men don't have to hide paper inside instruments. Whoever wrote this had intended it for someone — or against someone — and had chosen a hiding place that required a clockmaker's hands to reach. Which meant the next pair of hands expected in that cavity belonged to a professional. To someone like me. Possibly, when this letter finally surfaced, the story would need a clockmaker in it, and I already knew which shop on which street the story preferred.

I am not brave. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone, because the accounts written later will make it sound like courage, and it wasn't. What I did next was done by a frightened apprentice whose body chose survival faster than her conscience could convene a meeting. I rewrapped the letter in its oilcloth. I replaced the brass panel, seated it flush, and dressed the seam with dust from the case bottom so it read untouched. Then I closed the trunk, climbed down from the stool, and finished repairing the escapement — which, for the record, needed a single drop of oil and a lightening of the pendulum, forty minutes of ordinary work — and when Director Ishikawa returned at noon to find the great clock ticking true to the second, he shook my hand and praised my father's teaching and never once asked what I had found.

I said, "Dust, Director. Mostly."

He beamed. Men believe what flatters their boredom.

* * *

I walked home the long way, down through the terraced streets where the foreign traders' houses sit with their backs to the sea, past the fish market where the ice was melting silver on the cobbles, and I carried the letter against my ribs inside my jacket like a second heartbeat. Every policeman I passed, and Nagasaki in that season had policemen the way a dog has fleas, I catalogued: height, gait, whether he looked at me, whether he looked at my jacket. None did. The city went about its morning with its sleeves buttoned and its ledgers balanced, and I understood for the first time that orderliness is not innocence. Orderliness can be a costume rot wears to church.

At the top of our street I stopped. Through the shop window I could see my father at the bench, hunched over a customer's watch, his loupe in his eye, his hands doing the small sure work they'd done my whole life — the hands I had learned everything from, the hands I'd boasted about to Director Ishikawa not six hours ago. He looked exactly like himself. That was the unbearable part. Villains, I had assumed, looked like villains; the harbor novels Emilio loved were full of smugglers with scarred faces and gold teeth. Nobody had warned me that an architect of crimes looks like your father squinting at a balance wheel, humming off-key, the coat he shouldn't have been able to afford hanging on the hook behind him.

Emilio was there too, cross-legged on the floor, sorting screws by size into a wooden tray, chattering at Papa about a steamer he'd watched come in that morning, asking questions about tonnage and routes with the hunger of a boy who measures his whole future in hull lengths. Papa answered between adjustments, patient, warm, generous with details I now recognized as inventory. He wasn't lying to Emilio. That was worst of all. He simply knew which truths were safe, the way a man who owns a tide table knows which hours the water hides the rocks.

I stood outside my own front door in the afternoon sun with a smuggler's letter against my heart, and I could not make my hand reach the latch.

Because here is the arithmetic that seized me, there on the step, and never entirely let go again: the letter named my father. Whatever it proved, whatever it accused, the moment it left my possession it became evidence, and evidence does not distinguish between the man who signed the arrangement and the daughter who found the paper. I had spent twenty years learning that a mechanism is only as honest as its weakest gear, and I had just discovered that my family's mechanism had a gear in it I couldn't inspect without stopping the whole works.

I walked past the house. I walked to the end of the street and back. On the second pass, Papa looked up through the glass and saw me and smiled and beckoned, and Emilio waved the screw tray at me, and I went in, and ate rice and salted fish at my own table across from my own father, and answered questions about the observatory job in a voice I kept level by main strength, and laughed once, at something Emilio said about a seagull, and the laugh came out sounding like mine.

After dinner, when the lamps were lit and Papa had gone up the hill to return a tool to Ueda-san, I took the letter from my jacket and held it over the stove.

I want it known that I tried. I held it there until I could feel the heat through the oilcloth, and the wax seal began to soften, and the flame was right there, one tilt of the wrist from solving everything — no letter, no evidence, no paragraph four, just a repaired clock and a family that ate together. One tilt. That's all treason against the truth requires. It's a very small motion. Clockwork taught me that: the smallest escape wheel controls everything downstream.

I couldn't do it.

Not because I was good. Because the letter was the only object in the world that knew what my father was, and burning it felt less like protecting him than like agreeing with him, and some stubborn gear in me refused to be soldered into that movement. Also — I will admit this much — because a letter naming a Sandoval could, held in the right hands, be a shield as easily as a knife. I was already thinking like a smuggler. It took me three days to admit that too.

So I hid it instead. Inside the movement of a customer's mantel clock awaiting repair, wedged behind the strike plate where only a Sandoval would ever think to look — the one hiding place in Nagasaki I trusted absolutely, because it was built on everything my father ever taught me, which struck me, even then, as the bitterest joke the year had played.

Then I went back up the hill to finish the master clock, keeping time for a harbor that was lying to everyone.

I told myself I had three days to decide what to do.

The harbor gave me two.
