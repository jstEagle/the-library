## 3. Father's New Coat

There is a particular smell a house makes when it is hiding something. I had never named it before that evening — I was too busy naming smells like a clockmaker names parts, which is to say obsessively and without being asked — but walking up our lane from the harbor road, I caught it plainly: lamp oil, rice steam, and under it all something faintly metallic, like the inside of a locked drawer.

Or perhaps I only smelled my own guilt. The letter rode against my ribs, sewn into no pocket at all because I hadn't dared leave it at the observatory and hadn't dared open it again either. I had folded it small, wrapped it in a scrap of chamois, and carried it home the way one carries a cracked mainspring — carefully, close to the body, aware that at any moment it might do damage.

Our house stood where our house has always stood, on the slope above Dejima road, close enough to hear ships but not see them, which my father always said was the correct arrangement for a family of landlocked Spaniards in Japan. *We take the sound of the sea,* he liked to say, *and let other men take the sea.* He said it as though this were wisdom rather than poverty wearing its good jacket.

The jacket, as it happened, was new.

I saw him through the window before he saw me. Tomás Sandoval stood in the lamplight of our narrow front room wearing a coat I did not recognize, and this is not a thing I say lightly. I know coats the way I know escapements. My father had owned three coats in my living memory: the black one with the burned cuff from a soldering accident in 1931; the gray one with the missing button replaced by a wooden disc he sanded himself and refused to replace on principle; and the winter coat, which was less a coat than an argument between wool and moths. This fourth coat was charcoal wool, double-breasted, with buttons that caught light like small moons. It fit him across the shoulders the way good things fit — without apology.

I stood in the lane for perhaps ten seconds longer than a daughter should, looking at her father through glass.

Then I went inside, because the alternative was standing in the street forever, and clocks have taught me that everything must eventually be wound forward whether you like it or not.

"You're late," my father said, not turning around. He was setting the table, which he did badly and always had — the bowls placed with the confidence of a man who has never once considered that bowls have a correct orientation.

"The master clock at the observatory doesn't agree with me about time," I said. "It's four minutes slow and it thinks that's my fault."

"Ah." He smiled at the bowls. "A stubborn patient."

"The worst kind." I hung my bag on its peg, feeling the letter shift against me like a second heartbeat. "You bought a coat."

"I bought a coat," he agreed, with the serene dishonesty of a man announcing the weather.

"It has buttons."

"Most coats do."

"Real buttons, Father. Not wooden ones. Not ones you sand yourself out of shame."

"Mirabel." He turned then, and his face did the thing it always does when I press him — a soft folding, warmth laid over stone like felt over a watch case. "Sit down. Emilio will be back any minute smelling of fish, and if we argue before dinner the fish wins."

I sat. The letter pressed warm against my side. And I want to be honest here, since honesty is apparently the currency I intend to spend recklessly later in this story: there was a moment, sitting at that table with the new-coat man who raised me, when I genuinely considered saying nothing forever. Some truths are heavy. But most are merely inconvenient, and inconvenience is a thing a person can live with, folded small against the ribs, carried carefully, never opened. I told myself this. I am very good at telling myself things; it is how I fall asleep.

* * *

Emilio arrived the way weather arrives — announced by a distant rumbling and then suddenly everywhere. He came through the door at fourteen years old and moving at approximately the speed of every ambition he'd ever had, dropped a parcel of dried fish on the counter, kissed my father's cheek, punched my arm (affectionately; Emilio has exactly one register and it is loud), and asked, in this order: what was for dinner, whether the harbor pilot had been drunk today, and whether I had seen any warships.

"No warships," I said.

"You're lying. There were gunboats past the breakwater Tuesday. Kenji Arai says—"

"Kenji Arai says a great many things for a man whose job is paperwork."

"He's going to be an admiral someday," Emilio said with the certainty of the young, who have not yet learned that most men become neither admirals nor anything else but simply continue. "He knows everyone down at the customs office. He let me look at the tide tables."

"How thrilling," I said, and meant it sarcastically, and would think about that sarcasm later, much later, with regret.

Dinner was rice, mackerel, pickled radish, and the specific silence of a family negotiating around an object nobody would name. The object sat across from me wearing charcoal wool and moonlight buttons. Twice my father reached for the teapot and twice his sleeve pulled back just enough to show a shirt cuff that was also new — white, crisp, priced somewhere I didn't want to calculate.

"So," Emilio said, mouth full, "the observatory. Did you fix it?"

"Not yet. The escapement's worn. I have to cut a new pallet stone."

"A what?"

"A little tooth that catches the wheel and lets it go. Catch and release, catch and release. That's all time is, really. Something letting go over and over."

Emilio considered this with the seriousness he reserved for mechanical questions and food. "And if the tooth breaks?"

"Then the clock runs wild until someone fixes it. Everything runs fast or everything stops. There's no middle."

My father looked at me across the teapot. Just for a moment — half a second, the duration of a catch and release — something moved behind his eyes that I could not read, and I have spent my whole life reading small mechanisms. Then it was gone, and he was asking Emilio about the fish market, and the moment closed like a case snapped shut.

* * *

After dinner Emilio performed his nightly ritual, which was to plant himself beside my father and demand stories of ships. This has been the liturgy of our household since Emilio learned to talk. Tomás Sandoval worked the docks as a young man — cargo tallying, manifest checking, the honest drudgery of paper and rope — before his hands found their true trade in clockwork, and those dock years are Emilio's scripture.

"Tell about the Dutch ship," Emilio said, settling in. "The one with the rats."

"There was nothing remarkable about the rats."

"You said they were the size of cats."

"I said no such thing."

"You said—" Emilio appealed to me. "Did he say it?"

"He said it," I confirmed. "He also said the captain tried to pay him in cheese, and the cheese was also mostly rat, so it came to the same thing."

My father laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him — and for a moment the coat didn't matter, and the letter didn't matter, and we were only three people in a small room while outside Nagasaki settled into its evening sounds: temple bells, a dog with opinions, the far hoot of a ferry. These are the moments that make liars of us all. You sit in the warm circle of them and you think: whatever truth costs, surely it cannot cost *this*. Surely nothing is worth breaking this exact lamp-lit arrangement of people.

"Tell about the storm," Emilio said. "The typhoon year. Tell how you tied down the crates."

So my father told it, and I listened, and I watched his hands move as he spoke — those hands, quick and precise, hands that had taught me to file a pivot flat enough to pass the fingernail test. They gestured now at imagined ropes, imagined crates, imagined weather. And some cold, meticulous part of me — the part that had spent the afternoon elbow-deep in a clock housing, the part that had found a sealed letter addressed to men with power and bearing my family's name — that part was not listening to the story at all. It was watching the hands and thinking: *those hands have been tying down different crates lately. Real ones.*

Because I had done arithmetic on the walk home. It is what I do instead of praying. New coat: perhaps thirty yen for wool of that weight. New shirt: five. Emilio's school fees, paid in full last month without the usual agonized negotiation. The debt collector Mr. Okabe, who has visited our door eleven times in two years with the punctuality of a cuckoo clock, had not been seen since spring. And my mother's ledger — but no. We'll come to the ledger in its own terrible time. Suffice it to say that evening, at the dinner table, I knew the sum of visible money did not balance against visible work, and a clockmaker's daughter learns early that when the numbers refuse to match, the mechanism contains a lie.

"Mirabel." My father touched my wrist. "You're somewhere else."

"Nowhere. Tired. The clock kept me bent over all day."

"You love that clock."

"I love fixing things," I said. "The clock is incidental."

"That's not true," Emilio said. "You love the clock. You talk about it like it's a person. You said the observatory clock has 'dignity.'"

"I said it has *wear patterns*."

"Same thing."

And here is where I should record, for the sake of the record I am apparently keeping against myself: my brother was right. I do love clocks like persons. I believe objects hold what happens near them — that a well-used tool remembers its owner's hand, that a house keeps the shape of its arguments, that a master clock which has stood over a harbor for sixty years holds sixty years of arrivals and departures and lies told on the docks below it. I believe this the way some people believe in saints. Which is why, when I found that letter folded into the dark of the mechanism like a confession hidden in a confessor's own heart, I understood immediately that it had been put there to rot or to wait, and I chose, alone, unasked, at nineteen years old, to make it wait.

* * *

Later — dishes washed, Emilio asleep in the room we share, breathing in the deep uncomplicated rhythm of the young — I heard my father in the front room, talking to someone low through the shuttered window. Not entering. Just voices at the threshold, brief, businesslike, in Japanese too quiet for me to follow, though I caught one word repeated twice, and the word was *Friday*.

Then the outer latch, footsteps receding down the lane toward the water.

I lay on my futon with the letter beneath the loose floorboard under my bedding — I had moved it there after dinner, unable to bear even the fiction of wearing it — and I stared at the ceiling beam where a hairline crack runs the length of the room like a river seen from very high up. Friday. My father had a new coat, a vanished debt collector, and appointments at the door after dark.

In four days, I decided, I would know more. I would watch. I would be the small precise tooth in the mechanism, catching and releasing, catching and releasing, until I understood what wheel was turning and why it turned so smoothly on money that wasn't ours.

Catch and release. That's all vigilance is.

I fell asleep planning to follow him.

Outside, the harbor breathed and shifted on its tide, keeping its own counsel, keeping its own time, and somewhere below us in the dark a ship's bell rang the hour — four strokes, patient, indifferent, counting down toward everything.
