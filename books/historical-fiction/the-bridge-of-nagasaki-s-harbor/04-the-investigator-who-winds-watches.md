## 4. The Investigator Who Winds Watches

The bell above my shop door had a crack in it, so it rang a half-tone flat, and I had learned to tell my customers by their bells the way a mother tells her children by their footsteps. The mail boy rang twice, quick. Mrs. Otsuka from the tea house let the door swing shut behind her so the bell got confused and rang three times. And on the morning the harbor investigator came in, the bell rang once — long, deliberate, like a man clearing his throat.

I looked up from the bench with my loupe still screwed into one eye, which meant I saw him first as a blur: a tall shape in a good coat, hat held correctly in both hands instead of jammed on his head. When I took the loupe out, the world sharpened into Kenji Arai, and I confess my first thought was not *investigator* but something considerably less professional.

"Señorita Sandoval." He said it carefully, the way he said everything, each syllable set down like a part being fitted. "You repaired my colleague's pocket watch last spring. He spoke well of you."

"He overpaid me," I said. "But I'll take the compliment."

He smiled — a small one, rationed out, the kind of smile a man keeps in a drawer for occasions — and set his watch on my counter. It was a fine piece, a Swiss cylinder escapement in a case worn silver at the edges from years of a thumb worrying it. The kind of watch that gets carried more than looked at.

"It runs fast," he said. "Two minutes a day. Sometimes four."

"Sometimes four," I repeated. "That's not a mechanical problem. That's a nervous one."

His eyebrows went up a fraction. "Meaning?"

"Meaning either the mainspring's gummy or the owner keeps winding it when he's anxious." I turned the watch over in my palm. "Which is it, señor?"

"You tell me. You're the expert."

It was a polite way of saying *I'm going to stand here and watch you work*, and that is exactly what he did. He did not sit in the customer's chair by the window. He stood at the counter, hands folded, while I opened the case back and let the movement breathe under my lamp. Around us the shop did its small noises: the regulator clock on the wall ticking its honest tick, the sea sounds through the glass, a cart rattling past on the stones outside.

"The observatory clock," he said, conversationally, as if remarking on weather. "They say you fixed it."

"They say correctly. It runs better than it has in twenty years."

"That must have been satisfying. A mechanism that size. Most apprentices never see inside anything larger than a carriage clock."

"I'm not an apprentice anymore. Not formally." I kept my eyes on the escapement. "My father says a certificate is just paper that proves you were patient."

"And are you? Patient?"

"I'm patient with machines." I set the balance wheel free and watched it swing. "Machines don't lie to you. They only repeat what you've done to them."

There was a pause, and in the pause I felt him file that sentence away somewhere, the way a clerk files a receipt. I had said too much and too little at once, which is apparently my natural condition.

"Your father," he said. "Tomás Sandoval. He does brass fitting work at the docks, doesn't he? Fine reputation."

"Fine reputation," I agreed.

"The harbor authority uses him often."

"When the harbor authority pays its invoices, yes."

Another of those rationed smiles. He was good at this, I realized. Good at making an interrogation feel like two neighbors talking about rain. I found myself liking him, which annoyed me considerably, because I could see exactly how useful that would be to a man whose job was asking questions until people forgot they were questions.

* * *

Here is what I should tell you about lying, since I am apparently bad at it: a lie is a mechanism too. It needs every gear cut to tolerance, every wheel meshed, or the whole train binds and stops. My father can lie the way other men breathe. I cannot. Every lie I tell has a rough edge somewhere, and anyone who handles it feels the burr.

So when Arai asked — lightly, turning his hat brim between his fingers — whether I'd noticed anything unusual inside the observatory clock, I heard myself say, "Unusual? Like what?" in a voice pitched half a tone too high, and then I filled the silence after it with too many words, which is the second symptom of a bad liar.

"Because clocks are all unusual, really, if you look closely. Old ones especially. There was rust in the striking train, and someone had oiled it with machine oil instead of clock oil, which is a crime, frankly, and the pendulum suspension spring was fatigued, and — there was dust. Dust is normal. Dust isn't unusual."

"Dust," he agreed gravely. "Not unusual."

"I also replaced the hour hand bushing."

"Did you."

"It was worn."

"Naturally."

He wasn't mocking me, exactly. That was the worst part. He was watching me hang myself with small talk, patiently, the way you watch a child wind a watch backward, and there was something almost kind in it, and kindness from an investigator is more frightening than suspicion. Suspicion you can fight. Kindness makes you want to confess just to stop deserving it.

"There was a letter," I said.

The word left my mouth before my judgment could catch it at the door. I actually felt it go — felt it leave my chest the way a gear tooth leaves a wheel when it snaps, that little mechanical shudder of something now missing.

Arai did not move. Not a muscle. But the quality of his attention changed; it came forward, leaned into me like lamplight.

"A letter," he said.

"In the clock. Old wax seal, stuck behind the strike plate. Observatory correspondence, presumably. Some director from thirty years ago complaining about coal deliveries, for all I know." I laughed, and my laugh sounded like a cracked bell. "I gave it to Director Ueda."

"You gave it to Director Ueda," he repeated.

"Yes."

And there it was — the burr on the gear. Because I hadn't given it to anyone. It sat at that moment wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of my toolbox, under my spare files, where I had put it three nights ago with hands that shook so badly I'd dropped a screwdriver and woken Emilio through the floorboards.

Why did I say I'd delivered it? I have thought about this many times since. Partly because delivering it was the correct thing, the expected thing, and a lie shaped like the truth fits the mechanism best. And partly — I see this now — because some part of me wanted it on record. Wanted a witness to the version of events where I did my duty. If I ever had to produce the letter later, I wanted there to have been a moment when handing it over was still possible.

Arai nodded slowly. "Ueda didn't mention it."

"He's a busy man. It was probably routine."

"Probably." He took out a small notebook — not dramatically, not like in the novels, just the way a man takes out a notebook — and wrote something in it. Two words, maybe three. Then he closed it, and the click of it closing sounded very loud in my little shop.

"Your watch," I said, desperate to change the weather. "Two minutes fast. I'll have it ready Thursday. The cylinder needs cleaning and the hairspring has a kink I can ease out. Someone dropped it."

"I did. On a dock." He paused. "Chasing someone."

"Did you catch them?"

"No." Something moved behind his face then, briefly — not the investigator, but the man under him. "They went into the water. By the time I got down the ladder, there was nothing but wake." He looked at the regulator clock on my wall. "Everyone thinks harbors forget things. They don't. Water holds everything. It just doesn't testify."

I wound the mainspring of his watch a few turns, mostly to have something to do with my hands. "Thursday," I said again.

"Thursday." He put on his hat, and at the door he stopped, hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "Señorita Sandoval. If you ever found something in a clock — anything — something that worried you. Something that made you afraid." The bell hung silent above him, waiting. "Where would you take it?"

I looked at his back, at the straight shoulders and the careful hands, and I thought: *to you*, and then I thought: *never*, and the two thoughts sat together in my chest like gears that wouldn't mesh.

"To my father," I said.

He stood there a moment longer. "Yes," he said finally. "I suppose you would." And the door closed behind him with its single flat ring.

* * *

I did not touch his watch for an hour. I sat at my bench with it open under the lamp, looking at the tiny kinked hairspring, thinking about a man who chases strangers into cold water and loses them and comes in the next day to have the damage repaired, politely, by the daughter of a smuggler.

Because that is what my father is. I had stopped pretending otherwise sometime around three in the morning, staring at my ceiling. The crates under false manifests. The men who moved them without speaking. The new coat hanging on its hook by the door like a confession nobody read.

And now an investigator had come into my shop and asked me, in the gentlest possible language, whether I was drowning too.

I cleaned his watch. That's the truth of it — I cleaned it with more care than it needed, because care was easier than thinking. I eased the kink out of the hairspring with two probes and a breath held so long I went dizzy. I oiled the pivots, one drop each, no more. A watch like his wants to run true; it only needs someone to stop interfering with it long enough.

By the time I finished, the light through the window had gone amber, and the regulator on my wall struck six, and I realized I had spent the entire day avoiding home.

Emilio met me at the door with salt on his sleeves and a story already halfway up his throat — a freighter in from Shanghai, cranes working past dark, a foreman who'd promised him real wages soon, real work, work on the water. His eyes were shining the way they shine, and our father stood behind him in the doorway wearing the new coat, and between the two of them I felt the whole family lean toward some future I could suddenly see clearly, the way you see a wreck through clear water.

"Mirabel," Tomás said. "You're pale. Come in, your dinner's getting cold."

I went in. Of course I went in. I am my father's daughter; I know how to keep a mechanism running by refusing to look at the broken part.

But that night, after the lamps were out, I lay awake doing arithmetic I couldn't stop: one letter, hidden in oilcloth. One investigator, holding a notebook with my lie written in it. One brother, walking toward the water with his arms open.

Three moving parts, all of them meshing toward something. And somewhere in the mechanism, I could feel it — the tooth about to snap.
