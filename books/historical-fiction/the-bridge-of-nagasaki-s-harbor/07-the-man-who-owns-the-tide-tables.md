## 7. The Man Who Owns the Tide Tables

The summons came the way a summons from a powerful man always comes: not as a letter, not as a knock, but as a small, polite catastrophe that rearranges your whole day while pretending to be nothing at all.

I was at the bench, fitting a balance spring no thicker than a spider's argument, when a boy in a spotless uniform appeared in the doorway of our shop. He couldn't have been more than fifteen. He held his cap in both hands like it was something breakable.

"Señorita Sandoval? I'm to take you to Colonel Vasquez. He requests your services."

"Requests," I said. "At nine o'clock at night."

"He said you'd understand. He said—" the boy swallowed— "he said to tell you the tide tables are wrong this month, and he'd like your opinion on why."

My hands went very still over the movement. That was not a message meant for a clockmaker. That was a message meant for me specifically, and it had been written by someone who knew exactly which key turned in my lock.

"Give me a moment," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect.

I put on my good coat—the one with the mended cuff, because good coats in our house were a matter of degree—and I told Papá, who was mending a net by lamplight, that I had an evening repair call. He looked up at me with an expression I couldn't read, and for one strange moment I thought he might stop me. His mouth opened. Then it closed.

"Take the lantern," he said instead.

That was the first time I understood that Papá knew who was calling. He knew, and he let me go, and he gave me a lantern as if light were the thing I'd be needing most where I was headed.

* * *

The Vasquez house sat on the hill above the harbor, in the district where the foreign traders used to live before they moved somewhere even more expensive. It was a Western house, all columns and shutters, lit from within like a lantern itself, and I remember thinking, as the boy led me up the drive, that from here the harbor must look like a drawing—everything arranged, every ship in its place, every motion legible. From here, the harbor was a thing you read. Down there, it was a thing that read *you*.

Colonel Vasquez received me in a study lined with books he had clearly never opened. He was smaller than I expected—that's always the surprise with powerful men, isn't it? The way they fit inside their reputations with room to spare. He was perhaps fifty-five, silver at the temples, with the kind of face that had been handsome once and had since been promoted into authority. He wore civilian clothes, but he wore them the way other men wear uniforms: everything pressed, everything intentional.

"Señorita Sandoval." He rose and shook my hand, and his grip was dry and precise, like a man handling an instrument he respected. "Thank you for coming. May I offer you tea?"

"I'd rather know why I'm here."

Something flickered across his face—not anger. Amusement. The amusement of a man watching a cat arch its back at a dog ten times its size.

"Direct," he said. "Good. I find directness saves so much wear on everyone's gears." He gestured me into a chair. "You repaired the observatory master clock last week. A delicate job. The escapement had been tampered with—did you notice?"

I kept my face still. In my trade you learn to keep your face still; a watchmaker who flinches at every tick gives away more than she knows.

"The governor was worn," I said. "Not tampered. Worn."

"Mm." He poured tea himself, which surprised me. He handed me a cup I did not want. "And in the course of the repair, you opened the housing. All of it. Including the compartment behind the fourth gear train."

There it was. My heart performed a maneuver I can only describe as a full escape-wheel release—all the power in the mainspring let out at once, everything racing, nothing controlled. But my hands stayed in my lap, and my face stayed a clock face, and I drank the tea because refusing seemed somehow more dangerous than accepting.

"I opened the housing," I agreed. "That's how one repairs a clock."

"And did you find anything unusual?"

"No."

He smiled. It was a warm smile. That was the worst part—it was genuinely warm, the way a stove is warm, without any opinion about what is being heated.

"You're lying," he said pleasantly, "and you're bad at it, which I confess I find reassuring. A good liar would frighten me. A bad liar is simply a person who hasn't yet decided whose side she's on." He set down his own cup. "Let me save us both some time. There was a letter in that compartment. You found it. You did not deliver it to Director Ishikawa, though protocol required it. You took it home. It is currently hidden inside the case of a mantel clock in your workshop—second shelf, behind the French carriage clocks, wrapped in oilcloth. Have I missed anything?"

The room tilted slightly. I gripped the arm of the chair. Not because of the letter—he could have learned of the letter a dozen ways—but because of the *mantel clock*. Because he knew the shelf. Because somewhere between my shop and this study, my hiding place had been weighed, measured, and catalogued, and I had never once felt the touch of the hand that did it.

"If you already know where it is," I managed, "why am I here?"

"Because knowing where a thing is and owning it are different matters entirely." He stood and went to the window, hands clasped behind his back, and looked down at his harbor. "Do you know what I do, Señorita Sandoval? Officially?"

"Harbor administration. Shipping schedules."

"Shipping schedules." He repeated it with relish. "Yes. Do you know what a tide table is? It is a promise the sea makes to men who cannot read her handwriting. Every ship in this port lives and dies by those tables. When the water is deep enough to load, when the patrols change watch, when the customs launches are refueling and their officers are ashore eating dinner they'll complain about later. The entire harbor breathes according to a schedule, and I am the man who writes the schedule." He turned back to me. "I own the tide tables, Señorita. Which means I own the tide."

* * *

I want to record, honestly, what happened inside me during the next hour, because afterward people asked me—well. No one asked me. But I have asked myself, many times, in the years since, and I owe myself an honest answer.

What happened was this: I was afraid. Not the hot fear of the warehouse, not the sick fear of the letter. This was cold fear, fear with manners, fear that poured tea and complimented my work. And the terrible thing—the thing I am still ashamed of—is that some part of me responded to it the way a mechanism responds to a well-cut gear. With recognition. With a horrible professional respect.

He talked, and I listened, and this is what I understood:

The smuggling operation was larger than Papá. Larger by orders of magnitude. Papá was a cog—a well-placed cog, a cog with a daughter who could fix clocks, but a cog. The crates under false manifests, the ships that came and went at hours no honest ship keeps, the dockworkers who loaded and asked nothing—they were all synchronized. Runs timed against patrol rotations. Customs inspections scheduled to arrive forty minutes after a hold was sealed and clean. Everything moving to a rhythm, and the rhythm written by the man in the study with the unopened books.

And now he wanted a new rhythm-keeper.

"The observatory clock controls the harbor bell," he said. "The harbor bell regulates the pilot boats. The pilot boats report to my office. It is a beautiful chain, and chains are only as honest as their weakest link. Your father vouched for you. He says you are the finest apprentice in Nagasaki, possibly in Kyushu, and that you keep your mouth closed, which in my experience is rarer than talent." He sat again, leaning forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the courtesy dropped away and I saw the machinery underneath, and it was vast and patient and utterly without malice, which made it worse. "Here is what I offer. One: your family's debts are settled. All of them. Tonight. Two: your father's name comes off certain lists that exist, and never goes onto certain other lists that will come to exist. Three: you continue your apprenticeship, your little shop, your life, undisturbed. In exchange, you maintain certain timing mechanisms for me. Nothing illegal in themselves—chronometers, synchronizers, ordinary instruments. What they're *for* is not your concern. You fix clocks, Señorita. You don't ask the clocks what hour they serve."

"And if I refuse?"

He spread his hands, regretful, a man disappointed by weather.

"Then the letter you hid becomes evidence in an investigation, and investigations have a way of gathering families the way wrecks gather barnacles. Your father first, naturally. Then whoever else the current takes. I would be sad about it. Genuinely. I have a great deal of respect for craftsmanship, and your father is a craftsman in his way, and so are you." He picked up his teacup. "But the sea does not respect anything, and neither do the men above me. I am, if you can believe it, the reasonable one in this arrangement."

I set down my cup. My hand was almost steady. Almost.

"You said the tide tables are wrong this month."

His eyebrows rose—genuine pleasure, the pleasure of a teacher whose student has done the reading.

"Yes."

"Why would you tell me that?"

"Because," he said, "you're going to tell me why. That's the work. Someone has been feeding corrections into the tables—small ones, minutes only, the kind no one notices until two ships meet where they shouldn't. Someone is using my own instrument against me, or testing whether I notice, or both. I want my timing mechanisms examined, and I want the mind that examines them to be a mind I've already bought." He smiled again, warm as a stove. "You see? I'm not asking you to be a criminal. I'm asking you to be a clockmaker. The rest is just what time it is."

* * *

I walked home through streets gone quiet, past shuttered shops and a shrine where someone had left oranges for a god who presumably also kept accounts. The lantern threw my shadow long ahead of me, and I watched it walk, and I thought: *that is what he wants. A shadow that moves when he moves.*

Here is what I did not say in that study, what I carried home under my ribs like contraband:

I said yes. Of course I said yes. What was I going to do—refuse a man who knew the shelf, the oilcloth, the French carriage clocks? Refusal wasn't courage; refusal was arithmetic done badly. I said yes, and I shook his dry precise hand, and I drank his tea, and I walked out of his house owned.

But I am my father's daughter and my mother's daughter both, and there is a thing about mechanisms that Colonel Vasquez, for all his talk of gears, does not appear to understand.

A clockmaker who repairs a mechanism learns it. Entirely. Whether she means to or not. The worn tooth on the third wheel, the hairline crack in the plate, the spring that's been replaced with one a half-size too strong—you cannot open a machine without memorizing it. It goes into your hands and stays there. He thinks he is hiring a pair of hands.

He is hiring a memory.

When I got home, Papá was still awake, still sitting by the lamp, and he looked at me with a question he didn't dare ask aloud. I set the lantern on the table between us.

"He knows about the letter," I said.

Papá closed his eyes. For a long moment he was silent, and in the silence I heard the harbor bell strike eleven, faint and far, keeping everyone's time but its own.

"What did he want?" Papá asked finally.

"He wants me to fix his clocks."

"That's all?"

I thought of the tide tables, and the corrections hidden in them like splinters, and the mind clever enough—or desperate enough—to slip them in. I thought of a chain of bells and boats and schedules, every link visible from a hilltop window, and how a chain tells you everything except where it's bolted down.

"That's all," I said.

It was the second lie I told my father that week, and I was getting better at it, which frightened me more than the Colonel did.

Upstairs, Emilio slept with his door open, the way he always did, one arm hanging off the bed, dreaming whatever boys dream when they beg for stories of ships. I stood in his doorway a moment and listened to him breathe.

Three days earlier I had found a letter in a clock. Tonight I had shaken hands with the man who wrote the harbor's future in pencil. And somewhere out in the dark water, the tide was turning on a schedule, right on time, exactly as promised.

I went to bed and did not sleep, and at dawn I got up and began, very carefully, to remember everything.
