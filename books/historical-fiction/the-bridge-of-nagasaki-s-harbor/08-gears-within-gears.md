## 8. Gears Within Gears

The summons came by messenger boy at seven in the morning, which told me two things before I opened the envelope: that Colonel Vasquez considered himself important enough to interrupt breakfast, and that he was careless enough to let a twelve-year-old read the address aloud on a public street.

I paid the boy a sen and burned the envelope in the shop stove after I'd memorized what it said. *The Colonel requests the pleasure of Miss Sandoval's skill at eleven o'clock. A mechanical matter. Discretion assumed.*

Discretion assumed. Not requested. Assumed — the way gravity is assumed, the way the tide is assumed. The word had the weight of something already decided about me.

I spent four hours preparing, and I want to be honest about what that preparation consisted of, because it was not courage. It was inventory.

I cleaned my tools. Every one. The escapement files, the tweezers fine enough to lift a human hair, the loupe with the crack in its left rim that I'd learned to look around the way you learn to look past a scar on someone's face. I oiled the pivots of my movement holder. I laid everything out on green felt in the order I would use it, left to right, the way a surgeon lays out knives, because order is the only prayer I have ever trusted.

And then I did something else. I took a fresh notebook from the drawer under the register — the kind I use for sketching wheel trains before I cut brass — and I wrote at the top of the first page, in my smallest hand: *Nothing.* And beneath it, also nothing. An empty page waiting to be filled, the way an empty harbor waits for a ship.

My father watched me pack from the doorway of the workshop with his coat already on, though he wasn't going anywhere. He'd taken to wearing the coat indoors lately, the way some men take to drink.

"You could refuse," he said.

"He'd send the messenger back."

"He might not."

"No," I agreed, snapping the clasps of my tool case. "He might send something worse than paper."

Tomás Sandoval looked at his daughter then with an expression I will describe as precisely as I can, because precision is all I have ever owned: he looked at me the way a man looks at a clock he knows is running fast and cannot bring himself to open. He knew something inside me had slipped a tooth. He could hear it ticking wrong. He did not know how to reach the mechanism without breaking the case.

"Mirabel."

"I'll be home for dinner."

"That's not what I—"

"Dinner," I said, "is at six. You taught me that a promise about time is the only kind worth keeping." Which was a cruel thing to say to him, since time was exactly what he'd been selling, and we both knew it, and neither of us said so. He stepped aside. I walked out into a morning so clean and bright it felt like an insult.

* * *

Colonel Vasquez received me in a house above the foreign quarter where the ceilings were too high for the rooms, built by someone who had wanted to seem larger than he was, or perhaps by the Colonel himself, who struck me as a man permanently engaged in that project. Two guards at the door who did not check my tools, which told me they were not guards so much as furniture. A maid who brought tea and did not meet my eyes. And on a table by the window, under a linen cloth like a patient awaiting surgery: the device.

He talked first. Powerful men always talk first; it's how they mark the hour.

"You'll forgive the early hour. Harbor business keeps naval hours." He poured the tea himself, which surprised me until I understood it was a performance — the gracious officer, the man of simple tastes, the colonel who pours his own tea. "You repaired the observatory master clock, I'm told. Remarkably well. Dr. Ueda says it hasn't run this true in nine years."

"Dr. Ueda is generous."

"Dr. Ueda," said Vasquez, "is frightened, which he mistakes for generosity. But your work speaks for itself. As does your discretion." He smiled. "Regarding certain correspondence."

I kept my hands still in my lap and my face arranged into the expression I wear for customers who blame their broken watches on the weather. Attentive. Mild. Slightly stupid.

"I found nothing but dust in that housing, Colonel."

"Of course you didn't." He said it warmly, the way you agree with a child about Santa Claus. "Dust. And now — if you're finished with tea?"

The cloth came off, and I confess that for a moment — one full rotation of the second hand, no more — I forgot to be afraid, because the device was beautiful.

It sat in a mahogany case the size of a hatbox, and it was, at heart, a clock — but a clock that had been asked a question no clock should answer. Through the glass panel I could see three separate movements mounted on a common frame, coupled by a differential gear assembly of a design I had only seen described in a German journal I could not afford to buy. Three sources of time, reconciled into one output. My fingers ached. That is the shameful truth of that morning: whatever else I was feeling, some traitor part of me simply wanted to *touch it*.

"What does it do?" I asked, though I suspected.

"It keeps appointments," Vasquez said. "The harbor patrol runs on a schedule. The coastal freighters run on another. This reconciles them — takes the patrol's official timetable here"— he tapped the first movement —"and the actual timetable here, which drifts, patrols being patrols, men being men — and produces this." He tapped the third. "A window. When the third dial says go, there is water between the ships and the guns, and when it says stop, there is not. Do you follow?"

"I fix clocks, Colonel. I don't sail them."

"But you understand synchronization." He said the word like a man handling a jewel he'd stolen. "It stopped yesterday. Escapement fault, I'm told — though the man who told me has been promoted beyond his competence. Fix it. Take whatever time you need." He rose. "Take as long as the afternoon. I'll be at the garrison."

"And if I can't fix it?"

He paused at the door, genuinely pleased by the question, the way a chess player is pleased when you finally see the trap you've been sitting in for six moves.

"Then I'll find a clockmaker who can. There are four in Nagasaki. But none of them," he added pleasantly, "have a father with debts, a brother with sea fever, and a name already written in pencil. You are uniquely motivated, Miss Sandoval. It's why you're here." And he left me alone with his beautiful machine, locking the door behind him from the outside with a sound like a full stop.

* * *

Alone, then. Locked in a stranger's house with the machinery of a crime, and four hours, and every tool I owned.

Here is what I did first, and I record it without pride: I put my head down on the table next to the case and I breathed, slowly, ten times, until my hands stopped shaking. A watchmaker learns early that tremor ruins more work than ignorance ever will. Fear is a vibration like any other. You damp it or you ruin the movement.

Then I opened the case, and the world narrowed to brass.

Let me tell you what I found, because I am going to spend the rest of my life knowing it, and knowledge this heavy wants company.

The primary movement was Swiss — a lever escapement, beautifully finished, keeping the official patrol time fed to it by wire signal from the garrison. The secondary was Japanese, cruder, driven by a pendulum that was meant to track the *actual* comings and goings of patrol boats as logged by whoever in the harbor office was on Vasquez's payroll. The differential between them — the marriage gear, we call it — resolved the two into the output train that drove the window dial.

But it was the fourth thing, the thing that wasn't supposed to be there, that made me sit down on the Colonel's expensive chair with my heart going like a flywheel.

Wired into the output train, small as a secret, was a cutout cam — a rotating disc with a profile cut into its edge, tripping a contact once per revolution. I traced its gearing back. One revolution per week. The profile had seven lobes. I copied the profile into my notebook, lobe by lobe, measuring each with my depth gauge and writing the figures down in code of my own devising — gear ratios disguised as dress measurements, angular offsets disguised as recipe quantities, because if they searched me I intended to be carrying nothing but a woman's scribbles. Seven windows a week. Seven appointments the harbor made with itself while everyone in Nagasaki believed the water was empty.

And then the arithmetic, which was the worst part, because arithmetic doesn't flinch:

The cam's timing correlated — I checked it twice against the tide tables printed in the *Nagasaki Press*, which any child could buy — with spring tides. The smuggling runs ran on the dark water of the month's deepest tides. Which meant the schedule wasn't hidden at all. It was published, weekly, in the newspaper, in plain figures, invisible unless you had the key. Unless you had *this*. Vasquez hadn't built a machine to hide his crime. He'd built a machine to make the crime look like weather. Like the moon. Like something no one could arrest.

I sat there hating him with a completeness I had never managed toward any broken object in my life. I have been angry at machines before — at springs that snap, at mainsails of rust eating a barrel arbor — but machine-hatred is really just frustration, and it passes when the work goes right. This was different. This was hatred of a *design*. Somewhere a man had sat at a desk and drawn these seven lobes, and each lobe was a night, and each night was crates, and the crates were opium or arms or people — I didn't know, and the not-knowing had teeth — and he had signed his drawing the way I sign repair tickets, with a little flourish, satisfied with his work.

Well. So was I going to be satisfied with mine.

Because here is the thought that arrived while I held the cutout cam up to the light, turning it, watching the lamplight run along its edge like water along a hull — the thought that changed me from a girl locked in a room into something else, something I am only now, years later, willing to call by its name:

A clockmaker who understands a mechanism owns it.

Not legally. Not in any way the Colonel would recognize. But every machine is a confession. It cannot help telling you what it does and when and how, and it cannot refuse to keep telling you, forever, to anyone who asks correctly. Vasquez had built his empire out of timing — and timing was *my country*. He had handed his secret to the one trade in Nagasaki whose entire discipline is reading what a mechanism does and making it testify.

I memorized the wheel train. All of it. Tooth counts, module estimates, pivot diameters, the escape wheel's geometry, the governor that kept the output dial from racing when the wire signal dropped. I memorized the wiring runs and the terminal board and the serial number stamped inside the case lid — *KV-4, Meiji pattern, altered* — and I memorized the alteration, because the alteration was the crime, made metal. Four hours is a long time when you are frightened, and fear, I have found, is an excellent mnemonic. I remember that afternoon the way other people remember their wedding days.

I fixed the escapement, too. Of course I did. That was the bargain and I honored it, because a broken promise is the one thing I have never been able to abide in a workshop, even a workshop I hated. I replaced a worn pallet stone, trued a bent arbor, oiled what needed oiling, and by five o'clock the window dial was sweeping its slow circles again, smooth as a lie.

When Vasquez returned he examined the output dial, then me, with the same appraising squint.

"It will run a year now without attention," I said. "Longer, if the wire signal stays clean. But the cutout cam is brass, and brass wears. In eight months the lobes will be soft and the windows will drift."

"Then in eight months I'll send for you again."

"Yes," I said. "You will."

He smiled at that, thinking he had bought a craftsman. Perhaps he had. Craftsmen are cheap; there were four clockmakers in Nagasaki. What he had actually done was show a witness the murder weapon and ask her to polish it.

At the door, one of the furniture-guards relieved me of nothing, because I carried nothing. Just a tool case and a notebook full of dress measurements and soup recipes. The maid held the door. The evening came down over the harbor, gold and ordinary, fishing boats coming home on honest water, and somewhere out past the breakwater, on a schedule set by a cam with seven lobes, something was moving that shouldn't exist.

* * *

I got home at six minutes to six and washed the machine oil off my hands for a long time, longer than oil requires, watching the harbor scum swirl gray in the basin. At six exactly I sat down to dinner across from my father, who had cooked, badly, because he always cooked badly on days his conscience was loud.

Emilio talked with his mouth full about the freighter — the *Kestrel*, her name was, he'd memorized her tonnage, her draft, the captain's name, everything, hoarding facts the way he used to hoard marbles. Tomás listened with his face doing that terrible thing fathers' faces do: loving the boy and fearing for him in the same expression, like a coin spinning and refusing to land.

"And she sails Thursday," Emilio said. "Coastal run first, then — Papa, when do I get to say where?"

"Soon," said our father.

Thursday. I turned the word over while I ate. Thursday. I thought of seven lobes on a brass disc, and I thought: *which lobe are you?*

After dinner I climbed to my room and took out the notebook, and by lamplight, with my loupe and my memory, I began to draw the Colonel's machine from the inside of my skull — every wheel, every lobe, every figure — in drawings so careful that any engineer in Tokyo could rebuild the device from them and know exactly what it was for.

Halfway through the second sheet, my pen stopped.

Because I had just realized something, and it arrived cold, the way the best and worst realizations do: the drawings weren't enough. Paper burns — I had watched enough of this city's secrets burn to know that paper is the most perishable material a truth can live in. Evidence could be found and confiscated, notebooks seized, letters destroyed. Vasquez owned clerks and policemen and, apparently, the tide itself.

What he did not own, and could never confiscate, was the version of the machine that lived in my head.

I blew out the lamp and lay in the dark listening to the harbor, and beneath the water sounds, beneath Emilio breathing through his wall, I could almost hear it — the whole rotten mechanism of the city, ticking away on its secret schedule, every gear in its appointed place.

Every gear but one.

In the morning, I would begin looking for the investigator who winds watches.
