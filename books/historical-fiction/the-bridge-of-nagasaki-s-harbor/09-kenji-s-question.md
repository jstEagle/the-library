## 9. Kenji's Question

The bell over the shop door had a particular voice — a bright, unbothered little *ting* that meant a customer with money and no troubles. For three weeks it had been silent more often than not, which suited me fine. Silence was easier to keep when no one came in to break it.

So when the bell rang on a gray Tuesday morning and I looked up to find Kenji Arai standing in my doorway with his hat in his hands and no watch anywhere on his person, I knew two things at once: the pretending was over, and I had not prepared nearly enough lies.

"Miss Sandoval," he said. "I've come about a clock."

"You're wearing one," I said, nodding at his wrist. "It's running."

"It is." He glanced down at it as if surprised to find it there. "I've come about a different clock."

"The observatory master." I turned back to my bench and picked up a gear I did not need, because hands that look busy give the mouth somewhere to hide. "I told you before, Director Mori was satisfied."

"He was," Arai agreed. He did not move from the doorway. Rain had come in with him, or the smell of rain, or the smell of the harbor under rain — tar and salt and something faintly like smoke that I chose not to name. "He was satisfied, and then he was transferred to Sasebo with remarkable speed, and now a man named Okada keeps the observatory, and Okada does not ask questions about anything, which makes him very good at his job."

"That sounds like gossip," I said. "Gossip doesn't keep time."

"No." He stepped inside and closed the door behind him, gently, as though the door might be listening too. "It doesn't."

* * *

Here is what I had learned about investigators in the weeks since he first brought me his watch with its too-casual questions: they do not pounce. Pouncing is for cats and amateurs. An investigator sits down across from you the way water sits down into a crack, patient and without malice, and waits for the material itself to decide it would rather be somewhere else.

Arai sat down in the customer's chair by the window. He put his hat on his knee. He looked at my wall of clocks — all of them ticking, none of them agreeing, which is the honest condition of any collection of clocks — and he let the silence do a full circuit of the room.

Three dockworkers, he said, finally, the way a man says something he has already rehearsed against the sound of his own voice. Gone since the end of last month. No papers filed, no families notified, no bodies found. One of them, a man named Ishida, had been seen last on the night of the twenty-sixth, near Warehouse Row, carrying nothing, going nowhere anyone could account for.

"I don't know any dockworkers," I said.

"I didn't ask if you did."

"You asked a question with a dockworker inside it."

Something moved through his face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile kept its coat checked. He had a way of looking at me that made me feel less accused than *read*, which was worse. Accusation you could fight. Being read required only that you stand still and contain things.

"Miss Sandoval," he said. "You repaired the master clock at the harbor observatory. You are, by reputation, the best apprentice clockmaker in this city, and your father is a respected man with a growing business and a daughter he's clearly proud of. None of that interests me." He leaned forward slightly. "What interests me is that when I mention the observatory, your hands stop moving."

I looked down. My hands were, indeed, stopped — the gear held in the tweezers, suspended mid-air like a small brass moon waiting for an orbit that wasn't coming. I set it down with exaggerated care, the way one does when one has been caught doing nothing.

"They stop moving when anyone mentions the observatory," I said. "That job paid for our winter coal. It was the most important work I've ever done and the most frightening. Big clocks are like big men — everyone assumes they're sturdy because they're large, and then you open them up and find they've been held together by habit and hope."

It was a good answer. I was almost proud of it. He let it sit between us for a moment, respectful, the way one respects a well-made mechanism right up until the moment one takes it apart.

"When you repaired it," he said, "did you find anything inside that shouldn't have been there?"

The room got very quiet. Even the clocks seemed to lean in — forty-odd pendulums holding their breath, which is not how pendulums work, but that is how fear works; it makes physics sentimental.

"Inside a clock," I said slowly, "there is never anything that shouldn't be there. That's the whole religion of it. Every part belongs or the whole thing stops. If I'd found something foreign — dust, a dead moth, a coin some boy dropped through the vent fifty years ago — I'd have removed it and noted it in my log."

"And your log?"

"Is a log," I said. "It records repairs. It doesn't record moths."

He studied me for a long moment. Outside, a tram went past on the wet street, singing its electric song, and the light through the window shifted from gray to grayer. I became aware that my heart was behaving badly — racing, then catching, then racing again, like a mainspring wound by someone who didn't understand the mechanism. Which, in fairness to my heart, described exactly what was happening to the rest of me.

Because here was the arithmetic I was doing while my face performed calm: *he knows*. Not everything — perhaps not even much — but he knew enough to sit in my shop and say *Warehouse Row* and *the twenty-sixth* out loud, and every word of it landed on me like a hand on a bruise I'd been telling myself wasn't there. And beneath the arithmetic ran the other current, the shameful one, warm and rising: *tell him*. Tell him about the letter sealed behind the strike-train plate. Tell him about following Father through the fog to the warehouse, the crates with their false manifests, the smell of camphor and gun oil where the manifests said silk and tea. Tell him about Colonel Vasquez's office with its tide tables and its courtesy like a drawn blade laid flat on the desk. Tell him everything, because he was the first person in months who had asked me a question and actually wanted the true answer, and God help me, I was so tired of carrying the truth alone in a body too small for it.

And then the third voice, the cold one, the practical one, the one that sounded uncomfortably like my father: *three dockworkers, Mirabel. Gone. No papers. No bodies. And you want to add your own name to a list that short?*

"Miss Sandoval." His voice had changed — lower, stripped of its official furniture. "May I ask you something directly? Not as an investigator. Just as a question."

"You may ask," I said. "Asking is free. It's the answers that cost."

"Are you afraid of someone?"

* * *

There are questions and there are questions. Most questions are keys looking for locks. This one was a mirror.

I made myself look busy at the bench — oiling something, adjusting something, performing the liturgy of the craft while my mind stood in the back of the church screaming. Because yes. Yes, I was afraid of someone. I was afraid of a colonel with clean fingernails who spoke about tides and schedules as though human beings were cargo that happened to walk. I was afraid of my father, which was a newer fear and a worse one, because love is supposed to be the safe harbor and mine had become the smuggler's cove. I was afraid of the letter hidden in my mother's old sewing box beneath a false bottom I'd cut myself, sealed in wax, naming my family in a hand I couldn't identify, and I was afraid most of all that someday soon I would have to choose between the truth and the people whose names were written in it.

"Not yet," I said.

He waited. He was good at waiting. It occurred to me, not for the first time, that patience might be the entire profession, and everything else — the badge, the questions, the careful notebook — was just costume for it.

"'Not yet' isn't 'no,'" he observed.

"'Not yet' is the most precise answer I can currently offer," I said. "Precision matters in this trade. A clock that's wrong by half a second today will be wrong by a full second next week, and by a minute by spring, and nobody notices until suddenly the trains don't meet and the ships arrive at empty piers. Fear works the same way. It accumulates." I set down the oiler. "So no, Investigator. Today, at this hour, in this shop, I am not afraid of anyone in particular. Ask me again on a day when the answer changes."

He looked at me for a long time. The rain picked up against the glass, soft and insistent, a thousand small fingers asking to be let in.

"If the answer changes," he said, "you should know that there are ways to protect a witness. There are ways to protect a family. They aren't perfect — nothing in this work is perfect — but they exist, and they start with one sentence spoken early rather than late."

"And if the witness's family is the thing the witness needs protecting *from*?" 

The words were out of my mouth before the better part of me could catch them at the door. I heard them land in the room like a dropped tool — that bright, terrible clatter of metal on wood floor — and I watched him go absolutely still, the way a hunter goes still, the way a clock goes still when the escapement finally releases a tooth it's been holding for hours.

"Then," he said quietly, "the earlier sentence matters even more."

* * *

He stayed another ten minutes and mercifully talked about nothing — the weather, the price of rice, a rumor that the ferry line was adding a night crossing. Ordinary words, deployed like sandbags. I understood the tactic even as it soothed me: he was telling me, without saying it, that he would not press today, that doors opened on their own schedule and forcing them broke the hinges. When he rose to leave, he paused at the door with his hat in his hands.

"For what it's worth," he said, "your repair is holding beautifully. Best-running watch in Nagasaki."

"You wound it properly for once," I said. "Even the finest mechanism fails in careless hands."

"Yes," he said, and something passed across his face — a flicker, a signal run up no flagpole I could see. "That's been my experience exactly. Good day, Miss Sandoval."

The bell gave its cheerful little *ting* behind him, oblivious, and I stood alone among my forty disagreeing clocks with my heart going like a runaway train and my hands steady as bedrock, because that is the trick of this life, the one no one tells you: the outside learns to keep perfect time precisely because the inside cannot.

* * *

I did not touch the letter that night. I wanted to — I lay awake cataloguing reasons, each one dressed as necessity — but touching it felt like striking a match in a room I suspected was already full of gas. Instead I did what I always do when the world exceeds my tolerances: I worked. I dismantled and cleaned a mantel clock that needed nothing, reassembled it, and listened to it tick with the fierce, useless satisfaction of a woman who can fix anything except her own circumstances.

It was near midnight when I noticed the paper folded under the shop's front door — a note, delivered in the dead hours, unsigned, the handwriting blocky and careful:

*THE OBSERVATORY CLOCK RUNS FAST NOW. COME CORRECT IT. — O.*

Okada. The new director who asked no questions. Asking me, by note, in the middle of the night, to come to the harbor observatory — to the place where the letter had been hidden, where whatever remained of my safety was buried in brass.

I read it four times. Then I carried it to the stove and watched it curl and blacken, because paper remembers ink long after people wish it wouldn't.

Through the window, far off, the harbor lights burned in the rain, and beyond them the observatory stood black against the blacker sky, keeping its own counsel, winding toward some hour I did not want to meet.

I reached for my tool bag anyway.
