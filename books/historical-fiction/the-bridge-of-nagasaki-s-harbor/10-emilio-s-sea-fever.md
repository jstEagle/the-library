## 10. Emilio's Sea Fever

My brother came home smelling of salt and triumph, which is a combination I had learned to distrust the way one learns to distrust a sweet-tasting mushroom — not because sweetness is inherently wicked, but because in my experience it so often grew on rot.

He burst through the door at half past six with his collar open and his hair wind-scoured and announced, before he had even removed his shoes, "I've got work."

Father looked up from his ledger. I looked up from a disassembled carriage clock that had been sulking on my bench for three days. The clock did not look up at all, being a clock, but I felt it judging us both.

"Work," Father said carefully. He set down his pen in the manner of a man setting down a live coal.

"Coastal run. The *Kanagawa Maru* — no, wait, she's the freighter, the *Kanagawa Maru* is where I interviewed." Emilio dropped into a chair across from me, upside down in spirit if not in body, fifteen years old and vibrating like an escapement wound too tight. "Deckhand, first season. Real wages, Papa. Not errand wages. Not 'sweep the shop and call it learning' wages."

I felt my stomach perform a small, precise maneuver, the way a mainspring does when someone has overwound it and it begins, very quietly, to consider snapping.

"The *Kanagawa Maru*," Father repeated.

"You know her?" Emilio's whole face opened like a window. This was the trouble with my brother: he had never once in his life suspected a door of being anything other than a door.

"I know of her." Father's voice was level in the way that ice is level. "She runs the coastal route. Kagoshima, Moji, sometimes down to Shanghai when the manifests allow."

"Manifests!" Emilio said the word as though it were a festival. He turned to me. "Mira, did you hear? Real manifests. Cargo holds. I'll be *paid* to be at sea."

I was, at seventeen, already practiced at the art of saying nothing while thinking everything, which is perhaps the most marketable skill a girl in Nagasaki could acquire in the year 1938. So I said nothing. I picked up my tweezers. I looked at the carriage clock's balance wheel as though it contained the answer to a question I had not yet permitted myself to ask aloud.

The question was this: whose ship?

Because here is what I knew, and what I had known since the night I stood in the shadow of Warehouse Nine watching crates move under false manifests while my father counted them with another man's abacus: there were only a handful of coastal freighters running regular schedules out of Nagasaki harbor that summer, and every one of them that mattered ran on Colonel Vasquez's tide tables. My father had shown me the schedule himself, in the terrible quiet after his confession, spread across our kitchen table like a patient's chart. The *Kanagawa Maru* had been third from the top. Her departure times matched patrol gaps the way a key matches a lock, because both had been cut by the same hand.

"First season," Father said now. "Who signed you?"

"A Mr. Oshiro. Harbor office." Emilio produced a paper from his jacket with the flourish of a magician producing doves. "Look — proper papers. Stamped and everything."

Proper papers. Stamped and everything. In my experience — which by then was considerable, though I did not yet know how much more considerable it would become — documents stamped by the harbor office fell into two categories: the kind that meant something, and the kind that meant someone had paid for ink.

Father took the paper and read it the way I read a broken movement: slowly, looking for the flaw. I watched his eyes travel the lines. I watched them stop somewhere in the middle and go very still, the way water goes still when a stone has just gone through it.

"Papa?" Emilio said.

"It's good work," Father said. And then, after a silence exactly long enough to be noticed by everyone except the person it was performed for: "It's honest work."

Honest. I nearly laughed. It came up my throat like a swallowed pin, and I turned it into a cough, and bent lower over the carriage clock as though its malfunction required my immediate and total attention.

"Mira doesn't think it's honest," Emilio said, wounded, because my brother had the unerring instinct of the young to detect condescension even through plaster walls of silence. "Look at her face."

"My face is concentrating."

"Your face always does that when you think someone's being stupid."

"Then it's working overtime today."

"Children," Father said mildly, and the mildness had a blade in it. "Emilio. When do you sail?"

"Friday. High tide, Mr. Oshiro said. Seven days out, back the following week. Kagoshima and back with machine parts." He beamed. "Machine parts, Mira! Maybe you can tell me what they are so I sound clever."

Machine parts. I thought of the timing device I had been summoned to repair in a room that smelled of cigarettes and cologne, the device whose escapement synchronized itself to patrol rotations, the device whose design I could now draw from memory with my eyes closed, and had, several times, on scraps of paper I burned afterward in the shop stove like love letters from a man I hated.

"They're probably gears," I said. "Everything is gears."

"Gears," Emilio repeated reverently, as though I had said *stars*.

* * *

That night I lay awake doing arithmetic, which is what I did instead of praying, having concluded sometime around my fourteenth year that God kept irregular hours but numbers never slept.

The facts, arranged on the bench of my mind:

One. The *Kanagawa Maru* sailed on Vasquez's schedule. I had seen the schedule. I could still see it, printed in the neat block capitals of my memory alongside the serial number of the timing mechanism, because my mind had decided without consulting me that certain things would be retained whether I wished it or not.

Two. Vasquez knew I had found the letter. He had told me so himself, in a voice like warm oil, offering me protection in exchange for silence and skill. Protection, he had called it. I had learned since then what happened to people under Colonel Vasquez's protection: they disappeared, or they worked, or — in the case of three dockworkers whose names Arai had spoken to me across my own counter — they simply stopped appearing anywhere at all.

Three. My brother was now employed on Vasquez's ship, holding papers stamped by an office Vasquez effectively owned, sailing on a schedule designed to avoid the patrols Vasquez bribed, carrying cargo whose manifests Vasquez dictated.

Four. Arrests were coming. I did not know when. Arai did not know when. But the machinery was tightening — I could feel it the way a clockmaker feels a bearing beginning to bind: not yet seized, but no longer running free.

And five, which was the fact I kept returning to the way a tongue returns to a cracked tooth: boys who worked one season on a smuggler's ship had a way of becoming men who worked many seasons on it, because the sea pays better than honesty and Vasquez's ships never wanted for cargo. Emilio would learn the routes. He would learn the signs — which crates went aboard at night, which inspectors looked away, which tides meant money. He was fifteen and luminous with joy about all of it, and the machine did not care. That was the thing about machines, I thought, staring at the ceiling: they do not care what they grind. They grind what is put into them. Someone had put my brother in.

I got up. I dressed. I sat at my bench in the dark and did not light a lamp, because lamplight at two in the morning asks questions, and instead I held the carriage clock's balance wheel between finger and thumb and let its oscillations slow against my skin until it stopped entirely, and I listened to our house settle around its sleeping secrets, and I understood with perfect clarity that I had perhaps four or five days to get my brother off that ship, and no earthly idea how.

You cannot tell a boy of fifteen that his dream smells of diesel and corruption. He will hear none of it. Worse — he will repeat it. At dinner, at the harbor office, to Mr. Oshiro, whose reports went upward like smoke. I knew now how information moved through this city: not in letters sealed in brass, though those existed, but in small careless sentences spoken by people who believed doors were doors.

* * *

Wednesday evening, Arai came in.

I want to be fair to him here, because history — my history, the small private version I keep wound inside me like a second movement — has been kinder to Kenji Arai than I deserved to be. But that Wednesday evening, when the bell above my door rang and I looked up from a customer's wristwatch and saw him standing there in his gray coat with his hat in his hand, my first feeling was not warmth. It was the specific dread of a person who has left a watch running on a man's desk with a note containing a date and a warehouse number, and has spent every hour since calculating the blast radius.

He had received the note eleven days earlier. The raid on Warehouse Nine had come six nights ago. It had failed. Vasquez had burned the evidence before Arai's men reached the second floor, and Arai had spent the days since suspended, technically, pending review — though he still wore the gray coat and still carried himself like a man with authority, which told me either that the suspension was theater or that Arai considered paperwork a formality invented by people who had never been in actual danger.

Neither of these possibilities comforted me.

"You're closed," he observed, noting the sign, which I had flipped out of habit rather than intention.

"I'm winding down."

"May I wind up?"

"That's terrible," I said, and he smiled, and I hated how much room the smile took up in the shop. "We're closed. But you're already inside, so the point is academic."

He set his repaired watch on the counter — the one I had fixed weeks ago, the pretext that had long since worn through like cheap plating. He did not pick it up. He just rested his hand beside it, which was, I understood, his way of resting a hand on a table in an interrogation room.

"Three names came off the harbor rolls this week," he said quietly. "Clerks. Men who processed manifests for the coastal line."

My hands, which had been cleaning a pivot, went still. Clerks. Not dockworkers now — clerks. The machine was eating its own paper trail, and the paper trail was made of people.

"One of them was a Mr. Oshiro," Arai continued.

The shop became very quiet. Somewhere in the walls, the shop clock ticked on with the indifference of its kind, keeping time for a family that might not have much of it left.

"He signed my brother's papers," I heard myself say. Which was stupid, which was handing him a thread, but fear makes poor craftsmen of us all.

Arai looked at me for a long moment. Outside, a tram sang past on its wire; gulls argued over something in the gutter; the ordinary city went on performing its ordinary evening, magnificent and oblivious.

"Mirabel." He said my name the way he wound watches — precisely, without hurry. "Whatever you know about the *Kanagawa Maru*, whatever you suspect about why your brother's employment matters enough to make you look like that — you need to tell me before Friday."

"And if I don't?"

"Then Friday comes anyway. It always does. That's the one thing about time nobody needs a clockmaker to explain."

He left his hat on the counter by mistake, or not by mistake, and walked out into the blue dusk, and I stood there among my clocks with my heart going like a flywheel gone loose, understanding two things with terrible clarity.

The first was that I had maybe forty-eight hours to pull my brother out of the machine before the machine closed around him, and that telling him the truth would endanger everything — Father, the berth I did not yet know existed, the fragile conspiracy of silence holding my family together like solder on a cracked joint.

The second was that Emilio was upstairs right now, humming, packing a seabag with a rope coil and a paperback novel, glowing like a lamp with a fresh wick, and that the next person to speak to him about that ship would decide what he believed about it for the rest of his life.

I climbed the stairs. He was sitting cross-legged on his bed amid the glorious chaos of departure, and when he saw my face he grinned and said:

"Mira, you won't believe what Mr. Oshiro told me about the cargo hold—"
