## 11. The Berth

*Mirabel*

My father had a way of announcing terrible news the way he announced dinner — with his coat already off and his hands folded, as though the words were simply another course and we would be ungrateful not to eat them.

He waited until Emilio had finished his second bowl of rice and I had refilled the tea, which I understood later was deliberate. Tomás Sandoval did not deliver catastrophes to empty stomachs. He believed, with the same stubbornness he brought to escapements, that a body fed could survive almost anything told to it.

"There is a ship," he said.

Emilio looked up at once, the way he always did at the word *ship*, like a compass needle finding north. "Which one? The *Kaiyo Maru*? Is it taking apprentices again?"

"It is taking one passenger." My father's eyes came to me and stayed there, and something in my chest went cold before he even said the rest. "One berth. Leaving on the morning tide, four days from now. Before—" He stopped. He never stopped mid-sentence; it was one of the things I had counted on about him all my life, that his sentences arrived whole like finished work. "Before certain difficulties arrive in this city."

I set down the teapot very carefully, because my hands wanted to shake and I would not give them the satisfaction.

"Difficulties," I said.

"Arrests, Mirabel."

The word sat on the table between us like a dropped gear. Emilio's spoon stopped halfway to his mouth.

"Arrests of whom?" Emilio asked, and there it was — the question I had been carrying for weeks like a stone sewn into my lining, and now it was out in the open air of our kitchen where anyone might trip over it.

"Of men involved in harbor business," my father said. "Men whose names are written in places they should not be. Colonel Vasquez has arranged—"

"Vasquez." I said the name flatly, and watched my father flinch. Good, some petty part of me thought. Let him flinch. "The man who owns the tide tables. The man who offered me a job fixing timing mechanisms and made it very clear that refusing was not among the options. That man has arranged a berth on a ship."

"One berth," my father said quietly. "For one of us. To go somewhere the law cannot follow. It is a gift, Mirabel."

"It is an arithmetic problem," I said. "That is what it is. Three Sandovals, one seat, and a colonel doing sums in the background. Gifts don't come with conditions like that."

"Mira," Emilio said, using the name he hadn't used since he was small enough to fit inside the shop's delivery crate. "What did you do?"

And there was the cruelest part of the evening, the part I still turn over at night like a coin worn smooth: my brother asked *what did you do* and not *what did Papa do*, because in Emilio's ledger of the world, our father was weather and I was the one who made things happen. I had fixed the master clock. I had come home strange and quiet. Whatever storm was coming, Emilio assumed I had built it, gear by gear, the way I built everything.

"I found a letter," I said, because lying to Emilio had become impossible somewhere along the way without my noticing. "Inside the observatory clock. A letter with our name in it."

Our father closed his eyes. In the lamplight he looked suddenly old in a way I had trained myself not to see — the gray at his temples, the tremor he hid by holding cups with both hands.

"You were meant to leave," he said. "That was the design. You take the berth, you vanish clean, and the rest of us face whatever comes with the family name intact somewhere else in the world. One seed survives the frost. That is how it has always worked."

"That is how *seeds* work," I said. "We are people, Papa. People are worse at this."

* * *

*Tomás*

I had rehearsed the speech for two days. I want that recorded somewhere, if anything of mine is ever recorded: I rehearsed it, walking the long way home past the shuttered warehouses, mouthing the words to myself like a schoolboy with a recitation. I am a man who believes that most disasters can be prevented with sufficient preparation, and I prepared this one down to the pauses.

It fell apart in four minutes. My daughter dismantled it the way she dismantles clocks — gently, without breaking a single part, until the whole mechanism lay exposed and useless on the table.

She said, "Who decided it should be you who tells us? Did Vasquez tell you to say it just this way?"

And I said, "He said a family that argues tears itself apart before the law even touches it."

And she said, "Then he understands families better than you think, and that should frighten you more than it does."

I had no answer for that. I drank my tea, which had gone cold, because drinking gave my hands employment and my hands, left idle, wanted to reach across the table and take her face in them the way I did when she was six and feverish, and tell her that Papa would fix it, Papa fixes everything, Papa has never in his life left a broken thing unrepaired.

But I had repaired nothing. That was the truth under all of it. I had built the machine that was now grinding toward my children, and Vasquez had handed me one escape hatch out of it, and the escape hatch was large enough for exactly one, and he knew — he must have known — what such arithmetic does to a family. It was not mercy. It was a demonstration. *Look what I can give,* the berth said. *Look what I can withhold.* And whichever Sandoval sailed away would carry the lesson across the water forever.

Emilio spoke then, and I confess his voice startled me. He had been so quiet, pushing rice around his bowl, that I'd half forgotten him.

"I'll go," he said.

"No," Mirabel said.

"You haven't even heard the terms—"

"There are no terms worth hearing. You're sixteen."

"I'm sixteen and I've been working the docks since spring and nobody there thinks I'm a child." His jaw had set in a way I recognized with a lurch, because it was his mother's jaw, set exactly that way the day she signed herself into the clinic and told us not to visit until she looked like herself again. "Papa. Tell her I can do it. Tell her I'm the one who should go."

And here is what I have not told anyone, what I will perhaps never tell anyone: for one full second — I counted it, I count things — I wanted to agree. Because Emilio is young and strong and adaptable, because he would land in some foreign port and be hired within a week, because grief slides off the young like rain off oiled canvas. Because Mirabel is the one who keeps us stitched together and I did not know how the cloth would hold with her gone.

Because, if I am honest down to the brass, Emilio going hurt less to imagine than Mirabel going.

One second. Then I saw his mother's jaw again and hated myself with a thoroughness that surprised me.

"Nobody is deciding tonight," I said. "Nobody decides anything tonight."

"Papa—"

"*Tonight*, nobody decides." I stood, and my knees complained, and I let them, because a father's aching knees are sometimes the only honest argument he has left. "We sleep. We eat breakfast tomorrow like a family that isn't being divided into portions. And then we talk."

Mirabel looked at me across the cold tea and the cooling rice and the lamp burning its small determined flame, and she said, "You already know who you want it to be."

I did. God forgive me, I did. But I said, "Good night, Mira," and went to my room, and lay awake listening to the house tick and settle around me like a clock I had wound carelessly and could not stop.

* * *

*Emilio*

Here is what nobody at that table understood, because nobody at that table was sixteen and in love with the ocean: when Papa said *a ship*, my heart did the thing it always does, the lift, the lean, like a gull catching wind. And then everyone started talking about arrests and berths and who deserved to be saved, and my heart kept doing the lift anyway, and I hated it. I hated that some traitor part of me heard *escape on a ship* and sang.

But I meant it. That's the thing I need written down too, since apparently everyone in this family keeps records. When I said *I'll go*, I meant it.

Mira said I was too young, which is funny, because Mira has spent my whole life telling me I'm old enough — old enough to sweep the shop, old enough to mind the counter, old enough to hear about money troubles in the careful code adults use. Old enough when it's useful, too young when it counts. I didn't say that. I'm learning when not to say things. It seems to be the family trade.

After Papa went to his room, Mira washed the dishes, which is what she does instead of crying or shouting. She washes like she repairs — completely, methodically, as if the dishes' feelings matter. I dried. We didn't talk for a while. The harbor foghorn went off twice, far away, patient as ever.

"If I go," I said finally, "you'd write to me. Both of you. Real letters, not those three-line things."

Mira's hands stopped in the water.

"And you'd tell me the truth in them," I said. "Whatever the truth turns out to be. About the letter, about Vasquez, about what Papa's been doing at the warehouses at night — yes, Mira, I know about that too, I work the docks, I see things, everybody assumes the boy sees nothing."

She turned around slowly, dripping. For a second she looked so much like Mama that my throat closed.

"When did you get wise?" she said.

"About a year ago. Nobody noticed."

Something happened to her face then — not a smile, but the place a smile comes from. She flicked suds at me, which is how our family says *I love you* when the regular words won't fit through the door.

"Sleep," she said. "We'll fight about it tomorrow. Properly. With diagrams, probably. Papa will make diagrams."

"He will," I said. "He'll have them labeled."

I went up to my room and lay there listening to the foghorn and thinking about the ship. Four days. Morning tide. Somewhere out there past the breakwater was a vessel with one empty bunk and a name line waiting to be filled, and my whole family was going to spend four days doing arithmetic with each other's lives, and the sea didn't care, the sea never cares, that's why I love it.

Then I heard Mira's floorboards. Not going to bed — going *out*. Down the stairs, soft as a repairman, and the front door easing shut behind her, and the street swallowing her footsteps.

I gave her ten minutes. Then I got my coat.

* * *

*Mirabel*

I told myself I was going out to walk, which was true in the way that a clock's hands are technically moving forward while the mechanism underneath is doing something else entirely.

I walked toward the harbor. Of course I walked toward the harbor. Every road in Nagasaki slopes toward the water eventually; it's the one direction this city cannot help.

I needed to see the ship. That was the whole of it. Somewhere behind customs sheds and mooring lines was the vessel that held our family's future in a single berth, and I am my father's daughter, and I cannot reason about a thing I have not examined. Show me the mechanism. Let me look at the parts.

The night watchman at the pier knew me by sight — the clockmaker's girl, the one who services the harbor office regulator — and waved me through with the indifference of a man paid not to be curious. I stood at the rail in the fog-smell and the rope-smell and found her: a coastal freighter, black-hulled, riding low, her running lights doubled in the black water. The *Kaiyo Maru*. Emilio had known her name before Papa said it. Of course he had.

One berth. Three names. And a colonel somewhere warm and well-lit, watching the equation resolve itself, learning exactly what each of us was worth.

I gripped the rail and did sums of my own. If I took it, Papa would face arrest alone and Emilio would grow up parentless in a house of silence. If Papa took it — I laughed aloud at that, one short ugly note; Tomás Sandoval would sooner flee than abandon a debt, and besides, Vasquez would never allow the architect to vanish with his knowledge. If Emilio took it—

If Emilio took it, he lived. He lived, and he carried nothing, and someday he would ask his sister in letters what had happened, and I would decide, letter by letter, how much truth a saved life could bear.

Behind me, a board creaked on the pier.

I turned, hand already closing on the little wrench in my coat pocket — habit, armor, absurdity — and saw Emilio standing in the fog with his collar up, caught, sixteen, and wearing the exact expression I imagined was on my own face.

"You followed me," we said, at the same time.

Above us, the freighter's deck lights burned steady against the dark, and out on the water the tide was already turning, indifferent, punctual, right on schedule — carrying the whole harbor, whether it consented or not, toward morning.
