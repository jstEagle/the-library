## 13. A Watch Left Running

The watch took me four days, which was three days longer than it should have.

I want to be honest about that, because honesty — real honesty, the kind with edges on it — was in short supply that week. A craftsman would have finished Kenji Arai's watch in a day and a half: mainspring replaced, escapement cleaned, the balance wheel trued and poised. Simple work for anyone whose hands didn't shake every time they thought about what they were about to do.

But my hands did shake. So I slowed down. I made slowness into a discipline, the way my father taught me — *a steady hand is a hand that has decided not to hurry* — and I sat at my bench under the lamp each night after the shop closed, working the same small gears over and over until they were cleaner than any customer had a right to expect, because if I could not be brave I could at least be precise.

That's the thing nobody tells you about betrayal. It doesn't look like a knife in the dark. It looks like a watch, polished too well. It looks like a note written eleven times and burned ten of them. It looks like a girl sitting very still in a lamplight circle, listening to her own heartbeat count seconds she can't take back.

* * *

Let me tell you what I knew by then, so you understand what I was carrying when I climbed the observatory hill.

I knew about the timing device. Colonel Vasquez's beautiful, vicious little machine, the one I'd spent two afternoons repairing while he watched me from across the room with the courtesy of a man feeding something he intended to eat later. I knew its design the way you know a face you've seen once in a nightmare — every cam, every contact point, the way it married the harbor patrol schedules to the tide tables and spat out windows of darkness where crates could move without witnesses. I hadn't written anything down. I didn't need to. My mother used to say I kept things in my head the way other people keep them in trunks, and for once in my life that inheritance felt like a weapon instead of a burden.

I knew about the three dockworkers. Kenji had told me their names — or told me there were names, which was worse, because names meant wives, meant children, meant someone standing at a door at dusk waiting for footsteps that weren't coming. He hadn't said Vasquez's name. He hadn't needed to say mine either, though I heard it anyway, underneath his careful questions: *Are you afraid of someone, Sandoval-san?*

Yes, I had wanted to say. Yes, and also I am more afraid of myself, of the woman I am becoming, who winds a smuggler's clockwork with these two capable hands and says nothing while men disappear into the water like dropped coins.

And I knew about the berth.

One berth. One name. That was the arithmetic my father had laid out at our kitchen table with the desperate tenderness of a man dividing a single loaf among five mouths. Emilio glowed when he talked about the freighter now, about salt air and wages and being *useful*, and every time he glowed something in me curdled, because I knew what ship he'd signed onto, and I knew whose machine kept that ship's comings and goings invisible.

So here is what I finally understood, sitting at my bench with Arai's watch open under the lamp like a small silver confession:

Silence wasn't keeping us safe. Silence was just deciding, night after night, which strangers got to drown so that my family could float a little longer. And the harbor didn't forget. The harbor kept everything — that was its whole nature, its whole terrible gift. Every crate, every false manifest, every missing man, filed away in salt and stone. If I stayed silent, the harbor would remember without me, and it would remember *me*, too, as one more person who saw and said nothing.

I couldn't allow that. Not even to save us.

Especially not to save us.

* * *

The problem was method.

You cannot simply walk up to an investigator of the harbor authority and say, *Good morning, I have been repairing machinery for a colonel who arranges murders, please arrest everyone including possibly my father.* Words spoken aloud can be denied, twisted, traced back. Words get people disappeared — I had three dockworkers' worth of evidence for how that worked. Whatever I gave Kenji Arai had to arrive the way my best work arrived: unimpeachable, self-contained, impossible to disavow once received.

A watch, I decided. His watch, repaired, returned. No one could question a craftsman finishing a job. And inside — no, not inside, I wasn't going to hide anything in the case; that was my family's trick, hiding letters in mechanisms, and look how well it had gone for all of us — but *with* it, a note. Nothing elaborate. Elaboration was confession's cousin.

A date. A warehouse number. Nothing else.

Warehouse Nine. The date was the next run — I knew it from the timing device itself, from the window of darkness the machine opened on the night schedule, a gap wide enough to drive a harbor through. I had memorized it against my will, the way you memorize the face of someone shouting at you.

Eleven drafts, I told you. Let me spare you ten of them. They ranged from the cowardly (*I may have information of interest*, which would earn me exactly one polite interview and one visit from Vasquez's men) to the suicidal (an actual account of everything, in my handwriting, naming my father in ink that could be matched to any letter I'd ever written a supplier). What survived the burning was this:

**Pier 9. Thursday. After midnight.**

Six words. I checked the ink dry, folded the paper twice, and held it over the lamp flame long enough to scorch the edge — not for drama, though I'll admit the drama steadied me, but so it would look handled, incidental, a scrap that might have blown off some dock ledger rather than been composed at a bench by a girl with shaking hands.

Then I put it in the watch box, closed the lid, and sat there listening to my heart hammer out a rhythm no escapement would certify as accurate.

* * *

I almost didn't do it.

I want that on the record, wherever records are kept for people like me. At the door of the shop, with the box in my coat pocket pressing against my ribs like a second heartbeat, I stopped. The morning fog was coming off the water in gray ropes, and somewhere below a ship's horn moaned, and I stood there thinking: *Turn around. Go home. Wind the clocks. Let the world arrange itself.*

Because here was the truth under all my fine resolutions: what I was about to do might destroy my father. Not metaphorically. The arrests, when they came, would come for him first — he was the architect, Kenji would see to it, the law would see to it, and my note would be the keystone that brought the whole arch down on our own house. Emilio's berth would evaporate. My mother's debts, already paid in blood and smuggling, would have been paid for nothing.

And yet.

My mother again. She kept surfacing in my thoughts that season like a coin rising through bathwater. I remembered — God help me, I remembered — her last lucid week, when the morphine loosened her tongue and her fear in equal measure, when she gripped my wrist with those papery hands and said, *Mirabel. When they come asking questions in this country, the ones who stay quiet are the ones who are guilty of something.* I'd thought she was delirious. I'd thought it was the fever talking about old grudges, about the creditors, about the way the world had ground our family thin.

Maybe it was. But fever-truth is still truth sometimes. And I had been quiet for months, and I was drowning in guilt the way she'd drowned in debt, and both of us knew — whatever *both of us* means between the living and the dead — that quiet was not innocence. Quiet was compounding interest.

I walked down the hill toward the harbor authority offices.

* * *

Kenji Arai's desk, I learned from a clerk I bribed with nothing more than a repaired alarm clock and a shy smile, sat in the second room on the left past the registry. He arrived at seven forty each morning and left at six, except Thursdays, when he went to the docks himself to walk the manifests. Of course he did. Of course the man who wound watches also walked the piers, checking the world's pulse with his own feet. I found this unbearably endearing and refused to examine why.

Thursday morning, then. But not Thursday evening — the note named Thursday *after midnight*, meaning Wednesday's small hours were the run itself, and if I delivered the note Thursday morning he'd have less than a day. Too tight. Too suspicious. A trap smells like a trap when it's rushed.

Wednesday morning, then. Give him thirty-six hours. Enough time to verify, to position men, to do whatever investigators do besides bring broken watches to young women who lie badly.

I dressed plainly. I carried the box openly, because a hidden parcel reads as contraband and a visible one reads as errand. The fog had lifted into a hard bright cold, the kind of morning where Nagasaki shows you its bones — the masts, the cranes, the terraced hills stacked with roofs like drawers in a cabinet. Beautiful. I hated how beautiful it was. I hated that I loved this city that had eaten my family's honor and was chewing.

At the office door, the clerk — a boy named Oshiro who owed me for the alarm clock and knew it — nodded me through without logging the visit. Small kindnesses, small corruptions; the whole city ran on them; that was rather the point of everything I'd discovered.

His door was closed. Through the frosted glass, his silhouette bent over papers.

For one long moment I stood in the corridor holding a box containing a perfect watch and an imperfect act, and I listened to my own breathing, and I understood with total clarity that once I knocked, or once I set the box down and walked away — and walking away was the plan, leaving it was the plan, anonymity was the only armor I'd ever afford — there was no version of my life that came back from this. There was before-the-note Mirabel and after-the-note Mirabel, and the hinge between them was a scrap of scorched paper with six words on it.

I thought of Emilio's face when he talked about the sea.

I thought of the dockworkers' doors, opening at dusk onto nobody.

I thought: *Both of those things are true, and I can only spend my loyalty on one of them.*

I set the box on the floor beside his door — not on the desk; I lost my nerve for the desk, forgive me, I was brave but I was not yet brave enough to enter a room — and I placed it precisely, square to the wall, the way I'd square a clock to a mantel. Craftsmanship to the end. Then I turned and walked, fast, not running, never running, down the corridor, past Oshiro, who looked up and wisely asked nothing, out into the cold bright street where the harbor lay glittering and oblivious and full of everything it remembered.

* * *

I did not go home. Home held Tomás's tired eyes and Emilio's shining plans and every surface of our house seemed lately to be upholstered in secrets. Instead I walked the length of the waterfront like a penitent, past Warehouse Nine itself, squat and gray behind its chain, where tonight the crates would move in the dark window my own hands had cut for them.

Here is the terrible part. Here is the part I've never said aloud, not even to Kenji, not even years later when saying it would have cost nothing:

Standing there in front of that warehouse, having done the deed, having lit the fuse — I felt *good*.

Not triumphant. Not righteous. Good the way a fever breaks good, the way a tooth stops aching good. For four months I had carried the letter, the knowledge, the machine's design, the whole rotting cargo of it, alone, in a body too small for the weight. And now it was out of me. Six words of it, anyway, traveling through the city's bloodstream toward the one man paid to care. Terror was still there — terror sat in my chest like a swallowed stone, and would sit there for days, and would stand up and walk around at three in the morning whispering *Vasquez knows, Vasquez always knows* — but under the terror ran this clean cold current of relief, and I floated on it, appalled at myself, grateful, ashamed, alive.

I bought sweet chestnuts from the old woman by the fish market and ate them burning hot and tasted nothing. I watched gulls fight over a fish head. I existed, obscenely, ordinarily, in a world where I had just betrayed my blood for an abstraction called truth, and the chestnut seller wished me a good day, and I said, "And you," and meant nothing by it and everything.

* * *

By evening the fear had organized itself into scenarios, which is what fear does when you're a person who thinks in systems.

Scenario one: Kenji finds the box, reads the note, dismisses it as a crank's nonsense. Result: nothing changes, I've betrayed my family for a drawer in a filing office.

Scenario two: he takes it seriously, raids the warehouse, finds only legitimate cargo, because Vasquez moved the run the moment the timing device came back from repair — because Vasquez assumed, correctly, that the girl who fixed his machine might have memorized it. Result: nothing changes, and Vasquez sends someone to my shop to discuss discretion.

Scenario three: it works. Men come. Evidence is taken. Names are named. And the first name, the loudest name, the name at the center of the arch —

I didn't finish that scenario. I let it sit unfinished at the table of my mind like an unwelcome guest, growing louder.

Tomás's face kept arriving behind my eyes. Not angry — that I could have borne. *Disappointed*, the way he looked at a mechanism someone else had botched: sorrowful, professional, already calculating what it would cost to make it right. *You were supposed to fix things,* that face said. *It's what your hands are for.*

And my hands had fixed things. His watch was perfect. I'd tested it against the regulator three times; it would run true within two seconds a day for years. Somewhere in a harbor office, in a box on a floor beside a door, was the most honest piece of work I'd ever produced, ticking steadily toward a Thursday after midnight.

I slept — or lay down adjacent to sleep — and woke before dawn, and opened the shop early, and puttered among the clocks like a ghost haunting her own life. Every chime made me flinch. At nine-fifteen, the bell over the door rang and my whole body became a struck bell itself—

—and it was Mrs. Hattori, collecting her mantel clock, complaining about the damp, paying in exact change, existing in the ordinary world where daughters did not burn their families down with six words.

At ten, the bell rang again, and it was a child with a broken cuckoo, and I fixed it while barely hearing his mother's chatter, and at noon I ate rice I don't remember tasting, and at half past one the bell rang and I said "We're — yes, hello, we're open" to the empty doorway of my own cowardice, because it was only wind.

He didn't come that day. Or the next.

Which meant, I told myself, nothing. Investigators were busy. Notes took time. Perhaps he'd sent it up the chain, perhaps men in better coats than mine were already moving through the harbor's veins toward Pier 9.

Or perhaps the box sat unopened. Perhaps Oshiro had swept it aside. Perhaps the clerk's kindness had a price I'd learn about later, in a room with Vasquez in it, being thanked for my craftsmanship.

Two days. I gave it two days before the fear curdled into something harder and more useful: resolve. If the note failed, I would go to him myself. Face to face. All of it — the letter, the machine, the designs, the dates, my father, everything, poured out like a mainspring spilled across a bench. I'd lose everything, but I'd lose it *telling the truth*, and there were worse inheritances.

On the third morning, the bell rang, and I looked up expecting Mrs. Hattori again, or wind, or ruin —

— and Kenji Arai stood in my doorway with his hat in his hands and an expression I had never seen on him before. Not the investigator. Not the watch customer. Something rawer, held carefully, the way you hold a mechanism you've just realized matters far more than its size suggests.

"Sandoval-san," he said quietly, and closed the door behind him. "We should talk about Warehouse Nine."
