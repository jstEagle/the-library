## 16. The Night Before the Ship

I did not sleep that night, which is the habit of liars and clockmakers both — we sit up listening for the moment when something stops.

I had left Arai's watch on his desk at the harbor office with a note bearing a date and a warehouse number. Eleven words, if you counted the number as one word, which in my accounting it was. Eleven words to undo everything my father had built and everything Colonel Vasquez had protected, eleven words that weighed more than the master clock I'd spent three days repairing, and I carried them out of my shop the way you carry anything too heavy — badly, breathing wrong, glancing over your shoulder at people who were only buying rice.

That was Tuesday. The raid was set for Wednesday night. Which means I spent two full days doing the worst thing a person can do: waiting while pretending to work.

A customer brought in a mantel clock that ran fast by four minutes a day, and I told him it was the escapement, which was true, but I adjusted it twice because my hands wanted occupation and not accuracy. Emilio came home smelling of tar and salt and glory, talking about the freighter's departure, how the first mate had let him stand at the wheel, how he'd felt the ship answer him like a living thing. I smiled at my brother. I want that recorded somewhere: even then, with the machinery of ruin already turning, I smiled at him, because he was seventeen and happy and I did not yet know how few of those hours he had left on this particular dock.

Father ate his dinner in silence and watched me eat mine in silence, and between us the table held its usual cargo — rice, pickled radish, a candle stub burning down — plus everything unsaid, which has always been the heaviest freight in our family's manifests.

"You're thin," he said finally.

"You're rich," I said, before I could stop myself, and he flinched the way a man flinches when a gear tooth catches, and neither of us apologized, because apology would have required naming what we both knew.

* * *

Wednesday night I told them I had mending to finish and sat in the shop with the lamp turned low, watching the street through the gap in the shutter. A clockmaker's shop is a good place to wait in. Everything around you is counting, patiently, without judgment, and after a while you begin to believe the night has a mechanism too, and that if you could just see its works you would know exactly when the hammer falls.

The warehouse district lay east, toward the water. From my window I couldn't see it, only the glow of the harbor lamps reflected off low cloud, and so I did what any sensible woman would do, which is nothing, and hated myself with great efficiency while doing it.

I imagined the raid the way Arai had described such things once, in the shop, in his careful voice, when he still pretended his visits were about watches: men moving quiet through fog, lanterns hooded, the manifest books seized before anyone could feed them to a stove. I imagined Vasquez's timing device sitting in an evidence room, its little brass heart exposed, telling investigators everything I'd memorized about patrol schedules and tide tables and which nights the *Kagerō-maru* ran dark.

I had done the arithmetic a dozen times. Three dockworkers missing. Twelve smaller families ruined in Mother's ledger. One brother on a ship owned by the man who owned the tide. Against all of it: eleven words, one date, one warehouse number. It came out correct. It came out correct the way my clocks run correct — beautifully, uselessly, in a world that does not care what time it says.

Somewhere past midnight I heard boots. Not many. Moving with purpose, westbound, toward the offices rather than the docks, and my stomach understood before my mind did that boots going *that* direction at *that* hour are not boots going toward a victory.

* * *

Thursday morning I opened the shop because opening the shop is what I do; it is the one sentence I know how to write in any weather.

The news arrived by newspaper first, which told me how confident someone was: nothing on the front page at all. Then by rumor, which travels faster than print and lies better. By noon I had the shape of it. Investigators had gone to Warehouse Nine on the night indicated. They found it swept clean — floor bare, cradles empty, the air still holding the ghost of coal smoke where ledgers had been burned in a brazier, someone said, burned *recently*, burned with the thoroughness of a man who reads other people's mail.

They found, instead of contraband, a single crate of machine parts, properly manifested, dutifully inspected, stamped with the naval office's own approval seal.

And they found Kenji Arai standing in an empty warehouse holding a warrant signed on information from an unnamed source, looking like a man who had wound a watch for a year only to discover the mainspring had been cut before he ever touched it.

By afternoon the rumor had grown teeth. An inquiry into the source of the intelligence. Questions about why Investigator Arai pursued a private theory against the judgment of his superiors. By evening, suspended, pending review — the word *pending* doing the kind of quiet work that pending words do in empires.

He came to the shop at dusk.

I knew before he spoke. There is a way a man carries suspension — not like disgrace, exactly, more like a coat that no longer fits, worn anyway because there is nothing else in the wardrobe. He stood in my doorway with his hat in his hands and did not come in, which was its own message.

"They were waiting," he said. "Or rather, they weren't, which was the tell. Warehouse Nine was never the real site. It was the site we were meant to find."

"I gave you—"

"You gave me truth," he said. "Accurate truth. That's what frightens me. Someone fed me accurate truth about a false target, which means either your information was old, or someone read your note." He looked at me then, and his eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "Mirabel. Who else knew?"

The lamp behind me ticked. My father's face crossed through my mind — Tomás, who had begged me at low tide to choose family over truth, who had gone pale as bone when I told him what I intended, who had known, *known*, the way a man knows weather in his knee, that Vasquez would smell the betrayal before the ink dried.

"No one," I said.

It was a lie. We both heard it. He didn't press, and I will be grateful for that gentleness until I die, because pressing would have broken me open right there among the pendulums, and what spilled out would have drowned us both.

"Then here is what happens now," he said quietly. "I am off the case. Formally. If I come near the harbor records again they'll have me for more than insubordination. And the names of people who assist suspended investigators have a way of migrating onto lists." He put his hat back on, slowly. "You should hear me say this plainly: I cannot protect you anymore. I could barely protect you before. But whatever you do next, do it knowing the cost is no longer hypothetical."

"Arai." My voice came out smaller than I intended. "Did it buy anything? Any of it? Even one day, one crate stopped, one—"

He was silent a long moment.

"It bought me the certainty that Vasquez is guilty," he said. "Which I had. And it bought you the attention of men who now know exactly how much you know." He stepped back into the street. "Truth told late isn't worthless, Mirabel. But it isn't free, either. Right now you're paying retail."

He walked away toward the water, and I watched him go, and the shop was very quiet, and every clock in it kept perfect time, indifferent, immaculate, useless.

* * *

The list came to me the way these things come — sideways, through a frightened mouth.

Mrs. Oshiro, whose husband runs the chandlery, came in near closing to collect a wall clock she'd left for cleaning, and she lingered, and she wrung her hands, and finally she said, very fast, staring at the counter, that her husband drinks with a clerk from the harbor police office, and that the clerk had shown the table a paper, and that the paper had names on it, names of persons to be detained when the order comes, and that Sandoval was on the paper. Both Sandovals. Father and daughter.

She left the money on the counter without waiting for change and hurried out as though the shop itself might incriminate her.

I stood alone among my clocks and did the thing I do when I cannot move: I counted. Escapements ticking. Second hands sweeping. Somewhere across town a church bell rang six o'clock, slightly slow, and some distant part of me noted that it needed regulating, and I thought: *there is a version of me that goes on forever noticing things like that, small corrections, small kindnesses of craft, while the large gears of the world grind everyone I love into powder.*

My name on a list. Written down. Reduced to seven letters in a clerk's hand, filed in a drawer in a building owned by a man who owns the tide.

I thought of Mother then — not the sainted memory Father invokes at dinner, but the actual woman, sharp-tongued and frightened at the end, hiding bills under the tea caddy because she couldn't bear for Emilio to see them. She would have understood lists. She lived her last years on one — creditors' lists, doctors' lists, the list of treatments they could no longer afford. Debt was her arrest warrant, and it came for her on schedule, and nobody raided the warehouse of it in time.

And I understood, standing there, what I had actually purchased with my eleven words. Not justice. Justice requires evidence, and evidence burns, and men like Vasquez keep spare matches. What I had purchased was exposure. I had lifted the curtain an inch, seen the machine whole, and then been forced to stand in front of it announcing myself.

Truth told too late buys nothing. Arai said it wasn't worthless. He was being kind. Kindness is a currency too, and I was learning its exchange rate.

* * *

I walked home through streets going blue with evening. Nagasaki at dusk is beautiful the way a loaded spring is beautiful — all that tension dressed up in lamplight and gulls. Fishing boats coming in with their catch. Soldiers at the tram stop, young, laughing about something. A boy selling roasted chestnuts whose cart wheel needed oiling so badly I nearly stopped to fix it, because fixing things is my prayer, and God knows I needed to pray at something that would hold still.

Emilio was on the step when I arrived, sitting with his knees up, waiting. His face in the lamplight was still boyish, still lit from inside by ships, and seeing it hurt worse than the list had.

"There you are," he said. "Papa's been pacing. He got papers today — berth papers, real ones, from the shipping office. He wouldn't show me. He's been drinking the sake Mrs. Tanaka gave us at New Year's and staring at the wall."

"Papers," I repeated.

"One berth," Emilio said, and his voice went strange, older. "Miri, I can read upside down. I've been reading upside down since I was six. The name line is blank."

The word *blank* hung in the air between us like a struck bell that refuses to finish ringing.

Inside, Father sat at the table with a document before him and a pen beside it and his hands flat on either side, the posture of a man who has been sitting in front of an impossible choice long enough that the choice has worn a groove into the wood. The candle had burned down to its collar. He looked up at me, and I saw that he knew about the list — of course he knew, Vasquez would have made sure he knew, that is how men like Vasquez negotiate, they send the bill ahead of the goods — and I saw something worse than anger in his face.

Relief. Terrible relief. Because now the arithmetic was simple. Now there was only one name worth writing, and every hour we sat there arguing, the ship drew closer to its tide.

"Miri," he said, and pushed the pen an inch toward me across the table. "Sit down. We have until dawn."
