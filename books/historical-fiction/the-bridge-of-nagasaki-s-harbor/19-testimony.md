## 19. Testimony

The naval courthouse smelled of ink and floor wax, which struck me as fitting. Institutions always smell of the things they use to keep themselves polished over the truth.

I had dressed carefully that morning — not in my best clothes, because my best clothes were a clockmaker's work apron and no one testifies in an apron, but plainly, deliberately: grey wool, dark collar, hair pinned back so that nothing about me could be called ornamental. I wanted to look like what I was about to be. An instrument. Something built to measure accurately and not to flatter anyone's expectations.

Kenji Arai met me at the steps. He had been reinstated two days earlier, and the reinstatement sat on him like a coat cut for a larger man — he wore it correctly, but you could see the seams straining. His eyes were bruised underneath from nights I didn't ask about.

"You don't have to do this," he said, which was a lie we both permitted because it was polite.

"Yes, I do."

"They'll be thorough."

"Good." I adjusted the strap of my bag on my shoulder. Inside it were three notebooks, a set of drawings done from memory down to the last escapement tolerance, and a small brass key. "Thorough is the only thing I trust anymore."

He almost smiled. Almost. "Mirabel. When they ask you why you waited — and they will ask —"

"I know why I waited. I'll say it."

That was the whole of our conversation, and it was enough. Some things you rehearse until they're dead; better to carry them alive into the room.

* * *

The hearing room was smaller than I'd imagined. I had expected something vast and echoing, all marble and menace, the architecture of empire. Instead there was a long table, five officers behind it, a stenographer with fingers like restless birds, and a window through which I could see, absurdly, a slice of the harbor. The harbor where it had all begun. The harbor that never forgot anything and never said a word either, which is more than could be said for me after that morning.

They swore me in. My voice held. I noted this with professional interest, the way I note whether a mainspring holds tension under load. Somewhere in the past months I had learned to observe myself as I would observe a mechanism — what part is failing, what part is merely noisy, what part can be relied upon.

Then they asked me to begin, and I did what I do best.

I started with tolerances.

"Colonel Vasquez commissioned a timing device," I said. "A synchronization unit. On its face it regulated signal intervals between the harbor pilot station and patrol launches. In fact, it did the opposite. It created a gap — a predictable, repeating gap of eleven minutes and forty seconds during which the patrols would be at the far end of their circuit and the inner channel would be unobserved. The device didn't fail. It was designed to fail, visibly, on paper, so that any inspection would conclude it was faulty machinery rather than deliberate design."

One of the officers, a Commander Oshiro with a face like a closed ledger, leaned forward. "You can substantiate this design?"

"I can reproduce it." I opened the first notebook. "From memory. Every gear ratio, every cam profile, every tolerance. I rebuilt it in my head the second time I handled it, and I've kept it there since. You may consider my memory a locked case, gentlemen. Today I'm giving you the key."

For four hours I testified, and I will tell you something strange: it was the finest work I have ever done. Better than the observatory clock. Better than anything. Because a clock, however fine, only measures time. What I built in that room was a machine made entirely of truth, each statement fitted to the next, no play in the joints, no slop in the bearings. When Oshiro tried to rattle me — and he tried, twice, circling back to questions I'd already answered to see if my story would drift the way lies drift — I simply repeated myself, word for word, the way a well-made movement repeats its beat. Word for word. He stopped trying after the second pass. I think he understood he was testing a mechanism, and mechanisms don't improvise.

The hardest hour was not Vasquez's device. The hardest hour was my father.

"State your relationship to Tomás Sandoval."

"He is my father."

"And his role in the operation?"

I looked at my hands. They were steady. I had checked.

"My father was one of its architects. Not the master of it — Vasquez owned the tide tables, as it were — but my father designed the false manifests, recruited the warehouse men, and took payment to cover debts incurred by my mother's illness. He believed he was saving this family. He was, in the way that a man who sells the roof beams believes he is keeping the house warm."

The stenographer's birds fluttered. Someone behind me shifted. And I heard myself say the sentence I had been building toward for weeks, the one Kenji warned me they would ask about:

"You will want to know why I did not come forward sooner. Here is why. Colonel Vasquez offered me protection in exchange for silence and skill. I accepted long enough to learn the machine from inside. Then my brother was hired onto one of Vasquez's ships, and I learned that the price of my conscience might be paid in his life. So I waited until I had evidence worth having, and I passed it to Investigator Arai, and the raid failed because Vasquez had bought a clerk whose name I hope you now possess. Everything I did, I did late. I am not here to be forgiven for the lateness. I am here so that the lateness ends today."

Silence. Even the stenographer's hands went still.

Oshiro consulted his papers. "And the original letter? The document found in the observatory clock naming your family?"

Here is where craftsmanship matters most, because this was the piece I had fitted together in secret, alone, at night, the way a clockmaker fits the final wheel into a train when no one is watching and everything depends on the fit.

* * *

Let me go back, briefly, because testimony is linear and truth rarely is.

When I first found the letter sealed inside the observatory clock's housing — brass foil, wax, the whole theatrical arrangement of a man who wanted it discovered eventually but not immediately — I understood within a day that delivering it to the director would be delivering it to Vasquez, or to whoever Vasquez had not yet bought. And destroying it would leave nothing but my word, and my word was a Sandoval word, and by then Sandoval words were currency in a market that had crashed.

So I copied it. Every line, in my own hand, and then I hid the original where no investigator would search and no rival would think to look: inside a customer's clock. Not just any customer's clock — old Mrs. Hattori's mantel piece, the one she'd left with me for repair in September and never collected, because Mrs. Hattori had gone to live with her daughter in Shimabara and considered her clocks, like her opinions, safely stored with me indefinitely. That clock had sat on my bench for months, patient as a saint, its case holding a dead letter the way a reliquary holds a bone.

No one searches a broken clock. People assume a broken clock contains nothing but its own failure. This is the great advantage of being underestimated, and I recommend the condition to anyone who can achieve it.

I had retrieved the original two days before the hearing, wrapped it in oilcloth, and carried it against my ribs like a second heartbeat. All night it had lain on my table while I looked at it and thought about my mother, whose ledger I had read, whose debts had built all of this, who would have hated every brick of it. She had kept accounts her whole life — honest ones, in a fair hand, every entry balanced. I liked to think that what I was about to do was bookkeeping. Closing out a ledger that had run unforgivably long.

* * *

"The original," I told the board, "is in this room."

That got their attention. Five officers sat up in a way that would have been comical if I hadn't been so frightened.

I took out the oilcloth packet and unwrapped it on the table before them, and there it was: the letter, the wax seal cracked along the line I'd scored it open those months ago, the handwriting of a man named Delgado — Vasquez's clerk, the honest one, the dead one now, though I did not know that yet — naming the Sandoval family, the manifests, the berths, the sums.

"I copied it the week I found it," I said. "The copy is in the third notebook, with an affidavit of authenticity signed by me. But you should have the original, because originals are what survive. Copies are disputed. Originals are simply *there*, the way a mountain is there. Gentlemen, I give you the mountain."

Commander Oshiro put on gloves — actual white gloves, as if the letter were a relic, and perhaps it was — and read it. I watched his face do very little, which told me he had suspected most of it already, and then watched him stop at one line and go still, which told me there was something in it even he hadn't known.

"This names a berth," he said quietly. "One berth, arranged through the *Kaiyo Maru*, for 'the designated Sandoval.'"

"It does."

"And was that berth used?"

I thought of Emilio somewhere across the water — Manila, we hoped, then Sydney — alive because I had written his name in my father's hand and put him on a ship before dawn while Tomás stood beside me on the pier and did not stop me, because Tomás understood, in the end, that love is not arithmetic and a family is not saved by counting who remains.

"No," I said. "It was surrendered. By me. To the truth."

Which was not entirely accurate — the berth had been used, gloriously, illegally, lovingly used — but the papers bore no name in the end, and I had burned the draft copies myself, and some tolerances are mine to keep. Let them call it evasion. I called it the last private room in a house that had been searched to its foundations.

* * *

They kept me six hours. When it ended, my voice was worn smooth as an old pivot, and the notebooks stayed on the government's table, and I walked out into a late afternoon that seemed ordinary in a way that felt almost insulting. Gulls. Fish smell. A tram bell. The world does not pause for testimony; it doesn't even slow down.

Kenji was waiting at the bottom of the steps, and he didn't ask how it went, because he'd been standing outside for six hours and needed no report. He simply fell into step beside me, and we walked toward the harbor without deciding to.

"You gave them everything," he said.

"Not everything. I kept one thing back."

"The berth."

"The berth," I agreed. "A girl is allowed one secret compartment. Even clocks have them — a space behind the dial where the maker hides her signature."

He glanced at me. "Is that what you did? Signed it?"

I thought about it honestly, which is the only way I think about anything anymore; dishonesty had gotten expensive.

"No," I said. "My mother signed it. Every debt in that ledger is finally paid, and I balanced the account myself. It's the only inheritance our family ever produced that I'm proud of."

We reached the seawall. Below us the water did its ancient indifferent work, and somewhere out past the breakwater the *Kaiyo Maru*'s route lay empty, and Emilio was a continent away writing letters I wouldn't receive for months.

"Mirabel," Kenji said, and there was something in his voice I hadn't heard before — not the investigator, not the man who winds watches, but something younger and less certain. "There's a warrant being drawn up tonight. For Vasquez. Oshiro wants it airtight — he says your letter is the keystone, but keystones need weight on both sides. There may be questions. There may be pressure from people above him who'd prefer this all sink quietly."

"Then they should have sunk me too," I said. "I float. Ask anyone. I'm mostly stubbornness and brass."

He laughed — actually laughed, brief and startled, as if the sound had escaped custody — and for a moment we stood there in the salt wind, two people who had spent months lying to each other by omission and had finally run out of omissions.

Then a runner came up the seawall path, navy uniform, breathless, and handed Kenji a folded message, and I watched him read it, and I watched the color change in his face by degrees, like metal cooling.

"What is it?" I asked.

He looked up at me, and the wind pulled at the message in his hand.

"They've moved the arrest up," he said. "Vasquez is at the harbor now. Tonight. And Mirabel — he's asking for you. By name. He says he has something of yours to return."

I turned toward the harbor, where the lights were coming on one by one along the piers, each one a small precise point of fire against the coming dark, and I thought: whatever he has of mine, I want it back. I have spent a year of my life in his mechanisms. It is time, at last, to take apart his.
