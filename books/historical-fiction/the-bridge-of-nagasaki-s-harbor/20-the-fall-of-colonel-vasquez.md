## 20. The Fall of Colonel Vasquez

### MIRABEL

The harbor at night is a different instrument than the harbor by day. By day it is commerce, noise, the clatter of crates and the shouts of men who all believe themselves indispensable. By night it becomes what it truly is: a machine of small lights and large silences, each lamp a bearing, each shadow a tolerance too wide to trust. I walked toward it knowing I might be walking into a mechanism designed to close on me, and I went anyway, because a year of my life was wound inside that place and I wanted it back.

Kenji walked beside me. He had not asked whether I wanted company. He had simply appeared at the observatory steps with his coat collar up and his repaired watch — my watch now, in every way that mattered — ticking in his waistcoat pocket like a second heart we shared.

"You don't have to come," he said.

"You said that already."

"And you ignored me already."

"I'm consistent," I said. "It's a professional virtue."

He made a sound that was almost a laugh, and then stopped making sounds altogether, because ahead of us, under the great crane at Pier Four, stood Colonel Vasquez with two soldiers and a lantern, and beside him, wrists bound but spine straight as a mainspring, sat Tomás Sandoval, my father, on an overturned crate.

I had imagined this moment a hundred ways. In none of them did my father look calm. In none of them did he lift his head when he heard my footsteps and say, quietly, "You came. Good. Now listen to me."

"Father—"

"He has nothing," Vasquez said. His voice was pleasant. It had always been pleasant; that was the horror of the man, that his cruelty came gift-wrapped in courtesy. "No ship. No berth. No arrangements left worth the name. What he has is a daughter who writes very precise letters to naval investigators." He turned the lantern so its light fell full on my face, testing me, the way one tests a gear for wobble. "And I have something of yours."

He drew from his coat a folded paper and held it out between two fingers.

My letter. The original. The sealed note I had found inside the master clock's housing a lifetime ago — or eleven months ago, which amounts to the same thing when you measure in fear. My own hands had hidden it; other hands had copied it; and now here it was, traveling back to me through the machinery of the state like a part returned to its maker for inspection.

"It surfaced," Vasquez said, "this afternoon. In a most inconvenient place. Do you know, Mirabel-san, I have spent thirty years learning how men hide things, and I never once considered that a woman would hide a document inside another clock." He smiled without warmth. "You are either the most honest person I have ever threatened or the most thorough. I confess I no longer know which."

"The arrests are tonight," I said. "Arai told me they'd moved them up."

"They were moved up because your copy moved up," he said. "Cause and effect. Timing. You understand timing better than anyone I've employed." He let that word sit in the cold air between us — *employed*, as if I had ever been anything but a hostage with tools. "So. Here is the last honest transaction of my career. Your father cooperated. Fully. Names, manifests, routes, the whole ledger of it, given freely to the investigators this morning before I knew. For that cooperation he will serve a sentence measured in years rather than the rest of his life." He glanced down at Tomás, and something flickered across his face that in a better man might have been regret. "You cost me everything, old man. And you saved your daughter. I genuinely cannot decide whether to admire you."

"You could decide not to speak," my father said.

"I could. But I find, at the end, I prefer honesty. It's so rare it's nearly ornamental." Vasquez pressed the letter into my hand. His fingers were cold. "Take it. It's yours. You'll want it for your testimony — the investigators will, anyway. Consider it the final repair. I broke the mechanism; you've restored it to working order. Just not for me."

The soldiers stepped forward. He raised one hand, and they stopped, and then he bowed to me — a small bow, shallow, insulting in its precision, the bow of a man conceding a game he believed he'd only lost to luck.

"It wasn't luck," I said.

"No?"

"It was tolerances. You built your machine assuming everyone had a price. My father's price was my mother's debts. Mine was my brother. But Arai—" I nodded toward the shadow behind me, where Kenji stood very still. "Arai's price was nothing. You can't calibrate for nothing. That's the flaw in your design."

For the first time since I had known him, Colonel Vasquez looked at me as though I were real. Then the soldiers took his arms, gently, because even arrested colonels are arrested gently in Nagasaki, and led him away down the pier, and the lantern light went with him, and the harbor closed over the space where he had been like water over a dropped coin.

I went to my father.

His wrists were bound. His coat — the new coat, the prosperity coat, the coat I had hated for a year — was buttoned wrong, one hole off, and there was grease on his cuff from the crate. He looked smaller than I remembered him being, and older, and more himself than he had been at any dinner table in our house.

"Emilio's ship cleared port Tuesday," he said. "Singapore by now. Maybe farther."

"I know. I got the cable."

"He doesn't know yet. About all this. He'll learn it in letters."

"Yes," I said. "Letters across water. They take weeks. It'll give him time to be angry somewhere safe."

Tomás laughed — a short, broken sound, quickly mastered. Then he looked at me with my mother's eyes, because I had always known those eyes were mine too, borrowed and returned.

"You gave him the berth," he said. "You wrote my name in my own hand so I couldn't argue."

"You taught me the hand. All those invoices."

"I taught you well," he said, "and it destroyed me, and it was the best thing I ever did." He lifted his bound wrists slightly, absurdly, gesturing at the pier, the crane, the whole ruined edifice of his ambition. "They'll give me three years, maybe four, for cooperating. I can survive four years. What I cannot survive is you hating me for choosing him."

"I don't hate you."

"You will, some days."

"Some days," I admitted. "Mostly Tuesdays."

That startled a real laugh out of him, and then the soldiers came for him too, and he went without resistance, walking between them the way he walked to the shop every morning, as though arrest were merely an errand he had finally gotten around to. At the end of the pier he turned once. He didn't wave. He just looked at me the way a man looks at the last light of a harbor he is leaving, memorizing it, and then the dark took him.

Kenji came and stood beside me. We watched until there was nothing to watch.

"Well," he said finally. "That went better than my plan."

"You had a plan?"

"I had several plans. All of them involved me doing something heroic and getting shot." He exhaled, long, the breath of a man setting down weight carried too far. "Instead everyone behaved reasonably. I find it deeply suspicious."

I held up the letter. "One thing left to do with this."

"Testimony," he said. "Day after tomorrow. Nine o'clock. Don't be late — Captain Mori hates lateness almost as much as he hates me, and you're the only witness whose evidence survived the fire. Everything depends on your precision."

"Then everything," I said, "is in excellent hands."

### KENJI

There is a particular hour outside a courthouse that I have come to think of as the hour of imperfect justice. It comes after the verdicts, when the clerks have gone home with their stamps and their satisfied little sounds, and the people who made the verdicts possible stand on the steps blinking in ordinary daylight, wondering why the air tastes the same as it did before.

Vasquez had been taken away in a black car an hour earlier, escorted by naval police who saluted him on the way in and did not salute him on the way out, which struck me as the entire moral of the story compressed into a single gesture. The charges would hold. They had to hold; too many names on the manifests, too many dockworkers missing, too many families ruined along the waterfront for the navy to let it slide. Justice, this time, was not going to be permitted to wander off.

But imperfect — yes. Imperfect because Tomás Sandoval was serving four years for building the machine while the man who owned the machine would serve perhaps eight, and the difference between those numbers was the difference between a man who sinned and a man who sinned with rank. Imperfect because three dockworkers would stay missing regardless of any verdict, their wives standing at the harbor rail every evening with lamps, as if light could reach wherever they had gone. Imperfect because a boy named Emilio Sandoval was somewhere past Singapore, safe, and would never entirely understand what his safety had cost, and perhaps that was the mercy of it.

I found her on the courthouse steps, third stair from the top, exactly where she'd said she'd be. She was holding her father's letter — her letter — folded into her coat pocket, and she was looking at the harbor the way she looked at a movement she was diagnosing: completely, patiently, missing nothing.

"Captain Mori sends his compliments," I said. "He said, and I quote, 'the Sandoval girl's testimony was the cleanest work this office has seen in a decade.' He then glared at me, so I believe he meant it."

"He called me *girl*?"

"He's sixty-three. Everyone is *girl*. I'm fairly sure he calls the admiral *boy* behind his back."

She almost smiled. With Mirabel Sandoval, an almost-smile was a decoration worth crossing town for.

We stood there a while. Below us, Nagasaki went about its business — carts, gulls, a ferry loading at the commercial pier, the water doing its eternal indifferent shining. Somewhere in the city, clocks were striking eleven, all of them slightly out of agreement with one another, all of them wrong together, which is the closest thing to harmony most machines ever achieve.

"Arai," she said.

"Hm."

"What happens to you now? Officially?"

"Reinstated. Back pay. An apology from a section chief who will deny having apologized." I shrugged. "And a transfer request on my desk I intend to lose in a drawer."

"You could go anywhere. After this. Anyone with a clean record would leave a harbor like this one."

"Would they?"

"Wouldn't you?"

I thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty and because I had learned, this past year, that lying to Mirabel Sandoval was a waste of good material. "I spent eight months pretending to bring you watches," I said. "Nine watches. I still have the list. Every time I ran out of excuses I had to invent a new ailment for a timepiece — a loose balance spring, a worn pallet fork. My landlady thinks I am the unluckiest man in Japan with watches." She laughed then, really laughed, brief and low, and I let myself memorize it the way she memorized gear trains. "The point is, I got very good at finding reasons to walk to your shop. I don't propose to stop having reasons just because the case is closed."

She turned and looked at me fully. Her eyes were steady. Her hands, tucked into her pockets around a letter that had nearly killed her family, were steady too. She was the steadiest person I had ever known, and I suddenly understood that this steadiness was not peace — it was discipline, maintained at cost, the way she kept a clock running true against friction and wear.

"Gratitude," she said carefully, "does not ask for more."

"No," I agreed. "It doesn't. But it leaves the shop door unlocked."

She studied me for a long moment. Then she reached into her pocket and drew out something small, and put it in my palm: a watch crystal, polished to a mirror shine, fitted to a case I recognized as the one I'd brought her a year ago with my manufactured excuse.

"You fixed it again?" I said.

"I improved it. New escapement. It'll keep time within two seconds a month, in any weather, for the rest of your life." She closed my fingers over it. "Consider it a thank-you that asks for nothing."

"And if I ask for something anyway?"

"Then," said Mirabel Sandoval, turning back toward the harbor, toward the observatory, toward the clock waiting on the hill for her hands, "you'll have to keep coming by the shop until I decide whether to answer. I warn you — I'm very busy these days. There's a city full of broken things, and somebody has to keep the time."

She started down the steps. After a moment, I followed — not because she asked, and not because I expected anything, but because following her had turned out, against every probability, to be the truest thing I had ever done.

Behind us the courthouse doors swung shut. Ahead, the harbor waited, keeping its own kind of time.

And in her pocket, folded flat against her heart, the letter rode home — the whole year of it, the debt and the betrayal and the berth given away at dawn, every gear of it — carried lightly, carried completely, the way she carried everything: as a memory that would need winding, every morning, for the rest of her life.
