## 21. Letters Across Water

The letters come on the second Tuesday of every month, which is fitting, because Emilio was born on a Tuesday and has never once in his life managed to be punctual about anything except being loved.

They arrive at the shop — never the house — in envelopes gone soft and gray from the long crossing, stamped with ports I have to look up on the atlas behind the counter: Singapore, Colombo, Port Said, Marseille. He is working his way west the way water works its way through stone: slowly, patiently, without any plan he'd admit to. The handwriting starts out cramped at the top of each page, as though he's trying to fit an entire ocean into a margin, and loosens by the bottom until it's all loops and enthusiasm, the ink smudged where his thumb dragged through it because he writes standing up, I'm certain of it, probably against a bulkhead, probably grinning.

I keep them in the drawer where Mama kept her ledger. It seemed right. One kind of accounting replaced by another.

* * *

The prison sits above the harbor road like a bad tooth you can't stop tonguing. Gray stone, iron gate, a cypress leaning over the wall at an angle that suggests even the trees here are doing time. The guards know me now. There's a guard named Okabe who winds his pocket watch badly — lets it run down, then shoves the crown forward without setting the hands — and every visit I fix it for him while he pretends not to be grateful, and every visit I think: there is no machine so broken that a man won't trust it more than he trusts himself.

The visiting room smells of disinfectant and cold rice. A wooden partition runs down the middle, glass above it, scratched by years of foreheads pressed hopefully against the other side. My father sits on his stool the way he sits at his workbench, spine straight, hands flat on his knees, as if waiting for me to hand him something small and delicate that requires both our attention.

Which, in a sense, I do.

"Tuesday," he says.

"Second Tuesday," I say. "He's getting better."

Tomás Sandoval has been inside for seven months. Reduced sentence, they called it — cooperation, they called it — as if the word could be folded small enough to fit in a cell. Four years. He'll come out when I'm twenty-four, when Emilio will have crossed enough water to circle back around the world, when the harbor will have changed its shapes the way harbors do and pretended it always looked like that.

I unfold the letter against the glass so he can read along, but really he closes his eyes and listens, because my father has always trusted ears more than eyes. Ears can't be forged.

"Dear Mira," I read. "Or dear jailbird's daughter, depending on how the mail censors are feeling this week."

My father makes the sound that is almost a laugh. In this room it counts as one.

* * *

The first letter came in March, three weeks after Emilio's ship cleared the straits, and I read it aloud to an empty shop because Papa wasn't allowed visitors yet — the first month is silence, they say, time to adjust, as if a man adjusts to a cell the way he adjusts to a new coat. I stood behind the counter with the page shaking slightly and read my brother's voice into the dust motes:

*Mira. They gave me a hammock and a bucket and told me the sea would make a man of me, which so far has only made me seasick, but I am reliably informed that this too becomes a man eventually.*

*I know what you did. Papa wrote to me before they took him and told me the berth was yours to give and you gave it away, and I have been angry about this for eleven days straight, which is difficult to sustain while vomiting over a rail. I am done being angry. Mostly. Don't ever do it again or I will come home and be furious at you in person, which will take years, because I am going the long way.*

*Fix the clock. Fix all the clocks. I'll count the hours wherever I am and know whose hands are moving them.*

*Your idiot brother, E.*

I read it four times. Then I put it in Mama's drawer and went upstairs and ate an entire plate of rice alone, which is a thing grief does sometimes — it arrives dressed as appetite, and you only recognize it later.

* * *

"Go on," Papa says today. "From the top."

"He describes the canal." I flatten the latest letter — Port Said, dated six weeks ago, held up somewhere by a censor with a slow hand. "He says the desert comes right down to the water on both sides and he kept waiting for the sea to end and it wouldn't, and the pilot boat men rowed alongside shouting prices for postcards, and he bought forty."

"Forty."

"He says most of them are for you. He says—" I squint at Emilio's looping hand. "He says, *tell Papa the ships go through sideways, almost touching the sand, like needles through cloth, and that I thought of him because that is what he taught me about patience.*"

Papa looks down at his hands. They've aged in seven months the way tools age — not weaker exactly, but marked, the knuckles swollen, a new scar across the left thumb I don't ask about. Prison workshops teach a man things. He repairs the guards' watches now. There is a joke in that somewhere and neither of us goes looking for it.

"He remembers that?" Papa says quietly.

"You told him everything twice. You told *me* everything twice. Usually while holding a gear up to the light as though it were evidence."

"It *was* evidence. Everything is evidence, Mira. That's what your testimony taught me." He says it without bitterness, which is somehow worse. "That's what it taught all of us."

I don't answer that. Some sentences you let pass through the room like weather.

Instead I read on. Emilio describes Colombo: the heat like a wet blanket, the harbor full of ships flying flags Papa would want catalogued, the chief engineer — a Scotsman named McAllister — who discovered that Emilio can strip and clean a fuel pump in under an hour and has consequently decided my brother is some manner of prodigy. "He says, *I did not tell him about the workshop. Some inheritances you carry quietly.*"

Papa's mouth does the thing it does when he's pleased and refusing to show it — a small structural failure at one corner, quickly repaired.

"The last part," I say, and hesitate, because I've been deciding all week whether to include it, and I've decided that withholding things from my father is how we got here, all of us, the whole sorry mechanism of it. "There's a paragraph about Vasquez's trial. He must have read about it in a paper at some port."

The visiting room goes very quiet. Even the guard by the door stops shifting his weight.

"He says the newspapers in Marseille had it on the third page, below a story about a flooded mine. He says—"

I read it. My voice holds. I've practiced; I'll admit that. I sat in the shop Tuesday night and read this paragraph aloud to a customer's carriage clock until I could do it steady.

*He says: tell Papa that the man who owned the tide tables is now owned by a smaller room than any of ours, and that justice came late and crooked and smelling of bribed clerks, but it came, and that I intend to spend my whole life being the reason it came to the right person instead of the wrong one. Tell him the berth wasn't wasted. Tell him I'm making it into something.*

Papa doesn't move for a long moment. Through the scratched glass his face is slightly distorted, one eye magnified, and I think absurdly of looking through a jeweler's loupe, of how the truth of a mechanism only shows itself under magnification, and how my whole family spent two years avoiding the lens.

Then he bows his head — not far, just enough — the way he used to bow over finished work before he signed the case.

"Tell him," he says, "that a needle through cloth still leaves a hole. But cloth can be mended. Tell him to learn mending as well as piercing."

"That's very him."

"That's very him," Papa agrees, and something in the room lightens by a degree, one notch on a dial nobody else can read.

* * *

Afterwards, Okabe walks me to the gate, holding out his watch wordlessly. I open it, sigh theatrically — the ritual requires the sigh — and reset it properly.

"How is the boy?" Okabe asks. He asks every month. He has never met Emilio. I suspect half the guards feel they know him; my brother's letters get read aloud in that visiting room twice a month, and prisoners press their foreheads to the glass and listen, and for a few minutes the room smells less of disinfectant.

"Growing," I say. "Salty."

Okabe nods gravely, as though *salty* were a technical assessment of considerable value.

Outside, the harbor road runs down toward the water, and the light is doing that late-afternoon thing it does here, turning the bay to hammered tin. The observatory stands on its hill above the mole, and I can see the clock tower's face from here, pale as a coin. I wind it myself now. Every dawn. I climb the stairs in the dark with the oil can and the winding key and I bring the weights up and set the escapement breathing and stand there a moment in the tick of it, alone with the biggest heart in the harbor.

It keeps better time than anything I've ever touched. I like to think it knows why.

* * *

At the shop there's mail waiting — the ordinary kind, repair slips, a bill for lamp oil, and beneath it an envelope I don't recognize. Not gray and soft this time. Crisp. Official. The seal of the naval district, which I have learned to read the way sailors read weather: with a tightening in the chest.

Inside, a single sheet. A summons, formal and brief. The naval investigators request my presence Thursday morning regarding *matters arising from prior testimony*. No further explanation. There's a signature I don't know and a line at the bottom that makes my stomach drop through the floorboards into the harbor mud:

*You may wish to bring the original letter.*

But the original letter is in the courthouse archive. I handed it over myself, watched it logged, watched the clerk's pen scratch the date beside my name. Which means either someone has lost it —

— or someone has found the copy. The one I hid inside Mrs. Otsuka's mantel clock two winters ago, wrapped in oilcloth, ticking distance between it and discovery, patient as any mechanism I ever built.

Someone has opened that clock. And I need to know whether what they found saves my family or finishes burying it.

Thursday. Two days. Outside the window, the observatory clock strikes five across the water, steady and clear, counting down whether I asked it to or not.
