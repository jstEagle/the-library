## 22. Keeping Time

The observatory director retired in the spring, and no one replaced him. There was no money for a replacement — there was never any money for anything anymore — and so the master clock of Nagasaki harbor passed, by a process no one bothered to make official, into the hands of the clockmaker's daughter who had once been summoned to fix it.

I did not apply for the position. I simply began arriving. First once a week to oil the escapement, then twice, then every morning, until one day the harbor master looked up from his ledgers, saw me on the ladder with my tools, and asked — not unkindly — whether I was staff.

"I'm the clock's staff," I said. "The clock employs me. The wages are terrible, but the hours are regular."

He laughed, which surprised us both, and after that no one asked again. This is how careers are made in a city rebuilding itself: not by appointment, but by the slow accumulation of mornings until your presence becomes load-bearing.

The clock and I had come to an understanding. It had tried to kill me once, in its way — had hidden a letter in its own chest like a swallowed secret, and let me be the one to find it. I could have hated it for that. Instead I learned its moods. The great regulator ran three seconds slow when the humidity climbed off the harbor in June, and fast in the cold snap of January, and it had a tremor in the fourth wheel that no replacement part could cure because the part no longer existed anywhere in the world. I machined one myself, from memory and stubbornness. The tremor softened but did not die. I decided I respected that. Some things you don't fix. You keep them.

Every dawn, before the fishing boats went out, I climbed the stairs with the winding key and gave the clock its weight back. Two hundred turns, no more, no less. The old director had left a chalkboard tally of the count, and I had kept it going out of something between superstition and arithmetic. The harbor below would be gray and breathing. The cranes stood like herons. And the water — the water remembered everything, though it never once let on.

* * *

Emilio's letters came on the second Tuesday of each month, irregularly, which I pointed out to him was a poor showing for the brother of a clockmaker. His handwriting had improved. His spelling had not.

*Dear Mira,* the first one began. *The sea is enormous. I know you will say I am stating the obvious, but you cannot know it from a harbor. A harbor is a room. The sea is the world with the walls taken down. I work the cargo winch and I am not bad at it, and the cook likes me because I fix his scale, and I have not been seasick since Thursday of the first week, which I consider a triumph of the Sandoval constitution.*

He did not write about Vasquez. He did not write about the berth, or the dawn we stood on the pier, or the way I had forged our father's hand so smoothly that for one terrible moment I had admired my own forgery. He wrote about the sea, and the cook's scale, and a girl in Colombo who sold mangoes and could calculate faster than any adding machine. He wrote like a boy who had been given a life and intended to use all of it. This was the point. This had always been the point. I read his letters standing at the clock, and I let the ticking count the seconds I allowed myself to be proud.

Once he wrote: *Do you ever regret it? Sending me instead of yourself?*

I wrote back: *I regret nothing that can be wound and set right. Ask me again when you learn to spell "Colombo."*

He never answered that letter directly, but the next one contained a pressed hibiscus flower and a single line: *The cook's scale is fixed. Some things stay fixed.*

* * *

My father came home in the autumn of the second year, thinner, grayer, with a sentence served down to eighteen months for cooperation and a way of moving through the shop as though he were a guest in it. He did not touch the lathes for the first month. He sat by the window with the light on his hands and watched me work, and I pretended not to notice, because noticing would have required one of us to say something, and we had spent our whole allotment of unsayable things years ago.

On the first Sunday of his freedom, he came to the observatory. He had never been. In all the years of the clock's service, in all the years of the smuggling it had silently timed, he had never once climbed those stairs. I heard his footsteps on the iron steps — slower than they used to be, but even — and I did not turn around.

"You wind it every day?" he asked.

"Every day."

"It's a big responsibility."

"It's a small one," I said. "Two hundred turns. The clock does the rest. That's the whole philosophy of the thing — you don't keep the time, Papa. You just keep the clock, and the time keeps itself."

He stood beside me a while. Below us the harbor lay in its morning silver, the ferries stitching their patient lines across it. Somewhere down there was a warehouse with a new tenant. Somewhere was the pilaster where Arai had once waited for me in the rain with a broken watch and a question he already knew the answer to. The harbor did not look like a place where men had disappeared. Harbors never do. That is their great talent.

"I'm proud of you," my father said. "I want you to know that. Not despite everything. Because of it."

I kept winding. "You could have said that with a coat," I said. "Instead of buying one."

He laughed — a real laugh, cracked and surprised out of him — and after that it was easier. Not easy. Easier. We were a family of people who had learned the hard way that love unspoken curdles into something else, and we had resolved, without ever discussing it, to speak.

* * *

Arai came by the shop on Thursdays, ostensibly to have his watch regulated, though the watch had been running to within a second a month since the second repair and we both knew it. He would sit on the stool by the bench and watch me file a pivot or true a balance spring, and he would talk about nothing — the weather, the price of rice, the scandalous behavior of the harbor gulls — and then, eventually, about something.

He had been reinstated with a commendation that the ministry worded so carefully it could have been read aloud at his funeral. The raid had failed; the testimony had not. Between those two facts, his career had been suspended, examined, and resurrected, and he carried the whole sequence with the particular lightness of a man who has learned that institutions are like clocks: they can be right for decades and still need someone to open the case and see what's actually inside.

"You could take the watch back," I told him once. "It's fine. It's better than fine. You're just paying me to sit here."

"I'm paying you for the sitting," he said. "The watch is incidental."

I looked at him. He looked at the bench, at the little brass shavings curled like eyelashes in the light. He was not a man of grand declarations; he was a man of Thursdays. I had come, over the long slow months, to find that worth more than grandeur. Grandeur had been Vasquez's currency. Thursdays were honest.

"Come to the observatory," I said, before I could decide not to. "At dawn. I'll show you the escapement. It's the most honest mechanism in Nagasaki. It only knows how to do one thing, and it does it two hundred times a minute, and it has never once lied to me."

"And you?" he asked, mild as milk. "Have you ever lied to it?"

"Once," I said. "When I told the director I'd found nothing in the housing. The clock knew. I think it's been running three seconds slow on purpose ever since, out of principle."

He smiled. He came at dawn on Sunday. I showed him the escapement, and the harbor, and the tally on the chalkboard, and he stood very close to me in the cold bell-loft, and neither of us said anything that needed saying, because we were both people who understood that the most important mechanisms work quietly.

* * *

The city changed around the clock, as cities do. New cranes. New ferries. New names on the warehouse doors. The war that had been gathering on the horizon like weather came, in its season, and went, in its season, and took things I will not write down here because this is a chapter about keeping time, and there are other chapters, other hands, other witnesses, for what the years took. What I will say is this: when the worst of it came, the clock never stopped. I wound it through blackouts. I wound it the morning after the sirens, with glass on the observatory steps and the harbor smoking, and the escapement ticked its two hundred ticks a minute as though the world were a mechanism that could be repaired — which, I have decided, is not a foolish belief. It is the only one I have ever been able to hold without my hands shaking.

People began to come to me, in those years, with things to fix. Not just clocks. A music box that had belonged to a daughter who did not come home. A ship's chronometer salvaged from a hull. A pocket watch with a bullet's dent in the case, still running, that a widow asked me to keep running forever, and I told her honestly that forever was beyond my tolerances, but fifty years was achievable, and she said that would do.

That is the trade, in the end. You cannot give people back what they lost. You can only keep something running — accurately, faithfully, without opinion — so that they have somewhere to set their grief by. I understood then why I had been given the clock instead of the berth. Anyone could have escaped. Not anyone could stay and keep the count.

* * *

Last spring, a boy of about fourteen came to the shop with a broken alarm clock and a manner of great urgency, and while I worked he watched my hands with the particular hunger I recognized from photographs of my own younger face. His father fished. His mother took in washing. He had taken the clock apart four times already, he confessed, and only gotten it back together twice.

"Twice out of four is a fine average," I said. "My father's rule was: take it apart as many times as it takes. The clock doesn't mind. Clocks are patient. It's people who are in a hurry."

"Could I learn?" he asked. "Properly?"

I looked at him for a long moment. The shop smelled of oil and brass shavings and the salt that comes in off the harbor no matter how tightly you shut the door. Somewhere in the back, in a customer's clock that no one has ever come to collect, there is a letter in a sealed envelope that I have never destroyed and never will, because a record is a record, and the harbor remembers everything, and someone should too.

"Come back tomorrow," I said. "At dawn. I'll show you the escapement first. It's the most honest mechanism in the world. It only knows one thing, and it has never once lied to me."

He came back at dawn. Of course he did.

And I climbed the observatory stairs with the winding key in my pocket and the boy's footsteps behind me on the iron treads, and I gave the clock its two hundred turns over a harbor that was turning silver with the first light, and I thought of my mother's ledger, and my father's coat, and Emilio's terrible spelling, and the letter in the brass, and every name I ever carried — and I carried them still, all of them, every one.

I had sold almost everything else by then. The tools fit in one case. The case fit under one arm.

But the arm never tired, and the case was never light, and I would not have had it any other way.

I carry little. I carry all of it. And the clock, faithful liar, honest machine, keeper of the only inheritance worth keeping, ticks on above the water — two hundred times a minute, every minute, remembering for us all.
