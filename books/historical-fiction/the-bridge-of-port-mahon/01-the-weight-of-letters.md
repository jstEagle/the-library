## 1. The Weight of Letters

The kettle had a voice like a woman clearing her throat before saying something difficult, and Edmund Iversen had learned, over three years in the Marsh household, to listen for it the way other men listened for church bells. He set it on the ring in the scullery at half past ten each night, precisely, because Adeline Marsh retired at half past ten and did not stir again until dawn unless the cause required otherwise. The house settled around him as he waited—the tick of the hall clock, the groan of a beam over the kitchen that remembered some older weight, the sea beyond the garden wall making its long argument with the rocks below Port Mahon.

He took the day's letters from the japanned tray on the hall table and carried them into the scullery, where the light was bad and the door locked with a click no louder than a knuckle against wood.

There were seven tonight. He weighed them in his palm first, always, the way a doctor weighs a heart before deciding what it can bear. Two were thick—correspondence from the northern branches, likely, full of resolutions and grievances and the particular fury of women who had been told to wait. One was thin as a wafer and smelled faintly of lavender, which meant Mrs. Pym in Brighton, who wrote every fortnight and signed herself *your oldest friend* though Edmund had gathered, from three years of her letters, that they had not spoken between 1898 and 1904 and that whatever had passed between them then was still bleeding. One bore a foreign stamp, creased from its long journey. And one—he held it up to the hissing kettle—was addressed in a hand so violent the pen had torn through the paper twice, *Miss A. MARSH* underlined as though underline were insufficient and the sender had wanted to strike her through the post itself.

Edmund set the kettle to steaming and did not think about any of them yet. That was the discipline. First the water, then the work, and only afterward—if there was time, if the hands were steady enough—the feeling.

The steam rose in a thin gray ribbon. He held the first envelope over it, mouth down, letting the moisture find the glue, counting under his breath the way his mother had taught him to count when kneading bread: *one, two, three, four*—not too long or the paper cockled, not too short and the flap tore when you lifted it. His mother had taught him many things that had turned out to be useful for purposes she never imagined. She had taught him to write a fair copperplate hand; he now wrote four different hands depending on which name he was being that week. She had taught him to keep a clean hearth; he now knew exactly how long a document burned and how fine the ash must be crushed before it could be scattered without evidence. She had taught him that a man's word was his whole estate. He had given his word, in one form or another, to eleven people across two countries, and every promise contradicted at least two others.

The flap lifted like a small mouth opening.

Inside: three pages in a round, schoolroom hand. A treasurer's report from the Leeds branch. Receipts. A complaint about a hall that had been let to them and then withdrawn when the landlord learned the purpose. And at the bottom, in a different ink, added later and hurriedly: *Tell Miss Marsh the men who came asking were not from the Council but from somewhere else. They knew names. Be careful whom you trust even among friends.*

Edmund read it twice. Then he read it a third time, because this was the habit, this was the discipline: he read everything twice, once as a servant protecting his mistress's interests and once as a man memorizing lives he would never live. The treasurer in Leeds had a daughter at Girton. The treasurer's husband had died of the lung in '06 and she spoke of him only in the present tense until the third paragraph of every letter, where the past tense ambushed her, and then she recovered by the fourth. Edmund knew this. He knew the color of Mrs. Pym's regrets and the exact temperature of the quarrel between Adeline and her cousin Winifred, which had been cooling now for eight months and might yet be saved if someone wrote first, and he knew these things the way a lighthouse keeper knows the ships—not because they were his, but because it was his work to stand in the dark and watch them pass.

He folded the pages along their original creases, breathed on the gum, pressed the flap down, and laid the letter flat beneath the iron trivet to set.

*One, two, three, four.*

The lavender letter next. Mrs. Pym, Brighton. *My dear Addie—I have taken to walking on the downs in the early morning, when the gulls are at their most impertinent, and I pretend the wind is arguing with me, since you are not here to do it.* Edmund's hands went still over the page. There it was again, the thing he had no business reading and less business feeling: the tenderness underneath the sparring, two old women separated by pride and forty miles and a silence neither would name. He sealed it carefully, more carefully than the rest, as though the care could travel through the post and do its work in Brighton.

The thin foreign-stamped letter he saved for last, after the furious one—which proved to be from a colonel's wife in Portsmouth threatening, in language of remarkable invention, to have her husband's regiment look into "the morals of these unsexed viragoes," a threat Adeline would read aloud at breakfast tomorrow with relish and pin above her desk—and after the two thick ones, which contained nothing but the ordinary machinery of revolution: minutes, subscriptions, dates.

The foreign stamp was from Vienna. The postmark was six weeks old, which meant it had been held somewhere, examined somewhere, passed from one desk to another somewhere, before being allowed to continue its slow journey to a fishing town on the English coast. Edmund turned it over twice. The address was in German, in a hand he knew better than his own, because he had watched it form across a kitchen table for the first nineteen years of his life: the strong downstrokes, the little hooks on the *m* that made every word look like it was reaching for something.

*Marta.*

His sister had written to him eleven times since 1904. He had received nine of the letters. Each one had crossed borders that did not want it to cross, had been opened and resealed by men whose steam kettles were certainly less gentle than his own, and each one had arrived carrying the same cargo beneath its news: *I am still here. Do you remember me? Am I remembered?*

He held it over the steam. Counted. Lifted the flap.

*Dear Edmund—or should I say Mr. Iversen now, since that is the name you wear—*

He stopped. Set the letter down. Went to the window and looked out at the black harbor, where the naval tender's lights moved in their patient pattern, where they had been moving in their patient pattern for five weeks now, more ships each week, coaling barges coming and going at hours that made the fishermen mutter. Everyone in Port Mahon said *if there's a war* the way people said *if it rains*, meaning *when*. Edmund said nothing at all, but he had begun, in the nights, to make lists.

*I am writing this in the dark because Anna is asleep beside me and the landlady counts our candles. We are well. Lukas has grown; he is tall as my shoulder and asks about his uncle constantly, though I have had to tell him his uncle is a very dull man who works for a very dull lady, so that he will not boast of you at school. Little Greta lost her first tooth and made me pay a pfennig for it, the extortionist.*

Edmund read the lies in it the way he read everything else twice. *We are well.* Marta had never once in eleven letters written *we are well* except when they were not. It was their mother's code, older than either of them: the worse the truth, the brighter the surface. *We are well* meant the coal was gone. *Lukas is tall* meant Lukas needed boots. And the mention of the landlady counting candles—that was new. That was a woman telling her brother, in the only language left to them, that they had come down in the world to rooms where even light was metered.

He finished the letter. It contained, on its final page, a single sentence that was not code at all:

*If there is ever a way, Edmund, I would come to you. Not for myself. For the children. Say whether there is a way, and I will never ask again.*

He sat in the bad light of the scullery for a long time with the letter open on his knee, listening to the sea argue with the rocks, and then he folded it along its creases, sealed it, and set it on the tray to be carried up in the morning with all the others, unread by anyone, its question traveling on to a woman who would open it over breakfast and read only the surface, the bright surface, *we are well,* and smile at the part about the tooth.

That was the arrangement. That was the discipline. He read the letters so that Adeline Marsh need never be surprised; he answered to no one for what the letters cost him.

* * *

Afterward, when the tray was dressed and the scullery restored to its innocent disorder, Edmund climbed the back stairs to his room under the eaves and performed the second office of his night: the cataloging.

It was not morbid. He was firm with himself on this point. A man who kept accounts was not morbid; a captain who knew his ship's tonnage was not morbid. The world had taught him—in 1900, in the space of one November night it had taught him thoroughly—that survival belonged to those who knew, at all times, exactly what could be carried and what must be left on the platform. So he kept the account. He updated it the way other men updated their diaries, and he kept it nowhere but in his head, because paper burns and memory, faithfully kept, does not require a match.

One satchel: leather, scarred at the left corner where a soldier's boot had caught it in 1900, brass buckles polished, containing nothing at present but a change of shirt, a razor, and a false bottom that had carried other things once and could carry them again. The satchel hung behind the loose panel in his wardrobe, where the house's original builder—a smuggler, by local legend—had hidden brandy a century before Edmund hid himself.

Two photographs. The first was of his parents, taken in '93 outside the print shop, his father's arm stiff with dignity around his mother's shoulders, both of them looking slightly offended by the camera, as though it had accused them of something. The second was newer and smaller and he permitted himself to look at it only on Sundays: Marta on the dock at Trieste, 1900, holding Lukas—who was not Lukas then, who had another name then, a name that had died with the papers that carried it—her face turned toward the ship and away from the photographer, so that what remained was her profile and the wind taking her hair and the absolute certainty in her jaw that she was doing the right thing, which she had maintained ever since, and which Edmund had spent eleven years trying to believe on her behalf.

A brass key. Small, worn smooth, belonging to a locker at a station he would not name even in his own head, containing a parcel he would not describe. The key lived on a string under his shirt, against his skin, and had worn a groove in his sternum that he sometimes thought he could feel with his fingers, the way an old horse feels the trace.

And that was all. Eleven years of a life, reduced to inventory: one satchel, two photographs, a brass key. Everything else—the copperplate hands, the four names, the languages, the routes memorized and abandoned, the faces of the men at the checkpoints who had looked at his papers and seen only what the papers told them to see—all of that traveled with him invisibly, packed in the one container no customs officer had ever thought to open.

He blew out the candle and lay down and did not sleep, which was also part of the discipline, or perhaps the failure of it. In the dark, the arithmetic began, as it always did. The letter from Vienna lay three floors below him on the japanned tray, and its question lay on top of it like a stone: *Say whether there is a way.*

There was a way. There was always a way; that was the terrible thing about him, the thing his father had seen in him at fourteen and wept over, quietly, thinking his son asleep: *you will always find the way, Edmund, God help you, and the way will always cost.* Papers could be made. Names could be found—names of the dead, which was the only currency he had ever traded in, and the spending of which had bought his sister's life once already and sat on his conscience ever since like a stone in a shoe, unnoticed in motion, unbearable at rest.

But papers required a forger, and forgers required proximity to official ink, and official ink in Port Mahon had lately developed a taste for scrutiny. The census was coming in the spring—everyone knew it, the newspapers were full of it, the great counting of every soul in the kingdom, every name entered in a book, every book kept forever. A country taking attendance. And Edmund Iversen, who had been three people in his life and was currently none of them, would be asked to raise his hand and swear to a name.

He turned his face to the wall. Below him, the sea went on with its long argument, and the naval tender's lights moved in their patient pattern, and somewhere across the water a woman who counted candles slept with her hand over her son's shoulder, believing her brother dull and safe.

In the morning he would carry the letters up on the silver salver, and Adeline Marsh would take the Vienna envelope last, as she took all foreign post, holding it at arm's length and squinting, and she would say—as she always said—"Nothing from anyone interesting, Iversen?" and he would answer, as he always answered, "Nothing at all, miss."

And the census man, when he came, would come to a house where every letter was read twice, and every answer rehearsed, and every soul accounted for except the one doing the accounting.

The clock in the hall struck midnight. Edmund closed his eyes and began, out of habit, to count.
