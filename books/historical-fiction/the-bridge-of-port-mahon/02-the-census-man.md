## 2. The Census Man

The morning the census man came to Wrenfield House, Edmund Iversen was polishing the bonnet of the Daimler and pretending, as he had pretended for eleven years, that his hands belonged to a man with nothing to hide.

It was the second of April, 1911 — census night's eve, when every soul in England was to be counted like coins in a miser's palm — and the light came off the harbor in long silver blades that caught the chrome and made it burn. Edmund worked the chamois in slow circles. He had learned long ago that a man who moved too quickly drew the eye, and a man who drew the eye was a man who was remembered, and a man who was remembered was, in the end, a man who was found.

Inside the house, Adeline Marsh was dictating letters at a pace that would have frightened a court stenographer. He could hear her voice through the open window of the morning room, rising and falling like weather: *tell Mrs. Pankhurst we will have two hundred women on the quay if it kills us; tell the printer the pamphlets are late and I will come down there myself.* The household had learned to arrange itself around that voice the way iron filings arrange around a magnet. Cook planned meals she could carry up to the study on trays. The maids had stopped flinching at slammed doors. And Edmund — Edmund drove, read, remembered, and kept his own history sealed inside a locked satchel beneath the false bottom of his wardrobe, where no voice, however magnetic, would ever think to look.

He heard the gate before he saw the man.

That was the first thing wrong with him, though Edmund did not know it yet: he announced himself. Most visitors to Wrenfield House crashed through the gate like tide through a breakwater — suffragists, journalists, the occasional constable with more courage than sense. This man opened it gently, latched it behind him, and stood for a moment on the gravel path as though admiring the rhododendrons.

He was small and neat and gray in the way of things designed not to be noticed: gray suit, gray hat, gray eyes behind round spectacles. A leather portfolio under one arm. A pencil tucked behind one ear like a carpenter's. He walked up the drive with the unhurried gait of a man whose business would wait for him because it was, in fact, waiting for everyone in the country tonight.

"Good morning," the man said. His voice was soft, almost apologetic. "Mr. Quill. Census enumerator, Port Mahon district." He produced a card and held it out with both hands, as though returning something borrowed. "I'm afraid I must trouble the household with a few questions before tomorrow night."

Edmund set down the chamois. He wiped his hands on his trousers — an ordinary gesture, a chauffeur's gesture — and took the card. *E. QUILL, ENUMERATOR,* it said, and beneath it, in smaller type, *By Appointment of H.M. Government.*

"Miss Marsh is at home," Edmund said. "Though her time is much spoken for."

"I shan't take five minutes of it. In fact—" Mr. Quill smiled, and the smile was genuinely kind, which was the second thing wrong with him, "—it isn't Miss Marsh I've mostly come about. It's the household. The schedule asks after *every person who slept under this roof on Sunday last*, you see. Servants included. Especially servants." He said it without malice, the way a man might say *especially the corners* while dusting. "The government is very keen on servants this year. There's been such movement of population, such coming and going. They want the picture complete."

"Of course," Edmund said.

He felt it then — not fear exactly, but its quiet cousin, the one that lived permanently in the basement of him and climbed the stairs only on special occasions. Eleven years since anyone official had asked him to account for himself. Eleven years of being furniture, of being *the driver*, of existing in other people's records only as a name on a wage book: E. IVERSEN, CHAUFFEUR, written by Adeline's own hand, blessed by her indifference. He had built his invisibility the way masons build arches — stone by stone, each lie bearing the weight of the next — and he had nearly forgotten it was architecture at all. It felt like simply standing there.

"Shall we go in?" Mr. Quill asked.

* * *

They sat in the morning room, which Adeline had vacated with a look at Edmund that said, plainly, *handle it, you're better at this than I am at being polite.* She had taken her correspondence upstairs. Through the ceiling came the faint percussion of her pacing.

Mr. Quill opened his portfolio and laid out the household schedule on the low table between them. It was a printed form, dense and official, ruled into little boxes like a window of a prison. At the top, in a clerk's hand already: *WRENFIELD HOUSE, HARBOUR ROAD, PORT MAHON.*

"Now," he said, pencil poised. "Miss Adeline Marsh, head of household. Born 1868, London. Occupation —" he glanced up, and there was the ghost of humor in it, "—shall I write 'agitator,' or has she a preference?"

"'Suffragist' is the word she'd insist upon," Edmund said. "'Agitator' she'd frame and hang over the mantel."

Quill wrote *suffragist* and seemed to mean it respectfully. Then the pencil moved down the form, box by box, and Edmund answered each question in the flat, unmemorable voice he had practiced in front of dark windows for a decade.

Cook: Margaret Doyle, born County Cork, forty-nine years of age. Housemaid: Alice Penhale, Cornish, nineteen. Under-gardener, part-time: Samuel Voss. All true, all verifiable, all dull as ditchwater. Edmund gave their ages and birthplaces without hesitation because he had memorized them years ago — not for the census, but because knowing the staff thoroughly was part of keeping the household safe, and keeping the household safe was the one honest religion he had left.

"And yourself," Quill said. "Edmund Iversen. Chauffeur and — the wage book says 'general man.' Is that right?"

"That's right."

"Born?"

Here it was. The little box. The little window.

"Bergen," Edmund said. "Norway. 1874."

He said it the way he always said it: smoothly, with just enough accent remaining in his English to make the claim self-evident, with just enough boredom to make it uninteresting. He had been Norwegian longer than he had been anything else now. There were days — fewer than there used to be, but they came — when he woke and reached for his mother's language before he reached for any other, and found the Norwegian arriving first, obedient, fluent, a servant in its own right.

"Parents?" Quill asked. "Names of parents, for the schedule. It helps with future identification."

"They are dead," Edmund said. "Some years ago. I don't — the names wouldn't be useful."

"Still, the form prefers—"

"Halvor and Greta Iversen," Edmund said, and gave the dead their borrowed names calmly, without blinking, because he had given them so many times they had become real to him, poor ghosts, carrying his sins for him across the water. Halvor and Greta Iversen of Bergen, who had never existed, who had died childless in a country that had never held them, and whom Edmund honored every day of his life by keeping them alive in the mouths of strangers.

Quill wrote it down. The pencil scratched, small and dry, like a mouse in a wall.

"And how long have you been in England, Mr. Iversen?"

"Eleven years."

"Arrived how? Ship, or—"

"The ferry. From Bergen to Hull." Smoothly. "Then rail south. I'd rather not reconstruct the whole itinerary; it was a miserable journey and I've spent eleven years trying to forget it."

Quill laughed — a small, genuine laugh, apologetic even in its amusement. "Quite understood. My own crossing from Ireland in '89 was enough to put me off boats for life." He turned the schedule toward himself and began transcribing the boxes into his master ledger, and for a moment the room was silent except for the pencil and, overhead, Adeline's ceaseless pacing, and beyond the window the gulls crying over the harbor where, these past weeks, the gray ships had been gathering like crows on a fence.

Edmund let himself breathe.

That was his mistake, or perhaps it was simply the truth of him: he let himself believe, for the length of that silence, that it was finished. That the armor had held. That invisibility, faithfully maintained, was its own kind of citizenship.

Mr. Quill finished writing. He squared the pages. He put his pencil back behind his ear. And then he did not leave.

Instead he looked out the window at the Daimler, gleaming in the light, and said, mildly, conversationally, the way a man remarks on weather:

"She's a fine car. You keep her beautifully. Not many chauffeurs take that kind of pride." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "You can tell a great deal about a man by how he maintains what's entrusted to him. Whether he treats what isn't his as though it were." He turned back, smiling his kind gray smile. "My father was a sexton. He used to say the same about gravestones. *The ones worth trusting are the ones who tend stones that aren't their family's.*"

"How interesting," Edmund said.

"Mm." Quill gathered his portfolio. Then, at the door, with his hand already on the frame, he paused — and it was done so naturally, so artlessly, that a less practiced man would have missed it entirely — and said:

"Oh — one small thing. The 1901 schedule. For this district." He tapped the portfolio. "I cross-check the old books against the new; it's how we catch errors, duplicates, that sort of thing. And your name doesn't appear in the 1901 returns for Port Mahon. Nor for Hull, though I confess I've only the index so far." He adjusted his spectacles. "People move, of course. Constantly. It explains itself nine times in ten."

"And the tenth?" Edmund asked. His voice was perfectly level. Later, alone, he would marvel at that — that the voice had held, that the hands hanging at his sides had not shaken, that somewhere inside him there was still a boy from the checkpoint nights who could stand absolutely still while a wolf sniffed along the wall.

"Oh, the tenth is usually just my predecessor's bad handwriting," Quill said cheerfully. "The man drank." He tipped his hat. "Thank you for your patience, Mr. Iversen. Give my regards to Miss Marsh. I do admire her — whatever one thinks of the cause, a woman that brave is a national treasure."

And he went, latching the gate softly behind him, walking away down Harbour Road with the unhurried gait of a man whose business would wait for him.

* * *

Edmund stood in the morning room for a long time after the front door closed.

The light had shifted. The silver blades off the harbor had swung around and now lay across the floorboards like bars, and he found he could not look at them without seeing another floor, another light, another country — the customs house at the frontier in the autumn of 1900, the smell of wet wool and kerosene, Marta's hand gripping his sleeve hard enough to bruise, the children asleep against her coat, and the papers trembling in his own fingers: papers meant for the dead, three names that had never breathed, carried past a lantern-lit table by a young man whose heart was going like a bird in a box. *Purpose of travel? Family visit. Duration of stay? Indefinite. Names?* And he had spoken the dead aloud, and the dead had vouched for him, and the lantern had swung on.

Eleven years. He had counted them like a miser counts coins, and he had believed — God help him, he had actually believed — that the counting was over.

He went upstairs, quietly, past the morning room's open door, past the kitchen where Cook was singing low over her dough, past all the warm ordinary machinery of the household that did not know what it was sheltering. In his narrow room above the coach house he knelt, dragged the wardrobe aside, pried up the board, and lifted out the satchel.

It was a plain thing, oilcloth over wood, brass-buckled, unremarkable — the kind of bag a commercial traveler carries samples in. Inside it lay everything he was: two photographs, a brass key to a house that no longer existed, and a sheaf of papers so old their edges had gone the color of tea. He did not open it. He only held it, feeling through the oilcloth the shape of the lives folded there, the way a man might hold a closed Bible and be comforted by the weight of it without reading a word.

Three people who no longer exist, the papers said. Three names on documents issued to corpses, carried across a border by a man who had then built himself a fourth name and lived inside it for a decade like a hermit in a cell he'd mistaken for the world.

And now a small gray man with a kind smile had gone looking through the old books, and found the cell empty in 1901, and written something down anyway.

Because that was the detail that would not leave him. Not the questions — questions were weather, questions came and went. It was the moment at the very end, when Quill had gathered his portfolio and squared his pages, and Edmund, watching with a chauffeur's idle attention, had seen the pencil come back out one final time and make a small notation on a separate slip — not on the schedule, not in the ledger, but on a private slip of paper that was then folded away into the breast pocket, not the portfolio.

A note for himself. A thread tied around a finger.

Edmund replaced the satchel, slid the wardrobe back, and sat down on the narrow bed with his hands between his knees. Outside, the gulls wheeled over the harbor, and somewhere among the gray ships a bell rang the hour, four strokes, unhurried, indifferent, the sound of a clock that did not know it was counting down.

He thought: *I will tell no one. It is nothing. A clerk's thoroughness. A drunk predecessor's gaps in the record. Nine times in ten it explains itself.*

He thought: *and the tenth?*

Overhead, Adeline had stopped pacing. In the sudden quiet he could hear her at the window, looking out — he knew her silhouette by heart, the sharp chin lifted, the whole body leaning toward the horizon as if the future were a ship she intended to board whether it stopped for her or not. Tomorrow night the whole nation would be counted. Every soul in its little box. And somewhere in a gray office, a small gray man would sit down with his ledgers, and unfold a private slip of paper, and begin — patiently, politely, with the terrible diligence of the harmless — to count backward.

Edmund Iversen got up, splashed water on his face, straightened his collar, and went downstairs to lay out Adeline's evening car with steady hands and a face like a locked room.

But that night, long after the house slept, he took the satchel out again, and this time he opened it, and by candlelight he began — for the first time in eleven years — to decide what could be burned.
