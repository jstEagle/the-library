## 3. Adeline's War

Adeline Marsh had learned long ago that the best arguments were made standing up.

She stood now at the head of the dining table in the house on Cavendish Row, the good silver pushed aside to make room for maps of Manchester, a stack of handbills still smelling of ink, and a teapot that had gone cold two hours ago because nobody remembered to send for fresh. The morning light came through the tall windows in grey bars and lay across the table like ruled lines on a page she intended to write all over.

"Saturday," she said. "Not Sunday — the mills let out Saturday at noon, and half of them are already angry about the wage reductions. We don't want them thinking about their supper. We want them thinking about the vote."

Mrs. Pankhurst's people wanted Sunday, but Mrs. Pankhurst's people weren't here, and Adeline had stopped deferring to London the way one stops believing in a distant monarch: politely, completely, and without announcing it. Manchester would have its own rally, its own voice, its own fire. She'd been building toward this for three years — the reading rooms, the letters columns she'd fought the *Guardian* to print, the quiet subscriptions from mill girls who gave sixpence they could not spare.

"Ellen, the banners. The old ones are fine for the march but I want something new for the platform. Something they can read from the back of Albert Square." She tapped the map. "Here. Steps of the Exchange if they'll allow it, which they won't, so — the cart. Tomas can bring the cart round, we'll drape it."

Tomas, the stable boy, looked up from where he was mending a harness strap by the door, pleased and terrified in equal measure, and Adeline felt the small warm pang she always felt around him and immediately converted it into efficiency, because feeling was fuel and fuel was not for idling.

"And the speakers." She counted on her fingers, a habit her mother had called vulgar and which Adeline had therefore made deliberately conspicuous. "I'll open. Miss Hartley on the wages question — she works it, she can speak it. Then the pledge, then the collection, then we're gone before the police decide we've been an inconvenience long enough."

"You think there'll be trouble?" Ellen asked. Ellen was forty, widowed, and had the unglamorous courage of a woman who had buried a husband and discovered nothing else could kill her.

"There's always trouble," Adeline said. "That's rather the point. If they left us alone it would mean we weren't saying anything worth hearing." She gathered the handbills into a tidy block and squared their edges against the wood. "But I don't want trouble on Saturday. Trouble is for when we choose it. Saturday should be too big, too orderly, and too respectable to break up. A thousand women standing quietly in their best coats frightens a magistrate far worse than a riot."

She was aware, as she spoke, of the clock on the mantel ticking through her sentences like a chaperone, and of the fact that she had been talking for eleven minutes without anyone interrupting her, which was both her right and her particular loneliness.

* * *

The car came round at ten as it always came round at ten, and Adeline went down the steps with her list still in her hand, dictating errands to the day itself.

Edmund Iversen held the door for her.

He always held the door. It was a small thing, a servant's thing, and yet he did it with a gravity that made it feel less like service than like ceremony — as though the door between her and the street were a border crossing and he were the official who decided, daily, in her favor. He was a tall man gone slightly stooped, Danish or Norwegian by his name though his English had no accent she could place, with grey coming into his hair early and hands that looked as though they had done work rougher than steering a motorcar before they ever came to her. He had appeared four years ago with references she had never checked — a failing she told herself was trust and knew was indifference; she hired drivers the way she hired causes, on instinct and momentum — and he had never once given her cause to regret it.

"Good morning, Miss Marsh."

"Mr. Iversen. The Banner office first, then Deansgate, then home by two — I've correspondence." She settled onto the leather seat and arranged her skirts with a briskness that had frightened two previous drivers into resignation. Edmund was not frightened by it. She had privately concluded he was not frightened by anything, which either meant great courage or great practice, and she had never decided which, and had never asked, because asking questions of servants about their pasts was the sort of thing her father had done and she had sworn off her father's habits along with his politics.

Except that lately — and she noticed this the way one notices a draft, peripherally, with irritation — he had begun to look at her as if he were memorizing her. Not admiring. Memorizing. There was a difference, and the difference had a weight to it, like a letter sealed but not sent.

"The rally," she said, as the car pulled out into the Row. "Saturday. You'll need to take the cart down Friday evening and park it behind the Exchange. Tomas will help you. If any constable asks, it's a delivery for Whitworth's the drapers."

"Yes, miss."

"I'm not going to ask you to lie to the police, Mr. Iversen. Merely to be economical with the truth in advance."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile; a recognition. "Economy," he said, "is the soul of good driving."

She laughed despite herself, a short bark of a laugh she'd been teased about at school, and turned to the window so he wouldn't see how much she'd enjoyed being understood. The streets of Manchester unspooled past the glass: soot-black brick, washing strung between chimneys like flags of some exhausted nation, men in caps walking fast to work they might lose by Christmas if the rumors of war kept hardening into orders. Ships were being taken up at the ports — she'd read it in the shipping pages, dry little notices that were not dry at all if you knew how to read them. *Requisitioned for government service.* The empire was quietly gathering its coat, preparing to stand up from the table.

And what would happen, she wondered — not for the first time — to a woman who had spent a decade making herself impossible to ignore, when the whole country began demanding silence and obedience? They would come for the suffragists first. They always came for the loud ones first. Force-feeding, cat-and-mouse, the prison van — she had catalogued the machinery of suppression the way other women catalogued recipes, and she was not afraid of it, or rather she was afraid of it in the way one is afraid of weather: with respect, and an umbrella, and no intention of staying indoors.

"Miss Marsh." His voice from the front, careful. "There's a man been standing at the corner of the Row since Tuesday. Grey coat. I've seen him three mornings."

"A journalist?"

"Possibly."

"Then he'll be bored to death by Saturday." But she filed it away, the way she filed everything — grey coat, corner of the Row, Tuesday — in the same relentless ledger where she kept every insult printed about her and every vote lost by two. She did not notice that Edmund's hands had shifted on the wheel, nor that he checked the mirror twice in the next mile. She was composing Saturday's speech. There was always a speech composing itself somewhere in her, like water finding a level.

* * *

At the Banner office — two rooms above a tobacconist on Oldham Street, smelling of paper and pipe smoke and other women's determination — the morning dissolved into the beautiful, exhausting machinery of the cause.

A girl named Winnie, nineteen and fearless, had been arrested at a meeting in Salford and released overnight, and arrived with her collar torn and her eyes blazing and was embraced by six women at once. Adeline held her at arm's length, inspected the torn collar, and said, "We'll say you fought them off with the hymnal," which made Winnie laugh until she cried, and then cry until she laughed, and Adeline understood — she always understood — that the laughing-crying was the truest report anyone would file that week.

Letters came and were answered. Money came and was counted twice. A printer demanded payment and was charmed into another fortnight's credit by a woman who could have talked a customs officer out of his boots. And through all of it Adeline felt the thing she felt only here, among these women, in these shabby rooms: *useful*. Undivided. Every hour accounted for, every grief converted to agenda, no space left empty enough for doubt to move into.

That was what the movement was, though she'd never say it aloud even to herself in quite these words: it was a room you could live inside. It had walls of purpose and a roof of sisterhood and it kept out the weather — the weather being everything else: her dead mother's disappointment, the suitors who had wanted her decorative, the slow horror of watching her father's world make war on everything soft and call it order. Inside the movement she was not a spinster or an eccentric or a difficult daughter. She was a weapon with a handle, and everyone agreed handles were necessary.

It was only later — much later, years later, when she would have time to count what the shield had cost her — that she would understand the movement had also been an exposure. That a woman who lives publicly cannot hide anything, least of all from herself, and certainly not the people she loves, because love requires privacy and she had donated all of hers to the cause like a widow donating her wedding ring to the war fund.

But that afternoon she was thirty-eight years old and invincible and planning a revolution for Saturday noon.

* * *

They got back to Cavendish Row near seven. The house smelled of boiled mutton and lamp oil. Ellen had laid out the post on the hall table, and Adeline sorted it standing up, because sitting down was for people with fewer obligations: a bill from the printer (again), a circular from London (she skimmed it with the affectionate impatience of a provincial who loved the capital the way one loves a brilliant, selfish relative), and a thick envelope with a foreign stamp, addressed to her in a hand she didn't know — but the name on it, *Iversen*, was written in the corner as sender, in a different hand entirely, smaller, older, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper.

Edmund's mail. Which was, she realized with a small start of surprise, the first piece of Edmund's mail she had ever seen in this house in four years. Did he receive none? Did he write none? A man of forty-some years with no correspondence was either a monk or a fugitive, and he was neither, presumably —

"Miss Marsh?" He had come into the hall to take her coat, and his eyes touched the foreign envelope for exactly half a second — she caught it, that flicker, because she missed nothing, missing things was for the defeated — and then went carefully blank.

"It's yours, I imagine." She held it out. "Foreign stamp. Someone writes to you after all."

"No one important, miss."

"That's a shame. Important letters are the only kind worth the postage." She smiled at him, teasing, testing, the way she tested everyone, and watched the blankness hold, and thought — briefly, absurdly — that behind those calm eyes was a locked room, and that she was a woman who picked locks professionally and had never once tried his door.

Why hadn't she?

Because he was useful and loyal and private, and privacy was a wage like any other, and she paid her people well. Because she had built a household on the radical principle that people were entitled to their own histories. Because she was tired, always tired, and the satchel of someone else's secrets was heavy, and hers were full enough.

All true. None sufficient. But the envelope passed between them, and the moment passed with it, and dinner was announced, and the empire of the ordinary closed over the question like water over a dropped coin.

* * *

She worked late. She always worked late. In the study with the green lamp lit, she drafted Saturday's speech in pencil, crossed out the first two openings as too gentle and the third as too proud, and settled on beginning with Winnie — *a girl of nineteen was dragged from a peaceful meeting this week, and I am told this is law and order* — and felt the old current come up through her, the one that made her voice carry to the back of Albert Square without effort, as if the words weighed less than air.

At midnight she put down the pencil and found herself, instead of going up to bed, standing at the study window looking down at the drive, where the motorcar sat dark under the plane tree.

And she thought about Edmund.

Not in the way the novels would have it — she was fifty years past mistaking gratitude for romance, and besides, there was something about him that made romance feel beside the point, like bringing flowers to a lighthouse keeper. She thought about him the way one thinks about a load-bearing wall: what the house would be without him. Four years of cold mornings started without complaint, of punctuality like a form of devotion, of the door held, the coat taken, the dangerous drives to hostile towns executed with a steadiness that let her step out onto platforms with her heart rate normal. Once, in Burnley, a stone had come through the crowd and he had stepped — not in front of her, that would have been theatrical — but *near*, casually near, the way a wall is near, and she had finished her sentence without losing her place.

He had never asked for anything. That was the thing that snagged her now, at midnight, like a splinter under the skin. Everyone asked her for something — money, signatures, courage, her name on their letterhead, her hours, her spine. The whole architecture of her life was load-bearing in both directions and she bore most of it gladly. But Edmund asked for nothing, offered nothing, revealed nothing. He was the only person in her acquaintance whose interior she could not sketch from evidence, and she was a woman who had built a career on sketching interiors from evidence.

*No one important, miss.*

Liar, she thought, without heat. Kind liar. Careful liar. Whatever was in that foreign envelope had mattered enormously, and he had carried it upstairs with a face like a locked room — no. Like a locked room with someone standing very quietly behind the door.

She ought to ask him. She was Adeline Marsh; asking was what she did; she had made a religion of the direct question. And yet some instinct — the same instinct that had kept her alive through fifteen years of public war — told her that this particular lock would not thank her for picking it, and that whatever was behind it, he was carrying it *for* someone, not hiding it *from* her, and there was a difference, and the difference deserved respect.

So instead she did the thing she would never have admitted to doing. She took a clean sheet of paper and wrote, in the shorthand of her private notes — the code she used for names that must never appear in seized correspondence — three lines:

*I. — steady. Ask nothing. Watch the grey coat for him. Saturday, keep the car ready at the side street, not the square. If it goes wrong he gets her out first.*

*Him* meaning herself, of course, rendered in the third person because even her tenderness had to wear a disguise to get past her own censors.

She looked at what she'd written, and snorted at herself — *sentimental old woman* — and almost burned it, and didn't, because her notes were her conscience's ledger and she kept everything.

Then she blew out the lamp, and in the dark she allowed herself one full minute — sixty seconds, she counted them, discipline even in this — of the truth, which was simply this: that the movement was her country and her marriage and her church, yes, and she would die for it, yes; but that on the night before a battle, the person whose steadiness she counted on the way soldiers count on ground beneath their feet was a quiet man with a foreign name who held her door every morning and told her nothing, and that she trusted him further than she had ever trusted anyone, and that this frightened her more than prison did.

One minute. Then she went up to bed, and slept the sleep of the just and the busy, and dreamed, if she dreamed at all, of Saturday.

She did not hear, below her window, the front door close softly at half past one, or the small sounds of a man burning papers in the yard — sounds that carried, faintly, all the way up to the room where Tomas, unable to sleep, lay staring at the ceiling and wondering what Mr. Iversen was doing with the fire at such an hour.

Nor did she know that the grey-coated man had been joined, that evening, by a second man, and that together they had walked the length of Cavendish Row once, slowly, like men taking measurements.

Saturday was four days away. The clock on the mantel ticked on through the sleeping house, keeping time for a household that did not yet know how little of it remained.
