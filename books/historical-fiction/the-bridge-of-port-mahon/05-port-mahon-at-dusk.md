## 5. Port Mahon at Dusk

### Mrs. Calloway

Mrs. Calloway had kept the kitchen of the Marsh household for eleven years, and in all that time she had learned to read the town the way other women read weather. Port Mahon spoke to her through the market bell, through the price of herring, through the particular quality of silence that fell over the fishwives when something was wrong. On this evening in late September, the fishwives were silent as stones.

She stood at her window above the scullery with a dishcloth forgotten in her hands and watched the harbor road fill with a procession that had no business existing in peacetime. Naval lorries, six of them, canvas-backed and rattling, their drivers young men who did not look at the shops or the children or the gulls but straight ahead, as though the town itself were an obstacle between them and some larger purpose. Behind the lorries came a requisition officer on a grey horse, checking houses against a list bound in red leather.

"Requisition," Mrs. Calloway said to the empty kitchen, tasting the word like spoiled milk. She had heard it first from the butcher's boy that morning—*they're taking the* Meridian *and the* Cora B, *ma'am, right out of the fishing fleet*—and now here was the proof, rolling past her window in a cloud of dust and diesel.

The kettle began its low complaint behind her, and she turned back to the stove because a kitchen, whatever else the world did to it, still required supper. But her hands moved without their old ease. Eleven years she had cooked for Miss Adeline Marsh, through the pamphlet raids and the hunger strikes and the winter when plainclothesmen sat across the street for three weeks smoking cheap tobacco and pretending to read the same newspaper. She knew what it looked like when the state turned its attention toward a household. It looked like this: polite, methodical, patient. A list in red leather.

She set out six plates instead of five. Then she looked at them, frowned at her own arithmetic—the master of the house, Miss Adeline, Mr. Iversen, herself and the girls—and put one plate away again. But the feeling stayed with her, that someone at this table would soon be missing, and she could not have said why, only that the harbor had told her so.

Down in the yard, the stable boy was brushing a horse that didn't need brushing, working the currycomb in long strokes while his eyes followed the lorries out along the coast road.

"Supper's at seven sharp," she called through the window, more loudly than necessary. "Whatever else is happening in the world."

The boy raised a hand. He didn't turn around.

### Tomas

Tomas had counted eleven ships in the outer roads that morning, and by dusk he counted fourteen, their riding lights coming up out of the darkening water like a string of beads someone was slowly drawing tight. He sat on the paddock fence with his heels hooked in the lowest rail, a position forbidden him twice daily by Mrs. Calloway and ignored always, and he watched the harbor do a thing harbors were not supposed to do.

Ships came into Port Mahon to unload. Ships came into Port Mahon to take on salt fish, coal, crates of lemons from the groves inland. These ships took on nothing. They lay at anchor in tiers, grey shapes with greyer superstructures, and around them the little steam launches of the Admiralty moved all day like water-beetles, ferrying officers whose uniforms were too clean for working sailors and too new for veterans. Requisitioned, the butcher's boy had said. Every hull that could make eight knots or better. The fishermen had stood on the quay and watched their livelihoods painted naval grey before their eyes, and nobody had said a word to them, not even thank you.

Tomas was fifteen and had grown up in the shadow of these waters; his father had drowned off the Cora B when he was six, which meant the navy had taken from him once already, quietly, without paperwork. He understood things about ships that he could not have explained to a schoolmaster. And the ships tonight were telling him something he did not want to hear.

He looked down the paddock toward the stables, where a light burned in Mr. Iversen's small room above the tack room. The chauffeur was at his window. Tomas could see the shape of him, bent over something—a book, a letter, Tomas couldn't tell—and there was a stillness about that shape that snagged in the boy's attention. Most people moved even when they sat. Mr. Iversen, when he read, went absolutely motionless, like a heron at the water's edge, like something waiting for a fish.

Mr. Iversen had taught him to drive. Had shown him, patiently, over four months, how the clutch found its bite, how to listen to an engine the way you'd listen to a horse's lungs. Tomas trusted him the way you trust anyone who gives you their time without making you feel small for needing it. But tonight, watching that motionless silhouette against the lamplight while the warships multiplied in the roads below, Tomas felt the first cold thread of something he couldn't yet name. The whole town felt it, he realized. Everyone was standing very still, like the chauffeur at his window. Like herons. Like they'd all heard something under the water.

A launch went chugging past the breakwater, its searchlight sweeping the moorings. The beam climbed the cliff face, touched the top of the garden wall, and slid on. Tomas hugged his arms against the sudden chill and told himself the shiver was just the evening air off the sea.

It was not just the evening air.

### Ellen

Ellen, who was nineteen and the under-housemaid and had been in service since she was twelve, knew everything that happened in the front half of the house, because dust was honest and told you where people had been and letters left their impressions on blotting paper if you knew your trade. She was dusting the study shelves at half past six when Miss Adeline came through with her arms full of printed matter and her hat still on, which meant she'd come in fast and meant to go out again fast.

"Is Mr. Iversen back with the car?"

"Not yet, miss. He went down to the harbor office."

Something crossed Miss Adeline's face—not worry exactly; Ellen had never seen Miss Adeline worried, properly worried, and suspected the woman lacked the equipment—but a tightening, a quick recalculation, like a bill being totted up behind the eyes.

"The harbor office. Whatever for?"

"He didn't say, miss."

"Well." Miss Adeline dumped the pamphlets on the desk—*THE FRANCHISE BILL AND THE WORKING WOMAN*, two hundred of them, ink still smelling faintly of the press—and began pulling off her gloves finger by finger. "Then we shall assume the car is being repaired, or registered, or whatever one does with cars at harbor offices, and we shall assume dinner at the usual hour, and we shall assume that the world is continuing to conduct its business in an orderly fashion, despite considerable evidence to the contrary."

"Yes, miss."

Miss Adeline paused, gloves in hand, and looked out the window toward the water. From the study you could see the masts, thin and black against the last light, and the smoke of the collier that had come in at noon flying the White Ensign where no White Ensign had flown in years.

"They're taking the boats, Ellen," she said, and her voice had changed—it had dropped its public register and become, briefly, the voice of a woman alone in a room. "Did you see? They're simply taking them. The Peverell family lost their trawler today. Forty years in that family. An officer gave Mr. Peverell a receipt."

"A receipt, miss?"

"A receipt." She laughed, but it wasn't laughter, it was anger compressed until it made a sound like laughing. "As if the sea were a lending library." She pulled the gloves taut over her fingers. "This is how it begins, you know. Not with declarations. With receipts."

Then the public voice came back like a lamp being lit. "When Mr. Iversen returns, tell him I'll want the car at nine tomorrow. There's a meeting in Valletta Road and I intend to speak, whatever the Admiralty thinks it's doing with our coastline." She swept toward the door, then stopped, one hand on the frame, and said without turning: "And Ellen—if any gentleman calls asking after the household, any official gentleman, you will say the residents are Miss Adeline Marsh and her staff, and you will say it pleasantly, and you will come find me directly. Do you understand?"

"Yes, miss." A beat. "Has someone called, miss?"

Miss Adeline's reflection in the darkened glass considered her for a moment.

"Not yet," she said, and was gone down the corridor, her heels striking the boards like a metronome set to allegro.

Ellen stood among the shelves with her duster going slack in her hand. Not yet. She turned the phrase over the way she turned coins over, looking for the false metal in them. Then she went back to work, because the study wanted finishing before seven, and whatever was beginning in the harbor, the house still demanded its order, and order was the only country she held title to.

### Mrs. Calloway Again

By seven the dining table was laid, and by ten past seven it was clear that neither the master of the house nor the chauffeur would be sitting at it.

Miss Adeline ate standing up, which she did when agitated, moving between the sideboard and the window with a plate she never finished. Mrs. Calloway brought up the second course and found her staring at the harbor, where the searchlights had begun to move.

"You'll eat something proper, miss, or you'll be no use to anybody."

"I am of no use to anybody whatsoever, Mrs. Calloway, that is rather my entire argument with the government." But she sat, and she ate, and that was the thing about Miss Adeline that eleven years had taught Mrs. Calloway to trust: underneath all the fire and the flying gloves was a woman who could be governed by a good meal and a firm hand, same as anyone.

At half past seven, the sound of the car came up the hill—engine laboring in second gear, the particular note it made on the steep pitch past the church—and Mrs. Calloway went to the side door and watched Mr. Iversen bring the Hupmobile into the yard. He sat in it a moment after the engine died. Just sat, hands on the wheel, looking at nothing. In eleven years she had never seen him do that. Edmund Iversen was a man who stepped out of machines the moment they stopped, a man for whom idleness seemed physically painful.

Then he got out, and he was himself again—or nearly. His face had that polished quality she'd noticed sometimes in men back home who'd come back from somewhere they wouldn't name. Smooth as lake ice. You could see your own worry reflected in it, but nothing of his own.

"There's a letter come for you," she said. "On the hall tray. Came with the afternoon post."

"Thank you, Mrs. Calloway."

"And there's supper keeping."

"Later, perhaps."

He went past her into the hall, and she saw him stop at the tray, and she saw him look down at the letters lying there, and she saw—she was certain she saw, and would remember seeing it all her life—the color leave his face. Not dramatically. The way a tide goes out. One moment the skin was ordinary flesh, the next it was the color of the underside of shells.

One envelope among the bills and the pamphlet proofs was different. Foreign stamps. A hand that trembled, or had been written by someone walking, or both. Mrs. Calloway couldn't read the address from where she stood, and wouldn't have tried; reading other people's post was Ellen's vice, not hers.

Mr. Iversen picked up that one letter and left the rest where they lay, and carried it upstairs in his flat palm the way you carry a live coal, and Mrs. Calloway closed the side door against the smell of the sea and the sound of the fleet anchoring, and went back to her kitchen, where the sixth plate still waited in the cupboard like a question nobody had asked aloud.

### The House

And the house itself—that should be recorded too, because houses know things before their people do. The old stone of it, raised when the harbor was a pirate anchorage and every wall built thick enough to stop a cannonball, settled into the September night with its usual creaks and exhalations. The chimney drew. The beams ticked as they cooled. In the nursery wing, long unused, a door that never quite latched worked itself open an inch and breathed the salt air down the corridor.

Below in the harbor, fourteen ships rode at anchor, and by midnight there would be seventeen, and the men aboard them slept badly in hammocks that smelled of paint and gunpowder, and the officers played cards under electric light and did not speak of the date circled on the chart table in the flagship's cabin.

In the small room above the tack room, Edmund Iversen sat with Marta's letter open on his knee and did not move for a very long time, and the lamplight held him in its circle the way the harbor held its ships: safely, temporarily, with the certain knowledge that the wind would change.

Down the hill, the clock over the customs house struck nine. Its bell had sounded over Port Mahon for a hundred and forty years, marking hours that had always come round again.

Tonight, for the first time in living memory, it sounded less like a clock than like a countdown.
