## 6. Marta's Letter

The letter had been sitting in his coat pocket for three days.

Edmund knew this the way a man knows a stone in his shoe—not as information but as a constant, low-grade pressure against the architecture of his attention. He had steamed it open on Tuesday night in the scullery, as he did all of Miss Marsh's correspondence, and he had read the first line—*Edmund, if this reaches you it means the address still holds*—and then he had folded it closed along its original creases, resealed the flap with the thin glue stick he kept for exactly this purpose, and set it on the tray with the others to be carried up in the morning as though nothing in the world had changed.

Nothing in the world had changed. That was the lie he was now living inside, and it fit him badly, like a coat cut for a narrower man.

He had not read past the first line. He told himself this was discipline. A driver who lingered over his employer's post was a driver who could be dismissed; a driver who read only what was necessary was invisible, and invisibility was the whole of his craft, eleven years practiced, eleven years perfected. But he knew, walking the car around to the mews that evening, checking the tyres by lamplight longer than tyres required, that it was not discipline at all. It was fear. The letter was from Marta. Marta was alive. And a thing that alive, that real, that suddenly *possible* after eleven years of impossibility, could break a man's careful life the way ice breaks underfoot—one crack, quiet, and then all of it gone beneath you.

* * *

On Thursday night the house went dark early. Miss Marsh had spoken until nearly midnight with two women from Manchester, and their voices through the study door had the bright, hard ring of plans being made—print runs, meeting halls, the names of magistrates said with contempt. Then the front door, then the motorcar pulling away into the fog, and the house settled into the particular silence Edmund knew best: the silence of a household that trusted him enough to sleep.

He sat in the kitchen with the letter before him and a single candle lit, because electric light felt too much like an audience.

The envelope was cheap paper, the kind sold in railway stations, and the hand was Marta's but thinner than he remembered, the letters leaning as if walking into wind. She had addressed it to *Mr. E. Iversen*, which meant she knew the name, which meant someone had carried word to her across borders and years—the old network, or what remained of it—and the thought that his name existed anywhere outside his own mouth made the back of his neck prickle. Names were how men were found. He had spent a decade teaching himself that lesson and here it was, taught again, in her handwriting.

He broke the seal he had already broken once and read.

*Edmund, if this reaches you it means the address still holds, which means you are still clever, which I never doubted even when I hated you for it.*

*I will not write the old story. You know the old story better than I do; you lived the worst hours of it and I slept through some of them in the hay, God forgive me, a girl of nineteen sleeping while my brother committed the crimes that saved us. I have thought for years that I would begin any letter to you with an apology and now that the letter is actually in my hands I find I cannot do it. Not because I am not sorry. Because sorry is a small coin and what happened between us was a large country.*

*I am in Trieste. Or near it. The less said of where the better, you will understand. Stefan is dead.*

Edmund stopped. The candle flame stood perfectly still, as if it too were waiting.

*Not in the way you are thinking—not politics, not soldiers. Fever, in the winter of '04, and there was no doctor because we had no money for a doctor, and I have made my peace with that in the way one makes peace with weather. He was six. He would be thirteen now. Sometimes I see boys of thirteen on the street and my body turns toward them before my mind can stop it, the way a compass turns north, uselessly, forever.*

*Lena is alive. Lena is fifteen and tall as a door and has my mother's temper and my father's stubbornness and no memory at all of the country we came from, which I used to grieve and now think may be the single mercy of our lives. She does not know what our name cost. I have kept it from her the way you keep papers from a child—wrapped, high on a shelf, spoken of never.*

*I am writing to ask you for the impossible, and I know it is impossible, and I am asking anyway, because you once told me that asking was the only prayer you believed in.*

*I want to come to you. Both of us. England, or wherever you are—I know only that it is an island, and islands have water around them, and water is the one border they cannot build a wall across. I have nothing. I mean this literally: I have a coat, a photograph of Stefan, and eleven years of work in other people's kitchens. If you cannot take us I will understand it completely and I will never think less of you, because I know what it costs to move a person across the world illegally, I stood in the hay cart, I heard the dogs.*

*But Edmund. Brother. If there is any arithmetic by which it can be done, do it. I have done my own sums every night for eleven years and they always come out the same: one sister, one niece, one ocean, zero chances. Change one number for me. Change just one.*

*Your Marta*

*P.S. — Lena asks if you are tall. I told her you were tall and serious and that you smelled of engine oil and ink. She says she hopes you are not serious.*

He read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way one reads a document one intends to survive by, because that was what it was—it was a summons, a plea, a bill of charges, and a map of everything he had lost, all in four pages of stationery that smelled faintly of coal smoke and another country.

Then he did the thing he always did with unbearable documents: he memorized it, folded it along its creases, and burned it over the sink, feeding it to the flame corner by corner so that no fragment larger than a fingernail survived, washing the ash down the drain with water hot enough to scald. The photograph of Stefan he could not burn because he had never seen it, and this absence hurt in a strange, specific place—he could picture the boy at six from memory, the gap in his teeth, the way he'd gripped Edmund's finger crossing the checkpoint field, but the boy was frozen there forever, six years old in 1900, and would be six years old until Edmund died, while the real boy had grown and sickened and died without him ever knowing. Eleven years. He had been eleven years away from a nephew who needed a doctor, and he had spent those eleven years learning the correct pressure to apply to a brake pedal on a wet hill.

He put out the candle and sat in the dark and let himself, for exactly five minutes by the kitchen clock, feel all of it.

* * *

In the morning he was himself again. This was the trick of him, the craft of him: grief filed neatly behind competence. He brought the motorcar around at half eight, held the door for Miss Marsh, took her dictation at the traffic lights—*tell Mrs. Pankhurst's office Thursday stands, tell the printer the pamphlets go out regardless of the police, tell my sister she may come Sunday and bring the children*—and none of it touched him, because he had built the man called Edmund Iversen precisely so that none of it could touch him.

Except that driving past the harbor he found himself counting ships.

It was involuntary, the way a gambler counts cards. The naval gray had multiplied along the quay since spring—destroyers now, and a pair of heavier silhouettes out beyond the mole that made even the fishermen stop mending their nets to look. Requisition notices had gone up on the customs house in official black. The ferry office had a new sign in the window: SAILINGS SUBJECT TO ALTERATION. And Edmund, who had crossed borders with forged papers in a hay cart while dogs bayed behind him, understood with the cold clarity of long experience what *subject to alteration* meant. It meant the clock had started. It meant that every week the water between England and the continent was becoming less a road and more a wall, and that the window through which a woman and a girl might legally—or even illegally—pass was closing at a speed no one civilian could see yet.

One berth, he thought, stopped at the lights on the harbor road, watching a steamer take on coal. Two berths. Passage for two, third class, from Trieste or Genoa or Marseille—Marseille was better, Marseille had people who owed the old network favors, if the old network still existed, if the people who owed them weren't dead or arrested or renamed. Papers for two. And papers, he thought, and his hands tightened on the wheel, papers were the problem, weren't they. Papers were the whole of the problem.

Because he could not forge anymore. That was the plain fact underneath everything, the fact Mr. Quill's polite questions had written into his bones last month. There was a man in a government office somewhere with a file, and the file contained discrepancies—three names on census records that matched three names on no baptismal roll, no parish register, no school ledger anywhere in England. Edmund Iversen, who had appeared from nowhere in 1901 with excellent references and no history. Somewhere a clerk had noticed, and clerks who noticed got promoted, and promoted clerks asked more questions, and every forged document Edmund produced now was not a bridge but a signature—an exhibit, evidence, a thread leading directly back through the false name to whatever remained of the true one.

For eleven years forgery had been his art. Now each stroke of the pen was a confession.

And yet. *Change one number for me.* Her voice was so clear in his head he almost turned to the passenger seat. Marta at nineteen in the hay, holding Stefan's mouth shut during the dog-pass, her eyes enormous in the moonlight, and afterward, on the ship, when the boy finally slept, she had said: *You counted us onto that boat like coins, Edmund. Don't ever count me. I'm not money.* And he had promised. He had promised a great many things in 1900, standing on a deck with salt wind tearing at them, watching the country they could never return to slide backward into haze.

The lights changed. He drove on.

* * *

That evening he took out the satchel.

It lived at the bottom of his trunk, beneath spare collars and a wool jersey, locked, the brass key on the same chain as his watch because the best hiding places are the ones worn openly. He waited until the house was asleep, then knelt on the floor of his room with the satchel open before him like a priest at an altar, and took inventory, because inventory was how he prayed.

Two photographs. His parents, taken the year before the arrests, stiff and formal, his father's hand on his mother's shoulder as though steadying her for what was coming—which, in fairness, it was. And the dock photograph: himself at twenty-two, Marta at nineteen, Stefan asleep against her chest, taken by a street photographer on the quay at the last port, a print they'd bought for a few coppers because it was the last time the three of them would ever stand in the same frame of light. In it he looked terrified. Marta looked furious. Stefan looked like a boy who trusted everyone, which was the truest tragedy in the image.

One brass key—to a flat in a city that no longer existed under that name, in a country he could not enter without dying, containing furniture he owned and a life he had buried. He kept the key not because the flat mattered but because a man should carry proof of what was taken from him, even if the proof opened nothing anymore.

Beneath these, wrapped in oilcloth: the tools. The fine nibs. The two bottles of ink mixed to match official shades. The blotting roller. The little brass stencil of seals, worn smooth at the edges from a thousand impressions in another decade, in another life, when his hands had been young and steady and the work had been holy—because it had been holy, he reminded the dark, it had been holy work, carrying the dead's clean names to the living, smuggling souls across checkpoints inside the paperwork of people who no longer needed them. Every document he had ever forged had been a resurrection. That was the theology of it, and he had believed in it absolutely, right up until the night a polite man named Quill sat in the Marshes' parlor and wrote something down.

He lifted the nib case and weighed it in his palm. His hands, he noticed, were not steady. They had not been steady since the census visit, and he despised them for it, and feared for them, because a forger's hands were his conscience made flesh—if they shook, the work showed, and if the work showed, people hanged.

Two passports. Two transit passes. Two sets of entry visas, or bribes in place of visas. A route: Trieste to Genoa by rail, Genoa to Marseille by coastal steamer, Marseille to England by the packet boats that still ran, that ran *now*, in this season, with the gray ships multiplying on the horizon like storm clouds deciding whether to arrive. Money—God, money. Third class for two was perhaps eleven pounds with food, fourteen with margin, and margin was survival; he had perhaps thirty pounds saved in eleven years of wages and tips, hidden in the lining of the trunk, and thirty pounds was enough, barely, if nothing went wrong.

Something always went wrong. Something had always gone wrong. In 1900 something had gone wrong at every stage and they had survived it anyway because he had been twenty-two and fearless and had not yet learned what fear cost.

He sat back on his heels in the dark room and did what Marta had begged him not to do, what he had promised her on a deck in 1900 he would never do again.

He counted.

One sister. One niece. One ocean. Thirty pounds. One man whose name was already in a government file, whose hands shook, whose every forged line was now a rope with a loop at the end. One employer—a woman who trusted him with her mail, her schedule, her cause, her life in the sense that a person trusts the driver who is present at every arrest, every rally, every midnight meeting. If he was taken, the inquiry would not stop at him; inquiries never stopped at anyone; they spread like spilled water across a floor, finding every crack. Adeline Marsh. The Manchester women. The printer. The names in the notebooks he steamed open every night and reseal every morning—names that would be worth freedom itself to any Home Office clerk with a promotion to earn.

*Change one number for me,* she had written. And the terrible, quiet truth of it was that the number most likely to change was not zero chances. It was one sister. It was one niece. It was Edmund himself, subtracted, arrested, deported, or worse—because the arithmetic of rescue had a column he kept trying not to look at, the column headed *cost to others*, and in that column, if he forged, if he moved, if he so much as wrote to Trieste through the old channels, he risked entering not one name but dozens.

He repacked the satchel. Locked it. Chained the key back to his watch. Then he sat on the edge of the bed in his shirt sleeves and looked at his hands in the light from the window, harbor light, the distant lamps of the quay where the gray ships lay at anchor, and he understood that he had already decided—that the decision had been made the moment he read the words *Stefan is dead*, that everything since was merely the mind catching up to the heart, arranging reasons around a fact as inevitable as gravity.

He was going to forge. Hands shaking, file open in some clerk's drawer, gallows implicit in every pen-stroke—he was going to do it, because Marta had stood in a hay cart holding a child's mouth shut while dogs bayed, because she had slept through the worst hours and woken to a lifetime of them, because a girl of fifteen with her grandmother's temper deserved to live in a country where no one could make her disappear.

The question was not *whether*. The question, and he felt it arrive in his chest like a stone dropped down a well, was *how*—how to produce two flawless sets of papers with shaking hands, how to raise or borrow what the route would cost beyond his thirty pounds, how to move a woman and a girl across a closing sea without the movement passing through a single living soul who could be questioned—

—and how to do all of it, every piece of it, without letting it touch the house above his head, the woman who employed him, the network that slept tonight believing its driver was only a driver.

Somewhere below, in the town, a church bell struck two. Out in the harbor, faintly, a ship's whistle sounded—late departure, or early arrival, the water keeping its own restless hours. Edmund lay down without undressing and stared at the ceiling, and the last thing he thought before exhausted sleep took him was not of Marta, or Lena, or the boy with the gap in his teeth.

It was of Mr. Quill's fountain pen, moving smoothly across the page, writing something down.
