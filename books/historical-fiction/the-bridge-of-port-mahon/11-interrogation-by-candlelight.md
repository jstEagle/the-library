## 11. Interrogation by Candlelight

The candle on Edmund’s desk burned low, its wax pooling into the grooves of the wood, a slow, amber pulse that seemed to mark the passing of minutes that ought not to exist. Outside, the harbor of Port Mahon exhaled the salt breath of a sea that had once carried him away from everything he’d known, and now seemed poised to swallow him whole. Rain tapped a measured, mournful rhythm against the windowpane, each strike a reminder that the world beyond this room was indifferent to the quiet lives huddled inside.

Edmund had been reviewing Adeline’s correspondence for nearly an hour, the ink on the pages still fresh in his mind, the names of women who dared to dream aloud in a century that preferred their silence. He set the letter down and reached for the satchel at his feet. It was a small thing, leather-worn, the lock tarnished from use, but it contained the only history he allowed himself: papers forged in the ink of dead men, identities stitched from the threads of strangers, and a single, trembling photograph of a sister he had not seen in eleven years.

A soft knock disturbed the quiet, and then the door swung open without waiting for invitation. Mr. Quill entered, his gait the easy, unhurried stride of a man who believed himself unobserved. He wore the uniform of the census bureau—a dark coat with brass buttons, a cap set rakishly upon his head—and in his hand he carried a leather satchel of his own, the kind that held ledgers and measuring tapes, not secrets.

“Mr. Iversen,” Quill said, his voice warm, conversational, the tone of a man who had no reason to suspect he was walking into a room full of ghosts. “I do hope I’m not interrupting. The census, you understand, is a matter of record. Sacred, even. But certain… discrepancies have come to my attention.”

Edmund smiled, a practiced curve of the lips. “I’m at your service, Mr. Quill. What discrepancies might those be?”

Quill stepped further into the room, his eyes scanning the space with the casual appraisal of a man who had long since stopped seeing anything but data. “Your household registration, filed under the name Edmund Iversen, driver and general aide to the suffragist household at No. 7 Crown Lane. Yet when I spoke with Adeline Marsh this morning, she mentioned a man by the name of Thomas Cade who has been residing with you for the better part of six months. Mr. Cade, I was given to understand, is a stable hand. Yet my ledger shows no Thomas Cade in the census for Port Mahon. In fact, no Thomas Cade has appeared in any official record for the better part of a decade.”

Edmund’s hand, resting on the satchel, remained still. His face, however, shifted—just a flicker, the barest tightening around the eyes. “Thomas Cade. Yes. A good worker. Adeline found him looking for work after the dockyards downsized. I offered him lodging.”

“And yet,” Quill said, setting his satchel on the floor with a soft thud, “the name does not appear on any ship manifest, no tax record, no voter registration. Moreover, when I asked after his origins, the description given matched closely a Thomas Cade who was reported missing, presumed deceased, during the unrest of 1898. A fire at the foundry. No body recovered, but the papers were closed.”

Edmund felt something shift inside his chest, a dull weight that had been settling there since the night he had first pressed a forged passport into the hands of a weeping woman at a train station. He let his eyes drift to the window, to the rain-streaked glass, and spoke with the measured calm of a man who had long ago made peace with the space between truth and necessity.

“Fires are cruel things, Mr. Quill. They leave few survivors, and fewer records. It would not be surprising if a name were lost in the smoke.”

“Perhaps.” Quill reached into his satchel and produced a folded bundle of charred paper. The edges were brittle, the surface blackened as if by flame, yet the ink—though smudged—was still legible in places. *“Edmund Iversen. Born 1878. Port of embarkation: Port Mahon. Destination: Alexandria. Papers issued under the name of one Arthur Vane, deceased 1892.”* Quill unfolded the fragment and laid it on the desk between them. The candlelight danced across the blackened surface, making the ink appear to pulse.

Edmund’s breath, which he had not realized he had been holding, escaped in a slow, quiet rush. He looked at the name Arthur Vane, at the date, at the handwriting that was not his own but had once been pressed into service by a man who had wanted to disappear. “A keepsake,” he said simply. “From the fire. A relative’s keepsake.”

“Perhaps.” Quill’s eyes narrowed fractionally. “But the fragment continues. *“Also listed: Marta Iversen, age 34, and two children, Tomas and young Marta, ages seven and four. Intended to travel under forged identities of the deceased. Papers to be surrendered upon safe arrival.”*”

Edmund felt his fingers grow still. The satchel at his side seemed to grow heavier, though he had not added anything to it. “Marta. Yes. An old acquaintance. She… she found her way to me after the borders closed. I assisted her, as I have assisted others, in finding passage.”

“Passage that requires papers,” Quill countered, his voice softening, losing none of its probing edge. “Papers that, according to this manifest, belong to men who have been dead for nearly twenty years. And yet here you are, Mr. Iversen, driving a wealthy woman’s car, living in a house that should, by all accounts, be registered to a deceased accountant.”

Edmund’s gaze flickered back to the charred fragment. “Records are not infallible, Mr. Quill. Fires, as you say, consume much. And people, in their desperation, do things they would not otherwise consider.”

“Indeed.” Quill reached again into his satchel and withdrew a second item, this one whole, unburned, and wrapped in plain brown paper. He set it on the desk with a deliberate care that made the air between them feel charged. “I also found this among the belongings of a man who shared your… peculiar circumstances. A photograph. Developing it took some time, but the image is clear.”

He lifted the paper. The photograph showed a young man, no older than twenty, standing on a dock’s wooden planking, the planks weathered and gray beneath his boots. He wore a dark coat, the collar turned up against a wind that seemed to blow from a sea that was not there in the stillness of the studio. In his arms, held close, was a woman—Marta, her hair unbound, her eyes bright with a mixture of fear and hope. Beside them, a boy of about seven clung to the woman’s skirt, his face turned toward the camera with an expression of wary curiosity. The back of the photograph, in a hand that was not Edmund’s, bore a single inscription: *“Edmund, Marta, the dock, 1900. For safekeeping.”*

The room seemed to tilt on its axis. Edmund’s throat tightened, and for a moment he had to swallow hard, the muscles in his neck corded and tense. He looked from the photograph to Quill, and then back to the photograph again. The young Edmund staring back at him was not the Edmund of 1911—the man who now drove carriages and read mail and kept secrets in a locked satchel. This Edmund was younger, harder, his jaw set in a determination that had been forged in the fires of a political persecution he had thought he had escaped.

“A memento,” Edmund said, his voice steadier than he felt. “From another life. One I have long since buried.”

“Buried, perhaps.” Quill’s gaze was unrelenting. “But the photograph names three people who no longer exist, Mr. Iversen. Arthur Vane, Marta Iversen, and the boy Tomas. And yet, here you are, living under a name that should have died with them. I must ask you, sir, how a man with such… gaps in his history comes to be in the employ of a suffragist who, I might add, is under constant surveillance for her own… unconventional activities.”

Edmund felt the weight of the candle’s flame, the way it cast long shadows across the desk, across the documents, across the living proof of a life he had built from the fragments of others. He had spent eleven years constructing this identity, brick by brick, name by name, until he had become someone new, someone who could move through the world without the weight of his past dragging him down. But the past, it seemed, had a way of resurfacing, of demanding to be counted.

“Mr. Quill,” Edmund said, and his voice was quiet, but it carried the weight of someone who had thought long and hard about every word. “The census is a tool of order. I understand that. But it is also a tool of erasure. And I have erasure enough to deal with, without the government adding to it.”

Quill studied him for a long moment, the candlelight catching the sharp angles of his face, the shrewdness in his eyes. “You speak of erasure as if it were a thing of the past. But the past, Mr. Iversen, has a way of catching up. The 1900 manifest. The burned papers. The photograph. Each one a thread, and when pulled, they unravel the whole.”

“Perhaps.” Edmund’s eyes met Quill’s, and in them was a flicker of something the census taker might have interpreted as guilt, or perhaps as something else entirely—something older, deeper, the terror of a man who had built his entire existence on the premise that he could disappear, that he could become someone else, and now found himself face to face with the evidence of that disappearance. “But I must insist, Mr. Quill, that these matters are… personal. Private. Beyond the scope of the census.”

“Perhaps.” Quill stood, adjusting his cap. “But the law, Mr. Iversen, does not concern itself with personal versus private. It concerns itself with accuracy. And right now, your history is… incomplete. To say the least.”

He turned toward the door, pausing with his hand on the latch. “I shall be back, Mr. Iversen. With further questions. And perhaps, if the mood takes me, I shall inquire after the whereabouts of the young boy in the photograph. Tomas, was it? I understand he is… still in your employ.”

The door clicked shut behind him, and the room fell back into the quiet of the candle’s glow. Edmund sat alone with the charred fragment, the whole photograph, the weight of a history he had thought buried. His hands, which had been still on the satchel, now moved of their own accord, fingers tracing the edge of the desk, feeling the grain of the wood, the imprint of the candle’s wax.

He thought of the night in 1900, the heat of the foundry fire, the smell of smoke and burning timber, the desperate reach for papers that would make him, Marta, and the children ghosts in the eyes of the law. He thought of the dock, the sea, the way Marta had held the boy Tomas close, her eyes wide with a hope that had been both a lifeline and a target. He thought of the photograph, taken in a moment of desperation, a smile frozen in time, and the way it had survived the fire, the years, the censuses, the changing governments, the wars that had come and gone.

And he thought of the satchel at his feet, the lock that had held his secrets for eleven years, and the question that now sat, heavy and unanswered, in the space between the candle’s flare and the door’s close.

The rain continued its measured tapping against the window, each strike a metronome counting down to something Edmund could not yet name. He picked up the charred fragment again, turning it this way and that, the ink legible in fits and starts, the name Arthur Vane staring up at him from the blackened surface. He had carried this fragment for years, a relic of a life he had left behind, a reminder of the price of survival. And now, here it was, in the hands of a man who asked questions he had long since learned to dodge.

* * *

The candle guttered, its flame dipping low, the wax thinning, the wick trembling as if it, too, knew that the moment of reckoning was at hand. Edmund set the fragment down, his movements slow, deliberate, as if handling something that might shatter if he moved too quickly. The photograph lay whole beside it, the faces of the young Edmund, Marta, and the boy Tomas staring up from the paper, innocent and unaware of the storms that would come to bear down on them.

He thought of the satchel, of the lock that had kept his history sealed, and of the choice he had made, years ago, to become someone new, to leave the past behind in exchange for a future he could not have had otherwise. But as he sat there, in the quiet of the room, with the census taker’s questions echoing in his mind, he wondered if the price of survival had not already been paid, and if he were now being asked to pay it again, in a currency he had thought he had spent long ago.

A soft knock disturbed the quiet, and then the door opened once more. This time, it was not Quill, but Adeline, her coat damp from the rain, her hair pulled back in the way she wore it when she was deep in thought. She carried a stack of flyers for an upcoming rally, the ink still fresh on the paper, the words *Votes for Women* bold and unapologetic against the white background.

“Edmund,” she said, her voice gentle, the concern in her eyes evident even as she tried to mask it. “Quill was here. He asked after you. After him, I mean. He seemed… interested in your history.”

Edmund looked up, the candle’s flame reflected in his eyes, and he offered her a smile that did not quite reach the corners of his mouth. “He was inquisitive, as one might expect. I answered as I always do. With politeness, with evasion, with the kind of silence that says much without saying anything at all.”

Adeline set the flyers on the desk, her eyes scanning the charred fragment and the photograph before settling on Edmund’s face. “He asked after Thomas Cade. After Marta. After the boy.”

“Among other things.” Edmund’s jaw tightened fractionally. “He has his methods, Adeline. As do we all.”

She moved to the window, looking out at the rain-slicked streets, the harbor lights flickering in the distance. “Quill is a clever man. But he does not know the half of it. None of them do.”

Edmund said nothing, but his hands, resting on the satchel, tightened around the leather, the lock cold beneath his fingertips. He thought of the photograph, of the young Edmund standing on a dock with Marta and the boy, and of the way the image had survived the fire, the years, the censuses, the changing governments, the wars that had come and gone. He thought of the satchel, of the locked history he had carried with him, and of the choice he had made, years ago, to become someone new, to leave the past behind in exchange for a future he could not have had otherwise.

But as he sat there, in the quiet of the room, with the candle’s flame guttering and the rain tapping its relentless rhythm against the window, he wondered if the price of survival had not already been paid, and if he were now being asked to pay it again, in a currency he had thought he had spent long ago.

“Adeline,” he said, his voice quiet but firm. “If Quill returns… if he presses further… I need you to know that whatever happens, whatever he finds, whatever he threatens—I will not betray you. I will not betray the cause, or the people who have become my family in the only way that matters.”

Adeline turned back to him, her eyes searching his face, and she reached out, her hand resting on the desk, near his own. “Edmund. I know you. I have known you since the day you walked into my life, driving my carriage with a quiet competence that suggested you had seen things no one should have to see, and yet you never spoke of them. I have seen the way you look at the satchel, the way you hold it as if it were something precious, something sacred. I have felt the weight of it, even when you have not spoken of it.”

She squeezed his hand, gentle but insistent. “Whatever Quill has, whatever he thinks he has, I want you to know that you are not alone in this. Not truly. You have me. You have the household. You have the people who have come to mean something to you, even if you have not said the words aloud.”

Edmund looked at her hand, then at her face, and for a moment, the mask of the driver, the reader of mail, the keeper of secrets, slipped, and he saw the man beneath—the man who had fled, who had forged, who had carried the weight of a past that would not stay buried. But he also saw the woman who had become more than a suffragist to him, more than a mistress, more than anything he had dared to hope for when he had first taken the job of driving her carriage.

“Thank you, Adeline,” he said, and the words were genuine, the gratitude real. “For… for seeing me. For seeing past the things I have not said.”

She smiled, a small, sad thing, but a thing filled with a fierce kind of understanding. “I see you, Edmund. Not the history you carry, but the man you have become. And I intend to make sure that history does not define you. Not entirely.”

The candle flared briefly, a brief surge of yellow light that illuminated the room, the documents, the faces in the photograph, the satchel at Edmund’s feet. Outside, the rain continued, the harbor lights flickered, and somewhere in the distance, a ship’s horn sounded, mournful and long, a signal that the world beyond this room was still turning, still moving, still full of possibilities and dangers alike.

Edmund picked up the photograph again, turning it over in his hands, the backs of the faces of the young Edmund, Marta, and the boy Tomas staring up at him from the paper. He thought of the night in 1900, the fire, the dock, the desperate reach for papers that would make them ghosts in the eyes of the law. He thought of the satchel, of the locked history he had carried with him, and of the choice he had made, years ago, to become someone new, to leave the past behind in exchange for a future he could not have had otherwise.

But as he sat there, in the quiet of the room, with the census taker’s questions echoing in his mind, with the charred fragment and the whole photograph sitting on the desk before him, he wondered if the price of survival had not already been paid, and if he were now being asked to pay it again, in a currency he had thought he had spent long ago.

He set the photograph down, his fingers tracing the edge of the charred fragment, the ink legible in fits and starts, the name Arthur Vane staring up at him from the blackened surface. He had carried this fragment for years, a relic of a life he had left behind, a reminder of the price of survival. And now, here it was, in the hands of a man who asked questions he had long since learned to dodge.

The door opened once more, this time without sound, and Quill slipped back into the room, his coat damp from the rain, a small, folded item in his hand. He moved to the desk and set the item down with a soft click, the sound echoing in the quiet space between the candle’s flare and the rain’s relentless tapping.

It was a single, small key, brass and tarnished, the kind that fit locks of a certain age, a certain make. It sat on the desk, between the charred fragment and the whole photograph, a silent, tangible thing that seemed to say, *I was here. I saw what you carried. And I have something that belongs to you.*

Edmund’s eyes lifted to Quill’s, and in the census taker’s gaze he found something he had not expected: not the satisfaction of a man who had caught his quarry, but something older, harder, the quiet recognition of a man who had walked a path not unlike his own.

“Mr. Iversen,” Quill said, his voice low, the words carrying a weight that went beyond the census, beyond the questions, beyond the need for accuracy. “I believe this belongs to you. Or at least, to the man you were, before you became the man sitting before me.”

Edmund reached out, his fingers closing around the cold brass of the key. He turned it over in his palm, the metal warm from his touch, and felt a strange, hollow sensation in his chest, as if a door had been opened that he had long since nailed shut.

“Where did you find this, Mr. Quill?”

“Among the belongings of the man whose papers you carried,” Quill replied, his eyes steady on Edmund’s. “Arthur Vane. The man whose identity you assumed, at least in part. The key fits a trunk. A trunk that was supposed to be burned, along with the rest of his things, after the fire of 1898. But somehow, it survived. Just as your history has survived, in one form or another.”

Edmund’s grip on the key tightened, the brass cool and unyielding beneath his fingers. He thought of the trunk, of the papers, of the life he had built from the fragments of a man who had been dead for nearly twenty years. He thought of the satchel, of the locked history he had carried with him, and of the choice he had made, years ago, to become someone new, to leave the past behind in exchange for a future he could not have had otherwise.

But as he sat there, in the quiet of the room, with the candle’s flame guttering and the rain tapping its relentless rhythm against the window, he wondered if the price of survival had not already been paid, and if he were now being asked to pay it again, in a currency he had thought he had spent long ago.

“Thank you, Mr. Quill,” he said, and the words were quiet, but they carried a weight that went beyond politeness. “For returning what is mine. For… for seeing what I have tried so hard to keep buried.”

Quill nodded, a small, almost imperceptible movement, and turned toward the door. “We all have things we keep buried, Mr. Iversen. Some of them, the world sees fit to dig up. Others, we keep, against all odds. The key is yours. Keep it, or don’t. It is, after all, just a thing. But the history it represents… that is yours to carry, or to lay down. The choice, as always, is yours.”

The door clicked shut behind him, and the room fell back into the quiet of the candle’s glow. Edmund sat alone with the key, the charred fragment, the whole photograph, the satchel at his feet. The rain continued its measured tapping against the window, each strike a metronome counting down to something Edmund could not yet name.

He thought of the night in 1900, the fire, the dock, the desperate reach for papers that would make him, Marta, and the children ghosts in the eyes of the law. He thought of the photograph, of the young Edmund standing on a dock with Marta and the boy, and of the way the image had survived the fire, the years, the censuses, the changing governments, the wars that had come and gone. He thought of the satchel, of the locked history he had carried with him, and of the choice he had made, years ago, to become someone new, to leave the past behind in exchange for a future he could not have had otherwise.

But as he sat there, in the quiet of the room, with the census taker’s questions echoing in his mind, with the charred fragment and the whole photograph sitting on the desk before him, and now this small, brass key sitting between them, he wondered if the price of survival had not already been paid, and if he were now being asked to pay it again, in a currency he had thought he had spent long ago.

He picked up the key, turning it over in his fingers, the brass cool and unyielding, and for a moment, he thought of all the locks he had opened and closed in his life, of all the doors he had walked through and locked behind him, of all the histories he had carried and left behind. And he thought of the satchel, of the history he had sealed inside it, and of the choice he would have to make, soon, about who would get the last berth, and who would be left behind.

The candle guttered, its flame dipping low, the wax thinning, the wick trembling as if it, too, knew that the moment of reckoning was at hand. Edmund set the key down, his movements slow, deliberate, as if handling something that might shatter if he moved too quickly. The photograph lay whole beside it, the faces of the young Edmund, Marta, and the boy Tomas staring up from the paper, innocent and unaware of the storms that would come to bear down on them.

Outside, the rain continued, the harbor lights flickered, and somewhere in the distance, a ship’s horn sounded, mournful and long, a signal that the world beyond this room was still turning, still moving, still full of possibilities and dangers alike.

And in the quiet of the room, with the candle’s flame guttering and the rain tapping its relentless rhythm against the window, Edmund Iversen sat with his history spread before him—charred fragments, whole photographs, small brass keys—and wondered, not for the first time, what it meant to survive, and at what cost.

The chapter ends here, with Edmund holding the key, the weight of his past—and the choices yet to come—settling around him like the damp, salt-laden air of the harbor outside. The questions of Mr. Quill linger, the photograph and fragment and key standing as tangible proof of a history he had thought buried, and the satchel at his feet the only thing standing between him and the reckoning he had long avoided. The night is dark, the rain is steady, and the road ahead is uncertain, but Edmund is no stranger to the road, or to the choices it demands. The next chapter will find him facing the consequences of a life built on forged identities, and the people he has come to love will be tested as never before.
