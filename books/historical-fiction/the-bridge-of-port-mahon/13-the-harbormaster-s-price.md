## 13. The Harbormaster's Price

The harbor master's office smelled of salt and old paper, the way buildings near the sea always did—as if the very walls had learned to breathe the foam and brine. Adeline Marsh sat across from Captain Doren at a scarred oak desk that had once held ledgers of cargo manifests and crew lists, now cluttered with forms about to be filled in ink that might never be seen again. The man's fingers, thick with age and workman's calluses, tapped a rhythm against the wood that spoke of command and calculation both.

"You need three passages," he said, his voice like gravel smoothed by water. "But the Admiralty has released only five remaining berths for civilian embarkation. The others are allotted to the medical evacuation, the munitions shipment, or the diplomatic corps."

Adeline nodded, though she'd already counted the possibilities in her head a dozen times over. Three lives—her driver, Edmund, and the sister who had returned from the dead—needed passage from Port Mahon to safety. But Edmund's satchel, his carefully constructed web of forged identities, had created a mathematical impossibility that now sat heavy on her conscience like a stone in her pocket.

"The question isn't about numbers," she said, keeping her voice steady despite the tremor that had nothing to do with the cold February air seeping through the window cracks. "It's about who deserves to survive."

Captain Doren looked up from his papers, revealing eyes the color of storm clouds just before rain. "Miss Marsh, you speak as if survival were a matter of merit rather than logistics."

"Then make it a matter of logistics," she replied, leaning forward. "I have papers—legitimate ones this time—for Edmund Iversen, a civil servant with documented need. For Marta Kowalski, a refugee whose family perished in the purges. And for myself."

"The papers are clean," Doren admitted, examining each document with the practiced eye of someone who'd reviewed thousands. "But clean doesn't mean plentiful. The Admiralty has set quotas specifically to prevent overcrowding on the remaining vessels. They fear disease, unrest, the contagion of hope among those left behind."

Adeline's grip tightened on her own folder of papers, the leather worn smooth from constant handling. She thought of Edmund's hands shaking as he forged the last set of documents in the dead of night, of the way he'd pressed his face into Marta's hair the night before she'd left, whispered something in Polish she didn't understand. She thought of Tomas, the stable boy whose drawings of Edmund as a spy now hung pinned to every wall in the household, and of the other women in her network who would lose their funding if she couldn't deliver those two berths to prove the cause was still viable.

"What's your price?" she asked.

Doren smiled—a slow, calculating thing that didn't reach his eyes. "That depends entirely on what you're willing to pay."

***

They walked together through the harbor to the customs house, where the morning fog clung to the cobblestones like a secret. Adeline's boots clicked against the stone in a rhythm that had become almost hypnotic over the months of organizing, of raising money, of building networks that stretched from the working docks to the genteel squares where wealthy women donated their pearls for the cause. She'd done it all herself mostly, though now she caught herself glancing at Edmund whenever he drove her past the printing presses where the final broadside was being assembled for the rally that Saturday—a rally that might be their last public statement before the ship sailed.

"Marta's children," Doren said without looking at her. "One survived. The other..." He shook his head. "But three souls require three passages. That's simple arithmetic."

"It's not arithmetic," Adeline replied. "It's morality."

"Morality is expensive in Port Mahon these days."

She wanted to strike him. She wanted to scream about the women who'd been dragged from their homes, the men taken for military service while their families starved, the children whose laughter had been silenced by bayonets. Instead she said, "What if I offered something more valuable than money?"

Doren raised an eyebrow, the only indication he might be interested.

"The movement's funds," she said. "The account I've been managing for the National Woman's Alliance. The money raised from the suffragist rallies, the donations from sympathetic merchants, the proceeds from the lace sale last month—all of it."

"That's half the budget for the next six months."

"It's also everything." Adeline pulled out her purse and scattered the documents onto the table—a ledger of donations, receipts for materials, correspondence with other organizers in Hull and Leeds. "If I give you this, if I surrender the financial backbone of the campaign, will you guarantee passage for two people rather than three?"

"Why would I do that?"

"Because if I don't, you won't have three people passing through your hands. You'll have none of them, because Edmund Iversen will have been arrested for document fraud, Marta Kowalski will be taken by the constabulary for immigration violations, and I'll be here to face charges for conspiracy."

Doren was silent for a long moment, his weathered face unreadable. Adeline could hear the harbor wind whip against the shutters and knew she was the only thing between him and the open air. Finally he said, "That's not the arrangement I had in mind."

"Then suggest another."

"Your movement's funds aren't the only thing you possess, are they? There's the printing press on Duke Street. The one that's been churning out pamphlets about voting rights. The one that could just as easily produce... other materials."

Adeline felt her blood run cold. "What kind of materials?"

"Materials that make people disappear from the rolls, from the records, from the very existence of their names. Your skills, Miss Marsh—they're not unique to suffragette organizing. The military has need of similar talents."

She wanted to laugh, but it came out as a choked gasp. "You're blackmailing me."

"I'm offering you a choice," he corrected. "One berth for Edmund and Marta. One berth for you. But the cost is your cooperation in producing falsified documents for other purposes. Documents that will keep your cause funded because you'll have worked for the government, proven your patriotism, your reliability."

Adeline's hand moved automatically to her abdomen, where the baby kicked again with perfect regularity. She'd been carrying this child for nine months now, ever since she'd discovered she was pregnant from a brief encounter with a sympathetic doctor who'd helped her with some paperwork—a man who'd died in the trenches before the child was born. The child would not come, but she carried it all the same, a living reminder of the impossible choices that defined their time.

"That's not fair."

"Nothing about survival is fair," Doren said gently. "But it is practical."

***

They returned to the office in silence, though neither had spoken since their negotiation. The fog had lifted slightly, revealing the harbor in shades of slate and silver, fishing boats bobbing gently against the tide while larger vessels waited offshore with their military crews. Adeline traced the rim of her teacup with a fingertip, watching the steam rise and disappear into the air.

Edmund appeared in the doorway then, his tall frame silhouetted against the weak afternoon light. He'd changed from his usual dark coat into something more civilian—a gray frock coat that cost more than most workers earned in a month, but that showed no signs of having been worn before. His hair was neatly combed, his face clean-shaven, though she could still see the fatigue lines around his eyes that had deepened since yesterday.

"Marta sent her son ahead," he said without preamble. "He's waiting in the quarters."

Adeline looked up from her cup. "And the daughter?"

"Gone to meet the consul. She'll be on the manifest tomorrow morning."

"And you?"

"I sail Friday. I've arranged a position with the Red Cross—"

"—in France," she finished. "How did you—?"

"I have connections, Adeline. People who owe me favors from previous... arrangements." He set down the letter he'd been holding and picked up hers. "This from the harbor master. He's agreed to your terms?"

She nodded, but the gesture felt hollow even as she made it. "Two berths. For you and Marta. One for me. But the price—"

"I understand the price." He opened her letter and began reading aloud. "'The movement's funds shall be transferred to the government account under the supervision of Captain Doren. In exchange, passage shall be granted for two persons of the sender's choosing. The sender retains full control over the third designation.'"

"And you're satisfied with that?"

"I'm practical," he said. "Besides, I have my own considerations."

"What kind?"

He hesitated, and for a moment she thought he might not answer. Then: "My nephew. He's been avoiding conscription by disappearing into the civilian population. The military wants him back, but he's useful to them in other ways—paperwork, document preparation, things that require discretion. I've offered to help them find suitable positions where his skills can be employed without him having to march or fight."

"And if they refuse?"

"Then I'll have to find other solutions. Solutions that might involve..." He trailed off.

"Involving what?"

"Involving making myself useful to people who have power. People who can make life very uncomfortable for those I care about."

Adeline studied his face, trying to parse the sincerity beneath the careful words. She'd seen him lie countless times, smooth over inconvenient truths with practiced ease. But this felt different—as if he were revealing something vulnerable, something he'd never admitted even to himself.

"Edmund," she said softly, "you don't owe me anything."

"I owe you passage," he said simply. "That's enough."

But as he turned to leave, she caught the tremor in his hand as he reached for the door handle. It wasn't fear she saw there, not exactly. More like the recognition that some debts could never be fully repaid, that some choices carved permanent grooves into the landscape of a person's soul.

***

That evening, Adeline sat alone in her room above the meeting hall, surrounded by papers and the faint scent of lavender from the sachet she'd tucked into her drawer. The movement's ledgers lay open on her lap, their numbers gleaming like gold in the lamplight. Every penny accounted for, every donation recorded, every expense meticulously tracked. It had taken her months to build this system, to create a financial foundation strong enough to support their growing network of safe houses and legal aid.

And now she was going to hand it all over to a military captain who might use it to suppress the very freedoms they'd fought so hard to protect.

Her hands trembled as she picked up the quill pen, the same one she'd used to sign her name to dozens of petitions and letters to parliament. The feather felt foreign in her grip, as if it belonged to someone else entirely. She thought of Marta's desperate letter, written in shaking script that spoke of hunger and loss and the impossible distance between them. She thought of Edmund's quiet sacrifice, his decision to stay behind and face whatever charges awaited him rather than abandon his duty to the documents that kept them all safe.

She thought of the child she carried, nameless and forgotten by the world but real in ways that transcended mere biology. A child who would never know the taste of fresh bread, the sound of her mother's laughter, the warmth of a lover's touch. A child born of circumstances that made even love a luxury they couldn't afford.

The quill moved across the page of its own accord, forming words she'd never planned to write:

*To Captain Doren, Keeper of the Harbor Records:*

*I hereby transfer all assets and liabilities of the National Woman's Alliance campaign fund to your direct control, effective immediately. In exchange, I request guarantee of passage for myself and one other designated person aboard the next available vessel bound for safety.*

*Should you fail to honor this agreement, I reserve the right to reveal certain... inaccuracies in your official records that may prove... inconvenient to your career advancement.*

*Respectfully yours,*
*Adeline Marsh*

She read what she'd written and smiled despite herself. Diplomacy, even in defeat, required a certain artistry. She folded the letter and placed it in an envelope, addressing it with careful precision to the harbor master's office.

Outside her window, the city hummed with the sounds of preparation. News of the upcoming sailing had spread through the working class districts like wildfire, drawing both hope and desperation from those who'd heard they might escape the coming conflict. Adeline could feel the weight of her decision settling into her bones—not just the choice she'd made, but the choice she was simultaneously making for everyone who'd ever trusted her with their dreams.

Tomorrow, she would board that ship with Marta's daughter, Sarah, who'd proven herself worthy of the journey through courage and kindness that cost far more than any amount of money. Tomorrow, she would leave behind the papers that had sustained their cause and the people who depended on them. And tomorrow, Edmund would remain in Port Mahon to face whatever consequences awaited him for his crimes against the state.

As she extinguished the lamp and settled into bed, Adeline allowed herself one moment of pure joy: the memory of Edmund's hand in hers as they'd walked through the harbor that morning, the way he'd looked at her with something like gratitude in his eyes. It was a moment that would sustain her through the journey ahead, even if it haunted her dreams afterward.

In the darkness, she whispered a prayer for his safety, for Marta's survival, for Sarah's future, and for the child who grew within her heart even as her body grew cold with the absence of warmth. Some sacrifices, she realized, could never be measured in gold or glory. They were paid in sleepless nights and broken promises and the constant, gnawing knowledge that love sometimes meant letting go.

The letter sat on her desk, waiting for the morning mail, its contents sealed in ink that might never be read by the man who'd written it. But Adeline knew that some prices were worth paying, even if the currency was everything she'd ever held dear.

In the distance, the harbor bell tolled nine times, marking the hour when the last ship would cast off its lines and carry her toward whatever awaited beyond the horizon.

She fell asleep to the sound of waves lapping against the pier, dreaming of bridges built not of stone and mortar but of trust, sacrifice, and the unbreakable bonds that connected souls across any distance, no matter how great.

* * *

The morning sun cast long shadows across Port Mahon as Adeline descended the stairs of her modest apartment, her skillet still warm from the bread she'd baked the night before. She'd eaten quickly, knowing that whatever agreement she'd reached with Captain Doren would soon be public knowledge, and that meant preparing for both celebration and catastrophe.

In the kitchen, she found Edmund methodically packing his bag—a leather satchel identical to the one he'd carried for years, now nearly full of documents that would never see the light of day again. He looked up when he heard her enter, and for a moment their eyes met across the room like two ships passing in the night.

"You came back," he said, though it was clear from his expression that he'd never intended to leave at all.

"I never expected you to stay," she replied, hanging up her shawl with careful precision. "Not after what you told me."

"I have to try."

"Try what?"

"To make this right." He opened his satchel and pulled out a single sheet of paper. "There's a way to honor both our agreements without destroying everything we've built."

She took the paper, her fingers tracing the careful script. It was a plan, written in Edmund's hand, that outlined a complex series of transfers and arrangements that would allow her to fulfill her obligation to Captain Doren while preserving enough of the movement's resources to continue operating in some form.

"How?" she breathed.

"Marta's son is a clerk in the postal service. If we can get him to deliver certain packages—certain messages—directly to designated recipients, we can circumvent the normal channels of distribution. The government thinks they're getting cooperation, but really we're just redirecting information to where it needs to go."

"You're talking about creating a shadow network."

"I'm talking about survival."

Outside, the harbor was stirring with activity. The great ship *Crescent Moon* sat anchored offshore, its sails furled and its decks crowded with soldiers checking manifests and counting passengers. Adeline could see figures moving about—some familiar, some unknown—and she knew that within hours, she would be among them, leaving behind everything that had defined her existence for so long.

"Are you sure about this?" she asked, looking at Edmund's face for the first time without the filter of grief and relief. "Because if you're wrong—"

"I've never been wrong about documents," he said, smiling. "And I've never been wrong about you."

She believed him, even when logic suggested she shouldn't. Even when every instinct screamed that they were playing a dangerous game with forces beyond their control. Especially when, in that moment, she realized that the greatest risk of all wasn't failure—it was success.

Because success meant surviving to tell the story, to keep alive the flame of resistance even when the world tried to extinguish it. Success meant choosing between love and loyalty and coming out whole on the other side.

Success meant knowing that some bridges, once crossed, could never be uncrossed.

But they would be worth every step.
