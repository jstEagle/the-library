## 17. Which One Gets the Last Berth

The satchel lay on the floorboards of the Marsh carriage, its brass lock gleaming in the lantern’s guttering light, and Edmund felt the weight of it not in his arms but in the hollow of his ribs. Every night for months he had steamed open Adeline Marsh’s correspondence, memorized the lives spilled across the pages, and resealed the envelopes with a practiced flick of his thumb. The satchel had begun as a professional necessity—Adeline needed her mail delivered, her network protected—but it had become a crypt of small deaths and quiet survivals. Inside, beside the sheaves of papers he did not dare name, lay two faded photographs: one of a woman with sharp eyes and a shawl wrapped tight against a wind that no longer blew, another of a child’s hand clutching a doll’s broken arm. And there, always, the brass key to a flat in Port Mahon that no longer existed, a key to a life he had buried under layers of forged names and quieter, more desperate lies.

Outside, the harbor was a throat clearing before a scream. Ships were being requisitioned, their hulls sheathed in canvas and coal smoke, their decks crowded with soldiers in khaki who laughed too loudly and stared too fixedly at the water. The last ship from Port Mahon, the *Vigilant*, sat at the pier like a restrained breath, its sails patched and its timbers seasoned by years of trade and trouble. Naval officers walked the quay with measured steps, clipboard in hand, already drafting the lists of who would go and who would stay. Rumors of war had swollen from a low hum to a chant that vibrated in the teeth and the temples of everyone who listened.

Edmund climbed into the driver’s seat, the leather worn smooth beneath his palms, and picked up the satchel. He did not need to look inside to know what it held; he had carried it for so long that its contents had merged with his pulse. But tonight, the air felt different. The census man, Mr. Quill, had been by earlier that day, his polite smile a mask for the questions that lingered in the ink of his ledger. *Other residents?* he had asked, tapping the page. *And your relation to them, sir?* Edmund had lied smoothly, as he had lied a dozen times before, but the ease of the lie had cracked, just a little, and Quill had written something down anyway. The paper sat on the side table now, unburned, waiting. Edmund had not burned it; he had not dared. To burn it was to admit that the papers inside were not merely administrative—they were ghosts, and he had learned that ghosts did not stay quiet when flames touched them.

A knock on the carriage door roused him from the spiral. It was Marta, her shawl drawn around her shoulders, her face drawn thin by hunger and the road, her daughter—small, quiet, clutching a bundle of rags—to her breast. She had come early, unannounced, as she always did, driven by a desperation that had not abated in the years since he had last seen her. Her son, the one she had carried across checkpoints and no man’s land, had died of fever and exposure two months past, and the grief had hollowed her out, leaving only the will to move, to flee, to survive.

“Edmund,” she said, her voice a thread of wind through cracked glass. “I have come for the berth. You said there was a place. For me. For her.”

Adeline stepped up beside her, her coat dark against the gaslit courtyard, her eyes sharp and assessing. “Marta. We discussed this. The *Vigilant* has limited capacity. One berth remains, if the harbor master’s latest decree is to be believed.”

“And you intend to give it to me,” Marta said, not a question. “After all I did. After I gave you the name, the papers, the life you now live.”

“I did not give you anything,” Edmund said, his voice steady despite the storm inside him. “I carried you. That is not the same.”

“It is exactly the same,” Marta snapped, and the daughter stirred, a small whimper escaping her. “You used my brother’s death to build your new life. You used my son’s silence to keep from being hanged. I am not a favor, Edmund. I am your sister. I am blood.”

The words landed in the carriage like stones in still water. Edmund felt the familiar ache behind his sternum, the one that had accompanied him since 1900, the night he had carried Marta and her children past the checkpoints using papers meant for the dead. He had felt then, as he felt now, that the papers were not merely ink and parchment; they were skin, breath, the thin line between a living man and a hanged one. He had pressed Marta’s child against his chest, felt the quick panic of a heart beating against his own, and had sworn—on whatever god would listen—that he would keep them both alive. He had not promised forever; he had only promised the next checkpoint, the next town, the next silence.

“You cannot demand a life you nearly destroyed,” Adeline said, her tone gentler than Edmund expected, but no less firm. “The movement needs Marta. If she goes abroad with her testimony, with her story of political persecution, with her face on posters and in pamphlets, the suffragist cause gains a martyr, a witness, a voice that cannot be silenced. She becomes more than a person; she becomes a banner.”

“And if she stays?” Marta’s eyes flashed. “If I stay, I die. If I stay, the regime finishes what they started in 1900. You think the movement needs a banner? I need my daughter to grow up. I need to not be a ghost.”

“Marta—” Edmund began, but she cut him off.

“You are choosing between us,” she said, and the words were not accusation but a statement of fact, bare and terrible. “You are choosing between blood and chosen family. Between the sister who never stopped waiting and the suffragists who became your people. I have waited for you for twenty years. Twenty years of silence, of letters that never reached me, of hoping that the man who drove the suffragist’s carriage was still alive, still free, still mine. And now you want me to walk away from that waiting as if it were a habit, as if it were nothing.”

The confession hung in the air, heavy and true. Edmund had known, in the quiet moments between sealing letters and driving through rain-slick streets, that Marta’s waiting was a thread tied to his heart. He had thought he could carry it without breaking, but the satchel’s weight suggested otherwise. Every forged name, every dead man’s papers he had tucked into the lining, every sleepless night when the census taker or some other authority had knocked at the door—all of it had been a stone placed on that thread, until now it threatened to drag him under.

Adeline stepped closer, her hand resting on the satchel’s edge, her fingers brushing Edmund’s. “Edmund. Look at me. The movement has given me a life, yes, but it has also given me a purpose larger than my own survival. Marta’s testimony could save others. It could force changes in laws that have killed thousands. You know this. You have read the correspondence. You know what we are fighting for.”

“I know,” Edmund said, and the admission cost him something fierce. “I know. But I also know that if Marta goes, she goes alone. Her daughter goes with her. And I stay. I always stay. The question is not whether the movement needs her. The question is whether I can let her go without losing myself.”

He opened the satchel, the brass key catching the lantern light, and for a moment he let Adeline and Marta see inside. Not the letters—those remained sealed—but the photographs, the key, the small, folded scrap of paper from 1900 that bore his father’s name, his mother’s signature, the stamp of a checkpoint that no longer existed. He let them see the weight of what he carried, the archive of deaths and rebirths that had shaped him.

“This is not a choice between two people,” Edmund said, his voice dropping to something ragged. “This is a choice between two versions of the man I might become. If I give Marta the berth, I stay here, face the inquiry, the questioning, the possibility that they will find the papers, the forged names, the history I have tried so carefully to bury. I become the man who sent his sister away, who chose the movement over blood, who became Adeline’s instrument and nothing more. If I keep the berth for myself, or for you, Adeline, I betray the promise I made to Marta in 1900, the promise that I would not let her die as her son died, alone and afraid.”

Marta’s face crumbled, the strength ebbing from her like a tide receding from a shore. “You said you would keep us alive. You said the papers would protect us.”

“I did,” Edmund said. “I do. But survival is not the same as living. And I cannot tell you which death I choose. I cannot tell you which life is worth more.”

Adeline was quiet for a long moment, her eyes scanning Edmund’s face as though searching for a flaw, a sign, anything that would tell her he was bluffing, that he would fold under the pressure. When she spoke, her voice was stripped of the rhetorical flourishes she usually deployed, left raw and honest. “I cannot ask you to choose. I will not. But I cannot watch you sacrifice yourself for a calculation that does not belong to you. If the movement needs Marta, let it have her. But let it not be at the cost of the man who has carried us this far. Let it not be at the cost of the silence between us.”

The three of them stood in the cramped space of the carriage, the lantern swinging, the harbor’s distant noises seeping through the walls. The math of it was simple and cruel: one berth, three claimants. Marta, who had waited twenty years. Adeline, who had built a cause on the backs of people like Marta. Edmund, who had forged a life from the bones of the dead. The satchel sat between them, a silent witness to the promises made and the ones broken.

“Edmund,” Marta said finally, and her voice was small, the sound of a woman who had finally stopped fighting the current and had begun to float. “If you give me the berth, you are choosing me. You are choosing the sister who waited. But know this: if I go, I will not forget that you chose me. And if I stay, I will not forget that you let me go. The choosing itself is a wound.”

“And you, Adeline?” Edmund turned to her. “If I keep the berth, you stay. The movement stays. But I become the one who stayed behind, who faced the inquiry, who let the cause continue without the testimony it needed. I become the silence.”

Adeline squeezed his hand, her grip firm but tender. “Then we will face it together. The movement needs witnesses, yes, but it also needs people who remember. You remember, Edmund. You have remembered everything, every name, every face, every death. That is its own kind of survival. That is its own kind of resistance.”

The words settled over him like rain on dry earth. He had spent years believing that memory was a burden, a ledger of sins and secrets that he had to carry in secret. But perhaps it was also a form of salvation. To be known, fully, by the people who had become his family—that was a terror and a comfort beyond measure.

“Marta,” Edmund said, turning to the sister he had not seen in years, “I cannot give you the berth and keep my freedom. I cannot give it to Adeline and keep my conscience. I cannot give it to myself and keep my heart.”

He reached into the satchel and withdrew the brass key, holding it out between them. “This is the key to the flat in Port Mahon. To the life I built, the lies I carried. I am offering it to the one who needs it most. Not because I can choose between you, but because I cannot choose at all. I am offering you the means to disappear, to start again, to live. But know that if you take it, you take the history with you. The forged names, the dead men’s papers, the silence that has kept us all alive. You take the guilt, and you take the freedom. You take the both of them, or you take neither.”

Marta reached out, her fingers closing around the cold brass. She looked at the key, then at Edmund, and something broke in her face—a surrender, an acceptance, a lifetime of waiting distilled into a single nod. “Then I take it,” she said. “But you must promise me one thing. You must promise me that you will not forget. That you will write it down, or speak it, or carry it in some way that does not die with you.”

“I promise,” Edmund said, and the word was a rope pulled tight around his throat. “I promise.”

Adeline took the key from Marta’s hand, her own fingers trembling slightly. “Edmund. If you stay, if you face the inquiry, you must know that we will not forget you either. The movement will remember. I will remember. And when the war is over, when the streets are rebuilt, we will find you, and we will sit at a table, and you will tell us everything. Every name. Every face. Every lie that kept us alive.”

“I intend to,” Edmund said. “I intend to tell it all. But not yet. Not until the water has calmed.”

The three of them stood in the silence that followed, the weight of the key circulating between them like a sacrament. Outside, the *Vigilant* shifted at its moorings, a dark shape against the star-bleached sky. The harbor was alive with the sounds of men preparing, of ropes being coiled, of voices raised in commands that would send men to sea and leave others behind. The last ship would not wait forever. The tide was turning, and with it, the fates of everyone in the courtyard.

“Marta,” Edmund said, “go to the pier. Speak with the harbormaster. If there is a berth, take it. If not, find another way. But go. Live. For your daughter, for yourself, for the sister who never stopped waiting.”

Marta kissed his cheek, quick and desperate, and then was gone, hurrying through the courtyard, her daughter clutched to her breast, the brass key hidden in the fold of her shawl. Adeline watched her go, her expression unreadable, her eyes dark with something Edmund could not name—grief, perhaps, or resolve, or both.

When Marta was out of sight, Adeline turned to Edmund, and the silence between them stretched, thick and alive with unsaid things. “You are going to face them,” she said. Not a question. A statement.

“Yes,” Edmund said. “I have to. The census man’s notes, the inquiries, the whispers—they are closing in. If I do not go, they will come for you, for the household, for everyone who has trusted me with their lives.”

“Then go,” Adeline said, and her voice was steady, but Edmund heard the tremor beneath it. “Go, Edmund. But know that the berth is not the only thing you are leaving behind. You are leaving me. You are leaving us. And I am not certain I can wait another twenty years for you to come back.”

“You will not have to wait,” Edmund said, and he meant it, though the words tasted of ash. “I will write. I will send word. I will carry the memory of you all in the satchel, and when the time is right, I will return. Or I will not. But you will not be alone. You have each other. You have the movement. You have the cause. I am merely the thread that holds some of it together. If I break, the rest will hold.”

Adeline reached up and touched his face, her palm warm against his cheek. “You are not merely a thread. You are the whole cloth. Do not forget that.”

“How could I?” Edmund asked, and he meant it again, though the question was a plea, a prayer, a lifetime of doubt distilled into a single breath. “I have been carrying you with me every night I steamed open your letters. I have been carrying Marta in the memory of her waiting. I have been carrying the dead in the papers I forged. I am never alone. I am just… heavily populated.”

He stepped out of the carriage then, the satchel heavy on his arm, and walked toward the harbor gate. The quay was crowded with men and women saying goodbye, loading trunks, checking documents. The *Vigilant* loomed larger now, its sails a pale triangle against the dying light. A naval officer stood at the gangplank, clipboard in hand, and behind him, Edmund could see the harbormaster’s office, a squat building with a sign that read *All Papers Checked. No Exceptions.*

He approached the gate, the brass key in his pocket, the satchel swinging against his hip. Mr. Quill was there, as he had been earlier, his notebook open on his lap, his pen poised over the paper. He looked up as Edmund drew near, and the politeness in his eyes flickered, replaced by something sharper, something that Edmund had seen before in the eyes of men who knew too much.

“Mr. Iversen,” Quill said, and his voice was mild, but the undercurrent was unmistakable. “I was just about to send for you. The census records… the discrepancies… I think we need to have a proper conversation. About your… associations. About the people listed in your—”

“Mr. Quill,” Edmund interrupted, and he kept his face neutral, his tone the same polite courtesy he had used a dozen times before. “I am afraid I cannot spare the time tonight. The carriage is waiting. The ship is preparing. I have matters to attend to.”
