## 19. The Inquiry

*The interview room was a cell of white paint and bare concrete, cold enough to make Edmund's teeth ache despite the summer heat pressing against the tall windows. Chairs sat in a semicircle facing a single table where a tin light flickered, casting long shadows that seemed to stretch toward the men standing over him like judges.*

*Mr. Quill had been called to examine the discrepancies in the Marsh household records. His office in the Ministry of Internal Affairs was described as minimal—two chairs, a desk cluttered with ledgers, and a window overlooking the market square where Port Mahon's merchants went about their business. But here, stripped of his usual surroundings, he appeared more like a policeman than a bureaucrat: pressed shoulders, rigid posture, the faint glow of fear beneath his polished exterior.*

*Edmund stood with his hands folded, though he could not help but tense them at his sides. Before him sat Quill, a thin man with spectacles that caught the light and eyes that examined rather than saw. The air between them was thick with the weight of potential accusation, of histories intertwined in ways neither party wanted to acknowledge.*

"Miss Marsh," Quill said quietly, though his voice carried no warmth beyond the procedural formality of the law. "We've reviewed your correspondence from the last decade. There are inconsistencies."

*Edmund felt the familiar pull of the satchel against his hip—a small leather thing worn smooth by years of carrying. Inside were the forged papers, the counterfeit identification, the careful architecture of a lie built to save a family from execution. Each sheet was a brick in a wall erected against the storm that came from above. Now they would be laid bare.*

"I have done my due diligence," Edmund said. His voice was steady. That steadiness had become a kind of prayer over the months of concealment. "These papers represent genuine persons. They existed. And I have represented them truthfully when needed."

"You used other people's identities," Quill pointed out. It was not a question. "The Marsh household registry lists three individuals who do not appear in any official record beyond this residence. A man named Harold Brennan. Two children registered as Thomas and Alice. Are you telling me these men and girls passed through our territory without ever appearing in proper census?*

*Each name Landed like a stone dropped into still water. Edmund felt the ripples spread through him—the ghosts of lives lived elsewhere, absorbed into the fabric of his own deception. His mother had been called Hilda Marsh. Her husband, James, had been listed last year as having moved to Geneva. She was buried in a cemetery outside Amsterdam. He had vanished along with the rest of their family six months earlier, transported across a border he now remembered nothing about.*

"But the point is, I was protecting them. My father would have imprisoned us if he knew we were fugitives. My sister Marta was building a new life. I gave her the best chance possible."

Quill leaned forward slightly. "But protection requires honesty, unless there is reason to believe the hidden person poses a threat to public order. The census instructions were explicit: verify all claims. The discrepancies we've noted suggest otherwise."

*There it was. The crack in the façade he had spent five years maintaining. Five years of lying convincingly, of creating a paper trail that might just allow his family to reunite with the world they had fled. The war was coming—not yet seen openly, but whispered through the cracks of news reports and gossip at the ports. Everyone knew who was building secret movements. Everyone expected the government to find and crush them.*

"Explain."

*He opened his satchel slowly, revealing the documents inside. Not all at once—too much information at once would overwhelm—for better and for worse. Some were legitimate papers he had obtained during his travels, copies of birth licenses, marriage certificates, military service records. Others were his forgeries, created from scratch: identification cards bearing the name of deceased men, residence declarations signed by false officials, a baptism certificate for children who had never been born in Port Mahon.*

"I took many risks," Edmund said. "When the German expansion threatened the western provinces, my family received orders to relocate. My father had connections. My younger brother and I were taken to a safe house in the countryside. My parents were... relocated. I know not where. I know not why."

"And you believed this constituted a crime?"

*The question struck him deeper than any physical blow. His father's execution had been certain. No evidence survived, no one had spoken of it after the war began, and when Edmund tried to speak of it directly, the witnesses had died or gone silent. He had assumed the worst. Surviving was enough. That had always been the rule.*

"I considered reporting those who helped my family escape," Edmund continued. "It would have required disclosure. Names. Connections. A confession that could destroy everything I had built. So I chose silence. A different path."

*Quill studied him with the detached interest of a man examining specimens. "A different path has costs. These three individuals you mentioned—their existence was documented. Their families may have believed them real. If you're hiding them now, by continuing this arrangement..."

"—my family is safe. Marta is safe. My children are learning Spanish and mathematics in school, not in the shadow of suspicion. And the work I did in Geneva, organizing supply routes for the underground—"

"The organization you worked for was dissolved. The purpose was abandoned when the conflict intensified. Under current circumstances, supporting such activities would be classified as treasonous collaboration."

*The words landed harder than expected. He had expected denial, perhaps, or a request for documentation. Instead, he faced a man who understood exactly why he was being questioned, and who saw through the pretense attempt.*

"If you will tell me where these individuals actually are," Quill said, "we can investigate properly. But if you simply refuse to identify them—"

"He will listen to me? Then I shall tell the truth. Every name, every location. I will stand before this tribunal as a witness to my own actions. I will accept whatever punishment the court deems appropriate. But I will not lie."

*This was the test. He had passed it before, in smaller versions. Bribes accepted, passports forged on the fly. But the scale of this confrontation was different. Here, every word mattered. Every refusal could become evidence. Every confession could be weaponized against him.*

"Consider carefully," Quill said. "My office has resources that extend far beyond this chamber. If you continue to obstruct, the consequences will be severe. You understand this."

*The threat was clear and unmistakable. Edmund nodded. He could almost feel the danger coiling around him, heavy and cold. But he also felt something else—a strange sense of clarity, as if lifting a curse from his chest. This was his moment. This was the answer he had been preparing since the night in 1900, since the night he first carried his sister and her children past checkpoints, since he realized that survival required building walls, and walls required walls require guards who must sometimes look the other way.*

"Then let us proceed," he said. "I am prepared to discuss the matter thoroughly."

*Quill rose and walked to a cabinet against one wall, pulling out a file. Within it lay folders, photographs, a stack of correspondence—documents that confirmed what Edith had suspected. Edmund recognized names from his past: a clerk named Robert Fenn. Two men whose photos showed tired faces and hollow eyes. A woman in a red coat who smiled too wide in a photograph from 1898.

And then, beneath these official records, he found the names Edmund had written into his own thoughts years ago, naming three people whose lives he had saved through deception—only to realize, reflecting now, that saving others sometimes meant becoming less than the self he had been trying to protect.*

"Miss Marsh." Quill's voice softened fractionally. "For the sake of the witness, I will address you directly. The individuals you mention—Harold Brennan, Thomas Marsh, and the children registered as Alice and Thomas—I have traced them. All three have been located. They are living. They are healthy. They have families who would appreciate their safety."

*Edmund felt the ground shift beneath him. Harold Brennan was a man of substance, a merchant who had served the administration for decades. His death at the front had been mourned by thousands. The fact that he was alive—actually alive—in obscurity, operating freely in civilian life, suggested either extraordinary luck or a secret he had kept well guarded. The children... Thomas and Alice, the ones registered at the Marsh household, were the youngest members of the group that had escaped in 1900. Had they survived? Had they grown?

He thought of Marta, who had left him that day at the station, promising to return once the situation eased. He had never seen her again. Perhaps she had perished. Perhaps she had joined one of the groups he had hidden. The thought terrified him, but it did not stop him from speaking.*

"I have identified the persons you refer to," he said. "I cannot pretend I did not know they existed. I constructed their presence deliberately—to create cover for my family. But knowing their reality does not change the fact that their lives depend on the continuation of the arrangements I helped establish. To inform on them would mean destroying lives that were themselves products of my choices."

"That is precisely the problem," Quill replied. "Your involvement enabled those families. Do you wish to continue that pattern?"

"No."

"Why not?"

*Edmund met those eyes, searching for the flicker of manipulation, the expectation that he would rationalize his position. But there was none. Only the terrible clarity of a mind that had seen too much to lie.*

"Because I decided, in the darkest hour of my life, that certain truths were worth dying for. Those three individuals are proof that survival can take forms most would call treasonous. But they are also proof that my own survival was always conditional upon others being destroyed. The price I paid to protect my family was the destruction of innocent lives. And I cannot in good conscience add three more to that count."

*The room fell silent except for the tick of the tin light. Quill's expression did not change, but something shifted in the space between them—a recognition, perhaps, that the man before him had been operating on principles that extended beyond personal loyalty.*

"Let me show you something." Quill produced a document from the folder—an old letter, handwritten, dated 1900. A train manifest listing passengers proceeding westward. Among them, clearly identified, were names that matched those he had spoken of.

"Harold Brennan traveled to Paris with a wife and two children," Quill read from the page. "Thomas Marsh was conscripted into the mountain militia and disappeared in the spring of that year. The daughter, Alice, remained in the village. Both have been found. They are married now. They have children. Lives built on foundations I helped construct."

*Edmund stared at the faded ink. The name Harold Brennan. A man who had served the very regime that was now hunting him. Had he known? Or had he witnessed something that changed his understanding of what he was fighting against?*

"There may be more," Quill added quietly. "Names in the archives that surface regularly. People who benefited from the networks I maintained. The system was flawed. My role in maintaining it was not entirely separate from the war effort."

*Allow me to rise. I must speak plainly, and soon.* Edmund straightened his collar. "I do not deny what I did. I do not minimize the harm caused by my arrangements. I made choices that allowed suffering to happen, even if those suffering were necessary outcomes of larger convictions. But those choices define my lineage. I have passed down a legacy of deception to my family, to everyone who depended on the covers I provided. To eradicate that chain would be to betray everyone who trusted me. And trust is what survives the war, whether you admit it or not."

*The words hung in the air. He had chosen to be the guardian of memory, even when memory meant protecting the guilty.*

Quill set the document aside. "You may walk away from this interview. Your cooperation has been noted, and your sentence delayed. However, I must stress that any future attempt to obstruct proceedings will result in prosecution under the Emergency Sedition Acts. You understand this."

*Under the Act, disobedience could mean imprisonment. Could mean fines. Could mean execution in the most extreme cases. But it could also mean the complete dissolution of the network that had protected his family and given them hope. The irony was not lost on him. He had built a web of lies to survive, and now he faced justice for building it.*

"Yes," Edmund said. "I understand."

*The formalities of the meeting concluded. Quill gestured to the door. "Take care of yourself, Miss Marsh. Consider the broader implications of your actions."*

*As Edmund stepped out into the corridor, the cool air of the interview room settling against his skin, he paused. Behind him, he heard the muffled sounds of the city below—merchants closing shop, children calling, the distant hum of naval vessels preparing for departure. The siege was coming. The war that had been whispered about was now materializing in ships and fortifications and troop movements.*

*He turned and looked back at the interview room one final time. The white walls, the bare concrete floor, the single light that cast elongated shadows. It had been the seat of his greatest fear, but also his greatest solace. Standing behind it, surrounded by the accumulated weight of forged documents and hidden lives, he felt something settle in his chest. This room had held his secrets for months. Now they were being released, examined, possibly condemned. And still he stood, walking toward his fate—or perhaps away from it—together with the people he had saved.*

*Behind him, Quill watched with the patient attention of a predator assessing prey. Whatever else the man had done, he would not let go of this. Until Edmund broke, or until justice finally arrived.*

---

*Edmund walked the four streets to the shore, his footsteps echoing on cobblestones slick with evening mist. The sea breeze carried the smell of salt and diesel—a mixture that would haunt him for the rest of his days. He thought of Harold Brennan, sitting comfortably in a Parisian salon, raising a glass to a wife and children he had never truly known. He thought of the three souls he had rendered immortal through falsehood, their lives woven into a tapestry of convenience that he now had to untangle.*

*Somewhere in Paris, the children of Thomas and Alice drew their first breaths of new life. Somewhere in the mountains, a soldier named James Marsh had disappeared, his body never recovered—another casualty of the war that edged closer every day. And somewhere, in the corridors of power, a censorship officer continued to draw lines on population registers, marking men for disappearance.*

*The weight of it all settled upon Edmund like a second skin. He had not asked for this burden. He had only sought to protect his family, to build a future for Marta, to give her children the beginning of a life free from the shadow of suspicion. But the universe had its accounting principles, and debts accumulated even in the darkest hours.*

*At the edge of town, where the harbor stretched wide and the dockyard hummed with activity, he finally reached the waterfront. A small rowboat waited at the end of the pier, its oars propped beside it. He pushed off, climbing into the cockpit, feeling the cold spray of the bay kiss his face.*

*Behind him, the city was stirring toward sleep. Somewhere within its walls, an interrogator sat in judgment. Somewhere farther away, a ministerial office processed his files. The machinery of state functioned without pause, grinding the hidden into visible, the brave into criminals.*

*But one boat could cross a distance. One life could reach another. That was what mattered—less than the grand narratives of war and surrender, but eternal nonetheless. The bridge he had crossed decades ago, built from paper and courage instead of steel, now burned in his memory. But the lesson remained: the act of reaching across water, of carrying someone home, was the defiance that kept the darkness at bay.*

*He turned the boat toward the horizon where the last ship from Port Mahon was preparing to sail. The wind filled his sails. Behind him, the city lights shimmered on the water like scattered stars waiting for their own voyages.*

*And on the deck of that ship, three souls—Marta, her daughter, and a stranger who had once been a prisoner of suspicion—waited to board. They would leave this place. They would begin anew. And in their passing, Edmund Iversen would walk away from the interrogator with his head held high, knowing that this was his true work: not merely surviving the war, but preserving the humanity within it.*

*The boat cut through the darkness, leaving the harbor behind, carrying the bridge-builder toward whatever came next.*
