## 21. Letters Read Aloud

The letter came on a Tuesday in November, the third since the armistice rumors had begun to circulate like birds before a storm, and Edmund knew it was from Marta before he touched it—knew it by the weight of the envelope, by the slant of the address written in the hand he had taught her to make legible when she was nine years old and furious about it, by the faint smell of coal smoke that clung to everything that crossed the water now.

He stood in the hall of the Marsh house with the letter in his two hands, and for a long moment he did not open it. This was ritual. It had become ritual over the months, this pause at the threshold of her words, because once he began to read there was no stopping until the end, and the end always cost him something.

The house had changed since the last sailing. War had come in August like a debt long deferred finally called due, and Port Mahon had filled with khaki and grey, with wounded men on stretchers carried up from the hospital ships, with women in armbands who had once been suffragists and were now nurses, the cause transmuted rather than abandoned. Adeline had raged for a week when the rally permits were revoked, then had gone quiet in a way Edmund had learned to fear more than her fury, and then had rolled up her sleeves and turned the ground floor of her house into a canteen for soldiers' wives. The drawing room where she had once plotted campaigns of window-smashing now held trestle tables, urns of weak tea, mending baskets overflowing with socks that needed darning. The banner—*Deeds Not Words*, stitched in purple and green—hung folded in a cedar chest upstairs, waiting, Adeline said, for a peace worth marching in.

Edmund still drove her. The car was older now, its engine coughing in cold weather, petrol rationed and precious, but he drove her through streets hung with bunting and casualty lists, past shop windows papered with recruitment posters, and every evening he came home to the kitchen where Mrs. Pellow the cook and young Tomas—no longer a stable boy but a messenger boy with a bicycle and a uniform cap too large for his head—and Elsie the maid, and whatever strays the war had washed into the household, sat down together to eat.

It was a strange family. He had never expected to have one at all, let alone one assembled from wreckage.

"Is it her?" Tomas asked from the stairs, where he materialized the way he always did, silent as a cat, his bicycle still leaning against the doorframe behind him, mud on his knees. He was fourteen now and taller than Edmund had believed possible, all elbows and appetite, and he watched Edmund with the particular intensity of a boy who had once drawn the wrong conclusion about burning papers and had been gently, painstakingly taught the right one.

"It's her."

"You'll read it up in your room like always."

"I expect so."

Tomas made a noise of protest—not quite a word, but eloquent nonetheless—and clattered down into the kitchen, where the smell of onion and barley soup was beginning to gather strength.

Edmund climbed the stairs to his room. It was a small room, a servant's room really, though Adeline had insisted he take the better one at the back and he had refused and they had reached the compromise of this one with its good view of the harbor. From the narrow window he could see the water, grey today under low cloud, and the masts of ships, and the bridge—the old bridge, the one everyone called the Bridge of Port Mahon without thinking much about it, iron-legged and salt-rusted, carrying carts and soldiers and grief back and forth across the channel all day long.

He sat on the bed. He opened the envelope with the brass key he kept for the purpose, sliding it under the flap the way he had once slid it under Adeline's correspondence, and drew out three pages in Marta's careful hand.

*Dear Edmund,* it began, as it always began, as if they were acquaintances corresponding about the weather. *Dear Edmund, I hope this finds you well. Lena has grown two inches, I am certain of it, though the woman at the relief office says children stop growing when they are frightened and start again when they are safe, and perhaps that is what has happened.*

He read it through once, silently, the way he had read every letter for fifteen years—silently, alone, hoarding her words the way a miser hoards coins, each sentence counted and recounted. That was the old habit. That was the habit of a man who had spent his life believing that what mattered most must be hidden: his name, his history, his sister, his love. He had kept Marta in envelopes. He had kept her behind locked doors of silence, shared with no one, not even Adeline until that night at the kitchen table when the truth had finally spilled out of him like water from a cracked jug, and Adeline had laughed, and wept, and started planning before his tears were dry.

He read it through twice. And then he sat for a while with the pages in his lap, looking out at the harbour, and something shifted in him—a small tectonic movement, barely perceptible, the kind of shift that changes the course of a river.

Lena was asking questions. That was the heart of the letter, beneath the news of rationing and the kindness of the Norwegian family who had taken them in, beneath Marta's careful descriptions of a country at once foreign and familiar. *She asks about you constantly. She asks what you look like—I tell her tall, serious, hands that can fix anything. She asks why you stayed behind. I tell her because someone had to be brave enough to stay, and you were the bravest of us, though she is seven and does not believe staying can be brave. She says heroes leave. She says heroes sail away and do great things. I cannot make her understand that you did the greater thing, the harder thing, standing on the quay while our ship went out. So I have decided to stop trying to make her understand and instead to ask you to help me. Write to her, Edmund. Tell her yourself. Tell her what it was like, the night we crossed. Tell her about Papa's voice singing on the road. Tell her the things I cannot tell because I was too frightened to see them clearly, and you saw everything, you always saw everything.*

Below that, in a different ink, a child's hand, round and earnest: *Dear Uncle Edmund. Mama says you are real. Are you real?*

Edmund put the letter down on the blanket and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes.

When he came downstairs, the whole household was at the table. This was the hour he loved best now, though he would have died before admitting it: the kitchen at six o'clock, lamplight yellow on the scrubbed wood, Mrs. Pellow ladling soup with the gravity of a priest distributing sacrament, Elsie arguing cheerfully with Tomas about whether the wireless had said four hundred casualties or fourteen, Adeline at the head of the table with her sleeves rolled and her hair coming loose from its pins, looking, in this light, nothing like the firebrand of the newspapers and everything like what she actually was—a tired, magnificent woman holding a household together with willpower and weak tea.

There was a place for him. There had always been a place for him, from almost the first week, though it had taken him a year to stop being surprised by it.

He stood in the doorway with the letter in his hand, and the room noticed him the way rooms notice things—one by one, the talk dying, faces turning.

"You've had news," Adeline said. Not a question. She could read him better than any census man ever had; she could read him better than he read himself, some days.

"From Marta."

Mrs. Pellow set down the ladle. Elsie's hands went still over her spoon. Even the kettle on the stove seemed to hold its breath.

"And?" Adeline said.

And Edmund found that his throat had closed. He stood there in the doorway of the warm kitchen, a man of forty-one years who had lied to census officials and forged documents and stared down interrogators without flinching, and he could not say the simple words *she is well, they are both well, Lena has grown.* Because saying them aloud, here, in front of these people, would make them real in a way that keeping them sealed had never allowed them to be. Fifteen years of silence had built walls around his sister's existence, and to speak her name in this room was to pull down the last of them.

Adeline understood. Of course she understood; she had understood him from the beginning, or near enough. She pushed back her chair.

"Sit down, Edmund," she said, "and read it to us."

"I don't think—"

"She's your sister. She's been a name in your mouth for years and half of us didn't even know she existed until you told us, and that was wrong, Edmund, that was wrong of both of us—all this secrecy, all this hiding, as if loving people were something to be ashamed of." Her voice softened. "Read it aloud. Let her into the house."

So he sat down. He unfolded the pages with hands that were not entirely steady, and he began to read.

*"Dear Edmund, I hope this finds you well."*

His voice cracked on the second word and he stopped, cleared his throat, began again. And something strange happened as he read: the letter stopped being a private document and became a shared one. When he read about the ration lines in Bergen, Mrs. Pellow clicked her tongue and muttered that it was the same everywhere, poor souls. When he read about the Norwegian family—the Hovdens, fisherfolk, who had taken Marta and Lena into their house above the fjord and asked nothing in return except help with the nets—Elsie said *that's Christian behavior, that is,* and meant it as the highest praise. When he read Marta's account of Lena's questions, Adeline set down her cup and looked at Edmund with an expression he could not bear to meet directly, so he kept his eyes on the page.

And when he reached the bottom of the second page, the child's handwriting, *Dear Uncle Edmund. Mama says you are real. Are you real?*—the whole table was quiet, and Tomas, who never sat still, who fidgeted through grace and prayers and sermons alike, sat perfectly still, staring at Edmund with his mouth slightly open.

"Well," Mrs. Pellow said at last, briskly, wiping her eyes with her apron in a manner that suggested she had not been crying at all, "you'll write back tonight, and none of your gloom either. A child wants to hear about horses and motorcars, not about checkpoints."

"She should hear the truth," Adeline said quietly. "In time. Children are sturdier than we think. They carry more than we imagine." She glanced around the table—at Elsie, whose brother was somewhere in France; at Tomas, whose father had been lost at Jutland; at herself, whose cause lay folded in a cedar chest—and Edmund understood that she was speaking of all of them, of the whole generation of children learning early what adults had hoped to spare them.

"The truth," Edmund said slowly, "in my experience, keeps better than comfort. Comfort spoils. Truth keeps." He looked down at the letter. "Marta used to say that. Our father said it first, I think. On the road, the night we crossed—he sang to keep us steady, and when I asked him how he could sing with the soldiers so near, he said, *Because the song is true, and true things are stronger than fear.*"

He had never told them that. In fifteen years, in all the nights of steaming envelopes and memorizing other people's lives, he had never given them a single piece of his own story unprompted. Now it came out of him easily, like water finding a channel long prepared for it.

"Tell us about the crossing," Tomas said. "The real one. You've only ever told me bits."

Edmund hesitated. Then he looked at the letter, at the child's round handwriting asking *are you real*, and he understood what Marta was asking him to do—not merely to write to her daughter, but to become real, to step out of the envelope, to let himself be known. And he understood that he could not begin that work across the water if he could not do it here, at this table, among these people.

So he told them.

He told them about the night in 1900, the fog on the road, the papers meant for the dead that weighed in his pocket like stones. He told them about Marta at nineteen, clutching her sleeping son, walking with the terrible steadiness of the exhausted. He told them about the checkpoint, the lantern swinging, the soldier's bored face, the moment when the whole future balanced on whether a tired conscript chose to look closely at a name. He told them about the ship, the whistle, the quay, and the choice he had made to stay behind—though he did not dress it up as bravery, because it hadn't felt like bravery; it had felt like arithmetic, like the only sum that came out even.

"And then you walked back up the hill," Adeline said softly, "and started a new life with a new name, and waited."

"And waited."

"For eleven years."

"For eleven years."

Mrs. Pellow blew her nose with tremendous dignity. Elsie was openly weeping and pretending to be interested in her soup. Tomas had pulled his knees up onto the chair, a habit from younger days, and was looking at Edmund with an expression stripped of all his usual adolescent armor—looking at him, Edmund realized, the way he himself had once looked at his father: as at a person who had done something impossible and made it look ordinary.

"That's why you read her letters up in your room," Tomas said. "Not because you're secret. Because you were saving them up. Like—" he groped for the word— "like seed corn. For later."

"Something like that."

"Well," Adeline said, rising and beginning to clear plates in defiance of all hierarchy, "later is now. And I'll thank you, Edmund Iversen—or whatever your name truly is, and one of these days you must tell us that too—to read us the next one at this table as well. Consider it household policy. We are a family of readers now."

"A family," Edmund repeated. The word sat strangely in his mouth and then, gradually, did not.

* * *

He wrote to Lena that same night, sitting at the little desk in his room with the lamp turned low, the blackout curtain drawn tight against the regulations. It was the hardest letter he had ever composed, harder than any forgery, harder than any lie—for it required the opposite of forgery. It required him to put down, plainly, in words a seven-year-old might hold, the truth of who he was.

*Your mother tells me you ask whether I am real. I am real, Lena. My name was not always Edmund Iversen—that name was a coat I wore to keep others safe—but underneath the coat there has always been the same man, and that man is your mother's brother, and your uncle, and he loves you though he has never seen your face.*

*I will tell you about the night we crossed, because your mother asked me to, and because it belongs to you as much as to me. But I will also tell you this: the bravest thing I ever did was not the crossing. It was the staying. Anyone can run. Staying still, with your grief and your guilt and your hope, and building something anyway—that is the harder courage. Your mother has it. I think you have it too. Two inches of growing in a frightened year proves it.*

He wrote until the candle guttered. He wrote about his father's singing, about the fog, about the horse named after a saint. He wrote about the empty berth on the last ship from Port Mahon, and why he had left it empty, and what it had cost, and why he had never regretted it—though that part he wrote carefully, because regret was a country he visited often enough without advertising the fact.

At the bottom, after he had signed it, he added a postscript:

*P.S. Yes, I can fix anything. Ask your mother about the clock in the hallway at home, the one nobody else could mend. Some things pass down the family like eye color. Perhaps you have them too, the mending hands. If so, use them on people as often as on clocks.*

* * *

Winter deepened. The letters came monthly now, the mail routes uncertain but stubborn, and every one of them was read aloud at the kitchen table—household policy, unbreakable now, sacred. The household grew: a Belgian widow named Colette who had arrived with nothing and stayed to help Mrs. Pellow; two evacuated children from the north, pale and watchful, who thawed by degrees under Tomas's boisterous guardianship; a wounded lieutenant convalescing in the front room who played chess with Adeline and lost gracefully and always.

And on a raw evening in February, when snow was falling into the black water of the harbour and the lamps along the quay burned halos through the flurry, another letter came. Edmund recognized the hand at once—but the envelope was thinner than usual, and there was something in the angle of the address, some tension in the strokes, that made his stomach tighten before he had broken the seal.

He carried it into the kitchen, where the table was already laid, where the family was already gathering, and he slit it open with the brass key, and he read:

*Dear Edmund. I have news, and I hardly know how to write it, so I will write it quickly before my courage fails. There is a man here, a Norwegian shipping agent, who says that when the war ends—if it ends, when it ends—there will be ships going to England again, proper passenger ships with proper berths. He says he can hold places for us. He says come.*

*Edmund, Lena is twelve now. She has never seen your face. She carries your letters in a tin box under her bed and reads them until the folds are wearing through. Come to us, or send for us, or meet us halfway—whatever the world allows. But the war is turning. Everyone says it. And I am done with waiting, and I know you are too.*

*Come home to us. Or let us come home to you. Either way, brother—soon.*

Edmund lowered the pages. Around the table, the household waited, every face turned toward him, the snow ticking softly against the dark window, and Adeline reached across the crockery and covered his hand with hers.

"Well?" she said. "What does she say?"

He opened his mouth to answer—and found that the answer was larger than the kitchen, larger than the winter, larger than anything he had yet dared to plan.
