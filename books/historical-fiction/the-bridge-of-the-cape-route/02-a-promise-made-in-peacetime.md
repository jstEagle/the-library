## 2. A Promise Made in Peacetime

The dispatch case weighed less than a loaf of bread. That was the thing no one told you about the end of things — how little they actually came to.

Petra Eastwick stood at the window of her rooms above the chandler's shop on Cordwainer Lane and watched the first light come up over the harbor of Vessany. Below, the morning carts were already rolling, and the fishwives had their stalls open, and the whole city went about its business with the particular loudness of people who did not yet know — or would not say — that the business was ending. Three years of war on two fronts had taught her to read a city the way she read a dispatch: not for what it said, but for what it was careful not to mention. Vessany was very careful this morning. The newspapers were thin. The queues at the bakeries were orderly in the way that orderliness is when it is enforced by fear rather than habit. And along the quay, where the naval yard flew its colors, someone had taken down the republic's flag from the customs house and had not yet put up another.

She turned back to the room. It took eleven minutes to pack everything she intended to keep.

That was not an affectation. She timed it because Corven had taught her to time it, back when she was nineteen and new to the service and still owned enough possessions to make packing an event. *A courier's life is measured in minutes between doors,* he had said, tapping his own battered case. *Everything you own must be able to leave through one door, in one armful, before the second knock.* She had laughed then. She did not laugh now.

Onto the bed, then: the dispatch case itself, black leather gone green at the corners, its brass seals stamped with the ministry's triple tower. Two shirts. One wool coat, because the Cape Route ran north into weather that did not care whose side you were on. Her papers — three sets, three names, each identity worn soft as an old glove. A knife she hoped never to use. A small tin of tea, because there were limits to what a person should be asked to surrender. And the money, sewn into the coat's hem in four places, in four currencies, because the republic's own coin might soon be worth exactly what it was printed on.

Everything else went into a trunk she would leave with the chandler's widow, Mrs. Halloway, who had asked no questions in six years and would ask none now. The books. The photograph of her mother. The good boots she could not run in. The blue dress she had worn to the harbor festival, folded once and laid on top like a flag over a coffin.

She looked at it a moment too long.

* * *

Three years ago. The festival of Saint Aldery's Tide, when the whole quay strung its lanterns and the fishing fleet came home decked in bunting and even the customs men drank on duty. Petra had been twenty-six, four years in the service, sent to the harbor reception because she spoke three languages and because the Foreign Ministry liked to display its young women the way a merchant displays his best cloth — beautifully, and with a price attached.

Aldric Vane had been standing alone at the rail above the water, a tall man gone silver at the temples, watching the fleet come in with an expression she could not place until later: the look of a man memorizing something he expected to lose.

"You're not drinking," she said, because it was the sort of thing one said at festivals, and because her handler had told her to circulate among the foreign delegation and be charming.

"Neither are you." He turned. Up close he was older than his reputation suggested, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour. "I've been watching the boats instead. In my country we have a saying — count the boats before you count the promises."

"And what do the boats tell you tonight?"

He considered the harbor. Forty hulls riding at anchor, lanterns doubled in the black water, the whole scene arranged like something painted by a man who loved the sea too much to paint it honestly.

"They tell me your republic is beautiful," he said. "And that beautiful things on water are always further away than they look." He smiled then, and the tiredness rearranged itself into something warmer. "Forgive me. Festival talk should be lighter than this. You'll think all envoys are funeral directors."

"I think envoys are paid to know things other people get to enjoy not knowing."

Something flickered across his face — surprise, then appraisal, then a kind of relief, as if he'd been carrying a weight and had just found someone strong enough to set it down near. "You're Eastwick," he said. "The courier. I asked about you. They say you've carried dispatches through the Salt Passes in winter, twice."

"They say a great many things."

"Do they also say why a woman of your evident intelligence serves a government that pays its couriers less than its customs clerks?"

She should have deflected. It was the answer the service trained into you: *I serve the republic; the republic is worth more than its clerks.* But the lanterns were gold on the water, and the band was playing something in six-eight time that made the whole quay sway, and Aldric Vane looked at her as if her actual answer mattered, which almost no one ever had.

"Because someone has to carry the true thing past the comfortable lie," she said. "And I'm apparently suited to it. God help me."

Vane laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him — and bought her a glass of the festival wine that tasted of cherries and turpentine, and they talked for two hours while the fleet came home.

* * *

Now, in the grey room on Cordwainer Lane, Petra folded the blue dress into the trunk and closed the lid on it, and let herself remember the rest, because she had learned that memory was like a dispatch case: if you didn't open it deliberately, on your own terms, it burst open at the worst possible moment.

They met seven more times over eighteen months. Never anything so crude as an affair — both of them understood discretion as a native language — but meetings: a gallery opening where they stood too long before a seascape neither liked; a dinner where they argued about tariffs with the genuine pleasure of people using politics as a screen for something else; a walk along the breakwater in rain, under one umbrella, where he told her about his daughter.

"Sera," he said. "Twenty-two. Fiercer than I ever was at that age, thank God. She reads everything, believes half of it, and checks the other half out of spite." He said it the way some men talk of saints. "Her mother died when she was small. It's been the two of us against the world since, and lately the world has been winning on points."

"Lately," Petra said carefully, "the world has been winning everywhere."

They stopped walking. Rain ticked on the umbrella. Somewhere behind them a gull complained about the weather, or the century.

"That's why I'm here," Vane said. "Not the umbrella. This." He drew from inside his coat a letter — sealed, plain, unremarkable, the kind of thing a thousand men were carrying in a thousand coats that night. "If things turn — and they will turn, Eastwick, I've counted the boats — I may not be able to reach her. Embassies close. Envoys get recalled, or worse, kept. Letters stop crossing borders long before armies do." He held it out. "But couriers cross everything. That's the whole point of you."

She didn't take it. Not yet. "You're asking me to carry private correspondence outside the service. If my handlers learned—"

"My handlers," he said quietly, "learned last month that I've been reporting more to my own foreign office than to yours. I'm asking you to carry a father's letter to his daughter. Nothing in it touches your republic. Nothing in it can hang you. It says: *if I cannot come home, go to your aunt in the north, and here is how you will know true news of me from false.*" His hand stayed steady, but she saw now that it cost him. "It is the only thing I cannot do myself, and the only thing that matters."

"The republic and your country may be at war within the year."

"Yes."

"And you'd trust me anyway."

"I'd trust you *because* of that," he said. "Anyone can keep a promise in peacetime. Peacetime doesn't test them. I need someone who keeps promises in wartime, and those people are rare, and I believe you are one." He smiled, tired again. "Also, you owe me nothing, which makes whatever you agree to worth having."

Petra looked at the letter. Plain paper, plain seal, weighing perhaps half an ounce. She thought of the dispatch case at her flat, the triple-tower stamp, the oath she had signed at nineteen in a room that smelled of ink and floor wax. She thought: *this is the moment the two lives divide,* though she did not yet understand which life was which.

"If things turn," she said, taking it, "I will put it in her hands. Not post it. Not pass it to an intermediary. Her hands."

"Her hands," Vane agreed. "Thank you." And then, because he was fundamentally a kind man pretending to be a diplomat: "Come inside. You're soaked, and the wine is better than the weather."

* * *

The bells of the harbor chapel rang seven, pulling her back. Petra buckled the dispatch case shut and slung it across her body, where it sat against her ribs like a second heartbeat. The sealed orders from the ministry rode inside it — the official cargo, the route memorized in three variants, the relay towns marked in her head where no paper could betray them. And beneath the false bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, the other letter. Aldric Vane's letter, three years old now, patient as a seed.

Things had turned.

Everyone in Vessany knew it and no one said it, which was how the republic faced all its deaths. The northern front had folded in the spring. The tolls on the Cape Route — the republic's one great artery, the mountain-and-sea road every merchant fleet between two continents had to pay to pass — had been pledged, secretly, twice over, to two different lenders. And last week the Foreign Ministry had recalled its envoys from four capitals in a single day, which was not diplomacy but triage. Now they were sending their couriers out along the Route itself, carrying heaven knew what final instructions to garrisons and magistrates who would read them by lamplight and understand, without anyone saying it, that they were reading wills.

She locked the trunk and left the key with Mrs. Halloway downstairs, accepting tea she did not drink and a blessing she did.

"North?" the old woman asked, eyes on the case.

"North," Petra said. "Family business."

"Give them my regards." Which was Mrs. Halloway's way of saying *come back*, and they both pretended it was nothing.

Outside, Cordwainer Lane smelled of tar and frying fish and the particular salt-sweetness of the harbor at morning. Petra walked toward the north road with her collar up, one courier among forty in the waking city, indistinguishable, invisible, exactly as designed. At the corner of Quayside Street she paused — just a moment, just habit — and looked back at the water.

The fishing fleet was going out. That was normal; the fish did not know about wars. But along the customs house, the naval yard, the harbormaster's tower, men on ladders were working their way down the row of flagpoles, and the republic's colors — the white tower on the blue field — came down one after another, furled and bundled and carried away like laundry. No new flags went up. The poles stood bare against the pale sky, and the wind moved through them making the sound flags make when there are no flags.

She remembered Vane at the rail: *count the boats before you count the promises.*

She counted. Then she turned north, put her hand flat against the dispatch case at her ribs — the republic's seals above, a dead man's promise below — and walked out of Vessany while the city still believed it was a capital.

The north road climbed away from the sea through orchards going wild, and by noon she had passed the last milestone bearing the republic's mark, and by evening the first checkpoint lamps were burning yellow in the dusk ahead, and a man with a ledger was waving down travelers one by one, asking his quiet questions, writing down his quiet answers.

Petra adjusted her satchel, found her second name, and went to meet him.
