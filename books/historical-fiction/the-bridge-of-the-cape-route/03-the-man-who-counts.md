## 3. The Man Who Counts

The north road unspooled like a ribbon of bruised gold through orchards that had gone feral, their gnarled trees heavy with fruit that no one would pick. Petra walked with her satchel pressed against her ribs, the leather worn thin at the seams, the metal clasps of the dispatch case cool and unyielding through her woolen cloak. She had counted her steps out of Vessany: three thousand, four hundred and seventeen, give or take the detours around flooded gullies and the place where a bridge had been knocked down by spring floods. She counted the days since the republic's flags had been lowered from the palace roof, the silk fraying in the wind like moth wings. She counted the things she had left behind—books, a silver tea set, a portrait of her father—each one a number subtracted from a life that was now measured in what could be carried.

The sun dipped behind the hills, and the first checkpoint lamps were burning yellow in the gathering dusk. They stood like fireflies along the road, little pools of light surrounded by gathering dark. A man was waving travelers down, one by one, a ledger open on his lap, a quill poised between his fingers. He wore the gray-green of the census bureau, a uniform that had once signered order and now felt like a shroud.

Petra adjusted her satchel, found her second name—Eastwick, the name she had inherited, the name she used when she needed to be someone else—and went to meet him.

Ivo Marsh looked up as she approached. His eyes were the color of overcast sea, and his face was thin, stretched tight over cheekbones that suggested he spent more time reading than eating. He didn't smile. He only watched.

"Name," he said.

"Petra Eastwick."

"Household?"

"Third house on the left from the market gate, before the fire."

He wrote it down without looking up. The quill scratched across the page, a small sound in the sudden silence of the road. Behind her, a cart creaked to a halt, a family of three slipping off into the dusk, their bundles swaying. Marsh didn't look up until they were gone.

"Occupation?"

"Writer. Occasional."

He glanced up then, briefly, and she saw the way his eyes narrowed fractionally. "The republic has need of writers now. Reports. Propaganda. Eulogies."

"Everyone has a role," she said, and meant it with the brittle calm of someone who had already decided what she was willing to lose.

He went back to his ledger. "Place of birth?"

"Vessany, the capital. District Nine, the writers' quarter."

"Family size?"

She had rehearsed this. "One brother. Departed. One mother, deceased."

"Siblings?"

"None living."

He was thorough. methodical. She had learned the rhythm of census takers from her father, who had filled out forms with the same careful hand, the same belief that if everything was recorded, everything could be controlled. But this was different. This was not counting heads; this was counting something else.

Marsh set the quill down. The ledger snapped shut with a soft sound. He folded his hands on the page, and for a moment she thought he was finished. Then he tilted his head, and his gaze drifted from her face to the bulge of her satchel, to the curve of the dispatch case beneath her cloak.

"May I?"

She hesitated. "Of course."

He reached out, his fingers deft but deliberate, and unfastened the clasp. The leather swung open, and the dispatch case lay inside, sealed with the republic's signet—a stylized crane in flight, stamped in red wax that had never been broken since she had taken it from the minister's hands. The seal was intact. The case itself was plain, unadorned, but the wax spoke: this was not ordinary correspondence. This was authority.

Marsh lifted the case. It was heavier than it looked, dense with the weight of sealed dispatches, of orders and counter-orders, of the republic's final attempts to hold the Cape Route against the fracturing provinces to the north. He set it back down, his fingers lingering near the wax.

"Household goods," he said. "Books. Papers. What else?"

"Letters. A few changes of clothing. A mother's ring, given to me before I left."

He picked up the ring from where it lay on top of the contents, thin gold band with a small obsidian stone. He turned it over in his palm, examining the engraving inside: *For Petra, in every sea.*

"Your mother's name?"

"Alta Eastwick."

"Deceased?"

"Five years. Fever."

Marsh set the ring down. He did not ask about the dispatch case's contents. He did not need to; the seal had already answered that. Instead, he looked up at her, and she felt the full weight of his scrutiny—not as a census taker counting households, but as something else, something hungrier.

"How many miles from Vessany to here?"

"Twenty-four. I walked most of it."

"And before Vessany?"

"Shehyn. I was born there."

"Shehyn." He wrote it down. "And what did you carry from Shehyn?"

"Nothing. I left everything."

He studied her face. "You lie well," he said. "But you lie too much. A body can only hold so many histories before they begin to leak."

The words landed like a stone in still water. Petra felt a flicker of something hot and sharp—defiance, or perhaps the first stirrings of the dread she had been carrying since the orders came. She had been lying since she packed the satchel, since she memorized the route, since she had shaken Aldric Vane's hand at the harbor festival and promised him she would deliver his daughter a letter if the republic ever needed a ghost. But she had not expected the lies to be counted. She had not expected anyone to watch her count.

"I lie to survive," she said. The words were true, though she had not meant to say them aloud.

Marsh's eyes stayed on her. "The republic survives on truths. On numbers. On the weight of what can be carried and what cannot."

He reached for the dispatch case again, his knuckles brushing her wrist as he lifted it. The wax seal caught the yellow light of the lamp, and for a heartbeat she thought he would break it, would open it and read the dispatches there, would see the names she had sworn to protect, the truths she had carried in her bones. But he only weighed it, his expression unreadable, and set it back in the satchel.

"Your route," he said. "North. Toward the Cape."

"Yes."

"Past the marshlands. Past the old lighthouse."

"I intend to keep walking."

Marsh closed the ledger. He stood, stretching his spine, and Petra saw then the small notebook tucked into his belt, the one he had not been writing in. He adjusted the brim of his cap, and when he looked at her again, there was something almost apologetic in his gaze—almost, but not quite.

"You have a quiet face," he said. "People often forget I'm watching."

He waved her through. The lamp shifted, casting a moving circle of light behind her as she walked past, into the dark road that unspooled northward. She did not look back. She could feel his eyes on her back, the ledger tucked under his arm, the quiet questions still hanging in the air between them.

The road ahead climbed, switching back through pine woods where the wind whispered through needles like a slow breath. Petra quickened her pace, her heels finding rhythm on the packed earth. She kept her hand flat against the dispatch case at her ribs, feeling the republic's seals above, the dead man's promise below. She had left Vessany believing it was a capital, a center of everything. Now she knew it was only a name on a ledger, and she was the ink.

Ahead, the road narrowed into a pass between two hills, and the yellow lights of the next checkpoint flickered, already burning. She had lied to Marsh. She had lied well. But as she walked, she felt the lies shifting inside her, the edges wearing thin, the truth beginning to show through the cracks. The census taker had not asked her the question that would expose her—not yet. But she knew, with a cold certainty that settled in her chest, that he was making a note of her, filing her away, and that the machinery she had entered was already beginning to turn.

She pulled her cloak tighter around her, and walked on.

The next morning, the road rose higher into the high meadows, where the grass was silvered with frost and the sky was a vast, indifferent blue. Petra's feet ached, and the satchel felt heavier with every mile. She had not slept well; the encounter with Marsh had left her stomach knotted, her thoughts looping around his words—*A body can only hold so many histories before they begin to leak*. She had buried so much deep, woven so many false threads into the tapestry of her life, that she had almost begun to believe them herself. But Marsh had not been fooled. He had seen the weight of the case, the seal, the way she had clutched it like a lifeline. And he had let her go.

Why?

She could not answer the question, not yet. She kept walking, her breath pluming in the cold air, the dispatch case warm against her ribs despite the frost. The republic was behind her now, a memory of smoke and falling flags, of ministers in polished rooms and census takers with ledgers. Ahead was the Cape Route, the enemy's territory, the daughter she was meant to find, and the promise she had made in peacetime that had now become her only compass.

By midday, she came to a crossing where the road dipped toward a river that wound silver through the valley. A ferry was moored at the bank, its hull patched with canvas and rope, a flag tattered at the mast. A handful of travelers were already waiting: a farmer with a sack of grain, a woman carrying a child on her hip, a young man with a rifle slung across his back. They all looked at her as she approached, the dispatch case visible at her side, and she felt the familiar weight of being watched.

The ferryman, an old man with a white beard and eyes that had seen too many winters, nodded her through without asking for papers. "You're heading north," he said. "Same as the rest of us, I suppose."

"Same as the rest of us," she agreed.

She stepped into the boat, the wood groaning under her weight, and sat beside the young man with the rifle. He did not speak, but his eyes flicked to the satchel, to the seal peeking out from beneath her cloak. Petra kept her face impassive, her gaze fixed on the river flowing past the boat's prow.

"You're a long way from Vessany," he said finally, his voice low.

"A long way from a lot of things."

He nodded, and fell silent again. The crossing took half an hour, the ferry creaking against the current, the river carrying them downstream toward the Cape Route. As they neared the far bank, Petra saw that the shore was lined with tents, a makeshift camp of people fleeing the same things she was fleeing. Smoke rose from a communal fire, and children ran between the tents, their laughter a sharp sound against the quiet of the river.

At the landing, a deserter from the republic's army was sitting on a crate, cleaning a rifle. He wore the green uniform but had cut the insignia from his sleeve. He looked up as Petra stepped off the ferry, and his gaze settled on the dispatch case.

"You've got the seal," he said. His voice was rough, unused to speech. "Republic seal. Fresh."

Petra stopped. "I do."

"Heard they're hunting for it. Courier got stopped at a checkpoint yesterday. Census taker. Marsh, they called him."

Her stomach gave a small flip. "Did he get it?"

The deserter smirked, a grim, humorless thing. "Depends on who you ask. Some say the courier got through. Some say she was turned back. Some say she was made an example of."

He set the rifle down and gestured to the seat beside him. "I'm Tarl. I deserted three days ago. I've been watching the checkpoints. The census is getting ugly. They're not just counting heads anymore. They're counting what people carry. What they know. Who they've seen."

Petra's hand tightened on the satchel. "And you're telling me this because..."

"Because I'm going north too. Same direction. And I figured someone carrying a republic seal might want to know the road is getting narrower."

She considered him for a long moment. Then, from the pocket of her cloak, she pulled the mother's ring—the obsidian stone, the engraved gold band. She set it on the crate between them.

"For your silence," she said. "And for your safe passage."

Tarl picked up the ring, turned it over in his light. "You'd give me your mother's ring for silence?"

"I'd give you anything that can be carried," she said. "And I need silence. I need the road."

Tarl looked at the ring, then at her face. He nodded slowly, tucking it into his pocket. "North," he said. "Past the marsh. Past the lighthouse. That's where you're headed, right?"

"North."

"North it is." He stood, shouldering his rifle. "Good luck, Petra Eastwick. Whatever that name is worth now."

"Thank you," she said, and walked away before she could hear the roughness in her own voice.

The ferryman was waiting on the far bank, taking the boat back upstream. Petra walked toward the camp, her satchel heavy at her side, the dispatch case warm and unyielding beneath her cloak. She had paid for silence with a ring that had belonged to her mother, a thing of gold and memory, now given to a man who had once worn the enemy's uniform. She had crossed a river, left a ferry behind her, and was now deeper into the republic's collapse, toward the Cape Route and the daughter of a man the state had declared a traitor.

Behind her, the river flowed toward the sea, carrying away the ferry, the camp, the yellow light of the morning, and everything else she had known. Ahead, the road wound into the unknown, and the census taker's ledger was already turning, the pages filling with names and secrets and the weight of what could be carried when the orders came.

She quickened her pace, her breath steady, her eyes on the horizon. The machinery was hunting her. She knew it now. She had felt it in Marsh's gaze, in Tarl's caution, in the way the country itself seemed to be folding in on itself, counting every step, every word, every secret. But she also knew that she was not alone. Somewhere ahead, at the end of the Cape Route, was Sera Vane, the daughter of the traitor, and the letter that could prove the republic's treachery. And somewhere behind her, in the dusk of the falling republic, was Ivo Marsh, the census taker who watched too long, and who might yet become something more than a hunter.

She walked on, the dispatch case at her ribs, the road unspooling before her like a ribbon of dark cloth, and the truth of what she carried beginning to settle into the bone.
