## 4. What the Republic Owes

The telegraph key clacked like a bone striking bone. Three sharp taps, a pause, two more. Corven didn't flinch. He had sat in this chair for seventeen years, in this room that smelled of ozone, coal smoke, and the particular sourness of secrets kept too long in hot air. The relay station at Vellern was a squat stone building wedged between a tannery and a cooperage, its windows permanently fogged with the breath of industry. Outside, the Cape Route's main artery thrummed with wagons and foot traffic, the republic's lifeblood flowing north toward the estuary and the sea.

Corven's fingers, thick-knuckled and ink-stained, rested on the brass key. The message coming over the wire from the capital had taken forty-seven minutes to transmit. Forty-seven minutes of dots and dashes that rewrote a woman's fate in the time it took to drink a cup of tea.

*OPERATION SEAGLASS RECLASSIFIED. DISPATCHES NOW CATEGORY ONE EVIDENCE. COURIER DESIGNATED LIABILITY ASSET. HANDLER AUTHORIZED FOR TERMINAL RESOLUTION. ACKNOWLEDGE RECEIPT.*

The paper tape curled from the receiver, spooling onto the floor in a pale yellow snake. Corven tore it off with a neat motion, folded it twice, and slipped it into the inner pocket of his waistcoat. His other pocket held a silver watch, stopped at 3:14. He hadn't wound it in three days. Time had ceased to be a measurable quantity and become something you spent.

He stood, joints popping, and crossed to the window. The glass was wavy with age, distorting the street into a funhouse mirror. A woman in a faded blue shawl herded two children past a wagon loaded with salt barrels. A dog snapped at the wheels. Ordinary life, ordinary as breath. Somewhere on that road, perhaps twenty miles south, perhaps fifty, Petra Eastwick walked with a satchel strapped across her chest. The case inside was oak, banded in iron, sealed with the republic's triple wax — red, black, and gold. Corven had chosen the case himself. He had chosen the route. He had chosen the woman.

*Asset.* The word tasted of copper and ash.

He pulled the ledger from the drawer — not the official one, bound in ministry blue, but the personal one, leather covers worn soft as skin. Its pages held names, dates, sums paid, favors owed, the quiet accounting of a career built on knowing which debts could be collected and which must be forgiven. He turned to the page marked *E*.

*Eastwick, Petra. Recruited 1912. Harbor Festival. Father executed 1909 (treason, posthumous). Mother died 1911 (consumption). Sister, Mara, placed in Orphanage of St. Vitus, annual stipend 40 crowns paid from discretionary fund. Psychological profile: high pain tolerance, low sentimental attachment, exceptional memory for terrain. Weakness: keeps promises.*

Corven's pen hovered. The nib was split; it would need replacing. He had meant to replace it for weeks. There was always something more urgent.

*Weakness: keeps promises.*

He wrote beneath it, in the cramped hand of a man who had learned to write in moving carriages and damp cellars: *Mission reclassified 1923-11-14. Status: LIABILITY. Disposition: PENDING.*

The pen scratched. Ink bloomed.

He had known this day would come. Knew it from the moment the ministry's faction — the *Veritas* caucus, they called themselves, as if truth were a political stance — had begun purging the old diplomatic corps. Aldric Vane's expulsion had been the first stone in the avalanche. Petra's promise to the man, made over spiced wine and fireworks at the harbor festival, had been a pretty sentiment. Sentiment was not in the ledger. Sentiment was not in the republic's charter.

The republic owed its servants nothing but the dignity of their function. When the function ended, the servant ended. That was the arithmetic. Cold, clean, absolute.

Corven closed the ledger. He locked the drawer. The key went around his neck on a chain that had once held his wife's wedding ring. She had left him in 1916, taking their daughter to a coastal town in the neutral zones. He had not seen either in six years. The republic had not granted him leave to visit. The republic did not negotiate with its instruments.

He returned to the telegraph key. His thumbs found the contacts by muscle memory.

*ACKNOWLEDGED. TERMINAL RESOLUTION INITIATED. WILL ADVISE ON EXECUTION PARAMETERS WITHIN FOUR HOURS.*

He sent it. The machine chattered its reply: *CONFIRMED. MINISTRY GRATEFUL FOR LOYALTY. END TRANSMISSION.*

Corven sat back. The silence in the room pressed against his eardrums. He could hear the tannery's water wheel groaning, the cooper's hammer ringing, the dog barking. Life, indifferent.

He needed to move. The four-hour window was not a suggestion. The *Veritas* caucus did not tolerate hesitation. They had eyes in every relay station, ears in every ministry corridor. Corven's own position — Senior Handler, Cape Route Division — was a comfort, not a shield. His predecessor had vanished in 1919 after questioning a reclassification order. His name was not in the ledger. Corven had made sure of that.

He pulled a map from the wall cabinet. The Cape Route unfurled: a thin red line stitching the capital to the estuary, marked with relay stations, toll bridges, census checkpoints, ferry crossings. Saltmere Ferry was circled in red ink. Corven's own hand, three days ago. He had anticipated the route. He always anticipated the route.

Petra would reach Saltmere by dawn tomorrow if she kept pace. The ferry crossed at slack tide — 06:47, according to the almanac. She would take the northern landing, the one closer to the Vane estate. That was the promise pulling her: the enemy's daughter, Sera Vane, waiting at the coastal manor with her father's name and her mother's silence.

Corven traced the route with a fingertip. *Vellern → Hessler's Ford → Census Station 7 → Saltmere Ferry → Vane Estate → Toll Bridge → Cape Route terminus.*

Seven checkpoints. Three census stations. Two ministry garrisons. One ferryman who owed Corven a debt from a smuggling case in 1918. One census taker — Ivo Marsh — whose file Corven had read with interest. Marsh was thorough, rigid, a man who believed the census was a sacred accounting rather than a weapon. That made him useful. That made him dangerous.

Corven picked up the telegraph key again. He would need to send three messages. One to the ferryman, activating the debt. One to the garrison at the toll bridge, alerting them to a courier carrying forged credentials. One to Ivo Marsh, via the census bureau's secure line, flagging a person of interest matching Petra's description.

His hand hovered. The ledger weighed against his chest through the waistcoat. *Weakness: keeps promises.*

He thought of Petra at the harbor festival, nineteen years old in a borrowed dress, standing beside the Vane envoy as fireworks painted the harbor gold. He had watched from the shadows, as always. He had seen the way she looked at Vane — not with the hunger of a recruit, but with the recognition of a kindred spirit. Two people who understood that the world was built on lies, and that sometimes a promise was the only true thing you could carry.

He had recruited her six months later. He had told her the republic needed couriers who could memorize routes, withstand interrogation, discard their lives like worn gloves. He had not told her that the republic would one day ask her to discard her honor.

*Asset. Liability. Terminal resolution.*

The words were clean. The arithmetic was sound.

Corven sent the first message: to the ferryman at Saltmere. *HOLD NORTHERN LANDING 06:47. FEMALE COURIER, DARK HAIR, OAK CASE. DETAIN QUIETLY. PAYMENT PER AGREEMENT 1918.*

The machine clacked. He sent the second: to the toll bridge garrison. *ALERT: COURIER EASTWICK COMPROMISED. CARRIES CATEGORY ONE EVIDENCE. APPREHEND ON SIGHT. LETHAL FORCE AUTHORIZED.*

Clack. Clack. Clack.

The third message, to the census bureau, required a code he used rarely. *PRIORITY FLAG: IVO MARSH. TARGET: FEMALE, 30s, TRAVELING NORTH CAPE ROUTE. SATCHEL WITH OFFICIAL SEAL. QUESTION THOROUGHLY. REPORT LOCATION IMMEDIATELY.*

He sent it. The receiver spat back confirmation tapes. Corven tore them off, folded them, pocketed them. Three messages. Three threads pulled in a net that had been weaving for years.

He stood. His body moved through the familiar sequence: extinguish the lamp, lock the ledger drawer, check the window latch, secure the telegraph key with its cover. The room was a cell he had built himself, stone by stone, decision by decision. He could leave it. He had the authority, the funds, the contacts. He could walk out the back door, take the southern road to the neutral zones, find his wife and daughter. The republic would hunt him, but the republic hunted everyone eventually.

He did not move toward the back door.

He moved to the front door, opened it onto the soot-streaked street, and walked north toward the census station. The arithmetic demanded oversight. The ferryman was reliable but greedy. The garrison was efficient but blunt. Marsh was the variable — a man who counted things because he believed counting gave them meaning. Corven needed to see the variable for himself, to calculate its tolerance for pressure.

The walk took twelve minutes. The census station was a converted customs house, its façade scarred by the same soot that coated everything in Vellern. A flag snapped above the door: the republic's tricolor, faded to pink and gray. Inside, the air was thick with dust and the scratch of quills. Clerks sat at long tables, copying names from ledgers into ledgers, the great machine of the state digesting its own population.

Ivo Marsh sat at the far end, a smaller table, a finer pen. He wore the census taker's gray coat with its brass buttons, his hair cut short, his posture the rigid line of a man who had once worn a different uniform. His file said: *Former artillery officer. Discharged 1914 (injury). Joined Census Bureau 1915. Exemplary record. No political affiliations. Married, no children. Weakness: believes in the work.*

Corven approached without announcement. Marsh looked up, eyes pale as winter sky. "Handler Corven. This is irregular."

"Inspector Marsh." Corven kept his voice level, devoid of the warmth he reserved for no one. "I require your attention on a matter of urgency."

"I am conducting the quarterly reconciliation. The ministry expects —"

"The ministry has issued a priority flag. You received it three minutes ago." Corven produced the folded tape from his pocket, laid it on the table. "A courier traveling the Cape Route. Female. Dark hair. Oak case with official seals. She is to be questioned thoroughly. Her location reported immediately."

Marsh picked up the tape. His fingers were ink-stained, precise. He read, his face unchanged. "This courier. She has a name?"

"Eastwick. Petra Eastwick."

The name landed in the space between them. Marsh's expression did not flicker. He folded the tape, placed it in his own pocket. "Petra Eastwick. Registered courier, Cape Route Division. Active since 1912. Clean record. Commended for the Hessler crossing in '19." His voice was flat, reciting. "Why is a clean courier flagged?"

"Reclassification. The dispatches she carries are now evidence in a treason investigation. She is a liability asset."

"Liability asset." Marsh tasted the phrase. "A new classification."

"Effective today."

"And the dispatches? What do they contain?"

Corven leaned a fraction forward. "That is not your concern. Your concern is location and apprehension. The ministry will handle the rest."

"Of course." Marsh's pen tapped once on the table. "And if she resists?"

"Lethal force is authorized at the toll bridge. At the ferry, quiet detention. Here —" Corven let the silence stretch. "Here, you ask questions. You count. You report."

"And if my questions reveal something... inconvenient?"

Corven's smile was a thing of muscle memory, not humor. "The census counts what exists, Inspector. It does not judge what it finds."

Marsh held his gaze. The scratching of quills continued around them, a monotonous chorus. Somewhere, a clerk coughed. A child cried in the street.

"You are asking me to hunt a woman I have never met," Marsh said quietly, "for carrying documents I have not seen, on orders I cannot verify, from a ministry faction that has spent two years purging everyone who knew the difference between law and power."

"I am asking you to do your duty."

"My duty is to count households. To record births, deaths, migrations. To provide the republic with the data it needs to govern." Marsh's pen stopped. "Not to serve as an executioner's index finger."

"The republic *is* the executioner's index finger. The census is the hand that guides it. You know this. You have always known this."

A long silence. The flag outside snapped. The tannery wheel groaned.

Marsh exhaled. "I will flag the name. I will alert the checkpoints north. I will question any woman matching the description." He met Corven's eyes. "But I will also record the answers. All of them. And if the answers do not match the flag — if the woman is not what you say she is — I will note that discrepancy in the official log. With my signature."

Corven inclined his head. "Noted."

"Is that all, Handler?"

"That is all."

Corven turned. At the door, he paused. "Inspector. Your file mentions a wife. Clara. She teaches at the primary school on Market Street."

Marsh's face hardened. "Yes."

"She signed a petition in 1921. Protesting the closure of the orphanage at St. Vitus." Corven's voice was flat. "The petition was... noted. By the ministry."

Marsh's hand tightened on his pen. "My wife's political activities are not my own."

"Of course not. But the census counts households, Inspector. Not just individuals. A household with a dissident spouse... that is a variable. Variables affect reliability. Reliability affects advancement."

"Are you threatening my wife, Corven?"

"I am stating arithmetic. The republic owes its servants nothing but the dignity of their function. When the function is compromised..." He left the sentence unfinished.

He walked out into the soot and noise. Behind him, the scratching of quills did not pause. The machine digested.

* * *

The ferryman's debt was simple. Hans Vell, a broad-shouldered man with a face like a weathered dock post, had been caught moving untaxed tobacco across the estuary in 1918. Corven had buried the case in exchange for a favor: one priority message, one priority passenger, no questions asked, redeemable at any time. Vell had never used it. Corven had wondered if the man had forgotten, or if he was saving it for something that mattered more than his own skin.

He found Vell at the Saltmere landing, coiling rope on the ferry's deck. The boat was a flat-bottomed barge, reinforced with iron bands, its single sail furled against the mast. The estuary stretched gray and wide beyond, the far shore a smudge of trees and the Vane estate's pale tower.

"Handler." Vell didn't look up. "Early for the crossing."

"Priority message." Corven handed him a sealed envelope. "Northern landing, 06:47 tomorrow. Female passenger, dark hair, oak case. She travels alone. You will detain her — quietly — and hold her until a ministry detail arrives. Do not open the case. Do not speak to her beyond necessity. Do not allow her to disembark on the northern bank."

Vell took the envelope. His thumb traced the wax seal. "And if she resists?"

"She won't. She's professional. But if she does —" Corven produced a purse, heavy with crowns. "Half now. Half on delivery. The debt is paid either way."

Vell weighed the purse. His jaw worked. "The northern landing is tricky at slack tide. Current runs hard past the point. If she jumps —"

"She won't jump. She has a promise to keep." Corven's voice hardened. "The Vane estate. The enemy's daughter. That is her destination. She will not jump."

Vell pocketed the purse. "Done. But if the ministry detail doesn't show by noon, she walks. I'm not a jailer."

" They'll show." Corven turned to go.

"Handler." Vell's voice stopped him. "You've used this debt before. Twice. Both times, the passengers... they didn't come back."

Corven didn't turn. "The republic collects what it's owed."

"Even from its own?"

"Especially from its own."

He walked back along the muddy bank, boots sinking in the salt marsh. The tide was coming in, a slow gray flood swallowing the mudflats. Gulls screamed overhead. The air tasted of brine and rot.

He had three hours until the next checkpoint. Hessler's Ford. A census station there, manned by a clerk named Dietrich who owed Corven a different kind of debt — a gambling debt, settled with a blind eye to a smuggled crate of wine. Corven would need to ensure Dietrich's ledger flagged Petra's description. He would need to send a runner to the toll bridge garrison with updated orders. He would need to report to the capital that terminal resolution was in motion.

The arithmetic expanded. Variables multiplied. Marsh at Vellern. Vell at Saltmere. Dietrich at Hessler's Ford. The garrison at the toll bridge. The ministry agents who would eventually intercept Petra at the Vane estate — or fail to, if she arrived first.

And Petra herself. The constant. The woman who kept promises.

Corven reached the relay station. He unlocked the door, entered, locked it behind him. The telegraph key waited. The ledger waited. The stopped watch waited against his chest.

He sat. He wound the watch. The ticking filled the room, sharp as a heartbeat.

*Tick. Tick. Tick.*

He opened the ledger to a fresh page. *TERMINAL RESOLUTION LOG — OPERATION SEAGLASS.*

He began to write.

*1923-11-14 14:22. Reclassification order received. Acknowledged. Three priority messages dispatched: (1) Ferryman Vell, Saltmere — detention authorized. (2) Toll Bridge Garrison — apprehension authorized, lethal force. (3) Census Inspector Marsh, Vellern — flagging and questioning authorized. Marsh variable noted: potential deviation. Wife's petition recorded as leverage. Contingency: if Marsh fails to comply, replacement inspector available at Hessler's Ford (Dietrich).*

He paused. The pen hovered over the paper.

*Petra Eastwick. Status: LIABILITY. Disposition: TERMINAL RESOLUTION PENDING. Personal note: Asset recruited 1912. High competence. High loyalty to personal code. Low loyalty to institution. Predictable in mission execution. Unpredictable in moral calculation. Recommend —*

He stopped. The nib poised.

*Recommend what?*

The arithmetic was complete. The orders were sent. The net was cast. Petra Eastwick would be caught, the dispatches seized, the promise broken. The republic would survive another day, its secrets intact, its enemies silenced. The ledger would balance.

Corven wrote: *Recommend monitoring for deviation in handler loyalty. End entry.*

He closed the ledger. Locked the drawer. The key clicked against his chest.

Outside, the tide turned. The Cape Route waited. Somewhere on that road, a woman walked with an oak case strapped to her chest, measuring her life in what could be carried. She did not know the net had closed. She did not know the arithmetic had been done.

But she knew the route. She knew the ferry. She knew the promise.

And Corven knew that promises, unlike ledgers, could not be balanced. They could only be kept — or broken.

He extinguished the lamp. The room went dark, save for the faint glow of the telegraph receiver's indicator light, pulsing like a slow heart.

*Tick. Tick. Tick.*
