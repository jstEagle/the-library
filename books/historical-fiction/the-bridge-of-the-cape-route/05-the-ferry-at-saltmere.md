## 5. The Ferry at Saltmere

The road north had a way of narrowing into a single thought: *keep moving.* Petra counted the miles not by the milestones — which were fewer now, many of them toppled into the ditches where winter rains had carved the earth — but by the knots in her satchel strap, each one marking a league since the capital. She had tied the first knot at the relay house outside Aldric, and the third somewhere beyond the river at Brannock, where the water ran brown and fast and the bridge had already been cordoned off with chalked warnings. The satchel was heavier than it had any right to be. The dispatches inside their leather case weighed almost nothing — paper and wax and sealed intent — but the case itself, fitted with its false bottom and brass clasps, had a density that pulled at her hip like a counterweight. She had learned to carry it high, close to the ribs, so that the sway of it against her body became a kind of conversation: *I am still here. I am still moving.*

The ferry was not where she had expected it. The maps she had memorized at the capital — the ones Corven had pressed into her hands with the flat, precise touch of a man distributing instruments of death — showed Saltmere as a riverside hamlet with a single landing, its ferryhouse roofed in slate. But the landing was gone. Or rather, it was still there, a rotting pier jutted into the grey water like a finger pointing at nothing, but the ferryhouse had been stripped to its frame and the boat itself was gone, taken by the ministry's quartermasters two weeks past for use in moving supplies to the garrison at the estuary. What remained was a chaos of refugees: canvas tents stitched together from curtains and tents-within-tents, a circle of lean-to shelters built from dock planks and salvaged tarpaulin, and the constant low murmur of a people who had already decided to abandon their homes and had not yet decided where to go next.

Petra tied her horse to the last standing post and shouldered the satchel. The leather of the strap was warm against her shoulder, shaped by weeks of wear into something almost organic, like a second skin. She had learned to move through camps of this kind with a dancer's economy — eye forward, stride even, hands loose at her sides, never touching anyone, never making a promise. The refugees watched her pass with the particular stillness of people who had learned that attention was a luxury they could not afford. A woman sat on a overturned crate, holding an infant who slept with its mouth open, and when Petra passed, the woman did not look up. A man with a bandaged arm sorted salvaged nails into piles, his movements methodical, his eyes tracking her without interest. Everyone was watching, and no one was watching. It was the same thing, she had learned, and it made the difference between surviving and being seen.

The ferryman was not at his post. He had taken the boat, or the quartermasters had taken him, or he had simply vanished into the refugee population like a drop of water absorbed into sand. What Petra found instead was a small rowing boat, pulled up on the mud bank, its owner sitting on a overturned barrel a hundred yards downstream, drinking from a clay flask. He was a thin man, perhaps fifty, with the hollowed cheeks of someone who had been hungry for longer than he cared to remember. His clothes were patched but clean, which told Petra something about his priorities — he had kept his garments intact even when everything else had dissolved around him.

She approached with the satchel held before her like a shield. "The crossing," she said. "How much for the boat?"

The man looked at her. Not at the satchel, though his gaze lingered there a beat too long. At her face. He had the sharp, assessing look of a man who lived by reading people — the kind of attention that had kept him alive when the war had taken everything else.

"The boat's not for hire," he said. "The ferryman took it. But I can take you across for a price."

"What price?"

He set down the clay flask. "Silver. Enough for a week's food."

Petra's hand drifted instinctively to the satchel. She had silver — two small coins sewn into the lining of her coat, a reserve Corven had provided for emergencies. But emergencies had a way of compounding. She had used the first coin at the relay house for a meal and a bed, and the second was meant for the ferry at Saltmere and nothing else. She had calculated every expense at the capital, down to the last farthing, and the math had held for three weeks. Now it threatened to break.

"I have a letter of passage from the ministry," she said, and when the man's expression did not change, she added, "I can show it to you."

He nodded slowly. "Show it."

She unclasped the satchel's top latch and lifted the case — the official dispatch case, its brass corners gleaming even in the overcast light, its leather stamped with the seal of the Republic's Ministry of Foreign Correspondence. The red-and-gold stamp was unmistakable: a bridge spanning a river, the motto *Per Aspera ad Portum* — through hardship to harbor. She held it out on open palms, the way one might present a calling card, though she knew the seal meant more than that to anyone who recognized it. It meant *government business*. It meant *protected*. It meant *do not ask questions*.

The man looked at the seal, and something moved behind his eyes — not recognition exactly, but a flicker of calculation, the way a man watching a tide turn might calculate whether he would be swept inland or out to sea.

"That's the new courier's mark," he said quietly. "I saw it on the orders at the relay house at Brannock. The second courier, the one who never arrived."

Petra's fingers tightened on the case. "And you are?"

"A man who rows." He held her gaze. "The question is whether I want to row for the ministry or against it. Today, I row for the highest bidder."

She studied him. The mud on his boots was recent — not the dried crust of weeks, but the dark, pliable slime of today's rain. He had been watching the road. He had been watching for the courier.

"The crossing," she said, "and your silence about what you've seen."

"That's two prices," he said.

"Is it?"

He considered this. The oar leaned against his barrel. The river moved sluggishly beneath the broken pier. Somewhere in the refugee camp, a child was crying, and its mother hushed it with a hand pressed flat over its mouth.

"One coin now," the man said. "And the ring."

Petra felt the words land in her chest like a stone dropped into still water. She knew what he was looking at — or thought he was looking at. The ring was simple, a band of pale gold with a small green stone at its center, the kind of stone that had been fashionable in the capital thirty years ago when her mother was young and beautiful and not yet buried in a plot at the edge of town where the grass grew thin. Petra had worn it every day of her adult life. Her mother had put it on her finger the winter she turned twenty and had said, with the quiet emphasis of a woman making a statement that would outlast her: *This is the only thing I gave you that I did not earn for you. Do not lose it.*

She had not lost it. Not until now.

She unclasped it with steady hands — she had practiced this, or imagined she had practiced it, the motion of sliding the ring off her finger and holding it out on her palm. The stone caught what little light there was and threw a faint green glint across the mud. The man looked at it, and for a moment his face was bare of calculation, stripped to something simpler: want.

"Your mother's ring," he said, and the words carried no cruelty, only acknowledgment.

"Yes."

"Green stone. Turquoise, I think. The old Aldric mine produced those."

Petra's breath caught. No one had spoken of the Aldric mine in years — it had been sealed when the border disputes had turned violent, and the miners had been scattered to the refugee camps like seed on barren ground. The man knew the mine. He knew the stone. He was not merely a rower.

"Your name," she said instead of handing over the ring.

"Eben." He held out his palm. "The ring, or the name. Not both."

It was a fair trade, and Petra understood immediately that she was being tested as much as priced. Eben wanted to know if she would give up everything — the ring, the name, the last tether to the life she had left behind — or if she would calculate which sacrifice was smaller and which larger, and choose accordingly. The woman who had carried dispatches through three weeks of frontier roads would not flinch from this. She would not haggle. She would not plead.

She placed the ring in his palm and closed her fingers over it, feeling the cool metal and the slight irregularities of the stone against her skin. Then she said, "Eben."

He nodded, as if this settled something between them. He took the ring, slid it onto his own finger with the practiced ease of a man who had worn other people's jewelry before, and pushed the rowing boat into the water with a long, steady pull of his oar.

* * *

The river at Saltmere was wider than the maps had shown. Petra had not been surprised to discover that the maps were lies — Corven had told her this at the capital, with the detached tone of a man explaining that the weather would be cold — but the reality of the width was something else. It was not merely a matter of distance; it was a matter of time, and time was what she had least of. Every minute on the water was a minute not spent moving north, and every minute not spent moving north was a minute the ministry's machinery could close the gap.

Eben rowed with the quiet efficiency of a man who had done this all his life — or had done it often enough to have made it a habit. The boat glided over the dark water without sound, and the only motion was the gentle sway of the hull and the occasional dip of the oar. The refugees on the far bank watched them pass with the same detached curiosity as those on the near side, and Petra wondered how many of them had family on the other side, how many were crossing toward something or away from something, and how many simply did not know anymore.

She sat with the satchel across her knees, her fingers resting on the brass clasps as if they were the only fixed points in a world that had begun to tilt. The case was heavy against her thighs, and she could feel the dispatches inside — not their weight, which was negligible, but their presence, a density of intention that pressed against the leather like a heartbeat. She had memorized the route at the capital: Saltmere to the estuary, then north along the coastal road to the Vane estate, three days' travel by horse, two by foot if the roads were cut. She had memorized it the way one memorizes a prayer — not because she believed it would save her, but because the act of memorizing itself was a form of faith.

"You're not a local," Eben said.

"No."

"Where are you from?"

"The capital."

He nodded, as if this answered his question, though it had answered only the first of many. "And where are you going?"

"North."

"North of what?"

The question was simple, but it landed in the space between them with the weight of all the questions Petra had learned to deflect. *North of the republic. North of the ministry. North of what was left.* She chose a smaller truth.

"North of the river."

Eben accepted this. He did not press. But his eyes, steady on the water, had a quality of attention that made Petra feel as though she were being read the way one reads a page of dispatch paper — line by line, looking for the place where the ink bled and the lie began.

* * *

They reached the far bank as the light began to fail. The sky had thickened into a pewter grey, and the first shadows of the trees along the far shore stretched across the mud like fingers reaching for something they would not grasp. Ebe beached the boat and secured it with a loop of rope around a stake that had been driven into the riverbank — a stake that had been there for years, proof that this crossing had been made many times before, by many people, under many circumstances.

"There's an inn," Eben said, nodding toward a building half a mile inland. "Or what's left of one. You'll find shelter there. It's cheaper for refugees."

"I'll pay for the crossing," Petra said.

"You paid." He stepped onto the bank and rolled his sleeve over his wrist, checking for blood where the oar had rubbed. "The ring covered it. The crossing is done."

Petra stood in the mud with the satchel against her hip and looked at the inn. Its windows were dark, its door boarded, but smoke was curling from the chimney — a sign of life, or at least of someone who had not yet surrendered to the cold. She thought of the refugees at the landing, the child with the mouth open, the man sorting nails, the woman with the infant. She thought of the maps that had been lies. She thought of the ring on Eben's finger, the green stone catching light that would soon be gone.

"The name," she said quietly. "What did you say your name was?"

"Eben Marsh."

She did not repeat it back to him — she did not need to, the sound of it was already lodged in her memory — but she let the weight of it settle. Marsh. A common name. A name that could belong to a rower, a census clerk, a deserter, a minister. A name that meant nothing, and everything, depending on who spoke it.

"Thank you, Eben," she said.

He inclined his head, a gesture so small it might have been nothing. Then he turned and walked toward the river, lifting the oar from the boat as if he meant to row back, though there was no one left to carry.

Petra watched him until the shadows swallowed him whole, and then she turned toward the inn, her horse standing patient at the post, the satchel heavy against her side. She had given the ring. She had given the name — or at least a name, a piece of herself she had not planned to spend. And she had done it without drama, without the theatrical gestures that novels would later invent to make this moment seem grander than it was. It had been a quiet transaction, conducted on the bank of a river at the end of a long day, and the cost had been measured in gold and identity and the last flicker of the belief that she could carry everything she needed and surrender nothing.

She was wrong about that now. The cost was being counted, and the ledger was filling fast.

* * *

The inn was what Eben had promised: a roof, a hearth, and a corner of the common room that belonged to no one. The keeper — a woman with gray hair cut short and hands red from scrubbing — accepted Petra's payment in coins without question and pointed to a narrow staircase that led to a single room above the stable. The room smelled of hay and woodsmoke and something older, something that lived in the walls: the accumulated scent of every traveler who had passed through this place and left a fragment of themselves behind.

Petra locked the door — a simple iron bolt, nothing more — and set the satchel on the bed. She did not remove the case from it. She sat on the edge of the bed with the satchel in her lap and listened to the inn settle around her: the creak of timber, the muffled voices from the common room, the distant complaint of the river against the bank. Outside, the night was closing in, and the road north was a darkness she could not yet see.

She thought of the deserter at the ferry, the man who had recognized the seal and had not flinched. He had known what the case meant. He had known she was carrying ministry dispatches. And he had priced his silence at a ring and a name — a bargain that was, by any reasonable accounting, too cheap. The thought unsettled her, not because she regretted the trade, but because it suggested a larger transaction underway, one in which the real currency was not silver or gold but information. Someone had told Eben Marsh about the courier. Someone had told him to watch the road. Someone had put a price on her head and dressed it as a ferry fare.

Or perhaps Eben had simply recognized the seal and calculated its worth on the open market. The republic was fracturing, and in the fractures, information was the last currency that held its value.

Petra opened the satchel and lifted the case from its padded lining. She held it in the lamplight, turning it slowly so that the brass corners caught and released the flame. The seal was intact — she had checked it at every relay house, every checkpoint, every moment of vulnerability. The wax was unbroken. The clasps were secure. Inside, the dispatches waited, patient and incriminating, their contents a architecture of lies that the ministry had built to justify its seizure of the Cape Route. She had memorized the route to deliver them. She had not yet decided what to do with them when she arrived.

The lamplight guttered. The room darkened. In the common room below, someone began to sing — a hymn, perhaps, or a drinking song, something stripped of words that rose and fell with the rhythm of the hearth. Petra closed the case, pressed it against her chest, and felt the weight of it against her heartbeat.

Tomorrow she would cross the estuary. Tomorrow she would reach the Vane estate and knock on Sera's door with the letter her promise demanded. But tonight she would sleep, if only for a few hours, and the sleep would be light and fractured, filled with the sound of the river and the memory of a ring sliding off her finger into another woman's palm.

She thought of the road ahead and the people who would be on it — the checkpoint clerks, the census takers, the men like Eben who rowed boats and read seals and priced the secrets of others. She thought of Corven's face at the relay house, flat and impersonal as a mask, when he had handed her the case. She thought of the minister's seal, the red-and-gold bridge, the motto that promised harbor through hardship.

Harbor, she thought, lay behind her now. The road ahead was all bridge.

Outside, the wind picked up, and the inn trembled on its foundations like a ship in a swell. Petra pulled the blanket over her shoulders and lay back on the straw mattress, watching the smoke curl toward the rafters, and waited for the dark to do what it always did: make the world smaller, until the only thing that remained was the sound of her own breathing and the weight of the satchel against her side.

Tomorrow the census would come for her name. She could feel it in the way the air had changed over the past two days — a tightening, a watchfulness, the particular quality of silence that precedes a knock at the door. The ministry was counting, and she was on the list. She had known this since the checkpoint at Brannock, when Ivo Marsh had looked at her too long and asked her too many questions about household size and occupation and the names of those who lived with her. She had lied well — she had always lied well — but she had felt the net tighten even as she slipped through the meshes.

Now the net was closing.

She closed her eyes and let the darkness take her, carrying the satchel against her chest like a child, carrying the dispatches and the ring and the name of a rower on a river who had seen the seal and asked for payment. The cost of carrying secrets, she understood now, was not measured in the danger they attracted but in the things one surrendered to keep them safe. And she had already begun to give things up — her ring, her name, her belief that she could carry everything and lose nothing.

The road ahead would take more. She was ready for that. She had been ready for it since the capital, since the moment she had accepted the case and the route and the silence about where she was going and why. The only thing she had not been ready for was the slow, quiet arithmetic of loss — the way it added up, piece by piece, until the sum was a woman standing in an inn room on the bank of a river, carrying almost nothing but the weight of what she had promised to deliver.

Sleep came at last, thin and fragile, and in its grip she dreamed of a bridge spanning a river, its planks burning, the water below black and fast and indifferent to the fire. She was crossing it. She could not stop. Behind her, the flames consumed the span behind her, and ahead, the far bank was nothing but shadow.

She kept walking.
