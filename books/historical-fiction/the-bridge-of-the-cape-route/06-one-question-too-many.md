## 6. One Question Too Many

The wagon creaked over cobblestones that had gone unpolished for too long, a sound like a dying breath, and Petra felt each jolt in her teeth. She had learned to travel without weight, to measure every possession against the moment she might have to abandon it. The sealed dispatch case sat against her ribs, cold and immutable, a stone of lead wrapped in waterproof canvas. Everything else—the extra pair of boots, the woolen cloak, the notebook she used for drafts—had been sold or left at the last relay. She carried only what could be held in one hand, and even then, she often held nothing at all.

They had reached the census station on the road north of Saltmere just as the sun was folding itself behind the hills, bleeding orange into the underbelly of the world. A long shed of canvas and lattice, stamped with the republic’s seal in faded red, dominated the crossing. Two men stood at a table cluttered with ledgers, quills, and a brass bell that hung like a promise of accountability. One was the official census taker, his posture rigid, his eyes scanning the crowd with the detachment of a man who believed his ledger could hold the shape of a nation. The other was an overzealous clerk, younger, with sleeves rolled up and a habit of humming while he worked.

Petra had prepared a name, a backstory, the careful architecture of a lie she had constructed in the quiet of inn rooms and ferry decks. Her cloak was patched at the shoulder, her boots scuffed but serviceable. She had no papers beyond what the state had issued her at the capital’s departure gate, and those were already beginning to feel like a noose of her own making.

“Name and purpose,” the clerk said, not looking up from his ledger. “And do not think I have not noticed the seal on your case. It is not the common issue of a traveling merchant.”

Petra felt a muscle in her jaw tighten. She had expected scrutiny, but not this soon, not from a man who already seemed to see the shape of the truth before she spoke it. “Petra of the eastern marches,” she said, and felt the name settle in her throat like ash. “I carry dispatches for the ministry’s coastal review. Personal matters. My husband—”

“Your husband is not with you?” The clerk’s head snapped up, and for a moment, the humming stopped. His eyes were the color of old copper, sharp and searching. “The Vane household register is extensive, you know. We have been compiling it for three years, cross-referencing every freeholder, every envoy, every soul who has walked the Route since the tariffs changed. I must ask—”

He stood, the chair scraping the wood, and moved around the table. Petra’s hand tightened on the satchel’s handle. She had rehearsed this moment a hundred times in her mind, but the rehearsal had always been calm, professional. The reality was a live wire.

“—connection to the Vane name,” the clerk continued, his voice losing its humming cadence. “Your accent is not eastern marches. Your hands are those of a woman who has handled swords or quills, not a plow. And the seal—” He reached out, his fingers brushing the canvas, and Petra flinched, not from fear of theft but from the recognition that this was a moment she could not smooth over with a smile or a bribe. “—bears the Vane crest. The vane, the stag, the motto *Through the Cape, Through the Light*. This is not a ministry dispatch. This is Vane.”

The words landed like a stone dropped into a still pond. Petra’s mind raced, frantically searching for a thread she could pull, a lie she could spin. *He knows. He knows, and he is checking the register, and the register will tell him I am a liar, a traitor, a thing that should not exist.*

“Petra,” she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. “I am a courier. The dispatches are mine to carry, and the seal is a formality—”

“A formality?” The clerk’s laugh was short, without humor. He picked up a ledger, the pages already turning with a frantic energy. “You do not understand what you carry, do you? I have seen the registers. I have seen the names. Vane’s household, his holdings, his envoys—all marked. And you, Petra—” He leaned closer, and she could see the faint sheen of sweat on his upper lip, the way his eyes darted to the brass bell. “—you are not on the rolls. But if I query the Vane register now—”

The ledger creaked open, and a hush seemed to fall over the station, though the crowd beyond the canvas continued their murmurs. Petra felt a coldness spread through her chest, a clarity that cut through the panic like a blade. The clerk’s quill hovered over a line, and she watched the nib dip into ink, the way it stained the paper with each letter she could not stop.

*Petra Vane. Petra of the Vane line. Petra, daughter of—*

The name she had chosen, the life she had built since leaving the capital, frayed at the edges. The clerk was not just checking a name; he was unraveling the story she had stitched together from silence and half-truths. Every answer she had given, every detail she had offered, was a knot she had tied in the dark, and now the light was being turned on.

“Stop,” she said, and the word came out as a command, though it felt like a plea. “The dispatches—”

“—are evidence.” The clerk’s voice had shifted, hardening into something final. He picked up the brass bell, and the sound it made when he struck it was a single, sharp note that seemed to vibrate through the very planks of the shed. “Census taker! We have a match. The courier is marked. Send word to the ministry. The Vane dispatch is in motion.”

The census taker, who had been watching with the indifferent gaze of a man counting beans, now straightened. His hand went to the hilt of a sword at his hip. “Petra of the Vane line,” he said, and the name sounded like a verdict. “You are under arrest for conspiracy against the republic. Your case is to be seized as evidence of treason.”

Panic, sharp and blinding, seized Petra’s throat. She had known, in the back of her mind, that the republic was fracturing, that the ministry was desperate, that the edges of the law were becoming teeth. But she had not believed it would come to this. That a single census taker, a single question, could unspool the whole of her carefully knotted existence. She had carried the dispatches across half a continent, burned bridges and discarded friends, and now a man with a ledger and a bell had undone her in a breath.

“No,” she said, and the word was ripped from somewhere deep, from the same place she had promised Aldric Vane she would deliver his daughter’s letter if the world turned sour. “I have to go. I have to—”

“You are not going anywhere,” the census taker said, and he reached for her.

Petra moved then, not with the grace of a dancer but with the desperate urgency of a trapped animal. She had spent years learning to move light, to be the ghost in her own life, and now the ghost had to become solid. She twisted her wrist, slipped the satchel from her arm, and in the same motion, pushed off the table. The clerk’s hand closed on empty air as she ducked beneath his outstretched arm, her shoulder connecting with the edge of the ledger, knocking quills and inkpots to the floor.

The window behind the shed had been left open, a slit of night and distant hills beyond the glass. Petra didn't hesitate. She launched herself toward it, the canvas of the shed brushing her back, the cold air of the outside world rushing in like a promise. She felt the sting of a hand on her cloak as she cleared the frame, but she was already airborne, her boots finding the rough plaster of the outbuilding’s side, her fingers gripping a wooden beam as she swung herself down into the muddy yard beyond.

Behind her, the bell rang again, and shouted orders rose from the station. Men ran, lanterns bobbed, and the world she had been crossing tilted on its axis. She didn't look back. She couldn't. The clarity that had come with the panic was absolute now: the state did not see her as a citizen or a courier. She was a name on a register, a Vane associate, a threat. And the net was already closing.

She ran toward the coast, away from the station, away from the road that had brought her north. Her lungs burned, her side ached with each step, but she kept moving, putting distance between herself and the ledger that now had her name in ink. The dispatches pressed against her ribs, a secret weight she had carried for too long, and now she understood, with a grief that was almost relief, that the republic she had served no longer saw her as one of its own. She was enemy stock, and the pursuit had already begun.

The road ahead wound toward the ferry at Saltmere, but she did not trust it. She had bought silence from a deserter once, with her mother’s ring, and she had no more rings to offer. Her mother’s finger had long been cold, and the ring was now a thing she had already spent, a payment made in peacetime that no longer had value.

Petra ran, her breath ragged, her mind looping the moment the clerk’s quill had touched the paper, the way the name *Vane* had sounded like a tomb closing. She had slipped the window, she had fled, and now the weight of what she carried was not just the dispatches, but the knowledge that she was marked, that every checkpoint, every clerk, every census taker from here to the Cape Route would now be watching for the name that was not hers, that had never been hers, but that the state had decided belonged to the enemy.

She reached the cliffs before dawn, the sea below a black slab of water, and she climbed down the rocky face with hands that shook, her heart hammering against the ribs that held the sealed case. Below her, the tide was out, leaving wet sand and the faint imprint of footsteps that were not hers. She found a path that wound inland, away from the main road, and she kept moving, putting one foot in front of the other until the world narrowed to the rhythm of her own breath and the cold weight of the truth she carried.

The night was young, but she knew she could not stop. The clerk’s bell had already sent word ahead, and the ministry’s machines were already grinding, their gears turning toward a woman who had once been a novelist, a wife, a citizen, and was now, in the eyes of the state, a ghost to be caught. Petra ran, and in her running, she found a strange, terrible peace: the truth was no longer a burden she could set down. It was the only thing she had left, and it was dragging her forward, into the dark, toward the only destination that now mattered.
