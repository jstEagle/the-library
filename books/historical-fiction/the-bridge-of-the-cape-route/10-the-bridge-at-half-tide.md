## 10. The Bridge at Half Tide

The estuary breathed.

Petra had crossed the Cape Route's great bridge a dozen times in her career — winter gales when the cables sang like harp strings, summer calms when the water lay so still it reflected the toll house's white paint perfectly. She knew the rhythm of the tides here: the six-hour swell, the hour of slack, the violent rush of the ebb. She knew which planks groaned under a loaded cart and which held silent beneath a lone traveler's boots.

She had never crossed it at half tide with ministry riders thundering down the coastal road behind her, the dispatch case heavy and wet against her hip, and a woman she had met three days ago matching her stride for stride.

Sera Vane moved like someone who had grown up on ships — weight centered, knees soft, eyes scanning the horizon without turning her head. Her riding habit was torn at the left cuff, mud-spattered to the knee, and a dark bruise was blooming along her jaw where a branch had caught her during their flight through the salt marsh. She carried nothing but the leather satchel Petra had pressed into her hands at the estate's back gate: the private letter, Aldric's seal unbroken, and a handful of silver coins.

*Travel light. Travel fast. The rest is ballast.*

The groundsman, Tomas, had stayed behind at the bridge's western approach. He stood atop the toll house roof now, a spyglass to his eye, the ministry's green pennant snapped down and trampled in the mud beside him. He had served the Vane family for thirty years. His wife lay buried in the little cemetery behind the chapel. His son had died at the Battle of Grey Reach, fighting for a republic that now hunted his master's daughter.

*He stays because he has nothing left to carry,* Petra thought. *We run because we still do.*

The bridge stretched ahead: two hundred yards of timber and iron, suspended over water that churned brown and white where the river met the sea. At half tide the current ran both ways — salt pushing upstream beneath fresh water sliding down — creating a chaos of cross-currents that could capsize a barge in minutes. The bridge's pilings groaned under the pressure. Debris raced past: a shattered crate, a tree trunk stripped of branches, the bloated carcass of a sheep.

"Riders on the road," Sera said. Her voice was steady. She did not look back. "Four. Maybe five. Hard to count through the dust."

Petra glanced over her shoulder. The coastal road curved down from the bluffs a quarter mile west, a pale scar on the dark earth. Dust rose in a thin column. Hoofbeats, faint but distinct, carried on the wind that smelled of brine and wet rot.

"Ministry livery?"

"Green and gold. The faction's colors." Sera's jaw tightened. "Not the regular garrison. The ones Corven warned us about."

*Corven.* The name still tasted like ash. Her handler. Her friend of fifteen years. The man who had signed her travel papers and smiled at her promotion ceremony and then, in a sealed room somewhere in the capital, had marked her file *LIABILITY — RECLASSIFY FOR RECOVERY.*

He had sent the warning, though. The coded message in the relay drop at Saltmere: *NIGHT OF LONG CENSUSES BEGINS DAWN TOMORROW. BRIDGE COMPROMISED. FIND ANOTHER WAY.*

There was no other way. The Cape Route was the only crossing for fifty miles in either direction. The ferries had been seized. The fords were impassable at this season. The bridge stood or fell with the republic's hold on the coast.

And Tomas had chosen to hold it.

"Half tide turns in twenty minutes," Petra said, measuring the water's surface. The current had slowed, the violent chop settling into a slick, oily texture. "Slack water lasts forty minutes. Then the ebb runs hard for three hours."

"Can we cross in twenty?"

"We cross *now*." Petra adjusted the dispatch case's strap, shifting the weight. The oilcloth wrapping was soaked through from the marsh crossing; she could feel the lead seals cold against her thigh through the leather. "The current's still pushing upstream. It'll shove us toward the eastern shore if we lose footing. Better than being swept out to sea."

Sera nodded once. "Then we go."

They moved onto the bridge.

The timber decking was slick with spray and salt. The iron cables, thick as a man's wrist, vanished into the fog that clung to the upriver reaches. No guardrails — the ministry had removed them two winters past after a storm carried away the originals, and replacement funds had been *reallocated*. The toll house on the eastern end stood dark, its windows shuttered, the toll keeper's chair empty.

Halfway across, the wind hit them.

It came upstream off the open ocean, carrying the scent of kelp and distant ice. It caught Petra's coat, tugging at the dispatch case, and she leaned into it instinctively, boots finding the gaps between planks where the grain gave purchase. Beside her, Sera did the same, one hand reaching out to brush Petra's sleeve — a touch light as foam, there and gone.

*Steady. Breathe. Count the planks.*

Petra had counted them once, years ago, on a bet with a fellow courier. Two hundred and forty-seven planks from toll house to toll house. She had won a bottle of southern wine and a story she still told in relay stations: *the bridge remembers every foot that crosses it. The wood knows the weight of empires.*

Plank forty. Plank forty-one. The vibration of hoofbeats traveled through the cables, a low hum in the soles of her feet.

"Tomas," Sera said quietly.

Petra looked up. The groundsman had lowered the spyglass. He stood silhouetted against the pale sky, one hand raised — not waving, not signaling. Holding. A fist clenched around something Petra could not see.

Then he moved. He descended the toll house ladder with the deliberate care of a man who has climbed it a thousand times, and when he reached the ground he did not run. He walked to the bridge's western abutment, where the ministry riders would come, and he stood there with his hands empty at his sides.

"Mother's mercy," Sera breathed.

The riders crested the bluff road.

Five of them. Green and gold pennants snapped on their lances. They rode ministry horses — big, black, bred for the parade grounds of the capital, not the mud of the coast. The lead rider wore a captain's tabs. He raised a hand, and the column slowed to a canter, then a trot, then a walk as they approached the abutment.

Tomas did not move.

Petra felt Sera's hand find her wrist, fingers digging through wool and linen. *Don't stop. Don't look. Keep walking.*

But Petra looked.

The captain dismounted. He was a young man, clean-shaven, his uniform too new for field work. He carried a clipboard rather than a sword. A census taker's clipboard, Petra realized with a jolt of cold recognition. *Ivo Marsh's kind. The counters.*

He spoke to Tomas. Too far to hear words, but the body language was clear: questions asked, answers not given. The captain's hand moved to his sidearm. Two other riders dismounted, spreading out.

Tomas said something. A gesture — one hand sweeping toward the bridge, toward the eastern shore, toward *away*.

The captain laughed. Petra saw it in the set of his shoulders, the tilt of his head. He signaled, and the two riders remounted. They spurred their horses onto the bridge.

The timber shuddered.

"Move," Petra said, and she was moving, pulling Sera with her, the dispatch case thumping a frantic rhythm against her hip.

The riders came fast. Horses on a narrow bridge have no room to maneuver, no space to turn. They are committed the moment their hooves leave solid ground. The first rider's mount slipped on the wet planks, recovered, slipped again. The second rider cursed and drew a pistol.

*They won't shoot. Not at the dispatches. Not at Sera Vane — not yet. They need us alive for the trial.*

But the pistol leveled anyway.

Petra shoved Sera toward the eastern toll house. "Run. *Now.*"

Sera ran.

Petra turned, the dispatch case swinging free as she dropped into a crouch, presenting the smallest target. The rider fired.

The crack split the air. A plank three feet ahead of her exploded in splinters. The horse screamed, rear hooves scrambling, and the rider went down hard — thrown or jumped, Petra couldn't tell. The second rider reined hard, his horse dancing on the slick wood, and the pistol came up again.

*No time. No cover. The toll house door — *

She sprinted.

The eastern toll house loomed: a squat stone building with a heavy oak door and narrow windows shuttered against the weather. Sera stood in the doorway, one hand braced on the frame, the other extended toward Petra. Her face was pale, her breath visible in the cold air, but her eyes were clear.

*She's not running. She's waiting.*

Petra hit the threshold at a stagger, Sera's arm locking around her waist, hauling her inside. The door slammed shut. The bolt threw home with a sound like a coffin lid.

Silence, save for their breathing and the bridge's groan.

Then: hoofbeats retreating. Shouts. The crack of a rifle — not a pistol, a rifle — from the western shore. A horse's scream, cut short.

Sera slid down the door, her back against the oak, legs splayed. "Tomas."

Petra sank beside her, the dispatch case clutched to her chest. "He bought us the crossing. That was the bargain."

"He *died* for it."

"He *chose* it." Petra's voice was harder than she intended. "We all choose. Every day. Every step. He chose to stand there so we could run here. Don't make it waste."

Sera's head snapped toward her. In the dim light from the shutter cracks, her eyes were bright with something — grief, rage, the sharp edge of a wound freshly opened.

"You speak like it's arithmetic," she said. "Like lives are numbers in a ledger."

"Sometimes they are." Petra leaned her head back against the door, feeling the vibration of the bridge through the stone. "Ask the census takers. Ask the ministry. Ask the men who signed the orders that sent my handler to his death and me to this bridge. They count. We *are* the count."

"And you?" Sera's voice dropped. "What do you count, Petra Eastwick? What's in that case that's worth Tomas's life? Worth mine? Worth yours?"

Petra's fingers found the lead seals through the wet oilcloth. Three seals. Ministry wax, black with the republic's eagle. The dispatches that proved the treason charge against Aldric Vane was manufactured. The evidence that the ministry's faction had seized the Cape Route's tolls by fraud and force.

*Truth,* she thought. *The truth is in this case. Heavy as lead. Heavy as a body.*

"Dispatches," she said aloud. "Official business. The kind that topples governments."

Sera was silent for a long moment. Then: "My father's letter. The private one. You still have it?"

"In my pack. Dry. Safe."

"Give it to me."

Petra hesitated. The pack was soaked through from the marsh, the leather stiffening as it dried on her back. She unbuckled it, fingers fumbling with the catch, and drew out the slim envelope. Cream paper. Aldric's seal: a silver fox rampant on blue wax. Unbroken.

She held it out.

Sera took it with both hands. She did not open it. She turned it over, ran a thumb over the seal, pressed it against her forehead.

"He wrote this," she said, "the night before he left the capital. The night the flags came down. He told me — he told me he was coming home. That the posting was ending. That we would walk the quay together again and eat those terrible fried dough things from the vendor with the blue cart."

Her voice cracked. "He lied. He knew he was lying. And he wrote this letter anyway."

Petra said nothing. There was nothing to say.

The hoofbeats had faded. The bridge was quiet again, save for the water's rush and the wind in the cables. Somewhere upstream, a bell buoy clanged — a slow, mournful sound.

"We should move," Petra said. "The ebb tide turns soon. The current will run hard against the eastern shore. If they bring boats — "

"They won't bring boats." Sera's voice had changed. The grief was still there, but something else had surfaced beneath it: calculation. The daughter of a diplomat, Petra reminded herself. The woman who had negotiated trade treaties at sixteen and faced down a ministry clerk with a loaded ledger at twenty. "The ministry doesn't have boats on this stretch. They seized the ferry fleet but the crews mutinied. The garrison at Saltmere burned their own launches rather than hand them over."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I read the intelligence reports my father sent home. Because I listened when the groundsman's son wrote from the front. Because I *pay attention*." She stood, the letter tucked into her bodice, the satchel resettled on her hip. "The ministry has horses and riders and census takers with clipboards. They don't have boats. They don't have men who know these waters."

Petra rose more slowly, testing her knees. "Then we walk. The coastal road east runs to the border. Forty miles. Three days on foot if we avoid the main towns."

"Three days." Sera's mouth twisted. "Three days of sleeping in ditches and eating raw turnips and jumping at every shadow. Three days of *running*."

"Until the dispatches reach the right hands."

"And whose hands are those?" Sera's eyes were sharp. "You never said. Corven's warning said 'find another way' — but you brought us *to* the bridge. You *chose* the bridge. Why?"

Petra met her gaze. The dispatch case felt heavier. The oilcloth was drying stiff, cracking at the folds. *Soon the seals will show. Soon the water damage will be visible. Soon someone will ask what I carry and I will have to decide what to tell them.*

"Marsh," she said. "Ivo Marsh. The census taker."

Sera went still. "The one who questioned you at the checkpoint. The one who let you go."

"He didn't let me go. He *followed*." Petra's voice was flat. "He's been tracking us since Saltmere. Two days behind, maybe three. He has his own ledger. Names the ministry erased. People who disappeared. He wants the dispatches for what they prove — not for duty. For *truth*."

"And you trust him?"

"I trust his ledger. I trust that he's been counting the disappeared longer than I've been carrying this case." Petra adjusted the strap, wincing as the wet leather chafed her neck. "He'll catch us before the border. He wants what we have. The question is what he'll do when he gets it."

Sera was silent. The wind rattled the shutters. Somewhere in the toll house, a mouse skittered behind the wainscoting.

"Then we don't go to the border," she said finally. "We go to the toll house at the midpoint. The old one, on the island. The ministry abandoned it when the new bridge was built."

"Marsh Island. It's a ruin. The roof collapsed in the '98 storm."

"The cellar's intact. And the signal tower." Sera's eyes gleamed in the dimness. "My father built it. A private line to the coast watch stations. If the equipment survives — if the cables weren't cut — we can send the dispatches' contents to every port on the Route before midnight."

Petra stared at her. *A signal tower. Private cables. A diplomat's daughter who knows the infrastructure of a route she supposedly never visited.*

"You're full of surprises, Sera Vane."

"And you, Petra Eastwick, are full of secrets. We make a fine pair." She extended a hand. "Partners, then? Until the dispatches are sent. Until the truth is out."

Petra looked at the hand. Pale, fine-boned, the nails bitten short. A diplomat's hand. A woman's hand who had never done manual labor — until three days ago, when she had waded through a salt marsh and climbed a bridge in half tide and watched a man die for her passage.

*Flight becoming partnership. Trust built in motion.*

She took the hand. The grip was firm, callused now at the palm's heel from the marsh grass and the bridge cables.

"Partners," Petra said. "Until the truth is out."

* * *

They left the toll house through a rear window, dropping into a skiff beached in the mud flats below the eastern abutment. Tomas had hidden it there weeks ago, Petra realized — part of a plan he had made and kept silent, a plan for a crossing he would not live to make.

The skiff was a ugly thing: flat-bottomed, tar-blackened, patched in a dozen places with copper sheets and pine tar. But it floated, and it had oars, and the current at half tide's turn would carry them upstream to the island without fighting the main channel.

Petra shoved off. The dispatch case went in the bow, wrapped in her oilcloth coat. Sera took the stern oar, Petra the bow. They pulled in silence, the rhythm finding itself without words: *dip, pull, recover, dip.*

The bridge receded behind them, a dark spine against the water. The western toll house was a smudge of stone. No riders visible. No sign of Tomas.

*He's gone. The bridge is lost. The ministry will hold it by nightfall.*

The island rose from the mist: a hump of rock and scrub grass, crowned by the ruined toll house and the signal tower's skeletal mast. The old toll house had been built on pilings driven into bedrock; the storm had taken the upper floors but the cellar, Petra knew, was a stone vault set into the island's core. The signal tower — a ministry experiment in rapid communication, abandoned when the cables corroded — stood separate, its base a concrete blockhouse with a hand-crank generator and a bank of brass keys.

*If the cables weren't cut. If the generator turns. If the keys haven't seized.*

A lot of *ifs*. But Sera moved with the certainty of someone who had seen the plans, who had memorized the schematics her father had smuggled out in diplomatic pouches.

They beached the skiff in a cleft of rock and climbed the path to the tower. The door was rusted shut; Sera kicked it open with a heel, the metal shrieking on its hinges.

Inside: dust thick as snow, the smell of bat guano and old oil. The generator stood in the center, a heavy brass flywheel and copper windings. The key bank lined the far wall: twenty keys, each labeled with a station name in faded ink. *Saltmere. Grey Reach. Vane's Landing. Cape Head. Border Post Seven.*

Petra ran a finger over the labels. "The cables run underwater. Buried in the mud. The ministry wouldn't have cut them — they'd have just stopped maintaining them."

"Salt water eats copper," Sera said. She was already at the generator, turning the flywheel by hand. It resisted, groaned, then spun free with a sound like a dying bird. "But the insulation is gutta-percha. It lasts fifty years in mud. My father specified it."

The flywheel spun. A needle on a brass gauge trembled, then rose. *Current flowing. The circuit lives.*

Sera moved to the key bank. Her fingers hovered over the keys, then settled on *ALL STATIONS — EMERGENCY BROADCAST.* A red key, larger than the others, with a brass guard to prevent accidental use.

"Wait," Petra said. She set the dispatch case on the dusty workbench, broke the seals with her thumbnail. The oilcloth fell away. The inner envelope was watertight — a ministry innovation, waxed linen with a rubberized seal. She cracked it open.

Three documents. Official ministry stationery, folded in thirds.

*Document one:* A memorandum from the Minister of Internal Security to the Cape Route Toll Authority, authorizing the seizure of Vane family assets "pending investigation of treasonous correspondence with foreign powers." Dated three weeks before the treason charge was publicly announced.

*Document two:* A ledger page showing toll revenue diverted to a private account — Minister Corven's personal account, Petra realized with a sick lurch. *Corven. My handler. The man who sent the warning.*

*Document three:* A ciphered message, partially decoded in a different hand. *...Vane's daughter is the leverage. Ensure she cannot reach the border. The dispatches must not reach Marsh. Burn the bridge if necessary.*

Petra's blood went cold. *Burn the bridge. They'd burn the bridge — the Cape Route, the lifeline of the coast — rather than let the truth cross it.*

"Petra?" Sera's voice from the key bank. "What does it say?"

Petra read the ciphered message again. The handwriting was familiar. Cramped, precise, the *e* always tilted backward.

*Corven's hand.*

He had written the order to burn the bridge. He had signed the ledger diverting tolls. He had authored the memorandum that started it all.

*And he sent the warning. He told us to run. He told us the bridge was compromised.*

Why?

"Petra!"

She looked up. Sera stood at the key bank, the red key depressed halfway, her face pale in the generator's flickering glow.

"The dispatches," Sera said quietly. "They implicate Corven. Your handler. Your friend."

"Yes."

"And you still want to send them."

"I *have* to send them. They're the truth. The only truth that matters."

"But Corven — he warned you. He saved us. If you send this, you destroy him."

Petra stood very still. The generator hummed, a low vibration in the floorboards. The bridge's cables sang in the wind outside, a deep harmonic resonance that traveled through the island's bedrock.

*Corven. Fifteen years. The relay at Saltmere. The promotion ceremony. The bottle of southern wine he'd given her when she made senior courier — the same vintage she'd won counting bridge planks. The coded messages in the margins of official reports: *STAY SAFE. TRUST NO ONE. THE MINISTRY EATS ITS OWN.*

He had known. He had always known. And he had stayed, and counted, and waited — until the moment came to send one final warning, and then to vanish.

*He didn't save us to save himself. He saved us because it was the only thing left he could do.*

"Send it," Petra said. "All of it. Every station. Every port. Let the ministry choke on its own ledger."

Sera held her gaze for a long moment. Then she slammed the red key home.

The machine screamed.

A sound like tearing metal, like a thousand wires singing at once. The generator's flywheel spun violently, the needle pegging hard against the stop. The key bank's brass contacts flashed — once, twice, a staccato rhythm of spark and ozone.

*Dot dash dot. Dash dot dash. The ministry's own cipher, stolen and turned against them.*

Sera's fingers flew across the keys. She didn't read the dispatches — she *transmitted* them, raw and unfiltered, the memorandum and the ledger and the ciphered order and the decoding key itself. *ALL STATIONS. EMERGENCY BROADCAST. MINISTRY FRAUD. TOLL SEIZURE. VANE INNOCENT. CORVEN COMPLICIT. BRIDGE COMPROMISED. TRUTH FOLLOWS.*

The machine hammered for three minutes. Three minutes that stretched into years.

When it stopped, the silence was physical — a pressure on the eardrums, a weight on the chest. The generator's flywheel spun down with a dying whir. The needle fell to zero.

Sera sagged against the key bank, her shoulders shaking. Not crying — Petra knew the difference. This was the collapse after the sprint, the body remembering what the mind had driven it through.

"Done," Sera whispered. "It's done. Every port. Every ship at sea with a receiver. The coast watch. The border posts. The foreign consulates in the capital." She looked up, face streaked with dust and sweat. "They know. *Everyone* knows."

Petra exhaled. A breath she felt she had been holding since the capital. Since the sealed case was placed in her hands. Since the promise made in peacetime.

"Then we run," she said. "For real this time. Not toward something — *away*."

Sera pushed off the key bank, straightening. "Where?"

"South. The old smugglers' path through the dunes. There's a fishing village at the cape's tip — neutral ground, technically. If we can reach it before the ministry reorganizes — "

A sound cut through the generator's silence.

Footsteps. Heavy, deliberate, on the path outside. Crunching on the gravel approach.

Petra's hand went to her belt — no pistol, never a pistol, couriers didn't carry weapons — and found only the dispatch case's strap.

The tower door swung open.

A man stood there. Tall, broad-shouldered, wearing a census taker's coat — gray wool, brass buttons, the ministry's eagle tarnished on the collar. He carried a clipboard under one arm and a lantern in the other. The lantern's light caught a face weathered by wind and salt, eyes the color of the estuary at half tide: neither blue nor brown nor green, but *water*.

He looked at Petra. At Sera. At the dispatch case on the workbench, the broken seals, the transmitted key still depressed.

He did not reach for a weapon. He did not shout. He set the lantern on the floor and opened his clipboard.

"Ivo Marsh," he said. His voice was quiet, carrying the weight of tides. "Census Division, Ministry of Internal Security. I have some questions for the record."
