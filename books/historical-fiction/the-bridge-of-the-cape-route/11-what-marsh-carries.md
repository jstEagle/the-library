## 11. What Marsh Carries

The rain found him three miles north of the salt pans, where the road turned inland and forgot the sea for a stretch of brown hills and standing water. Ivo Marsh had walked since the second bell, his census satchel slapping his thigh with every step, and he had not stopped, because stopping meant thinking, and thinking meant the small arithmetic his ledger demanded.

He had been counting people his entire working life. He had counted the living in upland hamlets where the roofs were thatch and the children ran barefoot into his columns. He had counted the dead in the parish books when the fever years came, a slow tally of crosses that he made with a steady hand, because a man who wobbled at the ledger was no man at all. He had been raised to believe that a count was a kind of prayer: an assertion that a soul had passed through the world and left its shape, like a footprint in wet clay.

He was thirty-one years old and he had begun, without intending to, to count the disappeared.

It had started small. A widow in a border village, name in the book, name struck through in red ink two weeks later, no explanation given. He had asked his clerk; the clerk had looked at him with the fish-eyed blankness of a man being asked to swallow something sharp. He had asked the relay officer in the town below; the relay officer had said the word *rectification* and then excused himself to check on a horse. Marsh had stood in the road and understood that there were two censuses being kept in the republic now: the one he wrote, in soft lead, and the one the ministry kept, in acid.

He had begun to copy the names down in a second ledger, hidden beneath the floorboard of his lodgings. He told himself it was a habit of diligence, the same impulse that made him check his inkwell at night. Then he had copied the third name, and the fourth, and the forty-first, and somewhere in the forty-first the habit had cracked open and what had poured out was something closer to grief.

He was not, he told himself, a man who grieved easily. He grieved the way he counted: by the column, in even figures, with his sleeves rolled up.

Now he walked north with the name Eastwick on his tongue like a pebble. He had asked her one question too many at the checkpoint, and he had seen the way her shoulders had moved when she lied, the small catch in the breath before the word *official* came out, and he had known, as he had known in the fever villages, that a lie in a healthy throat was shaped differently from a lie in a sick one. Hers was the lie of a woman in very good health who had practiced the lie until it ran like water.

He had let her go. That was the part he could not get right in his head. He had let her go, and then he had written her name in his ledger, and then he had written it again in the other ledger, beneath the floorboard, in a column he headed *the carried*.

It was almost dusk when he saw her.

She was sitting on a stone wall at the edge of a village that the new census had not yet reached. The dispatch case was between her feet, her hand resting on it lightly, the way a woman rests her hand on the head of a dog she does not entirely trust. She was watching the road.

She saw him before he spoke. He watched her body make its small decisions: the straightening of the spine, the breath drawn in, the weight shifted onto the balls of the feet as if to rise. He raised his hands.

"I am not here to arrest you," he said.

It was a stupid thing to say. He had been sent to arrest her. He had the warrant in his satchel, folded thrice, with the seal of the ministry's census office pressed into red wax. He had walked twelve miles to deliver it. He said it anyway, because it was true in the part of him that mattered, and he had learned, since the forty-first name, that the part of him that mattered was not the part that got paid.

She did not rise. She did not relax. She looked at him the way a woman looks at a man she has already decided is lying, and she said, "Then why are you here?"

"To be counted wrong," he said, and he did not know, until he said it, that it was the right answer.

* * *

He sat down on the wall beside her, but at the far end, where he would have to turn his whole body to reach her. He set his census satchel between them, the official ledger on top, the leather dark with road sweat. She watched his hands the way he had watched hers.

"Ivo Marsh," he said. "Census taker. You know that."

"You ask too many questions," she said.

"I have been told."

A cart passed on the road, the driver half asleep, the wheels throwing up a thin fan of water. They both watched it go. The rain came on harder. He took off his cap and held it over his knee; the water ran off the brim in a clear stream.

"The woman at the inn at Drovers' Bend," he said. "You left before the clerk could finish the cross-reference. The clerk was not very bright, but he was not so dull that he did not notice."

"I am not discussing my movements with you."

"No," he said. "You are not. But I am discussing mine."

He reached into his satchel and took out the second ledger. It was smaller than the official one, bound in calfskin rather than the ministry's standard marbled boards. The spine was cracked; he had opened it often. He set it on the wall between them, not offering it, not withdrawing it, and he let her look at it without speaking.

After a long moment she reached for it. He did not stop her. She opened it at random, and the page she opened was the page with the forty-first name, *Pell, W., wife of, border village, struck 14th of Sixth*, and beneath it the forty-second, and the forty-third, and on, in his careful hand, the long column of the erased.

She turned the pages slowly. He watched her eyes. They were the eyes of a woman who had been reading dispatches for a long time; they knew how to take in a page without lingering on any one line, the way a courier reads, the way a courier must. He could see her counting the names as she went. He could see the small flinch she gave at the child's name in the eighty-third row.

"How many," she said, not looking up.

"Two hundred and eleven," he said. "Last count."

"Last count," she repeated, and there was something in her voice that he recognized, because he heard it in his own when he said the words to himself. It was the sound of a man who knows the next count will be worse.

She closed the ledger and put it on the wall between them, exactly where he had set it.

"You carried this on the road with you," she said.

"I did."

"In the same satchel as the warrant."

"Yes."

She looked at him then, fully, and he felt the weight of it. He had been looked at by angry clerks and frightened villagers and a minister or two, and none of those looks had been like this one. She was not measuring him. She was weighing him, and finding him either honest or dangerous, and not knowing yet which was which, and not inclined to be wrong.

"Why?" she said.

"Because the ministry uses my census to find people," he said. "The census is a net. The net is drawn, and the fish are taken out, and the column is corrected, and I am required to come back the next season and ask the same questions of the people who are left. I have been required to do this for three years. I have done it. I have also, in a ledger I do not show to the ministry, written down who was taken, and when, and on what pretext, so that there is, somewhere in the republic, a record that does not lie."

"You are a census taker who keeps two censuses."

"Yes."

"And you expect me to believe that you are not, yourself, a kind of net."

He considered this. The rain was running down the back of his neck. His boots were soaked through; he could feel the leather give a little with each breath.

"I expect nothing," he said. "I am showing you what I carry."

She looked down at the dispatch case between her feet. He looked at it too. The seal on the case was the great seal of the republic's foreign office, and even from where he sat he could see the faint wear along the hinge where her hand had rested, the same place his hand rested on a ledger.

"What is in the case," he said.

"You do not ask that question of a courier."

"I am not asking it of the courier. I am asking it of the woman who is sitting on a wall in the rain with a warrant in my satchel and nowhere to go for the night."

* * *

She did not answer him. Instead she stood, slowly, the way a woman stands when she has been sitting a long time and the cold has gotten into her hips, and she picked up the case and walked three paces away from him and stopped, with her back to him, and looked out at the brown hills where the rain was beginning to lift.

He did not follow her. He sat on the wall and let her have the three paces.

"I am to deliver the dispatches," she said, to the hills, "or to die with them. That is the instruction."

"I am not going to kill you."

"I know," she said, and the *know* was weary in a way that made him think of the widow in the border village, the one whose name had been struck through in red. "I knew it when you sat down. That is the worst of it. If you were simply a murderer I could behave correctly. You are something else."

"I am a man who counts," he said, "and who has begun to suspect that what I am counting is not people but the spaces where people used to be."

She turned at that. She looked at him, and her face was the face of a woman who has heard, for the first time in a long while, someone say aloud the thing she has been carrying in the part of her that does not move.

He opened the ledger again, this time at the back, where the columns grew wider and the ink was fresher. He turned to a page marked with a small *x* in the margin.

"This is the warrant for your arrest," he said. "I received it at the relay town yesterday. The warrant names you as a courier in possession of documents prejudicial to the security of the republic, and it instructs me to take you and the case into custody and to deliver both to the ministry's sub-office at the bridgehead by the third evening bell."

"And yet you sit here."

"And yet I sit here."

She came back to the wall. She did not sit. She stood in front of him, the case against her hip, and looked down at the open ledger, at the names, at the careful columns, at the small *x* in the margin that marked the page where her own erasure would have begun.

"Show me," she said.

He turned the pages back to the beginning. She read the first entry, and the second, and the fifth. He watched her read. She read the way she had looked at him: not lingering, not skipping, taking the shape of the thing. At the forty-first she stopped.

"Pell, W.," she said. "Wife of. Border village."

"Yes."

"I knew her."

He looked up. She was very still.

"She sold fish at the quay when I was a girl," Petra said. "She had a son who worked the ferries. She used to give me a sprat when I came down to watch the boats."

He did not say anything. There was nothing to say. The name in the ledger was a name like any other name, and it was also a woman who had given a child a sprat, and there was no way to hold both of those things in the same hand.

"They said she was a sympathizer," Petra said. "Of the Vane faction."

"They say that," he said. "They say it often."

"And the son."

"The son is in the next column."

She looked. *Pell, J., ferryman, son of, struck 17th of Sixth.* She closed her eyes for a moment, very briefly, and then opened them, and the eyes were the eyes again, the courier's eyes, the woman's eyes, the eyes that had learned to carry.

"What do you want from me, Mr. Marsh?"

He took a breath. The rain had stopped. A thin sun was breaking through the cloud to the west, and the hills were turning the color of wet brass.

"I want to know what is in the case," he said. "Not for the ministry. Not for the warrant. I want to know because I have two hundred and eleven names in this ledger, and they were all erased because of something, and I have been walking the republic for three years writing down the something without ever seeing its face. I want to see its face. I want to know whether the something is worth two hundred and eleven names."

"And if it is not?"

"Then I will arrest you," he said. "I will deliver you and the case to the bridgehead, and I will go back to my villages, and I will continue to keep my second ledger until someone takes it from me, as they will, eventually."

"And if it is."

"Then I will help you."

"You," she said, "are a census taker."

"Yes."

"With a warrant."

"Yes."

"And no weapon."

"I have a pencil," he said.

She looked at him for a long time. The sun was lower now, and the wet wall was beginning to steam faintly in the thin light. Somewhere down the road a dog was barking, and a woman was calling it in, and the evening was coming on, and the two of them sat on a wall in the middle of a republic that was coming apart, and he could feel the weight of what he was carrying shift in his satchel, the way a weight shifts in a boat when a man steps from one side to the other.

"Open it," she said.

He blinked.

"The case," she said. "You want to see its face. Open it."

He looked at the case. He looked at her. He reached out, slowly, the way he had been trained to reach for documents, with both hands, with the flat of the fingers, the way you handle something that has been sealed. He laid his hand on the lid.

"Not here," she said.

He stopped.

"Not here," she said again. "The road has ears, and the trees have eyes, and I have walked enough roads to know when the air is listening. There is a place. A salt cellar at the coast. A friend of a friend. We go there at nightfall, and we open the case together, and you show me your ledger, and I show you mine, and we see what two sets of carried things make when they are set down side by side."

He took his hand off the case. He sat back. He looked at her, and she looked at him, and between them on the wall lay his small calfskin ledger with its two hundred and eleven names, and her sealed case with its great red seal, and the rain had stopped, and the evening was coming on, and somewhere down the road the dog had stopped barking.

"The salt cellar," he said.

"Ay," she said. "The salt cellar. And Mr. Marsh."

"Yes."

"If you are lying to me, the case will not save you. The case is a piece of leather. I am not."

"I am not lying," he said.

"No," she said. "I believe you are not. That is why I am afraid."

She picked up the case. He picked up the satchel. They stood, and they began to walk down the road toward the coast, two people carrying their separate burdens, not yet one burden, not yet two, but something in between, and the road was long, and the evening was coming on, and the republic was coming apart, and a man who had been a net was learning to be a man, and a woman who had been a courier was learning to be a woman with a witness, and the weight of what they carried was not lighter, but it was, for the first time in either of their lives, shared.
