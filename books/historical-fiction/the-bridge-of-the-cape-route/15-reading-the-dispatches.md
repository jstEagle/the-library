## 15. Reading the Dispatches

The dispatch case had traveled with Petra across three counties, over two mountain passes and a ferry that had listed dangerously in a rising tide, and now it sat on a scarred oak table in a room that smelled of woodsmoke and rain‑soaked wool. The seals were of twisted lead stamped with the republic’s double‑headed eagle, three of them, each one a promise of state security. They had been unbroken since the day she accepted them from Handler Corven’s trembling hands, since the day she was told they contained the final accounting of Vane’s alleged treason. Now, in a room whose windows were shuttered against the census takers’ lanterns prowling the streets below, Petra lifted the first seal.

It crumbled under her thumb, the lead falling away like dried skin. The second gave way with a soft metallic sigh. The third she snapped with a flick of her wrist, and the lid lifted.

Inside, wrapped in oiled parchment, were not the manifestos of war or lists of troop movements she had half‑expected. They were thinner than she remembered, the paper yellowed at the edges, the ink a deep, faded blue that had once been black. Petra unfolded the first sheet, her fingers pausing over the script she had memorized from briefings but never truly seen. The dispatch was dated seventeen months past, signed by Minister of Internal Affairs one Alistair Voss, and countersigned by a clerk from the Bureau of Coastal Surveys.

*“Charge: Treason against the Republic. Evidence: Unauthorized correspondence with foreign envoy Aldric Vane. Action: Seizure of Vane holdings along the Cape Route. Toll redistribution to Crown’s End garrison.”*

Petra read aloud, her voice thin in the quiet room. Sera Vane stood by the window, her silhouette sharp against the glass, the salt spray of the distant sea audible below. Her arms were crossed, knuckles white, but her eyes were fixed on the paper as if she feared it might dissolve if she blinked too long.

*“The treason charge,”* Petra said, the words landing like stones in still water, *“was manufactured. The correspondence was a diplomatic courtesy. The ‘unauthorized’ signatures were forged. The toll redistribution was already approved in secret session three weeks before Vane’s arrest.”*

Sera turned from the window, her face pale in the lantern’s glow. *“My father was a fool. I knew it. But not… not this. He wasn’t a traitor. He was a man who knew too much about how the tolls were being siphoned off, and they made him the face of the crime so the public would never look closer.”*

Petra felt a hollow space open in her chest, a cavity where the professional calm she had cultivated for years used to live. She had carried this case across half a republic, guarded it through checkpoints and census stations and the ever‑watching eyes of men like Ivo Marsh, and for what? To deliver a lie? The weight of the satchel, which had felt like a burden of lead and iron, now seemed a ridiculous thing. The truth was heavier. It sat in her bones, cold and sharp, a thing she could not set down.

She turned the pages. There were more dispatches, each one a thread in the same tapestry of deception. A memo from the Ministry of Finance authorizing the rerouting of Cape Route tolls into the ministry’s own private coffers. A census‑taker’s report, altered to show Vane’s household as harboring “subversive elements,” the signatures appended in a hand that was not the clerk’s own. A letter from a ship’s captain describing the ministry’s orders to “neutralize” the route’s independent operators, to clear the way for the republic’s own fleet. And at the center of it all, the frame‑up: a forged signature of Vane on a document calling for the republic’s overthrow, a document that had never existed until the ministry needed it.

*“They didn’t just kill him,”* Petra said, the realization crystallizing with the force of a physical blow. *“They killed him to steal the route. To steal the tolls. And they used my hands to do it. They used me to carry the evidence of his treason, and when I was no longer useful, they’d have branded me a traitor too.”*

Sera’s hands came up to her throat, a gesture old and instinctive. *“I had the ring. The one he gave me the night of the harbor festival. I thought it was just… sentiment. A promise. I never imagined it would become a chain.”*

Petra looked at her, really looked, and saw the grief that had been coiled beneath Sera’s silence, the quiet anger that had kept her from fleeing when the ministry’s men came for the Vane estate. The promise Petra had made in peacetime, at a festival where banners snapped in the salt wind and laughter spilled from taverns, now rang with a terrible clarity. She had promised to deliver a private letter to Sera’s hands if the republic ever turned on Aldric. She had not imagined the turning would be this thorough, this hungry.

*“The dispatches,”* Petra said, “prove the ministry manufactured the charge. Every seal, every signature, every witness statement used to justify the seizure—it’s all fabricated. The Cape Route’s tolls were the motive. Vane was the obstacle.”*

She closed the final sheet and laid it atop the others. The lead seals lay scattered on the table, their eagles tarnished, meaningless now. The case itself felt lighter, the wood warm beneath her palms, but the air in the room had grown thick, suffocating. The truth was a thing that demanded to be spoken, to be seen, to be hurled into the faces of those who had built their power on lies. And yet, to speak it was to invite the same fate as Vane. To publish was to damn Sera’s name, her family’s legacy, to whatever purgatory the republic reserved for traitors’ kin.

*“We can’t leave them here.”* Petra’s voice was steady, but the edges frayed. *“If the ministry finds these, they’ll burn this house, kill us both, and rewrite history so fast we’ll be ghosts in our own story. We have to move. Now.”*

Sera nodded, the motion jerky, as if her body were catching up to a thought that had already outrun her. *“Where? The republic is collapsing. The coast is being sealed off. The census takers are fanning out, door to door, counting and confiscating. If we’re seen with these…”* She gestured at the dispatches, “…we’re dead.”*

*“Then we go to the Cape Route itself.”* Petra stood, pulling her cloak from the back of a chair. *“Not to the ministers. To the edge. To the bridge. If the tolls are the lie, we stand at the bridge and make the truth the only thing that passes through.”*

She moved to the window, pulling back the shutter just enough to see the street below. Lanterns bobbed, casting long shadows against the cobblestones. Men in the grey-green of the ministry’s guard were already making their way up the avenue, staves in hand, their faces obscured by the brims of their caps. A census taker’s satchel—dark leather, the brass census symbol embossed on the front—was slung over one man’s shoulder. He was looking up, scanning the houses on the left side of the street, his eyes calculating the number of windows, the hearth smoke, the lives contained within each unit.

*“They’re already here.”* Petra lowered the shutter. *“We don’t have an hour. Maybe not even a half‑hour.”*

She crossed the room and pulled a small leather satchel from under the table, the one she had used for her own writing tools, her notes on the novel she had been drafting in between dispatches. Into it she placed the sealed dispatches, folding them carefully, tucking them against her chest. She took Sera’s mother’s ring from her finger—the gold band etched with a small ship, the Vane sigil—and pressed it into Petra’s palm.

*“Take it,”* Sera said. *“If they stop you, if they ask what’s in the case, you tell them it’s your father’s letters. Your own story. Not… not this.”*

*“I can’t lie to a census taker.”* Petra’s voice wavered, just for a breath. *“They ask one question, and I’ve spent twenty years learning how to answer wrong.”*

*“Then answer truthfully.”* Sera’s eyes were fierce, wet. *“But not about this. About anything else. The truth is a shield now, Petra. Use it.”*

The door below creaked. Footsteps on the stairs. Petra’s hand went to the dispatch case, her knuckles white. Sera moved to the hearth, pulling a blanket from the mantle, wrapping it around her shoulders as if the cold of the night had already begun to seep through the walls.

*“Who’s there?”* Petra called out, her voice level despite the hammer of her heart.

A man’s voice, rough and unamused, answered from the lower landing. *“Census taker. Name and purpose.”*

Petra’s mind raced. She had memorized the lies she could tell, the half‑truths that might pass muster. But the weight of the dispatches, the truth they carried, pressed against her ribs like a second heart. She thought of Ivo Marsh, the census taker who had stopped her on the road, who had asked after her household, her family, her intentions. She thought of his ledger, the pages of the disappeared he had shown her, the quiet doubt that had flickered in his eyes. She thought of the ministry’s men, the ones who wanted the case burned, the truth buried.

*“I’m Petra Eastwick,”* she said, stepping toward the door, the dispatch case hidden against her hip. *“I’m traveling the Cape Route. I have dispatches for the minister’s office in the capital.”*

The stairs creaked again. A second voice, higher, more urgent. *“She’s the one. The courier. Minister’s orders, she’s a liability.”*

Petra felt Sera’s hand grip her arm, tight and grounding. *“They know,”* Sera whispered. *“They already know.”*

The door began to open. Petra’s breath came sharp, short. She looked at Sera, at the ring on her palm, at the dispatches tucked against her heart. She thought of the bridge she had crossed, the one she had promised to burn, the one she was about to burn for real. She thought of the truth that was heavier than any satchel, and of the only way forward: through it, together.

*“Minister’s orders,”* Petra said, and she opened the door just enough to see the face of the man standing there, the census taker’s satchel slung over his chest, the brass emblem catching the lantern light. *“But the minister is dead. The republic is dying. And I’m carrying something now that belongs to no ministry.”*

The man’s eyes narrowed, the lantern light catching the edge of his jaw. He took a step forward, his hand moving toward the satchel’s clasp. *“We’ll be the judge of that. Name’s Marsh. Ivo Marsh. Census. And you’re coming with us.”*

Petra’s mind flashed to Marsh’s ledger, the pages of the erased, the quiet humanity beneath the bureaucracy. She thought of the truth she carried, and of the promise she had made, and of the bridge that was already burning behind her.

*“Mr. Marsh,”* she said, her voice steady despite the hand that trembled at her side. *“I have something for you. But it’s not what you think. And it’s not for the minister.”*

The door opened fully. Marsh stepped inside, his gaze sweeping the room, the dispatches, the ring, the fear and resolve etched on Sera’s face. He said nothing for a long moment, his eyes flicking from Petra to the scattered lead seals on the table, to the oiled parchment in Petra’s satchel. Then he reached into his own pocket and pulled out a small, worn notebook—the ledger she had seen him glance at on the road, the one that named the disappeared, the censused, the erased.

*“I know what these are,”* Marsh said, his voice soft, almost tender. *“I’ve been counting lives for ten years. I’ve been erasing them for five. This… this isn’t about tolls. This is about why.”*

He set the notebook on the table, open to a page filled with names, dates, and the briefest of annotations: *Vane, Aldric – framed. Toll Route – seized. Dispatches – manufactured.* Below that, in a different hand, smaller, shakier: *I helped her once. At the checkpoint. I lied for her. I didn’t know why.* 

Petra felt the weight of the moment shift, the air in the room changing direction. Marsh was not the enemy she had been led to believe. He was another carrier, another soul trapped in the same machinery, and now, with the truth in her hands, they were partners in a cause larger than survival.

*“Read them,”* Petra said, sliding the satchel toward him. *“All of them. And then tell me what we do next.”*

Marsh’s fingers traced the lines of the notebook, his eyes scanning the names, the dates, the quiet testimony of a life spent counting and erasing. He looked up, and for the first time since Petra had known him, there was no calculation in his gaze, no bureaucratic distance. There was only the weight of what he had seen, and the terrible knowledge that the truth he had been counting toward was the very thing the ministry had tried to bury.

*“The ministry,”* Marsh said slowly, “manufactured the treason charge. Used you as the courier. Used me as the counter. And for what? To steal the Cape Route’s tolls. To line their own pockets while the republic rotted from the inside.”*

He closed the notebook and looked at Petra, really looked, and she saw the cracks in the man she had thought was a hunter. He was a fellow traveler, a man who had been counting the wrong things for too long, and now, finally, he had something worth counting toward.

*“We go to the bridge,”* he said. *“Not as courier and census taker. As something else. Something the ministry didn’t account for.”*

The door behind them slammed shut, the sound echoing like a gunshot in the sudden silence. Outside, the lanterns continued their slow swing, the guards’ boots still crunching on the cobblestones, but inside the room, the air had changed. The dispatches sat heavy on the table, the truth they carried a stone in every heart, but for the first time since Petra had accepted the case from Corven’s hands, she felt a possibility she had not allowed herself in years: a way forward that did not require her to become a monster, or to let the lie win.

*“Sera,”* Petra said, turning to the woman who had lost her father to the same lie. *“We have a choice. We can let them burn us, and the truth dies with us. Or we can cross the bridge, and make the truth the only thing that matters.”*

Sera’s jaw set, the grief hardening into resolve. *“Then let’s cross.”*

Petra picked up the dispatch case, the lead seals long gone, the parchment inside warm from the room’s heat. She slung it across her back, the weight familiar now, but the burden different. She looked at Marsh, at the ledger open on the table, at the names of the erased staring up at her. She thought of the Cape Route, the bridge, the sea on either side, and the long, terrible road that had brought her to this moment.

*“Ready?”* she asked.

Sera nodded, the ring on Petra’s finger a small, burning coal of memory. *“Ready.”*

They moved toward the door, Petra leading, Sera close behind, Marsh falling into step at her side, the three of them a triangle of truth in a room full of lies. Outside, the ministry’s men still searched, the census takers still counted, the republic still crumbled around their ears. But inside, the dispatches had been read, the truth had been spoken, and the only way forward was through the only thing that had ever mattered: the truth itself.
