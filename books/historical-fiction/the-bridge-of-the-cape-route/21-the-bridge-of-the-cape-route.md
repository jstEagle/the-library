## 21. The Bridge of the Cape Route

The tide was out when Petra returned, leaving the estuary a vast glistening flat of wet sand and twisted rope, the Cape Route's great toll bridge a skeletal spine against the sky. She had not been here since the night the ministry's guns had turned the wooden superstructure into kindling, since the flight across half-tide in the dark, since the moment she had pressed the sealed dispatches into Sera Vane's hands and felt the fragile paper burn through the canvas of her satchel. Now the bridge stood half-standing, one arch collapsed into the muddy shallows, the iron toll cage dangling by a single hinge, its bell long silent, its floor planks splintered by fire and flood.

Petra had walked this stretch three years past, a lifetime ago in the republic's capital, when the flags still snapped from the quay and the census takers still counted heads with bureaucratic glee. She had crossed then with purpose, with the weight of sealed things in her satchel, with the quiet dread of a mission she believed in. Now she came back not as a courier, but as something like a mourner, carrying nothing but the memory of what she had done, and the names she had been commanded to protect.

She stepped onto the causeway, her boots sinking slightly into the soft mud, the salt air sharp on her tongue. Gulls cried overhead, indifferent. The ruined bridge groaned faintly in the wind, a sound like an old joint aching in the cold. Petra pulled her cloak tighter, the fabric worn thin at the elbows, and looked up at the span. Where once the toll collector had demanded passage with a ledger and a stare, now only the elements held sway. The ministry's faction that had garrisoned the bridge after the republic's collapse had fled inland, following the fall of the capital, leaving the structure to the tide and the whispers of those who had once crossed it with fear or hope.

She walked to the remaining arch, the one that still stood, its cables frayed but intact enough to hold a person's weight. Below, the water was low, revealing the pilings that had once been submerged, barnacled and silent. Petra placed a hand on the rough timber, feeling the vibration of the wind through the wood, and for a moment she was back on that night crossing, the ferry at Saltmere, the deserter's knowing eyes, the way Sera had held the letter at arm's length, distrustful, grief-stricken.

A promise made in peacetime, she thought. A whisper at a harbor festival, the brass of medals catching the lantern light, Aldric Vane's smile as he had taken her hand and said, *If ever the republic turns, if the orders come to brand me a traitor, you will take this to my daughter. You will see she receives it. No matter what.* She had promised, and in peacetime a promise was a thing of warmth and light, a bond between equals. But the republic had not stayed peaceful. The flags had come down, the ministries had reclassified, the dispatches had become evidence, and Aldric Vane had been declared an enemy of the state.

Petra's fingers traced the curve of the toll post's beam, where once a brass plaque had named the republic's tariffs on salt and oil and pilgrims. Now the plaque was gone, hacked off or burned, leaving only a stump of iron and the ghost of its letters. She knelt, her knees sinking into the damp sand, and reached into the pocket of her cloak. She had kept something from that final night, tucked against her chest when she had fled the relay town, when Corven had pressed a folded scrap of paper into her hand—a name, a ledger number, a warning she had not dared open until now.

Corven's name. She had meant to burn it, to erase it, to let the institution erase him as it had erased so many others. But she had kept the paper smooth, the ink faded but legible: *Handler Corven, Ministry of Internal Intelligence, clearance 4-B, last known position: Saltmere relay*. She had kept it as a token of what she had lost, of the loyalty that had cost him his life. And she had kept Marsh's ledger pages, folded small, the census pages that had named the disappeared, the men and women the ministry had erased from the rolls, their names listed beside crimes they had not committed, their existences denied by the stroke of a clerk's pen.

Petra unfolded the ledger pages. They were yellowed now, the print softened by time and salt, but the names remained stark: *Elder Miro, killed for sheltering a dispatch carrier; girl Nessa, disappeared for asking after her brother; Corven himself, struck from the rolls after his warning*. She had Marsh's own hand on some of them, annotations in the margin, stars drawn by a man who had begun to doubt the census he was paid to take. These pages were the truth she had carried in her bones, the proof that the republic's treason charge against Vane had been manufactured, the tolls seized under false pretenses.

She reached into the other pocket and pulled out the small, folded scrap of Corven's paper. She pressed it against the ledger, the two pieces of truth aligning as they had aligned on that road north, when Marsh had fallen into step beside her, his questions drifting from household numbers to the contents of her satchel, his eyes sharp but never accusatory. He had seen too much, counted too many disappearances, to remain a willing instrument of the ministry's erasure.

Petra found a rusted nail half-buried in the timber of the toll post, still holding a fragment of frayed rope. She tied the ledger pages and Corven's scrap to the nail, letting them catch the wind, letting them flutter like a tattered banner. She stepped back, looking at the memorial she had made. The pages snapped against the wood, a small, fierce thing against the vast, indifferent sky. The bridge was ruined, the republic was fallen, but here, on this scar of coastline, truth had found a place.

She stood there for a long time, the salt wind pulling at her hair, the tide creeping in around her boots, washing over the sand where she had once crossed with the weight of a nation's secrets. She thought of the enemy's daughter, Sera, who had finally chosen publication, who had sent copies of the dispatches to every port on the Cape Route, dooming the Vane name but saving the truth. She thought of Ivo Marsh, who had survived the capital's sweep, who had been uncounted at last, his own ledger of the disappeared now a quiet testament to the power of one man's doubt. She thought of Corven, who had burned his own archives and sent her a warning in the dark, paying for her passage with his own erasure.

Mourning, she realized, had not been a static thing. It had been a weight she had carried, page by page, seal by seal, until the carrying had become unbearable. And now, standing on the ruined bridge, she had converted that mourning into something public, something that would outlast the wind and the tide. The toll post, with its nail and its fluttering papers, was a marker for anyone who would cross this way in the future—a reminder that the republic had not been eternal, that its ministries could err, that one woman had refused to let the truth die.

Petra turned from the bridge, stepping onto the hard-packed sand of the far shore. The tide was coming in, the water rising around the pilings, lapping at the base of the span. She looked back one last time at the memorial, the ledger pages and Corven's name catching the low sun, turning the paper a brittle gold. Then she began the walk toward the Cape Route's open road, the path that would take her into exile, into the foreign shores where a census taker would one day ask her name and expect a lie.

She walked without looking back, the memory of the bridge's ruin behind her, the weight of what she had carried now light as a breath. The republic had fallen not with a battle, she knew, but with signatures and empty offices, with dispatches circulated and ministries collapsed. And she, Petra Eastwick, who had once carried sealed things across a fracturing coastline, now carried nothing but her own name, earned back through honesty and the quiet courage of a memorial set on a wind-blasted bridge.

The road stretched before her, flat and white under the dying light, the estuary widening into the open sea. She could not have known that years would pass before she would be asked, on a foreign shore, the single probing question that had begun all of this. She could not have known that the census taker who would approach her then would not be the same man who had stopped her on the road north, his questions then innocent, his ledger blank. But she walked on, the memory of the bridge and its memorial guiding her, the truth she had delivered now a part of the landscape, as permanent as the tide and as fleeting as a name written in salt.

She crested a dune and saw the foreign shore's village below, its roofs of red tile, its harbor filled with boats that had not known the republic's flags. A figure stood at the edge of the dunes, waiting, a census taker with a clipboard and a calm, expectant face. Petra slowed her pace, her hand resting on the empty satchel at her side, the case she had burned years ago, the dispatches now a part of the wind and the water and the memory of that ruined bridge. She had walked this distance, crossed this route, delivered her truth, and now she would be asked the question that had started it all. She would answer it truthfully, as she had answered every other question on this journey, from the first lie to the last honest word.

The figure began to walk toward her, the clipboard held low, the questions ready. Petra straightened her spine, let the wind lift the ends of her cloak, and prepared to speak her name into the air, a name that had survived the fall of a republic, the burning of a bridge, the erasure of those who had tried to make her nothing. She would speak it, and the answer would be true, and for the first time in years, the weight of a secret would not sit in her bones.

She crested the final rise and stood before the census taker, the village behind her, the sea beside her, the bridge and its memorial far back, a memory turned into public memory, a mourning converted into something that would outlast the regime that had sought to erase it. The question hung in the air between them, simple and unbearably complex: *What is your name?*

Petra looked at the woman's face, at the clipboard, at the way the salt wind stirred the pages of the ledger she had left behind. She thought of Corven, of Marsh, of Sera, of the bridge that had burned and the memorial that stood in its place. She thought of the dispatches she had delivered not to ministers but to an enemy's daughter, of the bridge she had made a memorial, of the truth she had carried and had finally laid to rest.

*Petra Eastwick*, she said, and the name sounded like a bell tolling in an empty tower, clear and resonant and true.

The census taker wrote it down, checked a box, and looked up. *And what brings you to this shore, Petra Eastwick?*

Petra hesitated only a moment. *I carry the truth*, she said. *And I carry my name, at last my own.*

The woman nodded, as if she had expected nothing less, as if the answer had been the only possible one all along. She made a mark on her clipboard, folded it, and stepped back, giving Petra room to pass. *The republic has been gone for years*, she said. *You are free to go wherever the tide takes you.*

Petra walked down the dune toward the village, the sand firm beneath her boots, the sea salt on her skin, the memory of the bridge and its memorial fading behind her but never truly gone. She had returned to the ruined bridge to keep one final promise, and in doing so, she had made the crossing a memorial, had converted mourning into public memory, had carried her truth across the Cape Route and into a new day. The republic had fallen, the ministry's faction had collapsed, and she, Petra Eastwick, had survived not by luck but by the quiet, stubborn act of remembering, of honoring, of finally laying down the weight she had carried for so long.

The village awaited, its sounds the clatter of market stalls, the distant cry of a gull, the low hum of a life that had no need for secrets. Petra stepped onto the cobblestones, the name she had reclaimed echoing in her ears, the truth she had delivered now a part of the wind and the water and the stories that would be told along this coast for as long as people crossed the Cape Route. She had walked the distance of a life in what could be carried when the orders came, and now, at last, she carried only herself.
