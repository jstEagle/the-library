## 1. The Ledger Under the Floorboard  

The granary had once stored grain in neat, golden sheaves, but now its beams groaned under the weight of shattered limbs and whispered prayers. Winter had crept into the valley like a thief, its breath sharp and biting, seeping through the cracks in the wooden walls and nesting in the hollows of the floorboards. The air was thick with the scent of iodine, sweat, and something older—rot, maybe, or the damp earth beneath the building’s roots. Petra Okonkwo moved through the chaos of triage with the practiced efficiency of someone who had learned to navigate disaster long before this war.  

She knelt beside a stretcher, adjusting the cloth bandage around a young man’s thigh. His face was pale, almost translucent in the flickering lamplight, and his dark eyes fluttered open as she pressed a finger to his wrist. “You’re not going to die here,” she said, more to steady herself than him. The words felt false the moment they left her lips.  

The stretcher-bearer—a wiry Frenchman with ink-stained fingers—grunted a noncommittal reply. Petra ignored him. She had learned long ago that men like him spoke more often in silences than in words.  

The morning report had come in an hour ago, delivered by a sergeant whose uniform was more stained than military. Petra had read it aloud, as she did every day, her voice echoing slightly in the vast, empty space between the granary’s rafters. *Two killed, seven wounded. Serbian front.* The names were crisp, official, final. She had marked them in the ledger with a black ink that bled slightly into the parchment, as if the paper itself recoiled from the permanence of the words.  

But there was another name.  

She had seen the boy—Serbian, maybe seventeen, no older—being carried in two hours before the report was filed. His leg had been crushed beneath a collapsed trench wall, and his lungs were filling with blood. She had held his hand as he died, murmuring things in a language she did not know, while the stretcher-bearer muttered something about “not worth the effort” and walked away.  

The morning report did not mention him.  

Petra closed her eyes and let her fingers trace the edge of the ledger, its cover worn smooth by her touch. She had begun writing in it on the third day, when the first man had been forgotten. A French corporal, struck by shrapnel in the shoulder, whose name the lieutenant had dismissed with a shrug: “Dead men don’t need names.” But Petra had written his name anyway, followed by the date, the unit, and a single sentence: *He begged me to tell his mother he tried to protect the village.*  

She had done the same for others. A German mechanic, amused by her persistence. An Ottoman courier, whose accent she had mimicked in the dark to save his life. A Bulgarian soldier who wept when she bandaged his face, torn by barbed wire. Each name was a small rebellion, a refusal to let the machinery of war erase the man behind the uniform.  

The ledger was hidden beneath the floorboard in the corner of the granary, where the roof had caved in during the last bombing raid. She had widened the gap just enough to slide it in, then covered it with a loose plank and a rusty bucket. No one looked twice. Officers passed through without curiosity, soldiers shuffled past without noticing, and the few civilians who entered the building only paused to cough or curse the damp.  

Petra stood and stretched, her back protesting. The day was young, and the hospital was already filling with the wounded. She had work to do.  

***  

The first scream came from the doorway.  

Petra turned to see a young Austrian private stumbling in, his face streaked with mud and frost, his uniform torn open to reveal a gash across his chest. The stretcher-bearer stumbled back, wiping his hands on his tunic. “Too late,” he said, but Petra was already moving.  

She tore strips of gauze from a supply crate and began packing the wound, her movements steady despite the blood seeping through her gloves. The boy’s breathing was shallow, his skin mottled with fever. “You’re going to be alright,” she repeated, then caught herself. The words felt hollow.  

The stretcher-bearer stepped closer. “She’s been hit in the chest. We can’t—”  

“We can.” Petra didn’t look at him. “Move him to the south ward. I’ll meet you there.”  

She tore another strip of gauze and pressed it against the boy’s shoulder, holding it in place as he whimpered. His accent was thick, almost unintelligible. “Where… where am I?”  

“You’re safe now.”  

He grabbed her hand, his grip desperate. “My name is Franz. My mother—”  

“I know.” She released his hand and stepped back. “Franz, I think you need to rest.”  

But Franz was already fading, his eyelids fluttering as she worked. Petra wiped her hands on her apron and glanced toward the ledger, hidden beneath its plank. It had grown heavier in the past week. Twenty-three names now, maybe more. She had stopped counting.  

The lieutenant had passed by earlier, his polished boots clicking against the wooden floor. He had glanced at her, then at the bucket where she kept her supplies, and raised an eyebrow. *Standard procedure,* she had told herself. *Nothing more.*  

But the lieutenant’s eyes had lingered.  

She heard footsteps behind her and turned to find the major—Aldous Reeve, tall and angular, with a clipboard clutched in his gloved hand. His uniform was immaculate, a stark contrast to the grime of the ward.  

“Everything in order?” he asked, nodding toward the triage table.  

Petra straightened, offering a small smile. “As ordered, Major.”  

He glanced around the room, his gaze lingering on the bucket, then on the floorboards near the broken window. “Good. I expect nothing less from such a well-run facility.”  

She nodded, her stomach tightening. *Immaculate anonymity,* he had said once, when the chaplain had praised the hospital’s “unblemished reputation.” Petra had wondered then what he meant by that. Now, watching him take inventory of the room, she understood. He wanted it to be clean, efficient, forgettable.  

Reeve moved on, his boots fading into the corridor. Petra exhaled slowly, her fingers curling into fists. She closed her eyes and tried to focus on something else—the rhythm of her breath, the sound of the wind whistling through the cracks in the walls, the distant creak of the granary settling into the earth.  

But her thoughts drifted to the boy in the south ward, and the name she had written in the ledger just before Franz.  

The morning report had listed him as *missing in action*. No death, no wound—just absence. Petra had found him half-buried in the snow outside the field hospital’s perimeter, his uniform torn, his throat slit cleanly across. She had wrapped him in a blanket and brought him inside, but he had died before dawn.  

She had written his name in the ledger, followed by: *He was not a ghost. He was a soldier. He deserves to be remembered.*  

The lieutenant would not have understood.  

Petra knelt beside the broken window and pressed her palm to the cold glass. The sky was a dull gray, the sun hidden behind a veil of cloud. Somewhere beyond the valley, the war raged on, and she did not know if it mattered.  

But here, in this place of broken bodies and whispered names, it mattered.  

***  

The evening report came late, delayed by the storm that had begun to lash against the granary’s windows. Petra sat at the table in the south ward, ledger open before her, pen poised. The room was nearly empty now—only two patients remained, and both were beyond saving.  

She wrote slowly, deliberately. *Kladovo Massacre. Three Serbian soldiers found in the rubble of the bridge. Names unknown. All executed.*  

The words felt heavy as she set down her pen. She had heard the accounts from the survivors, scattered soldiers who had stumbled into the hospital with stories of fire and blood. The bridge, they said, had been a trap. Men had been lined up and shot, their bodies left to melt into the Danube.  

But the morning report mentioned none of it.  

Petra closed the ledger and slid it into her coat. She had until dawn to hide it again. The bucket by the door would have to wait.  

Outside, the wind howled, and the granary shuddered. Inside, the silence pressed close, broken only by the occasional cough or the scrape of a boot on the floorboards. Petra walked to the window and pulled aside the curtain, watching the snow fall in thick, suffocating waves.  

She thought of the boy whose name the report had omitted. Of the soldiers whose stories would never be told. Of the ledger, hidden beneath its plank, holding truths that no one else would record.  

History, she had learned, was not written by the victors. It was written by those who refused to let names be erased.  

And she would not let them be forgotten.  

The first snowflake landed on her glove. She did not brush it away.  

The door creaked open behind her.  

Petra did not turn.  

“You’re very diligent, aren’t you, Mrs. Okonkwo?”  

She froze.  

Major Reeve stood in the doorway, his coat dripping with melted snow. His expression was unreadable.  

“I was just… finishing the report.” She kept her voice low.  

He stepped inside, his boots crunching against the floorboards. “You write a great deal in that book of yours.”  

Her heart quickened. “It’s for record-keeping. Patient details, treatments—”  

“Patients.” He repeated the word as if tasting it. “Yes. A hospital is a place of healing. Not of… documentation.”  

She nodded, but her fingers tightened around the edge of the ledger.  

Reeve lingered near the window, his breath fogging the glass. “You know, Mrs. Okonkwo, there are rules to this work. Procedures. I would hate for the higher command to find… irregularities.”  

Petra met his gaze, steady. “Of course, sir.”  

“Hm.” He turned, his eyes sharp. “Well. I trust you’ll continue your excellent work. The cold is setting in, and we cannot afford delays.”  

She inclined her head. “Certainly, sir.”  

When his footsteps faded down the hall, she exhaled.  

Then she looked at the ledger in her hands and remembered the boy in the snow.  

She opened it again.  

And wrote.
