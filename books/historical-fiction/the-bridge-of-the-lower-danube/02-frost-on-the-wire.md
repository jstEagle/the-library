## 2. Frost on the Wire

The ledger lay open on the cracked wooden table, its pages already thick with the names of men the morning reports had deemed unworthy of ink. Petra had just finished recording the conscript from Belgrade, his name a single line among the smudged charcoal and the quiet, aching geometry of his wound, when the door to the converted granary hospital swung open on frozen hinges. 

Major Aldous Reeve stepped inside, his greatcoat stiff with ice crystals, the brim of his cap casting a shadow over his face. The warmth of the stove had scarcely begun to thin the air that clung to his uniform. Behind him, the winter light slanted through the high loft windows, pale and watery as bleached bone.

“Mrs. Okonkwo.” Reeve’s voice was a low rasp, polished by habit and the weight of decades in field administration. He did not remove his gloves. “Inspection. Usual.”

Petra looked up from the ledger. Her hands were stained with the faint, persistent scent of antiseptic and the winter damp that seeped through every floorboard. “Of course, Major. I was just—”

“Hm.” He moved toward the center of the ward, his boots crunching lightly on the scattered straw that served as makeshift bedding. The men shifted in their sleep, murmurs rising and falling like distant surf. Reeve’s gaze swept the rows, and Petra felt the familiar, phantom tug of his attention on the invisible threads of each man’s identity. He paused at a cot where a Serbian officer, his arm bound in a hastily fashioned sling, stared at the ceiling. The officer’s name had not appeared on the morning report. Neither had the name of the young Albanian rifleman two cots down, his leg gashed by shrapnel, his eyes sunken into a face that had not seen clean water in a week.

“Immaculate anonymity,” Reeve murmured, not quite to himself. He turned, and Petra saw the faintest flicker of something behind his eyes—approval, perhaps, or something crueler, the satisfaction of a system running smooth. “No confusion. No complications. Just the quiet work of healing, unburdened by the particulars.”

He circled the ward, his gloved hand resting lightly on the back of a chair, pushing it aside with a faint squeak that seemed to echo in the stillness. Petra continued her work, folding a bandage, but her eyes tracked the Major’s progress. She watched how his gaze skipped over the names she had written, how it slid past the ledger tucked beneath the table, the cover worn soft from nights of secret inscription. He was a man who believed that if a name did not appear on paper, it had never existed. In his world, that was not cruelty; it was efficiency.

“Paperwork,” Reeve said, stopping at the end of a row where a French medic was rewrapping a gangrenous foot, “is the theater’s greatest illusion. It makes the chaos manageable. It makes the man… manageable.” He glanced at Petra. “You understand, I presume, the importance of order. The higher command—”

“They rely on it,” Petra said, not looking up. “I have seen the forms, Major. They omit what they cannot carry.”

“Precisely.” Reeve’s lip quirked, not quite a smile. “And we must be careful, Mrs. Okonkwo. Rules exist for a reason. Procedures. I would hate for the higher command to find… irregularities. A ledger, perhaps, tucked where it does not belong. A name where it should not be.”

Petra set the bandage down. She met his gaze, steady. “Of course, sir. I am merely recording the convalescent’s progress. It is… habit.”

“Hm.” Reeve turned, his coat shifting. “Well. I trust you’ll continue your excellent work. The cold is setting in, and we cannot afford delays.”

He walked toward the door, and Petra watched the play of light across his shoulders, the way the winter frost crinkled at the edges of his uniform. When his footsteps faded down the hall, she exhaled, a breath that fogged the air between her and the window.

Then she looked at the ledger in her hands and remembered the boy in the snow—the Serbian conscript, his breath a thin white thread, his eyes wide and unseeing as the cold took him. She opened the ledger again, the page already crowded, and wrote the name she had almost let slip into the void of the report. *Petar Jovanović.* The ink bled slightly into the fibrous paper, a small, defiant red star against the beige.

*** 

The inspection continued through the granary’s other sections. Reeve moved into the ward of the wounded officers, where the air smelled of tobacco and the polished wood of crutches. Here, the paperwork was thinner, the names more likely to appear, but still, Petra noticed, the forms omitted the conscripts, the foreigners, the men whose statelessness made them inconvenient to the ledgers of empire. An Italian deserter, his uniform frayed, his eyes darting toward the door. A Romanian peasant, conscripted at seventeen, his real age hidden beneath a forged signature on a recruitment paper that the Major’s office had presumably filed without question.

Reeve did not mention these omissions. He could not, without admitting that the system he praised was porous, that the anonymity he valued was a fiction built on the erasure of men who did not fit the neat columns of regimental rolls. Instead, he offered praise that felt like a sentence.

“Sister Kamara’s ward is exemplary,” he said, stopping at the doorway where Petra’s friend and fellow nurse, Sister Kamara, was checking the pulse of a young man from the Sudanese contingent, his uniform stained with mud and something darker. “Immaculate. Truly.”

Kamara inclined her head, her own face a mask of polite composure. “We do what we can, Major.”

“Indeed.” Reeve’s gaze lingered on the young man’s arm, where a fragment of shrapnel had lodged near the brachial artery. He made no note of it in his own mind, or if he did, he filed it away where it would never surface. “The granary itself is a model of efficiency. I daresay the War Office could do worse than to study its organization.”

Petra glanced at the ledger beneath the table. Every name she had recorded was a fracture in the smooth surface Reeve was polishing. She thought of the boy in the snow, of Petar Jovanović, of the Serbian conscript whose body they had found frozen near the riverbank, his papers lost to the current, his name known only to the earth that had taken him. She thought of how the morning report would list him simply as “frostbite, unknown unit,” and how the ledger would ensure that somewhere, somewhere beyond the reach of the officers’ certainty, his name would survive.

“Major,” she said, as he turned to leave the granary’s far end. “If I may. The ledger—”

“Yes?” Reeve’s hand rested on the doorframe, the frost outside catching the edges of his silhouette.

“It is merely a record of those who have passed through here. Names, that is all. I find it… helps me remember why we do this work. Why each man matters, even if the reports do not.”

Reeve looked at her, and for a moment, the polite mask slipped, revealing the smallest crack of something else—curiosity, perhaps, or the faintest inkling that she was not as compliant as she appeared. He recovered quickly, his expression smoothing into the mask of administrative certainty.

“A useful habit, Mrs. Okonkwo. Though I must impress upon you the need for discretion. The ledger has no place in the official record. If it were to be seen—”

“I understand, sir.” Petra inclined her head. “It stays where it is.”

“Good.” He pushed the door open and stepped out into the corridor, the cold rushing in after him like a breath. “We are of the same mind, then. Order. Efficiency. The war is large enough without adding confusion.”

He was gone, and the door clicked shut behind him. Petra stood alone in the center of the ward, the stove’s heat barely touching the edges of the room. She looked at the rows of men, at the rise and fall of their chests, at the quiet, persistent work of healing that had become her life since she had left the Niger Delta and the humid, noisy streets of Lagos for this frozen valley on the edge of the Danube. She thought of the river, freezing over slowly, the pontoon bridges groaning under the weight of ice, the way the world narrowed and expanded all at once when you were responsible for the lives of men who had been discarded by the very systems that sent them to war.

She crossed to the window and looked out. The river below was a slate-grey surface, cracked in places where the current was stronger, and beyond that, the distant hills were already dusted with a thin, powdery snow. The bridge spans, usually a chaotic tangle of timbers and ropes, now stood as skeletal outlines against the low sky, their pontoons submerged or locked in place by the freezing water. Soon, the engineers would detonate the charges, close the last crossing, and the valley would be sealed off from the world for the depth of the winter. The supply ships would stop running. The inquiries would have to wait until the thaw, if they ever came at all.

Petra turned back to the ledger. Her fingers, slightly numb from the cold, found the space where Petar Jovanović’s name would go. She wrote it in a careful, sloping hand, the ink dark against the aged paper. Then she added another, and another, each one a small act of reclamation against the erasure that Reeve represented, against the morning reports that would never name them, never account for the weight of what they had suffered.

Outside, the wind picked up, rattling the panes of the windowpane. The hospital’s own windows frosted over in delicate, branching patterns, like veins drawn in ice. Inside, the men slept, the living and the dying intertwined in the same quiet space, and Petra knew that as long as she had ink and paper, as long as she refused to let the names be erased, the truth would not be entirely lost. It might be hidden, tucked beneath floorboards and beneath the weight of administrative silence, but it would not vanish.

She closed the ledger and slipped it beneath the floorboard, in the same hollow where she had placed the first name, weeks ago. The wood shifted under her hand, a faint sound lost in the creak of the building settling around her. She straightened, her breath visible in the sudden chill, and moved to tend the next man, the next name, the next quiet defiance.

The inspection was over. The Major’s voice lingered in the corridors like a cold draft, but Petra had already turned her back on it, walking toward the rows of cots, toward the work that mattered to her, to the men who had become more than numbers, more than the omissions of officers who believed that if a name was not on paper, it had never been.

*** 

The day wore on, and the hospital settled into the rhythm of winter necessity. Petra tended the wounded, her hands moving with the certainty of long practice, changing dressings, easing pain, speaking words of comfort in languages that sometimes matched the men’s origins and sometimes did not. The ledger remained hidden, a quiet companion beneath the floorboards, each entry a stone placed in the river to mark the crossing.

Reeve’s inspection had left its mark, though not in the way he perhaps intended. As he had moved through the wards, Petra had found herself watching him more closely, noting the way his eyes skipped over the things he did not want to see, the way his voice hardened when the subject of omissions arose, the almost imperceptible tension at the corners of his mouth when a name was mentioned that did not belong to a recognized regiment. She had seen the flicker in his gaze when she had spoken of the ledger, and she had filed it away, a piece of a larger puzzle she was only beginning to understand.

That evening, as the sun dipped below the hills and the hospital’s oil lamps were lit, throwing wavering circles of light against the frost-etched windows, Petra sat at the table with the ledger open before her. The names accumulated: *Petar Jovanović, Serbian conscript; Ilya Voss, claimed Russian and German, possessed neither; Marcel Dubois, French infantry, lost at the Battle of the Somme three winters past, now recovered enough to walk with a limp; Ahmed Al-Mansur, Sudanese rifleman, his file marked “missing, presumed dead” despite having walked through the granary’s doors six weeks past.* Each one was a thread, and as she wove them together, the fabric of the thing she was building grew denser, heavier, undeniable.

A knock at the door interrupted her. It was not Reeve’s heavy tread, nor the expected call of a orderly. It was Sister Kamara, her face drawn, the lamplight catching the shadows beneath her eyes.

“Petra,” she said, lowering her voice. “The Major has been asking after you. Through the corridor, discreetly. He wants to know if the ledger… if you have been recording anything beyond the official reports.”

Petra’s fingers stilled over the page. She looked at the names, the careful script, the quiet rebellion of ink on paper. “I record what I see, Sister. That is all.”

“And what you see, the Major sees differently.” Kamara paused, her gaze flicking toward the door’s narrow window. “He spoke of irregularities this morning. Of procedures. I thought he was referring to the men, not the paper.”

Petra stood, the ledger in her hands, and moved to the window. Outside, the river was darker now, the ice shifting with a slow, mournful groan. The bridge spans stood as silent witnesses to the season’s turning, their timbers creaking as the water beneath them froze and shifted, as the world outside the granary’s walls continued its inexorable, indifferent turning.

“He is a man who believes in the power of what is not said,” Petra said finally, not turning from the window. “But he is not the only one who believes that.”

“No.” Kamara’s voice was quiet. “Perhaps not. But be careful, Petra. The inquiry is coming. I have heard whispers. The armistice investigators, they are not as cut off from the front as we are. They are moving, gathering, and when they arrive, everything will be examined. Every paper, every name, every silence.”

Petra turned then, the ledger clutched against her chest. “Then we write with more care. And we hide what we must.”

“Always.” Kamara inclined her head, a fleeting smile. “Now. Let us finish the night’s rounds. The men are cold, and the ledger can wait.”

They moved together through the wards, two women in a frozen hospital, bound by a shared secret and a stubborn belief that the men they tended were more than the sum of the omissions that sought to erase them. Outside, the wind rose, rattling the panes, and the river below continued its slow, stubborn freeze, the bridge spans groaning under the weight of the season’s turning. And in the quiet, beneath the floorboards, the ledger waited, its pages filling with names that the world outside wanted to forget, but that Petra Okonkwo, and the men she tended, and the quiet sister at her side, would not let be lost.

*** 

Night deepened, and the hospital’s own sounds—the rustle of straw, the faint moan of a feverish man, the steady drip of meltwater from a cracked window—settled into a low, persistent hum. Petra lay awake in the small quarters she had carved out of the granary’s attic, the ledger folded beside her, its pages a map of the men she had known and the ones she had yet to know. The Major’s words echoed in her mind: *Irregularities. Procedures. I would hate for the higher command to find… irregularities.*

She thought of the ledger’s first entry, the Serbian conscript whose name she had not known until the morning report had failed him. She thought of Petar Jovanović, of Ilya Voss, of the endless cascade of men whose names the world had decided were inconvenient, whose deaths the machinery of war had smoothed over with a few lines of ink and a stamp of approval. And she thought of the river, freezing over, the bridge closing, the valley sealing itself off from the world, the ledger’s only chance of survival the supply ship that would come on the morning tide, if the ice held and the engineers had not yet detonated the charges that would shut the crossing forever.

A soft sound from the cot next to hers stirred her. It was not a patient—she had no cot, only the table and the ledger and the narrow pallet she used when the ward was quiet. It was the sound of someone shifting in their sleep, a faint murmur that resolved into a name, spoken in a language she did not know but whose rhythm she had learned to recognize.

*Petra.*

She sat up, the lamplight casting long shadows across the floor. The cot was occupied by a young man, his face pale in the orange glow, his bandaged arm resting on the blanket. He was a German conscript, she had learned, though he spoke French when he was awake, and English when he thought no one was listening. His name was Karl. He had been brought in three days past, shrapnel in his thigh, a look of exhaustion in his eyes that had nothing to do with his wound and everything to do with the war he had been told was his duty, the home he had left behind that no longer existed, the future that had been erased by the same machinery that now sought to erase his name.

“Karl,” she said softly.

He opened his eyes, blinking in the lamplight. “You are awake.”

“Always,” Petra replied. She reached out, her fingers brushing the blanket near his hand. “What are you thinking?”

He turned his head slightly, his gaze sliding toward the corner where the ledger lay hidden. “I heard you writing tonight. Earlier. The ink.”

“A habit.” Petra smiled, faint. “You do not need to worry about the ledger, Karl. It is… private.”

“Is it a secret?” The question was direct, the words weighted with something she could not quite place—fear, perhaps, or a quiet hope. “Because I have a name, too. A name they gave me, a name I gave myself. But if the reports do not have it, does it belong to me, or to no one?”

Petra considered this, the lamplight catching the lines around her eyes, the quiet strength that had carried her from the Niger Delta to the Lower Danube, through winters and wars and the slow, relentless erosion of men’s lives by the systems that sent them to fight and die.

“A name belongs to the one who bears it,” she said finally. “And to those who refuse to let it be erased. The ledger is not a secret to shame us, Karl. It is a record of who we were, and who we are, even when the world tries to tell us we never existed.”

Karl was quiet for a long moment. Then, softly, he spoke a name she had not heard in the ledger yet. “Friedrich Weiss. That is the name on my papers. But my father called me Elias. And my mother, before the trains took her, called me Karl. I do not know which is me. I do not know which survives.”

Petra’s breath caught. She reached for the ledger, the ink pot already set beside her from her earlier writing. She opened a fresh page, the paper slightly rough from the winter damp, and wrote the name he had given her.

*Karl. Friedrich Weiss. Elias.*

The ink bled slightly, a small red blossom against the beige, but the name was there, fixed in time, a small anchor against the current that sought to wash it away.

“Now,” Petra said, closing the ledger and setting it gently aside. “Now you have a place. And I will make sure it stays, even if the Major, even if the reports, even if the world outside this granary tries to forget.”

Karl nodded, a small, grateful smile touching his lips. “Thank you, Petra. For not letting me disappear.”

“You will not disappear,” she said. “Not while I am here. Not while any of us are here, remembering.”

Outside, the wind howled against the granary’s walls, the river beyond groaning under the weight of the ice that was thickening with each hour. The bridge spans creaked and shifted, their timbers straining under the pressure of the frozen water, and somewhere in the distance, the engineers’ charges were being placed, the detonators set, the season’s last crossing being prepared for silence. But inside the granary, beneath the frost-etched windows and the oil lamps’ wavering glow, a small, stubborn light burned: the ledger, the names, the quiet, relentless act of insisting that every man who had ever lived had a name worth recording, and that history, however it was written, happened wherever someone refused to let those names be erased.

She lay back down, the ledger beside her, the winter night pressing in on all sides, and she waited. Waited for the morning tide, for the supply ship, for the inquiry, for the moment when the ledger would finally leave the valley and carry these names into the world beyond the river. But for now, in the quiet dark of the granary hospital, with the men sleeping around her and the name Karl Friedrich Weiss Elias fixed in ink on the page, she allowed herself a moment of peace. The world outside was cold and indifferent, a machine that sought to smooth over every rough edge, every name that did not fit its neat columns. But inside, in this frozen valley on the edge of the Danube, there was a counter-machine, too: a woman with ink-stained fingers, a ledger hidden beneath floorboards, and a handful of men who refused to be forgotten.

The night was long, and the ice was thickening, and the ledger was growing, and somewhere in the quiet between the heartbeats of the wounded, a name was being written that the world would one day have to hear. And as Petra drifted toward sleep, the lamplight flickering and the wind outside rising to a keening, she knew that whatever came—wherever the inquiry led, whatever the Major’s procedures sought to erase—the names would remain. Not because the world was kind, but because she, and Sister Kamara, and Karl, and the men who could not speak their names but felt them nonetheless, would not let them vanish.

The chapter ended not with a bang, but with the quiet, persistent turning of a page, and the slow, inevitable approach of the morning tide that would carry the ledger—and the truth it held—out of the valley, and into a world that had nearly forgotten how to listen.
