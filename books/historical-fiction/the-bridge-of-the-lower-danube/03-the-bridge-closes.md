## 3. The Bridge Closes

The first charge went off at 0647, a dull thump felt in the marrow before the ears caught it. Petra had been kneeling beside Bed 14, adjusting the tourniquet on a Hungarian private whose femoral artery had decided, three days post-amputation, that it was not finished bleeding. The granary's converted beams shuddered. Dust, ancient and gray as sackcloth, sifted down from the roof timbers and settled on the private's sweat-sheened forehead.

She did not look up. Her fingers knew the pressure points, the rhythm of compression and release, the precise tension that stanched without severing. The tourniquet held. The private's breathing, ragged as a torn sail, steadied.

*One.*

The second charge came four minutes later. This one cracked the air like a whip, sharp enough to make the glass ampoules on the supply shelf sing. A few of the conscious men flinched — a Serbian corporal with a chest wound gasped, his bandages darkening; a Romanian artilleryman, both legs gone below the knee, clutched the iron frame of his cot until his knuckles blanched white.

Petra's hand moved to the corporal's dressing. *Pressure. Elevate. Observe.* She spoke to him in the pidgin Serbian she had cobbled together from three months of wounded mouths: *"Mirno. Mirno. Ja sam ovdje."* Calm. Calm. I am here.

The third charge arrived while she was rewrapping the artilleryman's stumps, the wool batting stiff with frost from the night's ventilation. The explosion was closer now, or perhaps the ice carried sound differently — a flat, concussive *crump* that vibrated through the soles of her boots, up the wooden floorboards, into the very grain of the building.

She paused, wool in hand, and looked toward the high windows.

Through the grimed panes, the Danube lay white and still as a shroud. The pontoon bridge — that clumsy necklace of iron barges and timber decking that had stitched the valley's two shores together since October — was coming apart in slow, deliberate violence. Sappers in greatcoats moved along the spans like dark ants, placing charges at each mooring. Smoke curled from the first severed connections, drifting low over the frozen river.

*Three spans down. Four to go.*

Major Reeve had announced the closure at yesterday's briefing, his voice clipped over the clatter of mess tins. *"The ice pack is thickening at three centimeters per day. The current beneath is scouring the anchors. We cannot risk the bridge, and we cannot maintain it. The last supply convoy crossed at 1400 hours. The next will not arrive until the spring thaw permits navigation."* He had not looked at Petra. He rarely did. But his eyes had flicked, once, to the floorboard beneath her cot in the nurses' alcove — the loose one, the one she had pried up with a stolen bayonet on her third night here.

She had not flinched. She had not looked down.

Now, standing at the window with the artilleryman's fresh bandages in her lap, she watched the fourth span go.

The detonation lifted the entire section — barges, decking, railings — in a single grotesque heave. Timber and iron flew upward, silhouetted against the pale winter sky, then crashed back down in a fountain of ice shards and black water. The river, freed from its constraint, surged through the gap with a roar audible even through the granary's thick walls. Chunks of pack ice, some the size of coffins, ground against each other in the sudden current, a sound like giant bones breaking.

*Four.*

The valley was an amphitheater of gray limestone, the river a frozen artery at its bottom. The bridge had been the only artery out. The mountain road over the pass had closed in November, buried under six meters of snow that no engineering corps could clear. The railhead at Turnu Severin was three days' march through territory that shifted allegiance with each battalion's passage. The Danube, when it flowed, was the only reliable road — and now the river itself had been cut.

Petra felt the knowledge settle in her chest, cold and heavy as the river stones she had once skipped across the Niger's shallows as a girl in Onitsha. Her father had taught her to read the water's skin — where the current ran deep, where the crocodiles sunned, where the sandbanks shifted overnight. *The river remembers what the maps forget,* he had said. *It keeps its own counsel.*

This river, this gray-green Danube choked with ice and history, had just sealed its counsel shut.

She turned from the window. The ward held thirty-seven men tonight — down from forty-two yesterday. Three dead, two evacuated on the last convoy. Thirty-seven names in the official register. Forty-three in the ledger beneath her floorboard.

The six omitted men occupied Beds 3, 7, 12, 19, 24, and 31. A Bulgarian deserter shot by his own officers. Two Serbian conscripts listed as "missing, presumed dead" after a trench raid that never appeared in any communiqué. A Romanian peasant pressed into a labor battalion, crushed by a supply wagon whose driver had been drunk on requisitioned slivovitz. An Austro-Hungarian corporal who had refused an order to execute civilians and been "disciplined" with a bayonet through the kidney. And the Hungarian private on Bed 14, whose name — István Kovács — would never reach his wife in Debrecen because the morning report listed only "one Hungarian private, GSW thigh, evacuated."

Petra's fingers brushed the pocket of her apron where she carried the stub of a pencil and a scrap of paper — today's names, waiting for tonight's transcription. The graphite dusted her fingertips, gray as the river ice.

*"Sestra?"* The Serbian corporal on Bed 3 — the one omitted from the register, the one whose name was *Dragan Petrović* and whose chest wound wept blood and air — turned his head toward her. His eyes were the color of the river in spring, pale and depthless. *"The bridge. Is it...?"*

She moved to him, checked the flutter-valve dressing on his sucking chest wound. The hiss of air had diminished; the lung was re-expanding. *Good. Hold.*

*"Da, Dragan. The bridge closes."* She spoke softly, the Serbian vowels still clumsy in her mouth. *"No more convoys. No more evacuations. We stay."*

His hand, thin as a bird's claw, caught her wrist. The grip was surprisingly strong. *"The major. He knows?"*

*"The major ordered it."*

*"And the ledger?"* The word was barely a breath, shaped more by the movement of his lips than any sound.

Petra's heart gave its familiar stutter — the one that came whenever someone named the thing she hid. Only three people knew: herself, the old boatwright Mirko who had shown her the workshop's hollow keel, and Dragan. Dragan who had watched her scribble in the dimness of the night ward, who had asked *what are you writing* with the directness of the dying, and who had nodded once when she told him *names. the names they erase.*

*"The ledger is safe,"* she said, covering his hand with hers. *"Mirko keeps it. The workshop stands."*

*"Mirko."* A ghost of a smile cracked his gray lips. *"The old fox. He hides more than boats."*

*"He hides what matters."*

Dragan's eyes closed. His breathing slowed, the terrible rattle settling into something quieter. Sleep, or its cousin. Petra withdrew her hand and moved on.

***

The ward smelled of iodine, unwashed bodies, woodsmoke from the iron stove, and the sweet-sour undertone of gangrene that no amount of ventilation could banish. It was the smell of 1916 on the Lower Danube, the smell of a war that had forgotten why it started and now only knew how to continue.

Petra made her rounds. Bed 7: the first Serbian conscript, *Marko Vasić*, fever burning through him, typhus or sepsis or both — the distinction mattered less than the cooling cloths she reapplied to his wrists and neck. Bed 12: the second conscript, *Luka Jovanović*, unconscious, his skull fractured by a rifle butt during the "discipline" that had followed the trench raid. Bed 19: the Romanian laborer, *Ioan Popescu*, his crushed chest rising in shallow, agonized gasps; she gave him the last of the morphine ampoules, watching the relief wash across his face like a benediction. Bed 24: the Austro-Hungarian corporal, *Franz Huber*, awake and lucid, his German precise and bitter as he dictated the name of the officer who had ordered the execution — *Hauptmann Klaus von Ritter, 14th Jäger Battalion* — and made her promise to write it down. Bed 31: the Bulgarian deserter, *Dimitar Kolev*, seventeen years old and weeping silently into his pillow, his own side's bullet still lodged near his spine.

*Forty-three names. Forty-three lives that the war would pretend never existed.*

At each bed, she paused. Listened. Recorded the pulse, the temperature, the character of breathing. And in her mind, she wrote the ledger entry: *Name. Rank. Unit. Wound. Date. Circumstance. Witness. Truth.*

The pencil stub grew shorter.

***

By midday, the last span had fallen. The bridge was gone, reduced to debris frozen into the river's edge and a few charred mooring posts standing like blackened fingers from the ice. The sappers had withdrawn, their work complete. The valley was sealed.

Major Reeve stood on the granary's loading dock, field glasses raised, studying the wreckage. His greatcoat collar was turned up against the wind that funneled up the gorge, carrying ice crystals that stung exposed skin. Beside him, Sergeant Kowalski — a Pole conscripted into the Austrian army, his accent thick when he spoke German — held a clipboard.

*"Total severance confirmed, sir. Ice pack moving freely. Current velocity estimated at six kilometers per hour. No crossing possible until breakup."*

Reeve lowered the glasses. His face was a study in controlled lines — the jaw tight, the eyes narrowed against the glare off the ice. He was a man who measured his worth in order, in the clean execution of duty, in the paperwork that made chaos legible. The bridge's destruction was a failure of logistics, and he wore it like a stain.

*"Document it,"* he said. *"Full report to Division by courier. If the courier can get through the pass."*

*"The pass is closed, sir. Has been for three weeks."*

*"Then the report waits. The duty does not."* Reeve turned, his boots sharp on the wooden planks. His gaze swept the granary's facade — the high windows, the smoke-stained chimney, the nurses' alcove window where Petra had stood not an hour ago. He did not pause on it. But Petra, watching from the ward's shadows through a crack in the blackout curtain, felt the weight of that glance like a physical touch.

*He knows. He suspects. He has always suspected.*

She had seen it in the way he reviewed the morning reports — the pauses, the frown at discrepancies he could not quite name. In the questions he asked about "administrative irregularities" during his weekly inspections. In the single time he had entered the nurses' alcove unannounced, found her on her knees beside her cot, and said only *"Good night, Sister Okonkwo"* before withdrawing.

He had not reported her. She did not know why. Perhaps he needed the hospital's mortality figures to remain low — her omissions kept the official death count down, the evacuation numbers manageable, the supply requests modest. Perhaps he was a man who understood that some truths were too large for forms, and that a ledger hidden beneath a floorboard was a kind of order he could not officially sanction but privately required.

Or perhaps he simply did not care about the names of the erased, so long as the machinery of his command functioned.

*"Sestra Okonkwo."*

She started. The voice came from the doorway — not Reeve's, but the hospital's quartermaster, a stubby Serbian named Miloš who had lost his left eye to a sniper in 1914 and now ran the stores with the ferocity of a man guarding his own heart.

*"The supply tally. You need to sign."*

Petra followed him to the dispensary, where the last crates from the final convoy lay open: bandages, sutures, three tins of condensed milk, a case of aspirin, two bottles of chloroform. No quinine. No antitoxin. No new morphine after the ampoules she had just used on Ioan Popescu.

*"This is it,"* Miloš said, his good eye flat. *"Until spring. Unless the ice breaks early and a barge gets through. Which it won't."*

*"We make do."*

*"We always do."* He thrust the clipboard at her. *"Sign here. And here. And here. Three copies. One for Division, one for the depot at Turnu Severin, one for us. As if any of them will ever be read."*

Petra signed. *P. Okonkwo, SRN.* The initials felt like a small rebellion — her father's name, her profession, the identity she had carried across two continents and a war that had no place for either.

*"The men,"* she said, handing back the pen. *"The wounded. How many can we hold?"*

*"Forty beds. Thirty-seven occupied. We can squeeze fifty if we use the floor. Sixty if we don't mind them dying on top of each other."* Miloš's mouth twisted. *"The real question is not beds. It's blood. It's plasma. It's the surgeon's hands when the typhus takes him — and it will, because we have no vaccine and the lice are already in the straw."*

*"Dr. Vuković is healthy."*

*"Dr. Vuković is sixty-three and coughs blood into his handkerchief when he thinks no one watches. I watch. I see."* Miloš tapped the clipboard. *"You should see too, Sestra. You see everything else."*

She did not answer. There was no answer that would not sound like evasion or arrogance.

***

The afternoon brought a thaw — not of temperature, but of silence. The men, understanding in the way soldiers always understood before officers told them, began to talk. The ward's low murmur rose into something like conversation.

Petra moved among them, refilling water tins, adjusting pillows, offering the condensed milk diluted with boiled water to those who could swallow. She listened.

*"My wife,"* said Franz Huber from Bed 24, his German precise despite the pain. *"She writes every week. Letters from Vienna. The children have scarlet fever. The youngest, Greta, she is four. She draws bridges in her letters. Always bridges. She does not know there are no bridges left."*

*"My mother,"* whispered Dimitar Kolev from Bed 31, his Bulgarian accented with the dialect of the Rhodopes. *"She will be told I died fighting the enemy. Not that I ran. Not that my own lieutenant shot me in the back. She will light a candle for a hero. I am not a hero, Sestra. I am a boy who wanted to go home."*

*"My land,"* murmured Ioan Popescu from Bed 19, the morphine softening his voice to a slurry. *"The oil fields at Ploiești. The Germans want them. The British want them. My government sold them. My sons work there. They do not know I am here. They think I am at the front, fighting for Romania. I was never at the front. I loaded crates. I loaded crates until a drunk driver crushed me. This is not war. This is waste."*

Petra knelt beside each bed. She did not offer false comfort. She did not say *you will survive* or *your sacrifice matters* or *the war will end soon*. She said: *"I hear you. Your name is written. You are not forgotten."*

And she meant it. The ledger beneath the floorboard, the duplicate in Mirko's workshop keel — these were her candles. Small flames against a darkness that stretched from the Danube to the Somme, from Galicia to Mesopotamia.

***

At 1600 hours, the temperature dropped sharply. The wind shifted, coming down from the mountains with a scream that rattled the granary's shutters. Snow began to fall — not the gentle flakes of morning, but hard, driven pellets that struck the glass like thrown shot.

Petra was in the nurses' alcove, the loose floorboard pried up, the ledger open on her knees. The light was failing, gray and thin through the single window. She wrote by feel as much as sight, the pencil scratching across the lined paper she had stolen from the quartermaster's stores months ago.

*Kolev, Dimitar. Private, 3rd Bulgarian Infantry. Age 17. GSW back, self-inflicted per own statement — actually inflicted by Lt. Georgiev during desertion attempt. Admitted 14 Jan. Witness: P. Okonkwo. Status: Critical.*

She wrote the truth as she knew it. Not the truth the army wanted — *Kolev, D., GSW back, accidental* — but the truth that lived in the boy's weeping, in the entry wound's angle, in the lieutenant's name she had coaxed from him before the morphine took hold.

*Georgiev, Lt. Ivan. 3rd Bulgarian Infantry. Shot subordinate attempting surrender. No report filed. Witness: D. Kolev.*

Two lines for one boy. Two names that would never appear in any official casualty list.

The pencil lead snapped.

She cursed softly, in Yoruba — *Ṣugbọn!* — and fished the small knife from her pocket to sharpen it. The blade caught the light as she worked, a flash of steel in the gloom.

The alcove door creaked.

Petra's hand froze. The ledger snapped shut. The floorboard settled back into place with a practiced thump, the dust settling over its edges like snow.

*"Sestra?"*

It was Ilya.

He stood in the doorway, a shadow against the ward's dim gaslight. He was not her patient — not officially. He slept in the loft above the stables, a space the hospital had claimed for convalescents who could walk but not march. He had been here six weeks, since a shell fragment had taken a chunk from his right shoulder and left his arm hanging useless. The surgeons had saved the limb; whether it would ever work again was a question for spring.

Ilya. *Ilya Petrović. Or Ilya Kovač. Or Ilya Dimitrov.* He had given three names on three different admissions, each with a different regiment, a different nationality, a different war. Serbian. Croatian. Bulgarian. He spoke all three languages, plus German, plus a smattering of Romanian and Turkish. He had no papers. His dog tags were blank — he said he had lost them, or never been issued them, or traded them for tobacco in a trench near Belgrade.

*No nation claims me,* he had told her once, in the kitchen while they peeled potatoes. *So I claim all of them. Or none. It depends on the day.*

Petra rose, brushing dust from her knees. *"Ilya. You should be resting. The arm—"*

*"The arm is fine. The shoulder aches. The cold makes it ache more."* He stepped inside, closing the door behind him. The alcove was small — a cot, a trunk, the floorboard secret. He filled it with his presence, broad-shouldered despite the injury, his dark hair falling over a forehead creased with something that might have been pain or might have been thought. *"The bridge."*

*"Gone."*

*"I watched from the loft window. The last span — it went down like a dying horse. Slow. Heavy. Final."* He moved to the window, pressed his good hand to the cold glass. *"My village is on the other side. Or maybe it's this side. I don't remember anymore. The border moved three times since 1912. First the Turks, then the Bulgarians, then the Serbs, then the Austrians. Each time, the bridge stayed. The river stayed. The mountains stayed. Only the lines on the maps changed."*

He turned, and his eyes met hers — dark, depthless, the same pale gray as Dragan's, as the river in spring.

*"Now the bridge is gone. The lines don't matter. The river decides."*

*"The river always decided,"* Petra said quietly. *"We just pretended otherwise."*

Ilya smiled — a crooked expression that transformed his face from harsh to something almost boyish. *"You speak like my baba. She said the Danube remembers every hand that dipped in it. Every body it carried. Every bridge that tried to chain it."* He shrugged, wincing as the movement pulled his shoulder. *"She also said the Germans would lose because they don't know how to make proper rakija. So her prophecy is... mixed."*

Petra let out a breath that might have been a laugh. *"Your baba sounds wise."*

*"She died in 1913. Cholera. The war before this war."* His face closed. *"She didn't live to see her grandson — my sister's boy — conscripted by the Austrians. He's fourteen. They took him anyway. 'Total war,' they called it. 'Every hand for the Fatherland.' Which Fatherland? The one in Vienna? The one in Budapest? The one in Sofia? The one in Belgrade?"*

He took a step closer. *"You write them down. The names. The ones they erase. I saw you. In the night. The pencil scratching."*

Petra's hand went to her apron pocket. The pencil stub. The paper scrap. *"I write what I see."*

*"Do you write me?"*

The question hung between them, large as the valley.

*"You are not erased, Ilya. You are here. You have a bed. You have rations. You have a name — three names."*

*"Do I?"* He laughed, short and sharp. *"Three names. Three nations. Three armies. Which one is mine? The Serbian boy who fought at Cer? The Croatian who deserted at Drina? The Bulgarian who surrendered at Kladovo? I was all of them. I am none of them. The only name that fits is the one the officers don't use."*

*"Which is?"*

*"Ilya. Just Ilya. No surname. No nation. No number."* He held up his good hand, fingers spread. *"Five letters. Two syllables. The name my mother gave me before the world broke into pieces and demanded I choose a side."*

Petra studied him. The ledger pressed against her spine through the floorboard, a phantom weight. *Ilya. Just Ilya.*

*"What would you have me write?"* she asked.

He was silent for a long moment. The wind screamed against the granary walls. Somewhere in the ward, a man coughed — the wet, tearing cough that meant typhus or tuberculosis or simply lungs worn out by gas and cold.

*"Write this,"* Ilya said finally. *"Ilya. Witness. Kladovo. November. I saw what happened. I did not stop it. I could not stop it. But I saw. And I remember."*

*Kladovo.* The name struck Petra like a physical blow. The massacre Captain Hoss would investigate — the one that hadn't happened yet, the one the inquiry hadn't arrived for. The one Petra had only heard in fragments: *a village, a unit, civilians, an order.*

*"You were at Kladovo."*

*"I was there."* His voice was flat, stripped of the crooked humor. *"I was the one who didn't shoot. I was the one who watched. I was the one who ran — not away, but toward. Toward the river. Toward the bridge that was still standing then. I crossed it. I came here. I have been waiting for the bridge to close ever since."*

*"Waiting?"*

*"For the truth to be trapped with me. For there to be no escape. For the only way out to be through."* He met her eyes, unflinching. *"You have the ledger. You have the names. You have the truth. Now you have the witness. What will you do with us, Sestra Okonkwo? What will you do when the spring comes and the ship arrives and the world demands its accounting?"*

The question was enormous. It carried the weight of the valley, the river, the war, the forty-three names beneath the floorboard, the six men dying in the ward, the boy on Bed 31 who wanted his mother to light a candle for a hero.

Petra thought of her father in Onitsha, reading the Niger's skin. *The river remembers what the maps forget. It keeps its own counsel.*

She thought of the ledger. The pencil stub. The workshop by the water where Mirko built boats that would never sail.

She thought of the ship on the morning tide — a rumor, a hope, a deadline.

*"I will write them all,"* she said. *"Every name. Every truth. Even the ones that hurt. Especially those."*

Ilya nodded, once. The crooked smile returned, thinner now, edged with something that might have been trust. *"Then I will help you carry them. When the time comes. My arm may not work, but my legs do. My voice does. My memory does."*

He turned to leave, then paused at the door. *"Mirko's workshop. The hollow keel beneath the workbench. Third plank from the bow. I know. I've seen it. I've sat there, nights, when the ward was too loud and the ghosts too close. He doesn't know I know. Or maybe he does. Mirko knows everything."*

The door closed behind him.

Petra stood alone in the alcove. The floorboard beneath her feet felt different now — not a hiding place, but a foundation. The ledger's weight pressed against her spine through the wood, a second heartbeat.

*Forty-three names. Forty-three lives. One witness who chose to stay. One bridge gone. One river frozen. One valley sealed.*

She knelt, pried up the board, opened the ledger to the next blank page.

The pencil found the paper.

*Petrović, Dragan. Private, 2nd Serbian Infantry. Age 24. GSW chest, sucking wound — trench raid 12 Jan, location unreported. Admitted 13 Jan. Witness: P. Okonkwo. Status: Critical. Name omitted from morning report 14 Jan by order of Capt. Milić.*

*Vasić, Marko. Private, 2nd Serbian Infantry. Age 21. GSW abdomen — same raid. Admitted 13 Jan. Witness: P. Okonkwo. Status: Critical, typhus suspected. Name omitted from morning report 14 Jan by order of Capt. Milić.*

*Jovanović, Luka. Private, 2nd Serbian Infantry. Age 19. Skull fracture, rifle butt — "disciplinary action" 13 Jan. Admitted 13 Jan. Witness: P. Okonkwo. Status: Unconscious. Name omitted from morning report 14 Jan by order of Capt. Milić.*

Three names. Three boys from the same raid. Three erasures by the same captain.

She wrote on.

*Popescu, Ioan. Civilian laborer, 5th Romanian Labor Battalion. Age 38. Crushed chest, supply wagon accident — driver intoxicated. Admitted 14 Jan. Witness: P. Okonkwo. Status: Dying. Morphine administered 14 Jan 1400 hrs. Name never entered in any register.*

*Huber, Franz. Corporal, 14th Jäger Battalion, Austro-Hungarian Army. Age 29. Bayonet wound kidney — refusal to execute civilian hostages, Kladovo sector, 10 Nov. Admitted 11 Nov. Witness: P. Okonkwo. Status: Stable. Name listed as "evacuated" 12 Nov; actually concealed in ward. Officer responsible: Hauptmann Klaus von Ritter.*

*Kolev, Dimitar. Private, 3rd Bulgarian Infantry. Age 17. GSW back — Lt. Ivan Georgiev, desertion attempt. Admitted 14 Jan. Witness: P. Okonkwo. Status: Critical. Name listed as "KIA 13 Jan" in battalion report; actually wounded and captured.*

*Kovács, István. Private, 4th Honvéd Infantry, Austro-Hungarian Army. Age 26. GSW thigh — combat, sector unknown. Admitted 14 Jan. Witness: P. Okonkwo. Status: Stable post-op. Name listed as "evacuated 14 Jan" in morning report; actually remains in Bed 14.*

Seven names. Seven truths.

She turned the page. The next line waited, blank and hungry.
