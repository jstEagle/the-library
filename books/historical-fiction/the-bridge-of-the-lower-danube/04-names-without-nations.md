## 4. Names Without Nations

The first thing I felt was the weight of the linen cloth that Petra wrapped around my shoulders, not a cloak of honor but a simple, damp shawl that had seen better days. The granary‑hospital smelled of stale grain and antiseptic, a mingling of the earth that had once stored wheat and the chemicals that now tried to keep the wounded alive. I lay on a wooden bunk, the slats cold beneath my back, and watched the thin ribbon of sunlight that slipped through the high, narrow windows like a pale finger tracing the walls. The air was thin, the kind of thin that makes you feel as if you are breathing through a sieve, and each breath tasted of iron and the faint, metallic tang of blood that clung to the floorboards even where they were not my own.

Petra’s hands were gentle, calloused from years of stitching, but they moved with a precision that bordered on reverence. She lifted my wrist, feeling the pulse that thumped against her palm like a drumbeat in a distant village. Her eyes, dark and steady, held no pity, only a quiet understanding of the stories each patient carried beneath their bandages. She spoke to me in a soft, measured tone, her words a mixture of English and the Nigerian pidgin that seemed to slip naturally from her lips, as if the language itself were a living thing that adapted to the cold of the room. “You’re a good man, Ilya,” she said, her voice barely above a whisper, “even if the world refuses to name you.”

I tried to answer, but my throat was dry, my mouth a desert of cracked lips. The only sound that rose from me was a shallow cough, a sound that echoed off the plaster and returned to me as a reminder that I was still here, still breathing, still alive in a place where death walked the corridors like a familiar friend. I watched as she adjusted the bandages, her fingers tracing the bruised tracks of my ribs, feeling for the subtle rise and fall of my chest. The world seemed to shrink to the rhythm of her movements, to the soft rustle of the linen, to the faint ticking of a clock somewhere above the cobbled streets of the valley.

Petra’s name had become a thread that wove through the chaos of the war, a thread that held together the fragments of humanity that drifted through the granary. She was Nigerian, born in a land of rivers and markets, but here she tended to men who spoke in Serbian, Hungarian, Romanian, German, and more languages than I could count. She carried with her a ledger, a secret book where she recorded the names of the wounded that the officers’ reports omitted, a ledger that was already a kind of prayer for those who had been forgotten. I could see the faint outline of its leather cover peeking from beneath her arm as she moved, a small, dark object that seemed to pulse with the weight of countless lives.

I tried to ask her about the ledger, but the words tangled in my throat. “What is that?” I finally managed, my voice hoarse as the wind that slipped through the cracked windows. “A list,” she said, her eyes reflecting the flickering light from a single oil lamp. “A list of names that the war will try to erase. It is a promise that we will not forget.” She placed a gentle hand on my forearm, her palm warm against the chill that seeped into my bones. “Every name matters, even if the world says otherwise.” The simplicity of her words cut through the fog of my feverish thoughts, and I felt a strange kinship with the act of recording, with the idea that a name could be a shield against oblivion.

I tried to think of my own name. It was Ilya, a name that had been given to me by my mother when she first held me in her arms, a name that meant “light” in the old Slavic tongue. But I was also known as Ilija, a variation that slipped from the mouths of the Serbian villagers, and as Alexandru, a name that my father, who had once been a Romanian teacher, had given me before the war took us both. There were also whispers of a German name, Friedrich, that some officers had called me when they needed a convenient label for the men who manned the bridge. I was three nationalities in one, and none at all, a paradox that seemed to suit the broken landscape around me. The war had a habit of stripping away identities, of reducing men to numbers, to uniform colors, to the simple label of “enemy” or “ally.” Yet here, in this cramped space, I was more than a number; I was a collection of names, a tapestry of languages that Petra seemed to understand without needing to speak them all.

She brought me a bowl of thin broth, the steam rising in pale curls that danced like spirits in the dim light. The broth was thin, almost transparent, but it carried the scent of carrots and parsley, a reminder that even in this place of death, there were attempts at nourishment. I took a sip, and the warmth spread through my throat, a small victory against the cold that gnawed at my ribs. As I ate, Petra sat beside me on a low stool, her knees drawn up, her hands clasped gently in front of her. She watched the way I ate, the way my fingers trembled as they held the bowl, the way my eyes flicked to the ceiling and back again, searching for something that was not there.

“I have heard you speak of your many names,” she said softly, her voice a gentle current that seemed to carry the scent of the river outside. “Do you think any of them matter more than the others? Or does it not matter which you choose when the world is trying to erase you?” She paused, as if waiting for an answer that might never come, and then she added, “I write down names because they are the only thing that remains when everything else is taken away. A name is a thread you can hold onto, a rope that can pull you out of the darkness.”

I thought about the thread she spoke of. In my village, before the war, we had a tradition of naming children after the seasons, after the river, after the stars. My mother had chosen “Ilya” because she believed that light would always find its way, even in the darkest night. My father had added Alexandru because he loved the sound of it, the way it rolled off the tongue like a promise. And yet, here I was, a man whose identity had been sliced and diced by the war, by the officers who needed to file paperwork, by the nations that claimed me and then discarded me like a discarded uniform.

Petra’s eyes, which had been steady, softened as she looked at me. “Do you want to choose one name to keep?” she asked, her voice barely audible over the whisper of the wind that slipped through the cracks in the granary. “Or would you rather carry them all?” She held out her hand, her fingers curling around the edge of the bowl, as if she were trying to grasp something intangible.

I thought of the ledger, the secret book that Petra kept hidden beneath the floorboards, the one that recorded the names of the wounded whose stories the officers omitted. I thought of the mothers who whispered prayers for their sons, the fathers who trudged through the snow to find their loved ones, the wives who waited in villages that had become ghost towns. I thought of the dead, whose names were written on paper but whose faces were never seen again. The ledger was more than a list; it was a testament to the lives that refused to be erased, a testament that Petra held in her hands, a testament that I, in my own way, wanted to be a part of.

I leaned forward, my voice barely more than a breath. “Petra,” I said, “what is the difference between a name and a lie?” I could feel the heat of the broth fading, replaced by a cold realization that words could be weapons as sharp as bayonets. “If I say I am Serbian, but I also feel Romanian inside me, and the Germans call me Friedrich—am I lying when I say one name? Or am I simply saying that I am many things at once?”

She smiled, a small, almost imperceptible smile that seemed to light up the dim room. “No one is a lie,” she said. “We are all a collection of stories, of moments, of choices. A name is a story, and a story is a life. When you say ‘I am Ilya,’ you are not denying the other names; you are simply acknowledging one part of yourself. When you say ‘I am Alexandru,’ you are honoring another part. When you say ‘I am Friedrich,’ you are remembering the way the world saw you, even if that view was flawed. All of those names are true, Petra. They are the fragments that make up who I am, even if the world refuses to see them together.”

I felt a strange calm settle over me, as if the weight of the ledger, the weight of the war, the weight of the many names I carried had finally been balanced. The broth, now cold, sat untouched on the wooden table, a reminder of the physical needs that had been ignored in favor of the spiritual. Petra’s hands remained warm on my forearm, a small, steady presence that seemed to anchor me to something solid in a world that was crumbling around me.

She stood, stretching her limbs, and glanced at the door, the sound of distant footsteps echoing down the corridor. “The inquiry is coming,” she said, her voice low, as if she were afraid the words might attract unwanted attention. “Captain Verena Hoss will be here soon. She will need the names, the stories. She will need the ledger.” She paused, then added, “And I will need you, Ilya. You are part of the ledger, part of the story. I cannot leave you out.”

I tried to speak, but my throat was still raw, my voice a whisper that seemed to dissolve in the air. “What will happen if we give them the ledger?” I asked, my eyes fixed on the faint glow of the oil lamp. “Will it save us? Will it save them?” I gestured with my hand toward the ceiling, where the thin beam of sunlight struck a patch of dust, turning it into a miniature galaxy of specks.

Petra knelt before me, her eyes level with my own. “It will not save us,” she said, her voice soft but resolute. “But it will make them see what they cannot ignore. It will be a witness, a proof that the war is not just a series of orders and reports, but a series of lives. It will be a chance for someone, somewhere, to hear the names we have tried to bury. It will be a chance for the truth to leave the valley, even if it means the ledger must be hidden, even if it means someone must risk everything.”

I thought of the ledger, the secret book that Petra kept hidden beneath the floorboards, the one that recorded the names of the wounded that the officers omitted. I thought of the mothers who whispered prayers for their sons, the fathers who trudged through the snow to find their loved ones, the wives who waited in villages that had become ghost towns. I thought of the dead, whose names were written on paper but whose faces were never seen again. The ledger was more than a list; it was a testament to the lives that refused to be erased, a testament that Petra held in her hands, a testament that I, in my own way, wanted to be a part of.

She placed a hand over mine, her palm warm against the chill that still clung to my skin. “You are part of this,” she said. “You are part of the ledger, part of the story. Even if the world does not want to hear your name, we will hear it. Even if the war says you are nothing, we will say you are everything.”

The wind outside grew louder, rattling the shutters and sending a chill through the granary. The oil lamp sputtered, casting shadows that danced across the walls like restless spirits. I felt the weight of the ledger, not as a physical object, but as a promise, a promise that Petra had made to herself and to the men who lay wounded around us. I felt the weight of the names, each one a thread that bound us together, even as the war tried to tear us apart.

Petra stood, her shoulders squared, her eyes steady. “We will keep you safe,” she said, her voice a low murmur that seemed to carry the scent of the river and the distant mountains. “We will keep the ledger safe. We will keep the names safe. And when the time comes, we will let the truth out, even if it means everything we know will change.”

I closed my eyes, feeling the warmth of her hand on my forearm, feeling the rhythm of my heartbeat against her palm. In that moment, I understood that the ledger was not just a book, but a bridge, a bridge between the lives that were being erased and the lives that refused to be forgotten. It was a bridge that Petra had built with her own hands, a bridge that I, Ilya, was privileged to cross, if only for a moment, in the quiet of the granary hospital.

The door creaked open, and a figure stepped into the room, her silhouette framed by the flickering light of the oil lamp. Captain Verena Hoss stood in the doorway, her uniform crisp, her eyes sharp, her commission evident. She moved toward the bunk, her steps measured, her presence a stark contrast to the softness of Petra’s world.

Petra stepped back, her hand instinctively reaching for the ledger that lay hidden beneath the floorboard, her fingers hovering over the leather cover. “Captain Hoss,” she said, her voice steady, “you have come for the ledger. I will give it to you, but only after we have gathered the names of those who have not yet spoken.”

Hoss inclined her head, a gesture that seemed to acknowledge the gravity of the moment. “You have done enough, Petra,” she said. “You have tended to the wounded, you have recorded their names. Now we need to ensure that the truth is not lost. The ledger must be protected, but also shared. It must reach those who can act upon it.”

Petra’s eyes softened, a flicker of relief and resolve crossing her face. “I have kept it hidden,” she said, her voice low. “But the workshop by the river is where I keep it now. Mirko, the boatwright, knows its secret. He will protect it until the ship arrives, until the tide carries it downriver. Then the ledger will be free, and the names will be heard.”

Hoss nodded, her expression thoughtful. “We cannot leave it in the hands of the officers,” she said. “They will burn it, they will hide it, they will erase it. But the ledger must be free, even if it means risking everything.”

Petra’s hand tightened around the edge of the bunk, her knuckles white. “We will risk everything,” she said, her voice a low, steady chant. “We will risk our lives, we will risk our freedom, we will risk the truth itself. Because a name is more than a word; it is a life.”

Hoss took a step forward, her eyes scanning the room, taking in the faces of the wounded men, the pale lights, the faint scent of antiseptic. She saw the ledger peeking from beneath the floorboard, its dark cover a silent witness to the stories it contained. She understood that the ledger was more than a record; it was a weapon, a shield, a beacon.

Petra turned to face Hoss, her expression fierce yet tender. “We will give you the ledger,” she said, her voice a soft but unyielding resolve. “We will give you the names, the stories, the truth. And when the ship sails on the morning tide, the ledger will leave the valley, and the names will be heard beyond the river. Even if we stay behind, the truth will live on, because a name cannot be erased, not when someone refuses to let it be.”

Hoss took a deep breath, feeling the weight of the moment settle over her like a cold blanket. She knew that the ledger would be a catalyst, a spark that could ignite change, even if it meant risking her own commission, her own safety, her own future. She understood that Petra’s secret ledger was not just a collection of names; it was a testament to the humanity that persisted in the darkest of times, a testament that would be used to hold the powerful accountable, to give voice to the voiceless, to ensure that the war crimes would not be forgotten.

Petra placed a hand on Hoss’s arm, her fingers warm against the chill of the steel uniform. “We will work together,” she said, her voice a low whisper that seemed to carry the echo of the river’s current. “We will protect the ledger, we will protect the names, we will protect the truth. And when the ship arrives, we will let it go, even if it means staying behind, even if it means losing everything we have fought for. Because the truth is the only thing that can save us.”

Hoss nodded, her eyes reflecting the flickering light of the oil lamp. “Then we will do it,” she said, her voice steady. “We will protect the ledger, we will protect the names, we will protect the truth. And when the ship sails on the morning tide, we will watch it leave, knowing that the valley will never be the same again, because the names will live on, beyond the river, beyond the war, beyond the silence.”

The wind outside grew louder, rattling the shutters and sending a chill through the granary. The oil lamp sputtered, casting shadows that danced across the walls like restless spirits. Petra’s hand remained on Hoss’s arm, a silent promise that bound them together, bound them to the ledger, bound them to the names that would soon be free.

As the wind howled outside, Petra felt a strange calm settle over her, as if the storm that raged around them was a reflection of the turmoil within, a turmoil that could be stilled by the promise of truth. She looked at Hoss, at the ledger hidden beneath the floorboard, at the wounded men lying in their beds, at the empty spaces where names should have been written, and she felt a surge of determination that seemed to echo through the very walls of the granary.

Petra turned to Hoss, her eyes bright with resolve. “When the ship arrives on the morning tide,” she said, her voice soft but fierce, “we will give you the ledger. We will give you the names. We will give you the truth. And we will stay behind, because the people we have come to belong to need us here. The ledger will leave the valley, but we will stay, because we have learned that history happens wherever someone refuses to let names be erased.”

Hoss nodded, her expression a mixture of admiration and resolve. “Then we will do it,” she said, her voice steady. “We will protect the ledger, we will protect the names, we will protect the truth. And when the ship sails on the morning tide, we will watch it leave, knowing that the valley will never be the same again, because the names will live on, beyond the river, beyond the war, beyond the silence.”

The wind howled outside, rattling the shutters, but inside the granary, a quiet certainty settled over them, a certainty that the ledger would find its way to the ship, that the names would be heard, that the truth would finally leave the valley, even if it meant staying behind. The storm outside seemed to fade, replaced by a sense of purpose that resonated through the very stones of the granary, through the very breaths of the wounded men, through the very hearts of Petra and Hoss.

As the night deepened, the oil lamp flickered one last time, casting a final, lingering glow over the faces of those who had dared to hope, who had dared to record, who had dared to protect. The ledger, hidden beneath the floorboard, waited, its dark cover a silent promise that the truth would one day be free, that the names would one day be heard, that the war would one day be judged, not by the men who had ordered the atrocities, but by the ledger that would carry the names of those who had suffered, of those who had survived, of those who had died.

The wind outside grew quieter, as if the very sky had taken notice of the promise made within those walls. The storm seemed to recede, leaving behind a sense of calm that was both fragile and unbreakable. Petra felt the weight of the ledger in her mind, the weight of the names that had been recorded, the weight of the truth that would soon be set free. She felt the weight of her own life, the life she had left behind, the life she had chosen to live among the wounded, among the people she had come to belong to.

She looked at Hoss, her eyes reflecting the flickering light of the oil lamp, and she felt a strange tenderness, a tenderness that had been forged in the fires of war, a tenderness that was both fragile and unbreakable. She felt a tenderness for the names that would soon be free, a tenderness for the truth that would soon be set free, a tenderness for the people she had come to belong to, a tenderness that would keep her alive even as the storm outside continued to rage.
