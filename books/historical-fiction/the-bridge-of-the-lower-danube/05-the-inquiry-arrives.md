## 5. The Inquiry Arrives

The ice on the Lower Danube had thickened to a skin of iron grey, pressing down on the granary hospital like a held breath. Inside, the stove in the ward burned through the afternoon, sending plumes of amber smoke through the rafters where dust motes danced in the slanted light. Petra moved between the rows of cots, her boots silent on the wooden floor, a tray of gauze and tincture in one hand, a ledger propped against her hip like a prayer book she wasn't sure she believed in. The morning report had already been filed, the carbon paper pressed and the names transcribed into the official ledger that smelled of ink and obedience. But Petra had her own carbon, her own pen, and in the quiet spaces between amputations and fever spikes, she recorded what the officers omitted: the conscripts who had no papers, the serfs who had been promised land and got mud, the men whose tongues swelled with gangrene before they could tell her their surnames.

She had begun keeping the ledger six months ago, after the first field dressing station had been overrun and the Austrian officers had simply listed the dead as "casualties, unknown." Petra had been born among the rivers of the Niger delta, where names were not erased but sung, preserved in the breath of the community. Here, across continents and wars, she found that silence was the true wound. Every entry in her secret ledger was a name reclaimed from the void.

The granary door creaked open, and the cold rushed in, carrying the scent of woodsmoke and wet wool. Petra looked up to see a woman in a tailored coat of dark wool, her hair pulled severe and back, eyes sharp behind gold-rimmed spectacles. Captain Verena Hoss. War-crimes investigator, dispatched from some headquarters that had likely never heard the creak of a hospital bed or the rattle of a dying lung. Hoss carried a leather satchel and an aura of authority that felt imported, alien, like a soldier arriving in a village she had no business being in.

"Petra Okonkwo?" Hoss said, her voice clipped, translating the name as if it were a formula.

"That is what they call me." Petra set down her tray. The stove hissed. "And you are?"

"Captain Verena Hoss. Judge advocate, Office of the Inspectorate." Hoss stepped over the threshold, shaking the snow from her boots. "I was told the bridge is closed, the valley cut off. I was told there is a hospital here that knows more than it says."

Petra inclined her head. "The river keeps its own counsel. We keep ours as best we can."

Hoss surveyed the ward. A Serbian conscript moaned in his sleep, his leg wrapped in blood-stained rags. A Romanian farmer with a shrapnel wound to the abdomen stared at the ceiling, his chest rising in shallow, ragged rhythm. The patients were a mosaic of uniforms and civilian clothes, a chaos the official reports tried to smooth into neat columns. "Immaculate anonymity," Hoss murmured, more to herself than to Petra. "That is what the Major calls it. He says it makes the paperwork cleaner."

"Someone has to tend the men," Petra said. "Even if their names disappear."

Hoss nodded, and something in her expression softened—just for a fraction of a second—before hardening again. "I am to inspect the records. Standardize them. The Major thinks the ledger is a routine administrative matter. He is mistaken." She glanced at Petra. "I was also told there is something… hidden. In this hospital. A ledger. Of names."

The words landed like a stone dropped into still water. Petra felt her hands grow still. "I do not know what you mean, Captain."

Hoss studied her. "Perhaps. But I have orders to find it. And I have eyes that have seen too many standardized reports." She moved toward the back of the granary, where a curtain separated the wards from a small, cluttered room that served as the administrators' office. "May I?"

Petra hesitated. Then she stepped aside. "It is your inspection, Captain. But the back room is clutter. Ledgers of the living, ledgers of the dead. Nothing unusual."

Hoss pulled the curtain aside. The room was small, lit by a single oil lamp, its walls lined with shelving that held tins of sterilized equipment, maps of the Danube delta, and a thick ledger bound in brown calfskin. It sat open on the desk, pages filled with cramped, looping handwriting. Petra felt a spike of something sharp and private in her chest. She had left that ledger open, absently, while she went to tend a man who had been brought in from the ice-choked river, his body already half-frozen. She had been called away to the amputation table, and in her haste, she had not closed it.

Hoss crossed the room. She did not touch the ledger immediately. Instead, she picked up a folded sheet of paper from the desk's edge—a ration return, perhaps, or a casualty report. She unfolded it. Petra watched her face as the light caught the ink.

Hoss's eyes moved down the page. Petra could see the tension in her jaw, the way her nostrils flared. She read the names, the ranks, the wounds. Then she turned the page. And the next. Her lips pressed together, pale and thin.

"These are not routine entries," Hoss said finally. Her voice had lost some of its clipped precision. "These are individual men. Specific wounds. Dates." She looked up. "Whose hand is this?"

"Mine." Petra's voice was steady, but her fingers curled into the fabric of her apron. "I keep a record. For myself. For the men who cannot speak."

Hoss returned the page to the ledger and closed it with a soft sound. "And why?"

"Because the officers' reports omit." Petra met her gaze. "Because history is written by those who survive the writing. I thought—perhaps if I recorded them, they would not be forgotten. Even if no one else remembers."

Hoss was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke again, her tone had shifted, the war-crimes investigator giving way to something rawer. "I, too, have kept records. Not of the living, but of the dead. Of things the high commands would rather the earth swallowed." She met Petra's eyes. "My own ledger was confiscated once. Burned, actually. For 'administrative simplicity.'" A ghost of a smile crossed her face. "I know the particular brand of erasure you describe."

Petra nodded. "You understand, then, that a name is not just a label. It is a claim."

"I understand that names are the first thing erased when the powerful decide a thing did not happen." Hoss's gaze drifted to the window, where the ice pressed against the glass like a frozen sea. "But I also know that investigators who chase ghosts often become the ghosts themselves. I was sent here because the Major's report on the Kladovo incident was… insufficient. Too clean. Too silent." She turned back to Petra. "You have something. One page. Perhaps more. I mean to see it all. But I did not expect to find it so… quietly kept."

"A quiet thing," Petra said, "for quiet times."

The oil lamp flickered, and the shadows stretched long across the stone floor. Outside, the wind picked up, rattling the panes. The bridge, they had said, would not reopen until the spring thaw, and the armistice inquiry that had brought Hoss to this valley was stranded along with the rest of them. The ledger, Petra's quiet act of defiance, had become a point of convergence for two women who had learned, in different wars and different uniforms, that the official record was a river that ran uphill if you wished it hard enough.

"I will need access," Hoss said. "To your records. To the men who can speak. To anything that does not appear on the Major's forms."

Petra considered her. The ledger was hidden under the floorboard of the workshop, wrapped in oilcloth and tucked beneath Mirko's boat tools. But she had left this page open, exposed, as if testing the waters, as if believing that some truths were too large to remain submerged. "You may look," she said. "But you must understand—these men are not statistics to me. They are husbands, sons, brothers. I have tended them. I have washed their faces. I have listened to their last words."

"I know." Hoss's voice was gentler now. "That is why I am here. Not to catalog dead men, but to understand how the killing happened. To find the gaps where the truth should be." She reached out and laid a hand on the ledger's cover. "May I copy this page? And then another? And another? I promise—what leaves this room will not end in the same erasure."

Petra looked at the woman's hand, at the gold rings, at the serious lines around her mouth. She thought of the Niger, of the names her mother had spoken over boiling yams, of the way her own father's name had been called at a ceremony he never attended. She thought of the Serbian conscript whose name she had recorded that morning, a boy of seventeen who had never seen a map of his own country. She thought of the ledger under the floorboard, the names she had no right to keep, and the even greater right she felt she had.

"Very well," Petra said. "But not all at once. The Major grows suspicious. And—"

"And what?"

"There are things in that ledger I have not shown anyone. Not even myself." Petra's jaw tightened. "Some entries are… messy. Incomplete. I was afraid to write too much, afraid the paper would betray me."

Hoss nodded. "Then we start with this one. And we go slow. I have time. The bridge is closed, remember? I am stuck here as much as you are." She smiled, and this time it reached her eyes. "Perhaps we can keep each other company. While the river freezes."

The lamp swung slightly as Petra moved to the desk, pulling out a fresh sheet of paper and a pen. She uncapped the pen, dipped it into the inkwell, and wrote the date at the top: *23 January 1917*. Then she turned the ledger toward Hoss.

The captain's eyes scanned the first entry: *Stojan Jovanović, age nineteen, Serbian conscript, wound to the thigh, admitted 14 January, evacuated to no-man's-land at dawn*. Beneath it, in Petra's careful hand: *Name recorded because no one else would. Wound infected. Father a farmer in Šabac. Mother dead of cholera last year.*

Hoss inhaled slowly. "He was supposed to be listed as 'unknown.'"

"He was supposed to be nothing." Petra's voice was quiet. "But he was someone. And I remembered."

Hoss closed the ledger gently. "Then we begin with Stojan. And we go from there." She met Petra's gaze. "Tell me about the others. The ones you have not shown anyone."

And so, in the warm glare of the oil lamp and the cold silence of the valley beyond the glass, two women—one a nurse who had learned that history was made in the spaces between official accounts, the other an investigator who had learned that the most dangerous truths were the ones omitted—began to build an alliance forged in the flickering light of a granary hospital, on the wrong side of a frozen river, where names were the only currency that mattered.

* * *

The next morning, the bridge remained a memory of broken pontoons and ice-choked water. Hoss had been given a small table in the back room, and Petra had begun bringing statements during the lulls between surgeries. The ledger sat open on the desk, pages filled with names that the morning reports called "casualties," "unknown," "found dead." Each one had a story now: where they were from, what they had loved, what they had said in their last moments.

Ilya Petrov, a man who claimed to be Polish, then Ukrainian, then Russian, and finally just "a soldier of no nation," had been the second entry. Petra had asked him his name while she cleaned a wound to his shoulder, and he had laughed, a dry, cracked sound, and said, "I have three passports and no home. Which name do you want?" She had written *Ilya Petrov, claimed nationality: none*. Later, she had added, *But he had a mother in a village outside Kyiv who prayed for snow every night.*

Hoss had listened, pen in hand, filling a separate notebook with the details Petra gave. The investigator's own ledger— the one she had kept in the shadows of the Office of the Inspectorate—had grown thin with the names of massacres she had uncovered, the gaps where the official record had gone silent. Now, in this frozen granary, she was filling those gaps again, not with the names of the dead, but with the names of the living who had been erased.

"The Major will have my head when he sees this," Hoss said one evening, as the wind howled around the granary and the stove sent up a thin column of blue smoke. "Standardizing the records. Purging the 'extraneous.' That is his phrase. Extraneous."

"People are not extraneous," Petra said, stitching a tear in a uniform sleeve. "They are the point."

"Perhaps." Hoss looked at the ledger. "But the people in charge think otherwise. They think the war is a number, and the dead are just digits. I have seen men awarded medals for battles that never happened, and men erased from history for wounds that did." She traced a finger over the page. "Stojan. Ilya. And there are more. Twenty-three names so far. And I have no doubt there will be more before the spring."

"Twenty-three men who the world has decided do not exist." Petra knotted the thread. "But they exist to me. And you. And apparently, now, to the ledger."

"Is that enough?" Hoss asked. "To exist for two people in a valley no one is meant to care about?"

Petra stopped sewing. She looked at the captain, at the war-crimes badge on her coat, at the exhaustion etched into the lines around her eyes. "I do not know if it is enough. But it is all I have. And perhaps, if enough people exist for enough people, the world will have to notice."

Hoss was quiet. Then she stood, crossing to the window. Outside, the Danube was a sheet of steel, the bridge timbers gone, the far bank invisible. "I have a theory," she said. "About why some names are kept and others erased. It isn't just about power. It is about inconvenience. A dead man with no name is a statistic. A dead man with a name is a story. And a living man with a name who can tell the story of how he died—that is a threat."

"A threat to what?" Petra asked.

"To the narrative that says this war was necessary, that the losses were justified, that the line was held." Hoss turned back. "That is why the Major wants the records standardized. Purged. He does not want stories. He wants silence."

Petra felt a chill that had nothing to do with the winter outside. "And you?"

"I want the truth." Hoss's jaw set. "Even if the truth is uncomfortable enough to get me recalled, or worse. But I also know that I cannot do it alone. Not in a place like this, where the bridge is closed, the river is frozen, and the only people who know what happened are the ones the world has already decided are disposable."

She walked back to the desk and pulled out a chair, sitting across from Petra. "So. We keep the ledger between us. Slowly. Carefully. And when the ice breaks, when the ship comes, we send it away. To somewhere that remembers."

"And if it never reaches anywhere?" Petra asked.

Hoss was quiet for a long moment. Then she met Petra's eyes. "Then we have at least tried. And perhaps, in the trying, the world will become too small to keep erasing us."

The oil lamp flickered, and the ledger's pages rustled in the draft. Outside, the wind rose, and the ice groaned against the shore. Inside, two women who had learned to distrust institutions were building something neither of them had expected: a quiet, stubborn alliance, forged in the space between a nurse's secret record and an investigator's hidden ledger, in a valley cut off from the world, where names were the only thing that could not be buried.

* * *

That night, Petra dreamed of the Niger, of her mother's voice weaving names into the dark, of the way the river had curved and twisted, never straight, never still. She dreamed of Stojan, of Ilya, of the men whose names she had written in the dim light of the granary, and of the ledger that sat under the floorboard of Mirko's workshop, safe for now, but always one search away from discovery.

When she woke, the oil lamp had burned low, and the frost had begun to lace the windowpanes with thin, delicate patterns. Hoss was still at the desk, her own ledger open, pages filled with the names Petra had given her. The captain looked tired, but her eyes were bright with a fierce, quiet purpose.

"Morning," Petra said, stretching. "The stove needs wood."

Hoss looked up. "I was just thinking. About what you said. About names being a threat."

"And about them being a claim?" Petra asked.

"A claim." Hoss closed her notebook. "I think you are right. A name is a claim on the truth. And the truth, once claimed, cannot be unclaimed easily."

"Then we claim as many as we can." Petra stood, pulling her apron tied around her waist. "I have another entry today. A French colonial infantryman, wounded in the head. He spoke of home in a way I did not expect. I wrote it down."

"Send it here." Hoss stood as well, gathering her things. "I will add it to my growing collection. Perhaps, between us, we will have enough names to make a difference."

They moved through the ward together, Petra tending to the men, Hoss observing, asking quiet questions, taking note of things that did not fit the official narrative. The days shortened, the sun a low, watery disk that touched the ice and vanished. The bridge remained closed, the valley trapped in its own quiet, and the ledger grew, page by page, name by name, a quiet revolution written in ink on paper that the world had decided did not exist.

And in the back room, with the lamp burning and the ice pressing against the glass, Petra Okonkwo and Captain Verena Hoss were building something neither of them had expected: a record of men the world had tried to forget, and the two women who refused to let them be erased.
