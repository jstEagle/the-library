## 6. What the Officers Omit

The lamp in the granary’s back room did not flicker. It burned with a steady, greedy hiss, drinking kerosene and spitting a cone of yellow light onto the table where two women sat surrounded by the paper ghosts of Kladovo.

Petra Okonkwo’s fingers were stained black to the second knuckle. Ink, carbon, the dust of old ledgers — it lived under her nails now, a second skin. She had scrubbed until the water ran gray, but the smell clung: iron gall and river mud and something sweeter, older, like dried plum paste her grandmother pressed into tins. The smell of records that refused to stay buried.

Across from her, Captain Verena Hoss moved through the ration returns with the precision of a surgeon. She did not read; she excavated. Each page turned was a layer of soil brushed aside. *Flour: 420 kilos. Salt pork: 18 barrels. Hardtack: 1,200 units. Issued 14 November. Returned spoiled: 3 barrels pork, 200 units hardtack. Spoilage rate: 14%.* She murmured the numbers, a litany. Her pencil, a stub of blue graphite, scratched marginalia in a cipher Petra had not yet learned.

“Fourteen percent,” Hoss said, not looking up. “In a garrison that size, with supply lines that short? That’s not spoilage. That’s diversion. Or it’s men who never came to collect.”

Petra’s throat tightened. She reached for the ledger she had brought from beneath the floorboard — its cover warped, its binding held by surgical tape and prayer. The pages she had filled in the weeks since the bridge closed: names, dates, wounds, the small particulars that made a man more than a casualty code. *Milan Jovanović, 22, shrapnel left thigh, fever 39.2, mother’s name Ana, village of Brestovac. Died 0300 hours. No report filed.* She had written it in the dark, by the light of a candle stub, while the ward breathed around her.

She opened the ledger to the Kladovo pages. The script was cramped, urgent, each entry a small defiance.

“Read them to me,” Hoss said. “Slowly. I’ll match them to the gaps.”

Petra began. Her voice was low, roughened by the ward’s dry air and the cigarettes she didn’t smoke but inhaled secondhand from the orderlies. “Dragomir Petrović. Eighteen. Conscripted from a village that doesn’t exist on any map the Austrians printed. Gunshot wound, chest. Died before the surgeon saw him. The major’s report lists ‘light casualties, morale high.’”

Hoss’s pencil moved. *Petrović, D. — cross-reference: no pay record after 12 Nov. No next-of-kin notification sent.*

“Lazar Stanković. Thirty-four. Farmer. Shrapnel to the abdomen. He lived three days. Told me his wife was pregnant with their first. He asked me to write her a letter. I did. I put it in the outgoing bag. The bag was burned ‘for fuel’ on the major’s orders.”

*Stanković, L. — ration draw stops 15 Nov. Wife’s name: Jelena. Village: Kladovo proper. No pension file opened.*

“Two brothers, the Markovićs. Nikola and Stefan. Sixteen and nineteen. They lied about Stefan’s age. The recruiting sergeant winked. Both took a shell in the same trench. Nikola died screaming for his mother. Stefan died silent, holding his brother’s hand. The report says ‘two enlisted men, KIA, sector 4.’”

*Marković, N. & S. — single ration card issued for ‘N. Marković’ only. Second card never created. Fraud or mercy?*

The list went on. Thirty-seven names Petra had pulled from the silence of the dying ward. Thirty-seven names the morning reports had swallowed. Each one a stone in her pocket, weighing her down, keeping her from floating away into the anonymity Reeve prized.

Hoss worked without pause. She cross-referenced the ration ledgers — three thick volumes bound in cracked leather, smelling of mice and damp — with the hospital admission logs Petra had smuggled out of the clerk’s office, with the burial detail’s tally sheets Mirko had filched from the engineer’s shed, with the fragmentary telegrams the radio operator had copied before they were censored. She built a scaffold of numbers and dates, and Petra hung the flesh of memory on it.

“Here,” Hoss said, tapping a column in the ration book. “Fourteen November. The day the 3rd Battalion was rotated out. Rations drawn for 840 men. But the hospital admitted 112 wounded from that sector in the preceding seventy-two hours. The burial detail logged 63 graves dug. The telegram to division reports ‘light skirmishing, minimal losses.’” She looked up, her face sharp in the lamplight. “That’s a discrepancy of nearly two hundred men. Two hundred men who ate, fought, bled, and vanished.”

Petra felt a chill that had nothing to do with the frost seeping through the granary’s stone walls. “They didn’t vanish. They were erased. One by one. By clerks who knew exactly what they were doing. By officers who signed without reading. By a system that counts bullets but not breath.”

Hoss’s jaw tightened. “Or a system that counts breath only when it’s convenient. When the breath belongs to the right nation, the right regiment, the right side of the line.” She turned a page. “Kladovo wasn’t a battle. It was a culling. The 3rd Battalion — mostly Serbian conscripts, some Romanian, a handful of Roma — was ordered to hold a salient the high command knew was untenable. They were left without artillery support, without reserves, without withdrawal orders. For three days. The ration records show the supply wagons stopped arriving on the 12th. The men ate leather, bark, each other’s rations. And when the relief column finally came — ” She stopped. Her pencil hovered.

“When it came?” Petra prompted.

“It didn’t come for them. It came for the weapons. The wagons that arrived on the 15th carried crate after crate of ammunition. No food. No medical supplies. The quartermaster’s receipt: ‘Ordnance recovered, sector 4. Personnel status: N/A.’” Hoss’s voice went flat. “N/A. Not applicable. As if the men were equipment that had already been written off.”

Petra’s hand found the ledger’s edge, gripping it until her knuckles whitened. “I held Stefan Marković’s hand while he died. He gave me a button from his coat. Said his mother would know it. Said she’d know he hadn’t run. I have the button in my coat pocket. It’s brass, stamped with a crown that doesn’t exist anymore. That’s not N/A. That’s a son.”

Hoss was quiet for a long moment. The lamp hissed. Somewhere in the granary, a patient coughed — a wet, racking sound that spoke of lungs filling with fluid. The river, frozen solid a hundred yards away, groaned as ice shifted beneath the weight of snow.

“Then we give them back their names,” Hoss said. “All of them. Every single one. We build a record that cannot be burned, cannot be ‘lost in transit,’ cannot be redacted by a major who thinks anonymity is hygiene.” She met Petra’s eyes. “We make it so complete, so undeniable, that when the ship comes — if the ship comes — the world will have to look at Kladovo and see men, not numbers.”

Petra nodded. The motion felt like a vow. “I have more names. In the floorboard. In my head. In the pockets of the coats I haven’t had the heart to give to the burial detail.” She hesitated. “There’s a boy. Ilya. He’s awake now. He remembers things. He was there. At Kladovo. He says — ” She stopped, the words catching on something raw.

“He says what?”

Petra looked at her hands, ink-stained, callused from years of lifting bodies, changing dressings, holding the weight of men who slipped away. “He says the order to hold came from Belgrade. Or maybe Vienna. He says the officers who stayed behind — the ones who ate the officers’ rations while the men ate bark — they had maps showing the relief column’s route. They knew it wasn’t coming for the battalion. They knew it was coming for the guns. He says Major Reeve was there. At the forward command post. He says Reeve watched the wagons unload ammunition and turn away.”

The silence that followed was heavier than the granary’s beams. Hoss’s pencil snapped. The sound was a tiny crack in the night.

“Reeve,” Hoss repeated. The name was a stone dropped in a well. “Our major. The man who inspects this hospital and calls it ‘immaculately anonymous.’ He was at Kladovo.”

“Not as a patient,” Petra said. “As a staff officer. Ilya recognized his face from a photograph in a discarded newspaper. The one with the medal ceremony. Reeve stood beside the Crown Prince. He was decorated for ‘exemplary logistical coordination under fire.’” Petra’s voice curled with a bitterness she rarely allowed herself. “Coordination. That’s what they call it when you feed guns and starve men.”

Hoss stood abruptly. The chair scraped loud on the stone floor. She moved to the window — a narrow slit high in the wall, covered with oiled paper instead of glass. The lamp light caught the edge of her jaw, the tension in her shoulders. “If Reeve was at Kladovo, and he knows what happened there, and he knows *we* know…” She turned back, her face pale in the dimness. “Then the lamp burning late isn’t just an annoyance to him. It’s a threat.”

Petra followed her gaze to the window. Outside, the hospital compound lay under a muffling blanket of snow. The sentry’s hut by the gate showed a thin line of light. Footprints — fresh, boot-heeled — led from the main building toward the back room’s external door. They stopped at the threshold. Then turned back.

“He knows,” Petra said. Not a question.

“He suspects,” Hoss corrected. “Which is worse. Suspicion can be managed. Knowledge requires action.” She returned to the table, gathering the ration books, the telegrams, the burial tallies. Her movements were quick now, efficient. “We need to move the primary evidence. The ledger. The cross-referenced sheets. Everything that ties Reeve to Kladovo. Tonight.”

“Tonight?” Petra’s pulse jumped. “The workshop. Mirko’s workshop. He said — ”

“Mirko’s workshop is the first place Reeve will look if he decides to search.” Hoss shook her head. “He knows Mirko. He knows Mirko’s sons died in this war. He knows Mirko has no love for officers. The workshop is compromised the moment Reeve thinks to ask.”

“Then where? The river’s frozen. The bridge is gone. The only way out is the ship, and that’s weeks away — if the ice breaks.”

Hoss’s eyes gleamed in the lamplight. “We don’t move it out. We move it *under*. The granary’s foundation sits on old Ottoman vaults. There’s a cellar the engineers sealed when they converted the building. The entrance is in the mortuary — behind the false wall where they stack the coffins.” She saw Petra’s expression. “No one goes in the mortuary unless they’re carrying a body. And even then, they don’t linger. It’s the one place in this valley where an officer’s curiosity stops at the door.”

Petra exhaled slowly. “The mortuary. The dead protecting the living’s truth. There’s poetry in that. And blasphemy.”

“There’s survival in it,” Hoss said. “Which is the only poetry that matters right now.” She began stacking papers, aligning edges with sharp taps. “Help me. We’ll take the ledger, the Kladovo reconstruction, the ration extracts. The rest — the hospital logs, the admission books — we leave. Let Reeve find his ‘immaculate anonymity’ in the official record. The truth travels light.”

They worked in a silence that was not empty but charged, each movement deliberate, each paper handled as if it were a sacrament. Petra’s ledger — the original, the one with the floorboard creases and the coffee stain on page twelve and the pressed wildflower between the pages for the Marković brothers — went into a oilcloth satchel Hoss had brought. The reconstruction sheets, Hoss’s neat cipher script mapping the massacre’s anatomy, followed. The ration book pages, torn out with a penknife (a small violence that made Petra flinch), were folded and tucked beside them.

The satchel was heavy. Not just with paper. With the weight of thirty-seven names, and the hundreds more waiting in the ration gaps, and the thousands that would never be known because no one had bothered to write them down.

“Ready?” Hoss asked, strapping the satchel shut.

Petra nodded. She blew out the lamp. The darkness that followed was absolute, textured only by the smell of ink and kerosene and the distant cough of a dying man.

* * *

The mortuary was cold in a way the wards never were. The wards held the heat of living bodies, the steam of poultices, the warmth of breath. The mortuary held only the cold the dead brought with them from the river, from the trenches, from the frozen ground where they had lain before the burial detail found them.

It smelled of pine resin and lime chloride and something older — the scent of earth that has swallowed too many winters. Stacked against the far wall, the coffins were rough pine, unplaned, their joints gaping. Some were occupied. The lids sat beside them, waiting for the morning detail.

Petra had been here often. She knew the rhythm of this room: the way the orderlies joked to keep the silence at bay, the way the priest crossed himself before each closing, the way the names on the crude wooden tags — burned in with a hot iron — were often guesses. *Unknown Serb. Unknown Romanian. Unknown.* She had added her own tags, slipped inside the coat pockets: *Milan Jovanović. Lazar Stanković. Nikola Marković. Stefan Marković.*

Tonight, the room was empty of the living. The false wall — a section of planking behind the bottom tier of coffins, disguised as the back of the shelving — gave way with a sigh of old nails. Hoss had found it on her second day, probing the masonry with a pocketknife. Behind it yawned a space the size of a wardrobe, floored with flagstones, walled with brick that had not seen light in a century.

They lowered the satchel into the dark. Petra’s fingers brushed the cold brick as she let go. A goodbye. A committal.

“It’s done,” Hoss whispered, replacing the planks. She pressed the edges until the gaps vanished. “Now we go back. We sleep. We wake. We do our jobs. And we wait for the ship.”

Petra nodded, though her throat was too tight for speech. She followed Hoss back through the granary’s corridors, past the wards where the night nurses moved like shadows, past the clerk’s office where the official logs slept in their locked cabinet, past the major’s door.

It was closed. But a line of light showed beneath it.

They froze.

The light was thin, yellow. Steady. Not the flicker of a candle. A lamp. Burning late in the major’s quarters.

Hoss’s hand found Petra’s wrist, fingers cold and strong. *Don’t move. Don’t breathe.*

A sound from within. A chair scraping. Boots on floorboards. The creak of a door opening.

They retreated silently, pressing into an alcove where the shadows were deepest. The major’s door opened fully. Light spilled into the corridor — brighter than the ward lamps, the light of a man who has fuel to burn while his patients freeze.

Major Aldous Reeve stepped out. He wore his greatcoat over his nightshirt, the collar turned up, a wool cap pulled low. In his hand he carried a lantern — not the standard issue, but a brass officer’s model, polished to a shine that caught the corridor’s dimness. He looked neither left nor right. He walked with the measured stride of a man on a known path.

He headed for the external door. The one that led to the compound, to the sentry’s hut, to the workshop by the river.

Petra felt Hoss’s grip tighten. *Follow?*

She shook her head, barely perceptible. *Too dangerous. He’ll hear us.*

They waited until the outer door closed, until the lantern’s glow receded across the snow-covered yard, until the sentry’s challenge — low, routine — faded.

Then Hoss released her. “The workshop,” she mouthed.

Petra nodded. They moved back to the back room without speaking. The lamp was still unlit. The papers they had left on the table — the hospital logs, the admission books — sat exactly as they had been. But the chair Hoss had used was pulled out slightly. The pencil stub lay snapped in two.

Reeve had been here. He had read. He knew.

* * *

The next morning dawned the color of bruised iron. The sun, a pale coin dropped in a gray sky, gave no warmth. The river lay white and silent, its current a rumor beneath feet of ice.

Petra moved through the wards on autopilot. Changing dressings. Feeding broth to men whose hands shook too hard to hold spoons. Writing the morning report — the official one, the one Reeve would sign. *Ward 3: 12 patients. 3 critical. 2 improved. 1 deceased overnight. Cause: wound infection. No unusual occurrences.* She wrote it with her left hand, her right still cramped from the night’s copying.

The deceased was a boy named Petar. Sixteen, maybe. He had no tag. She had given him one in the mortuary: *Petar, no surname known. Died 0400. Fever. Burial 1000.* She had slipped a scrap of paper into his pocket with his true name, if he had ever told her. He hadn’t. He had only cried for his *mama* in a dialect she didn’t know.

At 0900, the major’s orderly appeared at the ward door. “Nurse Okonkwo. The major requests your presence. In his office.”

Petra wiped her hands on her apron. The starch scratched her raw skin. “Tell him I’m with a patient. I’ll come when I can.”

The orderly, a young Austrian named Klaus who had once given her a chocolate bar from a Red Cross parcel, looked uncomfortable. “He said ‘now,’ Nurse. He used the word ‘inspection.’”

Petra glanced at the boy in the next bed — a Serbian corporal with a shattered femur, reading a dog-eared novel in German. Their eyes met. He gave an almost imperceptible nod.

“I’m coming,” she said.

The major’s office was warm. A stove burned anthracite, the smell sharp and clean. Maps covered the walls — the Danube’s loops, the Carpathian passes, the rail lines that fed the front. A desk, orderly as a parade ground. Reeve sat behind it, uniform impeccable, cap on the rack. He did not look up as she entered.

“Nurse Okonkwo. Close the door.”

She closed it. The latch clicked loud.

“You’ve been with us since October,” Reeve said. Still not looking up. He was writing something — a letter, perhaps, or a report. His pen moved smoothly. “Before that, Salonika. Before that, a British hospital ship in the Mediterranean. Before that…” He paused, pen hovering. “Your file says Lagos. Nursing school. Mission hospital. Quite a journey for a woman of your… background.”

Petra stood at attention, her spine rigid. “Major. My background is nursing. The journey was necessary.”

“Necessary.” He finally looked up. His eyes were the color of the river in winter — pale, depthless. “We all have our necessities, Nurse. Mine is order. This hospital runs on order. The reports are filed on time. The rations balance. The dead are buried with the correct paperwork. Anonymity, as I’ve said, is a form of mercy. It spares families the details. It spares the service the… complications of individual grief.”

“Mercy, sir.” The word tasted like ash.

“Yes. Mercy.” He set down his pen. “But mercy requires trust. Trust that the system works. Trust that the records reflect reality. Trust that no one… *supplements* the official account with private… projects.”

Petra’s heart hammered a steady, dangerous rhythm. Her face remained impassive. She had practiced this face in the mirror of her mother’s dressing table, in the reflection of the ship’s porthole, in the dark glass of the granary’s windows. *I am a wall. I am a floorboard. I am the silence between heartbeats.*

“I don’t understand, sir.”

Reeve stood. He moved to the window, looking out at the compound. The sentry’s hut. The workshop’s roof visible beyond the granary’s bulk. The river, a white scar.

“Last night,” he said, “I walked the lines. As I do. A commander must know his ground, even ground he’s held for months. I saw a light in the back room. Past curfew. Past the blackout hour.” He turned. “The lamp was out when I tried the door. But the chair was warm. The ink on the table was still wet.”

Petra said nothing. Her hands, clasped behind her back, gripped each other until the knuckles ached.

“Captain Hoss was seen entering that room yesterday,” Reeve continued. “She has been seen entering it frequently. She has access to the ration stores, the burial records, the signal logs. She is an investigator. Her commission is… broad.” His lip curled. “Investigators investigate. That is their nature. But sometimes they investigate the wrong things. Or the right things in the wrong way. Or they bring people into their investigations who have no business being investigated.”

“Captain Hoss is here by orders of the High Command, sir. Her commission is — ”

“Her commission is a piece of paper,” Reeve interrupted sharply. “Paper burns. Paper gets lost. Paper can be… reinterpreted. What matters is what serves the service. What matters is that this hospital continues to function without… *distraction*.” He stepped closer. “You are a good nurse, Okonkwo. The best I’ve seen in this theater. You have a way with the men. They trust you. They tell you things. Things they wouldn’t tell a doctor, or a chaplain, or an officer.” His voice dropped, intimate and terrible. “That makes you valuable. It also makes you dangerous. If you were to… *misplace* that trust. If you were to let a captain’s curiosity lead you into places where the light doesn’t reach.”

“Sir, I — ”

“No.” He raised a hand. “No explanations. No denials. I am not a fool, Nurse. I know what happens in the back room. I know what the captain is building. And I know what you carry in that ledger of yours — the one you think is hidden so cleverly.” He smiled then, a thin, cold expression. “But I also know that you are a nurse. Not a spy. Not a politician. You want to save lives. You want to ease suffering. That is your necessity. And I respect it.”

He reached into his desk drawer and pulled out a folded paper. Held it out to her.

“This is a transfer order. Effective immediately. You are reassigned to the forward dressing station at Iron Gate. It’s a hard post. Cold. Dangerous. Underfire most days. But it needs a nurse of your caliber. A nurse who knows how to… *manage* records. How to keep things… *anonymous*.”

Petra took the paper. Her fingers trembled, just once. The ink on the page was fresh. The stamp was wet. *Major A. Reeve, Commanding. Authority: Operational Necessity.*

“Iron Gate,” she repeated. “That’s… forty kilometers upriver. The road is closed. The river is frozen.”

“A sled leaves in an hour,” Reeve said. “You’ll be on it. Your things are already packed. The orderly will escort you.”

“And the ledger? The names?”

Reeve’s smile vanished. “The ledger doesn’t exist. You know that. I know that. Captain Hoss… suspects. But without you, without your access, without your particular knowledge of which floorboard, which mortuary vault… it’s just a captain’s theory. Theories can be dismissed. Theories can be buried.” He leaned in. “Go to Iron Gate, Nurse Okonkwo. Do your duty. Save your men. Keep your head down. And when the spring comes, and the ship sails, you’ll be far from here. Far from the inquiry. Far from the consequences.”

He straightened. “Dismissed.”

Petra turned. Her hand on the door latch. She paused.

“Sir,” she said, her voice steady. “The boy who died this morning. Petar. He had no name on his tag. I gave him one. In my ledger. In the mortuary. Where the light doesn’t reach.” She opened the door. “Mercy, you called it. Anonymity. But mercy isn’t forgetting, Major. Mercy is remembering. Even when the system tells you not to.”

She stepped out into the corridor. The cold hit her face like a slap.

Behind her, the major’s pen scratched on paper. *Nurse Okonkwo transferred. Operational necessity. No further action required.*

* * *

The sled was a rough farm wagon on runners, pulled by a pair of shaggy horses that blew steam in the freezing air. The driver, a corporal named Haas, smoked a pipe and said nothing. Petra sat in the back among her few possessions: a trunk, a medical bag, the coat with the brass button in its pocket.

The granary receded. The wards where she had learned the sound of each man’s breath. The back room where the lamp had burned. The mortuary where the satchel lay beneath the false wall, guarded by the dead.

The workshop by the river came into view. Mirko’s place. Smoke curled from its chimney — a good sign. Mirko was alive. The workshop stood.

But as they passed, Petra saw something that made her blood run cold.

A squad of engineers. With axes. With saws. With barrels marked *PETROLEUM*.

They were not repairing the workshop.

They were preparing to burn it.

* * *

The sled did not stop. Haas cracked the whip, the horses lunged, and the granary, the hospital, the valley fell away behind them.

Petra clutched the brass button in her pocket. She thought of Ilya, still in the ward, waiting to give his testimony. Of Hoss, who would wake to find her ally gone and the workshop — their fallback, their fortress — marked for destruction. Of Mirko, who had lost two sons and gained a daughter of the heart, now watching his life’s work doused in petrol.
