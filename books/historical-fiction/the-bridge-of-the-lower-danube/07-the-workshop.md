## 7. The Workshop

The granary hospital was cold even by the standards of a Danube winter that had long since ceased to surprise. The snow had settled into a brittle crust that cracked under the wheels of the supply carts, and the river below the windows had turned from flowing water to a slow, iron groan of shifting floes. Petra had spent the better part of the morning in the converted granary, her fingers stained with ink and the faint metallic tang of blood she had long since stopped trying to wash away. The ledger sat on her lap, a thin notebook she had begun six months prior, a secret ledger of names that the morning reports omitted, the casualty lists that erased men as efficiently as a pencil erases a sketch. She had started it out of habit, really, a way to keep track of the Serbian conscripts and Romanian volunteers and the occasional German prisoner who passed through her triage table. But habit had turned into something else, something she could no longer name without her throat tightening.

She closed the book and looked up. The granary was full of the wounded and the dying, the moans of men who had not been given names in any official record blending into a low, continuous undertone. Outside, the world was a monochrome of white and gray, but inside, the stove in the corner threw out orange light that danced on the plaster walls. She stood, stretching her shoulders, and looked at the ledger. The page was nearly full. Every name she recorded felt like a stone placed in a pocket, heavy and unnecessary until the moment came when those stones might build a wall no one could climb over.

She had meant to hide it. She had known, from the moment the first officer had frowned at her neat handwriting and asked why she bothered recording men who would not be counted, that the ledger had to leave the hospital. But where? The valley was watched. The officers' reports flowed upward to headquarters, and those reports carried the weight of erasure. Petra had no authority, no rank that could protect a book of names. She needed a place beyond the reach of the quartermaster's ledgers, beyond the inspection of Major Reeve, whose polite smiles and casual inspections had begun to feel like the closing of a door.

The workshop.

It was not far from the hospital, a small shed on the riverbank run by Mirko, a boatwright who had once built passenger vessels for the summer trade between Belgrade and Budapest. Now, in the winter of 1916, the shed was a skeleton of ribs and tarpaulin, the floor covered in shavings that smelled of pine and salt and something older, something Mirko carried in the quiet of his own chest. Petra had passed the workshop many times on her way to and from the front lines, nodding at a man whose face was as weathered as the hull of an old barge, whose hands moved with a slow, deliberate grace over pieces of wood that had already survived more than a century.

She had not spoken to him more than a greeting, until now.

The snow crunched under her boots as she walked the path that wound between the hospital grounds and the river. The ice was thickening along the banks, a skin of white that creaked and shifted with the breath of the water below. She kept her collar turned up, her breath a thin plume in the air. The workshop was dark when she arrived, the only movement the slow swing of a door that had not been opened in days, perhaps weeks. Mirko was not inside. The scent of woodsmoke and river water was stronger than she expected, and she realized with a jolt that the shed was not as abandoned as it had seemed from the road.

She pushed the door open and stepped into the dim light. Shavings covered the floor in drifts, and along the walls leaned hulls-in-progress, their ribs curving like the spines of great beasts. A small stove smoked in the corner, and against the far wall, a row of tools hung on a pegboard, their edges dulled by decades of use. And there, in the center of the room, sitting on a pile of offcuts and wrapped in a greased cloth, was a notebook.

Petra's hand went to the ledger in her own coat, a protective gesture. "I—"

A voice from the shadows. "You are the nurse. The one who writes."

Mirko emerged from behind a tarp, his face a map of lines that the cold and the war had etched there. He was an older man, his hair white as the snow outside, his eyes the color of a winter sky just before a storm. He wore a wool sweater that had seen better decades, and his hands were stained with oil and something darker. "I know you. You ask after the men no one remembers."

"I record them," Petra said. Her voice was steady, but the ledger was suddenly pressed against her ribs, alive with the weight of every name she had written. "Because someone has to."

Mirko nodded, once, a small gesture that seemed to cost him nothing and everything. "The river takes what it will. The paperwork takes what it wants. I have my own ledger, you know. Names of my sons. Two of them, gone to the eastern front, the southern front, somewhere the maps do not name. I keep them on a piece of paper in my coat pocket. I ask no one to read it. I ask no one to judge."

Petra looked at him, really looked, and saw the exhaustion beneath the stoicism. "Then you understand."

"I understand that history is what is left behind when the shouting stops." Mirko crossed the room, his movements slow but sure, and pulled out a chair. It creaked in protest, a sound that seemed to echo in the quiet workshop. "Sit. You have something to hide."

The ledger was placed on the table between them. Its cover was worn, the pages filled with a hand that was careful, deliberate, each name followed by a rank, a regiment, sometimes a home village if Petra had been able to pry it from a delirious or dying man. There were Serbian names, Romanian, German, and a handful of Russian officers who had been brought in after the chaos of a retreat. Some entries were just a surname and a date; others had full sentences—*Died screaming that his mother did not know he was here*, *Asked only for a letter to be sent to a town he had not seen in five years*. Petra had stopped trying to categorize the grief; she had long since stopped believing that any format could contain it.

Mirko picked up the notebook, his fingers tracing the edge of a page. "This is not a ledger of the dead," he said slowly. "This is a ledger of the unseen."

"It is what the officers omit," Petra said. "What the reports do not say."

"Men are more than the holes they leave in paperwork." Mirko's voice was rough, but there was a gentleness in it now, as if the admission of understanding had opened a space between them. "My sons were more than numbers in a casualty list. They were the ones who repaired the roof when the rain came, who whistled while they worked, who had faces I cannot forget even now."

Petra felt a lump form in her throat. She had not expected this. She had expected suspicion, perhaps, or a cold dismissal. Instead, she found a man who had already been carrying his own ledger of loss, one he had kept hidden from the world, just as she had. The recognition was a bridge, fragile and sudden, spanning the gap between two people who had both learned that the world did not care to remember certain names.

"I have a hiding place," Mirko said, setting the notebook down. "Under the floorboards. The shed has not been floored properly since the ice last winter, and the wood is soft enough that no one will think to look. But you must promise me something."

"Anything."

"If they find it, if the Major or any other officer decides the workshop is a navigation hazard or a place for storage or—" He stopped, his jaw tightening. "I will not be the one who gives you up. But I cannot protect you forever. You must understand that."

"I understand." Petra's grip on the ledger tightened. "I will not ask you to risk more than you already have."

Mirko studied her face for a long moment, then nodded. "Very well. The floorboards are loose near the back, where the boat ribs are stored. The shavings will cover the book. No one enters this shed but me, and I have my reasons for keeping to myself."

He rose from the chair and moved to the back of the workshop, where a section of floorboards had indeed been lifted, the wood darkened by age and moisture. He pulled up a board, revealing a hollow space beneath, dry and sheltered from the damp of the riverbank. Into this space, Petra slipped the ledger. It fit snugly among the pine shavings that littered the gap, and she replaced the board with a careful hand, ensuring it lay flat, indistinguishable from the others. The scent of pine and old oil rose around her, grounding her, making the ledger feel less like a secret and more like something that belonged here, in this place of wood and silence and the river's slow, relentless movement.

When she stood, Mirko was watching her. "You will come back?" it was not a question.

"I will," Petra said. "When I can."

She turned to leave, and Mirko did not follow her to the door. Instead, he stood by the workbench, his hands resting on the curved edge of a boat rib, and she thought she saw his shoulders relax, just a fraction, as if the weight of her presence had become something he could carry, too.

The days that followed were a blur of cold and routine and the quiet, accumulating weight of the ledger that now lay beneath the floorboards of Mirko's workshop. Petra continued her work in the granary, her days filled with the sounds of moans and the rustle of bandages, the clatter of instruments, the endless tally of men who came in and went out—some walking, some not. She recorded every name she could, even as the morning reports grew thinner, as if the officers themselves were beginning to understand that something was being withheld from them.

The workshop became her refuge, a place she visited in the gaps between shifts, when the light was failing and the hospital was quiet enough that she could slip away without drawing attention. Mirko was usually there, working on a piece of wood or sitting by the stove, his presence a quiet counterpoint to the chaos of the hospital. They did not speak of the ledger often, but its existence hung between them like the scent of pine, undeniable and comforting.

One evening, as the sun dipped below the frozen river and the sky turned the color of bruised plum, Petra found Mirko outside the shed, standing at the water's edge. The ice had pushed close to the bank, a chaotic heap of frozen panels that creaked and shifted in the growing dark. Mirko held a bundle of rope in his hands, and the smell of tar and fish skin clung to him.

"Winter is nearly upon us," he said, not turning around. "The crossing will close soon. The bridge will be the last thing to go."

Petra stood beside him, her gloved hands tucked into the sleeves of her coat. "I know."

"The ledger—" Mirko started, then stopped. He turned, and in the fading light, Petra could see the lines around his eyes deepen. "You have taken on more than a nurse's burden."

"I have taken on a history that was being erased." Petra's voice was steady, but she felt the ledger's weight shift against her ribs, a living thing now, a collection of lives that refused to be forgotten. "Someone has to record them. Someone has to ensure they are not simply gone."

Mirko was quiet for a long while. When he spoke again, his voice was softer, the edges of the grief she had glimpsed earlier smoothing out into something more manageable. "My father used to say that a name is a candle. If you light it, it burns for a time, but the wind will always try to snuff it out. If you hide the candle, the darkness grows around it, but the flame survives longer."

"I understand the metaphor," Petra said. "But I am not sure I believe in candles. I believe in records. In print that can be read, reread, passed hand to hand until the names are no longer just Petra's or Mirko's, but everyone's."

"And if the world does not want to read them?" Mirko asked. He took a step closer to the water, his boots crunching on the thin ice that had formed along the shore. "What then?"

"Then we keep reading them ourselves." Petra looked at him, really looked, and saw a man who had spent a lifetime learning how to survive by making himself invisible, and yet, in this moment, he had chosen to be seen. "Then we become the readers. The keepers. The ones who refuse to let the names be erased."

Mirko turned back to the river, and after a moment, Petra joined him. They stood in silence as the light faded, the river below them a dark, restless thing, the ice groaning and shifting as if in conversation. The workshop was behind them now, warm and sheltered, but the ledger—the secret, the risk, the promise of a truth that might yet reach the outside world—hung between them like the faint, persistent glow of a star that had long since died but whose light was still traveling.

"I will keep it safe," Petra said finally. "As long as I draw breath."

"And I will keep the floorboards loose," Mirko replied, a hint of a smile touching his lips. "And I will ask no questions. That is my part of the bargain."

They stood until the cold became too sharp, until their breath came in visible clouds that dissolved into the night. Petra left the workshop then, the ledger safe beneath the floorboards, the knowledge of Mirko's quiet sacrifice settling into the same space as her own resolve. She walked back to the granary hospital, the snow falling softly around her, each flake a small, silent erasure and also a small, silent recording, a name added to the world without a report to file it.

When she entered the granary, the wounded were still there, the moans still blending into the undertone of the room, but Petra no longer heard only noise. She heard individual names, individual lives, the quiet, stubborn truth that she and Mirko had agreed to protect, together, in exile.

The next morning, the bridge crossing was officially closed. Engineers detonated the ice-choked pontoon spans from a safe distance, the explosions echoing across the valley like distant thunder, and the road out of the valley shut for the season. The supply ship that had once come weekly, carrying mail and fresh provisions and the occasional officer with a new set of reports, would now not arrive until the ice broke in spring. The valley was trapped, sealed off by water and ice and the weight of a winter that showed no sign of ending.

Petra stood by the hospital window, watching the last of the bridge timbers being hauled away on sleds, the men in heavy coats shouting commands, the air filled with the smell of diesel and woodsmoke. Behind her, the granary was warm, the stove glowing, the wounded men settling into their places for another night. She thought of the ledger beneath Mirko's floorboards, of the names she had recorded, the ones she had not yet had the strength to write. She thought of Mirko's sons, of the ledger he kept in his coat pocket, of the quiet bargain they had struck.

The armistice investigation was due any day now, though no one in command seemed eager for it to arrive. Major Reeve's inspections had become more frequent, his manner more polished, his questions more pointed. He asked about the hospital's capacity, the number of beds filled, the flow of patients. He did not ask about the ledger, but Petra had learned that the things officers did not ask about were often the things they were most afraid of.

She returned to her station, picking up her ink and her notebook, and began to record the name of a Serbian conscript who had been brought in during the night, his body frozen on one side, his eyes open and unseeing. The morning report would not name him; the officers had already moved on to the next batch, the next report, the next erasure. Petra wrote his name anyway, and the act felt like a small, defiant prayer.

The ledger was growing, and with each name, the weight of it shifted, becoming less a burden and more a promise. Petra believed, now, that history happened wherever someone refused to let names be erased. She had learned that in the quiet of Mirko's workshop, in the space beneath floorboards and between the ribs of boats, in the silence shared between two people who had both known loss. The river would freeze, the bridge would fall, the officers would change, the reports would be rewritten—but as long as there were people like Petra and Mirko, willing to keep track, willing to remember, the names would not truly be gone.

She closed the ledger and placed it in her bag, the book a small, hard shape against her side. Outside, the world was turning white, closing in, but inside, in the space she had carved out with ink and resolve, the names lived on. And somewhere, beneath the floorboards of a boatwright's shed by the river, another name was being kept, another life remembered, another candle lit against the dark.

The valley would be trapped, but the truth, however small, however hidden, was already on the move.
