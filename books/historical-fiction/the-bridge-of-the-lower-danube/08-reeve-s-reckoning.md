## 8. Reeve's Reckoning

The major’s boots clicked on the wooden planks of the granary’s main floor, each step echoing like a metronome counting down to some unnamed verdict. Major Aldous Reeve had come to inspect the hospital’s paperwork, as he did every full moon, his coat dusted with the grey ash of the valley’s relentless hearth fires. He carried a clipboard and a manner of such profound bureaucratic calm that the wounded, propped on their straw pallets or draped in thin, frozen blankets, went still in their sleep, as if the mere presence of a form-filler could arrest the progress of their pain.

“Immaculate anonymity,” Reeve announced, his voice thin and dry in the granary’s vast, barn-like silence. He swept his gaze across the rows of cot-side tables, over the rows of moaning, shivering men whose limbs were held together by wire and will. “The records are… functional. Efficient. But they lack rigor. Standardization. That is what the front requires. That is what the chain of command demands.”

Petra knelt at the far end of the room, adjusting the blanket around a Serbian conscript whose chest rose in shallow, ragged bursts. She had learned, in the two years since she had crossed the Niger and the Alps to reach this frozen spit of Europe, that paperwork was the war’s quietest front. It was where men disappeared without a sound, where names were smoothed into numbers, where the dead were reduced to entries in a ledger that no one would ever read. She had begun her own ledger in the margins of a ration form, in the crook of a darkened corridor, when the morning report had omitted the name of a young man from Belgrade who had been brought in with a gut wound and a silence that stretched forever.

“Standardization means,” Reeve continued, not looking at her, “that redundant names are culled. That the ledger is trimmed to fit the telegraph’s capacity. That the hospital’s output matches the expectations of those who have never seen the inside of a field ward.” He paused, and for a moment the silence between them was filled only by the creak of the floorboards under the weight of the wounded. “I trust you will see to it, Nurse Okonkwo. The records will be… harmonized. By the week’s end.”

He swept out, the door banging shut behind him like a gunshot, and Petra remained kneeling, the warmth of the dying man’s skin seeping through the wool of her gloves. She looked down at the ledger lying open on her lap, its pages filled with names in a hand that was becoming increasingly a hybrid of English and the cadences of the languages she had heard in the ward: Hausa, Serbian, German, Romanian. Each name was a refusal. Each entry was a small, furious act of witness.

Night fell over the valley like a thick, suffocating blanket. The river below the granary’s shattered windows had begun to freeze in sheets, the ice shifting and groaning under the pressure of the current, pushing against the pontoon bridges that had been the valley’s only lifeline. Inside, the fever wards were alive with the sound of breath rattling in throats, the occasional cry of a man delirious from cold or wound, the soft rustle of sheets being adjusted by tired orderlies. The temperature inside the granary had dropped to near freezing, and yet Petra felt a different kind of heat rising within her, a fierce, radiant warmth that had nothing to do with the stove in the corner and everything to do with the ledger in her lap.

She picked up her pen, the nib cold against her fingers, and began to copy names from the morning’s report onto fresh pages of her secret ledger. The report, typed on thin, brittle paper, listed only numbers: Case 142, Case 309, Case 417. No names. No nationalities. No wounds that might betray a man’s origin. Petra’s hand moved with a memory of its own, translating the numbers into names she had come to know by the sound of their breath, the feel of their pulse, the particular way their fingers curled when they gripped the iron rails of their beds.

Ilya, the Polish-Lithuanian-Ukrainian conscript who had claimed three nationalities and none, whose tongue shifted like a compass needle depending on who was listening. Petra had written his name three times already, in three scripts, and each time she had erased it, afraid that the mere act of naming him might make him a target. Now she wrote it again, *Ilya*, and beneath it, the notation: *Claims three. Owns none.* The ink blurred slightly as her hand trembled, not from cold but from the effort of maintaining this double life, this secret geography of witness.

Around her, the fever spiked. A young French infantryman, his face a map of burns and bandages, began to mutter in his sleep, his hand gripping Petra’s arm with surprising strength. “Paris,” he whispered. “The bridge. They said it would hold.” His temperature was a dangerous 40.4, and Petra wiped his forehead with a cloth wrung out of ice-water from the bucket by the door. Another, a Turkish officer with a shattered femur, thrashed in his sleep, his eyes rolling back to reveal only the whites, and in his delirium he spoke of home, of the Bosphorus, of a city that no longer seemed to exist on any map she had seen. Petra moved from bed to bed, her apron stained with sweat and spittle, her own cheeks damp with the same thin, salted air that hung over the ward.

She worked by the dim light of a single oil lamp, its flame flickering against the soot-stained ceiling. The smoke from the lamp curled upward, a gray spirit that seemed to bind the room together, binding the living and the dying in the same breath. Outside, the wind howled against the granary’s walls, and the ice on the river cracked and shifted, a low, continuous rumble that vibrated through the floorboards and into Petra’s bones. She was exhausted, bone-weary, her eyes burning, her hand cramping around the pen, but as she wrote each name, as she traced the letters that would otherwise be erased, a strange transformation occurred. The exhaustion did not lift; instead, it became something else. It transmuted into devotion. Each name she wrote was a promise, a small monument built against the tide of forgetting.

Petra had begun this ledger believing that history happened elsewhere, to other people, in distant capitals and polished halls. She had believed that her place was in the quiet work of healing, that the war’s grand narratives were not hers to shape. But as she had watched men die without their names being spoken, as she had seen the officers’ reports strip the wounded of their identities, she had come to understand that history was not a single, relentless river flowing toward some predetermined sea. It was a series of eddies and backwaters, of individual choices made in the dark, of people who refused to let the current take them without a trace. She had become one of those people. The ledger was her eddy, her backwater, her quiet defiance against the erasure.

A knock at the door stirred her from her work. She looked up, the lamp’s flame casting long, dancing shadows across the walls. It was not Reeve; he did not knock. It was an orderly, his face pale in the lantern light, his uniform damp at the shoulders.

“Major Reeve requests your presence in the back room, Nurse. He says the records need your immediate attention.” The orderly’s voice was flat, but his eyes flicked nervously to the ledger on Petra’s lap, then away.

Petra set the pen down, the nib still dripping a small bead of ink onto the paper. She stood, her joints stiff from kneeling, and wrapped the ledger in a cloth, tucking it into the fold of her apron, against her chest where her heart beat its own fierce, steady rhythm. She followed the orderly through the dim corridors of the granary, past the rows of feverish men, past the place where the Serbian conscript still breathed his shallow, ragged breath. The back room was a small, cluttered space off the main ward, filled with stacks of forms, rusted medical instruments, and the ever-present smell of antiseptic and decay. Reeve was there, standing before a table littered with the morning’s reports, his back to the door as she entered.

“Nurse Okonkwo.” He did not turn around immediately. When he did, his expression was a mask of professional neutrality, but Petra could see the tension in the line of his jaw, the way his fingers tapped a rapid, impatient rhythm against the edge of the table. “We have a situation. The headquarters’ latest directive requires that all hospital records be reconciled with the central registry. By midnight. The ledger you maintain—” he gestured with a clipped motion toward the cloth bulge at her side “—must be surrendered for standardization. Purge the names that do not appear in the official reports. We cannot afford discrepancies when the inquiry arrives.”

Petra’s grip on the cloth tightened. “The ledger is a personal record, Major. It is not part of the hospital’s official output.”

“It is a ledger of the wounded,” Reeve said, his voice hardening. “And as of this moment, it is a liability. The command wants clean, uniform data. Not… anecdotes.” He stepped closer, his eyes searching hers. “You have been… cooperative, Nurse. I had hoped we could reach an understanding. But the deadline is firm. By midnight, the records must be standardized. That means the ledger, too. Or I will have it seized and its contents incorporated into the general files, where the names you have fought so hard to preserve will be lost to the bureaucracy’s indifference.”

The words landed in the room like stones dropped into a still pond. The feverish groans of the men beyond the door seemed to pause, as if the entire hospital held its breath. Petra looked at Reeve, really looked at him, and for the first time she saw not the faceless functionary of the morning inspection, but a man burdened by something he could not name. The lines around his eyes were deeper than she had thought, the skin beneath his eyes bruised with a fatigue that mirrored her own. He was afraid—of the inquiry, of the consequences of what had happened at Kladovo, of the ledger itself perhaps, or of the truth it contained.

“You speak of standardization,” Petra said softly, “but standardization is erasure by another name. Every name I have written is a man who has been erased once already. By the war, by the reports, by the men who send them.” She straightened her spine, her voice gaining the steel she had cultivated over two years of this work. “I will not surrender the ledger. It is the only record some of these men will ever have. I will copy it, by hand, into a form the command can accept, but the names will remain. Every one.”

Reeve stared at her, his jaw working as if he were chewing on a thought too large for his mouth. Then, with a sigh that seemed to exhaust him, he straightened his collar and turned back to the table. “Very well. You will transcribe the ledger into the standardized format. But the names must match the reports. If a name does not appear in the official record, it will be listed as ‘unidentified.’ Do I make myself clear?”

“Crystal, Major.” Petra inclined her head, a small, almost imperceptible movement. “I will begin immediately.”

She turned to leave, but Reeve’s voice stopped her at the door.

“Petra.”

She turned back. He was looking at the table, at the reports spread out before him, at the blank spaces where names should have been.

“Thank you,” he said, and the words were stripped of their usual bureaucratic sheen, stripped of the weight of command. “For what you are doing. For what you are trying to save.”

Petra nodded, and she left the back room, the cloth-wrapped ledger cradled against her breast, and walked back into the fevered, humming heart of the granary.

Night deepened. The wind outside had shifted, bringing with it a sharper cold that seeped through the cracks in the granary’s walls and settled into the marrow of the wounded. The lamp’s flame dipped and rose, a steady, companionable pulse in the dark. Petra found a clean table in the far corner, away from the worst of the drafts, and she set to work. She unfolded the ledger, its pages already filled with names she had written over the past months, and she began the painstaking work of transcribing each entry into the standardized form Reeve had demanded.

But she did not strip the names of their particularities. She added notes in the margins, tiny annotations that only she would understand: *Ilya, Polish-Lithuanian-Ukrainian, claims three, owns none. Sergeant Major Jovanović, Serbian, wounded at Kladovo, omitted from morning report.* She wrote each name with a care that bordered on the ritualistic, the pen moving across the paper in a slow, deliberate dance. The ink was cold, but her hand moved of its own accord, driven by a force that had grown beyond mere stubbornness. It was devotion now, fierce and absolute, a love for these men that had been forged in the crucible of shared silence and shared survival.

Around her, the ward was a symphony of suffering and endurance. A young man from the Punjab, his bandages soaked through with dark fluid, his breath coming in ragged, shallow bursts. An Italian alpine soldier, his feet frozen to the point of necrosis, his eyes bright with a delirium that saw his sister dancing in the snow. A French lieutenant, his arm amputated above the elbow, his stoicism a thin veil over a terror that he kept carefully locked behind his eyes. Petra moved from one to the next, her hand never stopping its writing, her ears attuned to the subtle changes in the men’s breathing, the shifts in their moans, the quiet prayers they whispered in languages she did not know but felt in her bones.

The fevers rose and fell like tides. Some men drifted into a silence that lasted until the morning, their bodies giving out under the combined weight of wound and cold. Others fought back, their temperature spiking, their hands gripping the rails, their eyes opening to meet Petra’s with a flash of recognition, of gratitude, of something that looked almost like hope. Petra wrote their names with the same reverence she gave to the dying, for she knew that to name a man was to assert his existence in a world that was desperate to make him disappear.

Hours passed. The oil lamp’s flame grew shorter, its smoke thickening, the room filling with a scent that was part burning oil, part antiseptic, part the faint, metallic tang of blood and sweat. Petra’s vision began to blur, the edges of her world fading into a soft, watercolor haze. She blinked, trying to clear her sight, but the effort required felt disproportionate to the small movement. Her hand, cramped and stiff, continued to move, the pen a natural extension of a will that refused to let go.

She reached the entry for the Serbian conscript, the first name she had ever written in this ledger, the one that had set her on this path of secret witnessing. She had written it in the margin of a ration form, under the flickering light of a hurricane lamp, the ink smudged by a trembling hand. Now she copied it again, the letters forming with a precision that surprised her, given the state of her body. *Petrović, Mihailo, Serbian conscript, gut wound, admitted 0300 hours, 12 December 1916, not listed in morning report.* She added a small cross beside his name, a symbol she had adopted for those who had died without their stories being told. She would go back to it later, when the ledger was safe, when she could mourn them properly.

Her eyelids grew heavy. The room seemed to tilt gently, the rows of cots and the faces of the wounded drifting in and out of focus. She felt a hand on her shoulder, light and almost ghostly, and looked up to see the Italian alpine soldier staring at her, his eyes bright despite the fever that consumed him.

“You write well,” he murmured, his voice thin and cracking. “The names. They stay.”

Petra managed a smile, the muscles of her face moving with difficulty. “They must. Someone must remember.”

“Yes.” He closed his eyes, and his breath slowed, the ragged edges smoothing into something closer to peace. “Someone must.”

Petra looked down at the page she had been working on. The ink was beginning to freeze in the nib, a thin, white filament stretching from the tip before snapping. She dipped the pen into the inkwell again, the motion stiff and awkward, and continued writing. She wrote until the page was full, until the ledger was filled with the names of men who had been erased by the war and the bureaucracy, and then she turned the page and began again. The night wore on, the cold outside finding new ways to penetrate the granary’s walls, the wind’s mournful howl becoming a constant, keening presence in her ears.

She wrote until her hand could no longer grip the pen, until the ink had run dry, until the paper itself began to warp under the moisture of her breath. She wrote until the first pale light of dawn crept through the high windows, casting long, blue shadows across the floor. And when she finally set the pen down, her hand trembling, her body swaying slightly, she looked at the ledger spread before her, a testament to the men she had come to love in the quiet, dark hours of the hospital’s night.

The ledger was full. Every name she had ever written, every name she had rescued from the void of the official reports, was there, written in her careful, determined hand. The pages were crowded, the margins filled with notes and symbols, the whole a dense, living thing that pulsed with the presence of the men it described. Petra leaned back in her chair, the room spinning gently around her, and felt a profound, almost spiritual exhaustion settle into her bones. It was not the weariness of defeat, but the weariness of one who has carried a great weight and found, against all odds, the strength to keep walking.

Outside, the river had frozen solid, the ice a vast, white plain that stretched to the horizon, broken only by the dark, restless water that still moved beneath the surface. The pontoon bridges, once the valley’s lifeline, now stood as skeletal structures, their pontoons encased in ice, useless now until the spring thaw. The hospital was a island of warmth and human connection in a world that had turned cold and indifferent, and Petra, exhausted, fever-tired, and fiercely devoted, sat at her table, the ledger open before her, and knew that she had done what she had set out to do. She had refused to let the names be erased. She had borne witness. And as the first rays of sun touched the ice outside, turning it from white to rose-gold, Petra closed the ledger, tied it with a strip of white cloth, and tucked it into the hidden compartment beneath the floorboard, the place where she had begun, the place where the truth had first taken root.

She stood, her joints protesting, and moved to the window, looking out at the frozen river, at the silent, ice-bound valley. The major’s order hung in the air behind her, a weight she had chosen to carry rather than surrender. She would transcribe the ledger, yes, but the names would remain. They had to. Because history was not something that happened only to other people, in other places. History was happening here, in this frozen granary, in the hands of a nurse who had come from a land across the sea, in the quiet act of writing down a name when the world was busy forgetting.

Petra turned from the window and walked back to the center of the room, where the wounded still breathed, where the feverish moans and the soft whispers of prayer rose and fell like the tide. She pulled a blanket around her shoulders, the fabric rough and familiar, and she moved among the beds, touching foreheads, adjusting pillows, speaking words of comfort in English and Hausa and Serbian and the soft, rolling cadences of the languages she had learned here. Her own temperature was high, her body fighting an invisible battle, but she did not stop. She could not stop. The ledger was safe, for now, and the names were recorded, and that was enough for now. There would be time enough later for grief, for reckoning, for the full weight of what she had done. For now, there was the work of the night, the witnessing of men who had been written out of history, and the fierce, stubborn determination to keep their names alive, one stroke of the pen at a time.

* * *

The morning came early, pale and watery, flooding the granary with a light that was cold and unforgiving. Petra woke to the sound of Reeve’s boots in the corridor, the heavy tread of a man who was already thinking of the day’s tasks, of the reports he needed to file, of the ledger he had all but forgotten in the heat of his own anxieties. She rose from the chair where she had dozed, the ledger clutched against her chest, and moved to the corner where the morning report lay ready for her signature.

Reeve was at the table, his back to the door, his pen moving across a form with a rhythmic, automatic precision. He looked up as she entered, and for a moment, the professional mask slipped, revealing the tired lines around his eyes, the small, fleeting shadow of doubt that crossed his face before he schooled it back into place.

“You have the forms, Nurse?”

“Yes, Major.” Petra set the ledger on the table, opening it to the first page. “I have transcribed every name. The ledger will remain in my possession. The names will not be purged.”

Reeve nodded, a slow, deliberate motion. “As you wish. But know that the command’s patience is not infinite. The inquiry is drawing near. And the ledger… it will be scrutinized.”

“Let it be.” Petra’s voice was steady, despite the fatigue that dragged at the corners of her eyes. “The truth has a way of finding its own path.”

She picked up her pen and began to sign the morning report, her hand moving with a deliberate, unhurried grace. Around her, the wounded began to stir, the feverish groans giving way to the quieter sounds of recovery and rest. The day would be long, the work unending, but Petra knew, with a certainty that grounded her like the river’s current grounded the valley, that she had done what she had come to do. She had named the unnamed. She had refused the erasure. And in doing so, she had begun to build a history that was not of the officers or the command, but of the men she had come to love, and of the woman who had refused to let them disappear.

She signed the report, dipped her pen in the ink, and wrote her name at the bottom: *Petra Okonkwo, Nurse, Field Hospital, Lower Danube.* beneath it, in the margin, she added a single line: *And these names are not lost.*

She closed the ledger, tied it with the white strip, and tucked it into the hidden compartment beneath the floorboard, the place where she had begun, the place where the truth had first taken root.

She turned from the window and walked back to the center of the room, where the wounded still breathed, where the feverish moans and the soft whispers of prayer rose and fell like the tide. She pulled a blanket around her shoulders, the fabric rough and familiar, and she moved among the beds, touching foreheads, adjusting pillows, speaking words of comfort in English and Hausa and Serbian and the soft, rolling cadences of the languages she had learned here. Her own temperature was high, her body fighting an invisible battle, but she did not stop. She could not stop. The ledger was safe, for now, and the names were recorded, and that was enough for now. There would be time enough later for grief, for reckoning, for the full weight of what she had done. For now, there was the work of the night, the witnessing of men who had been written out of history, and the fierce, stubborn determination to keep their names alive, one stroke of the pen at a time.
