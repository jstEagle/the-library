## 9. Ilya's Confession

The fever broke on a Tuesday, in the hour before dawn, when the granary was so quiet that Petra could hear the river groaning under its new skin of ice. She knew it had broken because Ilya stopped speaking in the language without words — the muttering, the pleading, the names he called out to no one — and began instead to speak in hers.

"Water," he said. "Please."

She brought it to him in the tin cup with the chipped rim, the one she kept for patients who were returning to themselves and would notice such things. His hands shook as they closed around it, and she saw how thin they had become, the knuckles standing up like knots in driftwood. Three weeks ago they had been the hands of a young man who worked for his bread. Now they were the hands of an old man borrowing a young man's body.

"You were talking," she said, easing him back against the pillows. "In your sleep."

"What did I say?"

"Names, mostly. Some I wrote down." She said it lightly, but her eyes stayed on his face, and what she saw there was not embarrassment. It was something older and colder than embarrassment. It was the look of a man standing at the edge of a hole in the ice.

He drank the water down in small, careful swallows, as though even swallowing were a decision that required deliberation. Outside, the frost had climbed so far up the granary windows that the light came through grey and thick as wool. Somewhere down the ward a man coughed, and coughed again, and then was quiet, and Petra made a note to check on him.

"Nurse Okonkwo." He said her name carefully, the way he said everything now, weighing each syllable like coins. "You have a book. Mirko told me. A book where you write the names."

"The ledger." She kept her voice level. In this hospital, word traveled through fevers and dressings and the long nights between, and she had known for some time that her secret was not entirely secret. "It's nothing official. Just names. The ones the reports leave out."

"And mine is in it?"

"Yes. Ilya. That's all you ever gave me. Just Ilya."

* * *

He did not answer at once. He turned his face toward the window, toward the whiteness pressing against the glass, and when he spoke again his voice had changed — flattened, stripped of the gentle irony he used like a coat against the cold.

"I want to tell you what my name is. My whole name. And then I want you to decide whether to keep it or burn it."

Petra sat down on the stool beside his bed. She had learned, in these months on the Danube, that the most important work she did often happened sitting still — that listening was a kind of nursing too, that some wounds festered in silence and drained only into speech. She folded her hands in her lap and waited.

"My mother called me Iliya Vuković," he said. "That is Serbian, if you want it to be. My father was a Serb from near Požarevac, a cooper, a good man who smelled of oak shavings all his life. But my mother's mother was Romanian, from across the river, and my father's father — this is the part nobody believes — my father's father was born in Galicia, a Jew, who walked south one summer and married a Christian girl and never spoke of it again except once, drunk, at Easter, when he wept and sang songs in a language none of us knew." He smiled, but it was a wintery smile, without warmth in it. "So. Serbian, Romanian, Jewish, and the empire that drafted me didn't care which. They gave me papers that said one thing and put me in a unit full of men who said another, and by the second winter I had learned to be whatever the man asking wanted me to be. Hungarian when they searched us. Serb when we traded with the villagers. Nothing at all, when the officers counted us."

"That's why you told me you were three nationalities and none."

"It wasn't a joke. Or it was, but the kind that has a knife inside it." He looked at her directly then, and Petra felt the temperature of the room change. "I have to say the rest quickly, nurse, because if I stop I will never start again, and you are the only person in this valley I can say it to. The unit I served with — the one that marched along the river last autumn, before I took the bullet in my leg — we passed through a town called Kladovo."

Petra's breath went still in her chest. Kladovo. Captain Hoss had said the name only twice, always late at night, always behind the closed door of the back room, and each time she said it she said it the way one touches a bruise — to learn whether it still hurt.

"There was a village on the far bank," Ilya went on, and now the words came faster, tumbling, as if he feared his own courage would fail mid-sentence. "Ferrymen, farmers, a few families who had lived there since before anyone could remember. Our orders said there were irregulars among them. There were no irregulars. There were old men and a schoolteacher and boys who ran away and hid in the reeds. An officer whose name I will not say yet, because saying it is a thing that must be done properly, decided the village was guilty, and what happened after that—" His voice caught. He pressed the back of his hand against his mouth, hard, and breathed through his nose, and after a moment continued. "I did not fire. I want you to know that first, because it is the only clean thing I own. I stood at the edge of the square with my rifle shouldered and I did not fire. But I stood there. I watched. And afterward, when they ordered the well filled and the houses burned, I carried wood. I carried wood to burn the house of a woman who had given our column bread two days earlier, and I did it, and I did not die doing it, which means I obeyed. Do you understand what I am telling you? I am not a murderer. I am worse than nothing and better than a murderer, and both of those things together are exactly enough to hang me."

The stove ticked as the iron expanded in the heat. Down the ward, someone moaned in his sleep and was hushed by the man in the next bed, gently, the way one quiets a child.

"They will shoot me," Ilya said. "Not the enemy — us. My own army, if the inquiry finds what I know and traces it to me. Or theirs, if I am ever taken and they learn which unit I wore. There is no country on either bank that would claim what I did, so there is no country that will protect me from it. I have thought about this every night for three weeks, nurse, lying here listening to the ice. And so I am asking you — begging you — leave my name out of your book. Write me as you found me. A soldier, unnamed, dead of his wounds if it comes to that. Let the ledger carry the men who deserve carrying. I am not one of them."

* * *

Petra did not answer immediately. She rose, checked his pulse because her hands needed work and his needed pretending, straightened the blanket that did not need straightening. Through the window the river lay white and seamless from bank to bank, the bridge gone, the road gone, the whole world narrowed to this valley and its secrets.

"When I was a girl in Lagos," she said at last, "my grandmother told me that a name is not what you call yourself. It is what you leave behind in other people's mouths. She said the worst curse you could give a person was silence — that to erase someone's name was to kill them twice." She turned back to him. "I started this book because the morning reports lie. Because Major Reeve writes 'expended: forty rounds, three casualties' as if men were ammunition. Because a Serbian conscript died in my arms in October and by evening he had become a number, and numbers do not have mothers." She sat down again. "And now you are asking me to make you a number. To do to you what I built the book to refuse."

"I'm asking you to protect me."

"I know. And I understand why." Her voice softened. "Ilya — Iliya Vuković — listen to me. You carried wood. You also carried bread, maybe. You also carried the memory, which is more than most of them carry; most of them will drink and boast and sleep like children until they die old in their beds. You lay in a hospital bed and chose to hand your life to a stranger rather than let the truth go unwitnessed. That is not nothing. That may be the only thing."

"It won't save me."

"No," she agreed quietly. "It probably won't. But that was never what the book was for."

He stared at her. In the grey light his eyes were very dark, and very tired, and beneath the exhaustion something moved that might have been hope arriving against its will.

"If you write it," he said slowly, "they will find it. Reeve is already sniffing at the back room. Hoss's lamp burns till midnight and he counts the hours, I can see him counting. If your book falls into his hands—"

"Then it falls. Mirko keeps it safe. And when the ship sails, if it sails, the truth goes with it, wherever it goes, whoever it damns." She reached out and covered his hand with hers, his cold fingers under her warm palm. "I cannot promise you mercy from any court or any army. But I can promise you this: you will not be erased. Not by Reeve's reports, not by your fear, not by me. You witnessed Kladovo, Iliya. You are the only living witness in this valley. If I omit you, I don't save you — I just bury the last man who can testify alongside the dead."

He was silent for a long time. The frost on the window had begun to melt where the sun touched it, sending thin runnels of water down the glass like slow tears.

"Hoss," he said finally. "The investigator. Could I tell her? What I told you?"

"She would move heaven and earth to hear it. But it would put you in her report, and her report travels to headquarters, and headquarters is full of men who solve problems like you with wire and a post."

"I know." He laughed — a small, cracked sound, almost a boy's laugh. "Three weeks ago I begged God to let me die cleanly. Now I'm bargaining for a hanging that happens after spring." He drew a breath. "Write my name, nurse. All of it. Iliya Vuković. And write beside it what I did and what I failed to do, both columns, the way you keep accounts. Let history sort me. It's more than I deserve and less than I owe."

Petra opened the ledger that evening, after the lamps were trimmed and the ward had sunk into its breathing dark. By the light of a shielded candle she found his line — *Ilya, wounded, leg, October* — and beneath it she wrote the full name, and the date, and then, after a long moment, the sentence she would carry in her chest for the rest of her life:

*Witness of Kladovo. Carried the wood. Remembered everything.*

She blew out the candle. And as she lay down in her cot, she heard, distantly, boots on frozen mud outside the granary — unhurried, official boots, making their rounds past the back room where a lamp still burned — and understood that the night had grown shorter, and the walls closer, and that somewhere in this valley a man named Vuković had just become evidence.
