## 10. A Name Withheld

The ledger lay open on the crate that served Petra's desk, its pages swollen with damp and ink, the cover warped from weeks beneath the floorboard. Dawn had not yet reached the granary's high windows; the light that filtered through was the color of bruised plums, thin and cold. Around her, the hospital breathed — the wet rasp of chests, the rustle of straw pallets, the occasional cry bitten back against a pillow. Somewhere a man was dying. Somewhere another was waking to the same war he had fallen asleep in.

Petra's fingers rested on the blank line.

She had ruled it herself, the night before, with the straight edge of a ration tin and a pencil stolen from the quartermaster's stores. A clean horizontal stroke across the cream paper. Beside it, the column for *Name* waited empty. The columns for *Unit*, *Wound*, *Date Received*, *Disposition* — those she had filled. *Serbian 3rd Infantry, Shrapnel to thigh and belly, 14 January 1916, Died 03:17 hours.* All true. All verifiable. But the name column — *Vuković, Ilya* — she had left as white as the ice on the Danube.

*Witness of Kladovo. Carried the wood. Remembered everything.*

The words she had written in the margins, small and slanting, as if the paper might reject them. As if the ledger itself might shudder and spit them out.

She had seen men die without names before. John Does pulled from the river, their pockets empty, their faces swollen beyond recognition. She had written *Unknown, male, approx. 20* and felt the weight of that erasure settle in her chest like a stone. But this was different. This was a name she *knew*, a name attached to hands that had trembled when he spoke of the pine forest near Kladovo, to eyes that had watched her with something like hope when she changed his dressings. A name attached to a man who had begged her, in the dark hours before dawn, to let him vanish from the record.

*Please. Sister. Do not write me. I have done things — I have seen things — and if my name goes down, it will not be as a victim. It will be as one of them. Let me be the blank. Let me be the space between the lines.*

His voice had been a thread pulled thin. His fever had broken that night, the crisis passing like a storm, leaving him pale and hollowed but alive. Alive, and therefore dangerous. Alive, and therefore a witness.

Petra lifted her fountain pen. The nib caught the weak light, gold and sharp. The ink in the reservoir was dark as winter water.

Behind her, the granary's heavy door groaned open. Boots on the wooden floor — slow, deliberate. Not the shuffling of orderlies, not the light step of the night sister. These boots knew their weight.

She did not turn. She did not cap the pen.

"Sister Okonkwo."

Captain Hoss. Her voice was lower than usual, stripped of its official crispness. The smell of cigarette smoke and cold air followed her in — she had been outside, walking the perimeter perhaps, or standing at the river's edge where the ice groaned and shifted.

"Captain." Petra kept her eyes on the blank line. "You're up early."

"Couldn't sleep. The back room is cold, and the stove eats wood faster than Mirko can bring it." A pause. The boots stopped behind Petra's right shoulder. "You're still at it."

"At what?"

"The ledger. I saw the light under the door at three. At four. At five." Hoss's hand came to rest on the crate's edge, fingers pale against the rough wood. "You've been copying names all night."

"Someone has to."

"Reeve's standardization order comes down today. He posted the notice at midnight. All records to be consolidated, 'irregularities resolved,' 'duplications eliminated.'" The words carried quotation marks in her voice. "By his definition, your ledger is nothing but irregularity."

Petra's thumb rubbed the pen's barrel. "Reeve's definition serves Reeve."

"Does it?" Hoss leaned closer, her breath warm against the chill air. "Or does it serve the same machine that made Kladovo possible? The machine that decides which deaths count and which are... administrative adjustments?"

The question hung between them, heavy as the river ice.

Petra set the pen down. Finally, she turned.

Captain Verena Hoss looked different in the bruised dawn light. The severe bun had loosened, dark strands escaping to frame a face carved by exhaustion and something fiercer — something that might have been anger, or grief, or the particular exhaustion of a woman who has seen too much and been believed too little. Her greatcoat was buttoned to the throat, collar turned up against the cold. A bruise shadowed her left cheekbone, old but not yet faded.

"You read the entry," Petra said. Not a question.

"I read all of them. Last night, while you were in the ward." Hoss's gaze flicked to the open ledger, to the blank line. "You left it empty."

"I haven't decided."

"There's nothing to decide. He has a name. He has a story. He has a place in what happened at Kladovo — not as a perpetrator, but as a witness. Witnesses are what we have, Petra. Witnesses are the only thing that makes history something other than the victors' minutes."

"History." The word tasted of iron. "You speak as if history is a court of law. As if writing his name down makes him safe."

"Writing his name down makes him *real*. Unerasable. You know this better than anyone — you've spent months making the erased real again. Every Serbian conscript, every Romanian peasant, every Bulgarian boy the reports forgot. You gave them back their names. Why does this one stop you?"

Petra's jaw tightened. She thought of Ilya's hands, folded on the blanket. The way he had said *three nationalities and none* with a laugh that had no humor in it. The way he had looked at her when he confessed — not with shame, exactly. With a kind of terrible clarity. As if he had been waiting for someone to ask, and now that he had answered, the asking was done.

*He carried the wood. He remembered everything.*

"Because he asked me not to," she said quietly. "Because he looked me in the eye and begged me to let him disappear. And I — I have spent my whole life watching people disappear. My father. My brother. The patients whose names the colonels cross out with red pencil. I swore I would not be the one holding the eraser."

Hoss was silent for a long moment. The granary's sounds rose around them — a cough, a murmur, the clatter of a bedpan. Somewhere, a man took his last breath. No one rushed. No one needed to.

"Then don't be the eraser," Hoss said finally. "Be the archivist. Be the witness to the witness. Write his name, and beside it write *asked to be omitted*. Write *refused the record*. Write *carried the wood, remembered everything, chose silence*. That is not erasure. That is truth in its full dimension — the truth of what he did, and the truth of what he could not bear."

"And if his name on that page brings the investigators to his bed? If Reeve's men come for him, or the Serbians, or the Austrians, or whichever court claims jurisdiction over a man with three nationalities and none?"

"Then we protect him. The way we protect the ledger. The way Mirko protects the workshop." Hoss's voice hardened. "You think I came here for a clean prosecution? I came because the report from Kladovo smelled of ink and not blood. Because the numbers didn't match the graves. Because someone — *someones* — decided that two hundred and forty-seven dead civilians were an acceptable margin of error in a counter-insurgency operation. Ilya Vuković is not the enemy. He is the crack in their wall. His testimony — *his name* — is the lever."

"A lever that could break him."

"A lever that could break *them*." Hoss straightened, her hand leaving the crate. "You're not protecting him by omitting him. You're completing their work for them. You're doing the erasure they couldn't be bothered to do themselves."

The words struck something in Petra — a place she had fortified against precisely this argument. She had built her ledger on the principle that *every name matters*. That the act of recording was itself a form of resistance, a declaration that these lives existed, that they would not vanish into the maw of bureaucracy. But she had never imagined a name that *refused* to be recorded. A name that carried its own danger, its own moral gravity.

*What is a name worth if no state will claim it?*

Ilya had asked her that, weeks ago, in the ward. She had not answered then. She was not sure she could answer now.

"I need to think," she said.

"You don't have time." Hoss's tone softened, just slightly. "Reeve's order takes effect at noon. The guards on the workshop double at shift change. The supply ship — if it comes — sails on the morning tide after the ice breaks. Every hour you spend thinking is an hour they spend shredding."

She turned and walked out, her boots echoing in the granary's vast space. The door swung shut behind her, and the sound was final.

Petra stared at the blank line.

* * *

The boatwright's workshop smelled of pine tar, wet wool, and the river's ancient rot. It sat at the water's edge on pilings driven deep into mud that had swallowed generations of boats, their ribs bleaching in the summer sun like the bones of great fish. Now the river was a white plain, the workshop a dark island in a frozen sea.

Mirko sat at his workbench, planing a plank for a hull that would never sail. His hands — gnarled, scarred, the fingers thickened by decades of tools — moved with the slow precision of a man who has made the same motion ten thousand times. *Shhh-thick. Shhh-thick.* Curls of pale wood fell to the floor, joining the drifts of shavings that carpeted the planks.

He did not look up when Petra entered. The cold followed her in, swirling around the stove's meager heat.

"Petra." His voice was gravel in a tin cup. "You stand in the door like a ghost. Come in. The stove has tea. Mint, from the package the Italian ship brought before the bridge closed. Tastes like sawdust and hope."

She closed the door behind her, the latch catching with a click. The workshop's single window, facing the river, was frosted over in fern patterns. Through it, the Danube's ice field stretched to the far bank — a desert of white scored by pressure ridges, dark water showing in the cracks where the current still fought.

"Mirko." She moved to the bench opposite him, leaving the ledger in her satchel. "I need your counsel."

He stopped planing. The silence stretched, filled only by the stove's tick and pop, the distant thump of the hospital's generator. When he spoke, his eyes remained on the plank.

"My counsel is free, little sister. But it comes with no guarantee of wisdom. Only age. And age mostly teaches you how many ways there are to be wrong."

"Then tell me how an old boatwright sees it. A man with three nationalities and none. A man who carried wood to a pyre he did not light. A man who asks to be forgotten."

Mirko set the plane down. Slowly, deliberately, he wiped his hands on a rag, each finger cleaned in turn. Then he reached into his coat pocket and withdrew a tin — tobacco, rolled thin in newspaper. He lit it with a match struck against the bench, the flare brief and golden in the dimness.

"My eldest, Dragan," he said, smoke curling from his nostrils. "He was eighteen when they took him. Said he'd be home for slava. Said the war would end by winter. He wrote letters — beautiful letters, full of jokes about the food, the officers' boots, the mud. Never wrote about the killing. Never wrote about the friends who stopped writing back."

He exhaled, the smoke rising toward the rafters.

"My younger, Stefan. Sixteen. Lied about his age. Said he couldn't let Dragan go alone. As if war were a thing you went to together, like a dance." The old man's voice did not waver. "They died three days apart. Same offensive. Different trenches. The telegrams arrived on the same morning. I read them standing right here, at this bench. I planed this same plank — oak, from the forest above Donji Milanovac — and I did not stop planing for three days. My hands bled. The wood was perfect when I finished. The smoothest hull I ever made. Never launched it. Couldn't bear to put it in water that had taken my sons."

Petra waited. The workshop held its breath.

"Two years ago, a deserter came here. Austrian. Hungarian. Didn't matter. He was nineteen, shaking with fever, his boots rotted off his feet. He had killed a man — an officer, he said. Said the officer ordered them to shoot villagers. Women. Children. Said he shot the officer instead, and ran. He asked me to hide him. Said he didn't deserve to live."

Mirko's eyes found hers, dark and deep as the river's winter channels.

"I hid him. Fed him. Let him sleep in the loft above the sail canvas. For three weeks. The gendarmes came twice. I lied both times. Said I hadn't seen anyone. Said I was an old man minding my own business, building a boat for no one."

"And the deserter?"

"He left when the fever broke. Stole my best knife. Left a note — *Thank you. I don't deserve it.*" A dry chuckle. "As if deserving had anything to do with it. As if any of us deserve the mercy we give, or the mercy we receive."

He tapped ash onto the floor.

"You ask me about a man who carried wood. Who watched. Who did not stop it. Who asks now to be erased." Mirko leaned forward, elbows on knees. "I tell you this: a man is more than his worst winter. Dragan and Stefan — they were more than the bullets that stopped their hearts. The deserter was more than the officer he killed, or the villagers he couldn't save. And your Ilya — he is more than the wood he carried, or the silence he kept, or the name he wants to shed."

"Even if that name brings death to his door?"

"Especially then." Mirko's voice hardened. "You write the name. You write it because he lived. Because he breathed, and bled, and lay in your ward and let you stitch his flesh back together. Because he trusted you with the truth, and that trust is a gift you do not return by burying it. You write the name, and you write the rest — *witness, carried wood, asked silence*. You let the ledger hold the complexity. That is what the ledger *is*, Petra. Not a list of saints. A list of *people*. People are complicated. People are contradictory. People carry wood to pyres they did not build, and they wake in the night remembering the heat."

He reached across the bench, his rough hand covering hers where it rested on the cold wood.

"The officers' reports — they want clean lines. Heroes and villains. Victims and perpetrators. Numbers that add up to justification. Your ledger... your ledger says *no*. It says *here is a man who did this, and also this, and also this*. It says *history happens wherever someone refuses to let names be erased*. Even the names that ask to be erased. *Especially* those names."

Petra's throat tightened. The workshop seemed to expand around them, the rafters lifting, the river receding. She thought of her father's name on a memorial in Lagos she had never visited. Her brother's name on no memorial at all — lost in a mining strike the company called a riot, the papers called a disturbance, the government called nothing.

*History happens elsewhere, to other people.*

She had believed that once. Believed she was merely a witness, a recorder, a pair of hands moving a pen across paper. But the ledger had grown heavy in her satchel. The names had weight. The blank line had weight.

"What if I'm wrong?" she whispered. "What if writing his name kills him?"

"Then you carry that too." Mirko's grip was warm, callused, unyielding. "You carry it the way I carry Dragan and Stefan. The way I carry the deserter's stolen knife in my pocket, twelve years later. You carry it, and you keep writing. Because the alternative — the blank line — that is not mercy. That is surrender. That is the river winning."

* * *

The hospital's back room had been a storage closet once — sacks of lime, coils of bandage, the smell of dust and time. Now it held a desk borrowed from the quartermaster, a chair with one leg shimmed by a medical textbook, a stove that smoked when the wind blew wrong, and Captain Hoss's maps.

Maps of the valley. Maps of Kladovo. Maps of the Danube's course from the Iron Gates to the Black Sea, annotated in red and blue pencil, in ink that had bled through to the other side. Pins marked positions, movements, the locations of mass graves inferred from aerial photographs and peasant testimony.

Hoss stood over the table, her fingers tracing a blue line — the river. The ice. The bridge that was no longer a bridge.

She heard the door before it opened. The granary's acoustics carried everything.

"Captain."

Petra. No *Sister* this time. No *Captain Hoss*. Just the name, offered like a coin across a counter.

Hoss did not turn. "You've decided."

"I've decided I hate you both." Petra's voice was rough, unsteady. "Mirko for making sense. You for making sense differently. Myself for needing either of you to tell me what I already knew."

"And what's that?"

"That the blank line is a lie. That it's not mercy. That it's me deciding his story ends in silence because I'm afraid of what happens if it doesn't." A step closer. The creak of the floorboard. "I wrote his name. In the ward register. In the death log — though he didn't die. In the medication ledger. His name exists in half a dozen official documents, all of them sanitized, none of them true. The only place it *hasn't* existed is the only place that matters."

"The ledger."

"The ledger." Petra's breath came ragged. "I wrote it just now. *Vuković, Ilya*. In the column. Black ink. It looks like all the others. It looks like it belongs."

"Does it?"

"No. It looks like a target."

Hoss turned then. Her face was pale in the lamplight, the bruise on her cheekbone a shadow. "Then we make sure the target holds. We take statements. We build the case. We put the ledger on the ship, and we make sure the ship sails, and we make sure the world reads what's on those pages — *all* of them. Including the margins. Including *asked to be omitted*. That marginal note is as important as the name. It says: *this man had agency. This man chose. This man was not a statue.*"

Petra was silent. The stove popped, a spark spiraling up the flue.

"There's something else," she said finally. "Something I didn't write. Something he told me after — after he asked."

Hoss waited.

"He said... he said the wood wasn't for a pyre. Not exactly. He said they were building a bridge. A temporary span, downstream from Kladovo. The civilians — the villagers — they were *working* on it. Forced labor. The wood he carried was for the bridge decking. And when the shelling started — our shelling, the Serbian batteries — the officers ordered the villagers to keep working. Said the bridge was strategic. Said the schedule couldn't slip. He said... he said the screaming didn't stop until the bridge was finished. And then they burned the bodies *on* the bridge. To clear it for the tanks."

The words fell into the room like stones into a well.

Hoss's pencil snapped. The sound was sharp, violent.

"Kladovo wasn't a massacre," she said, her voice stripped bare. "It was a *construction project*. They built a bridge on top of the people they killed building it."

"History happens elsewhere," Petra whispered. "To other people. That's what I used to think. But it happened here. It happened to him. It happened to the wood he carried."

"And you wrote his name." Hoss's hand found the edge of the table, gripping hard. "You wrote his name, and you made him part of the record. Part of the evidence. Part of the thing that cannot be denied."

"I wrote his name," Petra agreed. "And now I don't know if I saved him or condemned him."

"You *witnessed* him. That's what we do. That's all we can do. The rest — the courts, the commissions, the politicians with their maps and their margins of error — that is not ours. Ours is the ledger. Ours is the name. Ours is *he was here, he lived, he carried the wood, he remembered, he asked for silence, we gave him truth instead*."

A knock at the door. Sharp. Official.

Both women froze.

The door opened before either could move. A sergeant — young, pale, his greatcoat too large — stood in the frame. Behind him, the corridor stretched dim and empty.

"Captain Hoss. Major Reeve requests your presence. Immediately. In his office."

Hoss's face did not change. "For what purpose?"

"The standardization review, ma'am. He wishes to discuss... irregularities in the hospital's documentation procedures."

The sergeant's eyes flicked to Petra. To the ledger peeking from her satchel on the chair. To the maps on the table.

"Ma'am," he repeated. "Now."

Hoss looked at Petra. One breath. Two.

"Tell the Major I'll be there when I've finished my tea," she said. "And tell him that if he wants to discuss irregularities, he should start with the ration logs from Kladovo. The ones showing three hundred men fed for two weeks — and two hundred and forty-seven civilians disappearing from the village census in the same period."

The sergeant's face went carefully blank. "I'll... relay that, ma'am."

"See that you do."

He withdrew. The door closed.

Petra exhaled, a long shuddering breath. "He knows. Or he suspects. The guards on the workshop — "

"Were doubled at shift change, yes. I saw them." Hoss moved to the stove, poured tea from a dented pot into two enamel mugs. She handed one to Petra without asking. "Drink. We have work to do before noon."

"What work?"

"Statements. Testimony. The men well enough to speak — we take their words down, formally, witnessed, signed. Including Ilya. *Especially* Ilya." Hoss blew on her tea, the steam curling between them. "Reeve can purge the official records. He can standardize the reports until they're smooth as glass. But he cannot unpublish what we put on the ship. He cannot unhear what the wounded say when they're asked, *what did you see?*"

Petra took the mug. The tea was bitter, hot, tasting of mint and sawdust and hope.

"Three days," she said. "You said three days until the ship."

"Three days. Maybe less. The ice is shifting — I heard it groaning all night. If the current breaks it early, the ship comes early. If not..." Hoss shrugged. "We work with what we have. Three days. Four nights. However many names we can get on paper before the tide."

"And if Reeve stops us?"

"Then we do what Mirko does. What you do. What every person who has ever refused erasure does." Hoss met her eyes, and in the lamplight her face was fierce and strange and beautiful. "We cheat. We hide. We smuggle. We lie. We survive long enough to let the truth outlive us."

* * *

The ward was quiet at midnight, or as quiet as a ward ever is. The generator had been shut down for the night, its coughing exhaust silenced. Lamps burned low at each station, their flames drawing oxygen from air thick with the breath of thirty-seven men.

Petra moved between the beds with her notebook, her pencil, her lantern. The pencil because ink smudged in the cold, because pencil could be erased — though she never erased. The lantern because the overhead lights had been dimmed by Reeve's order, *energy conservation*, though everyone knew it was to make the ledger harder to copy by lamplight.

She had three days. Three days to turn a ledger of names into a ledger of *testimony*. Three days to make the blank lines speak.

Bed twelve: Corporal Dragan Petrović, Serbian 3rd. Shrapnel to the chest, sucking wound sealed with a flutter valve. He had been at Kladovo. He had seen the bridge. He had heard the screaming.

His eyes opened when she approached — dark, fever-bright, fixed on something beyond the canvas ceiling.

"Corporal." She set the lantern on the bedside crate. "I'm taking statements. For the record. For the ship."

His lips moved. No sound came. She waited, her hand steady on the notebook.

"Water," he whispered finally.

She gave him water, spoon by spoon, the tin cup cool against his cracked lips. When he had swallowed, she asked again.

"Kladovo. The bridge. What did you see?"

His gaze drifted, focusing on some inner horizon. "The villagers. Women. Old men. Children carrying planks. The officers... they laughed. Said the bridge would hold better with Serbian blood in the mortar. Said it was *traditional*." A cough, wet and tearing. "I didn't laugh. I wanted to shoot them. My own officers. But I didn't. I carried planks too. I carried them next to a boy — fourteen, maybe. His name was Marko. He sang while he worked. A folk song. *Oj, Moravo, ti tečeš...* He sang until the shell hit. Then he didn't sing anymore. Then nobody sang."

Petra wrote. *Petrović, Dragan. Corporal, Serbian 3rd Infantry. Witness: forced labor construction of pontoon bridge at Kladovo, 12-14 January. Civilian casualties estimated 200+. Officers observed and encouraged. Refused to fire on officers. Carried planks alongside civilian boy (Marko, approx. 14) killed by friendly fire.*

"Sign here," she said, offering the pencil.

His hand shook. The mark was barely a scrawl. But it was his.

"Thank you," she said.

"Thank *you*," he whispered. "Someone knows. Someone will know."

She moved on.

Bed nineteen: Private Ionuț Georgescu, Romanian 11th Division. Frostbite, both feet, amputation pending. He had been a translator — spoke Serbian, Bulgarian, German, Turkish. He had been at the interrogation tent when the villagers were brought in.

Bed twenty-three: Lance Corporal Ahmet Yılmaz, Ottoman 15th Corps. Dysentery, dehydration. He had driven the supply wagons. He had seen the wood stacked by the river. He had heard the orders.

Bed thirty-one: Ilya Vuković.
