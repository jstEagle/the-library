## 11. The Frozen Crossing

The cold arrived that night as if it had been waiting behind the hills for permission. Petra felt it first in the mortar between the granary stones, a fine white powder that breathed from every joint when the wind moved. Then in the kerosene, which would not light. Then in the hands of the men she was dressing, gloves stiffening on her fingers as she wound the bandages, so that when she tried to release them her own skin came away with the wool. She had not known that cloth could adhere to flesh. She knew it now.

She had been on her feet since three. The line of wounded that came in with the dusk was thinner than yesterday's — the war, in this corner of it, had gone quiet, which only meant the quiet was being filled elsewhere with louder work. A boy from Vidin with no chin left to speak through wrote his mother's address on the back of Petra's hand. A Hungarian whose name she did not record because he could not tell it and she could not read it on his lips — *b, m, a,* perhaps, perhaps *Balogh,* perhaps *Barna* — died at twenty past nine. She wrote what she could in the margin of her ledger: *Hungarian, unknown, twenty-ish, the cold took him before the wound could.* Then she closed the book and tucked it back into its place beneath the loose board under the cot where she slept when she slept, and she went outside to see what the sound was.

The river had stopped being a river. Where there had been water there was a long, pale, irregular field, like a sheet thrown down without care. The moon was up. The moon had been up for an hour and would not set until morning. It lit the ice with the merciless clarity of a theatre lamp, and under that light the ice was not white but a cold, honest blue, the blue of a bruise that has finished spreading and is now simply the colour of what it is. From the granary steps Petra could see, far down the slope, the dark mass of the pontoon bridge that the engineers had blown two weeks ago — the twisted iron, the rotted planking, the slow slide of the upstream end into the current that still ran, thin and muscular, beneath the crust. The detonation had been called a precaution. She had stood beside Major Reeve and watched the small bright puffs of charge march down the span one after another, like a row of heads bowing. He had smiled when it was finished. *The road out of the valley,* he had said, *is closed for the season.* She had understood then, and understood better now, that he had meant more than one road.

The sound she had come out to hear was not the ice. The ice made no sound she could hear at this distance, only the occasional low pop and the long, slow grind along the banks where the current still pressed. The sound was voices. Not many. Three, maybe four, lowered and ragged with effort, somewhere down by the waterline near the old ferry landing. She could not see the men because the willows along the bank had not yet shed their last yellow leaves, and the leaves, even in moonlight, were enough to break a body into pieces. But she could hear the particular breath of men pulling themselves forward on ice — the hard, short exhalations, the slip and recover, the clink of metal against the frozen surface. Rifles. They had brought their rifles.

She stood very still on the steps and tried to remember what she had been told. Deserters attempting to cross the ice were to be challenged by the guard post at the bend. If they refused the challenge, they were to be fired upon. If they reached the far bank, they were to be pursued and brought back. She had heard Major Reeve say these words in the same tone in which he said *the soup is excellent today* and *your bandages are a credit to the service.* She had watched his mouth form them. She had not, until this moment, thought she would be required to choose whether she believed them.

She went back inside. The granary was warm with bodies and with the iron stoves that had been fed all day and were now fed again. She passed Ilya's cot. He was awake, propped on one elbow, watching the door. He had been watching the door, she realised, since she had left it. His eyes in the lamplight were the colour of weak tea and twice as clear.

"What is it," he said. Not a question. He had learned, in three weeks of being carried from ward to ward on her ledger, that when Petra went out at night she did not go out for fresh air.

"Men on the ice," she said. "Deserters, I think."

He lay back. His hand found the edge of his blanket and folded it over once, precisely, the way a man folds a letter he has decided not to send. "Then they will be shot," he said, "or they will drown. It is only a question of which happens first."

She did not answer. She was already moving toward the back of the granary, where the supply cupboard stood beside the door that opened onto the lower path and the bank. She pulled her coat on over her apron. The coat was a Serbian infantryman's greatcoat that had been left behind by a patient who had no further use for coats or anything else; it smelled of someone else's tobacco and came down to her ankles and the sleeves she had rolled twice. She did not stop to wrap her head. The cold, when she stepped out, took her ears first, then the bridge of her nose, then the soft skin beneath her eyes, and she understood for the first time why the dying men of the previous night had wept without being able to say why.

The path down to the ferry landing was treacherous. She did not run. She walked, placing her feet with the exaggerated care of a woman crossing a floor she knows is mined, and she listened. The voices were louder now, and she could hear, beneath the breath and the scraping, a thin, terrible sound that was not speech at all but the small percussion of ice under a sudden weight. A crack. A shift. Then a scream, brief and bitten off, as if the screamer had been pulled under by the hand that made the sound.

She reached the willows. She parted them with her forearm and looked out.

There were four men. She could see three clearly. The fourth was already gone — she knew he was gone because she could see the place on the ice where he had been, a dark wet star opening slowly against the blue, and beside the star a rifle lying on its side as if it had been set down gently by someone intending to come back for it. The other three were some twenty metres further on, clawing at the surface, their breath coming in white plumes that the moonlight took and turned to silver. One of them had stopped moving and was lying face down with his arms spread; she could not tell if he was alive. The other two were trying to stand, and the ice beneath them was speaking now, a low continuous complaint that meant it was tired of holding them and would not hold them much longer.

She did not think about Major Reeve. She thought, later, that she ought to have. At the time there was only the simple arithmetic of the body — three men on ice that was breaking, one woman on ground that was not, and the distance between them shorter than she would have liked. She stepped onto the ice. The cold bit through the sole of her boot as if she had stepped on a coin pressed into snow. She moved forward, keeping her weight on the balls of her feet, her hands out at her sides the way she had seen skaters do in the parks of her childhood, in the place she had come from, in the life that seemed now to belong to someone she had once met at a station.

The first man she came to was the one lying face down. She rolled him. He was a Romanian, by his coat, and young — nineteen, perhaps twenty, the pale soft beard of a boy who has not yet had to shave twice in the same week. His eyes were open and filmed. He was not dead. He was simply past the point at which cold permits a man to move. She got her hands under his arms and dragged him backward, and the ice groaned under them both but did not give. She dragged him until she reached the bank, until the willows caught at her coat and held her, until her heels found mud. She left him there, half in the water, half out, and went back.

The two who were still upright had stopped trying to stand. They had gone down on their knees, then on their bellies, and were now attempting to crawl with a slowness that was more terrible than any speed could have been because it showed exactly how much they had left. One of them looked up and saw her. She saw his face in the moonlight and it was a face she had seen before — not this man, but this expression, on the cot beside her own cot in a hospital outside Lagos where she had nursed in a different war, a war she had been told was not a war at all. It was the face of a man who has just understood that he is going to die and is not yet ready to forgive the world for it.

She reached him. She reached the other. She did not speak, because she had no language in which to speak that would not be a lie. She hooked her hands under their arms, one under each, and pulled. The ice sang. The ice sang a long, clear note, as if a glass were being struck by a wet finger, and then the surface near her left foot simply left, sliding sideways with a soft sound like a curtain being drawn, and the black water came up through the gap and took the boot from her foot and went on looking for more.

She did not let go.

She pulled them backward, step by step, until the bank came up to meet her, until she was on her knees in the mud with two strangers hanging from her arms like children who have fallen asleep on a walk. The third man, the one who had been lying face down, was still where she had left him, breathing in shallow tatters. The fourth was not anywhere.

From the direction of the hospital she heard, then, what she had not allowed herself to hear before — the sound of boots on the path, several pairs, moving at the quick march. The guard from the bend. Coming, perhaps, to do what she had undone. Coming, perhaps, to find her here on the bank with three men she had no permission to save and one she had failed.

She did not stand. She sat where she was, in the mud, with her arms around two men she did not know, and she waited for whatever would happen next to happen. Her foot, the one the ice had taken the boot from, was in the water and she could no longer feel it. She was aware, distantly, that she was weeping. She was aware that she was not weeping for the man who had drowned. She was weeping for the man who had not, because she knew, with the certainty of a nurse who has seen it often enough to be tired of it, that the cold had already done its work on his hands and his feet and the soft parts of his face, and that what she had saved was not his life but only the long, slow business of his dying.

The boots came closer. A voice called across the ice — sharp, young, bored, the voice of a man who has been posted to a duty he does not believe in. *Halt. Halt or we fire.* She lifted her head. The moonlight caught the wet on her face and made it shine. She did not call back. She sat with her arms around the two men and she let the guard see her, and she waited.

The guard did not fire. The guard stopped at the edge of the willows and stood there, his rifle half raised, his face uncertain in the way that faces are uncertain when the order one has been given does not match the picture one is looking at. He was, she saw when he stepped closer, very young. Younger than the Romanian on the ground. He looked at her, and at the men, and at the black water with its slow-moving star of darker black, and he lowered his rifle by degrees, as if his arms had grown tired of holding it.

"Miss," he said, finally. "Miss, you can't—"

"I am a nurse," she said. Her voice sounded like someone else's voice, an older voice, a voice that had smoked too much. "I am a nurse, and these are patients. Bring the stretcher. There is one at the granary door."

He stood a moment longer. Then he went.

She did not let go of the two men. They were shaking now, the deep shaking that is not cold but the body's last effort to warm itself by any means available. The Romanian at her side made a sound that might have been a word, might have been a prayer in a language she did not speak. She held him and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that would be true, and she had learned, in the long winter of this year, that a lie spoken over a dying man is a weight that has to be carried afterwards.

She heard the stretcher. She heard voices. She saw, coming up the path, not only the young guard but also Hoss — Captain Verena Hoss, in her greatcoat and her boots, her hair down for once, her face without its usual careful mask of competence. Hoss had come out because Hoss, too, had heard the ice speak and had come to see what the river had taken. She was carrying a lantern, and the lantern light met the moonlight on the path and they did not agree, the one being warm and the other being cold, and the small argument of their colours fell on Petra's face like a sentence in two languages.

"Three," Petra said. "There were four. One is gone."

Hoss looked out at the ice. The dark star had widened and was no longer moving. The rifle still lay where it had been laid down, as if it were waiting for someone to come back for it.

"I see," Hoss said, in the tone of a woman who does not see at all and intends to see later, when she is alone and can afford to.

They brought the stretcher. They brought the men up the path. Petra walked between them with her arms around two and her bare foot leaving prints in the frost that would still be there in the morning when the sun came up and did not warm them. She did not feel heroic. She did not feel defiant. She felt only the specific, intimate fury of a woman who has been told, by the machinery of the world, that certain men are not to be carried and has carried them anyway, and has discovered that the world, in this small place, at this small hour, has not known how to stop her.

She got them inside. She put them on cots near the stove. She cut the boots from the Romanian's feet and looked at what the cold had done and did not permit her face to show what she saw. She wrapped him. She wrapped the other. She went back outside for the third, the one she had first pulled ashore, and brought him in too, and by the time she was finished her hands were so cold that she could not close them. She stood at the stove with her fingers spread and her palms toward the iron and waited for the feeling to come back, and when it came it came as pain, and she was grateful for the pain because pain meant that the hands were still hers.

It was then that she heard, from the back of the granary, the sound of a board being lifted. She knew that sound. She had made that sound herself, every night for weeks, in the small hours when the rest of the ward slept and she had work to do that was not in any report. Hoss was in the back room. Hoss had found the loose board. Hoss had found the ledger.

Petra crossed the ward. Her bare foot left wet prints on the stone. She did not hurry. She reached the doorway and stood in it and looked at Hoss, who was kneeling on the floor with the ledger open on her knees, reading by the light of the candle she had brought with her, and who looked up at Petra with an expression that Petra had not seen on another woman's face before and that she recognised at once as the expression of a person who has been told, by a piece of paper, something that the world had gone to some trouble to keep from her.

Hoss closed the ledger. She did not close it quickly. She closed it the way one closes a book one intends to open again, and she set it on the floor in front of her, and she looked at Petra over the top of it, and the look passed between them like a current through water.

"How many," Hoss said. Not *what is this.* Not *where did you get it.* How many.

"I have not finished counting," Petra said.

Hoss nodded. She did not pick the ledger up. She left it on the floor between them, and the candle flame wavered, and the shadows of both women leaned toward each other across the small bright page, and outside, beyond the granary, beyond the willows, the river held its new dead and did not let him go.

She was still standing there when she heard, from the ward behind her, a cough. Then another. Then a third, longer, wetter, and she knew before she turned that the cough was not the cough of a man who had been pulled from a frozen river but of a man who had been breathing, all night, the breath of a man who had been pulled from a frozen river, and that the granary, which had been a hospital, was about to become a ward.
