## 12. Reeve Learns of the Ledger

The granary's interior smelled of dried hay, iron gall ink, and the faint, sour tang of typhus that no amount of lime could fully scrub from the floorboards. Morning light slanted through high windows, catching dust motes that danced above the rows of cots. Petra moved between them, her skirts whispering over straw, her hands checking pulses, adjusting bandages, recording the unseen. She had long ago stopped counting how many times the morning report had been filed, how many names had been smoothed into neat columns of "missing," "transferred," "died of exposure." The ledger she kept beneath the floorboard—tucked between a joist and the packed earth of the granary's cellar—was her quiet rebellion against the erasure.

Today, however, the usual rhythm was broken.

A convoy of officers' wagons had rolled up the muddy track from the river crossing, their wheels rutted and slow. Major Aldous Reeve stepped down first, his greatcoat stiff with frost, his mustache immaculate despite the chill. Behind him came three orderlies carrying ledgers, ink pots, and a portable desk. They moved with the purpose of auditors, not healers.

"Major Reeve," Petra said, removing her gloves. Her hands were raw at the knuckles from long hours of dressing wounds. "The triage is nearly complete. The Serbian conscript you saw yesterday—"

"Reports are filed," Reeve interrupted, not looking at her. His eyes scanned the room with an efficiency that made the wounded seem like inventory. "I am here to standardize the records. To ensure no discrepancies remain for the inquiry."

Petra felt a muscle in her jaw tighten. She had heard that word—standardize—applied to men before. It usually meant pruning the branches that grew too wild, cutting away the names that didn't fit the tidy report. But she said nothing, only stepped aside as the orderlies began pulling cot sheets, rifling through straw tickings, lifting false bottoms in the supply chests.

They searched the area where the granary's cellar met the main floor, where Petra had secreted the ledger's copper-bound case beneath a loose plank, weighted down with a sack of dried beans she used for circulation exercises. The men prodded the straw, poked at the wood, but the ledger remained still, its cover too worn, its stitching too familiar, to draw attention. When they lifted a section of floorboard near the hearth, charred soot and ash came up, but the space beneath was empty of anything but a lost button and a fragment of bone.

Reeve straightened, brushing dust from his gloves. He surveyed the granary with an approving nod. "Immaculate. Truly. No names out of place. The report will reflect the hospital's order."

He turned to leave, but as he reached the door, he paused. His gaze lingered on Petra. She was crouched by a cot, smoothing a blanket over a soldier's shivering shoulders. Her eyes were downcast, but he had seen her—seen the way her hand had hovered over the floorboard, the faint hesitation before she moved on. He had seen her ledger pages during the night shift, the shorthand she used when she thought no officer watched. The names she recorded were always the ones the reports omitted: the conscript without a regiment, the man whose nation no longer existed, the woman who had been caught in the wrong village at the wrong time.

Reeve's jaw tightened. He had spent years in the administrative corps, learning that a name was a liability, a complication, a thread that could unravel a neatly woven narrative. He had his own ledger of omissions, names he had buried to protect his own standing, his own safety. But Petra's ledger was different. It was not a tool of convenience; it was a witness.

He inclined his head, a brief, polite gesture, and walked out into the frosty morning, the cold biting at his cheeks. Behind him, the granary fell silent once more, but the quiet no longer felt like safety. It felt like a held breath.

*****

Captain Verena Hoss had been stationed in the granary's back room for two days. She was a woman of sharp angles and sharper instincts, sent from headquarters to investigate rumors of a massacre at Kladovo, a village upriver where a whole company had vanished overnight. Her own commission was provisional, a stopgap measure while the main inquiry was delayed by the frozen roads. She had been cut off from her superiors the moment the bridge closed, the pontoon spans blown to deny the enemy advance, and now, with the river a sheet of glass and the roads impassable, she was entirely dependent on the locals and the hospital staff for information.

She had found Petra's ledger by accident. On the third night, unable to sleep, she had wandered the dim corridors of the granary, looking for a quiet place to read a report she had carried in a waterproof satchel. She had passed Petra's improvised desk, where a stack of papers glowed under an oil lamp. One page had slipped free, caught by a draft from the cracked window. Hoss had bent to retrieve it, her fingers brushing the ink, and had seen the column of names—Serbian, Romanian, German, and, at the bottom, a single entry in a language she did not recognize, followed by a note in Petra's looping script: *"Died without a nation. erased from the morning report."*

She had felt a chill that had nothing to do with the winter outside. She had replaced the page, her mind racing. If this ledger existed, if it contained the names of men who had been killed, tortured, disappeared, and then written out of the official record, then the inquiry she was meant to lead could become something far larger than a investigation of a single massacre. It could become a reckoning of the entire system, a system that relied on erasure to function.

Hoss picked up her ink pot and dipped her quill. She began to copy the page she had found, her hand moving with a deliberate slowness. She knew she should turn it over to Reeve, the ranking officer present, but something in the way Petra had looked at her—the quiet defiance, the belief that history happened elsewhere, to other people—had kept her silent. She had nodded, said nothing, and returned to her own work, but the ledger now lived in the back of her mind, a seed she had planted in soil she could not fully control.

Later that afternoon, as she walked the perimeter of the hospital grounds, she met Reeve near the riverbank. He was watching the ice shift and groan under the weight of the remaining pontoon cables, the sound like distant thunder.

"The inquiry," Hoss said, her voice steady despite the wind. "Do you think it will reach us? The bridge is gone. The supply ships won't come until the thaw."

Reeve didn't turn from the river. "The inquiry will report to headquarters. And headquarters will decide what is relevant. Why do you ask?"

Hoss hesitated. She had seen the ledger's first page, the names that shouldn't exist. She had also seen the way Petra tended to the wounded, how she refused to let a man die without at least a name on a page. Hoss herself had lost colleagues to the war's indifferent machinery, had watched reports filed under "combatants" while the men beneath the labels were husbands, fathers, sons. She had her own reasons for believing that a name mattered, even if the world insisted otherwise.

"Because," Hoss said slowly, "if the ledger is found, and it implicates men higher than a field major, the commission I hold may be recalled before it even begins. Headquarters prefers silence. They prefer the gaps."

Reeve was quiet for a long moment. When he turned, his expression was unreadable. "You think I am unaware of the politics, Captain?"

"I think," Hoss replied, "that you and I are both playing a game where the stakes are larger than our commissions. And I think the ledger is the only thing keeping the game from being ended before it truly starts."

They stood in silence as the river sighed, the ice shifting beneath the remaining cables. Above them, the sky was the color of tarnished steel, and the world beyond the valley seemed to shrink with every hour the bridge remained closed.

*****

Petra did not sleep that night. The ledger, still hidden beneath the floorboard, felt less like an object and more like a living thing, its pages pulsing with the names of men who had been forgotten by the world and remembered by her. She had taken to sleeping near the granary's entrance, her cot placed so she could hear the slightest sound, the creak of a floorboard, the shift of a shadow outside.

Before dawn, she heard the crunch of boots on packed snow. She sat up, her hand going instinctively to the floorboard, but the heavy plank remained undisturbed. Through the small window above her cot, she saw figures moving toward the workshop by the water—a small, rambling building run by Mirko, the boatwright, who had lost two sons to the front and asked no questions about the men Petra brought in or the ledger she left with him.

A group of officers' orderlies was approaching the workshop. Petra slipped from her cot, wrapping a woolen shawl around her shoulders, and moved to the door. She could not allow the ledger to be found here, not now, not when the walls were already closing in.

She opened the workshop door just as an orderly stepped inside, lantern in hand. Mirko looked up from a half-finished oar, his face a map of grief and resignation.

"Major Reeve sends word," the orderly said, his voice flat. "The hospital records are to be standardized. That means a full inventory. All documents are to be surrendered for review."

Mirko's hands stopped. He set the oar down slowly. "I have no documents, sir. Only wood. And questions I do not ask."

"The order stands." The orderly gestured to the back of the workshop, where stacks of crates and a workbench dominated the space. "Search it. Thoroughly."

Petra stepped forward, her voice calm but edged with steel. "Major Reeve, if I may—"

"I said the order stands." He did not look at her. "And Captain Hoss is to be informed. Her commission is under review as well."

The orderly moved into the workshop, Mirko trailing behind him, their boots scuffing against the wooden floor. Petra lingered at the threshold, her eyes scanning the space. The ledger was not here; it had been moved last week, smuggled across the yard to a false compartment in a burial cart Mirko used for the dead. But the orderlies would see the crates, the tools, the detritus of a life spent on the water. They would not find the ledger here, but the suspicion would linger, a shadow over everything.

Outside, the wind picked up, rattling the workshop's windows. Petra watched as the men opened a crate of spare planks, sawdust puffing into the lamplight. She felt the familiar tightening in her chest, the sensation of being hunted not by a person but by a system that required erasure. The ledger was safe for now, but the space where it had lived was becoming smaller, the walls closing in like the river ice outside.

*****

Hoss had returned to her room in the granary's east wing, the one with the window overlooking the river. She sat at the small table, the oil lamp casting long shadows across the papers spread before her. She had copied the page from Petra's ledger, the names, the notes, the single entry in the unknown language. She had also copied the ration records she had begged from the quartermaster, the ones Reeve had dismissed as irrelevant. But Hoss had looked at them differently. A pattern emerged: clusters of men listed as "died of exposure" or "transferred to rear hospitals" whose service numbers matched the missing company from Kladovo. The dates aligned. The positions coincided.

She had found the massacre, though she had not yet spoken its name aloud. And Petra's ledger contained the human column the ration records omitted—the faces, the testimonies, the fact that these men had been someone's brother, someone's son, someone who had cried out for water before the ice took them.

Her commission, she now realized, was precariously perched. If she turned the ledger over to Reeve, she risked exposing a conspiracy that could end her career, perhaps her freedom. If she kept it hidden, she risked being recalled for "failure to disclose," a charge that would follow her like a shadow. The bridge was closed, the supply ships grounded, and the only way the truth could leave the valley was by the ship that would come in the morning tide—when the ice broke just enough for a neutral vessel to navigate the frozen channel.

She picked up her quill and dipped it into the ink. She would not send the ledger to Reeve. Not yet. Instead, she would use it. She would reconstruct the massacre in her own report, weaving in the ration records and the names Petra had given her, anonymized just enough to protect the novelist, but enough to force an inquiry. She would make the ledger a weapon of truth, not a relic of secrecy.

But as she wrote, a knock came at her door. Not the heavy tread of an officer, but a lighter, more tentative rhythm.

"Come," she called.

The door creaked open, and Petra slipped inside, closing it behind her. Her face was pale, her eyes bright with the fever that had been circling the hospital for weeks. In her hands, she clutched a small, folded bundle of cloth.

"I couldn't let them search the workshop," Petra said, her voice barely above a whisper. "Mirko moved the ledger. To the burial cart. Under the coffin of the young man who died last night."

Hoss set down her quill. "And you thought it safer here?"

"Safer?" Petra laughed, a sound broken and brittle. "There is no safer. Only less exposed. The guards are posted on the workshop, Hoss. On Mirko's building. They will watch it until the spring. They will watch it until the ledger rots."

Hoss stood, moving to Petra's side. She took the bundle from Petra's hands and unfolded the cloth. Inside was a single page, the ledger's most recent entry, written in Petra's careful script: *"Ilya, Serbian/Romanian/Greek, wounded in the Kladovo retreat, last seen dragging a comrade across the ice. Alive. Hiding in the granary's cellar."*

Hoss's breath caught. Ilya. The soldier with three nationalities, the witness to the massacre, the man Petra had been tending to, the one who had begged her to omit his name from the ledger, who had begged her to let him be erased like the others.

"The walls are closing in," Hoss said slowly. "Reeve has posted guards. The workshop is watched. The granary is being searched. And now the ledger is moving again."

"Then we must move it again," Petra said, her eyes steady despite the fever dancing in them. "But not here. Not where they expect us."

Hoss looked at the page, at the name Ilya, at the truth it contained. She thought of her own commission, of the political machinery that wanted silence, of the men whose names had been erased from the Kladovo report. She thought of Petra, who believed history happened wherever someone refused to let names be erased.

"You are unwell," Hoss said. "The fever—"

"I have tended to dying men all winter," Petra replied. "This is different. This is the ledger. This is the truth."

Hoss stood abruptly, her chair scraping the floor. "Then we will take it to the river. Before the morning tide. If the ledger is to leave this valley, it must move before the ship sails, and before Reeve's men realize what we are doing."

She crossed to the window, looking out at the ice-choked river. The sun was just beginning to push back the night, painting the ice in shades of bruised purple and pale gold. Somewhere in the distance, a cable groaned as the ice shifted.

"Mirko knows the water," Hoss said. "He has lived on it his whole life. If anyone can move a ledger past a garrison undetected, it is he."

Petra nodded, a small, fierce gesture. "Then we begin at once. Before the sun is fully up."

They slipped out into the pre-dawn cold, the hospital still and shadowed behind them. The river stretched before them, a vast, frozen plain leading to the gap where the bridge had been. Shadows lengthened across the snow, and the world seemed to hold its breath, waiting to see whether the truth would be carried forward or buried deeper beneath the ice.

As they reached the burial cart parked near the riverbank, Mirko was already there, his face drawn, his eyes scanning the tree line. He had heard the orderlies' footsteps, had felt the weight of Reeve's suspicion like a physical presence. He had moved the ledger in the night, wrapping it in oilcloth and placing it beneath the shroud of the young soldier who had died of exposure just hours before. The cart was meant for the dead, but now it carried something far more fragile: a record of the living.

"Ready?" Hoss asked.

Mirko nodded, gripping the cart's handles. "I know a path the guards do not. The ice is thinner near the old mill. If we move fast enough, we can be on the other side before the morning report is filed."

They loaded the cart, Petra bundling her own blankets and a flask of warm tea, Hoss taking the lead page she had copied, the one that would become the spine of her report. As the cart creaked forward, the three of them moving in silence toward the river's edge, the frost crunched under the wheels, and the ledger's copper cover pressed against Petra's chest, cold and real and urgent.

The valley was vast and quiet, the river a white artery cutting through the landscape. Behind them, the granary windows glowed with the faint light of lamps, the lives within them unaware that their names were about to be set free. Ahead, the ice stretched toward the broken span of the bridge, a gap that had become the only path forward.

The morning tide was still hours away, but the three of them moved as if the tide were already upon them, carrying the ledger toward the only chance it had of reaching the world beyond the river.

*****

As the first light of dawn bled across the frozen river, Petra, Hoss, and Mirko reached the mill's ruins, half-swallowed by the ice's advance. The guards posted on the workshop were a half-mile upstream, their lanterns bobbing like fireflies against the white. The path Mirko indicated wound through a gully where the ice was thinner, treacherous but passable if they moved swiftly.

"Keep the ledger covered," Hoss whispered, her breath visible in the cold. "If they see the copper, they will know what it is."

Petra held the ledger against her chest, her fingers tracing the spine, feeling the grain of the wood beneath the leather. Every name she had recorded was a rebellion, a refusal to let a man be nothing more than a statistic. Ilya's name was there now, along with the Serbian conscript, the German medic, the Romanian deserter, the woman from the village whose name the officers had smoothed into "casualty." They were all there, pressed against her heart, alive in ink.

Mirko guided the cart with the surety of a man who had navigated these waters since boyhood. The ice groaned and shifted around them, a low, vibrating sound that seemed to speak of the valley's weight, of the centuries of history that had flowed through this place. The gully grew narrower, the ice slick and unstable. Petra kept one hand on the cart's side, feeling the vibrations through the wood, listening to the water beneath—dark, restless, remembering every name that had ever been lost there.

Halfway through the gully, a crack sounded behind them. Not the ice, but a branch snapping. Petra's head snapped up. Two figures emerged from the trees near the gully's entrance—orderlies from the granary, sent to check on the workshop's status. They had evidently noticed the cart's absence, its tracks leading toward the river.

"Halt," one called, raising a lantern. "Where do you think you're going with that?"

Mirko stopped the cart, the wheels sinking slightly into the slush. He turned slowly, his face impassive, but his eyes flickered with a calculation Petra had come to recognize. "Ambulance," he said simply. "Dead soldier. Taking him to the river for the tide."

The orderly lowered the lantern slightly, squinting into the gully. "We have orders to search the workshop. We did not see you leaving with—"

"We are leaving," Mirko interrupted, his voice steady. "The winter is hard on the dead. They deserve the river."

The other orderly stepped forward, his hand moving toward the cart's side. "Let me see what you're hiding."

Petra stepped between the man and the cart, the ledger pressed against her sternum. "You will not see it. This is a medical transport. Refuse, and you will answer to Major Reeve for delaying a burial."

The orderly hesitated. The name Reeve carried weight, even here, especially here. He exchanged a glance with his companion, then nodded reluctantly. "Move along, then. But do not think we have forgotten the workshop."

They stepped aside, and the cart creaked forward again. Petra let out a breath she had been holding, her fingers tightening around the ledger. The ledger was still here, still moving, still dangerous. But they had bought themselves time, a few precious minutes before the next obstacle.

They emerged from the gully onto a stretch of slightly thicker ice, the river's surface stretching out before them, a vast, white plain leading to the broken bridge. In the distance, the supply ship's silhouette could just be made out, a dark shape against the pale sky. It was anchored in the deeper channel, waiting for the ice to break just enough to allow passage.
