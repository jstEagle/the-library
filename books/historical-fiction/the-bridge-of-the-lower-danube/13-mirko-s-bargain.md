## 13. Mirko's Bargain

The workshop smelled of pine tar and old iron, of sawdust soaked through with river water and the particular musk of things left too long in damp dark. Mirko Petrovic stood at his workbench, a plane in his hand, and did not plane. The blade rested against a plank of oak, curled shavings frozen at his feet like pale wood-flowers. Outside, the Danube muttered against the ice, a low, constant sound like a great beast breathing in its sleep. The guards Reeve had posted — two young Austrians with rifles slung and boredom etched into their cheeks — stamped their feet on the slipway, their breath pluming in the grey morning. They had been there since before dawn, since the major’s order came down: *Secure the boatwright. Nothing leaves. No one enters.*

Mirko’s fingers, knotted as olive roots, tightened on the plane’s tote. He was seventy-three winters old, though the army doctors had written sixty-nine on his conscription papers twenty years past, when they took his eldest, Dragan. They had taken Luka two years later. The papers had burned in a stove in Belgrade when the Austrians came the first time, but Mirko remembered the numbers. He remembered the weight of each boy’s rifle when they brought them home in boxes, the way the wood had smelled of foreign soil and cheap varnish. He remembered the workshop standing empty, the boats half-built on the stocks, the river rising and falling without him.

Now the workshop was full again. Not with boats — no timber came downriver anymore, no commissions from the garrison — but with secrets. Under the floorboard beneath the cot in the loft, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed canvas, lay the ledger. Petra’s ledger. The names of men the reports forgot. The names of men Reeve’s reports erased.

Mirko set the plane down. The metal sang against the bench. He moved to the window, a square of grimy glass that looked out on the river. The ice had thickened overnight, a white skin stretching bank to bank, broken only by the dark V of the current where the water ran too fast to freeze. The pontoon bridge was gone, blown by engineers three weeks past. The valley was an island now, cut off from the world by frozen water and military decree.

The guards shifted. One lit a cigarette, cupping the flame. The flare showed a face too young for the lines around the eyes. *Hans*, Mirko thought. The other was *Franz*. He had learned their names the first day, offered them rakia from a chipped enamel mug. They had refused, polite but wary. *Orders, starina. Nothing personal.*

Nothing personal. The phrase was a knife.

He turned back to the bench. On it lay a stack of foolscap sheets, the official stationery of the Imperial and Royal Army, requisitioned from the hospital stores by a sympathetic quartermaster’s clerk — a boy named Jovan who had lost a leg at Cer and now counted bandages with meticulous fingers. The sheets bore the stamp: *K.u.K. Feldspital Nr. 14, Untere Donau*. The ink was iron-gall, dark as dried blood. The pen was a steel nib fitted into a wooden holder, its tip worn to a scratchy flexibility.

Mirko had spent three nights forging the inventory. Three nights of candlelight and trembling hands, not from age but from the precision the lie demanded. He had copied the format from a genuine inventory he had seen years ago, when the garrison had still requisitioned boats for the crossing. He knew the language of bureaucracy: *Item, quantity, condition, valuation, disposition.* He knew the signatures — the quartermaster’s sprawling hand, the major’s tight, angular scrawl. He had practiced them until his wrist cramped, until the ghost of Dragan’s voice in his head said *Tata, you press too hard.*

The forged document listed everything the workshop *should* contain: oak planks, pine spars, copper nails, tar, pitch, hemp rope, canvas, paint, tools. It listed them in quantities that would have made the workshop a going concern, a vital asset to the garrison’s riverine operations. It listed them as *serviceable* or *repairable* or *condemned*. It assigned values in kronen that were neither too high nor too low. It bore the quartermaster’s signature, dated three weeks prior — before the bridge fell, before the investigation arrived, before the guards came.

It was a masterpiece. It was a betrayal of everything Mirko had taught his sons: *A man’s work is his word. A boat either floats or it sinks. There is no middle water.*

But the ledger was not a boat. The ledger was names. And names, Mirko had learned, could be sunk as easily as any hull.

* * *

Major Aldous Reeve sat in his office in the converted granary, the window framed with frost feathers. The stove choked on damp lignite, filling the room with a sulphurous haze that caught the lamplight and turned it bruised. On his desk, the morning report lay unfinished, the column for *Deaths — Other Causes* blinking at him like an accusation.

He had not slept. The inquiry from Vienna — Captain Hoss’s inquiry, though she called it an investigation — had settled into the hospital like a second winter. Her questions were precise, her gaze unflinching. She had asked for the ration records from Kladovo. She had asked for the burial registers. She had asked, with quiet violence, *Where are the men who were not wounded but simply disappeared?*

Reeve had answered with procedure. He had ordered the records standardized. He had posted guards on the boatwright’s workshop because the boatwright was old and sentimental and had once been seen speaking at length with the Nigerian nurse. He had done what officers do: he had contained the problem.

But the problem had a way of leaking.

The door opened. Reeve did not look up. “Enter.”

Boots on wooden floor. Heavy, deliberate. The scent of river and tar and woodsmoke.

“Major.” Mirko’s voice was a rasp, worn smooth by salt and smoke. “You wished to see the inventory.”

Reeve looked up then. The old man stood in the doorway, framed by the grey hall light. He wore his usual sheepskin vest, the wool greasy at the cuffs, a wool cap pulled low over ears that had heard too many artillery barrages. In his hands he held a folded sheaf of papers, the official stationery crisp against the grime of his fingers.

“Petrovic.” Reeve’s voice was flat, stripped of the politeness he wore for Hoss, for the visiting generals, for the nurses. “You were told to remain in your workshop.”

“I am here.” Mirko did not step further into the room. He held out the papers. “The inventory. As requested. Standardized.”

Reeve took the documents. He unfolded them slowly, the paper whispering. His eyes scanned the columns: *Oak, 200 planks, serviceable. Pine, 150 spars, repairable. Copper nails, 50 kg, serviceable.* The numbers were precise. The valuations were reasonable. The signatures — he knew that sprawl, that tight scrawl. He had signed a thousand such forms himself.

He turned to the last page. The quartermaster’s stamp. The date: *12 November 1916*. Three days before the bridge demolition. Three days before the inquiry arrived.

“This is thorough,” Reeve said. “Unexpectedly so.”

“I am a thorough man, Major.” Mirko’s eyes, pale as winter sky, held Reeve’s. “I have built boats on this river for fifty years. A boat that leaks sinks. A list that lies gets men killed.”

“Is that a threat?”

“It is a fact.” Mirko did not blink. “The workshop is clean. The inventory proves it. Your guards can return to the hospital. The river will not wait for bureaucracy.”

Reeve studied the old man. He saw the tension in the shoulders, the whiteness of the knuckles on the folded hands. He saw the calculation: *This is a trade. The workshop’s legitimacy — its legal existence, its right to operate, its protection from seizure — for the safety of whatever is hidden beneath the floorboard.* The major’s own anxiety, the gnawing fear that Hoss would find the discrepancies in *his* reports, that Vienna would cashier him, that his name would join the omitted — that anxiety made the bargain attractive. Accept the inventory. Remove the guards. Gain time. The ledger could be dealt with later, when the ice broke, when the ship came — if the ship came.

But there was a catch. The inventory was forged. If Reeve accepted it, he became complicit. If he rejected it, he admitted he knew it was forged, which meant he knew the workshop was hiding something. Either way, his hands were dirtied.

He had risen from the ranks, the son of a Birmingham metalworker, his accent scrubbed clean by Sandhurst. He knew the price of clean hands: they never held anything real.

“You understand,” Reeve said, his voice dropping, “that if this inventory is false, you have committed fraud against the Crown. Punishable by imprisonment. By execution, in wartime.”

“I understand.” Mirko’s face did not move. “I also understand that the workshop has supplied the garrison for twenty years without a single complaint. That the quartermaster — *Oberleutnant* Kostic — signed this three weeks ago. That if you question it, you question his signature. His competence. His loyalty.”

“Kostic is dead. Dysentery. Ten days ago.”

“Then he cannot defend himself. Nor can he deny it.” Mirko took a breath, the sound rough in the stale air. “The workshop stands, Major. The ledger — *your* ledger, the one you pretend does not exist — it is not here. It never was. But if you tear up this floor, you will find only sawdust and rat droppings. And you will have destroyed a man’s life’s work for nothing.”

“You are very certain.”

“I am certain of the river. I am certain of the ice. I am certain that you need this problem to vanish more than you need a confession.” Mirko’s voice softened, barely. “My sons died for this empire. Dragan at Cer. Luka at Drina. They died in mud and cold, their names on lists that no one reads. I ask only that my workshop — their workshop — be left to rot in peace. That is the bargain.”

Reeve looked at the papers again. The forged stamp. The dead quartermaster’s name. The lie that would hold the wolves at bay for a few more days.

He thought of Hoss, in the back room with her lamp burning late, her fingers stained with ink and righteousness. He thought of the Nigerian nurse, her dark hands moving over white bandages, her ledger growing fat with the uncounted. He thought of the inquiry from Vienna, the telegram he had intercepted: *Commission recalled pending review. Await further orders.* The review would never come. The bridge was closed. The ice was the border now.

He picked up his pen. Signed the receipt at the bottom of the inventory. *Received and verified. A. Reeve, Major, Commanding.*

He slid the papers across the desk. “The guards will be withdrawn at noon. The workshop is… cleared.”

Mirko did not reach for the papers. He stood motionless, the ghost of a smile touching the corner of his mouth — not triumph, not relief, something older. “Thank you, Major.”

“Do not thank me.” Reeve’s hand tightened on the pen. “You have bought time. Time is all any of us have. Use it well.”

Mirko nodded once, a small movement, like a boat rocking on a swell. He turned and left, the door closing softly behind him.

Reeve stared at the closed door. The stove gave a dying rattle. He picked up the inventory, the forged stamp, the dead man’s signature. He should burn it. He should arrest the boatwright. He should report the fraud to Vienna.

He did none of those things. He placed the papers in the bottom drawer of his desk, beneath the unopened telegram from Vienna, beneath the list of names he had omitted from the morning report.

*Use it well,* he whispered to the empty room.

* * *

Petra Okonkwo stood at the window of the nurses’ station, the converted loft above the granary’s main ward. Below, the patients lay in rows of iron cots, their breath a collective cloud in the unheated space. The smell was familiar: antiseptic, sweat, gangrene, the sweet rot of frostbite. The sound was familiar too: the wet cough of typhus, the moan of men dreaming of home, the creak of the building settling into the frozen ground.

She watched Mirko cross the compound, a small dark figure against the white snow. The guards at the workshop gate did not stop him. They stood aside, rifles at ease, as he passed through and disappeared into the gloom of the boatwright’s shed.

Her fingers tightened on the windowsill. The cold bit through the wood into her palms, a sharp clarity.

She had known what Mirko would do. They had spoken the night before, in the workshop’s loft, the ledger spread between them on a crate, a single candle guttering in a bottle. *He will offer the inventory,* she had said. *He will trade the workshop’s name for the ledger’s life.*

*He will,* Mirko had replied, his voice low. *And Reeve will take it. Because he is afraid. And because he knows that if he refuses, he admits he fears what we hide.*

*Is that enough?*

*It buys us days. Perhaps a week. The ice will not hold forever. The ship — if it comes — will need a channel.*

*And if the ship does not come?*

*Then we are all drowned together. But the ledger… the ledger will be on it. Or it will be buried where no major, no captain, no empire can find it.*

Petra had not asked where. She had not asked how. She had learned, in this valley where nations dissolved into mud and frozen breath, that some questions were prayers, and some answers were acts of faith.

She turned from the window. The ward needed her. Ilya, in cot twelve, had spiked a fever again. The Serbian conscript — *or Croatian, or Bosnian, or none of the above, as he insisted* — had been delirious for three days, muttering in languages she did not know, his hands clutching at the blanket as if it were a lifeline. Hoss had taken his statement two days past, her notebook filling with a story that matched the ration records from Kladovo: a unit ordered to “clear the village,” a lieutenant who refused, a captain who shot the lieutenant, a massacre that became a “security operation.” Ilya had signed his name — *Ilya Kovač* — with a hand that shook, the ink blotting into the paper like blood into water.

He had not asked to be omitted. Not this time.

Petra moved to his cot, her step silent on the boards. She carried a basin of water, a cloth, a thermometer. The work was ordinary. The work was holy.

“Sestra,” Ilya whispered, his eyes opening, pale blue in a face wasted to bone. “The water… is it from the river?”

“Melted snow,” she said, dabbing his forehead. “Clean as we can make it.”

“Good.” He closed his eyes. “The river… it remembers. Even when we forget.”

She did not answer. There was no answer. She took his pulse, counted the beats against her watch. One hundred and twelve. Thready. The typhus was winning.

She moved on. Cot fourteen: a Hungarian artilleryman with a shattered femur. Cot sixteen: a Romanian peasant conscripted into the Austro-Hungarian army, his lungs filling with fluid. Cot eighteen: a boy, no more than sixteen, his uniform too large, his eyes hollow with a terror that had no language.

She worked through the morning, the rhythm of care a shield against the larger horror. She gave water, changed dressings, wrote doses on the official charts — *morphine, quinine, digitalis* — and in her pocket notebook, the one she kept beneath her apron, she wrote the names the charts omitted. *Janos Varga, 23, Budapest. Fever. Died 04:17. Not in report.* *Ion Popescu, 19, Iași. Pneumonia. Died 06:02. Not in report.* *The boy. No name. No papers. Died 07:45. Not in report.*

The ledger in the workshop grew. The names accumulated like stones in a cairn.

At noon, the guards at the workshop gate shouldered their rifles and marched back toward the hospital, their boots crunching on the frozen path. Petra watched them go, a small thaw in her chest.

*One bargain kept. One danger deferred.*

But the valley was full of dangers, each with its own timetable.

* * *

The message came with the supply sled that struggled up the valley road from the railhead at Negotin, the horses blowing steam, the driver wrapped in a bearskin that had seen three wars. The sled carried flour, salt, tinned beef, a crate of medical supplies — and a sealed dispatch case for the major.

Petra saw it from the ward window: the sled halting before the granary’s main doors, the driver handing the case to an orderly, the orderly saluting and running inside. She knew the rhythm of this place. Dispatches from Vienna came by sled now, the river closed, the telegraph lines cut by falling trees or partisan sabotage. The case would be opened in Reeve’s office. The contents would be read. The consequences would ripple outward.

She waited, her hands busy with a dressing change, her mind racing. *The inquiry. The recall. The ship.*

Captain Hoss had shown her the telegram two nights past, the paper crumpled in her fist. *Commission recalled. Await further orders.* Hoss had laughed, a harsh, broken sound. *They want me gone before I find what they buried. But the bridge is closed. The ice is the wall. I cannot leave. And neither can the truth.*

Petra had said nothing. She had only touched the ledger’s oilcloth wrapping, hidden in her coat pocket during the day, returned to the workshop at night.

Now, as the afternoon light thinned to bruised violet, the hospital’s telegraph key — silent for weeks — began to chatter in the signals office. The sound carried through the ventilation shafts, a metallic staccato that made the patients stir in their sleep.

*Dot dash dot. Dash dash dash. Dot dot dot.*

SOS. Or perhaps just a message. The signals corporal, a boy with a stammer and a Silver Bravery Medal, would decode it, write it on a flimsy, run it to the major.

Petra finished the dressing, washed her hands in the basin of cold water, dried them on a towel that had once been white. She moved to the door of the nurses’ station, listening.

The major’s door opened. Boots on the stairs. Heavy, fast. Reeve’s orderly burst into the ward, his face pale, his eyes wide.

“Frau Hauptmann!” he called, using the title Hoss had forbidden but the staff used anyway. “Captain Hoss! The major wants you. Now. And… and the nurse. Okonkwo. Both of you.”

Petra did not run. She walked, her stride measured, her face calm. She found Hoss in the back room, the lamp burning, the ration records spread like a map of atrocity. The captain looked up, her expression sharpening.

“What is it?”

“The telegraph.” The orderly gasped. “A message. From… from the Danube Flotilla. A ship. A neutral ship.”

Hoss was on her feet, the notebook forgotten. “Neutral? Which flag?”

“Swedish. Red Cross. The *Svalan*. It’s… it’s at the iron gates. Waiting for the ice to break. It’s taking mail. Witnesses. *Evidence*.” The words tumbled out. “The major… he’s sending a reply. He says… he says the hospital is under quarantine. Typhus. He’s sealing the valley.”

The air in the room went thin. Petra felt the cold of the river in her lungs.

“Quarantine,” Hoss repeated. “A legal fiction. He can’t — ”

“He has,” the orderly said. “The reply is sent. The *Svalan*… it’s supposed to leave at the morning tide. When the ice breaks. Two days, maybe three. The major says no one leaves. No mail. No witnesses. The ledger… if it’s on that ship…”

He looked at Petra. At Hoss. At the ledger’s hidden weight in the room.

“The morning tide,” Petra said. Her voice was steady, surprising herself. “The ship leaves on the morning tide. We have two days. Maybe three.”

Hoss’s face hardened. “Then we work tonight. And tomorrow night. Every name. Every statement. The ledger goes on that ship. *All* of it.”

“And us?” Petra asked.

Hoss met her eyes. “I go. My commission… it’s recalled, but the ship is neutral. I can testify in The Hague. In Stockholm. Wherever they’ll listen.” She paused. “You?”

Petra thought of the ward, the coughing men, the boy with no name. She thought of Mirko, alone in his workshop with his forged inventory and his dead sons. She thought of Ilya, burning with fever, his name finally written.

“I stay,” she said. “The names are here. The work is here. The river… the river keeps what we give it.”

Hoss nodded, once. “Then we’d better start writing.”

* * *

Night fell like a blanket over the valley. The hospital slept, or tried to. In the back room, two women bent over paper, the scratch of pens the only sound. In the workshop, an old man sat by a stove, feeding it scraps of oak, watching the flames eat the wood he had shaped, the wood that would never be a boat.

On the river, the ice groaned, a deep, tectonic sound. Somewhere upstream, a crack was forming. The current, patient and relentless, was eating the winter from below.

The morning tide would come. The ship would come. The ledger would sail.

But first, the night had to be survived.

And in the major’s office, the forged inventory lay in the bottom drawer, a paper shield against the truth. Reeve sat in the dark, the unopened telegram from Vienna burning in his pocket, listening to the river’s slow, inevitable victory over the ice.

He had made his bargain. So had Mirko. So had the women in the back room.

The river would collect them all.
