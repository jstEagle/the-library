## 14. The Ship on the Morning Tide

The message arrived wrapped in oilskin, sealed with the mark of the International Red Cross—a crimson cross stitched over a black sunburst. Petra held it to the light, her breath fogging the paper, and knew, with a certainty that settled deep in her bones like river clay, that the world had shifted again.

"Morning reports?" she asked Mirko, who was oiling the boat’s keel, his weathered hands working methodically despite the hour.

He looked up, his grey eyes softening. "Not this time, *mama*."

She unsealed the message with trembling fingers. The words were brief, but they carried the weight of all the winters that had come before and all the ones yet to come: *Neutral vessel *Sankt Laurentius* cleared for departure at first break of spring tide. Capacity for mail, passengers, and witnesses. One vessel, one opportunity.*

Petra read it twice. Then a third time, her eyes scanning the lines as if they might offer further secrets. The Red Cross ship—Swedish, officially neutral—would carry whatever they chose to entrust to its holds. Mail. Witnesses. And if they were fortunate, the ledger that had become their collective heartbeat.

When she finished reading, she pressed the message to her chest as if it might explode.

"You must be dreaming," Mirko said, noticing her stillness. His voice was rough from winter winds, but gentle. "The ice won't break for months."

Petra turned, her face pale against the dim light filtering through the workshop's single window. "Not months. Weeks. The tide is rising. They say the ice is thinning near Orsova."

She handed him the message. Mirko read it slowly, his expression shifting from disbelief to calculation. When he finished, he folded the paper carefully and tucked it into his vest pocket, close to his heart.

"There's a ship," he said quietly. "And there's a ledger."

The words hung between them like the smell of sawdust and river water that clung to every surface in the workshop. Outside, the Danube had settled into its winter sleep—a gray ribbon of ice and shadow extending into the dark beyond the pontoons. Inside, the warm smell of woodsmoke and oil mingled with the sharper scent of Petra's fear.

She found Captain Hoss in the back room, where they had been reconstructing the Kladovo massacre for three days straight. The German officer sat at a rickety table, a candle burning between them, illuminating maps and lists of names. His face was pale, drawn, but his hands moved with steady certainty as he traced the route of the missing column.

"Petra," he said without looking up. "You wanted to see me about something."

She held out the message. Hoss's face changed instantly—hope warring with caution. He read the words, his jaw tightening, and then looked up to meet her eyes.

"It's true?"

"The *Sankt Laurentius*," Petra said. "One departure. One chance."

Hoss leaned back in his chair, running a hand through his neatly trimmed mustache. "And what does the Red Cross know about war crimes?"

"They know about neutrality," Petra said. "About carrying things that matter. About getting them past lines that shouldn't be crossed."

She could see the wheels turning in his mind. He was a soldier, trained to see angles and obstacles, but also a woman who had chosen to investigate rather than to obey. Something in her posture, the way she held the message, told him she wasn't asking for permission—she was offering him a path forward.

"When does it leave?" he asked.

"At first break of spring tide. When the ice gives way."

Hoss was already calculating. "We need to be there before then. The ledger—"

"We'll get it on board."

They had talked about this moment in fragments, in glances across the hospital ward, in whispered conversations after Mirko's stories of his sons. But now it was real, and the weight of it made her knees weak.

The ledger was thick now, bound in oilskin and leather, its pages filled with names that should never have been erased. Serbian conscripts who died in the snow. Bulgarian laborers conscripted into units they never chose. A Russian deserter who'd found refuge in their infirmary. Even names that had haunted Petra—men whose crimes she could not forget, but whose humanity she could not deny.

It was no longer just her ledger. It belonged to all of them.

* * *

The workshop buzzed with activity the next morning, despite the bitter cold that made breath visible in the dim light. Mirko had arrived before dawn, his coat patched and his beard frosted, carrying supplies for the journey. He worked with quiet efficiency, stacking blankets and bandages, checking the boat's rigging, and warming the ledger in his hands before placing it carefully in a waterproof case.

Petra found him there, humming an old Serbian folk tune as he sanded a section of the boat's prow. The melody was foreign to her ears, but she recognized the rhythm—the same steady beat of a heart that refused to stop.

"You've been awake since before first light," she said.

He nodded without looking up. "The ice is singing tonight. It cracks in the distance, like bones breaking. I thought I'd hear it before it came."

She understood. In the old stories, the river would sometimes communicate with those who listened. The ice didn't just threaten—they had to treat it as a living thing, something that could be reasoned with, bargained with, respected.

"What time is the ship due?" she asked.

"They say first light. But the tide waits for no man, *mama*. We move when it moves."

Petra examined the boat, noting the fresh repairs, the reinforced hull. Mirko had transformed it from a simple river craft into something more—something worthy of the burden they carried. In the bow, where the ledger rested in its case, he had carved a small cross into the wood, its arms spread wide.

"It's beautiful," she said.

He smiled, showing teeth that were more pearl than tooth in the gray light. "A blessing for safe passage."

They worked in companionable silence, preparing the boat for its journey. Petra wrapped the ledger in layers of linen and oilcloth, then placed it in the case Mirko had prepared. It felt impossibly heavy in her hands, despite its size. A small chest of memories, a repository of truths that might save lives or damn others.

When she was done, she stood back and looked at her work. The ledger was no longer just hers—it had become something larger, something that belonged to the wounded, the forgotten, the nameless. It was their voice, their testimony, their refusal to let history be written by those who never had to look into the eyes of the dying.

"Did you sleep at all?" Mirko asked.

"Not really."

"You look like hell."

"I look like survival."

He should have taken it as an insult, but instead he laughed—a sound like wind through pine trees. "Good. We all look like survival. It's what we've earned."

* * *

By midmorning, the workshop was a hive of activity. Ilya arrived first, his face pale but determined, his uniform stained with the marks of fever and longing. He had spent three days recovering from the typhoid that had threatened to claim him, and now he moved with the careful grace of a man who understood his own fragility.

"Captain Hoss," he said when he saw her, "I've thought about it."

She looked up from the map she was studying, her expression softening. "About what?"

"About being a witness. About testifying."

Hoss studied him for a long moment, then nodded. "And if they try to stop you?"

"I'll go as mail," Ilya said simply. "Or as a deserter. Or as whatever they need me to be to get past their guards."

Petra watched this exchange from across the room, feeling something shift in her chest. She had expected resistance, fear, the kind of paralysis that comes when you realize you're being asked to cross a line you never intended to cross. But Ilya—who had once claimed three nationalities and finally settled on none—was choosing to own his story.

"Your name," she said.

He looked at her, really looked at her, and for the first time since she'd met him, she saw something other than the soldier, the refugee, the man with too many faces. She saw a person who had made a choice.

"Aleksei Volkov," he said. "I'll sign my real name."

Petra's hands trembled as she reached for the ink. It had taken her three days to decide what to do about his confession, about the unit he'd served, about the men he'd helped to disappear into the snow. In the end, she had chosen to record him as he was—not as he wished to be, not as the world needed him to be, but as he had chosen to become.

Now, watching him sign his name, she understood that this was what they were all doing: choosing to become something more than the circumstances that had shaped them.

The signature was careful, deliberate. When he finished, he held the paper up so they could all see it.

"I'll go with you," he said. "If they'll take me."

Hoss was already moving, gathering her papers, her credentials. "The ship's manifest is limited. We have to be selective."

"But he's the only one who knows—"

"I know," Hoss said. "We'll find a way."

Outside, the sun was rising, painting the frozen river in shades of gold and steel. The ice was indeed cracking, you could hear it now—a low, continuous sound like distant thunder or the moaning of an ancient beast. They had less than twelve hours before the *Sankt Laurentius* would slip free from its moorings and begin the journey downstream.

Petra worked with a feverish intensity, packing the ledger into its case, checking the seals, making sure every page was secure. Mirko had arrived with his own supplies—blankets, food, medical kits—and was helping organize the evacuation of whatever else might be useful to the investigators.

"They're talking about the typhus," Ilya said as he helped load bandages. "Reeve's making moves."

Petra paused in her work. "What kind of moves?"

"Saying the whole ward's infected. Locking down the place."

The words hit her like a physical blow. She had suspected as much, but hearing it confirmed made her stomach clench. They had to get out before they could be trapped inside their own salvation.

"You can't let him quarantine us," she said. "Not if we're taking the ledger."

"We won't be quarantined," Hoss said, appearing beside them. Her voice was sharp with urgency. "We'll leave before he can think of it. The ship doesn't leave for another six hours, and we have to make sure the ledger gets there."

"But what about the others?" Ilya asked. "The wounded."

Petra looked at him, seeing the fear behind his brave face. "They'll be safe once we're gone. The records will follow."

"And if Reeve finds out we're leaving?"

"Then we'll have to outrun him," she said. "Or convince him that we're not worth chasing."

The irony wasn't lost on any of them. They were fugitives now, not for their actions, but for their refusal to let silence win.

* * *

By midafternoon, word had spread through the hospital that the ship would depart at dawn. Petra had spent the morning taking statements from the remaining patients—men who were well enough to speak but not yet well enough to travel. Each testimony was another thread in the web they were building, another proof that history was not written by generals and bureaucrats, but by those willing to bear witness.

Ilya had been the most forthcoming, his confession detailed and chilling. He spoke of the unit that had carried out the massacre at Kladovo, of the orders that had been given, of the men who had followed them without question. But he also spoke of the moments when doubt had crept in, when the weight of their actions had become too heavy to carry.

"They made us do it," he had said to Hoss during their interview. "But we didn't have to make it worse."

Petra had nodded, understanding the difference between survival and complicity. She had recorded his testimony in the ledger, alongside the names of the dead, and now she wondered if they had made the right choice in letting him go.

The workshop was nearly ready. Mirko had arranged the supplies in the boat's hold, and Hoss had secured her papers and evidence in a separate compartment. They had agreed on a system—if anything happened to them, the ledger would be the last thing anyone would find, safely sealed and marked for retrieval by whoever the Red Cross might send.

But as evening approached, Petra found herself thinking about what came next. Not the immediate danger, not the risk of capture or death, but the longer journey—the miles downstream, the questions they would face, the truth they would have to defend before any audience that might care to listen.

She found Hoss on the dock, staring out at the river. The ice was indeed breaking, small sections shifting and cracking with a sound that was almost musical in its regularity. The water beneath, visible through gaps in the frozen surface, was dark and swift.

"They're coming," Hoss said without turning around. "The patrol boats. Reeve must have sent word."

Petra joined her, wrapping her coat tighter around her shoulders. "We leave when they're not looking."

"And if they are?"

"Then we go anyway."

Hoss finally turned to face her, her expression unreadable. "You're not afraid?"

"I'm terrified," Petra admitted. "But I'm not going to let fear write history for us."

They stood there for a moment, two women on the edge of something larger than themselves, watching the ice respond to the moonlight like a living thing. The ledger was ready, hidden in the boat's hold where it would be safe until they reached the other side. Ilya was waiting to sign his name, and Mirko was preparing to drive them to the ship's dock.

All that remained was the choice to step forward.

* * *

The ship arrived at first light, its silhouette stark against the pale sky. Petra had never seen a vessel so large, so imposing, carved from dark wood and brass. It slipped through the channel with a quiet grace that belied its purpose as a military transport, and as they approached, the crew came down the gangplank with the efficiency of people who had seen much of the world and less of its mercy.

Hoss stepped aboard first, her uniform crisp, her demeanor unflinching. She had spent her career presenting an image of authority, but Petra had seen the woman beneath—the same woman who had chosen to investigate rather than to ignore, who had risked everything to give voice to the voiceless.

"Captain Hoss," the ship's captain said, a man whose face was a map of weathered years. "What cargo do you bring?"

"Passengers," she said. "And correspondence."

Petra followed, her boots crunching on the wooden planks. The ledger was secured in its case, hidden beneath a pile of medical supplies. Mirko stayed on shore, his figure a dark shape against the brightening sky, watching as the boat was loaded with their precious cargo.

Ilya lingered behind, his face drawn. "Are you sure about this?"

"About what?"

"About me going. About leaving you here."

Petra knelt to his level, her hands trembling slightly. "You're not leaving me. You're joining the story."

He looked at her, really looked at her, and for a moment she saw something she had never seen before—not the soldier, not the refugee, but a man who had found his way home to something worth fighting for.

"After all," she said, "history happens wherever someone refuses to let names be erased."

He smiled then, a real smile that reached his eyes. "I'll sign my name at the other end of the world if I have to."

The ship's horn sounded—a long, mournful note that seemed to echo across the water. The crew began to move, preparing for departure. Petra checked the seals on the ledger one last time, then turned to face the open river, where the ice was beginning to crack in earnest.

They had made it this far. They had defied every expectation, every attempt to silence them. And now, as the first boats were lowered to take them aboard, she felt the weight of what they had accomplished—and what still lay ahead.

The Danube would carry them downstream, past the frozen bridges and the checkpoints and the borders that had seemed so impenetrable. It would carry them to a world that might, if they were lucky, listen.

And it would carry the ledger, heavy with names and testimonies and truths, to somewhere beyond the reach of those who had tried to erase them.

Petra stepped onto the gangplank, feeling the wood creak beneath her feet. Behind her, the workshop stood silent against the morning light, its windows dark but its purpose fulfilled. Ahead, the ship waited, its sails unfurled to catch whatever wind might blow.

The river would decide when they left. But they would go anyway.
