## 15. Testimony of the Wounded

The gangplank shuddered under Petra’s weight, a seamless of salt‑wet wood that sighed with each step. Behind her, the workshop’s single window glimmered dark against the thinning dawn; ahead, the supply ship rode the sluggish swell of the Danube, its hull black and low in the water, sails furled like the wings of some great, patient bird. The river was moving now, the ice sheet that had gripped the Lower Danube for months cracking and shoving itself apart in long, grinding sighs. Downstream, where the current was faster, the water ran dark and restless, carrying the valley’s grief toward a sea that did not remember the names of the dead.

Petra took the ledger from her breast pocket. The leather was softened by winter inside her coat, the pages stained with river mud and the faint scent of antiseptic. She had not closed it since Mirko had pressed it into her hands the night before the ship’s departure, the ink still wet on some entries, others left trembling half‑finished. Hoss had taken the other copy, the one she had kept hidden beneath the floorboard, and now it rested on the captain’s desk in the wardroom, a thin volume of paper that contained more truth than all the official reports from the front.

“Sit, nurse,” Hoss said. Her voice was low, the accent clipped and professional. She wore the grey of the inspectorate, and her eyes were the colour of a winter sky just before snow. “We have three days of downriver travel before the tide takes us past the border checkpoints. I intend to use the crossing to finish what we started in the granary.”

Petra nodded, settling onto a stool near the ship’s stern, where the wind carried the smell of brine and wet wool. The ledger lay open between them. The first page was a map of names—Serbian conscripts, Bulgarian volunteers, German stragglers, Russian deserters—each name a syllable of a life that the officers’ reports had reduced to a casualty count. The pages that followed were blank, waiting.

“The first entry,” Petra said, “is a Serbian conscript they called only ‘Wounded by shrapnel, evacuated to granary, 24 January.’ He lived for three days. I recorded his name, his mother’s village, the fact that he had been a baker before the mobilization. The report omitted him entirely.”

Hoss tilted her head, a faint frown etching the corners of her mouth. “And you believe the omission is deliberate?”

“History happens wherever someone refuses to let names be erased,” Petra said, quoting the phrase she had written in the ledger’s frontispiece. “But the officers—they have their own histories to protect. A name attached to a massacre is a charge. A number is just a statistic.”

The ship’s bell tolled three times, the sound thin and reedy across the water. Outside, the river’s edge was a thin line of slush and open water, and beyond that, the far bank was a silhouette of leafless trees against a sky the colour of gunmetal. The cold was still, but the ice was breaking, and with it came the sound of the valley waking, or at least, trying to.

They began with the men who were well enough to speak, those whose fevers had broken and whose wounds had knitted enough for conversation. The first was a Polish‑Lithuanian corporal named Tomasz, who had been found huddling in a barn with a shattered femur and a story he could not stop telling. His voice was a rasp, but his eyes were bright, flickering with a desperate need to be heard.

“They came at dawn,” Tomasz said, his words clustering around the edges of Polish and Lithuanian, mixing like the rivers of his homeland. “The bridge was already down. We were crossing on the pontoons, the ice shifting under our feet. The officers ordered us to keep moving, to not stop for the men who were drowning in the river. I stopped. I always stop.
