## 16. Reeve's Counterattack

The order came at first light, carried on a slip of paper that smelled of ink and the major’s tobacco. Petra read it where she stood beside the iron stove in the triage corridor, the heat failing to reach her fingertips. The script was neat, the language bureaucratic and absolute: *By authority of the Imperial Medical Corps, and in consideration of the preservation of garrison health, the field hospital at Lower Danube is hereby placed under strict quarantine effective this date. All personnel, patients, and materiel are restricted to the compound. No ingress or egress permitted without written dispensation from the commanding officer. Diagnosis: suspected typhus. Containment protocol: immediate.*

She folded the paper once, twice, the creases sharp as a surgeon’s incision. Outside the window, the Danube lay white and still beneath a sky the color of bruised plum. The ice had thickened overnight, a groan in its joints like an old man turning in bed. No ship would come. No ship could come. The bridge was gone, the pontoon spans blasted into the current two weeks past. The valley was a bottle, and Reeve had just stoppered it.

“Typhus,” she said, the word flat in the steam-thick air. “There is no typhus.”

Sister Agata, wrapping a bandage roll with practiced fingers, did not look up. “There is always typhus when the major needs there to be. Last winter it was cholera. The winter before, relapsing fever. The diagnosis follows the order, Petra. Not the other way round.”

“And the inquiry?”

“Captain Hoss is confined to her quarters. I saw the sentry posted at her door — young Kovacs, the one with the cough. He looked ashamed.” Agata tied off the bandage, snapped the roll onto the shelf. “The major visited the dying ward at midnight. Spoke with the sergeant-major for an hour. By dawn the quarantine notice was on every door.”

Petra’s mind moved fast, cataloguing. The ledger — *the ledger*. It lay beneath the loose floorboard in the back room where Hoss had worked, the same room where they had spent three days taking statements from men whose voices cracked with fever and fear. Ilya’s signature, fresh ink on the page, his true name finally written. Three hundred and twelve names now, each a small defiance. If the quarantine held, the ledger would never reach the ship. The ship — *the ship*. The neutral supply vessel rumored to be waiting at the ice edge, contracted to carry mail and witnesses downriver when the thaw broke. One departure. One chance.

And Petra herself, reassigned. The order had named her specifically: *Nurse Okonkwo is detailed to the dying ward for the duration of the emergency. Her duties in triage and records are suspended.*

Records. The word struck her like a blow. He knew. He knew what she did in the quiet hours, the names she copied from the margins of ration chits, from the whispers of men who had no one else to tell. He knew, and he had built a wall of procedure around it.

“Petra.” Agata’s hand on her forearm, calloused and warm. “You are not reassigned. You are *removed*. There is a difference.”

“I know the difference.” She pulled her arm free, gentle but firm. “I also know the dying ward. I know every man in it. I know which ones have names in my pocket and which ones have only numbers on their charts.”

“Then go to them. That is what you do.” Agata pressed a small packet into her palm — dried figs, wrapped in waxed paper. “Eat. You forget. The major counts on you forgetting.”

* * *

The dying ward occupied the east wing of the granary, a long room where the roof sagged and the windows wept condensation. Twenty beds, nineteen occupied. The air was a thick broth of breath, antiseptic, and the sweet rot of gangrene. Light came thin through the frost-laced glass, pale as milk.

Petra moved down the row, her steps silent on the planks. She knew each man by the sound of his breathing — the wet rattle of Private Vuk, the shallow whistle of Corporal Marek, the silence of the boy from Timișoara who had stopped breathing an hour before dawn. She had washed him herself, closed his eyes, written his name in the ledger with ink that trembled on the nib.

Now she stopped at the bed of Sergeant Kovač, the oldest of them, his face a map of campaigns. His eyes, clouded with cataract, tracked her movement.

“Nurse,” he rasped. “The major. He comes here?”

“He came. Last night.”

“And?”

“And he left with a paper that says we are all poison.” She adjusted his blanket, tucking it at his shoulders. “He does not want the captain to leave. He does not want the ledger to leave. He does not want *us* to leave.”

Kovač’s hand, gnarled and trembling, found hers. His grip was paper-thin, but the intent was iron. “The ledger. The names. They are here?”

“Hidden. Safe. For now.”

“Good.” He closed his eyes. “When the ship comes… you will put me in it. My name. My mother’s name. The village. All of it.”

“You are not dying today, Sergeant.”

“No. Today I am witnessing. That is enough.” His thumb brushed her knuckles. “Tell the captain… tell her the old man remembers. Tell her Kladovo was not the only place. Tell her the river remembers too.”

Petra leaned down, kissed his forehead — dry, hot, smelling of woodsmoke and time. “I will tell her. If I can reach her.”

“You will reach her. You are the river, Nurse. You find the cracks.”

She stood, the weight of his words settling in her chest alongside the figs. *The river. The cracks.* She had been thinking in straight lines — doors, windows, the gate, the ice. But water did not move in straight lines. It seeped. It undermined. It waited.

She moved on, checking pulses, moistening lips, recording the small deaths and the smaller rallies in the official log with a hand that did not shake. The major’s quarantine was a dam. But dams broke. They always broke.

* * *

Captain Verena Hoss’s quarters were the back room of the granary, the same room where the ledger had lived beneath the floorboard. Now it held a cot, a chair, a table, and a woman who had not slept in thirty-six hours.

Hoss sat at the table, a single candle guttering beside a stack of statements — signed, witnessed, sealed with the thumbprints of men who could not write. Her uniform jacket was unbuttoned, her hair escaping its pins. She looked up when the door opened, her hand moving instinctively to the revolver in its holster, then relaxing when she saw Petra.

“You’re late,” Hoss said. Her voice was raw, scraped by cold and something harder. “The sentry said you were reassigned.”

“I was. To the dying ward. I came the long way.” Petra closed the door softly, engaging the latch. “The major has posted guards at both ends of the corridor. He’s sealed the windows with shutters. The only way in or out is past Kovacs, and he has orders to shoot anyone attempting escape.”

“Escape.” Hoss laughed, a short, ugly sound. “He calls it escape. I call it doing my job. My commission — ” She broke off, pressing her fingers to her temples. “My commission is recalled. A courier got through before the bridge fell. Orders from Vienna. The inquiry is suspended. I am to return to headquarters ‘pending review.’ Which means they’ve found someone more pliable to bury the Kladovo report.”

“And the ledger?”

Hoss’s eyes sharpened. “The ledger is the only thing that matters now. The statements corroborate the ration records. The names corroborate the statements. Without the ledger, it’s all hearsay. With it…” She stood, moving to the window, pressing her palm against the cold glass. “With it, the massacre at Kladovo becomes a document. Not a rumor. A document that survives the war.”

“Can you get it out?”

“Not from here.” She turned, the candlelight mapping the planes of her face — exhaustion, resolve, a flicker of something Petra might have called fear. “The major has thought of everything. The quarantine is airtight on paper. Legally, he’s within his authority. Typhus is a Class A contagion. The garrison surgeon signed the certification — Dr. Weiss, who hasn’t examined a patient in three years but knows which side his bread is buttered on. By the time anyone challenges it, the spring thaw will have washed the evidence away.”

“Not if the ship sails.”

“The ship.” Hoss shook her head. “The ship is a rumor. A hope. Even if it exists, even if it waits at the ice edge, the quarantine extends to the riverbank. No one leaves the compound. No boats launch. No cargo moves. The major has posted artillery observers on the bluff with orders to fire on any unauthorized movement on the ice.”

Petra set the figs on the table, unwrapping them slowly. “Then we move *authorized* cargo.”

Hoss frowned. “There is no authorized cargo. The only traffic is burial details. The ground is frozen; they’re stacking bodies in the icehouse until spring. The major himself approved the protocol.”

“Burial details.” Petra’s mind raced, pieces clicking. “The icehouse is outside the compound. On the river road. The cart goes there and back. The driver…”

“Mirko.” Hoss said the name like a key turning. “The boatwright. He drives the cart. He’s the only one the major trusts with the horses.”

“And he asks no questions.” Petra felt the first spark of something that might be hope, cold and sharp as the river ice. “He has a workshop by the water. He has a boat. He has… ” She hesitated.

“What?”

“He has a mother. A woman who comes to the hospital every week to sit with the dying. She speaks to no one. The major’s men ignore her. She is… invisible.”

Hoss studied her, the candle flame reflecting in her eyes. “Invisible is good. Invisible moves things.” She picked up a fig, bit into it, chewed slowly. “But the ledger is here. Under the floorboard. To get it to Mirko’s cart, it has to cross the compound. Past the sentries. Past the major’s window.”

“Not the ledger.” Petra’s voice was quiet, certain. “Not all of it. The original stays. We make a copy. The statements — the signed statements — those go. The ledger… the ledger is names. The statements are testimony. Testimony is what the inquiry needs. Testimony is what the world needs.”

“And the names?”

“The names stay with me. They always have.” Petra met Hoss’s gaze. “I will copy them. Every one. Tonight. While the major thinks I am wiping brows in the dying ward.”

“Tonight.” Hoss repeated the word, tasting it. “The major expects you to break. He expects me to break. He expects the quarantine to rot the truth out of us before the thaw.”

“Then we don’t break.” Petra picked up the pen from the table, feeling its weight, the familiar balance. “We write.”

* * *

The dying ward at night was a different country. The lamps were lowered to blue shadows, the better to spare the eyes of men who would not see another dawn. The air grew colder as the hours passed, the stove in the corner coughing its last coals. Petra moved between the beds like a ghost, her notebook open on the fold-down shelf beside each pillow.

She wrote by feel as much as sight: the name, the rank, the unit, the village, the mother’s name, the wound, the date. She wrote the things the official reports omitted — the soldier who had sung to his comrades while they waited for the shelling to stop, the boy who had carried his wounded officer three kilometers on his back, the old reservist who had shared his last cigarette with a prisoner. She wrote the names of the dead who had no markers, the living who had no witnesses.

Private Vuk, 23, Šabac. *Mother: Jelena. Sister: Mara. Sang “Tamo Daleko” while the gas came.*

Corporal Marek, 31, Brno. *Wife: Anna. Children: Tomas, Eliska. Carried Lieutenant Horvat from the ravine. Frostbitten hands.*

The boy from Timișoara — no name on his dog tag, no papers. *Called himself Ioan. Spoke Romanian, Hungarian, German. Laughed when the morphine came. Died at 03:17.*

Her hand cramped, her eyes burned, the cold seeped through her boots into her bones. But the names flowed, a river beneath the ice. She wrote until the ink froze in the nib, then warmed it at the lamp, wrote more. She wrote until the first gray light seeped through the frost-laced windows, turning the breath of the sleepers into visible clouds.

She did not hear the door open. She did not hear the boots on the planks. She only knew when a shadow fell across her page, blocking the lamp light.

“Nurse Okonkwo.”

She did not start. She did not hide the notebook. She capped the pen, closed the book, and turned.

Major Aldous Reeve stood in the doorway of the dying ward, his greatcoat buttoned to the throat, his cap pulled low. Behind him, two sentries loomed, rifles at the ready. His face was gray in the dawn light, the skin loose over his jaw, the eyes sunk deep in sockets bruised by sleeplessness. He looked, Petra thought, like a man who had been fighting a war for years and had just realized he was losing.

“Major.” She stood, the notebook tucked against her hip. “The ward is quiet. Two passed in the night. I have recorded them in the official log.”

“Quiet.” He stepped inside, the sentries remaining at the threshold. “That is what I require. Quiet. Order. The appearance of discipline.” He moved down the aisle between the beds, his gaze skipping over the men — the living, the dying, the dead. “I received a report from Vienna this morning. The inquiry is suspended. Captain Hoss is relieved of her duties. Her commission is under review. She will be transferred to a secure facility in Belgrade as soon as the ice breaks.”

“And the statements?”

“Confiscated. Destroyed. The ration records she cited have been… corrected.” He stopped at Kovač’s bed, looking down at the old sergeant’s sleeping face. “Kladovo never happened. The massacre is a fiction invented by agitators and defeatists. The official record will show a skirmish. Casualties: enemy combatants. No civilians.”

Petra’s fingers tightened on the notebook. “The men who signed. They will know.”

“They will know what they are told to know.” Reeve turned, his eyes finding hers in the dimness. “And you, Nurse Okonkwo. You will return to triage. Your reassignment is revoked. You are too valuable in records. Too… *thorough*.”

“I am assigned to the dying ward.”

“You are assigned where I say you are assigned.” His voice dropped, intimate and dangerous. “I know about the ledger. I know about the floorboard. I know about the boatwright and his workshop and the mother who sits with the dying. I know about the ship — *if* a ship exists. I have men watching the ice. I have men watching the road. I have men watching *you*.”

The threat hung between them, cold as the river. Petra felt no fear, only a vast, calm clarity. *He knows. He has always known. He let it happen because he thought he could contain it. He thought the quarantine would be the lid on the jar.*

“Then you know I will not stop,” she said.

Reeve smiled — a thin, joyless expression. “No. I don’t expect you to stop. I expect you to make a mistake. To reach too far. To trust the wrong person.” He leaned closer, his breath smelling of coffee and cigarettes. “The quarantine is not about typhus, Nurse. It is about *you*. All of you. The captain. The boatwright. The old woman. The men in this ward. You are the infection. And I am the cure.”

He turned, his greatcoat sweeping the floor. “Sergeant Kovač. Move him to the isolation shed. His cough is… suspicious. The others — increase the carbolic spray. Double the guard on the captain’s door. No visitors. No messages. No exceptions.”

The sentries snapped to attention. “Jawohl, Herr Major.”

As Reeve swept out, the door closing behind him with a heavy thud, Petra exhaled a breath she hadn’t known she was holding. The lamp flickered. The men breathed on. Somewhere in the distance, the ice groaned, a deep, resonant sound that vibrated in the floorboards.

She opened her notebook to a fresh page. At the top, she wrote: *Quarantine declared 14 February 1916. Typhus diagnosis falsified. Major Reeve intends to suppress evidence of Kladovo massacre. Captain Hoss confined. Ledger secured. Statements secured. Plan: burial detail.*

She underlined the last word three times.

* * *

The burial detail assembled at noon in the compound yard, the sky a low white ceiling pressing down on the frozen mud. Four bodies, wrapped in canvas shrouds stitched from discarded tents, lay on a rough wooden cart drawn by two massive draft horses. The horses blew steam, their hooves clattering on the ice-rutted ground.

Mirko sat on the driver’s bench, his hands folded over the reins, his face impassive beneath a fur hat that had seen three wars. He wore a coat patched at the elbows, boots wrapped in sacking. He did not look at the hospital windows, though Petra knew he felt her eyes.

Beside the cart stood the old woman — Mirko’s mother, Petra realized with a start. She was smaller than she had seemed from a distance, bent double under a wool shawl the color of dried blood. Her hands, gloved in knitted mittens, clutched a rosary. She did not speak. She did not look at the sentries who flanked the cart, or at the sergeant who checked the burial permit, or at the major who stood on the granary steps watching with the patience of a spider.

Petra stood at the back of the formation, the nurse’s cape pulled tight, her cap pinned low. She held the official burial register, the names of the four men written in her own careful script: *Private Vuk, Corporal Marek, Ioan (unknown), Sergeant Kovač (transferred to isolation — pending).*

The last name was a lie. Kovač still breathed in the dying ward. But the register had to balance. The cart had to carry four bodies.

She watched as the orderlies lifted the shrouded forms — three from the icehouse, one from the dying ward, a man who had died at dawn, a replacement for Kovač. They loaded them with the mechanical efficiency of men who had done this a hundred times. The canvas was stiff with frozen blood and the sap of the pine boxes that had held them briefly.

Mirko’s mother moved then, shuffling forward on legs that seemed too thin to hold her. She reached out, her mittened fingers brushing the edge of the topmost shroud. She whispered something — a prayer, a name, a goodbye — and stepped back, her eyes dry.

Petra felt a sudden, fierce love for this woman she had never spoken to. *The mother. The watcher. The one who sees.*

The sergeant signed the register, barely glancing at it. “Proceed. Straight to the icehouse. No stops. No deviations. The major’s orders.”

Mirko clicked his tongue. The horses leaned into their collars. The cart groaned forward, wheels crunching on the frozen ruts.

Petra watched them go — the cart, the horses, the old woman walking beside it with the steady gait of someone who has walked this road all her life. The sentries fell in behind. The major turned and went back inside.

She looked down at the register in her hands. The four names. Three true, one false. The false one was a key.

She slipped the register into her coat pocket, next to the notebook. Her fingers brushed the figs Agata had given her — still there, uneaten. She would need the sugar. The night would be long.

* * *

The dying ward was colder now, the stove dead. Petra had been moved to a cot in the corner, ostensibly to monitor the isolation shed where Kovač had been taken. In reality, she was watched. A sentry paced the corridor outside the door. Another stood at the window, his rifle resting on the sill.

She lay still, listening to the breathing of the nineteen men. She listened for the sound of the cart returning — the crunch of wheels, the clop of hooves, the creak of harness. She listened for the signal: three taps on the water pipe that ran along the back wall of the ward, the code Mirko had taught her years ago when she first came to the valley, a language of knocks and pauses.

Time stretched. The light faded from gray to blue to black. The lamps were extinguished by order — *fuel conservation*. Only the blue shadows of moonlight on snow remained.

She reviewed the plan in her mind, each step a bead on a rosary.

*Step one:* The cart returns. Mirko’s mother creates a distraction at the gate — a faint, a prayer, a need for water. The sentries look away.

*Step two:* Mirko unloads the empty coffins at the icehouse. He loads the ledger — the copy, the statements — into the false bottom of the second coffin, the one meant for the unknown boy. The real boy is already buried in the frozen trench by the river.

*Step three:* The cart returns to the compound. The sentries wave it through — *authorized cargo, burial detail returning empty*.

*Step four:* At the workshop, Mirko transfers the package to the boat. The boat waits in the slip, hidden beneath a tarp painted with quarantine symbols — *Plague. Keep Away.*

*Step five:* When the ice breaks, the boat goes downriver. The ship takes it. The truth leaves the valley.

*Step six:* Petra stays. She stays with the men who have no names but the ones she gave them. She stays with the river that taught her how to move.

A knock. Three sharp raps on the pipe. Pause. Two raps. Pause. One.

*Cart returned. Package loaded. Mother distracting sentries. Clear to move.*

Petra rose from the cot, her muscles stiff with cold. She dressed in the dark — wool layers, boots, coat, cap. She took the notebook, the register, the pen. She blew out the candle stub she had kept hidden beneath her pillow.

The sentry at the window coughed, a wet, hacking sound. The one in the corridor shuffled his boots.

She opened the door a crack. The corridor was dim, lit by a single lantern at the far end. The sentry at the window had his back to her, staring out at the white waste. The other stood by the stairwell, smoking a cigarette, the ember a red eye in the gloom.

She moved like the river — silent, inevitable. She passed the smoking sentry, close enough to smell the cheap tobacco and the fear sweat beneath his wool. He did not turn. He had been told to watch the door, not the hallway. He had been told the infection was inside.

She reached the stairwell, descended to the ground floor. The main doors were barred, heavy timbers bolted into iron brackets. But the side door — the one that opened onto the woodpile and the path to the workshop — had a latch that could be worked from inside if you knew the trick. Agata had shown her. *For emergencies,* the sister had said. *For when the major locks us in.*

Petra’s fingers found the catch, lifted, turned. The door swung outward on hinges that had been greased with lard and silence.

The night air hit her like a physical blow — cold so intense it burned the lungs, so clear it made the stars look close enough to touch. The compound yard was empty, the cart gone, the icehouse a dark hump against the river road. The sentry posts were manned, but the men huddled in their greatcoats, staring at their boots, waiting for relief that would not come.

She moved along the shadow of the granary wall, keeping to the blind spots she had mapped in three winters. The path to the workshop was a narrow track between the icehouse and the latrine trench, slick with frozen sludge. She knew every stone, every dip, every place where the snow drifted deep.

The workshop loomed ahead — a long, low building of rough timber and corrugated iron, its windows dark. The slipway beside it cut into the riverbank, a notch in the ice where Mirko’s boat waited, a broad-beamed skiff painted battleship gray.

No light showed. No sound but the river’s slow pulse against the ice.

She reached the door, lifted the latch. Inside, the smell of pine tar and linseed oil and old sweat. The floor was dirt, scattered with shavings. Tools hung on pegs — adzes, caulking irons, a brace and bit. In the corner, a cot with blankets folded military-tight.

And in the rafters, a shape — a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with rope.

She climbed the ladder rungs nailed to the wall, her fingers numb. The bundle was heavy, dense. She untied the rope, peeled back the oilcloth.

Not the ledger. Not the statements.

A single sheet of paper, folded small. Mirko’s handwriting, blocky and crude: *Major searched workshop at midnight. Found nothing. But he knows. He will return with axes at dawn. Workshop to be demolished — navigation hazard. Boat moved to deep water. Ledger and statements in boat. You stay. I stay. Mother stays. We are the river too.*

Petra read it twice. Three times. The cold in her chest was not from the air.

*Demolished. At dawn.*

She refolded the note, tucked it into her pocket beside the figs. She replaced the oilcloth, tied the rope. She descended the ladder, her mind racing, recalculating.
