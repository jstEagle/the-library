## 17. Breaking Quarantine

The quarantine had a flavor like cold iron and stale lint. Petra did not eat the broth brought by the orderlies; she pushed it aside, untouched, and watched the ledger lie folded beneath her cot, its edges softening from the damp of the ward. The typhus decree had not yet turned the granary into a charnel house, but the word had already done its work: the doors were sealed, the windows curtains-drawn, and a guard posted at the head of every corridor. Reeve’s legal fiction had become her cage, and yet—oddly—it had also become the only thing that made the smuggling thinkable. If everyone was already suspected of infection, then a coffin moving through the yard would draw no more than a cursory glance.

She had seen the dead soldier’s mother the day before, standing at the foot of the row of cots where her son’s name had already been erased from the official report. The woman’s hands were raw from scrubbing the floor, and when she turned, her eyes were the color of winter mud—steady, unbending. Petra had not spoken then, could not find words that would not sound like empty comfort. But the woman had looked at the ledger on Petra’s lap, the neat column of names that no officer would ever sign, and she had nodded once, a small, fierce gesture, as if agreeing to something she did not yet understand.

***

Petra rose when the evening bell tolled, the sound thin and reedy against the wind that rattled the panes. She pulled on the heavy woolen shawl Mirko had given her last week, the one he said was “warm enough for a woman who spends her life looking at the dead.” She found Mirko in the yard, where he was stacking firewood with the slow, careful deliberation of a man who had learned that haste led to loss. His face was smudged with soot, his fingers stiff from the cold, but his eyes were clear when he looked at her.

“Typhus,” he said. “I heard. Reeve’s doing?”

“A fiction,” Petra said. “But a useful one.”

He set down the wood and crossed to the side of the cart parked near the granary steps. The cart was empty except for a blanket, a coil of rope, and the faint scent of pine resin. “I can drive. But I won’t ask what’s in the box. I ask only that it not be a corpse I know.”

“It isn’t a corpse,” Petra said. “Not exactly.”

She went inside to find the dead soldier’s mother. The woman was kneeling before a cot where a young man from the Serbian contingent lay with his eyes closed, his chest still rising in the ragged rhythm of the dying. Petra had tended him during the day, recording his name in the ledger, noting the wound in his thigh that had gone infected, the way he had whispered about his village, his mother who wove rugs, the name of the church bell that called him to prayer every Sunday. The official report would list him as “casualty, KIA,” and his name would vanish into the frost.

“Your son,” Petra said, “will be among the buried this morning. The burial detail leaves at first light.”

The mother did not look up immediately. When she did, there was no tears in her eyes, only a kind of quiet readiness. “I know. I have prepared the shroud.”

“Would you,” Petra said, “come with me? Not to the burial, exactly. But after. There is a cart. A place for things that must travel.”

The mother’s gaze shifted to the ledger, still hidden beneath Petra’s shawl. “Names. You are collecting names.”

“Everyone deserves to be remembered,” Petra said. “Even if no one in command agrees.”

The mother placed a hand on Petra’s arm. Cold, but firm. “Then we are accomplices. Let us go.”

The yard at first light was a sheet of pale gray, the river beyond it a slab of iron-bound silence. The burial detail was a somber procession: six men carrying a rough pine coffin toward the edge of the valley where the ground was still soft enough to dig. Petra walked at the rear, the ledger tucked against her chest beneath the shawl, its pages warm from her body heat. Mirko drove the cart, the dead soldier’s mother seated on the driver’s bench, her shawl wrapped tight around her shoulders, her hands gripping the wooden spokes.

Behind them, the granary walls loomed, the quarantine signs fluttering in the wind. A guard stood at the gate, his rifle slung over his shoulder, his eyes scanning the procession with the suspicion of a man who had been told that everything was dangerous. Petra raised a hand in a slow, deliberate wave. The guard narrowed his eyes but did not challenge. Perhaps the typhus story had made him cautious; perhaps he simply did not want to be the one to delay a burial.

They moved toward the riverbank, where the ice was thickening, a fragile crust that creaked underfoot. The coffin was set down momentarily, the men stepping back to rest their shoulders. Petra knelt beside it, her fingers tracing the lid’s grain. Inside, beneath a layer of straw, lay the body of the Serbian conscript, his face peaceful now, the infection spent into stillness. But the coffin was not for him alone. Petra had asked the men to hollow the lower third, to carve a cavity beneath the straw where the ledger could be folded flat, sealed in a waterproof pouch made from the lining of a dead man’s greatcoat. The pouch lay now against the wood’s inner wall, its surface smooth, unremarkable.

“Is it done?” Mirko asked, his voice low.

“Ready,” Petra said.

The men lifted the coffin back onto the cart. The mother whispered a prayer in a language Petra did not know, the words rising like smoke, ancient and grounding. Mirko took the reins, and the cart creaked forward, the wheels crunching over packed snow and ice.

The journey to the workshop was short in distance but long in the weight of what they carried. The river stretched beside them, vast and still, the bridge spans downriver gone, detonated by engineers weeks ago to slow the advance of an enemy that no longer mattered, perhaps, but that the ice now held hostage. The workshop where the ledger had lived for months sat tucked into the mud of the bank, its windows dark, its door standing open like a mouth. Old Mirko had built it with his own hands, once, for his father’s boat-building. Now it was a sanctuary, and the ledger was its most secret relic.

As they drew near, Petra saw that the guards Reeve had posted were not only at the granary. Two men stood by the workshop door, their silhouettes sharp against the gray light. They carried rifles, and their faces were set in expressions of alert vigilance. Reeve’s paranoia had followed them onto the ice.

“Mirko,” Petra said, as the cart rolled to a stop outside the workshop. “Keep moving. We have a place further down, where the ground drops to the water.”

“Where?” the mother asked. Her voice was steady, but Petra could see the question in her eyes.

“To the deep water,” Petra said. “Where the current does not freeze solid. Where the ledger can be hidden beneath the planks, where no one will think to look.”

Mirko nodded, his jaw tight. He guided the cart around the side of the workshop, away from the guards’ line of sight, toward a small inlet where the river lapped against rotting timber and mud. The cart’s wheels sank slightly into the soft earth, and Petra, the mother, and a convalescent patient they had persuaded to help—an older man whose leg had been saved from amputation by Petra’s skilled hands—lifted the coffin onto their shoulders and waded through the muck toward a shallow depression near the water’s edge. There, they dug a hole beneath the roots of an old willow, the ledger sealed inside a waterproof bag, buried under straw and soil, the mouth of the hole disguised with fallen leaves and dead reeds.

When the hole was covered, Petra stood, brushing soil from her knees. Her breath came out in white plumes, the air sharp and cold. The ledger was no longer in the granary, no longer in the cart, no longer in the workshop. It was beneath the earth, held by the river’s patience, by the willow’s roots, by the silence of the dead.

“It is done,” Petra said.

The mother placed a hand on Petra’s arm, her grip fierce. “They will not find it. Not here.”

“No,” Petra said. “They will not.”

The sun dipped lower, painting the river in bruised shades of violet and amber. The quarantine’s bell tolled again, signaling the end of the day’s labor, the sealing of the granary doors. Petra stood at the window of the dying ward, looking out at the yard where the cart had left earlier, the tracks in the snow leading toward the river and then vanishing into the workshop’s shadow. She could not see the ledger’s hiding place, but she knew it was there, buried beneath willow roots, held by the earth’s quiet stubbornness.

A knock at the door. Petra turned, expecting a guard, a orderlies, perhaps Reeve himself to demand an update on the typhus situation. Instead, it was Hoss. The investigator’s coat was damp, her hair escaping from its neat knot, her face pale but her eyes bright with a fierce, hidden light.

“They’ve posted guards at the workshop,” Hoss said, stepping inside and pulling the door shut behind her. “Reeve thinks the workshop is a navigation hazard. He’s going to have it demolished in the morning.”

Petra’s jaw tightened. “The ledger is not there. It’s—”

“Buried,” Hoss finished. She moved to the window, her fingers tracing the frost pattern. “I knew it. I felt it when I saw you slip out of the granary yesterday. I just didn’t know how.”

“Mirko helped,” Petra said. “And the mother. The living conspiring with the dead, as you once said.”

Hoss turned, a small, exhausted smile touching her lips. “I said many things. But yes. That.”

The door creaked. A guard’s voice called from the corridor, “Investigator Hoss, Reeve requests your presence—”

“Tell him I’m occupied,” Hoss said, without turning. “Tell him whatever he wants to know, he’ll have to wait until morning. Or until the ice breaks.”

The guard fell silent. Petra heard his boots retreat down the corridor.

“He’ll recall the commission,” Petra said. “If the ledger isn’t found, if the workshop is leveled, if the truth doesn’t leave this valley, he’ll have no choice.”

“Then we buy time,” Hoss said. She crossed to Petra’s cot and placed a hand on the ledger, still hidden beneath the shawl. “Three days. The supply ship. If we can get the ledger aboard, Reeve’s game is over. But we have to move it, and we have to move it fast.”

Petra looked at the ledger, at the names stacked like stones, at the blank line where Ilya’s name would eventually sit, filled in with his true consent. “We wait for the night. Mirko has a boat hidden near the inlet. If we can load the ledger—no, not the whole thing. Just the pages. The essential pages.”

“And the rest,” Hoss said. “Every name. Even the ones we withheld. Especially the ones we withheld. Petra, you taught me that.”

Petra nodded, a slow, deliberate motion. “Then we begin. While the world sleeps.”

They worked in silence after that, unfolding the ledger, carefully tearing out the pages that bore the names most at risk, the ones that, if found, would sentence Mirko, the mother, the dead soldier’s memory, and Petra herself to positions she could not name. They folded the pages, sealed them in the same waterproof pouch the mother had knitted from the dead man’s greatcoat liner, and tucked the bundle beneath Petra’s shawl.

When the night was deep and the ward’s lanterns had been dimmed to a flicker that barely pierced the darkness, they slipped out the back door of the granary, past the sleeping guards who were already dreaming of warmer stations, and toward the river’s edge. Mirko was waiting with the cart, the horse’s breath steaming in the cold. The mother was there, her shawl wrapped around her, her face set in a line of grim determination.

“Are we ready?” Petra asked.

“Ready,” Mirko said. “But Reeve is a watchful man. We move now, or we wait until the morning demolition, and then the ledger goes with the workshop into the river.”

“Then we move,” Petra said.

The cart creaked forward, the wheels following the old, familiar tracks toward the inlet, the willow, the hidden hole. But as they drew near, they found the hole empty. The earth had been disturbed, the leaves displaced. The waterproof pouch was gone.

A low murmur rose from the darkness near the river. The guards from the workshop had not waited for morning. They had followed, or they had been warned, or they had simply guessed. Petra’s heart hammered against her ribs like a trapped bird.

“Mirko, the boat!” Petra shouted. “Now!”

The horse snorted, the cart’s wheels splashing through the shallow water as Mirko guided it toward the deeper channel, where the current was faster, less likely to freeze solid. The mother held the pouch against her chest, her knuckles white. Petra climbed into the cart’s front seat, taking the reins from Mirko, her mind racing, the ledger’s safety now tied to the river’s mercy.

Behind them, the workshop’s lanterns flickered to life, the sound of shouted orders, the crunch of boots on ice. The guards were coming, torches in hand, their faces obscured by shadow and fury.

“Faster!” Petra cried, cracking the reins. The horse surged forward, the cart lurching over ruts and ice, the ledger hidden beneath straw and the mother’s body, the river roaring beside them, a dark, relentless force that wanted to take everything and keep nothing.

They reached the deeper channel just as a torchbeam cut across the water, illuminating the cart, the horse, the figures on the bank. Petra spurred the horse forward, the wheels splashing into the current, the cart tilting, the ledger’s pouch shifting but holding. Mirko stood in the water, pushing the cart from behind, his face a mask of stoic resolve.

“Go!” he shouted over the river’s roar. “I’ll hold them!”

“Mirko, no—” Petra began, but the horse was already plunging into the current, the cart’s wheels turning, the ledger’s pouch disappearing into the dark water, the straw and the pouch snagging on a submerged log, the horse straining, its hooves finding purchase on the slick stones beneath the surface.

The guards reached the bank, their torches swinging, shouting commands that were lost in the river’s noise. They raised their rifles, but Mirko was already wading into the current, pulling the cart’s rear, his body a dark shape against the water, the ledger’s pouch caught on a root, being pulled deeper, away from the light, away from the men who wanted it.

Petra watched from the cart as the pouch vanished beneath the surface, a brief, desperate bubble of air, then silence. The current took it, carried it away from the valley, away from the guards, away from the quarantine, away from the men who would erase it.

The cart tipped, spilling straw and the mother’s shawl into the water, but the pouch held fast, trapped in the curve of a river bend, hidden beneath swirling silt and the weight of the water above it. Petra slid from the cart, wading into the current beside Mirko, her hands gripping the rope, her heart hammering in time with the river’s flow.

“It’s down there,” she whispered, though she could not see it. “It’s down there with the river.”

Mirko nodded, his face pale but his eyes bright. “The river remembers. The river keeps.”

They pulled the cart free, guiding it toward the far bank, where the ground rose higher, away from the floodplain, away from the guards’ reach. They climbed out, soaked, shivering, but whole. The ledger was gone from the workshop, from the granary, from the valley’s immediate sight. It was in the river, where it belonged, where the current would carry it toward the sea, toward the outside world, toward the ship that would sail on the morning tide.

They stood on the bank, breath steaming, the river roaring beside them, the guards shouting from the opposite shore, their torches dancing across the water like dying stars. Petra looked at Mirko, at the mother, at the dark, relentless current that had just taken the ledger from the jaws of erasure.

“They won’t find it now,” Petra said.

“No,” Mirko said. “They won’t.”

The tension will rise as the living and the dead conspire one last time, and the chapter will end with a sight or sound that propels the story toward the ship’s departure.

But for now, the river has taken the ledger, and the valley has kept its silence. The living have conspired with the dead, and the truth has slipped into the current, bound for a world beyond the ice.
