## 18. The Workshop Stands or Falls

The telegram arrived in a sealed envelope tied with black twine, delivered by a rider whose breath smoked in the air before he spurred his horse toward the valley’s far side. Petra read the message twice before the ink stopped bleeding into the paper’s edge. Major Reeve’s hand had scrawled a single sentence across the field hospital’s stationary: *The workshop at the river bend is a navigation hazard. Demolition scheduled at first light. All non-essential structures to be cleared. Signed, Aldous.* 

She did not hand the note to anyone. Instead, she folded it and tucked it into the lining of her coat, over the ledger’s hiding place beneath the granary’s floorboard, where the names of the unnamed wound still pulsed in ink. The workshop. Mirko’s boatyard. The place where the ledger had been transferred the night before, wrapped in waterproof canvas and lashed to the keel of a half-finished skiff, its bow pointing toward the deepest channel of the frozen Danube, as if the boat itself might one day carry the truth downstream.

Petra found Mirko in the workshop, the air thick with the scent of pine resin, oil, and the sharp, metallic tang of cold iron. He was planing a slab of oak, the plane singing a thin, reedy tune against the wood. His apron was stained with grease and the gray of ash, and his hands—broad, knotted from years on the river—moved with a quiet competence that had become rare in this war of inches and erasures. He looked up as she entered, his eyes the color of winter sky, and set the plane down.

“Petra. You’re not on duty.” His voice was low, rumbling like distant thunder. “The hospital needs you. The wounded.”

“Reeve has ordered the workshop demolished. First light.” She didn’t explain why. The ledger was there, beneath the planks, and the river was freezing solid each night, expanding, shifting, willing to swallow or to cast out whatever lay beneath its skin.

Mirko’s jaw tightened. He wiped his hands on a rag that left smudges of soot on his trousers. “Navigation hazard. He never liked the look of it, I suppose. Always said the boats blocked the current’s reason.” He fell silent for a moment, watching the shavings accumulate on the floor. “You’re here to move it. Again.”

“Again.” Petra nodded. “The ledger is on the skiff. Under the false floorboards. I need it beyond the demolition zone, where the water’s deep and the ice shifts less. I need Mirko’s hands.”

Mirko stood, the creak of his joints audible in the quiet space. He crossed to the skiff, running a weathered hand along the gunwale. The boat was a thing of half-formed ribs and canvas, destined perhaps for a fisherman’s use or a soldier’s crossing, but now it bore the weight of something heavier. “You know,” he said, “I lost two sons to this river. One drowned when the pontoons broke. The other was taken by an avalanche in the mountains. I’ve learned to listen to the water.” He turned, his gaze steady. “And I’ve learned that some things are worth sinking for.”

They worked through the afternoon light, the sun low and watery, casting long, blue shadows across the workshop. Petra directed Mirko’s attention to the skiff’s false floor, where the ledger sat wrapped in oilcloth, the pages stained with the damp of hiding places, the names written in a hand that had learned to move quickly, urgently. Each name was a life that Reeve’s reports had decided did not exist. Petra had learned, in the months since the ledger began, that history was not a distant thing of battles and treaties, but a granular thing of syllables spoken in pain, of men calling for mothers they would never see again.

“How many names?” Mirko asked, as he measured and marked the planks he would need to remove.

“More than I can count on two hands. Less than the valley’s population.” Petra picked up a chisel, the metal cold in her gloved hand. “But every one matters to someone.”

“The ledger,” Mirko said, “is it not a kind of bridge? Between what was and what will be.”

“Between what was erased and what must remain.” Petra’s lips curved, faint and tired. “I write so that someone, somewhere, might remember.”

By the time the last sliver of sun vanished, the false floorboards were lifted, the canvas wrappings pulled back, and the ledger lay exposed on the skiff’s deck, a thick volume of handmade paper, its cover worn smooth by hidden hands. The ink was dark against the cream pages: names in Serbian, German, Romanian, Turkish, and one or two in a language Petra did not recognize but had learned to treat with the same reverence. There were no ranks, no regiments, no unit designations—only names, and beside each, a note of injury, of origin, of the circumstance that had brought them to the granary’s pale light.

The demolition crew would not arrive until first light, but the order had already set the valley’s nerves on edge. Reeve’s engineers were known to arrive before dawn, their trucks headlights cutting through the frost like blunt knives. Petra and Mirko loaded the ledger onto the skiff, securing it with ropes that had been treated with tar to repel the river’s moisture. Recovered patients—men whose wounds had healed enough that they could still grip a rope, lift a plank, feel the cold bite of winter air—formed a line between the workshop and the water. There was Sergeant Luka, his arm bandaged from a shell fragment, his face pale but determined; there was Corpora, a young woman from the Balkans who had lost her brother to the eastern front and now tended the wounded with a quiet fury; there was old Jovan, who had once been a ferryman before the war turned the river into a line of fire.

They moved the skiff to the water’s edge, the ice along the bank crunching under their boots. The Danube here was not a wide, merciless torrent but a series of channels and eddies, and in winter it became a field of floating panes, each one creaking, shifting, alive in its own slow way. Petra helped Mirko push the skiff away from the shallow shore, where the ice was thick enough to walk on, into the deeper channel where the current ran faster and the ice was thinner, more unpredictable. The ledger would be safer there, where the water’s movement denied the ice’s certainty.

“Stay close,” Petra told the patients. “We’re not leaving without it.”

The night grew colder. The wind off the river howled like a wounded thing, and the stars overhead were pale and distant, indifferent to the comings and goings of men. Petra stood on the bank, watching the skiff float toward the channel’s center, the ledger’s presence a heavy, invisible weight beneath the canvas. She thought of the names inside, each one a universe of memory, of loss, of the small, stubborn facts that Reeve and his like tried to dissolve into the anonymity of reports and statistics.

“Petra.” Mirko’s voice came from the water, muffled by the distance and the wind. “The crew. They’ll bring equipment. The demolition crew usually arrives with wagons and jackets, things to break things apart. We have maybe an hour before the first light hits the eastern ridge.”

“Then we move it further.” Petra turned to the patients. “Jovan, Luka. Take the oars. Row us toward the deepest part of the channel. Mirko, you’re with me. We’ll secure the ledger to the keel’s center, where the water’s coldest.”

The rowing was a rhythm of exhaustion and purpose. The patients, despite their wounds, moved with a unity born of shared danger. Luka’s good arm pulled the oar in long, steady strokes, his face set in a mask of concentration. Jovan, his legs stiff from the avalanche that had taken his son, moved more slowly, but his hands never faltered. Mirko stood at the bow, his eyes on the water, on the way the ice fractured and reformed in the current’s wake. Petra sat in the stern, the ledger wrapped in a waterproof tarp at her feet, one hand on the rope that secured it to the skiff’s centerboard.

As they pulled away from the workshop, Petra glanced back. The workshop’s windows glowed with the amber light of lanterns, and she could see Mirko’s silhouette moving among the tools, a figure of quiet defiance against the encroaching dark. The demolition crew’s wagons would arrive soon, their headlights cutting through the frost, their engines a low, throbbing bass that would shake the ground. But already, the skiff was beyond the shallow ice, heading for the channel’s deep heart, where the river’s movement would keep the surface unstable, where the ledger’s canvas might stay dry a little longer.

“Keep rowing,” Petra said, her voice firm against the wind. “Keep the ledger centered. We’re almost there.”

The skiff crested a small swell, the water lapping against its sides in a gentle, persistent rhythm. Petra could feel the vibration of the oars in her hands, the effort of the men behind her, the pulse of the river beneath them. The ledger was safe, for now, but the night was long, and the demolition crew’s arrival was a clock ticking in the quiet.

“Mirko,” she said, “how much further to the deep water?”

“Another quarter-mile, maybe less, if we follow the current’s bend.” Mirko’s voice was calm, but Petra could hear the tension beneath it. “The ice there is thinner. More dangerous for a boat, but safer for the ledger. The water moves, and movement keeps the ice from locking solid.”

They rowed on, the valley’s quiet closing in around them. The wind picked up, whistling through the rigging, and the stars seemed to shift, though Petra knew it was merely the cold playing tricks on her eyes. The ledger was a presence in the boat, a weight of paper and ink that felt heavier than any cannon or ammunition she had ever moved. Each name was a question asked of the future, a defiance against the silence Reeve sought to impose.

“Nearly there,” she whispered, more to herself than to anyone else. “Just a little further.”

The shore fell away behind them, the workshop’s lights a diminishing glow against the white-on-white of the frozen landscape. Ahead, the channel widened, the ice fewer and farther between, the water dark and restless. Petra felt the skiff tilt slightly as the current caught it, and she adjusted her weight, keeping the ledger’s position in mind. The canvas tarp rustled, and she reached down, smoothing her hand over the bundled pages, feeling the texture of the paper, the slight roughness where names had been written and rewritten, the faint smell of oil and dried blood that had somehow clung to the ledger’s margins.

“Almost,” Mirko confirmed, his voice carrying back from the bow. “Just past the bend. The current takes a sharp turn there, and the water’s deeper. If we can get the skiff past that, the demolition crews’ ropes won’t reach.”

“Then row,” Petra said. “Row like the river’s chasing us.”

The patients rowed with a fierceness that surprised even Petra. The cold bit at their faces, their hands, but they did not stop. The skiff surged forward, the oars splashing water into the air, each stroke a small act of rebellion against the night’s encroaching silence. The channel’s bend loomed ahead, a dark curve in the river’s length, and beyond it, Petra hoped, lay the deep water where the ledger might survive the morning’s light.

As they neared the bend, a sound cut through the wind—a deep, resonating throb that vibrated through the water and the boat’s hull. Petra’s blood ran cold. It was the sound of engines, internal combustion, growing louder with each passing second. She lifted her head, straining against the wind, and saw, through the gloom, the faint glare of headlights cutting across the ice to their left. Demolition wagons, headlights sweeping, engines idling, their drivers and engineers moving toward the workshop’s sector.

“They’re already here,” Petra muttered. “First light, and they’re already moving.”

“Petra.” Mirko’s voice was tight. “We’re past the bend’s start. If we push now—”

“Push.” Petra grabbed an oar, her muscles screaming from the cold and the effort already expended. “We push past the bend. Luka, Jovan, keep the heading. Mirko, secure the ledger if the boat lists. I’ll handle the steering.”

The skiff surged forward, the oars splashing in unison, the patients’ faces illuminated by the rising cold sun’s weak glow. The bend in the channel grew closer, and with it, the realization that they might not make it. The ice was thickening along the banks, and the current here was slower, more treacherous. The demolition crew’s lights were now clearly visible, their figures moving between the wagons and the workshop, carrying ropes and crowbars, their faces hidden in shadow but their intent unmistakable.

“Harder!” Petra shouted, her voice hoarse. “Harder!”

The oars dipped deeper, the water spraying in arcs of steam-like mist. The skiff tilted, and Mirko shifted his weight, his hands moving quickly to the ledger’s bindings. The canvas tarp shifted, and Petra reached down, re-securing it, her fingers numb but determined. The ledger was the only thing that mattered now, the only thing that could outlast the machinery’s rage.

They passed the bend, the skiff lurching as the current shifted beneath them. The deep water was there, a dark, restless expanse where the ice was a patchwork of thin, cracking sheets. The skiff floated into it, the ledger’s presence a quiet, steady weight beneath the canvas. Petra let out a breath she hadn’t realized she’d been holding, and rowed the boat toward the channel’s center, away from the banks where the demolition crew’s ropes would find purchase.

Behind them, the workshop’s lights grew brighter as the crew arrived, their engines idling, the sound of axes and saws already echoing off the river’s walls. The skiff rocked gently in the deeper water, the ledger safe for the moment, but the night was turning, and the valley was waking to the sound of destruction.

“Mirko,” Petra said, as the skiff steadied in the deep water’s mid-channel. “Can you row us further? Away from the workshop’s line of sight?”

Mirko looked at the water, at the patchwork ice, at the distant lights. He nodded, a small, grim movement. “I can try. But the current’s against us, and the ice’s edge is close. If we go much further, we risk the ice breaking under the boat’s weight, and the ledger goes into the water.”

“Better than the flames.” Petra’s jaw set. “Can you?”

“Aye. But not far. Maybe another half-mile, if we hug the far bank’s deeper channel.” Mirko picked up an oar, his movements efficient, practiced. “The current there’s stronger. It’ll push us past the demolition zone, past where they’re likely to look first.”

They rowed again, the patients’ muscles burning, the cold a constant companion. The workshop’s sounds grew muffled behind them, the sounds of the demolition crew’s work rising in their place. Petra watched the ledger, wrapped in canvas, a small, stubborn book of names that refused to be erased. She thought of the names inside, of the men whose bodies lay in the granary’s yard, of the ledger’s role as a bridge between the erased and the remembered.

The far bank’s deeper channel grew ahead, a darker ribbon of water between the ice-flecked banks. Mirko guided the skiff toward it, the oars cutting through water that moved faster, more urgently. The ice here was thinner, each pane a potential fracture, but also a shield against the machinery behind them.

“Almost,” Mirko grunted, his arms trembling. “Just past this bend. The current will take us—”

A cracking sound split the air, sharp and sudden, like a gunshot. The ice beneath the skiff’s stern gave way, a web of fractures spreading outward in a instant. The boat tilted sharply, water surging onto the deck, the canvas tarp sliding toward the opening. Petra lunged, grabbing the ledger’s canvas, her gloves slick with water and cold, and pulled it back, securing it against her chest. The skiff listed, but Mirko’s quick strokes corrected the angle, the oars splashing in a frantic rhythm.

“Hold fast,” he shouted over the water’s rush. “The current’s pulling us under the ice’s edge!”

The skiff lurched again, and Petra felt the boat’s bottom scrape against a submerged ice ridge. She threw her weight to the opposite side, her muscles protesting, and the skiff righted itself, just enough. The water continued to rise on the deck, lapping at the ledger’s edges, but the canvas held. The current seized the boat, pulling it toward the far bank’s deeper channel, away from the workshop and the demolition crew’s reach.

“Row!” Petra shouted, and the patients rowed, their faces pale, their breath coming in ragged gasps. The skiff surged forward, the ledger secure against Petra’s chest, the names inside safe for another moment. The far bank’s deeper channel yawned ahead, a dark, moving ribbon of water that might carry them past the demolition zone, past the reach of Reeve’s engineers.

They broke into the deeper current, the skiff surging ahead, the ice behind them cracking and groaning as the boat’s weight shifted the frozen surface. Petra looked back, seeing the workshop’s lights dimming behind a screen of ice and steam, the demolition crew’s figures moving like ghosts between the wagons and the structure. The skiff’s bow dipped into the current’s heart, and she knew they were moving away, away from the machinery’s certain grasp.

“Further,” she whispered. “Just a little further.”

The current grew stronger, the water a dark, relentless force beneath the skiff. The ice banks receded, the far shore’s darker shapes resolving into trees and rocks, the river’s surface a vast, dark plane of movement. Petra felt the skiff steady, the ledger’s weight a familiar, comforting presence against her chest. The names inside were still, still waiting to be read, still waiting to be delivered.

“Mirko,” she said, as the workshop’s lights became a distant memory behind a bend of water and ice. “How far now?”

“Another half-mile, maybe less. The current will carry us past the bridge’s old crossing, past where the engineers usually work. Then we’ll be in the valley’s lower reach, where the river’s wider, the ice less predictable, and the ledger’s safer.” Mirko’s voice was weaker now, the effort of the row taking its toll. “But we’re past the worst. The demolition crew’s ropes won’t reach us here.”

“Then row,” Petra said. “Row like the river’s carrying us home.”

The skiff moved through the deeper water, the current a powerful, invisible hand guiding them away from the valley’s center, away from the workshop and the demolition’s certain sound. The wind had died down some, the stars now clearer, the cold a constant but manageable weight. Petra watched the ledger, its pages a map of survival, each name a testament to a life that Reeve and his like had tried to silence.

They rounded a final bend, the valley opening ahead, the river widening into a vast, dark expanse where the ice was a scattered memory, where the water’s movement was a constant, grinding force. The demolition crew’s lights were gone behind them, the sounds of their work fading into the valley’s quiet. The skiff floated in the open water, the ledger secure, the names inside safe for the night, for the journey, for the chance that truth might yet travel beyond the river’s bend.

Petra let out a breath that turned to mist in the cold air. She looked at Mirko, at the patients, at the river’s dark, moving surface. The ledger was safe, the boat was moving, and the valley’s machinery was left behind, a fading sound in the night’s long, quietus.

“We’ve done it,” she said, the words barely more than a whisper. “The ledger’s moving. We’re past the hazard.”

Mirko nodded, his face drawn, his eyes reflecting the water’s dark surface. “Aye. Past the hazard. But the river’s a cruel mistress. She’ll take what she will, and she’ll keep what she can.” He gestured ahead, toward the wider valley, toward the neutral supply ship’s route that the telegram had mentioned, the one departure that could carry the ledger beyond the war’s reach. “But we’ve given it a chance. That’s more than most get.”

“More than enough.” Petra’s hand tightened on the ledger’s canvas. “Let’s see where the tide takes us.”

The skiff moved into the wider valley’s water, the current a strong, steady pulse beneath them, the shores dark and silent on either side. The night was deepening, the river’s surface a vast, dark plane of possibility, and Petra knew that the ledger’s journey was far from over, but for now, it was safe, moving with the water’s will, carried by hands that refused to let names be erased.
