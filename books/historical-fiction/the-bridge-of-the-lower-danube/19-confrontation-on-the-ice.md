## 19. Confrontation on the Ice

The first light was the color of weak tea, and the ice of it sat on Petra’s tongue as she came over the seawall onto the slipway. The boat was out beyond the winch, where she and Mirko had hauled it in the small hours of the night, a half-ton of pine and caulk riding low in the shallow water, the ledger in its oilskin sleeve wedged beneath the thwart. She could see, even from this distance, the dark shape of it against the slow gray shine of the river. There were ropes still to make fast, and a painter to set, and Petra had her breath coming in white puffs when she heard the boot on the plank behind her.

She knew the sound. She had heard Major Aldous Reeve’s boots on the boards of the granary ward for eleven months. She knew how they set themselves down — heavy at the heel, deliberate at the toe, as if each step were a sentence and he wished the grammar to be quite clear. She did not turn at once. She let her hands finish what they were doing with the painter, because she had learned in this war that turning too quickly was its own kind of confession, and that the men who came for you always watched your hands first.

“Petra,” he said, and his voice was almost kind, which was the worst possible thing about it.

She turned then. He was wearing his greatcoat to the throat and his cap low over his eyes, and behind him, on the slipway, stood four men of the garrison with their rifles at the slope. One of them — a boy with a beard like corn silk and a nose that had been broken before he came to this river — was shivering so badly his bayonet tapped the plank in a thin, irregular tattoo.

“Sir,” Petra said. She did not salute; nurses did not salute officers in this hospital, and Reeve had never insisted. He had insisted on other things.

He walked to the edge of the slipway and looked down at the river, then at the boat, then at Petra. Behind him, the dawn was making a pale mess of the hills on the far bank, the kind of colorless light that made everything on the ice look like a thing waiting to be judged.

“You have made me a great inconvenience,” he said.

“Sir,” she said again, because she had not yet been asked a question, and because questions in this valley often had teeth.

He turned and raised a gloved hand, and the soldiers behind him stiffened. The corn-silk boy stopped shivering, as if the gesture had frozen him. “The demolition crew will be here within the hour. The workshop is condemned. I am given to understand that you have spent the night moving certain — property — of the Crown’s hospital to the riverside.”

“It is not the Crown’s,” Petra said. “The ledger belongs to no one. It is the names of the men who died in this hospital, and of the men who lived, and of the men who were not counted because they were inconvenient.”

Reeve’s mouth made a small shape, as if he had tasted something sour. “You will step away from the boat, Nurse Okonkwo.”

She did not move. Somewhere below the slipway the ice gave a long groan and shifted; the sound of it traveled up the planks through her boots and into her ankles, as if the river were speaking to her in a language she could almost parse.

Then there was a second sound behind Reeve — the slap of a booted foot on the wet plank — and the click of a door being shut where no door should have been, and Petra knew, before she turned her head, that the woman who had been locked into the granary’s back room under the fiction of quarantine had come out into the dawn.

Captain Verena Hoss looked like a creature that had been made of something the cold could not quite kill. Her uniform was creased in the way uniforms crease when they have been slept in by a woman who has not been permitted to undress. Her hair, which was the precise blond of a wheat field in late August, had escaped its pins and lay in damp strings against her temples. She was carrying, in her left hand, the leather case in which her commission lived, and in her right, the folded sheet of paper that Petra had last seen her seal with red wax the night before Reeve’s men came to the granary.

She walked to the slipway as if she owned it, and as she did she spoke, and her voice was not loud, but it carried across the ice the way voices do when the air is so cold it has forgotten how to soften.

“Officers and men of the garrison of Kladovo,” she said, and the words came out in Serbian first and then in German and then in the English that Petra had taught her in the long nights by the stove. “I am Captain Verena Hoss of the Imperial Commission for the Investigation of Crimes Against the Laws of War. I am here under the authority of the same empire that has placed Major Reeve in command of this hospital, and under the authority of that same empire’s enemies, who have signed the same conventions, and whose wounded lie in this granary alongside ours. I am here because a bridge has closed, and because the ice has held me here, and because the truth does not require a road.”

Reeve turned to her. The corn-silk boy raised his rifle by reflex, and Hoss looked at him with such a calm, absolute attention that he lowered it again without being told.

“Captain,” Reeve said. “You are confined to quarters under medical order.”

“I am confined to quarters under your order,” Hoss said. “There is a difference, Major, and you know it. The medical officer who signed my confinement is a man named Aldous Reeve, and the signature on the paper is yours, and the paper is in this case,” she lifted the leather case by its handle, “and the case is sealed with my own seal, because I have been a careful woman in a careless war.”

She opened the case and drew out a paper and held it up so the garrison could see it. It was a single sheet, with a stamp at the top that even the corn-silk boy recognized, because men in this valley had been raised to recognize that stamp the way they recognized the river. It was the seal of the Commission, and beside it was a second seal — a strange one to see in this place — that of a foreign legation whose name Petra did not know but whose existence Reeve plainly did.

“You are recalled,” Hoss said. “I will not insult you by pretending otherwise. The recall was sent by wire the morning of the quarantine, and Major Aldous Reeve, as officer in command of this station, accepted the recall and chose not to deliver it.”

She turned the paper so the light fell on it. “He accepted it,” she said, “because to accept a recall is to admit that an inquiry has cause, and he is a man who has spent his career making sure that inquiries have no cause. He accepted it and he put it in his breast pocket and he walked past my door as if the door were not there. I have watched him do it, gentlemen. I have watched him every day for eleven days, and I have noted the hour and the direction of his gaze, and the precise degree to which he inclined his cap to the sentries, who, poor boys, had no idea what was in his pocket.”

Reeve’s face did something complicated. It did not change, exactly, but the lines of it rearranged, as if the bones beneath had been shifted by an unseen hand. He lifted his chin. “Captain Hoss,” he said, and his voice was very even, and very quiet, and Petra understood that a quiet Reeve was a Reeve who had decided what he was going to do, “you will understand that I cannot permit this display.”

“You cannot permit it,” Hoss agreed. “And yet it is happening. As the massacre at Kladovo happened. As the omission of the names of the dead happened. As the falsification of the morning reports happened, by the hand that now reaches for his sidearm.”

Reeve’s hand had been moving, and now it stopped. He was looking at Hoss, and Hoss was looking back at him, and for a long moment the two of them were the only people on the slipway, though Petra could feel, behind her, the weight of the boat against its mooring, and the men of the garrison, and the boy with the corn-silk beard, and Mirko somewhere in the rocks below the slipway with his own hand on a coiled rope, waiting for the order that would mean he was to pull the boat out into the current before anyone could stop it.

Hoss drew from the case a second paper. It was not a commission. It was a list.

“Major,” she said, “I will now read to you the names of the men who died at Kladovo, and whose names your morning reports omitted, and whose families have written to the Commission, and whose graves in the hills above this valley are marked with the wooden crosses that Nurse Okonkwo commissioned from the carpenter at Derdap, because the official crosses had no names on them and she could not bear that.”

She began to read. She read in Serbian, because the men of the garrison were mostly Serbian, and in German for Reeve, and in the English that Petra had taught her for any man who had survived from elsewhere. She read the first name, and the slipway, which had been a place of shuffling feet and small coughs, went silent. She read the second name, and the corn-silk boy’s bayonet began to tap again, softly this time, in a rhythm that matched her syllables. She read the third name, and the fourth, and the fifth, and by the sixth Reeve had turned away to face the river, and by the ninth his shoulders had come up toward his ears, and by the twelfth his right hand had closed into a fist so tight that the knuckles cracked in the cold.

Petra, standing by the painter, felt each name land in her chest like a small, heavy weight, because she had written each of these names into the ledger, in her own careful hand, in the hours between the dying and the dawn. She knew the slope of the letters, the way she knew the slope of the hills across the river. She knew which of the names had been written by lamplight and which by the gray of morning, which had been added at the bottom of a page in a hurried hand because the man had died while she was still on the ward, and which had been written neatly, on a fresh line, because the man had lived long enough to be lifted onto a train that morning and she had had time to do it properly.

Hoss did not read the names in order. She read them in the order of the list in her hand, and that order was the order of the days of the massacre itself, which had lasted three, and she read the first day’s dead and then the second day’s and then the third, and between the days she paused, and in the pauses the ice spoke.

“Dušan,” she said. “Dušan Jovanović, of the fourth reserve, of the village of Kladovo, who was twenty-six and unmarried, and whose mother believes he is buried in the field above the brickyard.”

“Milan,” she said. “Milan Petrović, of the second line regiment, of the village of Veliko Gradište, who was thirty-one and the father of three, and whose name appears in no morning report because the morning report of that day was rewritten by Major Aldous Reeve.”

She said that last phrase without inflection, as if she were reading a sentence from a regulation. She did not look at Reeve. She did not need to. The garrison looked at him for her.

Reeve turned. He did not turn slowly; he turned the way a man turns when he has been shot and not yet felt it, with the whole body at once. His face had gone the color of the ice, and his eyes were very bright. “Captain,” he said, “you will cease this recitation. You will surrender the document in your hand. You will submit yourself to the order of confinement, and you will permit me to discharge my duty to this garrison and to the men under my command.”

Hoss did not cease. She read the thirteenth name, and the fourteenth. “Stanko,” she said. “Stanko Marković, of the village of Tekija, who was a conscript and a conscript only, and whose death was entered in the morning report as a death from ‘exposure and minor wounds,’ because the word ‘massacre’ had not yet been authorized for use.”

Reeve stepped toward her. The corn-silk boy raised his rifle. Hoss did not flinch. She read the fifteenth name, and the sixteenth, and the seventeenth, and on the seventeenth name Reeve spoke — not to her, but to the garrison, in the parade-ground voice he had used to praise the granary’s ‘immaculate anonymity.’

“You see before you,” he said, “an officer who has lost her reason. You see before you a document that has been compiled without authority, by women who have no standing to compile anything, in a station that is under quarantine for the safety of you and your brothers. You see a boat on the river that has been prepared for flight, by persons who intend to flee this valley with evidence that does not belong to them. I ask you, men of Kladovo, to do your duty.”

The men did not move. The corn-silk boy did not move. The boy’s rifle stayed at the slope, and his eyes went to Hoss, and then to Petra, and then to the boat, and then back to Reeve.

“Petra,” Hoss said, without turning her head. “Now.”

Petra bent to the painter. She did not hurry. She had learned, in this war, that the men who chased you could not catch what they could not see, and the most invisible thing in the world was a woman doing her work as if the world were not ending around her. She slipped the knot, took a turn around the cleat, and called down into the dark below the slipway: “Mirko.”

Mirko’s voice came back from the rocks, low and rough, in the Serbian of a man who had spent his life on this river. “Da,” he said.

Hoss read the eighteenth name. She read the nineteenth. She read the twentieth, and the twenty-first, and when she came to the twenty-second, she paused, and in the pause she looked, for the first time, directly at Reeve.

“The twenty-second,” she said, “is a name that does not belong to a soldier of the empire. It belongs to a soldier of the empire’s enemy, a man who was taken at Kladovo and who was permitted to die slowly, by order, because the order came from a man who wished the empire’s records to read ‘no prisoners taken, no enemy dead on the field.’ That name is the name of a man who has not yet been recorded in any ledger of any nation, because no nation has claimed him, and because Major Reeve’s morning reports recorded him only as ‘one enemy conscript, expired.’”

Reeve’s mouth opened, and what came out of it was not a word. It was a sound — a small, broken sound, the kind a man makes when he has been carrying something for a long time and his arms have finally given way.

Hoss read the name. She read it in the man’s own language, because Petra had taught it to her, and because she had heard him speak it in his fevers. It was a name that did not belong to any nation, and it belonged to all of them.

Reeve’s hand came up from his side, not to his sidearm, but to his face, and he covered his mouth with his gloved hand as if he were trying to keep the name from going any further. It was too late. The name was already on the slipway, and on the ice, and in the ears of the men of Kladovo, who had heard the names of their brothers read aloud, and who were now hearing the name of a man who was not their brother and who had been, in some way that they did not fully understand, their brother’s brother.

Petra looked at Reeve, and what she saw was not a monster. She had expected a monster, and she had been ready to hate a monster. What she saw was a man who had spent a long winter making sure that no one would ever say his own name in this way, and who had at last heard that someone had been saying it all along, in a ledger under a floorboard, in a workshop by the water, in the small hours of a winter that was finally, slowly, breaking.

Hoss read the twenty-third name, and the twenty-fourth, and she did not stop until she had read them all.

When she was done, she folded the paper and put it back into the case, and she closed the case, and she held it at her side, and the slipway was so quiet that the groan of the ice was the loudest sound in the valley.

Then Petra, from her place at the painter, said: “Major Reeve.”

He looked at her.

“Sir,” she said, “the boat is in the water. The ship will take the ledger at the morning tide. The men of the garrison have heard the names of their brothers. I do not think you can stop what is already on the ice.”

Reeve stood for a long moment, his hand still at his mouth, his eyes bright. Then he lowered his hand, and he looked at the corn-silk boy, and the boy lowered his rifle without being told, and Reeve turned and walked back up the slipway, and the men of the garrison parted for him as if he were a thing that had been broken in their hands.

He did not look back. Petra watched him go, and then she looked at Hoss, and Hoss looked at her, and between them was a silence that held no words and did not need any.

Then Mirko, from the rocks, said: “Petra. The painter.”

She bent to the painter, and the morning tide was coming in.
