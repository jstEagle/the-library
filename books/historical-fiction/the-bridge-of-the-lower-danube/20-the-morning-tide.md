## 20. The Morning Tide

The painter’s real name was Mirko, though the garrison had long since taken to calling him by his trade. He stood on the rocks below the granary, his hands deep in the pockets of a woolen coat that had once been blue but was now the color of storm clouds. Behind him, the granary windows glowed with the pale, rectilight of lanterns, and inside, Petra had bent her head to the ledger one last time, the ink frozen in sharp ridges on the paper, the names a river of themselves that had flowed through her hands like a prayer she could not speak aloud.

“Petra. The painter,” Mirko said, and his voice was thin as wind through reeds. He did not look at her; he looked out across the river, where the ice had begun to fracture in thin, silver threads, singing as the current tugged at them. “The morning tide is coming in.”

Petra straightened, the wool of her scarf catching the low light. She had not slept. The ledger lay open on the workbench, a final page nearly full, and she had traced the letters with a finger that remembered the weight of frozen hands, the quiet breath of men who had given everything to a war that had forgotten their names. She thought of the valley, of the hospital, of the people who had become her people in the way that only shared silence can make kin. And she thought of the ship, waiting in the eddy below the falls, its hull iced-over, its boat lashed to the dock like a creature ready to flee.

“Yes,” she said. “The tide.”

They stood together for a long while, the both of them, looking down at the water that had been both barrier and bridge, a thing that had taken sons and husbands and fathers, and that now, in its letting go, might yet give back a reckoning. The river did not hurry, but the day was breaking, and with it came the quiet certainty that whatever happened next, the ledger would not stay here. It could not. Not with the inquiry coming, not with Reeve’s anger lurking in the corridors of the granary like a trapped beast, not with the bridge gone and the road shut and the valley suddenly, achingly alone.

A horn sounded from the river below, a long, mournful note that rose and fell like the breath of some great animal. It was the supply ship, its whistle calling the dawn. Petra turned from the rocks and walked back up the slipway, her boots crunching on the frost-bitten path. Hoss was already there, her coat buttoned against the cold, her eyes red-rimmed but alert. She had cut her hair short last month, the better to fit beneath a forensic inspector’s cap, and now the cropped length framed a face that had seen too much paperwork and too many graves.

“Ready?” Petra asked.

Hoss nodded. “As ready as any of it gets. The ledger’s in the canvas satchel? Under the floorboards?”

“Aye. Mirko’s moving it to the boat at first light. He says the ice is thin enough already without adding more weight to the crossing.”

They walked in silence toward the granary’s back room, where the floorboard was loose, the one Petra had pried up herself under cover of triage and fever and the endless stream of men who came in and went out and were gone from the records. The ledger was a secret kept in plain sight, a ledger of names that the morning reports omitted, the officers’ ink never touching the pages that Petra filled after lights out, by the light of a single lamp she had hidden in a biscuit tin. Every name was a rebuttal to the war’s silence. Every name was a vote against erasure.

Inside, Ilya was already packing a sack with the few belongings he had accumulated since arriving at the hospital— a cracked mug, a woolen sock, a small wooden horse carved from a broken rifle stock. He looked older than his years, his face thin, his eyes bright with a tired intelligence. When he had first arrived, he had claimed three nationalities and none, a soldier of no state, a man who had walked through the war’s machinery without being claimed by it. Petra had tended his frostbitten feet, changed the dressings on a wound that had never quite stopped bleeding, and in the quiet between treatments, they had spoken of homes they had lost, of languages that were now just sounds in their throats, of the strange, weightless feeling of being a witness to one’s own life.

“You’re going with her,” Ilya said. It was not a question. He was looking at the satchel, at the ledger’s corner peeking out from beneath the floorboard.

Petra met his gaze. “I have to stay. The hospital… the people. The ledger has to stay somewhere it can be found, but not here. Not where Reeve can burn it.”

“And I?” Ilya’s voice cracked slightly. “I’m part of what they’re investigating. Hoss said she could use a witness. She said… she said I could sign my name, and it would mean something.”

Hoss stepped forward, laying a hand on Ilya’s arm. “You would be testifying, not confessing. The commission needs to know what happened at Kladovo, and your testimony is the only thing that makes the ledger more than a list. It gives it context. Human context.”

Ilya was quiet for a long moment. Then he nodded, a slow, deliberate motion. “I’ll go. But Petra—I need you to know that if I sign my name, it’s not because I’m brave. It’s because I’m tired of being invisible. I want the world to know I was here. That I existed.”

“Then we’ll make sure the world knows,” Petra said. She reached out and touched the side of his face, her fingers cold from the morning air. “You’re not invisible, Ilya. You’ve been seen. By me. By Hoss. By everyone who’s had to dress your wounds.”

The ship’s horn sounded again, longer this time, and a low rumble of ice shifting came from the river. The valley was waking, though slowly; the granary windows threw rectangles of light onto the snow, and the figures of convalescing men moved like ghosts in the yards, shoveling, talking, waiting. The bridge was gone, the spans had been detonated the day before, the iron and wood now littering the riverbed like a drowned forest. The only way out was the supply ship, the neutral vessel that came once a season, carrying mail and witnesses and the occasional wounded who could be moved. This was the morning tide, the only channel through which the truth could leave the valley.

Petra went to the floorboard, pried it up, and lifted the canvas satchel. The ledger was inside, its pages thick with handwriting, each name a small act of defiance. She had begun it winter before last, after the first report had omitted the name of a Serbian conscript who had died on the operating table, his body unmarked, his story lost to the paper trail. She had continued it out of a belief that history happened elsewhere, to other people, and that if she did not record it, it would vanish. She had learned, in the months since, that history happened wherever someone refused to let names be erased. That was the lesson she had taught herself, and the lesson she was about to pass on.

She sealed the satchel and slid the floorboard back into place. Mirko was already at the boat, his shoulders hunched against the cold, the canvas boat swaying gently in the shallows. The water was a dark, mirrory surface, broken only by the thin ice that clung to the edges like a lattice of veins. Men from the garrison were already on the dock, their breath pluming in the air, watching with a mixture of resignation and curiosity. The ship was a lifeline, but it was also a signal: the world beyond the river was paying attention, and what happened here would not stay here.

“Petra.” Hoss was standing by the boat, her hand on the rail. “Ilya’s ready. He wants to go. I’ll see to it his testimony is recorded properly. But you—”

“Stay.” Petra turned to her. The words were simple, but they carried the weight of years. “I belong here. The ledger… it needs someone to keep it safe until it reaches someone who’ll listen. Someone who won’t let the ink be washed away by the next report.”

Hoss studied her face, searching for the fracture line, the place where the certainty might crack. She found only the same quiet defiance she had seen in Petra from the first day they met, the nurse who kept her own ledger in the margins of official forms, the woman who had looked at Reeve’s reports and seen the gaps, the erased names, the men who had been sent to the front without a rifle or a name. Hoss had spent her career fighting institutions that wanted to smooth over the rough edges of war, and she had found in Petra a kindred spirit, someone who understood that the paper trail was not the only trail, and that sometimes the most dangerous thing was a name that no one could erase.

“All right,” Hoss said finally. “But if the commission recalls me before the report’s filed, I’m taking what I can with me. The ledger, the testimony, everything. We don’t know what Reeve will do if he thinks he’s losing control.”

“Then we’ll make sure he doesn’t,” Petra said.

They walked to the boat together. Ilya was already there, his sack slung over his shoulder, the wooden horse clutched in his hand. Mirko had the lines cast off, and the boat was drifting free of the shallows, the oars ready. The ship’s silhouette was growing larger in the river, its hull a dark shape against the paling sky, its deck already showing figures moving as the crew prepared to receive the passengers and the ledger.

Petra stepped into the boat first, the wood cold and solid beneath her boots. Ilya followed, his face set in a line of determination. Hoss went last, her satchel of documents in one hand, the other extended to Petra. Petra took it, and for a moment, the three of them stood in the small boat, the river moving dark and inexorable beneath them, the valley spread out behind them like a sleeping animal, its rooftops white with frost, its fields silent under a sky that was beginning to turn from black to bruised blue.

“Take care of the hospital,” Hoss said. “And take care of yourself.”

“Always,” Petra said.

Mirko pushed off from the shallows, and the boat dipped into the current, the oars finding their rhythm in the quiet morning. The ship was close now, its side a dark bulkhead against the water, a rope ladder lowered for them. As they drew alongside, Petra saw the faces of the crew, rough and weathered, and she felt the weight of the ledger in the satchel at her side, a physical thing, a thing of paper and ink, but also a thing of will, of insistence, of a woman who had refused to let the world forget.

Ilya climbed the ladder first, Hoss following, and then Petra. The deck was warm with the heat of the engine and the bodies of the crew, and the smell of coffee and salt and woodsmoke filled her nose. She found a place to sit, cross-legged on the deck, the ledger on her lap, and watched as Hoss was ushered toward the cabin, the investigator’s satchel already in hand. Ilya stood at the ship’s railing, looking back at the valley, at the granary at the edge of the river, at the frozen stretch of water that had been his world for months.

“Petra,” he called out, his voice carrying across the water. “If they ask you about me… if they ask what happened… tell them I was a witness. Tell them I was there.”

Petra raised a hand, her fingers brushing the air between them. “I’ll tell them everything, Ilya. Every name. Including yours.”

The ship’s engine groaned, a low, vibrating sound that seemed to shake the ice from the rigging. Lines were cast off, and the vessel began to move, slowly at first, then with gathering speed, the oars pulled in, the sails unfurled, the hull cutting through the water that was beginning to break under the weight of the morning tide. Petra watched the valley recede, the granary getting smaller, the rocks where Mirko stood getting smaller, the very ice on the river beginning to fracture and shift as the ship’s wake passed over it.

Behind them, the valley woke slowly. From the granary windows, figures appeared at the glass, watching the ship move away, a dark shape on a dark water, carrying the ledger and the investigator and the witness away from the place where they had all been forged in frost and war. The garrison on the hill turned their faces away, some with expressions of loss, others with the blankness of those who had long since stopped believing that anything could change. And on the riverbank, Mirko watched the ship go, his hands in his pockets, his face inscrutable, but Petra thought she saw a faint nod, a quiet acknowledgment of the tide turning.

Petra turned from the railing and walked back toward the deck’s center, the ledger warm from her body, the paper soft from the heat of the ship. Ilya was still standing at the railing, his silhouette stark against the gray light. He turned as she approached, and for a moment, they stood side by side, watching the valley disappear into the distance, the river expanding and contracting beneath them, the ice groaning and breaking in the ship’s wake.

“It’s cold,” Petra said, not looking at him.

“Yes,” Ilya answered. “But the water moves. That’s what matters. The water moves.”

A crewman appeared at their side, a man with a thick beard and eyes that had seen too much of the river’s many moods. “Lady, the captain says we’ve got clearance to open the hatches. The ice is breaking up enough. We’ll be at the delta by nightfall.”

“Good,” Petra said. She looked at Ilya. “Ready?”

Ilya nodded, and together they walked toward the cabin, the ledger between them, a book of names that would soon be far from the valley, far from the river’s frozen grip, but never far from the hearts of those who had known them, who had tended them, who had refused to let them be erased.

The ship gathered speed, the sails snapping in the rising wind, the deck listing slightly as the water grew choppier, the ice farther astern shattering into smaller and smaller pieces under the hull’s weight. Petra found a spot near the bow, the wood worn smooth by generations of sailors, and she sat down, the ledger in her lap, and she watched the valley fall away. The granary was now a white square against the snow, the river a dark ribbon, the road gone, the bridge a memory of iron and wood now at the bottom of the current. And yet, she thought, the ledger was moving, and with it, the possibility of a different story, one where names mattered, where men were not just numbers in a report, where the winter of 1916 would not be the end of everything, but only the beginning of something else.

The wind grew stronger, and the ship leaned into it, the sails a triangle of canvas against the gray sky. Petra felt the movement of the vessel, the sway of it, the way it cut through water that was finally, slowly, relenting. The morning tide was not just a physical thing, a coming in of water that would float the ship and carry it downriver; it was a metaphor, a shift, a turning of the world toward something that might allow the truth to surface. She thought of Reeve, of his anger, of his need to standardize, to purge, to make the ledger disappear. She thought of Hoss, of her commission, of her relentless pursuit of accountability. And she thought of herself, Petra Okonkwo, nurse, keeper of names, woman who had believed that history happened elsewhere and had learned that it happened here, in the quiet, stubborn act of recording, of remembering, of refusing to let a man’s life be reduced to a blank space on a form.

The ship passed beneath a span of what had once been the bridge, the iron supports now submerged, the wooden planks lost beneath the swirling water. The river was clearing, the ice breaking into slush that spun around the hull like a centrifuge of white and gray. Petra wrapped her shawl tighter around her shoulders, the wool damp from the spray, and she kept her eyes on the ledger, on the names she had written, on the last page that was nearly full, the blank line at the bottom waiting for the last entry, the one that had taken her three days to fill, the one that Ilya had chosen to sign, the one that now existed because someone had refused to let it be withheld.

The valley was a memory now, a place of frost and silence and the slow, aching work of keeping people alive while the world tried to make them disappear. But the ship was moving, and the ledger was with it, and the tide was turning, and somewhere below, in the deep water where the bridge’s remains lay, the names she had recorded were already beginning to become part of the river’s story, a story that would not be silenced, that could not be standardized, that would outlast the officers’ reports and the inquiries and the men who thought they could erase everything with a stroke of a pen.
