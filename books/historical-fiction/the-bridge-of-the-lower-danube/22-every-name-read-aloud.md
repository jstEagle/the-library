## 22. Every Name Read Aloud

The workshop smelled of fresh wood and winter rain.

Petra noticed that first, standing in the doorway as the last of the townspeople filed in — a slow, murmuring stream of boots on plank floors, shawls dripping meltwater onto the cleanest patch of ground in the valley. The walls had been re-clad in timber Mirko himself had salvaged from the demolished granary's east wall, the same timber that had once held grain for an army that no longer existed. New joists braced the roof where ice had punched through in February, and the gaps had been stuffed with moss and clay so thoroughly that the interior held the warmth of a body rather than the bite of a barn. A stove stood central, black-banded, radiating a circle of heat that pushed the damp to the edges. On its iron hood, a pot of herbal tea smoked with sage and honey, and beside it a tray of bread — dark, seeded, the recipe Mirko had brought from his village before the war — lay cut into thick slices that would not last the afternoon.

The ledger sat on the central table, its leather cover new, the stamping done by a printer in Belgrade who had heard what the ship carried and what it meant. Petra had touched it three times on the journey downriver and had not been able to stop. The pages were thick, cream-colored, resistant to the damp that had once threatened to blur her cramped handwriting into illegible bruises. Every name now lived in ink that would outlast the river's flooding.

She had come back to the valley two weeks ago with Hoss, who had insisted on returning to supervise the reconstruction of the hospital's records before the commission's final review. The ship had deposited Hoss in Belgrade with a letter of transit and a dossier that contained, for the first time in the war's arithmetic, a column of names that matched the column of wounds. Petra had stayed behind because Mirko had asked her to, and because the wounded needed a nurse who knew their names, and because the valley — this low, ice-choked, forgotten valley — was where she had learned that history did not happen elsewhere.

* * *

The children came first, as children always do, squeezing through the doorframe ahead of their elders, their voices pitching the room with a bright, unguarded noise. They carried bundles of dry flowers — campion and chamomile picked in the last warm week of March — and laid them on the table around the ledger like a guard of honor for something sacred. A boy no older than seven placed a sprig of thyme on the cover and stepped back with the grave satisfaction of a boy who had done something important.

Petra knelt beside him. "Thank you," she said.

He nodded as though this were entirely logical, as though thyme on a ledger were the only sensible thing one might do. Then he ran off to find a friend and the noise of the workshop rose again, layered now with adult voices low in conversation, with the creak of chairs being arranged in a circle, with the clink of cups being filled from the tea pot.

She looked around and recognized them all — the faces that had become a kind of family in the way that shared suffering arranges faces into constellations. Mrs. Vuković, the widow who kept the laundry, standing with her arms crossed and her grief worn thin as old linen but still holding shape. The two field hospital orderlies, brothers, one with the limp Petra had splinted and the other with the scar Ilya had earned fetching water under fire. Old Mirko in the corner, his hands wrapped around a cup he was not drinking from, his gaze settling on the ledger with the expression of a man watching his last son walk into a room he has rebuilt. The Serbian conscript's mother, whose son's name had been the first Petra ever wrote beneath the floorboard, standing just inside the door as if she might still leave.

And Ilya.

He stood apart from the group, slightly back, his coat patched at the shoulder where the wool had worn through from leaning on a shovel in the workshop's rebuilding. He had not eaten the bread. He was watching Petra with an expression she could not quite name — something between gratitude and dread, as though the morning carried both a promise and its cost.

She met his eyes and gave the smallest nod. He returned it.

* * *

Major Reeve did not come.

This was the first anniversary of the ledger's completion, and Petra had wondered each morning whether his absence was a snub or a mercy. She had written him a letter three weeks prior, in the careful, impersonal hand she used for official correspondence, informing him that the records purged from the granary had been restored — all of them, she had written, with a truth that was technically a lie, because the restored records were only the ones that had ever existed at all. The others, the true ones, lived in the workshop now in print and in the memory of the people gathered around the table. She had enclosed a copy of the ledger's index, the table of contents that Hoss had insisted on including, so that any officer could see the architecture of the omission for himself. She had not signed her name at the bottom.

She had heard nothing from his office. The silence was its own kind of answer.

Captain Hoss arrived instead, walking in through the door with her own coat dusted with travel and her commission folded flat in her breast pocket. She looked older than Petra remembered her — the war had a way of subtracting years from a woman's face and adding them to her voice — but her eyes were the same sharp, hopeful grey that Petra had first seen in the granary's back room, the room where they had pieced a massacre out of ration receipts and human witness. Hoss carried a small leather case under her arm and set it on the table beside the ledger without ceremony.

"I brought the appendix," she said. "The supplementary testimony. The statements from the men who crossed the ice, the ones you pulled from the river. Their words, verbatim."

Petra opened the case. Inside, pages upon pages of handwriting — not hers, but theirs, the cramped, uncertain script of men who had learned to write in trenches and had never expected to use the skill for anything so permanent as truth. Each statement bore a signature, a date, a thumbprint where the hand had trembled too hard for ink.

"They're all here," Hoss said quietly. "Every one who could speak."

Petra closed the case and felt the weight of it settle into her chest, not as burden but as completion. The ledger held names. The appendix held voices. Together they made a thing that no officer's report could unspool.

"Begin," Mirko said from the corner. It was not a command so much as a permission, the way a host nods toward the door when the guest is ready to enter.

* * *

Petra sat in the chair that had been built for her — a simple thing, four legs, a back, no adornment, fashioned by the workshop's apprentice from the wood of the old pontoon. She opened the ledger to the first page and felt the leather warm beneath her fingers, as though it had been waiting for this exact moment of contact. The first name was written in her hand, small and precise, the way she wrote when no one was watching.

"Dragan Vuković," she read, and paused.

Mrs. Vuković stood and came forward, her face a map of weathering, her tears arriving without invitation and without shame. "He was my husband," she said, and the room went quiet enough that Petra could hear the snow melting on the roof, could hear the river moving beneath the ice, slow and patient, carrying the spring toward a sea that would not ask for names.

"Corporal, Serbian Fourth Infantry," Petra continued. "Wounded at the bridgehead on October fourteenth. Age thirty-seven. Occupation: schoolteacher. He taught mathematics in a village outside Valjevo before the war took his school and his leg and his life." She read the occupation aloud because Hoss had insisted — that each person be known not only as they died but as they had lived, that a man's profession was a kind of surviving self. "He is listed in the margin of the morning report as 'casualty, rank unconfirmed.' That is all the Serbian army's record says. That is all the Austrian army's record says. This ledger says his name."

She turned the page.

"Stojan Maglajlić." The name belonged to a Bosnian conscript who had spoken only Turkish at home and had learned Serbian in the trenches, stumbling over the grammar until his officers mistook his difficulty for defiance. Petra read his unit, his wound, his age — twenty-one — and then the detail that made the room lean forward: "He wrote a letter home the day before he was wounded. It was found in his coat and forwarded to an address in Sarajevo that no longer exists. The letter said he was learning to play the mandolin. He had not yet succeeded."

A young man at the back of the room, one of the orderlies' friends, laughed through his tears because that was the only way some joy could survive a morning like this — a laughter that was half grief, half recognition, the sound of a man who understood that learning to play an instrument in a war was a kind of heroism the officers' reports could not accommodate.

* * *

The names came in waves, each one a door opening onto a life the war had tried to erase.

"Milan Dolenc," Petra read, "age forty-four, farmer, three children, the youngest born the month before he left for the front."

"Ana Tesić," she continued, "nurse's aide, not a soldier but listed among the wounded when the shell struck the aid station. She is here today." The woman — slight, bandaged, with the steady gaze of someone who had chosen her suffering and found it worthwhile — raised her hand and touched the page as though she could feel the ink.

"Nikolai Orlov," Petra read, and felt the room shift. "Russian, captured at the crossing, wounded in the hospital here. The Austrian report lists him as 'enemy alien, deceased.' He is not deceased."

Ilya stood.

The room held its breath. Petra felt the weight of his standing — the courage it cost him, the history he was claiming by rising — and read his name as though she were speaking it into existence itself.

"Ilya Petrovich Sorokin," she said. "Russian, Fourth Siberian Regiment. Wounded at the crossing on February seventh. Age twenty-six. He was here. He is here." She looked at him and saw his face — the face she had first seen through a doorway of gauze and fever, the face that had begged her to omit him from the record, the face that had chosen his name three days ago when he signed the statement beside his bed while the fevers spiked around him. "He is listed in the Austrian report as 'deserter, fate unknown.' This ledger says he was a soldier. This ledger says he was wounded. This ledger says he was here."

Ilya's hand came up to his collar, as though the name had physically lodged there and he was trying to dislodge it, to set it free in the air. His voice, when it found him, was barely a whisper but the workshop caught it and carried it to every ear.

"I am here," he said. Then, louder, to the room: "I am here."

Mirko wiped his eyes with the back of his hand and did not look at anyone. Mrs. Vuković wept. The children, who had not understood most of what they had heard but understood enough about the shape of the morning, began to clap, and the clapping spread like a rumor through the adults until the workshop rang with it — a sound primitive and holy, the sound of a community affirming its dead and its living in the same breath.

* * *

Petra read until the light began to fail.

The names came faster now, tumbling over one another in the lengthening afternoon — names in Serbian, in Russian, in Turkish, in the fragments of German and Hungarian and Romanian that the war had sprinkled across the valley like seed. Each name was a world, and each world was small enough to fit on a page and large enough to fill the sky. Petra's voice, which had begun the morning as a nurse's voice — efficient, detached, clinical — had become something else by the final hour. It was a voice of witness, of succession, of the plain and extraordinary act of saying someone existed and mattered and was remembered.

The last name she read was her own.

"Petra Okonkwo," she said, and paused so that the room could hold her name the way she had held theirs — slowly, carefully, as though the syllables were a bridge being built beneath her feet. "Nigerian-born. Nurse. She kept this ledger because she believed that history happens elsewhere, to other people. She was wrong. History happened here, in this workshop, in this valley, in the winter of nineteen hundred and sixteen, because someone refused to let names be erased."

She closed the ledger.

The silence that followed was not empty. It was full — full of the river moving beneath the ice, full of the stove's low crackle, full of the children breathing, full of the sound of Mirko's hands resting on the table as though they were holding something precious that the world had tried to take from him and had failed.

Hoss stood and walked to the window. Outside, the valley stretched in the late afternoon light, the snow diminishing to patches of white among the dark earth, the river visible beyond the workshop's far wall — still frozen, still waiting, but no longer for long. "The commission's final report will include these names," she said, without turning around. "I have already sent the appendix. The Austrian inquiry will have to account for what the ledger records."

"And if they refuse?" Mirko asked, his voice steady as the old wood he worked.

"Then they will refuse," Hoss said, and there was no defeat in it, only the calm certainty of a woman who had learned that institutions could be persuaded but could not be mistaken twice. "The names will still be here. They will be in this room, in this valley, in the mouths of the people who heard them today. That is enough. That has always been enough."

Petra looked around the workshop — at the rebuilt walls, at the bread tray emptied and being refilled, at Ilya sitting in the chair beside hers with his hand resting on the ledger's cover as though he were keeping it warm, at Mirko who had built a home from the wreckage of war and had asked nothing in return but the continuation of his craft, at Mrs. Vuković who had stood and wept for a husband the war had never known by name.

She thought of the bridge, closed and gone, the ice that had trapped them all in a valley of suffering and truth. She thought of the ship that had carried the ledger downriver and the morning tide that had brought it back to her in the form of printed pages and appended testimony. She thought of the morning when she would open the hospital again under new command, the purge reversed, the record restored, and the wounded would come through the door knowing that their names would be written and kept.

History, she understood now, did not happen elsewhere. It happened in the space between a name and the voice that spoke it. It happened in a workshop by a river in winter. It happened wherever someone refused to look away.

* * *

The townspeople stayed long after the reading ended. They formed into small clusters, talking, arguing gently, embracing, the way people do when they have been through a winter together and are discovering that spring has arrived without permission. The children ran outside to kick at the remaining snow, their laughter carrying across the valley to the river's ear. Mirko began clearing the tea things, moving with the unhurried dignity of a man who knows that cleanup is itself a form of care. Hoss sat with Mrs. Vuković, translating the Serbian names into English for the woman's benefit, telling her about the commission, about the report, about the way the world beyond the river would now know her husband's name.

Petra remained at the table.

She opened the ledger one final time and traced her own name with her fingertip — the last entry, the last page, the name she had read aloud to a room full of people who had made it true. Then she closed it and held it against her chest, feeling the leather warm with her body heat, feeling the pages settle into the shape of the hands that had written them.

Outside, the last light of the afternoon caught the river and turned it to silver, and the ice that had held the valley captive for three months began, in its slow and patient way, to break.

She knew, even as the darkness gathered at the edges of the workshop, that the work was not finished. There would be more names — the ones the war would take before the snow melted completely, the ones the next winter would bring, the ones that history, in its careless enormity, would always threaten to omit. The ledger was complete for now, but ledgers, like rivers, are never still. They move. They carry. They deliver what they hold to places the writer cannot see.

Mirko found her there, alone with the closed book, and stood beside her in silence for a long time.

"You will write again," he said, not as a question.

"Yes," Petra answered.

"Then you will need more pages."

She smiled — a small, tired, luminous smile, the smile of a woman who had come to understand that belonging was not a place you arrived at but a practice you continued, that history was not a monument but a meal you cooked and shared and cooked again.

"I have plenty of paper," she said.

The workshop stood around them, warm and rebuilt, the walls holding the warmth of a thousand hands that had worked and wept and returned. Outside, the river moved toward the sea, carrying the spring with it, carrying the names that had been spoken and the names that would yet be spoken, carrying the quiet, relentless truth that a life remembered is a life that has never truly ended.

And somewhere downriver, on the morning tide, a ship sailed into a world that did not yet know it needed to remember.

* * *

The fire in the stove shifted and settled, and Petra added another log, watching the flame catch and climb. The ledger lay open before her, its last page blank — waiting, as all blank pages do, for the next name that the world would give it. She took up her pen.

The river whispered against the ice.

And the writing continued.
