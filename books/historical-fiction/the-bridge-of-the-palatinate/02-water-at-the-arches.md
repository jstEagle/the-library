## 2. Water at the Arches

The rain did not fall so much as surrender. It had been falling for three days when Astrid descended the observatory tower on the morning of the fourth, her tools wrapped in oiled canvas, her boots already surrendering to the mud that passes for a street in Falkenau. The Rhine, usually a silver ribbon at the valley's floor, had swollen into a brown beast that licked at the foundations of the great stone bridge. The bridge — the *Steinerne Brücke*, the townsfolk called it with a pride that had always struck Astrid as slightly defensive — was the only road out for twenty miles in either direction.

Astrid stood at the bridgehead, where the cobbles gave way to the first arch, and watched the water climb. It had already swallowed the lower steps of the western abutment. A gauge post, marked with iron bands at one-foot intervals, showed the river at seventeen feet above summer level. The highest mark, a rusted cross at twenty-two feet, bore the date *1732* and the initials *J.H.* — Johann Halloran, Astrid's grandfather, who had built the bridge and the observatory clock and half the other useful things in this valley before the name Ravensworth became a whisper in taverns.

"Twenty feet and they close it," said a voice beside her. "Twenty-one and the arches sing. Twenty-two and we pray."

Astrid turned. The speaker was a woman of indeterminate age — fifty, perhaps, or sixty worn smooth by wind and water — wrapped in a shawl the color of dried sage. Her hands, folded into the sleeves of a wool coat that had seen generations, were knotted as the roots of the oaks on the hillside. But her eyes were clear, a pale grey that missed nothing.

"Greta," Astrid said. "The ferryman's widow."

"Was the ferryman's widow," Greta corrected. "Hans drowned three winters back. Ice gave way under him. Now I'm just the woman who keeps the keys to the ferry house and remembers how to read the river." She tilted her head toward the bridge. "Magistrate Vogel will post the notice by noon. Bridge closed until the water drops below fifteen feet. Could be weeks. Could be months. The spring melt comes early some years."

Astrid's stomach performed a small, cold somersault. "Weeks. I have an apprenticeship in Heidelberg. Master Weber expects me back by Martinmas."

"Master Weber," Greta repeated, as if tasting a foreign fruit. "He'll have to wait. We all wait, here. The river decides." She turned and began walking back toward the town, her gait steady despite the slick cobbles. "Come. I have a room. It's cold, and the roof leaks when the wind blows from the east, but it's dry enough for a clockmaker's apprentice. And I make a stew that has outlived three husbands and two wars."

Astrid followed, her tool bag knocking against her hip. The unease that had prickled at her in the tower — the filed gear tooth, the whispers — settled into something heavier in her chest. *Stranded. Trapped. The words the magistrate used when he summoned me: "Urgency. The clock cannot wait."* She had thought it professional pride. Now it felt like a summons to a trial she hadn't known was scheduled.

***

The ferry house sat at the end of a lane that smelled of wet wool and woodsmoke and something sweeter, underneath — apples, perhaps, stored in a cellar. It was a timber-framed building, its beams black with age, the white infill patched in shades of ochre and cream. A sign hung crooked above the door: *FÄHRE — G. HALLORAN* in faded letters, the *H* of Halloran half-erased by weather.

"Hans was a Halloran," Greta said, catching Astrid's glance. "My mother's brother. Your grandfather's cousin, or some such tangle. We're all tangled here, like the roots in the floodplain. Come in."

Inside, the air was thick with the scent of onions browning in lard, and beneath it, the sharp tang of herbs — thyme, bay, something Astrid couldn't name. A fire crackled in a massive hearth, an iron pot suspended over it. The room was low-ceilinged, furnished with a table scarred by generations of knives, a settle stuffed with horsehair, and a cupboard whose glass panes reflected the flame in wavering duplicates.

"Sit," Greta commanded, not unkindly. "Eat. Then I'll show you the room. It's under the eaves. You'll hear the river in the walls. Some find it soothing. Others find it... reminding."

Astrid sat. The stew was thick with barley and carrots and chunks of meat that fell apart at the touch of a spoon — beef, or perhaps mutton. She ate with the hunger of a woman who had climbed a tower three times in two days and forgotten breakfast both times. Greta watched her, slicing a loaf of dark rye with a knife that had seen centuries of loaves.

"You're a Ravensworth," Greta said. It was not a question.

Astrid paused, spoon halfway to mouth. "Astrid Ravensworth. Apprentice to Master Weber of Heidelberg. Granddaughter of Johann Halloran, who built your bridge and your clock." She set the spoon down. "Is that a problem?"

Greta's mouth curved, a gesture that might have been a smile on a younger face. "In Falkenau, names are not problems. They are... weather. You learn to read them like the river gauge. Some names bring rain. Some bring drought. The Ravensworth name..." She shrugged, a fluid motion of one shoulder. "It brings questions. Always has. Your grandfather knew. He built the clock to keep time for the town, but he also built it to keep *his* time. The mechanism in the observatory — it doesn't just strike the hours. It records them. Every toll, every pause, every hand's turn. A ledger in brass and steel."

Astrid's fingers tightened on the spoon. "I've studied the mechanism. It's a masterpiece of regulation. A compensated pendulum, a deadbeat escapement — "

"A deadbeat escapement that someone filed a tooth from," Greta said softly. "A deadbeat that stopped dead. The town noticed, Astrid. The town *always* notices when the heart skips a beat."

"The town noticed the clock," Astrid corrected, heat rising in her cheeks. "Not the name. The name was whispered. By whom?"

"By everyone who remembers," Greta said. "And by some who don't, but have heard the stories. The Albrechts. You've heard of the Albrechts?"

Astrid frowned, searching the catalogue of family stories — the ones told at Christmas, the ones whispered in the kitchen, the ones her father silenced with a look. "A family from the next valley. Landholders. They... lost their lands. Thirty years ago. Debt, I think. My father said it was bad management. The older Albrecht drank."

"Your father," Greta said, and the words fell into the stew like stones into deep water. "Your father said many things. Konrad, your uncle, said others. The Albrechts said nothing, because they had no voice left. Old Albrecht died in the debtors' prison in Mannheim. His wife died of a fever the winter after, in a cottage she didn't own, on land she didn't own. They had a daughter. Lena. She would be... your age, perhaps. Twenty-three? Twenty-four? She vanished. Some say she went to the cities. Some say she drowned herself in this very river. Some say she's still here, wearing another name, washing other people's clothes, waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

Greta's eyes held the firelight. "For the truth to float up. Like a body in spring. The river keeps everything, Astrid. Bones, secrets, ledgers. Especially ledgers." She stood, collecting the empty bowl. "Eat your bread. Drink the last of the stew. Then I'll show you the room. And tomorrow, if the bridge is closed as I know it will be, you will go back to your tower. You will work on the clock. And you will ask yourself: *why did someone file that tooth? What hour did they not want struck?*"

***

The room under the eaves was exactly as Greta had described: cold, smelling of apples and old wool, with a window that looked out over the river. The roof leaked a single drop into a tin pail Astrid placed beneath it, a steady *plink-plink* that marked the seconds like a clock's heartbeat. She lay in the narrow bed, the horsehair mattress prickling through the sheet, and listened to the river.

It was not a gentle sound. It was a roar, a hiss, a guttural sucking at the bridge piers. She could feel the vibration in the floorboards, a low hum that traveled up her spine. The bridge was groaning. She knew the sound of stressed timber and stone from her grandfather's workshop — the complaint of materials pushed beyond design. The *Steinerne Brücke* had stood for sixty years. It would stand longer. But the road across it was gone.

*Stranded,* she thought again. *Like a gear stripped of its pinion, spinning free in the train.*

She thought of the clock. The filed tooth on the third wheel of the going train — a deliberate, precise sabotage. Not vandalism; vandalism is clumsy. This was surgery. Someone had known exactly which tooth to file to stop the escapement without damaging the rest of the train. Someone with access. Someone with knowledge.

Her uncle Konrad had the keys. The magistrate had the authority. The tower keeper, old Herr Schiller, had the duty but not the skill. And then there were the others — the townsfolk who watched her with flat eyes, who whispered *Ravensworth* like a curse.

*Why me?* She had asked Konrad that, in his comfortable house on the Marktplatz, over wine that tasted of plums and power. *Why not a master from Mannheim? From Frankfurt?*

"Because you are a Halloran by blood," Konrad had said, his fingers steepled. "And a Ravensworth by name. You belong to both worlds. The clock is a Halloran clock. The town is a Ravensworth town. You... you are the bridge."

At the time, she had taken it for a compliment. Now, listening to the river eat the bridge, she wondered if he had meant it as a warning.

She turned onto her side, pulling the wool blanket to her chin. The apples in the cellar below exhaled their sweet, cold breath through the floorboards. Somewhere in the house, Greta coughed — a dry, hacking sound that spoke of winters survived and winters yet to come.

*Claustrophobia,* Astrid named the feeling pressing against her ribs. Not the room — the room was fine. It was the town. The valley. The walls of history closing in, each stone laid by a hand she shared blood with, each mortar joint mixed with a secret she didn't know.

She had come to repair a clock. She had found a crime scene.

And the only way out was underwater.

***

Morning brought no sun, only a lighter grey pressing against the window. The river had risen another foot in the night — Astrid could see it from her bed, the brown water lapping at the bridge's second arch. The gauge post was half-submerged. The notice posting she had expected was unnecessary; the bridge was already closed by the river's own authority.

She dressed in the dim light, pulling on her wool stockings, her leather apron over her linsey-woolsey dress. Her tools she checked with ritual care: the file set, the pivot gauge, the depthing tool, the brass hammer, the oilstone. Each had its place in the roll. Each had been taught to her by Master Weber, who had taught her that a clockmaker's tools are her conscience made manifest.

Greta was already in the kitchen, kneading dough on the table. The fire had been rebuilt. A second pot simmered — porridge, thick and steaming.

"Eat," Greta said without turning. "Then go. The tower keeper will be waiting. He has the keys. He also has a temper and a rheumatism that makes him blame the weather for his own mistakes. Be patient with him. He loved the clock before it was broken."

Astrid ate in silence, watching the flour dust dance in the grey light. "Greta. The Albrecht daughter. Lena. You said she might still be here. Under another name."

Greta's hands slowed in the dough. "Might be. Might not. The town is full of women with no pasts. Laundresses, seamstresses, charwomen. They come from the wars, from the famines, from the prisons. They change their names like they change their aprons. But..." She paused, wiping flour on her apron. "There is one. Works at the magistrate's house. Does the washing for the tower keeper too. Calls herself Liesel. Dark hair, grey eyes, a way of moving like she's carrying something heavy. She never speaks of family. Never speaks of the past. But sometimes, when the church bells ring for the dead, she stops. Just stops. And listens. Like she's hearing a name she once knew."

"Liesel," Astrid repeated. "At the magistrate's house."

"Vogel's house, yes. And the tower keeper's. She goes everywhere. Sees everything. Knows more than the magistrate, I'd wager. But she's careful. Careful as a clockmaker filing a gear tooth."

The comparison hung in the steam between them.

"Finish your porridge," Greta said briskly. "The river waits for no one. Not even a Ravensworth."

***

The walk to the observatory was a navigation of puddles and mud, the town's streets turned to slurry by the relentless rain. Few people were abroad. Those who were moved with the hurried, head-down gait of folk who have business indoors and no desire to speak. Astrid passed the bakery, where the sign *BÄCKER MEYER* swung sodden in the wind; the smithy, where the ring of hammer on anvil was muffled by the rain; the tavern *Zum Goldenen Anker*, where smoke curled from the chimney and the smell of beer and pipe tobacco drifted heavy.

At the observatory, the tower keeper — Herr Schiller, a man whose face seemed carved from the same grey stone as the tower — admitted her with a grunt and a rheumy stare. His fingers, gnarled and swollen, fumbled the key in the lock.

"Late," he rasped. "The magistrate said eight. It's eight-fifteen by the church clock. Which is wrong, because the master clock is stopped. So who knows what time it is? Time is broken in Falkenau. Fitting."

"The church clock is driven by a separate movement," Astrid said, keeping her voice even. "I can look at it after — "

"Don't touch the church clock," Schiller snapped. "That's *my* clock. The master clock is *your* clock. Up there." He jerked a thumb toward the spiral stair. "Konrad said you'd fix it. Konrad says many things. The bridge is closed. The river is rising. The magistrate is looking for someone to blame. And you're the stranger with the famous name. Fix it. Then we'll see."

He turned and shuffled back to his stove, leaving the door open.

Astrid climbed. The stair was steep, the stone worn concave by centuries of feet. The air grew colder, damper, smelling of pigeon droppings and old oil. At the clock chamber, she paused, hand on the latch.

The silence was immense. The great clock stood in its oak case, motionless, its hands frozen at 3:17 — the hour it had stopped, two days ago. The weight lines hung slack. The pendulum, a rod of brass and steel with its compensating bob, hung dead still.

She approached it like a physician approaching a patient who might be sleeping, or might be dead. She opened the case door. The movement, exposed, was a cathedral of brass: the great wheel, the center wheel, the third wheel — there, the filed tooth, a clean V-shape where a curved flank should be. The fourth wheel. The escape wheel, its teeth sharp and perfect, waiting for the impulse that would never come.

She ran her fingers over the filed tooth again. *Deliberate. Precise. Knowledgeable.*

"Someone knew this movement," she whispered. "Someone knew exactly which tooth to file to stop the train without stripping the pinion. Someone who understood deadbeat escapements."

The words echoed in the chamber. Somewhere below, a door slammed. Footsteps on stone — heavy, deliberate. Not Schiller's shuffle.

Astrid froze, her hand still on the movement. The footsteps climbed the stair. Pause at the landing. The latch lifted.

"Fräulein Ravensworth."

Magistrate Vogel filled the doorway. He was a thick-set man in a wool coat the color of wet clay, a silver chain across his vest, his face broad and red as a boiled beet. Water dripped from the brim of his hat onto the stone floor.

"Herr Magistrate." Astrid withdrew her hand, straightening. "I was just — "

"Examining the sabotage," Vogel finished. His eyes, small and dark as beetles, moved from her face to the open case, to the filed tooth. "Herr Schiller tells me you have experience. Heidelberg. Master Weber. A promising apprentice." He stepped into the chamber, the floorboards groaning under his weight. "He also tells me you are a Ravensworth. Konrad's niece. Johann Halloran's granddaughter. A name with... history in this valley."

"History is what clocks measure, Herr Magistrate," Astrid said, her voice steadier than she felt. "I measure it. I repair the measures. That is my trade."

"Trade," Vogel repeated. "A convenient trade. Access to the clock. Knowledge of the mechanism. Motive..." He let the word hang, wet and heavy. "The bridge is closed, Fräulein. The river is rising. The town is... uneasy. Thirty years ago, the Albrechts lost everything. The Ravensworths gained. The ledger of tolls — the bridge tolls, the market tolls, the river tolls — was always in Ravensworth hands. Old Albrecht claimed it was falsified. He claimed Konrad Ravensworth and your father stole his lands through false accounts. He died in prison shouting it."

Astrid's throat tightened. "My father was a clockmaker. He kept time. He didn't keep ledgers."

"Didn't he?" Vogel's lip curled. "Konrad is the town's leading burgher. He holds the toll franchise. Your father held it before him. The Albrechts held it before *them*. A tidy transfer. Very tidy. And now the master clock — the clock that *records* the tolls, that *stamps* the ledger entries with the hour — is sabotaged. By someone who knew exactly which tooth to file."

"I didn't file it," Astrid said. "I just got here. Ask Konrad. He summoned me."

"Konrad," Vogel said, the name flat. "Konrad is a respected citizen. You are a stranger. An apprentice. A woman. A Ravensworth." He took a step closer. "The sabotage is a crime. Obstruction of public timekeeping. Fraud, potentially, if the clock's record was being manipulated. I have the authority to arrest. To detain. To question. And with the bridge closed, there is nowhere for a suspect to flee."

"I'm not a suspect," Astrid said, heat rising. "I'm the repairer."

"For now," Vogel said. "Finish the repair. Quickly. Before the water reaches the tower foundations. Before the town decides it needs a scapegoat more than it needs a clock." He turned, water dripping from his coat. "And Fräulein... do not leave the town. Do not leave the tower without my permission. The river is not the only thing rising."

He descended, his boots heavy on the stone.

Astrid stood alone in the clock chamber, the silence returning, thicker now. She looked at the filed tooth. At the silent escapement. At the weight lines, slack and useless.

*Claustrophobia,* she thought again. *The walls are not stone. They are names. Ravensworth. Albrecht. Halloran. Vogel. Konrad. Each one a wall. Each one a gear in a train I didn't build, turning toward an hour I don't know.*

She knelt, opening her tool roll. The file set caught the dim light. She selected a fine-cut file, a brass gauge, a microscope lens.
