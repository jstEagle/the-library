## 4. The Albrecht Daughter

The washhouse smelled of woodsmoke and damp wool, the kind of scent that clung to Lena Albrecht’s skin no matter how often she scrubbed. She had lived in Falkenau for twelve years now, long enough for the town to forget the shape of her face, long enough for the name Albrecht to feel like a thing she had lent out and never got back. She worked the river’s laundress row from dawn until the light turned the water the color of bruised plum, and in the spaces between the rinse and the spin, she kept her family’s history folded small in the palm of her hand—a small leather-bound ledger, its cover worn soft, its pages filled with the tidy hand of her father and the heavier, trembling script of her mother before the debtors came.

They had taken the land. They had taken the name. And somewhere in the space of a single winter, the Albrecht name had been erased from the town’s ledgers, replaced by a debt that none of them could name but all of them could feel in the cold that settled in their bones.

Lena laid another shirt on the wooden board, the fabric yielding under her palm. She had taken a new name—Lena Voss, washerwoman—because the town’s good people liked things neat, and an Albrecht who owned nothing but the clothes on her back was neater than one who once held a manor. She had learned to keep her head down, her hands busy, her mouth shut. The river did not remember, but the town did, and the town’s memory was a river of its own, swelling and receding in tides of suspicion.

This morning, however, the river had brought something other than laundry. A commotion at the bridge, the kind that drew crowds and quieted the usual chatter of women at their washboards. A young woman—an apprentice, someone had said, from Heidelberg—had been spotted climbing the observatory tower. The master clock there had not struck true in months, perhaps years, and the townsfolk said it was because the Ravensworth blood had run thin, or perhaps because the family had always been too clever by half. The floodwaters had come early this year, swallowing the great stone bridge, cutting off the road, stranding the living and the dead alike in the valley below.

Lena tied her apron strings a little tighter. She had learned, over a decade of invisibility, that the town’s currents were dangerous to swim against. But as she turned to gather her tubs, a piece of paper caught on the edge of the wash line. It was a ledger page, torn from a larger book, the ink faded but legible enough: *Tolls paid to Sir Aldrik Ravensworth, quarterly, in excess of the agreed rate. The surplus… routed to the family vault.*

Her breath hitched. The name. The Ravensworth. The hand was not her father’s, nor her mother’s. It was the hand of a man who had lived in comfort while others starved. She had seen the toll records once, before the land was taken, before the bailiffs came with their warrants. This was her family’s surplus, her father’s labor, her mother’s unpaid wages, all funneled into the Ravensworth vault under the guise of road maintenance and bridge repair. The Albrecht name had been erased, but the tolls had not stopped; they had only changed hands.

Lena folded the page and tucked it into the pocket of her woolen shift. She did not go to the tavern to gossip. She did not go to the magistrate’s house to complain. She went instead to the observatory, because she had heard—through the cracked walls of the laundress’s row, through the gossip of the men who came to have their shirts starched—that the apprentice was a Ravensworth by blood, though no one spoke the name aloud without a tremor.

The tower door was shut against the wind, but not locked. Lena pushed it open and stepped into the stone stairwell, the air cool and smelling of oil and old paper. Each rung was familiar from the times she had brought the magistrate’s linen here for mending, though she had never climbed past the first floor. The stairs creaked under her weight, a sound that seemed loud in the silence.

At the top, the observatory dome yawned open to the gray sky, the air inside thick with the scent of metal and the faint, electric smell of ozone. The master clock stood against the far wall, a towering thing of brass and walnut, its face glazed with dust, its hands frozen at a quarter past three. Its gears sat exposed beneath a glass case, a chaotic nest of wheels and pins that had not moved in living memory.

And there, beneath the clock, crouched the apprentice. Astrid Ravensworth, her sleeves rolled up, a slender bronze tool in hand, her face smudged with grease and concentration. She did not look up immediately, too absorbed in the task of coaxing a stubborn gear into alignment. When she finally turned, her eyes found Lena’s, and for a heartbeat, the silence between them was thick with the weight of unspoken histories.

“Lena Voss,” Astrid said, her voice steady but carrying the faint lilt of a foreign place. “I was just thinking of you. The town’s been talking.”

Lena set her tubs down with a careful clink. She did not curtsy; she had long since stopped performing the deference that the world expected of an Albrecht. “Astrid Ravensworth. I’ve been hearing your name, too. In whispers. In the washhouse. In the spaces between the wind and the water.”

“You’re here about the clock.” Astrid stood, wiping her hands on a rag. “Konrad sent for you, didn’t he? Said the mechanism needed a skilled hand. That it was… urgent.”

“Urgent.” Lena’s lips quirked, a ghost of a smile. “I’ve come about the ledger.”

Astrid’s hand stilled. The rag she held bunched in her fist. “The ledger. You’ve seen it.”

“Seen it.” Lena reached into her shift and withdrew the torn page. She unfolded it, the ink catching the slant light from the dome. “Tolls. Surplus. Routed to the Ravensworth vault. My father’s hand. My mother’s labor. My brother’s debt.”

Astrid’s jaw tightened. “Konrad… he said it was a test. Said the clock needed repair, and someone had jammed the works. He didn’t mention—”

“He didn’t mention much.” Lena’s voice was gentle, but the edges were sharp. “He never does. But I recognized the hand. I recognized the theft. My family’s name was used to pad a vault, and in exchange, we were given nothing but the cold.”

Astrid moved toward the clock, her steps measured. “I didn’t know. I came to fix a gear. I thought—”

“You thought what?” Lena set the ledger on the polished wood of the clock’s base. “That the world was as it seemed? That the tolls were fair? That the Ravensworth name was earned?” She met Astrid’s gaze. “I’ve spent twelve years rebuilding myself from the pieces they left me. I’ve washed the shirts of men who signed my father’s death warrant, and I’ve smiled while they talked of the family’s ruin as if it were a cautionary tale. I’ve learned that silence is safer than a name, but I’ve also learned that some truths are heavier than silence.”

Astrid looked at the ledger, then at Lena. “I didn’t sabotage the clock. I came here to repair it. Konrad summoned me from Heidelberg. Said the town needed the observatory’s data for the coming spring planting. He didn’t tell me why the works had failed.”

“No.” Lena nodded. “He didn’t. But you’re here now. And the ledger is here. And the floodwaters have cut the road, and the magistrate is looking for a scapegoat, and the town is waiting for someone to blame.” She spread her hands. “I could turn you in. I could say you did it, that you were jealous, that you hated the family that owned the land your people once served. But I won’t. Because I know what it’s like to have a name used against you. And I know that the truth is not mine to give or to keep—it’s ours to demand.”

Astrid was quiet for a long moment. The gears of the clock seemed to shift in the stillness, a faint metallic sigh. “You want me to help you bring it before the spring assizes. You’ll testify that the sabotage wasn’t my doing.”

“Not because I believe you’re innocent,” Lena said, “but because I believe the truth is bigger than any one person’s guilt. I’ll testify that the ledger was planted, that the clock was jammed by someone who wanted the Ravensworth name muddied. And in exchange, I need your help. I need you to see that the ledger reaches the assizes unburned. That the tolls are accounted for. That my family’s name is restored, even if just in the records.”

Astrid’s fingers brushed the ledger. “You’d trust me with that? After what your family—”

“After what my family suffered.” Lena’s voice softened. “I’m not asking you to be a hero, Astrid. I’m asking you to be a witness. And I’m asking you to let me be one, too. We’re both strangers in a story that started before we were born. Let’s write a new chapter, together. One where the ledger doesn’t burn, and the name Albrecht is spoken without shame.”

The weight of her words settled between them. Outside, the wind howled against the observatory windows, and the river below continued to rise, lapping at the stones of the bridge that would not open again until the frost released its grip. But in the warm, oil-scented space of the tower, two women—one a Ravensworth by birth, one an Albrecht by erasure—found a quiet accord.

“Very well,” Astrid said finally. “I’ll copy the ledger. I’ll see it gets to the assizes. But I can’t promise the town won’t burn it before we get there. Konrad… he’s powerful. He’s the town’s burgher, its respected clockmaker, its pillar. If he decides the ledger is a threat to the family name—”

“Then we burn it together.” Lena’s smile was genuine this time, a small thing, but real. “I’ll help. I’ll carry it myself if I have to. But I won’t let it be silenced. Not again.”

“Not again.” Astrid nodded, and something shifted in her expression—a softening, a recognition of kindred burden. “Spring assizes. I’ll have it copied by then. The clock’s repair can wait. The truth can’t.”

They worked side by side after that. Lena, who had spent twelve years erasing herself from the world’s ledgers, now helped Astrid trace the delicate lines of the original document, her hand steady despite the tremor of memory that ran through her. Astrid, who had come to Falkenau as a stranger in her own family’s story, found herself drawn into the quiet, dogged determination of this woman who had turned her name into a whisper and survived.

As the afternoon deepened, the light through the dome shifted, painting the clock’s face in shades of amber and rose. The gears, still frozen, seemed to catch the glow, their intricate patterns suddenly beautiful in the dying light. Lena found herself smiling at the absurdity of it all—a clock that would not strike, a ledger that should never have existed, two women from families on opposite sides of a betrayal, now bent over the same truth, stitching it back together with thread and intent.

“You know,” Lena said, setting down her stylus, “my mother used to say that a clock’s heart is not in its gears, but in the hands that wind it. She said if you wind it with malice, it’ll strike the wrong hour. If you wind it with truth, it’ll mark the moments that matter.”

Astrid looked up, the bronze tool poised between them. “Your mother sounds wise.”

“She was.” Lena stood, gathering her tubs. “She also said that sometimes the only way to fix a broken clock is to let it stop, and start again somewhere else. Somewhere the people who broke it don’t have a say.”

Astrid watched her go, the image of Lena’s retreating figure lingering in the tower’s stillness. She picked up her tool and returned to the clock, her hands moving with a new purpose. The gear that had refused to turn now seated easily beneath her touch, the tooth that had been filed down now fitting perfectly into its mate. She wound the mainspring, and for the first time in months, the clock’s heart began to beat, a steady, measured rhythm that filled the quiet space between them.

Outside, the wind still howled, and the river continued its relentless rise, but inside the observatory, a different kind of turning was taking place. Lena Voss, laundress and keeper of erased histories, had found a way to speak her family’s name again—not as a whisper, but as a claim. And Astrid Ravensworth, apprentice and reluctant heir, had found a reason to repair not just a mechanism, but a lineage.

The ledger was safe, for now. The alliance was fragile, but real. And as the first true strike of the master clock echoed through the tower, a sound that had been absent for too long, both women knew that the river would eventually recede, the bridge would reopen, and the world outside would demand answers. But for this moment, in this high stone room, they had built something together: a small, fierce certainty that the truth, however inconvenient, was worth fighting for.

Lena stepped out into the cold, the wind catching her shawl, the ledger tucked safely against her chest. She did not look back, but she felt the weight of Astrid’s gaze on her back, a quiet benediction. She had a bargain now, a path forward, and for the first time in years, she allowed herself to hope that the spring assizes would not just be a trial, but a homecoming.

She walked down the tower stairs, the stone cold beneath her feet, the town’s murmurs rising around her like a tide. She had a name to reclaim, a truth to deliver, and a winter to outlast. And somewhere in the back of her mind, she carried the image of the clock’s face, the hands finally moving, the hours finally counting, and she knew that whatever came next, she would not walk it alone.
