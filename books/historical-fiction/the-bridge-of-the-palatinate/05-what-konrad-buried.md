## 5. What Konrad Buried

The portrait did not blink. It never had, in the twenty-three years since Margarethe's fever took her, and Konrad had long since stopped expecting it to start. Oil on canvas, cracked at the left cheek where the carriage had jolted during the move from Heidelberg — a flaw the restorer had called "character," as though character were something you could varnish over. Her eyes, the color of the Rhine in March, followed him nowhere and everywhere. A painter's trick. Or perhaps a wife's.

He poured the wine — a Mosel, sharp and green, the kind she had hated — and set the glass on the small table beside his chair. The study smelled of beeswax and old paper and the particular dust that accumulates behind books no one opens. A fire hissed in the grate, throwing shadows that made the portrait's mouth seem to move. *Speak to me, then. Tell me I am a monster. It would be a change from the silence.*

"Margarethe," he said to the canvas. "I have done a terrible thing. Several, if we are precise. But the one that matters — the one that has walked through my door in the shape of a clockmaker's apprentice with my brother's eyes — began thirty years ago, on a morning that smelled of rain and lime."

He drank. The wine bit his tongue.

* * *

**1717. The bridge at Falkenau, before the flood of '22 took the third arch.**

The toll house stood at the western end, a squat stone building with a slate roof that leaked in November and a stove that smoked in January. Konrad, twenty-six and newly appointed toll-keeper by virtue of his brother Friedrich's marriage to the magistrate's niece, sat at the high desk with his ledger open. Rain drummed the roof. The river, swollen with autumn runoff, hammered the piers with a sound like distant artillery.

Old Albrecht approached on foot, his cart mired in the mud two hundred paces back. He was a big man, broad as a barn door, with hands that could crush walnuts and a face weathered to the color of saddle leather. The Albrecht family had worked the river road for three generations — hauling timber, grain, the occasional coffin — and they knew every pothole, every soft shoulder, every honest merchant and every smuggler between here and Mannheim.

"Morning, Toll-Keeper," Albrecht said. Water dripped from the brim of his hat onto his boots. "Three wagons. Timber, wheat, and a load of copper kettles for the market in Speyer. Same as last month. Same as the month before."

Konrad dipped his pen. The nib scratched. *Three wagons. Timber, wheat, copper. Standard toll: four groschen per wagon. Total: twelve groschen.*

"Twelve groschen, then," Albrecht said, counting coins from a leather pouch. His fingers were thick, clumsy with cold. "And a penny for the bridge fund. The magistrate's new idea."

"The magistrate's new idea is two pennies," Konrad said without looking up. "War chest for the Elector's latest dispute with the French. Everyone pays."

Albrecht's jaw tightened. "Two pennies it is." He added the coin. "Fourteen groschen. Your ledger, Toll-Keeper."

Konrad slid the book across the counter. Albrecht peered at the entry, his lips moving as he read. *Good eyes for a man of fifty. Better than Friedrich's, and Friedrich reads the Augsburg Gazette without spectacles.*

"Wait," Albrecht said. "You've written sixteen groschen."

Konrad's hand did not tremble. It never did, not then. "Two pennies for the bridge fund. Two pennies for the road maintenance levy. The magistrate's *other* new idea. Posted at the town hall last week."

"There was no posting. I was at the town hall last week. My daughter — "

"Your daughter was at the laundry," Konrad said. "Frau Albrecht does the magistrate's linens. I saw her myself. Perhaps she forgot to mention it."

Albrecht's hand closed on the ledger's edge. The knuckles whitened. "You're a liar, Konrad Ravensworth. A thief in a magistrate's coat."

"Careful, old man. The magistrate's coat is the only law between here and the Rhine. Accuse me falsely, and you'll be hauling more than timber. You'll be hauling your own belongings to debtor's prison."

The threat hung between them, wet and heavy as the rain. Albrecht's eyes — blue, faded, the same color as Margarethe's would be in eight years — held Konrad's. *I see you,* they said. *I see the rot under the varnish.*

"Fourteen groschen," Albrecht said quietly. "Is what I owe. Fourteen is what I've paid. Write it honest, or I'll walk to Speyer and tell every merchant on the road what kind of toll-keeper Falkenau keeps."

Konrad's pen hovered. The ledger waited. Behind him, the stove puffed a cloud of smoke into the room.

*Write it honest. The words tasted of ash.*

"Sixteen," Konrad wrote. "And may God have mercy on your soul, Albrecht. You'll need more than pennies where you're going."

Albrecht spat on the floor. "God sees. Even if magistrates don't."

He turned and trudged back toward his cart, shoulders squared against the rain. Konrad watched him go, the ledger open to a lie he had just signed with his own hand. The first of many. The easiest, he told himself later. The first cut always is.

* * *

**The portrait watched him pour another glass.**

"Friedrich came to me three nights later," Konrad said to the canvas. "He had that look — the one you hated. The one that meant he'd found a way to turn a small corruption into a large fortune. 'Brother,' he said, 'the Albrecht route carries forty percent of the river traffic. If we skim two groschen per wagon, every wagon, every month...' He did the sums on the back of a letter. 'Two thousand groschen a year. Twenty thousand in a decade. The magistrate gets his cut, the Elector gets his taxes, and we — we build something that lasts.'"

*You would have slapped him. I remember how you slapped him when he suggested watering the wine at your father's funeral. "We do not steal from the dead," you said. "And we do not steal from the living and call it building."*

"I did not slap him," Konrad told the portrait. "I poured him wine. The same Mosel, though it was better then — before the vineyards failed and the vintners sold to the French. We drank in this very room, though the furniture was different. Your mother's chairs, the ones you loved. He spread the ledger between us like a map."

*Show me,* you would have said. *Show me the numbers that make a man betray his neighbor.*

"The numbers were simple," Konrad said. "Albrecht's route: twelve wagons a month, average. Two groschen each. Twenty-four groschen. Multiply by the other haulers — the Schumachers, the Wagners, the widow Kreutz — and the total reached nearly fifty groschen monthly. Six hundred a year. Six thousand in a decade. Friedrich's calculations were conservative. The reality was worse."

*Worse how?*

"Worse because we didn't stop at two groschen. Worse because when Albrecht complained — and he did, loudly, at the town hall, at the tavern, at the magistrate's own door — we raised his tolls. 'Administrative review,' Friedrich called it. 'Compliance verification.' We invented infractions: overweight loads, improper documentation, deviation from chartered route. Each infraction carried a fine. Each fine went into a separate ledger. The one your niece found in the clock case."

* * *

**1722. The flood that took the third arch.**

The river rose in January, fed by a winter that refused to end and a spring that arrived in a hurry. By March, the water lapped the toll house floorboards. By April, it climbed the stairs. The bridge groaned — a sound Konrad would hear in dreams for decades, a low cello note played on stone and timber.

Albrecht's cart was gone. He had sold it in '20 to pay the fines. Sold his team in '21. By '22 he hauled by hand, a harness across his shoulders, his daughter walking beside him with a pushcart. Lena. Eight years old, her hair the color of wet straw, her eyes the blue of the Rhine in March. *Margarethe's eyes. No — her mother's. Anna Albrecht. Dead of the coughing sickness in '19, leaving a husband and a child and a debt that grew like fungus.*

Konrad watched them from the town hall window. The magistrate — a new one, Friedrich's cousin — stood beside him, eating a candied orange peel.

"Pitiful," the magistrate said. "The Albrechts were honest once. Before the debts."

"Debts have a way of making dishonest men," Konrad said. His voice sounded strange in his own ears. Hollow. *Like a clock case with the works removed.*

"Or revealing them," the magistrate corrected. "Albrecht petitioned the Elector's court in Mannheim. Claimed fraud. Claimed we falsified the tolls." He laughed, a dry sound. "As though the Elector cares about a Palatinate toll road. The petition was returned unopened. 'Local jurisdiction.' Meaning: our problem."

"Is it?"

"Is what?"

"Our problem." Konrad turned from the window. The floodwater had reached the bridge's second arch. The current carried debris: a roof beam, a drowned cow, a child's wooden shoe. "Albrecht has a daughter. She'll be a ward of the town if he goes to prison."

"The town has enough wards." The magistrate tossed the orange peel into the fire. "Besides, the girl has family. An aunt in Speyer. A cousin in Worms. They'll take her. Albrechts always land on their feet. Cats, the lot of them."

*Cats. Yes. Always landing. Always surviving. Until the fall breaks something that doesn't heal.*

The bridge gave way that night. Not all at once — first the third arch, with a sound like the world cracking open, then the second, then the first. The toll house, built on the western abutment, slid into the river like a toy. Konrad heard the scream from the town hall steps. He did not know, until later, that it was Albrecht. That the old man had been on the bridge when it fell, trying to save his pushcart — the last thing he owned — and had gone down with the stone.

They found the body three days later, caught in a willow thicket downstream. The harness was still across his shoulders. His hands were clenched around the cart's handle.

Lena stood on the bank, watching them pull him out. She did not cry. Konrad remembered that most of all. Eight years old, soaked in river water and rain, and she did not cry. She looked at the magistrate. She looked at Friedrich. She looked at Konrad.

*I see you,* her eyes said. *All of you.*

* * *

**The wine was gone. Konrad poured a third glass from a second bottle — a Rhinegau, sweeter, the kind Margarethe had loved.**

"She came to me in '29," he told the portrait. "Lena Albrecht. Seventeen. The aunt in Speyer had died; the cousin in Worms had married a tanner and wanted no extra mouths. She walked from Speyer to Falkenau in November, shoes worn through, carrying a bundle wrapped in oilcloth. She stood at my door — this door — and asked for work."

*What did you do?*

"I gave her the laundry room over the stable. A bed, a stove, a wage. Friedrich laughed. 'Charity, brother? From you? The girl's a spy. She knows.'"

*Did she?*

"She knew nothing she could prove. The ledgers were here, in this desk, in a false bottom Friedrich had built. The public ledger — the one the magistrate audited — was clean. We'd learned to keep two sets of books by then. The real one, the hidden one, recorded every stolen groschen, every falsified entry, every fine levied on a man who couldn't pay. It was a confession waiting to be read. Friedrich called it insurance. 'If the Elector ever sends an honest auditor,' he said, 'we burn the false one and show the true. We claim we were tracking discrepancies to root out corruption. We become the heroes.'"

*And you believed him?*

"I believed in survival. The Palatinate had been burned by the French, frozen by the Little Ice Age, bled by the Elector's wars. Families disappeared — whole lines erased by debt and disease and the soldier's torch. The Ravensworths were not old nobility. We were clockmakers and merchants, two generations from the workshop. Friedrich's marriage to the magistrate's niece gave us a foothold. The toll fraud gave us a foundation. I told myself we were building a wall against chaos. I told myself that if a few men like Albrecht were crushed in the building, that was the price of order."

*Order. Is that what you call it?*

"What would you call it, Margarethe? When the alternative was watching our mother beg on the Heidelberg bridge? When our father died of a fever caught in an unheated workshop because the guild master withheld his wages? When I was fourteen and you were twelve and we ate boiled turnips for three months because the harvest failed and the tax collector took the rest?" Konrad's voice rose, echoing in the study. "I call it *life*. I call it the only choice a man has when the world offers none."

The fire popped. A log shifted. The portrait's eyes caught the light — *alive, almost, alive* — and Konrad looked away.

* * *

**1735. The debtor's prison in Mannheim.**

Friedrich was dead. A fall from a horse, the magistrate's report said. A broken neck. Konrad had identified the body — his brother's face, pale and peaceful, the arrogance finally smoothed away. He had wept, genuinely, at the grave. He had also, three days later, opened the false bottom of the desk and counted the hidden ledger's entries. *Forty-three thousand, seven hundred and twelve groschen. Thirty years of theft. A fortune built on a ruined family.*

Lena Albrecht was twenty-three. She worked the laundry still, though she had taken in mending too. She lived in the stable room, paid her rent on time, spoke to no one about the past. The town called her "the Albrecht girl" with a mixture of pity and suspicion. *Charity case. Spy. Whore, some whispered, though Konrad had ensured — quietly, ruthlessly — that no man touched her without her leave.*

She came to him in October. The hidden ledger had been moved to the observatory tower, sealed in the clock case by Friedrich's own hand before he died. *A time capsule, he'd called it. A joke. A trap. Konrad didn't know which.*

"Uncle Konrad," she said. She stood in the study doorway, hands folded, spine straight. The oilcloth bundle was gone. She wore a decent wool dress, mended at the cuffs. "My father's name was Johann Albrecht. He died in the flood of '22. The magistrate's report says he was on the bridge illegally. Smuggling, they said. Contraband tobacco."

"Tobacco," Konrad repeated. "Yes. The customs officer found a crate in his cart."

"There was no crate. You know there was no crate. The customs officer was your brother's cousin. He testified that the crate bore the Albrecht mark — a mark my father never used, because his mark was a pine tree, not a crown. The crown was the Ravensworth mark."

Konrad said nothing. The fire hissed. The portrait watched.

"I found the pine tree stamp," Lena said. "In my father's old toolbox. The aunt in Speyer gave it to me before she died. I also found letters. From the Elector's court in Mannheim. My father's petition — the one the magistrate said was returned unopened — was *answered*. The court ordered an investigation. The order was dated March 1722. Three weeks before the flood. Three weeks before my father died on a bridge that collapsed under him while he was trying to save the only property he had left."

She took a breath. "The investigation never happened. The magistrate — your brother's cousin — claimed the order was lost in the flood. The records in Mannheim burned in a fire two years later. Convenient fires, Uncle Konrad. Convenient floods. Convenient deaths."

*She knows. She knows everything.*

"What do you want, Lena?"

"I want my father's name cleared. I want the land back — the farm outside Speyer that was seized for debts that were manufactured. I want the ledger in the clock case. I want you to give it to me."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I wait. I'm young. I have time. The magistrate is old. His cousin is older. You — " She paused. "You're not old. But you're tired. I can see it. You've been tired for a long time."

*Margarethe would have liked her. Sharp. Brave. The kind of woman who stares at a portrait and sees the soul behind the paint.*

"I cannot give you the ledger," Konrad said. "It implicates my brother. My family. The magistrate. Half the town council. If it surfaces, the Ravensworths fall. The magistrate falls. The town's reputation — "

"Is built on a lie," Lena finished. "As you said. A wall against chaos. But walls built on lies don't stand, Uncle Konrad. They rot from the inside. Like the bridge. Like the clock."

She left. The door closed softly. Konrad sat in the study until the fire died, staring at the portrait of a wife who had been dead twelve years and a daughter he had never had.

* * *

**The clock. The observatory tower. The master clock that had kept Falkenau's time for a century.**

Friedrich had loved the clock. Not for its beauty — it was a clumsy thing, a great iron heart beating in a stone chest — but for what it represented. *Authority. Order. The power to define the hour for everyone within hearing.* He had paid for its last overhaul in '38, and when the clockmaker died in '41, Friedrich had installed his own apprentice: a quiet man named Vogel, who became magistrate two years later.

*The same Vogel who now threatens your niece. The circle closes.*

Konrad stood and walked to the portrait. Up close, the cracks were deeper. The varnish had yellowed, shifting Margarethe's skin to the color of old parchment. Her mouth, curved in the half-smile she reserved for him alone, seemed to hold a secret.

"I did not kill Albrecht," he told her. "I did not push him from the bridge. I did not forge the customs report. I did not burn the court order. I *allowed* these things. I profited from them. I buried the truth in a clock case and told myself it was safe because no one would think to look inside a mechanism that tells time."

*And now?*

"Now the mechanism has stopped. The apprentice — my brother's granddaughter, though she doesn't know it; Friedrich's son died in '44, and the girl was raised in Heidelberg by a cousin — has found the ledger. She has the mind of a clockmaker: she sees the filed gear tooth, the deliberate sabotage. She will trace it. She already has, I think. She is clever. Like you. Like Friedrich. Like me."

He touched the frame. The wood was cold.

"She has also found the Albrecht girl. Lena. She's been here all along, washing the town's linens, mending its shirts, keeping her father's pine tree stamp in a box under her bed. Waiting. *Patient as a glacier.*"

*And you? What will you do?*

Konrad returned to his chair. The wine was sour on his tongue. He drank it anyway.

"I will do what I have always done," he said. "I will protect the family. I will tell the girl to burn the ledger. I will offer her the Ravensworth name — restored, legitimized, washed clean by thirty years of silence. I will tell her that justice is a luxury for people who can afford it, and we *cannot* afford it. Not anymore. The Elector's tax collectors are coming in the spring. The French are massing at the Rhine. The town needs stability. The family provides it. The ledger burns, the clock strikes, the bridge stands. Order persists."

*And Lena?*

"Lena will be dealt with. Vogel has already prepared charges — sabotage of municipal property, theft of public records. The evidence is... flexible. A planted gear fragment. A witness who remembers seeing her near the tower. She will be arrested, tried, transported. The ledger will disappear. The Albrecht claim dies with her."

*You sound like Friedrich.*

"I *am* Friedrich. That is the joke, Margarethe. That is the terrible joke. I became him the night I wrote sixteen groschen in the ledger. Every year since, I have become him more. The fraud didn't just steal from Albrecht. It stole *me*. It replaced me with a man who calculates the price of a soul and finds it affordable."
