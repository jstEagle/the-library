## 6. The Magistrate's Question

The magistrate's carriage came to the observatory gates at noon, the horses' breath steaming in the unseasonable chill. Magistrate Vogel stepped out, a man whose reputation for severity preceded him like a banners in winter wind. He wore a greatcoat of dark wool, the cuffs frayed, and his eyes were fixed on the great stone tower with the sort of scrutiny usually reserved for a fortress under siege. Behind him followed two constables, their sabres clinking against the cobblestones, and a scribe carrying a wax-sealed ledger.

Astrid had been polishing the escapement gear in the upper chamber when the knock came. She had nearly forgotten the world beyond the tower’s narrow windows, so absorbed was she in the intricate dance of weights and springs. The clock had not ticked properly in days, the silence of its stopped mechanism heavier than any accusation. She set down her burin, wiped her hands on her apron, and climbed the winding stone stairs to the door.

Vogel did not wait for her to curtsy. He pushed the door open and entered, the constables trailing behind him like shadows. "Astrid Ravensworth. Apprentice to the late Master Hark. Summoned from Heidelberg to repair a clock that has not kept time since the first frosts."

"Aye, my lord. The gear at the crown wheel—" She indicated the jammed tooth, still visible under the glass case. "—appears to have been filed down. Deliberately."

Vogel nodded, his gaze shifting to the ledger lying on the workbench, the hidden compartment she had uncovered only the night before. He did not look at it. "The town has suffered floods. The great stone bridge is closed. The river has taken the road out, and with it, the patience of the populace. Yesterday, a cart attempting to cross was swept into the whirlpool beneath the eastern arch. The driver survived, barely. He says the water rose faster than anyone expected, but he also says the bridge was already barred against traffic, as it is each winter."

"It is the season," Astrid said, keeping her voice steady. "The river knows its rhythm."

"Does it?" Vogel’s lips quirked, faint and humorless. "Perhaps. But the townsfolk do not agree. They have long memories, apprentice. And some of those memories are less kindly disposed toward the Ravensworth name. I have heard whispers in the ale houses. 'Ravensworth' is spoken with the same contempt one reserves for a disease."

Astrid felt a flicker of something hot and unwelcome in her chest. "I am here to fix the clock, my lord. Not to answer for my family's past."

"Perhaps not. But the law has a way of blurring those lines when the water rises high enough." He stepped closer, and she caught the scent of woodsmoke and old parchment on him. "The sabotage of the master clock is a serious matter. It is not merely an inconvenience. It is a disruption of the order we rely on to mark the passing of days, of harvests, of lives. And now, with the bridge closed and the ferry running at half-capacity, the populace is restless. They want a scapegoat. Someone to blame for the floods, for the scarcity, for the sense that the world has turned against them."

He reached into his coat and produced a folded sheet of paper, which he laid on the workbench between them. "I have it on good authority that the clock's mechanism was not the only thing tampered with. A ledger was found hidden within the case. Tolls. Unpaid. Routed through the Ravensworth counting house. Thirty years' worth. Payments that should have gone to the crown, but instead lined a family vault."

Astrid’s breath caught. She had known, in the back of her mind, that Konrad had brought her here for some reason, but she had not imagined the depth of the trap. "That ledger—"

"Is evidence." Vogel’s eyes bored into hers. "And you, as the foreign-born apprentice, are the most convenient person to bear the weight of it. The clock was sabotaged. The ledger was planted. But the charge will stick to you, I think, unless someone speaks otherwise. I intend to charge you with deliberate destruction of public property and fraud. The penalty is severe for one of your station. But if you confess, if you admit to the sabotage and the ledger’s contents, the punishment may be reduced. Exile, perhaps. A fine. But you will leave Falkenau, and the Ravensworth name will be cleared—or at least, tarnished enough that the matter can be closed."

Astrid’s hands curled into fists at her sides. The warmth of the hearth behind her seemed distant now, the tower’s stone cold as the river outside. "My lord, I—"

"Think carefully, Astrid." Vogel’s voice softened, just enough. "Your uncle Konrad summoned you here. He is a respected burgher. A man of standing. If he were to deny everything, if he were to claim the ledger was forged, or that you acted without his knowledge—well, the town would believe him. But would they believe you? You are a stranger here. An apprentice. A woman in a trade dominated by men who have little patience for either."

The constables shifted. The scribe dipped his quill. Outside, the wind howled against the observatory windows, and somewhere in the valley, the river gurgled against the stone piers of the bridge, the water level creeping higher with each hour.

"I did not sabotage the clock," Astrid said, the words firm despite the tremor in her throat. "And I had no knowledge of the ledger until I found it. I came here to repair a mechanism, not to bring ruin upon myself or my family."

"Perhaps not." Vogel inclined his head. "But the magistrate's duty is to the law, and the law demands accountability. I cannot ignore the evidence, nor can I ignore the clamor of the townsfolk. You have until the morning's first light to decide. Confess, and the ferry will take you out of the valley come the thaw. Refuse, and I will have no choice but to proceed with charges. The choice is yours."

He turned and left the way he had come, the constables flanking him like guards around a prisoner. The door clicked shut behind them, and the tower was suddenly quiet, the weight of his words settling over Astrid like a second silence.

She stood alone in the dim light of the workshop, the gears and springs scattered before her, the ledger tucked into the pocket of her apron where she had placed it. The river outside was rising, the bridge closed, the ferry running on a skeleton crew. She thought of Greta, the ferryman's widow, who had taken Astrid in without question, who had shared her hearth and her stories of the Albrecht family, the dispossessed, the land taken and the name erased.

Astrid went to the window and looked out. The great stone bridge arched over the river, its pillars half-submerged in dark, swirling water. The floodgates had been raised weeks ago, but the river refused to obey, spilling over its banks, turning the meadows into lakes, lapping at the foundations of the houses on the far shore. She thought of Lena Albrecht, living under a false name as a laundress in the town’s lower quarter, the ledger she had recognized, the bargain she had offered: help bring the truth before the spring assizes, and she would testify the sabotage wasn't Astrid's doing.

*A bargain,* Astrid thought. *A dangerous thing.*

She turned from the window and went to the small table where she had set her tools. Her hands were trembling, but she forced them still. She would not confess to what she had not done. She would not let the magistrate use her as a shortcut to justice that the law itself refused to deliver. But she also knew she could not stay here indefinitely. The ice on the river was thickening, and Greta had urged her, half in jest, half in desperation, to finish the repairs quietly and leave by ferry when the ice broke. One berth. Maybe two, if she was lucky. But the magistrate had made it clear: the ferry was a conditional thing, dependent on her cooperation.

*Astrid,* she said to herself, the resolve hardening like iron in a fire. *You are not here to be a scapegoat. You are here to fix a clock. And if there is a ledger, a truth, a family's honor at stake, then you will see it through. Not because the law demands it, but because you cannot be the one who lets it end this way.*

She picked up her burin and returned to the clock. The escapement needed a new tooth, carved to match the old. She had the steel rod in her kit, the file, the steady hand she had honed over years of apprenticeship in Heidelberg, where the work was precise and the winters were long, but the laws were not so easily bent against a stranger. She set to work, the rhythm of her tools against the metal a quiet rebellion against the pressure closing in around her.

Hours passed. The sun dipped low, casting long gold bars across the stone floor, and the wind outside shifted, a softer sound now, less a howl and more a mournful sigh. Greta appeared at the base of the tower stairs, her shawl wrapped tight around her shoulders, a basket of rye bread and salted fish in her arms.

"Astrid." Her voice was gentle, the way it always was. "The magistrate was here?"

"Aye. He left me a choice."

Greta set the basket on the step and climbed the stairs, her hands rough but kind. "And what did you decide?"

Astrid turned from the clock, the new gear in her hand. "I decided to finish the repair. And then I decided to leave. When the ice breaks. If it breaks."

Greta nodded, a slow, knowing motion. "The ice is thick this year. But there is a thaw coming, they say. Early. The river will rise, and the ferry will make one crossing before the water freezes solid again. There is only one berth left, Astrid. The magistrate knows this. He is waiting to see what you will do."

Astrid felt a cold knot form in her stomach. "What do you think I should do, Greta?"

Greta looked at her, and for a moment, the warmth left her eyes, replaced by something sharper, something that had seen too much of the world's cruelty. "I think you are a good girl, Astrid Ravensworth. I think you do not deserve the weight of a family's sins you never committed. But I also think the law, in the hands of men like Vogel, is a river—it flows where it wants, and it drowns whoever is in its path. If you leave now, with the ledger in your pocket, you save yourself. But Lena Albrecht—she has nothing. No name, no standing, no one to speak for her. If you stay, if you use the ledger to bring her home, then you stay to face whatever comes. The question is: which life is worth more to you? Yours? Or the truth?"

Astrid looked at the clock, the gears finally meshing smoothly under her hands. The escapement would rotate, the pendulum would swing, the hour would strike. And somewhere in that strike, she knew, lay the power to change everything—or to destroy herself in the attempt.

"I will stay," she said finally. "To finish the repair. And to see the ledger used as it was meant to be used. Not as a weapon against me, but as a bridge for Lena."

Greta smiled, a small, sad thing. "Then you are a fool, child. But perhaps fools are the only ones who can save the world without breaking it."

She turned and descended the stairs, her footsteps fading into the quiet of the tower. Astrid was alone again, the weight of the ledger in her apron pocket, the ticking of the clock now a steady, hopeful sound beneath the silence of the valley.

She returned to her work, the file singing against the steel, the new gear taking shape beneath her skilled hands. She thought of Konrad, of the crime he had buried, of the way he had looked at her when he had warned her to burn the ledger. She thought of her father, dead these many years, and the silence that had grown between them like moss on stone. She thought of the town below, the faces at the windows, the whispers in the ale houses, the fear and the hope coiled together like winter vines.

*Justice outside the law begins to look necessary because the law wants a shortcut.*

She would not be that shortcut. She would repair the clock, expose the truth, and in the end, she would make the choice that no one else would make. She would give the last berth to the wronged family's last surviving daughter, and she would stay behind to face the consequences. Let the clock strike the hour that convicts their own blood. Let the tolls be read aloud from the tower steps, naming their family. Let the magistrate act. Let Konrad's silence finally cost him everything.

She worked through the evening, the fire in the hearth dying to embers, the wind outside shifting and rising again. She did not look at the door, did not listen for footsteps. She focused on the gear, the curve of the tooth, the way the spring compressed and released, the delicate balance that made a clock a clock, and not just a pile of metal.

When the first star appeared in the sky above the observatory, the gear was complete. She fitted it into the escapement, and for the first time in days, the clock gave a soft, hesitant tick. Then another. The pendulum began to swing, a slow, measured arc, the weight descending steadily.

Astrid let out a breath she had been holding since Vogel had left. The clock was working. The mechanism was whole. And somewhere in the valley, the river continued to rise, the bridge remained closed, and the ferry waited with one seat remaining.

She packed her tools, the ledger still in her apron pocket, and climbed the stairs to the tower's upper chamber. From there, she could see the whole valley, the flooded meadows, the dark water pressing against the bridge's arches, the ferry landing empty and waiting. She could see the far shore, where the Albrecht property lay, now overgrown with weeds, the house silent and empty.

She went to the window and looked down at the landing. Greta was there, wrapping the ferry rope around the post, her eyes on the water. She looked up, saw Astrid, and gave a small wave.

Astrid waved back, and then she did something she had not planned. She went to the small table where she had placed the ledger, unfolded it, and dipped her quill into the inkwell she kept for recording the clock's maintenance. She began to write, not the maintenance log she had intended, but the names and sums from the hidden compartment, her hand steady despite the tremor in her chest. She wrote until the page was full, until the tolls, the dates, the routed payments were laid bare in her own script. She folded the page, sealed it with wax, and tucked it into the pocket of her apron alongside the original.

Then she descended the stairs, stepping out into the cool night air of the valley. The wind was softer now, the river's gurgle a constant, rhythmic sound. The ferry was illuminated by a single lantern, the rope taut between the shores. Greta stood at the prow, looking out at the water.

"Astrid." Greta's voice was warm, but there was something else there, too—a recognition, a farewell. "You're leaving."

"Aye." Astrid stopped at the edge of the landing, the ferry's wood planks creaking slightly under her weight. "But not the way the magistrate expects. I'm taking Lena Albrecht with me. The ledger is hers. And I'm staying here. To face the consequences."

Greta's eyes widened, the lantern light catching the edges. "You would stay? When the ferry could take you out? When the ice is sure to break soon?"

"I have my reasons." Astrid met her gaze, steady and sure. "And I have a ledger that the magistrate will want. But he won't get it from Lena. He'll have to go through me. And through the clock, which I have repaired, and which will strike the hour, and read the tolls aloud, from the tower steps. If he wants a scapegoat, he can have me. But he won't take Lena from me. Not if I have any say in it."

Greta was silent for a long moment. Then she smiled, a genuine this time, the lines around her eyes softening. "God help us both, Astrid Ravensworth. You're a stubborn girl. A foolish girl. But maybe that's exactly what this valley needs."

She picked up a blanket from the ferry's stern and wrapped it around Astrid's shoulders. "Then go. Get Lena. And whatever happens, know that you've given me a story worth telling. Even if it ends in tears."

Astrid stepped into the ferry, the wood firm beneath her boots. Greta pushed off from the shore with a practiced motion, the oars dipping into the dark water, the ferry gliding silently across the river. The far shore grew closer, the outlines of the Albrecht property emerging from the floodwaters. And behind her, the tower stood silent against the sky, the clock now ticking steadily, the hours counting down to something neither of them could yet name.

As the ferry pulled up to the landing on the far side, Lena Albrecht was waiting by the path, a basket of laundry over her arm, her face pale but her eyes bright with a hope she had not allowed herself in thirty years. She saw Astrid, saw the ledger in Astrid's pocket, and something shifted in her expression—relief, cautious gratitude, the beginning of a trust that had been long deferred.

"Astrid." Lena's voice was quiet, carrying across the short distance between them. "You came back."

"I did." Astrid stepped off the ferry, the blanket still around her, the ledger still hidden. "I brought you something. And I stayed for you. But I have to ask something of you. Something difficult."

Lena nodded, her gaze steady. "I have learned that the difficult things are the only ones worth doing."
