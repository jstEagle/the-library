## 7. Gears Against the Frost

The lantern in the observatory tower burned with a thin, wavering flame, its oil smoke spiraling upward through the stone vent like a ghost trying to remember how to rise. Outside, the Palatinate winter had grown impatient, the sky a bruised purple over frozen fields, the river below the great stone bridge a black slab of iron-hard ice that creaked and groaned with the breath of the world. Inside, the great master clock stood like a fallen giant, its face blank, its hands frozen at eleven-fifteen. The escapement was a nest of brass and steel, and somewhere in the tangle, a tooth had been filed down clean through, as if a surgeon had removed a nerve on purpose.

Astrid Ravensworth knelt on the wool-strewn floor, her hands cold enough that the metal of her tools stuck slightly to her skin. She had come from Heidelberg with ledgers and ambitions, but here she was, a foreign-born apprentice, alone in the dark with a mechanism that wanted to die. She ran a thumb over the filed tooth, feeling the clean, beveled edge. It wasn’t wear. It was sabotage. And the whisper that had followed her through the town’s misty streets—*“Ravensworth”*—had not been a compliment.

She reached into the pocket of her wool greatcoat and pulled out the small blank journal she had brought from Heidelberg, its pages waiting for the copying she meant to do during the long nights. The ledger she had found hidden in the clock’s base compartment was a thing of shadows: names, dates, sums paid in tolls that never reached the magistrate’s chest, payments routed through a Ravensworth cousin in some distant town, a trail of coins that had led straight to the ruin of the Albrecht family thirty years past. She had not yet dared to copy it fully; the risk of being caught with the evidence of her own family’s betrayal was a weight she had not expected.

A scratching sound from the stairwell made her look up. The door creaked, not from wind, but from someone’s hand. She stood, brushing soot from her skirts, and called out, “Who’s there?”

The stairwell lantern flared as someone descended, the steps heavy, purposeful. Uncle Konrad. His silhouette was outlined against the pale light of the moon filtering through the tower’s high windows, his face a mask of concern that Astrid had learned to read like a clock face—always watching, always calculating.

“Astrid,” he said, his voice low. “You’re still here. The town sleeps.” He stepped into the circle of lantern light, his wool coat shedding a faint scent of woodsmoke and old leather. “The frost is bitter tonight. Greta says the river’s holding, but she warns the ice may give way before morning.”

“Someone had to look at the clock,” Astrid said, gesturing to the great mechanism. “The escapement’s jammed. I was filing a replacement tooth.”

Konrad crossed the room, his boots silent on the stone floor. He knelt beside the clock, his fingers hovering over the exposed gears. “A filed tooth. Deliberate.” He didn’t sound surprised, only tired. “The question is why now, and how much you know.”

“Enough to know it wasn’t an accident.” Astrid met his gaze. “The ledger’s in the base. I found it yesterday.”

Konrad’s jaw tightened, a muscle jumping near his temple. “Burn it. Forget you saw it. The town has enough blame to go around without adding a Ravensworth name to the scaffold.”

“And if I don’t?” Astrid asked. “The magistrate is looking for a scapegoat. He’s already hinted that a foreign apprentice is a convenient target.”

The silence that followed was filled only by the distant creak of the ice and the faint hum of the wind through the vent. Konrad stood, stepping back, his eyes on the ceiling beams. “You’re not from here, Astrid. You have no name to lose. No land, no legacy. If you burn the ledger, you walk away. The family name is restored, your position secured, and the truth dies with me.”

Astrid felt a strange lurch in her chest. The offer was tempting—the family name, the belonging she had watched from the outside like a picture through a window. Konrad was offering her the very thing she had been denied: a place at the heart of the Ravensworth legacy, without the stain of the past. But the ledger was not just paper; it was proof. It was the difference between a town that moved on and one that finally answered for what it had done to the Albrechts.

“You want me to choose between my survival and their justice,” she said slowly.

“Between your survival and a name,” Konrad corrected. “The Albrecht lands were taken, their name erased. If this ledger is presented at the spring assizes, the restitution is automatic. But if it burns, the debt stays buried, and we all go on pretending the Palatinate is as just as its oats.”

Astrid looked back at the clock. The escapement lay before her, the missing tooth a gap in the rhythm of time. She thought of Lena Albrecht, who now went by the name Maren Voss, laundress at the far end of the market square, who had approached Astrid not with anger but with a bargain: the truth for a testimony, a chance to see her family’s name restored. Astrid had agreed, but she had not imagined the cost of keeping that bargain.

“Lena knows,” Astrid said finally. “She’s been copying portions of the ledger when she thinks no one watches. She has a duplicate already, hidden in her lodging. If I burn this one, she’ll have burned hers too, and the truth will still be out there, but with no hand to guide it to the assizes.”

Konrad stared at her, something shifting behind his eyes—guilt, perhaps, or the slow realization that he had underestimated the apprentice he had taken in out of pity. “You’d risk the family name for a laundress’s word?”

“I’d risk it for the truth.” Astrid picked up her file, the cold metal biting her palm. “The clock’s not going to fix itself, Uncle. And neither are we.”

He let out a long breath, as if the weight of three decades had been transferred into the space between them. “Then we work together. But quietly. If the magistrate learns we’ve both been copying—”

“He’ll charge us both,” Astrid said. “And Lena’s a foreigner too, by another name. We’re all convenient scapegoats.”

They worked side by side after that, the lantern’s flame dipping as the oil ran low. Astrid filed the new tooth with a steady hand, her movements practiced from years of apprenticeship under her father’s watchful eye. The metal shavings fell onto a cloth, glittering like tiny stars in the dim light. Lena, perched on a stool beside her, read the ledger’s entries aloud in a soft, measured voice, her eyes scanning the same lines Astrid had traced with her finger the day before.

*“Toll paid to one Heinrich Ravensworth, December 1718: twenty thalers. Reason: ‘special dispensation for observatory maintenance.’”*

*“Toll unpaid for the Albrecht carriage, March 1719: forty thalers. Reason: ‘family in disgrace, no right to use the bridge.’”*

*“Toll paid to one Heinrich Ravensworth, February 1721: thirty thalers. Reason: ‘repairs to family estates.’”*

Lena’s jaw tightened on the word *disgrace*. “They didn’t just take the land, Astrid. They took the memory of why it mattered.”

“And they used the clock to do it,” Astrid said. “The master clock was the town’s heartbeat. If it stopped, the tolls stopped being recorded, and the ledger could be rewritten.”

By the time the first pale light of dawn crept through the high windows, they had copied the entire ledger into Astrid’s journal and a separate sheet Lena had brought. The escapement was reassembled, the new tooth fitted, and Astrid wound the great spring. The clock’s gears groaned, then sang a hesitant, metallic chord as the pendulum swung.

*“Tick.”*

*“Tock.”*

The sound was faint, but it was there. The clock was alive again.

“It’s running,” Lena whispered, a small smile touching her lips. “Really running.”

“For now,” Astrid said, closing the journal. “But the ledger’s the real work. We need to get it to the assizes before the ice breaks.”

“And before Konrad decides the family name is more important than justice.” Lena’s tone was gentle, but the edge was there.

The stairwell door opened again, this time without Konrad’s heavy step. It was Greta, the ferryman’s widow, her shawl wrapped tight around her shoulders, her eyes red-rimmed from watching the river.

“Magistrate Vogel’s riding into town,” she said, her voice low. “He’s asking after you, Astrid. And he’s asking after the clock. Said he wants to see who’s been tampering with the works.” She glanced at the journal in Astrid’s hand, then at Lena’s sheet. “And he’s got a warrant for Lena Voss’s arrest, on suspicion of sabotage. Planted evidence, he claims, but the magistrate’s a man who believes what he’s told.”

Astrid felt a cold knot form in her stomach. “He can’t—”

“He can, and he will, if we don’t move fast.” Greta’s gaze shifted between them, shrewd and kind. “The river’s risen overnight. The ice is thin in places, but the ferry can still cross. There’s one berth left, and it leaves at first light, before the magistrate’s riders reach the landing.”

“One berth,” Astrid repeated. “For who?”

“For the truth,” Greta said. “Or for the name that protects it.”

The three of them stood in the tower’s dim light, the clock now ticking steadily behind them, the ledger’s weight heavy in Astrid’s pocket. Outside, the river whispered against the bridge’s stone arches, and the sky began to shift from black to a bruised blue, the first hint of the thaw that was supposed to come weeks too late.

“Konrad offered me the name,” Astrid said, the words feeling like a stone dropped into still water. “If I burned the ledger. If I chose the family.”

“And what did you choose?” Lena asked, her voice quiet but steady.

Astrid looked at the journal, at the names of the men who had taken land and names and lives. She thought of the Albrecht daughter, the life she had only just begun to understand. She thought of the clock, finally striking true, and of the hour that would one day mark not just time, but accountability.

“I chose the truth,” she said. “And I chose to make sure it arrived where it could do some good.”

She pulled the ledger from her pocket, the vellum warm from her body, and handed it to Greta.

“Take this to the assizes,” Astrid said. “And tell them the clock’s been repaired. The truth should strike the same hour.”

“Where will you go?” Lena asked.

Astrid looked at the tower, at the town below, at the river that would soon carry the ferry—and whatever choice she made—out into the wider world. She thought of the family name, of the legacy she had been offered and had refused. She thought of the cold that had brought her here, and the warmth she had found in the most unlikely of places.

“I stay,” she said. “For now. The magistrate will want a scapegoat, and if I’m not on the ferry, he’ll find one elsewhere. But I’ve made my choice. The ledger’s gone. The clock’s fixed. And I’ll face whatever comes, knowing I didn’t send an innocent woman to the scaffold so my family could keep a name it didn’t earn.”

She turned to Konrad, who had watched the exchange in silence, his expression unreadable. “Uncle. I’m sorry. For the ledger, for the trouble, for everything.”

Konrad’s jaw worked, and then he nodded, a slow, reluctant motion. “You always were the honest one, Astrid. Even when it cost you.”

“Goodnight, Konrad.” Astrid gave him a small, sad smile. “Thank you. For everything.”

She and Lena descended the stairs into the chill of the tower’s entrance, where Greta had already hitched the ferry’s small boat to the riverside railing. The ice was closer now, the river’s current pressing against it with a patience that promised breaking soon. The magistrate’s riders would be upon them soon, and the ferry’s berth was the only way out.

“Get in,” Astrid said, helping Lena into the boat. “I’ll meet you on the other side. I have to finish a few things here. Finish the clock’s winding mechanism, make sure it stays true. Someone has to make sure the truth keeps ticking.”

Lena took the oars, the wood creaking under her grip. “You’re making a mistake, Astrid. You know that, right?”

“Maybe. But it’s the right mistake.” Astrid stepped back, watching the boat push off into the gray light. She saw Lena’s silhouette against the dawn, the ledger journal clutched to her chest, and felt a strange lightness in her chest, as if a weight she had not known she was carrying had been set down
