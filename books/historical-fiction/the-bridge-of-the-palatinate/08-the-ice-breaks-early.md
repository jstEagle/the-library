## 8. The Ice Breaks Early

The sound that woke her was not the river.

Astrid had slept with the river in her ears for weeks — the low, constant *hush-slurp* of water against stone, the occasional *crack* of ice sheets shifting like tectonic plates. She knew its vocabulary by heart. This was different. A sharp, dry *snap* that vibrated through the floorboards of Greta’s attic room, followed by a groan so deep it lived in the chest rather than the ear.

She was on her feet before consciousness fully returned, bare toes finding the cold planks, hands already reaching for the wool stockings draped over the chair. The window — a small, leaded thing that rattled in its frame — showed a sky the color of bruised amethyst, lightening toward dawn. But the river…

The river was gone.

In its place ran a brown, boiling thing, choked with uprooted willows and slabs of ice the size of wagon wheels. The great stone bridge, the *Steinerne Brücke* that had stood since the Romans, was no longer a bridge but a dam. Debris piled against its arches, water foaming white over the keystones, the parapets vanished beneath the surge. The far bank had simply ceased to exist; meadow and orchard and the low road to Mannheim were swallowed.

*Weeks early,* her mind supplied, calm as a pendulum. *The thaw comes weeks early.*

She pulled on her stockings with trembling fingers, the wool scratching against skin gone cold. Her breeches, her tunic, her leather apron — she dressed in the half-dark, movements automatic, the rituals of the workshop asserting themselves against chaos. *Clean the pinion. Oil the pallets. Check the drop.* But there was no workshop today. Only the flood.

* * *

Greta was already in the kitchen when Astrid descended the narrow stair, the old woman’s broad back blocking the hearth. The smell of coffee — real coffee, not the acorn substitute — fought with the damp chill seeping through the flagstones.

“You hear it,” Greta said without turning. Her voice was flat, stripped of its usual grumble. “The ice broke at Oberwesel in the night. The messenger rode hard. Said the water rose three ells in two hours.”

Astrid set her jaw. “The ferry.”

“Aye. The ferry.” Greta turned, wiping floury hands on her apron. Her face was a map of worry, lines deepened by candlelight and sleepless nights. “Hans’s boat — God rest him — can take one more crossing. *One.* Before the current tears the moorings or the debris stove in her hull. The magistrate’s clerk came by an hour past. Vogel’s orders. One berth. He’ll decide who fills it.”

“One berth.” Astrid repeated the words, tasting iron. “For the whole town?”

“For whoever Vogel says.” Greta spat into the fire. “He’s got his eye on the Albrecht girl. Says she sabotaged the clock. Says he found a filed gear tooth in her laundry basket.”

The coffee cup trembled in Astrid’s hand. “That’s a lie. I — we — the tooth was filed *before* Lena ever touched the case. Konrad knows. Vogel knows.”

“Knowing and proving are different beasts, *Kind*.” Greta’s eyes, the color of winter sky, held Astrid’s. “And Vogel needs a scapegoat before the assizes. A foreign apprentice with a traitor’s name? Or a local girl who washes other folk’s linens? He’ll take the laundress every time. Less paperwork.”

Astrid set the cup down untouched. “Where is Lena?”

“Up at the tower. She came before dawn. Said the light’s better for copying the ledger.” Greta’s mouth twisted. “Stupid brave fool. Vogel’s men are watching the landing. If she goes near the ferry…”

“She won’t.” Astrid was already moving, shrugging into her greatcoat, snatching her hat from the peg. “She’ll stay at the tower. I’ll go to the landing. I’ll — ”

“You’ll do what?” Greta stepped into her path, solid as an oak door. “Argue with Vogel? He’s got six armed men and the law on his side. You’ve got a bag of gears and a conscience. Stay here. Let me — ”

“Let you what? Row the boat yourself?” Astrid’s voice cracked, not with fear but with the terrible, rising pressure of a mainspring wound too tight. “Greta, *please*. I need to see the landing. I need to know… I need to see the space.”

The old woman studied her — the pale face, the dark circles, the hands that had filed brass and polished steel and held a ledger that burned like coals. Something in Greta’s face softened, the flint yielding to something warmer.

“There’s a space,” she said quietly. “One space. And a hundred souls who’d kill for it. Go on, then. But you come back here. You hear me? You come back.”

* * *

The path to the landing was a slurry of mud and melted slush, the cobbles slick with algae. Astrid picked her way past the observatory — the tower stood sentinel against the swollen sky, its clock face dark, the hands frozen at 3:17, the hour the sabotage had stopped them. She could see the window of the clock room, a pale rectangle in the stone. No light showed. Lena would be working by candle, copying names and sums in a cramped hand, racing the flood.

The landing was chaos.

The ferry — a flat-bottomed punt, tarred and scarred — bobbed violently against its moorings, the rope singing under strain. The river roared, a beast unleashed, carrying trees and fence posts and the bloated carcass of a cow. Upstream, the bridge groaned again, a sound like a dying animal.

Vogel stood on the quay in his magistrate’s robes, the fur trim bedraggled, his face purple with cold and authority. Six constables ringed him, pikes rested on boots. Between them and the boat pressed a mob: farmers with bundles, a merchant guarding a strongbox, a woman clutching a squalling infant, old men leaning on sticks. All silent. All watching.

“Order!” Vogel’s voice cracked over the water. “There will be order! The ferry makes *one* crossing. *One.* The berth goes to the person I designate. Any man who rushes the gangplank will be thrown in irons — or into the Rhine, I care not which!”

A murmur ran through the crowd. A man near the front — the baker, Astrid thought, flour dusting his coat — shouted, “And who do you designate, *Herr* Magistrate? Your clerk’s cousin? The tax collector’s wife?”

Vogel’s head snapped around, eyes finding the baker. “I designate the guilty party. The saboteur who stopped the town’s heart. Lena Albrecht — ”

“She’s not here,” a voice said.

Astrid pushed forward, elbowing past a tanner, her boots finding purchase on the wet stone. The crowd parted reluctantly, faces turning — some curious, some hostile, most simply exhausted. Vogel’s gaze locked on her, cold and calculating.

“Astrid Ravensworth,” he said, the name a verdict. “The clockmaker’s niece. The apprentice who *found* the sabotage. Convenient, isn’t it? The outsider discovers the crime, repairs the damage, and now… perhaps inherits the berth?”

“Lena Albrecht is at the observatory,” Astrid said, her voice carrying over the river’s roar. “Copying the ledger. The ledger *your* clerk ‘found’ in her basket — a gear tooth, *Herr* Magistrate, filed with a jeweler’s file. Lena uses shears and soap. Ask any tailor in town.”

“Tailors lie.” Vogel stepped down from the quay, mud sucking at his boots. “And apprentices protect their own. Especially when their own bears the name Ravensworth. The name that stole the Albrecht lands. The name that falsified tolls for thirty years.”

The crowd stirred. A hiss. A woman crossed herself.

“That’s not for you to judge here,” Astrid said, heat rising behind her ears. “That’s for the assizes. In spring. *If* the ledger reaches Mannheim. *If* Lena reaches Mannheim.”

“Spring.” Vogel laughed, a harsh bark. “There is no spring for traitors. The ferry leaves in twenty minutes. The berth goes to the person who confesses the sabotage. Lena Albrecht confesses, she takes the boat. She refuses…” He shrugged, the gesture elegant in its cruelty. “The river decides. And you, *Fräulein* Ravensworth? You could confess. Your uncle Konrad has already offered to vouch for your innocence — if the ledger burns. A generous man, your uncle. Family above all.”

Astrid’s breath caught. *Konrad. He’s here. He made a deal.*

“Where is my uncle?” she demanded.

“Up at the great house. Packing silver. He’ll take the coach road over the hills — if the coach road holds.” Vogel’s smile didn’t reach his eyes. “He secured *his* passage weeks ago. Prudent man. But he left a message for you. ‘The name or the truth. Choose.’ His words.”

The wind tore at Astrid’s hat, whipping hair across her face. She thought of the clock room: the smell of oil and old pine, the gleam of the rebuilt escapement, Lena’s bent head over the copied pages, the way the candlelight caught the scar on her wrist — a burn from a laundry iron, years ago. *Not a saboteur’s hand. A worker’s hand.*

“Twenty minutes,” Vogel said, checking a pocket watch. “I’ll be at the gangplank. One berth. One confession. Choose.”

He turned back to his constables, issuing orders. The crowd surged forward again, a desperate tide.

Astrid stood frozen, the river’s roar in her ears, the magistrate’s words circling like carrion crows.

*Family above all.*

*The name or the truth.*

*One berth.*

She thought of Konrad in his warm great house, packing silver, buying his silence with her future. She thought of Greta’s coffee, untouched. She thought of the ledger pages hidden in her coat pocket, the ink still tacky, the names of the dead and the sums that killed them.

*Thirty years. Old Albrecht died in a cell. His wife died of grief. His daughter — Lena — grew up a stranger in her own town, washing the clothes of the family that ruined hers.*

And Astrid, Astrid Ravensworth, apprentice clockmaker, had held the proof in her hands. Had *repaired the clock that hid the crime*.

Her fingers closed around the ledger pages in her pocket. The paper crinkled, loud as a gunshot in the silence of her mind.

* * *

The observatory tower climbed in a spiral of stone, two hundred and thirteen steps — Astrid had counted them, had counted everything countable since childhood. Steps. Gear teeth. Seconds. Today the count was a prayer: *One, two, three…*

Her lungs burned. Her thighs screamed. The tower swayed imperceptibly, a living thing rooted in shaking earth. Through the arrow slits she saw the valley drowning: the churchyard wall collapsed, the tanner’s vats afloat, the bridge a skeleton in brown foam.

*One hundred forty-seven. One hundred forty-eight.*

The clock room door stood ajar. Candlelight spilled gold onto the stair.

Lena stood at the workbench, her back to the door. She wore her best dress — faded blue wool, mended at the cuffs — and a wool shawl pinned at the throat. Her dark hair was braided, practical. In her hands she held a quill; before her, sheets of paper covered in dense script. The original ledger lay open beside them, its pages yellowed, ink faded to sepia.

She didn’t turn at Astrid’s footfall. “You’re breathing hard. The stairs?”

“Among other things.” Astrid leaned against the jamb, gulping air. “Vogel’s at the landing. He’s giving the berth to whoever confesses to the sabotage. He says… he says Konrad offered me the family name if I burn the ledger.”

Lena’s quill scratched on, steady. “Did he?”

“He’s packing silver. He’s taking the coach road.” Astrid straightened, the words tumbling out. “Lena, the ferry leaves in — less than twenty minutes now. One berth. Vogel wants you. He’ll arrest you the moment you show your face.”

“Then I won’t show my face.” Lena dipped the quill, blotting. “You’ll take the pages. You’ll take the berth. You’ll reach Mannheim by water; the coach road’s washed out at the Kreuzberg pass. I heard the carters talking. The river’s the only way.”

“And you?” Astrid’s voice broke. “You stay? You *drown*?”

“I stay.” Lena turned then. Her face was pale, smudged with ink and exhaustion, but her eyes — gray as the river, hard as flint — held no fear. “I stay with the clock. Someone has to start it. Someone has to make it strike the hour Vogel thinks is stopped. The ledger’s copied. Every entry. Every name. Every pfennig. You take the copies. You take the original. You *go*.”

“No.” The word came from somewhere deep, a place Astrid didn’t know she had. “No. I’m not… I’m not the one. I’m the apprentice. I’m the niece. I’m the one who *found* things. You’re the one who *lost* things. You’re the one who *deserves* — ”

“Deserves?” A ghost of a smile touched Lena’s mouth. “Deserves is a word for fairy tales, Astrid. I have the truth. That’s more than most get. And I have the clock.”

She gestured to the great mechanism behind her: the frame of cast iron, the train of gears, the escapement they had rebuilt together over frozen nights. The pendulum hung still, a silent tongue.

“You fixed it,” Astrid whispered. “We fixed it.”

“We.” Lena nodded. “But *you* understand it. The remontoire, the gravity escapement — you see the music in the math. I only see the hours passing. You’ll keep it. You’ll keep *time*.”

“That’s not…” Astrid’s throat closed. She stepped forward, reaching out, then stopping. “Lena. The berth. It’s *one* seat. One. If I take it, you stay. If you take it, I stay. Vogel will — ”

“Vogel will arrest me for sabotage.” Lena’s voice was gentle. “He has the filed tooth. He has the clerk’s testimony. He has the name Albrecht. If I go to the landing, he takes me. The berth goes to him — or his clerk, or the baker’s cousin. The ledger burns. The clock stays silent. The Albrechts stay dead.”

“And if *I* go?”

“You’re a Ravensworth. Vogel hates the name, but he fears it too. Konrad’s niece. The clockmaker’s apprentice. You walk to the gangplank, you show the magistrate’s seal Konrad gave you — ”

“He didn’t give me a seal!”

“He will. If you ask. For the ledger.” Lena’s eyes held hers, unblinking. “He’ll trade the seal for the silence. That’s what he does. That’s what he *is*. But you won’t ask. Because you’re not him.”

Astrid laughed, a wet, strangled sound. “How do you know what I’ll do? You’ve known me three weeks. I’ve known myself twenty years and I’m still surprised.”

“Because you’re here.” Lena tapped the ledger. “Because you copied every line. Because you *read* them. Because you didn’t burn them when Konrad told you to. Because you’re standing in this tower, in this flood, arguing with me instead of running for the boat.”

The river hammered the bridge. Somewhere upstream, a house collapsed with a sound like thunder.

“Fifteen minutes,” Astrid said, checking her own watch — a verge fusee she’d made herself, brass case, enamel dial. “Maybe less.”

“Then we stop arguing.” Lena set down the quill. She pulled a small packet from her pocket — folded papers, tied with twine. “The copies. The original. The gear tooth — the *real* one, not Vogel’s plant. I kept it. From the first night. It’s in the packet too.”

Astrid took it with numb fingers. The paper was warm from Lena’s pocket.

“Go,” Lena said. “Out the back stair. The postern gate opens onto the garden wall. Drop to the orchard — the water’s not up that high yet. Run for the landing. Show the seal. Get on the boat. *Go.*”

“And you?”

Lena turned back to the clock. Her hands found the winding crank, inserted it into the arbor. “I start the clock. I make it strike. Vogel hears it. The town hears it. The hour that wouldn’t strike… strikes. And when the constables come up these stairs — ” She glanced over her shoulder, a strange light in her eyes. “ — they find me. And the clock running. And no ledger. And no apprentice. Just a laundress who knew too much about gears.”

“Astrid.” The name was a plea.

“Go.” Lena applied weight to the crank. The great weight rose, chain rattling, the mechanism sighing as tension returned. “*Go.*”

* * *

The postern gate gave onto a garden drowned to the wall tops. Astrid dropped into water that seized her legs like jaws, cold so intense it burned. She scrambled up the slope toward the landing, the packet clutched against her chest, the ledger pages a brand against her ribs.

The crowd had thickened. Shouts rose above the river. Fists flew. The constables held a line, pikes horizontal.

Vogel stood on the gangplank, one boot on the ferry’s gunwale. Beside him, Konrad — in a fine beaver coat, a leather valise at his feet — looked bored.

“…fifteen minutes past,” Vogel was saying. “No confession. No berth claimed. The boat waits for no — ”

“Herr Magistrate!”

Astrid’s voice cracked across the water. She stood at the quay’s edge, mud to her knees, water streaming from her coat, hair plastered to her skull.

Vogel turned. Konrad’s head lifted. The crowd fell silent.

“Astrid Ravensworth,” Vogel said. “You’re late. The berth — ”

“Is mine.” She held up a hand, the packet visible. “I have the ledger. The original. The copies. The evidence of thirty years’ toll fraud. The proof that the Albrecht lands were stolen by Ravensworths — by my father. By my uncle.” She looked at Konrad. “By *you.*”

Konrad’s face didn’t change. “A child’s fantasies. Burn them, Astrid. Get on the boat. Be a Ravensworth again.”

“The name or the truth,” she said. “You said choose. I choose the truth.”

A murmur ran through the crowd. The baker crossed himself again. The woman with the infant wept.

Vogel’s jaw tightened. “You confess the sabotage?”

“I confess *nothing*.” Astrid’s voice rang, steady as a struck bell. “The sabotage was done by Konrad Ravensworth. Filed the tooth himself. Planted the false tooth in Lena Albrecht’s basket. He told me to burn the ledger. He offered me the family name. I refused.”

“Lies!” Konrad’s voice cut the air, sharp as a chisel. “The girl is mad. The flood, the cold — she’s delirious. Vogel, put her on the boat. I’ll vouch for her. The ledger… the ledger is a forgery. The Albrechts forged it to reclaim lands they lost fairly.”

“Fairly?” Astrid laughed, wild and free. “Fairly? Old Albrecht died in irons in Mannheim. His wife starved. His daughter scrubs floors in the house that was hers. *Fairly?*”

She turned to the crowd. “You know the tolls. Every wagon, every boat, every head of cattle. You paid. Your fathers paid. The ledger shows where the money went. Not to the Elector. Not to the town. To the Ravensworths. *To us.*”

“Silence!” Vogel roared. “Constables — seize her! Seize the papers!”

The pikes moved. The crowd surged — *toward* Astrid, not away. Hands caught the pikes, shoulders braced against shafts. The baker shouted, “Read it! Read the names!”

Vogel hesitated, caught between law and mob.
