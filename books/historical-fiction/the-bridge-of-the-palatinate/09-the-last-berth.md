## 9. The Last Berth

The river had turned against them overnight. What had begun as a swollen trickle of autumn rain had become a roaring current that tore at the foundations of Falkenau like a living thing, hungry and unrelenting. Astrid stood at the window of Greta's little cottage, watching the water lap hungrily at the base of the stone bridge that had carried travelers across the gorge for three centuries—until the floods came, until the great arches groaned under the pressure, until the whole world narrowed to this one crossing that now refused to bear weight.

The master clock had been calling to them for hours now, its pendulum swinging with a rhythm that felt increasingly frantic. But Astrid couldn't bring themselves to climb those tower steps again. Not yet. Not while the weight of what they'd found pressed against their ribs like a stone.

The ledger lay open on the table between them and Lena, its ink-blotted pages spelling out thirty years of careful theft, of forged tallies, of a family name built on the ruin of another. Greta had brought them tea without asking questions—old souls always knew when to be quiet—and now sat in her chair by the fire, mending a sock with the methodical patience of someone who'd learned that small things done consistently could hold a world together.

"You should sleep," Greta said, not looking up. "Though I doubt sleep comes easy these days."

Astrid shook their head. "I keep thinking about the gear tooth. Someone filed it down so precisely. Not enough to stop the clock entirely, but enough to make it hesitate. To make it miss its mark."

"Like a heartbeat skipping," Lena murmured from where she sat, tracing the edge of the ledger with a finger stained black from handling the pages too much. "Like something trying to tell us something."

That night, Astrid dreamed of their father's workshop in Heidelberg, of the smell of linseed oil and brass shavings, of hands steady enough to set a hairspring but cruel enough to ruin a man's life. They woke with the taste of iron in their mouth and the sound of water still hammering against the bridge below.

By dawn, the river had begun its work of destruction more openly. Great chunks of ice—formed early by some cruel twist of weather—crashed against the pilings with a sound like thunder. The ferryman's boat, tethered at the landing, bobbed like a cork in a storm.

"They'll get one crossing," Greta announced, emerging from the kitchen with a basket of bread and cheese. "Before the ice makes it impossible. Before the spring melt makes it deadly."

Astrind looked at Lena, really looked at her for the first time since they'd met—not as the laundress with the secret, not as the enemy or the ally, but as a person who had carried grief for thirty years and still managed to walk upright beneath it.

"The magistrate," Astrid said slowly. "He knows now, doesn't he? About the ledger?"

"Vogel's been asking questions since yesterday," Greta replied, settling heavily into her chair. "About the sabotage, about the clock, about why a stranger came all the way from Heidelberg to fix what wasn't broken."

Lena closed the ledger gently and slipped it into the inner pocket of her dress, next to her heart. "And what happens when he finds the truth?"

"What happens," Greta said softly, "is that everyone gets exactly what they deserve."

* * *

The tower steps were slick with ice when Astrid climbed them that morning, each step a careful negotiation between urgency and caution. Below, the courtyard was a chaos of water and debris—branches, roof tiles, anything the floodwaters chose to carry. The master clock stood silent in its chamber, waiting for hands that might never come.

But Astrid had come to fix it. Had come to make it whole again, even if doing so meant breaking everything else.

They worked with the precision of training, methodically cleaning each component, replacing worn springs, adjusting the escapement until the mechanism hummed with the promise of accuracy. It would take hours to regulate fully, but the heart of the clock—its soul, if one could speak of such things in mechanical terms—beat steady once more.

When the final gear clicked into place, Astrid stepped back and listened. For the first time in months, perhaps years, the clock began its countdown to the hour. Tick. Tock. Tick. Tock. Each sound building toward something inevitable.

At the landing, the scene was surreal. Vogel stood with his arms crossed, watching the ferryman struggle to secure his boat against the rising current. A crowd had gathered—townspeople drawn by some instinct deeper than curiosity, perhaps, by the sense that history was about to unfold before them.

"Miss Ravensworth," Vogel called up, his voice carrying across the courtyard. "I trust you've finished whatever business brought you to our humble town?"

Astrid descended the stairs, their boots finding purchase on stone worn smooth by centuries of footsteps. They kept their expression neutral, but inside, something was shifting—settling into place like a gear finding its proper mesh.

"The clock will strike the hour soon," Astrid replied. "In exactly seventeen minutes."

Vogel's eyes narrowed. "And you plan to be where, exactly, when that happens?"

The question hung in the air like smoke. Around them, the town held its breath.

* * *

Seventeen minutes wasn't much time, but it was enough. Enough for the river to claim another victim, enough for a lie to crumble, enough for truth to find its voice.

Astrid found Lena near the edge of the crowd, her hands twisting the edge of her apron until the fabric threatened to tear. The ledger was safe in her pocket, but the weight of it seemed to press against her shoulders all the same.

"We need to talk," Astrid said quietly, steering her toward the relative shelter of the tower's shadow.

"I know what you're going to say," Lena replied without preamble. "That I should take the ferry. That I should go with the ledger and testify at the assizes."

"And you think I should stay?"

"I think," Lena said slowly, "that you think staying here means something. That it means you're choosing truth over family, justice over comfort."

Astrid felt the familiar burn behind their eyes—not tears, but the pressure of emotion held too long. "Do you know what Konrad offered me? Full restoration of the family name. A position in Heidelberg. Enough gold to start over, somewhere that doesn't know our history."

"And?"

"And I realized that everything he offered, he'd already taken from someone else."

The clock began its countdown. Tick. Tock. Tick. Tock.

"You don't have to do this," Lena said. "You don't have to sacrifice yourself for any of this."

"But I do," Astrid whispered. "Because if I take that ferry, if I disappear with the ledger, then the story ends with me as the hero and everyone else as the villain. But the truth isn't clean like that. The truth is messy, and complicated, and it involves people I love who did terrible things."

Lena reached out, caught Astrid's hand in both of hers. "Then let me help you carry it."

"No," Astrid said gently. "Let me carry mine."

* * *

The ferry was ready. The ferryman—a young man with eyes too old for his face—had managed to free his boat from the ice that had begun to form along the banks. One trip. That was all they had.

Vogel approached with his usual carriage of authority, but Astrid detected something underneath—fear, perhaps, or the dawning realization that the ground beneath his feet was shifting.

"Miss Ravensworth," he said formally. "I'm placing you under arrest for the sabotage of municipal property and for interfering with the duties of a public official."

Astrid blinked slowly. "On what evidence?"

Vogel's smile didn't reach his eyes. "Evidence isn't always what you think it is. Sometimes it's simply the weight of suspicion, the convenience of accusation."

The crowd murmured approval—or perhaps it was something else. Fear, maybe. Or recognition that justice and convenience often walked hand in hand.

Lena stepped forward. "You have no proof. The ledger proves everything."

"Does it?" Vogel raised an eyebrow. "Because as far as I know, that ledger could contain anything. Could be forgery. Could be—"

"The tolls are documented in the cathedral archives," Astrid interrupted, their voice carrying across the courtyard. "Every entry matches. Every discrepancy points to one family. One name."

The clock struck eight. Clear, bright, cutting through the tension like a blade.

"Then let's hear it," Vogel said, his composure cracking just slightly. "Let's hear what the Ravensworth name has to say for itself."

* * *

Astrid led with their voice, steady despite the storm of emotion threatening to overwhelm them.

"For thirty years," they began, climbing the tower steps with Lena close behind, "my family has lived under the protection of silence. My uncle Konrad, the town's most respected burgher, has spent decades building a reputation on the ruins of the Albrecht family. The tolls they collected, the debts they forgave, the land they seized—all of it documented in this ledger, hidden away like a shameful secret."

The clock behind them ticked steadily, each beat counting down to something inevitable.

"My father, may he rest in peace, was a good man who made terrible choices. He looked away when he should have spoken. He signed papers without asking questions. He let a neighbor die in debtor's prison rather than admit the extent of his own corruption."

Lena felt the crowd shift around her, sensed the weight of recognition settling over them like a shroud. These weren't just accusations—they were admissions. Confessions spoken aloud for the first time in thirty years.

"And I," Astrid continued, their voice growing stronger with each word, "am Astrid Ravensworth, apprentice clockmaker, daughter of this family, and I choose to stand here—not as a defendant, but as a witness. Because the truth deserves more than silence, and justice deserves more than convenience."

The clock struck nine.

In that moment, the heavy oak door of the tower burst open, and Konrad stumbled into the frame, his face pale with shock and something that might have been hope.

"Astrid, stop this madness," he called down, his voice echoing off the stone walls. "Whatever game you're playing, whatever scheme you think you're advancing—"

"I'm not playing games, Uncle," Astrid replied, their voice carrying clearly through the courtyard. "I'm reading from the ledger. The same ledger you tried to burn. The same ledger that proves you murdered a man for his land and spent thirty years pretending it never happened."

Konrad's hands clenched into fists. "You don't know what you're saying."

"Don't I?" Astrid's voice cracked, just slightly. "Because I do. I know about the night old Albrecht came to your shop, begging for more time. I know about the forged documents you had drawn up. I know about the letter you intercepted, the one that would have cleared his name if anyone had ever seen it."

The clock struck ten.

Around them, the crowd had fallen utterly silent. Even the river seemed to hold its breath, as if nature itself recognized that something irreversible was about to happen.

Konrad looked from his niece to his brother's portrait hanging in the clock case, then back to the ledger that had been his carefully guarded secret for so long.

"You have a choice," Astrid said quietly. "Burn with the lie, or stand with the truth."

For the first time in thirty years, Konrad Ravensworth had no answer.

* * *

The ferry was still there, bobbing gently against its moorings. The ice hadn't yet claimed it completely, and the young ferryman waited patiently, having heard enough to know that some passengers were more important than others.

Lena looked at Astrid, really looked at them for the first time since the crisis had begun. Not as the apprentice who'd fixed the clock, not as the stranger who'd arrived with questions, but as the person who was about to make the hardest choice of their life.

"You don't have to stay," Lena said softly.

"I know."

"And if you stay, they'll arrest you. They'll charge you with aiding and abetting, with conspiracy, with—"

"With being a Ravensworth," Astrid finished. "Which seems to be a crime in itself these days."

A bitter laugh escaped Lena's lips. "Maybe it is."

The clock began its final countdown, each tick marking another second closer to the moment when everything would change. Astrid could hear Vogel organizing his men, preparing for whatever came next. Could hear Konrad's footsteps as he paced the tower chamber, trying to decide between family loyalty and self-preservation.

"You've given up everything," Lena observed.

"No," Astrid corrected. "I've chosen what's worth keeping."

The ferryman called out, his voice carrying across the water. "Last call! Ice is forming fast!"

Lena moved toward the boat, her hand never straying far from the ledger hidden in her pocket. But she paused at the edge of the landing, looking back at Astrid with an expression that spoke of understanding rather than judgment.

"Go," she said simply. "Finish what you started."

And Astrid did. They climbed back into the tower, past the clock that now hummed with restored purpose, past the mechanism that would soon strike the hour with perfect accuracy, past the weight of family legacy and personal betrayal and the terrible clarity that sometimes love means letting go.

The clock struck eleven.

In the courtyard below, Vogel raised his hand for silence. Konrad's footsteps stopped mid-pace. The entire town waited for whatever pronouncement was about to come.

Astrid stepped to the window, the ledger clutched tightly in their hands, and began to read aloud the names of every toll that had been collected, every debt that had been forged, every lie that had been told in the name of family honor.

The names echoed off the stone walls, fell like rain upon the courtyard below, and settled into the hearts of everyone who heard them.

Including Konrad's.

Finally, after thirty years of silence, the truth had found its voice.

And the clock, faithful and true, marked its passing with the sound of justice finally arriving.

* * *

Spring would come early to Falkenau, as it always did, bringing with it the reopening of the bridge and the opportunity for new beginnings. But for now, in the space between one hour and the next, between truth and consequence, between family and justice, Astrid Ravensworth stood alone in the tower chamber, listening to the sound of their own name being spoken for the first time with something like pride.

The clock would strike twelve soon, and when it did, everything would be different. Everything would be exactly as it should be.

Everything would be true.
