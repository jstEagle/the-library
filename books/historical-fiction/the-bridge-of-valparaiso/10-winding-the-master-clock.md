## 10. Winding the Master Clock

The harbor in the last week of March wore a different light than it had a year before, though the water itself had not changed—still the same bruised silver, the same gulls stitching their ragged seams above the fish market, the same bells of the Iglesia de La Matriz counting hours that belonged to no one and everyone. Noor Zhou stood at the foot of the mole with a single trunk at their feet and a coat too thin for the wind off the Pacific, and understood that the light was the same and they were not.

A year. It seemed impossible that so much could be weighed in so small a span of time, and yet Noor had spent their whole life among instruments that measured exactly this—the slow, indifferent accumulation of seconds into consequence. The inquiry had opened in November. By January, the *Gaceta Marítima del Pacífico* had printed the third installment of the ledgers, and the words *deliberate reduction of provisions to the barrios altos* sat in cold type on cheap paper, in English and Spanish both, in a publication no Chilean court could suppress and no Chilean minister could pretend not to have read. Tomás Iriarte had insisted on printing both languages. "Let them read it in the tongue they pray in," he had said, "and in the tongue they lie in."

The inquiry had done what inquiries do: it had moved at the speed of a government's shame, which is to say, slowly, and only when watched. Two naval officers had been relieved of their posts. A third had died before he could be called—a heart, the newspapers said, though Noor noticed that no one had asked for an autopsy. The historians of the new regime had begun, quietly, to adjust their arithmetic. Not to confess. Confession was too much to hope for. But to adjust, the way a clockmaker adjusts a escapement that runs a minute fast—not because it is wrong, but because someone has finally noticed.

And Don Elías Vergara, dead man, had testified.

Noor had been in the gallery the day he walked into the chamber in Valparaíso, upright in a gray coat, gaunt as a winter branch, and gave his name in a voice that carried without effort. *Elías Vergara Arrieta, formerly chief registrar of customs, this port.* The room had made a sound Noor had never heard before and never forgot—not a gasp, not a shout, but a long collective intake of breath, like the sea drawing back before a wave. A dead man, testifying. The stenographers' pens had hovered a full three seconds before anyone dared write.

He had spoken for six hours. He had named names, dates, tonnages. He had described, in the flat voice of a man reading from a ledger because the ledger was the only way he could bear to say it, how the blockade orders had been written to starve the hill districts into submission, how the grain shipments had been turned away while the officers' messes on Cerro Alegre served fresh bread. He had not wept. He had not raised his voice. When the president of the commission had asked him—gently, the way one asks a man who has already given up everything—*why*, Don Elías had folded his hands on the table and said:

"Because I kept the accounts. And an account that is never read is a debt that is never paid. I am an old man, señor. I wished to die solvent."

He had died in July, in his sleep, in the house on Cerro Concepción that Ríos had kept for him through the long year of his death. Noor had gone to the funeral, which was not really a funeral, since the man had been mourned once already and the state could hardly mourn him twice without embarrassment. There had been perhaps thirty people. Iriarte had come, smelling of ink and cheap tobacco. A woman Noor did not know had placed a single white flower on the coffin and said, to no one in particular, "He kept the accounts." Wen had not come. But Wen had stood in the doorway of the workshop that morning, watching Noor dress in their one good coat, and had said nothing, and had handed them, at the door, a small oilcloth packet containing rice cakes for the road—and in that nothing, and in that packet, Noor had read everything the old man would never say aloud.

* * *

The guild had struck Noor's name from its rolls in February. It had been done quietly, without ceremony, in the way that such things are done—a letter delivered by a boy who would not meet Noor's eyes, citing irregularities of apprenticeship, questions of character, the unfortunate publicity. Master Horacio Delgado, who had signed the commission for the observatory clock and had been proud, briefly, loudly proud, to have a Zhou working under his mark, had not written. Noor had not expected him to. The guild was a mechanism like any other, and its first law was the same law Noor's family had lived by for two generations: *be precise, be useful, be invisible.* Noor had been precise. Noor had been useful. Noor had declined, catastrophically, to be invisible.

What surprised Noor was how little it hurt. There was grief in it—grief for the workbench Noor would never stand at again, for the smell of brass filings and clove oil, for the particular silence of a workshop at dawn when the day's first gear is set in the lathe. But the grief was clean, like the grief for the burned observatory, and it did not fester. Because exile from the guild was not exile from the trade. Iriarte had seen to that.

"Every port on this coast runs on clocks that don't run," the editor had said, the week after the funeral, pushing a ledger across his desk with a hand stained permanently blue-black. "Ships arrive when they arrive, cargoes spoil, insurers lie, and no one can prove a thing because nobody keeps honest time. You do. You'll audit the harbor masters from Callao to Guayaquil. You'll carry the *Gaceta* with you. And you'll write me a column—*The Honest Hour*, we'll call it—and you'll sign it."

"Sign it?" Noor had said. "With my name?"

"With your name. That's rather the point of you, Zhou."

So it was that Noor stood now at the foot of the mole with a trunk containing two shirts, a set of jeweler's screwdrivers wrapped in flannel, a bound copy of every installment the *Gaceta* had printed, and—wrapped separately, at the bottom, in a square of oilcloth that had once wrapped something far more dangerous—a single gear.

It was not a large gear. It was the third wheel of the observatory's master clock, or what had been the third wheel, salvaged from the ashes of Cerro Alegre by a fireman who had read the *Gaceta* and understood, in the way that simple men sometimes understand more quickly than learned ones, that the clock had been a kind of martyr and its remains deserved better than the scrap heap. He had brought it to the gazette office wrapped in his own neckerchief, embarrassed, saying he didn't know why he'd kept it, only that it seemed wrong to leave it in the rubble. Noor had known why. Some things survive fire because they are meant to. Some things survive because a stranger decides they should. Both kinds of survival, Noor had learned, are miracles, and neither asks permission.

The gear was blackened at its rim, but the teeth were intact, and when Noor turned it in the light it caught the sun the way it always had—honestly, without ornament. A clockmaker's whole creed in a circle of brass: *every tooth meets its hour, or the mechanism is a lie.*

* * *

The ship was the *Amparo*, a coastal steamer bound north for Coquimbo, and from there, in stages, for Callao. Noor had chosen the route deliberately, though they had not admitted it even to themselves until the gangway was before them. Coquimbo. Where a young lieutenant named Ignacio Fuenzalida had been dragged from the surf with a musket ball in his shoulder by a customs registrar who had waded into the water in his good coat. Where that same lieutenant, thirty years later, had signed a death notice for the man who saved him, and had spent a year learning what that signature cost.

They had met one last time, Fuenzalida and Noor, on the docks in April—Noor carrying proofs to the gazette, Fuenzalida standing in the shadow of a warehouse with his cap in his hands like a man who had forgotten what heads were for. He had not threatened. That time was past. He had said only, "He asked for me, at the end. Did you know that? Wrote to me. Said he bore me no ill will." A long pause, the gulls screaming over the water. "I could not answer the letter. I still cannot."

"You answered it today," Noor had said, "if you read the inquiry transcripts."

"I have read nothing else since January." And he had put on his cap, and straightened, and walked away down the mole with the gait of a man who had set down something heavy and did not yet know how to walk without it. Noor had not seen him since. But Iriarte had said, the week before, that a certain retired captain had sent the *Gaceta* an anonymous subscription for fifty copies, to be distributed free to the naval academy at Valparaíso, and Noor had understood that some debts are paid in the only currency the debtor has left.

Now the mate of the *Amparo* was calling the last of the passengers aboard, and the wind had freshened, and Noor picked up the trunk.

Before boarding, they did what they had come to the mole to do. They set the trunk down. They unwrapped the oilcloth. And they held the third wheel of the master clock up to the light one final time—not as a relic, not as evidence, but as an instrument, and they did the thing their hands had been made to do, the thing no guild could strike from them, the thing that was theirs as surely as their own pulse.

They wound it.

There was no mainspring, no escapement, no train of gears for the wheel to drive. It was a gesture, and Noor knew it was a gesture, and made it anyway: the thumb and forefinger finding the old familiar purchase, the small precise pressure, the imagined click of a pawl engaging, a spring taking its tension. *One last winding,* Noor thought, *for the clock that kept more than time.* Somewhere in the imagined mechanism, a pendulum that no longer existed began, in the only place it still lived—inside the person holding the wheel—to swing.

Then Noor wrapped the gear again, stowed it in the trunk, and walked up the gangway into the noise and the salt and the ordinary, magnificent machinery of the morning.

The *Amparo* cast off at half past nine by the harbor clock, which ran, as all harbor clocks ran, four minutes fast. Noor stood at the rail as Valparaíso fell away—the fish market, the workshop window where Wen would be opening the shutters about now, pretending not to watch the ship go, the hills where a dead man had finally been allowed to rest, the mole where a gazette office still smelled of ink. The truth was on the record. The ledgers were read. The accounts, as Don Elías would have said, were solvent.

The ship's bell struck the half hour, and Noor did not correct it. Let it run fast. Let it run however it liked. Time, Noor had learned at last, moves on without asking permission—and the only honest thing a person can do, the only thing that was ever asked of them, is to be there when it passes, keeping count, and to write the number down.

Ahead, the coast unrolled northward into haze—Coquimbo, Callao, ports that did not yet know they were waiting for a column called *The Honest Hour*, and a clockmaker with a blackened gear in a trunk, and a name signed at the bottom of every page.

Noor Zhou turned their face to the wind, and began, silently, to count.
