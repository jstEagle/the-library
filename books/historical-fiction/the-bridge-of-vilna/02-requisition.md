## 2. Requisition

The first thing I noticed when the boots thumped down the cracked stone hallway of the trading house was the sudden, metallic scent of polished brass and oil, a smell that cut through the stale, brine‑kissed air of the harbor like a knife. The officers moved in tight formation, their uniforms crisp as if they’d just stepped out of a barracks parade, and the heavy doors of the ledger room were torn open with a sound that made the plaster above the old fireplace crackle. I was still half‑asleep, the kind of sleep that clings to you like a wet towel after a dive, when the clamor erupted.

The company clerk, a wiry man named Jozef, had been the keeper of our lives and deaths for as long as I could remember. He kept the indenture ledger in a walnut chest, its hinges rusted from years of neglect, and he knew every family’s name, every debt, every secret whispered in the dim light of the diving pits. Jozef’s eyes were always darting, his fingers always tracing the margins where the divers’ own markings were etched in a language only they could read. When the officers burst through the double doors, Jozef’s pale face turned a shade I’d never seen—somewhere between ash and parchment—and he bolted for the back stairwell, his boots echoing down the spiral as if the building itself were trying to swallow him.

“Hold him!” one of the officers barked, and two soldiers lunged at Jozef’s coat tails. He slipped away, his fingers leaving a smear of ink on the floorboards as he vanished into the night. The ledger chest was left open, its lid swinging like a surprised jaw, exposing pages of names that looked like a graveyard’s roll call. I could see the black ink of the entries, each one a promise of labor, a debt owed to the company, a life bound to the sea.

The commander, a tall man in a double-breasted coat with a scar across his left cheek that seemed to smile when he turned his head, turned his gaze to me. His voice was smooth, almost musical, but the words cut through the chaos like a blade.

“Young Kavanagh,” he said, nodding at the name etched on my contract, “you have a talent for reading what the others cannot. We need that talent.”

I tried to keep my composure, but my hands trembled slightly as I stepped forward. The weight of the ledger, the weight of the room, the weight of the expectations that seemed to press down on my shoulders was oppressive. I could feel the eyes of the officers on me, not just as a curiosity but as a necessity. The clerk’s flight left a void that I, as the only other person who could decipher the divers’ marginalia, was expected to fill.

“May I?” I asked, though the question was almost rhetorical. I lifted the lid, and the cool air of the harbor drifted in, carrying with it the faint scent of kelp and the distant rumble of waves crashing against the pier. The ledger pages were thick, the ink dark, each entry a line of fate.

The commander’s eyes narrowed as he scanned the first page. “What is this?” he asked, tapping a name with his finger. “Kavanagh, Casper—”

“—indentured 1902, age nine, to pay for my mother’s illness,” I finished, my voice steady but thin. “And a note in the margin: ‘mother’s fever, cannot work,’”

He frowned. “That’s a marginal note. What does it mean?”

I swallowed, feeling the weight of the ledger pressing into my chest. “It’s a code the divers use to mark those who are too sick to dive. It’s a way to keep track of who can’t work without exposing them to the army’s conscription lists.”

He nodded, his expression shifting from curiosity to something darker. “And what else?”

I ran my fingertip over the next entry, a name that stood out among the rest—Tomas, my brother, listed as “alive, conscripted.” The margin beside his name was a simple line: a single ink stroke, a mark that read like a slash. I could feel the tug of memory, the day I’d seen that same slash in the ledger, the day my mother had whispered that Tomas had been taken away, that the army had claimed him, that the mark meant he was now a laborer for the state.

The commander’s eyes widened. “You know about Tomas?”

I forced a smile, the kind I’d practiced in front of the army officers during the earlier raids on the harbor. “Only what the ledger says,” I said, keeping my voice low. “The divers keep these marks so the army can see who’s fit for labor and who isn’t. It’s a protection, but also a sentence.”

He glanced at the other officers, their faces a mixture of curiosity and calculation. “You have a gift, Kavanagh. We need you to translate these margins for us. The army needs to know who can be conscripted, who can be forced to work, who can be left behind.”

My heart hammered against my ribs, a drumbeat that seemed to echo the thudding of the soldiers’ boots. I could feel the ledger’s pages whispering, each name a ghost, each marginal mark a promise or a curse. The thought of the army holding my community’s names, of them being able to reorder our lives with a single stroke of a pen, made my stomach twist into knots.

“Very well,” the commander said, his voice softening just enough to be deceptive. “You will stay with us. You will translate the ledger, and in return, we will protect you. You will be our interpreter.”

I nodded, the movement automatic, the weight of the ledger’s expectation pressing on my shoulders. The army officers stepped back, allowing me to approach the chest again, this time not as a diver’s daughter but as the keeper of secrets.

The ledger opened to a page where a name I recognized—Marta’s husband—had been marked with a small circle in the margin. I read the entry aloud, my voice barely above a whisper.

“Marta, wife of Jan, indentured 1898. Margin: ‘circle’ indicates he is fit for labor, but the family needs him to stay home.” I paused, feeling the tension in my throat. “The army will see this as a sign that Jan is healthy and can be conscripted, but the divers have marked him to protect his family. It’s a lie they tell to keep him safe.”

The commander’s eyebrows lifted. “You’re lying to us?”

I swallowed, the taste of iron in my mouth. “We lie to them, yes. We lie to keep each other alive. The ledger is not just a record of debt; it’s a shield.”

He stared at me, his expression unreadable. “And you will help us with that?”

I forced a smile, the kind that hid the terror bubbling beneath. “I will help you read it, but I will never let you use it against our people. The ledger belongs to us, not to the army.”

He inclined his head, a gesture that seemed to weigh the words. “You are a clever girl, Kavanagh. You will stay close. We will watch you.”

The officers began to file out, their boots echoing down the hall as they left the room. I stood there, the ledger chest still open, the pages fluttering in the breeze that had slipped through the broken window. The scent of the sea and the stale tobacco of the clerk’s pipe lingered in the air, a reminder of the world I knew before the army’s arrival.

I lifted the ledger, feeling the weight of each page, each name, each secret. The marginal notes were a language I had learned in the darkness of the harbor, in the cold water where my fingers had learned to feel the texture of the ink as surely as the sand on the seabed. They were a code that protected my family, that protected my community, but now they were in the hands of a foreign power, a power that could twist them into weapons.

I took a deep breath, feeling the cold stone under my boots, the rough texture of the ledger’s spine, the heat of my own palms. The army had taken the ledger, but they had not taken the knowledge that lived within its margins. I could read them, I could understand them, and I could choose what to reveal.

As the doors closed behind the officers, I turned to face the ledger one more time, my eyes catching the faint reflection of the scar on the commander’s cheek. It seemed to smile at me, a reminder that the world could be cruel and that cruelty often wore a uniform.

I lifted the ledger to my chest, feeling the weight of the names, the weight of the secrets, the weight of the future. And with a sigh that was both a breath and a promise, I whispered to the empty room:

“Let them think they have the ledger, but the real power lies in the margins. Let them read what they will, and I will write what they cannot see.”

The night air grew colder as the officers left, but I stood there, the ledger still clutched to my heart, the dread beneath my forced composure growing thicker, like the oil that coated the brass of the officers’ weapons. The army had taken the ledger, but they had not taken the knowledge that lived within its margins. And now, I would decide how to use that knowledge.

* * *

The hall was quiet again, the only sound the distant crash of waves against the pier and the soft rustle of the ledger’s pages as they settled back into the chest. I could feel the eyes of the army watching me from the shadows, waiting for me to make my next move. The ledger was theirs, but the truth of its margins still belonged to me, and I was about to decide what to do with it.

The hook: The commander’s scar seemed to smile, a silent challenge that promised trouble to come. The ledger’s weight pressed against my ribs, a reminder that the army’s power was only as strong as the stories I was willing to tell. And as the night deepened, a soft knock arrived at the door—a figure in a uniform I did not recognize, carrying a sealed envelope that bore the insignia of the Quartermaster’s office. The tension in my shoulders hardened, and I realized that the next chapter of my life—and perhaps the life of Vilna—had just begun.
