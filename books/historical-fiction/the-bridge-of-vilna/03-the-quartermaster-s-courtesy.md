## 3. The Quartermaster's Courtesy

The ledger lay open on Aldous Rehn's desk like a drowned thing, its pages swollen with the salt of a hundred harbors. I had been brought here not as a diver anymore, but as a reader of margins, a girl who could still make out the old clerk's cramped notes in the spaces between the names. The quartermaster himself was a man who smiled without moving his eyes, who offered me a chair and a cup of tea that was too sweet by half, and in that small kindness felt like a trap.

*“You have a gift for the code,”* he said, his voice the color of winter light. *“The divers leave marks only they can read. I need someone who can read them and not betray the source.”* He set a sealed envelope before me, wax still tacky. *“Translate what you can, Casper Kavanagh. In return, you eat better than the other divers’ children. You sleep in the quartermaster’s quarters instead of the hold. You are exempt from the labor draft.”*

I took the envelope. Inside were pages of the indenture book, seized from the trading company’s offices when the army requisitioned the harbor. Names were crossed out in ink that smelled of copper and urgency. I had learned to read these margins as a child, tracing the old clerk’s symbols with calloused fingers while my mother coughed in the next room. Each symbol was a secret: a widow’s plea for her son’s release, a father’s request that his daughter be spared the deep beds, a lover’s code for meeting after shift. The divers knew them by heart, but they never spoke them aloud. To speak first was to invite notice.

I bent my head over the pages. The first entry was Tomas’s name, crossed through with a thick line, and beside it, a symbol I had not seen in years: a spiral inside a circle, meaning “conscripted for harbor cleanup, dangerous.” Tomas had been missing for three years, presumed drowned in the winter storms, but here he was, marked alive, marked for work that could kill him. My breath hitched, and I forced it down. To show emotion was to give the occupiers leverage.

*“Your mother’s name is in here too,”* Rehn said, not looking at me. *“I saw the ledger taken from the company. Marta’s husband, half the diving fleet—gone. But your mother, Casper. She’s listed.”*

I straightened. *“She’s ill. She won’t last the winter regardless.”*

*“Perhaps. Perhaps not.”* He leaned forward, the buttons of his greatcoat gleaming. *“The army needs divers for the mine clearance detail. Your mother’s name makes her eligible for the evacuation berth when the armistice delegates arrive. But eligibility is not the same as selection. I can influence the selection. I can ensure she— or you— gets the spot. All I ask is that you keep reading. Keep decoding. Tell me what the margins say about the other families. Who is marked for conscription? Who can be swapped out?”*

I said nothing. The tea grew cold in my cup. The ledger was a map of every debt, every family tie, every life the trading company had owned and the army now owned with it. To decode it was to hold the town’s skeleton in my hands. And Rehn, with his courteous manner and his watchful eyes, was offering me the power to choose who lived and who died, in exchange for the one thing I had always guarded most carefully: my silence.
