## 4. Margins

There is a particular hour in an occupied town — I'd learned it by the third week — when the sentries stop believing in their own vigilance. Not asleep. Nothing so useful as asleep. But the body's arithmetic catches up with the mind's, and a man who has been counting the same empty quay for four hours begins to accept that the harbor will still be there if he blinks. I built my nights out of those blinks.

The requisitioned offices of the Baltic Trading Company sat above the chandlery on Salt Row, and the army had done what armies always do to a building they've seized: they'd made it uglier and called it security. New locks. New shutters. A sentry at the stair with a rifle and the particular boredom of a man guarding paper. They thought the ledger was the treasure. In a way they were right, which is the only compliment I will ever pay the quartermaster's judgment.

I had a key. Not stolen — I want that understood, though I suppose theft is a matter of which side of the counter you stand on. Old Verner, the company clerk, had kept a spare in the hollowed spine of a hymnal in the chandlery's back room, and he'd shown me the trick of it years ago, when he was teaching me the margins. "A ledger that can't be read twice," he'd said, "is a ledger that lies by omission." Verner fled the town the day the officers came, with his coat pockets full of someone else's money and his conscience, I assume, folded flat somewhere beneath. He left the hymnal. Perhaps he thought God would keep it safe. God has other appointments.

So: three nights a week, when the sentry's blink came due, I let myself into the dark of the requisitioned office with a stub of candle, a blank copybook bought from the stationer with pearl money, and my father's old diver's patience — the patience of a woman accustomed to holding her breath while the world tried to drown her.

Copying a ledger is not dramatic work. I want to be honest about that, because the stories people tell afterward make everything sound like thunder and running footsteps, and the truth was candle grease on my knuckles and the small, vicious cramp of writing in a hand not your own, small enough to hide. The ledger was a fat, sour-smelling thing, bound in green leather gone black at the corners, and it held the town the way a net holds fish — every diver family of Vilna, every name, every debt, every child entered at birth as so much future labor. My own name was in it. Casper Kavanagh, entered at six years old, the year my father's debt passed to me like a coat too large and too heavy, the year I stopped being a daughter and became a line item.

I copied the names first. Names are the town's skeleton; everything else hangs off them. Then the debts, because debts are the muscle, the thing that moves us. Then — and this took the longest, because it took courage I didn't know I'd been saving up — the margins.

You have to understand what the margins are. To the army, the ledger was a tidy column of obligations. To the divers, the ledger was a conversation conducted in the white space beside the entries, in a code old Verner had inherited from his predecessor and taught to exactly one living person: me. A hook drawn under a name meant the debt was fictitious, a kindness the company extended to a widow. A double stroke meant a man had been written off — dead, fled, or simply no longer worth chasing. A small circle meant a child. A circle with a line through it meant the child had been entered against a debt that didn't exist, which was the company's way of laundering charity, and Verner's way of keeping his soul from dying entirely.

And in the back pages, in a section the quartermaster had not yet thought to ask me about — conscription rolls. Men taken for labor. The army's own ink, but annotated, in the company's old habit, in margin code, because the clerk who'd maintained the rolls before the occupation had been a Verner man to his boots.

I was three nights into the conscription pages when I found him.

Tomas Kavanagh.

The candle guttered and I let it, because my hands had stopped being my hands. There it was, in the army's blunt print — *T. Kavanagh, laborer, 2nd works battalion* — and beside it, in the margin, the old code, a single stroke and a small notation I knew the way you know a scar: *transferred*. Transferred out of the northern sector. The northern sector. The front outside of Druskin where the shells had fallen all that winter, where the casualty lists had come south like weather, where I had stood in the square with every other diver's wife and daughter and listened to names read aloud and not heard his, and had concluded — because grief is lazy and hope is expensive — that no news was the worst news, and made my peace with it the way you make peace with a drowned man: you stop diving for the body.

He had been transferred. Eleven months ago. To a works battalion in the interior, hauling timber or gravel or whatever the army needed hauled, alive, breathing, writing his name into ration books somewhere while I lit a candle for him at Saint Anne's and told my mother — God forgive me, God forgive me — that it was better not to hope.

I sat in that dark office for a long time. Long enough that the candle drowned in its own wax and I had to light another from the stub, and the flame hissed, and somewhere below me the sentry shifted his weight and sighed at the harbor.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about good news in a bad time: it arrives with a bill attached.

Because my mother was dying. This was the silence I kept even from myself, most days — the doctor had said *months* in the tone men use for weather they can't change, and my mother had said *nonsense* in the tone she used for everything, and between the two of us we had constructed a house of polite lies with excellent ventilation. She was lucid in the mornings and elsewhere by evening, and in the elsewhere she called for my father, dead eleven years, and for Tomas, and I would sit by the bed and hold her hand and say *he's gone, Mam, he's gone* — and she would look at me with the terrible clarity of the dying and say, *You don't know that. Nobody told you that. You decided it, because deciding is easier than waiting.*

She had known me too long and too well.

And now I had the answer she'd been asking for, and the question underneath it, the one that made my stomach turn over: what would the truth do to her? A dying woman with a living son — was that a mercy or a sentence? Because she would not simply be glad. She would begin to wait for him. She would spend her last months doing what she'd spent her last year refusing to do: hoping. And hope, in an occupied town, is a debt like any other, and the collectors come.

Or — the other arithmetic, the one that kept me awake in my narrow bed after I'd locked the office and walked home past the sentry's blink — what if she died not knowing, and I had chosen her peace over her joy? What kind of daughter decides which a mother gets to feel?

I am the town's ledger-reader. People assume this means I'm good with numbers. What it actually means is that I'm good at seeing what a thing costs, and I could not price this. I turned it over for four days like a stone in the shallows, looking at what lived underneath.

* * *

On the fourth day Marta Ellsen caught me at the water cistern and did her trick of standing too close and speaking too quietly, the way the diving women had all learned since the occupation — a whole grammar of conspiracy conducted at a volume that couldn't reach a sentry's ears.

"You were at the offices again last night," she said. Not a question. The cistern chain clanked; she timed her next words to it. "Pieter's name. He's marked in the conscript rolls. A circle with a cross — you know what the army's cross means, Casper. It means they'll take him for the eastern works, and no one comes back from the eastern works with their lungs whole."

"I know what it means."

"Then you know what we're asking."

I did. They'd been asking for a week, in this same soft grammar — alter the ledger, move the name, do what you do with ink. And I'd been giving the same answer, which was no answer, which was *I need time*, which in an occupied town is a currency that devalues by the hour.

"Marta." I kept my eyes on the water. "The rolls are copied. The army holds duplicates now. If I strike a name and the copy disagrees, they don't punish the ledger. They punish the reader. And then they punish the family of the reader, as a lesson in arithmetic."

"Then don't get caught."

"That's the whole of your plan?"

"It's the whole of anyone's plan," she said, and there was no anger in it, only the flat exhaustion of a woman who had buried a father-in-law to the mines and could not afford philosophy. "You're the only one of us who can read the thing, Casper. Do you know what that makes you, out here? It makes you the ledger. Every name we have is written in you now." She filled her pail. "Pieter's wife is expecting in the spring."

She left me with the chain clanking and the water settling, and I stood there understanding that she was right, and hating her a little for it, and hating myself more for the hating.

* * *

That evening I went to my mother's room with the candle and the answer I'd been carrying for four days like a hot coal passed hand to hand.

She was in the lucid part of the day still — propped against her pillows, doing that thing she did where she watched me cross a room and made it feel like an examination. Her hands were bird-light on the blanket. Once they'd been strong enough to haul a breathing line; now they trembled at the weight of the quilt, and she pretended they didn't, and I pretended to believe her, and that was the marriage of truths we lived in.

"You've been out at night," she said. Not a question. She had known me too long and too well.

"Reading."

"You were always reading." A pause, and then, with the terrible gentleness the dying keep in reserve: "Casper. If you found something in those pages — something about Tomas — you'd tell me. Wouldn't you?"

And there it was. The question I'd been diving for, four days down in the dark, and I'd come up empty-handed, or rather full-handed and unable to open my fist.

I looked at her — at the sharpness still living in her eyes like a lamp in a sinking house — and I thought: *she would rather hope and break than rest not knowing. She told me so herself, a hundred times, in a hundred ways. She is not a woman you protect from the truth. She is a woman you owe it.*

"I'd tell you," I said.

"Good," she said, and closed her eyes, satisfied, and I stood there in the candlelight with the coal still burning in my fist, having told her a truth about a truth I hadn't yet told.

Tomorrow. Tomorrow I would go back to the offices, copy the conscript page entire, the transfer, the battalion, everything — and bring it to her, and let the hope in, and pray to a God I'd stopped invoicing years ago that it would arrive in time to be a gift and not a wound.

I snuffed the candle. Outside, the harbor lay black and patient, and somewhere east of it, eleven months deep into a war I'd written my brother out of, a man named Tomas Kavanagh was hauling timber and did not know that his name had just changed everything.

I only had to survive telling her. I had survived worse.

I told myself that, anyway. It was, like so much in Vilna that winter, a matter of which records you kept.
