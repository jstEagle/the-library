## 5. What the Divers Know

The divers of Vilna had never needed words underwater, and it turned out we didn't need many above it either.

It began the way all our commerce began: with a knock at the wrong hour. Not dawn, when the boats went out, and not dusk, when the ledgers closed, but the gray middle of a Tuesday evening when respectable people were pretending to eat dinner. I opened my door to find old Henrik Voss standing on my step with his cap in his hands and his eyes doing that thing diver's eyes do — fixed slightly past you, on some depth only they can see.

"Casper," he said. "You're awake."

"I'm always awake. It's a professional condition."

He held out his hand. In his palm lay three pearls, small and gray as hailstones, wrapped in a scrap of oilcloth. Harbor pearls, second-grade, the kind that wouldn't buy a coffin but might rent one for a night.

"My son's name," he said. "Pieter. He's in the third column."

The third column. Conscription. Every family in the diving quarter knew the columns now, though none of them could read them. The army had taken our book, but the army couldn't read our book either — not truly, not the margins where old Clerk Ambrose had written everything that mattered in a cipher of tides and knots that only a child raised under the ledger lamp would ever learn. So they kept me. And because they kept me, the town kept me too, the way you keep a knife you don't entirely trust.

"Henrik." I didn't take the pearls. Not yet. Taking them was the transaction; refusing them was just cruelty with better manners. "If I move Pieter's name, another name moves down. You understand that. There is no adding, there is only trading."

"I understand." His jaw worked. "The Lindqvist boy. He's sixteen. He'd be going instead."

"The Lindqvist boy or someone like him. Someone's son, always."

"Yes." He said it the way divers say *the water's high today* — acknowledging a fact that has already killed people and will kill more. "But Pieter is mine."

I took the pearls. God forgive me, I took them, and I told him to come back Thursday, and I watched him walk away with his back straighter than it had been since the requisition, carrying hope like a man carries a load he knows is too heavy but refuses to set down in front of the neighbors.

That was the first week. By the second week, they were queuing.

* * *

I want to be honest about what it felt like, because honesty is the one luxury this occupation hasn't taxed yet.

It felt like being the harbor itself. Everything came to me — grief, pearls, desperation, flattery — and everything pooled in me and grew cold and was expected to hold its shape no matter what was dropped in. The baker's widow brought me honey cake and asked if the ledger could lose her brother's debt notation, which wasn't even a conscription matter, just money, just the company's arithmetic. The Salo sisters brought me their father's diving knife, the good one with the bone handle, and asked nothing aloud, which somehow made it worse. Old Mrs. Kettunen simply sat in my kitchen for an hour saying nothing at all, then left, and two days later I found her grandson's entry marked in my memory as *movable* and understood that she had paid me in silence, which was the only currency she had left.

And every request was the same arithmetic. Move one name, sink another. Bribe one guard and the next guard learns the price. Save the Voss boy and spend the Lindqvist boy. There was no version of the ledger where everyone walked out clean, because the ledger had been written by people who believed some names were ink and others were water, and I had inherited their pen.

"You could refuse," Marta said to me once, in the low voice she used for things that were true. "You could tell them all no. Tell them the book is the army's now, let it lie where it fell."

"And then they'd find another reader. Or worse — they'd stop needing one, and start guessing. Rehn guesses well, Marta. Guessing is his hobby."

"Rehn." She said the name the way you spit a fishbone into your palm — carefully, so no one sees. "You sit in his office. You drink his coffee. He calls you *Fräulein Kavanagh* like you're a clerk and not a diver's daughter."

"I am a clerk. That's the whole trick of it."

"It's not a trick if it works on you too."

I didn't have an answer for that, so I gave her tea instead, and she drank it, and we both pretended the silence between us was comfortable rather than load-bearing.

* * *

Here is what the town saw when it looked at me, and I know because Marta told me later, and Marta never lied about anything except her own kindness:

They saw a girl who walked into the requisitioned offices each morning in her one good coat and came out each evening with her pockets full of other people's fates. Some of them saw a saint. Some of them saw a collaborator with a soft voice. Most of them, being divers, saw both at once, because we are a people trained from childhood to look at a thing and see its underside without surfacing.

Marta herself — Jüri Salo's widow's daughter, married to Anders, who dove the north beds until his ears gave out — watched me longer than most. She had known me since I was small enough to fit inside a cargo net. She remembered my mother before the cough, before the company doctor started charging by the visit. She remembered Tomas, which meant she was one of perhaps four people in Vilna who still said his name without lowering her voice.

So when she came to my door on the last Thursday of the month, I knew before she spoke that this visit would cost more than pearls.

* * *

Marta's account of that evening, which she set down years afterward in a hand far steadier than her heart deserved, runs thus:

*I went to Casper's house because my sister's boy was on the list. Name of Anselm. Seventeen, strong as a mooring post, dumb as a bucket — the army loves them like that. My sister had scraped together eleven pearls and a silver spoon off the English shipwreck, and she stood in my kitchen holding them like they were her own kidneys, asking me to carry them to Casper because she couldn't bear to ask herself.*

*And I went. But on the way I made myself look at it plainly, the way Casper must look at it every day. Eleven pearls buys one name moved. One name moved means one name sinks. Whose? Some mother I've never met, drying nets two streets over, who also has a sister with a kitchen full of scraped-together treasure. We are all of us bidding against each other for the same narrow mercy, and the girl in the middle takes every coin and keeps every wound.*

*When I got there she was tired. I could see it in how she held her shoulders, up around her ears like a woman walking into cold water. I put the pearls on her table and said, "My sister sends these for Anselm."*

*She looked at the pearls a long time. Then she looked at me, and her face did something I'd never seen it do — it cracked, just at one corner, like ice in spring.*

*"Marta," she said, "do you know what I've become?"*

*"A reader," I said.*

*"A market," she said. "I'm a market. They bring me their sons and I weigh them."*

*"Then weigh Anselm lightly," I said, because I am a selfish woman and I loved my sister more than my principles, and I have never pretended otherwise.*

*She weighed him. Of course she weighed him. That's the thing about Casper nobody understood then — she always paid. Every favor granted, she paid for somewhere else, out of some account of hers we never saw. I used to think it was conscience. Now I think it was arithmetic too. She was keeping books even then, just not the ones the army thought it owned.*

*She took the pearls. She said Thursday. She always said Thursday, as if the day itself were a sacrament.*

*Walking home I passed the guard post by the customs house, and one of the soldiers — young, chapped lips, boots too big — nodded at me almost politely, and I thought: he doesn't know. None of them know what's moving through this town at night, pearl by pearl, name by name. We've built a whole second country inside theirs, silent as the bottom of the harbor, and its only citizen who can read is twenty-three years old and running out of self to spend.*

*That's what I thought. I was wrong about one thing. She wasn't running out. She was saving something back. Divers do that — you never empty your lungs on the way down, because you'll need the air for the way up. I should have known. I'd watched her dive since she was nine.*

*But that night I only saw a tired girl taking bribes in a bad coat, and I loved her and pitied her in the same breath, and went home to tell my sister it would be done.*

* * *

Thursday came, and with it Henrik Voss, and with it the Lindqvist boy's mother, who came to my door two days after the change was made, having heard — the way this town hears everything — that her son's column had shifted. She didn't accuse me. That was the terrible part. She stood on my step with her hands folded and said, "You did what you could," in a voice that forgave me so thoroughly I wanted to drown.

I said thank you. What else does a market say?

That night I sat with the copied pages — my own shadow-ledger, hidden beneath a floorboard my mother's bed leg had worn smooth — and I added the week's entries in Ambrose's cipher. Voss, Pieter: moved. Lindqvist, Eerik: sunk. Salo, Anselm: pending. And beside each name, in the margin where Ambrose had once recorded tides and debts and the small true history of the harbor, I wrote what the official ledger would never hold: *paid in pearls; paid in a spoon; paid in a mother's forgiveness, which has no exchange rate.*

Because someone had to keep the true record. If not me, then the water, and the water keeps nothing but bodies.

I was closing the floorboard when I heard it — not a knock this time, but voices, out past the breakwater, where no one walks at night. Two men, low and urgent, near the warehouse steps. I put out my lamp and listened, and caught only fragments on the wind: *delegates*, and *before the port closes*, and once, unmistakably, the word *armistice*.

I knelt there in the dark with my heart going like a diver's pulse at thirty fathoms, and understood that everything was about to change — that soon there would be no columns to trade, only a single door out of Vilna, and every name in the ledger would be bidding for it.

Including, I would learn within the week, both of mine.
