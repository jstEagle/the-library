## 6. Armistice Weather

The harbor didn't freeze that winter, not properly. It turned instead into a slurry of grease ice and salt, the kind that clings to rope and skin alike, and the divers still went down because debt doesn't hibernate. I went down too, though my lungs had long since memorized the weight of water and the taste of copper on the tongue. By the time I surfaced, my basket held three pearls — two milky, one the color of a bruised plum — and my fingers were so stiff I had to pry them open on the gunwale with my teeth.

Marta waited on the dock, her shawl pulled tight against a wind that smelled of coal smoke and coming snow. She didn't ask how deep, or how long. She never did. The divers' wives learned early that questions only invited answers they couldn't bear.

"Three," I said, holding them out on my palm. "The plum one might fetch a winter coat for the boys."

Marta's eyes flicked over them, quick as a gull snatching a scrap. "Rehn's clerk offered forty rubles for a matched pair last week. Said the quartermaster's wife has a birthday." She didn't reach for them. "But the armistice talk — "

"Armistice talk." I let the pearls drop back into my pouch, the leather warm against my frozen fingers. "I heard the gulls singing it too. They're very well informed, gulls."

Her mouth twisted, something between a smile and a grimace. "The garrison's been different this past week. Fewer patrols. The officers drink their coffee standing up, looking at the horizon like it owes them money. Even the sentries at the warehouse gate — Hans the younger gave me an extra potato for Marta's stew yesterday. *Hans*."

"Hans who once made you kneel in the mud for a turnip?"

"Hans who has a sister in Riga and hasn't had a letter in fourteen months." She shrugged, the gesture heavy with layers of wool. "Men go soft when they think the killing might stop. Or they go hard. One or the other. There's no middle ground left."

I nodded, though my thoughts had already drifted to the ledger tucked beneath the loose floorboard in my room, the copied pages stacked beside it like a second, secret life. The names I'd memorized. The columns I'd learned to read sideways, upside down, backwards — whatever angle kept them hidden from Rehn's gaze.

"Did you hear?" Marta pitched her voice low, conspiratorial. "The delegates are coming. Real ones. Not the couriers who ride through with sealed pouches and dust on their boots. *Delegates*. Plural. With papers. The harbor master's nephew saw their launch at the outer marker yesterday — white flag, no guns."

"White flags can be painted on anything." I adjusted my belt, the leather cracking. "Even a rowboat. Even a shroud."

"Casper." She used my name the way divers use a lead line — to measure depth. "Your brother. Your mother. The ledger — "

"Doesn't change what the ledger says." I turned toward the path up from the dock, my boots finding the worn grooves in the planks without looking. "Only what people *do* with what it says."

* * *

Rehn's office smelled of pine oil and tobacco, the same as always, but today the air carried something else — a tension that made the quartermaster's usual courtesy feel like a blade wrapped in velvet. He stood at the window, his silhouette sharp against the gray light, one hand braced on the frame. The ledger lay open on his desk, its pages fanned like a patient's ribs.

"You're late," he said without turning. "The tide doesn't wait for armistices, Fraulein Kavanagh."

"Neither do the pearls, Herr Quartermaster." I closed the door behind me, the latch catching with a sound like a small bone breaking. "Three today. One plum. Forty rubles if you're buying."

He turned then, and his face was the careful mask I'd learned to read: polite interest, calculation beneath, and something new. A flicker at the corner of his jaw. Fatigue, perhaps. Or fear wearing a soldier's face.

"Sit." He gestured to the chair opposite the desk. "We have business beyond pearls."

I sat. The chair was cold, the wood hard against bones that ached from the dive. On the desk, the ledger's pages caught the light — columns of names, debts, family ties, all written in the company clerk's precise hand. But the margins. The margins held the code only divers knew: symbols for *sick*, for *pregnant*, for *run*, for *dead*. For *brother*, *sister*, *mother*, *child*. A whole language of survival written in the space between the lines.

"The delegates arrive in three days," Rehn said, his voice stripped of its usual warmth. "Two from the neutral commission, one from the provisional government. They'll review the harbor's status, the indenture contracts, the — " He paused, his eyes finding mine. "The evacuation provisions."

"Evacuation." The word tasted of salt and iron. "Provisions. Plural?"

"One berth." He said it flatly. "One launch. Capacity: twelve souls. Priority by ledger eligibility — dependents of indentured divers, conscripts with documented family ties, medical cases certified by the garrison surgeon. The delegates will ratify the list. The launch departs before the port closes permanently."

Twelve souls. One launch. The arithmetic settled in my chest like a stone.

"Eligibility," I repeated. "By the ledger."

"By the ledger." He leaned forward, palms flat on the desktop. "Which is why I need you to verify the marginal notations. The delegates will want to see the original. They'll want to know the code. And they'll want to know that the entries are... accurate."

A test. A trap. Or both.

"Accurate," I said. "The ledger is always accurate. That's the point of a ledger."

"Is it." His gaze held mine. "The old clerk — your teacher — he understood that accuracy and truth are not always the same thing. He taught you that, didn't he? In the margins."

The old clerk. Herr Voss. He'd died two winters back, his lungs finally giving out after forty years of breathing harbor dust. He'd taught me to read the margins, yes. And to write in them. And to understand that a name moved from one column to another could mean the difference between a winter coat and a shroud.

"He taught me to read," I said carefully. "What I write is my own business."

A muscle jumped in Rehn's jaw. "Fraulein, I am not a fool. I know you've been copying the ledger. I know you've been... supplementing the record. The question is whether your supplements serve the town, or yourself."

"Both. Neither. The town *is* myself." I stood, the chair scraping loud in the quiet room. "If the delegates want the ledger verified, they'll have it. But they'll get what the ledger says, not what you *wish* it said."

"Even if what it says condemns people you love?"

The question hung between us, barbed and deliberate. I felt the heat rise in my face, the old diver's reflex: hold the breath, don't surface too fast, don't let them see the bubbles.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Don't you." He rose, circling the desk to stand close enough that I smelled the tobacco and the pine oil and something sharper beneath — sweat, perhaps. Fear. "Your mother, Casper. Maria Kavanagh. Listed as *dependent — terminal*. Your brother, Tomas. Listed as *conscript — missing, presumed dead* until last month, when a hospital transfer from the eastern front put him in the *alive — recovering* column. Both eligible. Both with claims."

My breath caught. The arithmetic turned cruel.

"Only one berth for dependents," I said, my voice thin. "The rules — "

"The rules say *one* dependent per indentured household. Your mother qualifies as your dependent. Tomas qualifies as a conscript with family ties — *your* family ties. But the launch takes twelve *total*. Not twelve per category. Twelve *names*. And there are forty-seven names on the eligibility list as of this morning."

Forty-seven. For twelve seats. The odds were worse than a dive in a storm.

"Can the list be... adjusted?" The words tasted of ash.

Rehn's smile was thin, humorless. "The delegates will review the list. They'll expect it to reflect the *current* ledger. Which you, uniquely, can read. And which you, uniquely, have... access to."

He was offering me the pen. Or the knife. I couldn't tell which.

"I'll verify the ledger," I said. "For the delegates. As you asked."

"And if the verification reveals... discrepancies?"

"Then the ledger is wrong. Ledgers are sometimes wrong. That's why we verify them."

He studied me for a long moment, his eyes the color of the harbor at dusk — deep, opaque, hiding currents. "You're a dangerous woman, Casper Kavanagh. The old clerk chose his apprentice well."

"He chose a diver's daughter. We're all dangerous. We survive the bottom."

"Do we." He returned to his seat, picking up a pen. "The delegates arrive in three days. I'll expect your verification by noon tomorrow. The *complete* verification. Margins included."

* * *

The walk back to my room was a blur of gray streets and colder wind. The town moved around me — women queuing for bread, children kicking a rag ball through slush, a squad of soldiers laughing as they shared a cigarette — but I saw none of it. My mind ran numbers, combinations, permutations of a cruel equation.

Twelve seats. Forty-seven names.

My mother: *dependent — terminal*. Eligible. She'd survive the journey? The launch was heated, Rehn had said. Supplied. A week at sea to a neutral port. But the cold in her bones, the cough that shook the rafters at night — the journey might kill her as surely as staying.

Tomas: *conscript — alive — recovering*. Eligible. He'd survive. He was Kavanagh stock, broad-shouldered and stubborn, the boy who'd once swum the harbor mouth on a dare and come back laughing. But he was also a soldier now, or had been. The hospital transfer meant wounds. The *recovering* column meant he lived. But for what? To return to a town that would be empty, a harbor that would be closed, a sister who had bargained his name like a pearl?

And me. *Diver — indentured — literate*. Not eligible. Not a dependent. Not a conscript. Not terminal. Just the one who held the pen.

I climbed the stairs to my room, my legs burning. The floorboard was where I'd left it, the copied pages stacked neat beneath. I knelt, pulled them out, and spread them on the table in the dying light.

Page after page of names. The diver families I'd known since birth: the Nilsens, the Petrovs, the Kowalskis. Children I'd watched grow, old men I'd seen carried up from the depths. Each name a weight. Each margin notation a story I carried.

*Maria Kavanagh — dependent — terminal — cough — 3 months per surgeon*

*Tomas Kavanagh — conscript — alive — recovering — shrapnel left leg — hospital Riga*

My hand hovered over the page, the charcoal stick trembling between frozen fingers. One stroke. One smudge. *Terminal* to *stable*. *Recovering* to *fit for duty*. *Dependent* to *deceased*. *Alive* to *missing*.

The old clerk's voice echoed in memory: *The ledger is the law, child. But the margins are mercy. Never confuse them.*

Mercy. For whom?

A knock at the door. Three sharp raps. Marta's rhythm.

I shoved the pages back under the floorboard, slammed it shut, and opened the door.

Marta stood there, her face pale in the hallway's dimness. Behind her, two other women — Elsa Nilsen, whose husband had drowned last autumn, and Katya Petrov, pregnant with her fourth. They carried the silence of women who'd learned to speak without words.

"Casper." Marta's voice was low. "We heard. The berth. Twelve names."

"I heard."

"The list. Rehn posted it at the warehouse gate. Hans the younger let us see." She thrust a folded paper at me. "Forty-seven names. Twelve seats. The delegates choose. But the ledger *decides*."

I took the paper, unfolded it with fingers that didn't shake. Forty-seven names in the clerk's neat script. Two Kavanaghs. Maria. Tomas. No Casper.

"The divers," Elsa said, her accent thick, her eyes fierce. "We talk. We agree. The berth goes to the children first. Then the sick. Then the conscripts with families. That's the way. That's *our* way."

"Your way doesn't matter," Katya said, her hand on her belly. "The delegates decide. The ledger decides. And the ledger..." She looked at me. "You read the ledger. You *write* the ledger."

"I verify the ledger," I corrected. "For Rehn. For the delegates."

"For us," Marta said. "You verify it *for us*. You make the margins say what they need to say. My husband's name — he's on the list. *Convalescent — fit for labor*. But his lungs... he coughs blood. If the margin said *terminal*... "

"Then he takes a seat from someone else," I said. "A child. A mother. Another diver's husband."

"Then we decide who," Elsa said. "Together. The divers decide. We always have."

"Together." The word felt foreign in my mouth. "There is no together in a list of twelve. There's only arithmetic."

"We'll bring pearls," Katya said. "Gold. Whatever you need. Whatever Rehn needs. Just — " Her voice broke. "Just make the numbers work for us."

I looked at their faces. Marta, hard as flint. Elsa, desperate. Katya, terrified. The town's women, carrying the weight of the harbor on their shoulders while the men were down in the dark or away at war or lying in hospital beds.

"I can't make the numbers work for everyone," I said. "There are forty-seven names and twelve seats. That's not arithmetic. That's slaughter."

"We know," Marta said. "But you can make them work for *some*. And some is better than none. That's the diver's calculus. You know this."

I did. I knew it in my bones, in the salt crusted on my skin, in the ledger's margins I'd memorized and the copies I'd hidden. Some is better than none. But which some?

"Go home," I said. "All of you. I'll... I'll verify the ledger. By noon tomorrow. Rehn expects it."

"And the delegates?" Elsa asked. "When they come?"

"Then we'll see what the ledger says. And what the margins mean."

They left, three shadows merging with the twilight. I closed the door and leaned against it, sliding down until I sat on the cold floor.

* * *

The lamp burned low as I worked. The copied pages spread before me, the original ledger's pages memorized, the code fluent in my fingers. I wrote and rewrote, testing combinations, running the arithmetic until my eyes blurred and my head throbbed.

*Scenario A*: Mother terminal (eligible). Tomas recovering (eligible). Both on list. Two seats taken. Ten remain for forty-five others.

*Scenario B*: Mother deceased (ineligible). Tomas recovering (eligible). One seat. Eleven remain.

*Scenario C*: Mother terminal (eligible). Tomas fit for duty (ineligible — conscripts fit for duty return to unit, not evacuation). One seat. Eleven remain.

*Scenario D*: Mother stable (ineligible — not terminal). Tomas recovering (eligible). One seat. Eleven remain.

*Scenario E*: Mother deceased. Tomas deceased. Zero seats. Twelve remain for forty-five.

Each scenario a betrayal. Each a sentence.

The cruelest arithmetic: the rules didn't allow for *both*. The ledger's structure — one dependent per household, conscripts only if recovering — created a mutual exclusivity I couldn't finesse without breaking the code entirely. And if I broke the code, Rehn would know. The delegates would know. The whole edifice of the ledger's authority would collapse, and with it, any chance for *any* of the forty-seven.

Unless.

Unless I created a new name. A name not on the list. A name that *should* be there.

The drowned stranger's child. The orphan from the *Vera* wreck, three winters back. The diver who'd gone down in a storm and never surfaced, leaving a six-year-old daughter with no papers, no indenture, no name in the ledger because her father had died before registering her. The child had lived with Marta's sister since, unnamed, unrecorded, a ghost in the system.

*Elena. No surname. Age six. Orphan. Dependent of none. Eligible by humanitarian clause — if the clause exists.*

Did it? I'd read the evacuation provisions once, in the clerk's office, when Rehn wasn't looking. *Humanitarian considerations may be applied at the discretion of the reviewing commission.* Vague. Dangerous. A loophole wide enough to drive a launch through — or to hang a forger.

But if I added her... twelve seats. Forty-seven names plus one. Forty-eight for twelve. The odds worsened for everyone else. But for Elena — a child, alone, no diver blood, no indenture, no advocate — the odds were already zero.

Unless I gave her a name in the margin.

My hand moved before thought could stop it. Charcoal on paper. A new line at the bottom of the eligibility list, squeezed into the white space below the last entry:

*Elena — orphan — dependent — humanitarian — eligible*

I stared at it. The handwriting wasn't mine — I'd mimicked the clerk's precise slant, the way he dotted his i's, the flourish on his capital E. A forgery. A gift. A theft from the forty-seven.

The lamp sputtered. Shadows lengthened.

Outside, the wind rose, rattling the window. Somewhere in the harbor, a bell buoy clanged — once, twice, three times. The sound of water moving, relentless, indifferent.

Three days. The delegates would come. The ledger would be reviewed. Twelve names would be chosen. The launch would leave.

And I would stay. With the true record. With the margins that held the real names, the real debts, the real loves and losses of a town that had taught me to read the water and write the truth in the spaces between the lines.

I blew out the lamp.

In the dark, the arithmetic continued, cold and precise as a diver's depth gauge. But beneath the numbers, something else stirred — a resolve hardening like ice on a winter harbor. The forger's hand. The diver's breath. The daughter's choice.

Tomorrow I would give Rehn the verified ledger. The delegates would get their twelve names.

But the true record — the one that mattered — would stay with me. Written in margins no quartermaster could read. Carried in a memory no armistice could erase.

* * *

The knock came at midnight.

Not Marta's three raps. Not a soldier's heavy fist. Three short, two long, one short — the diver's signal for *surface, danger, wait*.

I was on my feet before the sound faded, the copied pages already burning in the stove, the ash scattering up the chimney like dark snow. The floorboard sealed. The lamp cold.

I opened the door.
