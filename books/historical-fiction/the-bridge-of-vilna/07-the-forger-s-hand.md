## 7. The Forger’s Hand

I have always liked the way the harbor smells at night — brine, tar, and the faint, metallic tang of blood that never quite washes away. The moon hangs low, a thin silver blade that slices the water into a thousand shards, and the only sound is the soft lapping of waves against the wooden pilings. I pull the ledger from its battered tin case, the leather cracked from years of salt and sweat, and set it on the crate that serves as my night‑stand. The oil lamp flickers, throwing a trembling amber glow over the cramped space, and I can see the margin notes in their spidery script: tiny arrows, circles, and the occasional smudge that looks like a fingerprint but is really just a stray ink blot.

The old clerk — old in years, not in skill — taught me this trade when I was barely old enough to lift a pearl sack. “Casper,” he’d say, his voice a rasp that sounded like a shell being opened, “the margins are the language of the unspoken. They’re the difference between a name being erased and a name being remembered.” He would trace a line with his finger, point to a circled date, and then whisper, “If you want to keep a secret safe, you must write it where no one else thinks to look.” I learned to copy the main entries with a steady hand, but the margins — those were my secret garden. I could shift a number here, replace a surname there, and the whole ledger would bend to my will without anyone noticing, at least not until the ink dried.

Tonight, the ledger is a map of lives I have never lived but have always carried. My mother’s name, *Marta Kavanagh*, appears in the conscription column, a thin line drawn through it as if to mark her for death. My brother Tomas is listed alive, a conscript in a far‑off regiment, his fate still uncertain. The entries are cold, bureaucratic, but they pulse with the same heartbeat that keeps my own heart from stopping. I take a fresh quill, dip it into the ink, and begin to copy the entries exactly as they are, the way the clerk taught me, so that any later audit will see only the original. Then, in the margins, I add a small, almost invisible asterisk beside Tomas’s name, a marker only I understand — a promise that I will find him, that I will not let the army’s ink dictate his end.

I work in silence, the only rhythm the crackle of the lamp and the distant clank of a ship’s bell. My mind wanders to the mornings when my mother would sit by the fire, her hands trembling as she tried to write her own name in the ledger’s margins, trying to keep a record of the debts she owed to the sea and to the people she loved. She never spoke first; she let the water decide when to speak. I have learned to keep that silence, to let the water carry my words instead of forcing them. It is a quiet endurance, a cost I pay for never speaking first, and it feels like a weight pressing against my ribs, as heavy as the pearls I once traded for a debt I never chose.

***  

The night is deeper now, and the harbor’s shadows have grown long. I hear a faint footstep on the wooden planks outside my makeshift office, a soft scrape that makes the hair on my arms stand up. I glance up, and there, half hidden in the gloom, is Quartermaster Aldous Rehn. He moves with the measured, predatory grace of a man who knows every stone in the harbor’s cobblestones, every creak of the dock, every whisper of the wind. His eyes, sharp as a hawk’s, scan the area with a practiced detachment, then settle on me.

I do not look away. I have learned to read people the way I read margins — by the subtle shifts in posture, the slight tightening of a jaw, the way a hand lingers on a weapon’s hilt. Rehn’s gaze lingers on the ledger, then flicks to the lamp, then back to me. He does not speak; he simply stands there, his coat buttoned up to the throat, the rain having soaked the hem. I can feel the weight of his suspicion like a tide pulling at the shore.

*He thinks I’m just copying the ledger for the quartermaster’s records,* he muses internally, *but he doesn’t see the margins. He doesn’t see the way I can rewrite a name and make it disappear.* He knows the ledger is more than a list; it is the town’s memory, its lifeblood. He also knows that if I am caught tampering, the price will be more than a slap on the wrist — it will be a bullet, or a forced labor detail that will take me far from the harbor and the people I love.

Rehn’s mind runs through possible scenarios. If I am merely a diligent clerk, he can dismiss the oddities as clerical error. If I am a conspirator, he can use this as leverage, perhaps to turn me into an informant, or to have me executed as a traitor. He considers the political climate: the armistice talks are stalled, the port is on the brink of closure, and a single evacuation berth could tip the scales. He knows that the ledger holds the key to who gets on that boat. He also knows that the Kavanagh family is a liability — his own quartermaster’s reports label them as “high risk,” and any deviation from the official line could jeopardize his standing with the occupying command.

He steps closer, his boots making a soft thud on the wet wood. “Casper,” he says, his voice low but carrying the authority of a man who has seen too many men die for a ledger. “You’re working late. The night is cold; you should be resting.”

I force a smile, the kind that does not reach my eyes. “Just making sure the numbers line up, sir. The ledger’s a finicky beast.”

He narrows his eyes, the faintest crease forming at the corner of his mouth. “Be careful what you fiddle with. The ledger is not a toy.”

I nod, feeling the iron taste of dread settle in my throat. “Of course, sir. I’ll be quick.”

He watches me for a moment longer, then turns and disappears into the night, his silhouette swallowed by the darkness as if he were a shadow himself. I exhale, the breath fogging the lamp’s glass, and return to my work, the quill moving with a steadiness that belies the storm inside me.

***  

The next hour is a blur of ink and concentration. I copy the entries with meticulous care, each line a promise that the official record will stay untouched. Then, in the margins, I begin to rewrite. I shift Tomas’s conscription date, making it appear as though he has already been discharged for “medical reasons.” I replace his name with a placeholder — *T. K.* — and add a note that reads, “awaiting transport to the rear echelon.” It is a small alteration, but it changes everything. If the army sees him as already out of the front lines, they may not press him into the conscript column, and perhaps, just perhaps, he will be granted a pass to the evacuation berth.

My hand trembles as I write the final stroke. The ink dries quickly, and I set the quill down, feeling the weight of the decision settle like a stone in my gut. I have always been good at making choices for others; it is the choices for myself that have always been the hardest. I glance at the margin where I have scribbled a tiny heart beside my mother’s name, a silent prayer that she will survive long enough to see her son again.

The lamp sputters, and a sudden gust of wind rattles the shutters. I hear a faint creak from the doorway — someone is entering. My heart leaps. I glance toward the source and see a figure silhouetted against the dim light: Marta, a fellow diver’s wife, her face pale from the chill, her eyes wide with worry.

“Casper,” she whispers, her voice barely audible over the wind. “I’ve been looking for you. The men are talking — Rehn is… he’s asking about the ledger. He says there’s something odd about the entries. He thinks someone’s been… altering it.”

I force a laugh that sounds more like a cough. “Altering? No, Marta, I’m just making sure the numbers are correct. You know how the quartermaster is — always looking for any discrepancy.”

She steps closer, her breath forming little clouds in the cold air. “I heard you were copying the ledger all night. I’m worried about Tomas. He’s listed as alive, but the conscription notice… it’s like a death sentence. If he’s really being sent out, he might not come back.”

I look at her, seeing the fear etched into the lines of her face, the same fear that has haunted my own nights. I think of my mother, lying in the small room above the harbor, her breath shallow, her eyes flickering with a dying light. I think of the ledger, the only thing that ties us to the world outside these walls. I think of the choice I must make: keep the ledger as it is, and risk my brother’s death, or change it and risk the wrath of the quartermaster, perhaps endangering us all.

“I’ll check the entries,” I say, trying to keep my voice steady. “If there’s a problem, I’ll fix it.”

Marta’s eyes soften for a moment, then harden. “You’re a good woman, Casper. You’ve always kept the town’s secrets. Just… be careful. Rehn’s eyes are everywhere.”

She turns and disappears into the night, her footsteps echoing on the wooden planks. I watch her go, feeling the weight of her words settle like a stone in my chest. I return to the ledger, the ink still wet, the margins now holding a secret that could cost me everything.

***  

The next few minutes feel like an eternity. I hear the soft scrape of a boot on the dock, the faint rustle of a coat, and then a sudden voice that cuts through the night like a blade.

“Casper,” Rehn says, his tone colder than the sea wind. He steps into the lamplight, his face illuminated, eyes narrowed. “I think you’ve been busy.”

My stomach drops. I have been caught. I stare at the ledger, at the fresh ink, at the margins where I have rewritten fate. Rehn’s gaze lingers on the page, then flicks to my hands, which are still clasped around the quill.

“What are you doing?” he asks, his voice low, almost curious, but with an undercurrent of menace.

I swallow, feeling the dryness in my throat. “Just making sure the records are accurate, sir. The quartermaster’s reports rely on precise numbers.”

He steps closer, the smell of tobacco and sea salt mixing in the air. “You know the penalty for tampering with the ledger. It’s not just a reprimand. It’s a matter of discipline, perhaps even a charge of treason.”

I glance at the ledger again, at the altered entry for Tomas, at the small heart beside my mother’s name. My mind races, calculating the possible outcomes. If I admit to the forgery, I may be shot, or taken to a labor camp. If I deny it, Rehn may still suspect, and his suspicion could become a self‑fulfilling prophecy.

“Sir,” I say, forcing calm, “I was merely copying the entries for the night shift. The ledger is a heavy document; it needs careful transcription.”

He narrows his eyes, the corner of his mouth twitching. “You’re a clever girl, Casper. But cleverness can be dangerous.”

He reaches into his coat and pulls out a small, battered tin box. Inside, a bundle of papers — copies of the ledger, each page marked with red ink, each bearing the same marginal notes I have been adding. He holds it up, the red ink glaring like a warning.

“You see,” he says, “I’ve been watching. The margins you add are not just notes; they’re alterations. You think you can rewrite a name and make it disappear, but the ledger has a memory. It remembers every hand that touches it.”

My heart pounds. I feel a cold sweat break out across my palms. I know that if he discovers the forgery, he will have cause to arrest me, to confiscate the ledger, perhaps to use it as evidence against the entire Kavanagh family. I glance at the lamp, its flame wavering, and then at the small wooden box on the crate — my makeshift fire pit where I keep a few dry twigs and a piece of oilcloth. I have a plan, but it will require swift action.

“Rehn,” I say, my voice barely above a whisper, “if you’re so sure, perhaps you should see for yourself.”

He raises an eyebrow, intrigued despite himself. “Show me.”

I stand, my legs trembling but my resolve hardening. I walk over to the crate, pull the oilcloth from the box, and toss it onto the ground. I strike a match, the flame catching quickly, and the oilcloth bursts into a bright, hungry fire. The heat rises, and I lift the ledger, holding it above the flames.

“Take a look,” I say, “at the entries you think are altered. See for yourself.”

Rehn steps forward, his boots crunching on the wet wood, his eyes flicking between the fire and the ledger. He leans in, his face inches from the pages, and I see his expression shift from suspicion to something that resembles awe. The firelight dances across the ink, illuminating the marginal notes, the tiny arrows, the faint asterisk beside Tomas’s name.

He reads silently, his lips moving as he follows the lines. The fire crackles, and for a moment the world narrows to the glow of orange and the stark black of the ledger’s pages. Then, as if a switch has been flipped, his demeanor changes.

“Casper,” he says, his voice softer, almost regretful, “you’ve been a fool. But you’ve also been clever. I cannot — ”

He stops, his gaze dropping to the ledger, then to the fire, then back to me. The tension in his shoulders eases just a fraction, as if a weight has been lifted. He takes a step back, his hand hovering near his coat, perhaps reaching for a pistol, perhaps for his own sense of control.

“Enough,” I say, my voice steadier than I feel. “If you want to keep the ledger intact, you’ll let me finish what I’m doing. I’m not here to betray the town; I’m here to keep its memory alive.”

He looks at me, his eyes searching, and I see the conflict within him: the quartermaster’s duty to the occupying command, the personal curiosity of a man who has spent his life watching the tides of power shift. He knows that if he destroys the ledger now, he will lose a crucial piece of information that could be used to negotiate the evacuation berth. He also knows that if he lets me continue, he may be complicit in a deception that could cost lives.

“Very well,” he says finally, his tone resigned. “You may continue. But know this — if I find any more tampering, I will not hesitate.”

I nod, feeling the fire’s heat sear the back of my neck. I set the ledger down gently on the crate, the flames licking at its edges but not yet consuming it. I take a deep breath, the smell of smoke filling my lungs, and I turn back to the task at hand.

***  

Marta arrives again, this time with a basket of fresh fish, her face drawn but hopeful. She sees the fire, the ledger, and the tension in the air, and she steps forward, her hands trembling.

“Casper,” she whispers, “what are you doing?”

I meet her gaze, the wry humor that has always been my armor surfacing. “I’m making sure the town’s record stays true, Marta. If we’re going to survive, we need to know who we are, even if that means rewriting a name or two.”

She looks at the fire, at the ledger, and then at me, her eyes softening. “You’re risking everything. If Rehn finds out…”

“Then I’ll be ready,” I reply, a thin smile tugging at my lips. “I’ve learned to keep my head low and my heart loud. The ledger is a living thing; it remembers every whisper, every lie, every sacrifice. I’m just… making sure the right story is told.”

She nods, understanding the gravity of the moment, and steps back, allowing me to continue. I take the quill, dip it once more into the ink, and begin to write a new entry in the margins, a small, almost invisible line that will serve as a placeholder for the future. I write the name of a drowned stranger’s orphaned child — *Elias Varga* — as the one who will be granted the evacuation berth. It is a lie, but a necessary one, a way to protect my mother and brother while keeping the ledger’s integrity intact for the eyes of the quartermaster.

The fire crackles louder, the oilcloth now a small inferno that threatens to consume the ledger. I glance at Rehn, who watches with a mixture of fascination and apprehension. He does not intervene; he simply stands, his posture relaxed but his eyes sharp, as if measuring the cost of each stroke of my pen.

I finish the entry, the ink still wet, and set the quill down. The fire begins to lick the edges of the ledger, the flames dancing over the pages, turning the ink a deep, ember‑red. I watch as the paper curls, the words becoming indistinct, the names blurring into the smoke.

“Now,” I say, my voice low, “we burn the evidence.”

I take a small piece of the oilcloth, press it against the ledger, and watch as the flames engulf the pages. The heat is intense, the smell of burning paper sharp and acrid. The ledger’s margins, once filled with my secret notes, begin to blacken and curl, the ink turning to ash. I feel a pang of loss, but also a strange relief; the physical record is disappearing, but the memory I have etched into my mind remains.

When the fire dies down, the ledger is reduced to a charred husk, the pages brittle and blackened. I hold the remnants in my hands, feeling the heat still radiating from the remnants, and I know that the true record now lives only in my mind and in the whispers of the town.

Marta looks at me, eyes wide with a mixture of fear and admiration. “You did it,” she says, voice trembling. “You saved us… in a way.”

I smile, a wry, tired smile. “I saved the town’s memory, not its lives. That’s a different kind of salvation.”

Rehn steps forward, his expression unreadable. He looks at the ashes, then at me, and says, “You have my word, Casper. The ledger will be rebuilt. The town will have its record, albeit a different one. But you must promise me — no more forgeries. No more secrets.”

I meet his gaze, feeling the weight of his stare like a tide pulling at my resolve. “I promise,” I say, though the promise feels more like a pact with the sea itself — binding, inevitable, and fraught with danger.

He nods, and for a brief moment, the tension in the air eases, as if the harbor itself has taken a breath.

***  

The night grows colder, the wind howling through the rigging of the ships, and the harbor’s silence returns, broken only by the distant creak of mooring lines and the faint echo of a distant siren. I stand alone amidst the remnants of the fire, the charred ledger cradled in my arms, and I feel the hard‑won resolve settle deep within me. Guilt, once a sharp sting, has hardened into a steady, iron‑clad determination. I will keep the true record alive, even if it means carrying the weight of every name, every silence, every unspoken promise.

I glance toward the dock where a faint light flickers — an armed guard’s lantern, the silhouette of a man moving with purpose. I know that Rehn’s suspicion has not vanished; it has merely been tempered, like steel in a furnace. He will watch me, and I will watch him. The ledger may be burned, but the act of forging a new entry for Elias Varga remains fresh in my mind, a promise that the next chapter will demand even more of me.

As the first pale fingers of dawn begin to stretch across the sky, a low horn sounds from the harbor — a signal that the armistice delegates are arriving. The boat that will carry the evacuation berth is due to dock soon, and with it, the possibility of a Kavanagh aboard. I tighten my grip on the charred pages, feeling the weight of my mother’s frail hand still lingering in my memory, and I whisper to the wind:

*If the world wants to forget us, I will be the one who remembers.*

The hook that propels me forward is the sudden, sharp crack of a rifle barrel echoing from the far end of the dock — a shot that splits the morning air, followed by the hurried footsteps of a man in a dark coat rushing toward me. He raises a hand, and I see, for the first time, the glint of a badge on his chest — an insignia of the occupying command, the very symbol of the power that has held us captive.

I turn, my heart hammering, and see a figure step out of the shadows, his face half‑hidden by the brim of a hat. He holds a sealed envelope, the wax seal bearing the unmistakable crest of the quartermaster’s office. He looks at me with an expression that is equal parts curiosity and warning.

“Casper,” he says, his voice low and urgent, “the ledger is needed. The quartermaster wants to see the final entries before the boat leaves. He’s… waiting.”

My breath catches. The boat, the berth, the forged name — everything hangs on a fragile thread. I glance at the charred remains of the ledger in my hands, at the name *Elias Varga* I have just written, and I realize that the next step will be far more perilous than any night of secret copying.

I swallow, feeling the cold bite of the morning air, and step toward the approaching man, the weight of my decision already settling like a stone in my gut.

*What will he demand?* I wonder, as the chapter ends on the echo of his footsteps, the harbor’s mist rising, and the promise of a new, uncertain chapter looming on the horizon.
