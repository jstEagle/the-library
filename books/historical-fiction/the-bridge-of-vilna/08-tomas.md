## 8. Tomas

The conscript yard lay beyond the town’s western wall, a mud‑slick compound of canvas tents and iron‑shod boots where men from every trade were herded like cargo bound for the harbor works. I had learned to move through Vilna’s shadows, but this was a different kind of darkness, a place where the ledger’s code meant nothing and a name was just another number to be filed. I had come here under the pretense of delivering a pouch of salted herrings to the quartermaster’s mess, but the real cargo beneath my coat was a thin sheet of onion‑skin paper, the first copy I’d managed to rub from the original ledger using soot and linseed oil pressed between the pages of a prayer book.

Casper Kavanagh, they called me now, though the indenture had once stripped my name away, leaving only a number in the margin alongside my father’s, my mother’s, Tomas’s. I had not seen my brother in three years, not since the winter he vanished into the northern forests after a failed smuggling run, and the ledger had marked him dead in the conscription column. Yet there he stood, third row from the left, alive and blinking in the grey light, his hands raw from digging, his coat hanging off one shoulder.

I found a moment when the guard shift changed, a breath between orders, and slipped through the gap where the barbed wire sagged lowest. The cold bit through my boots, but I hardly felt it. My fingers traced the edge of the paper in my breast pocket, feeling the faint ridge of ink I had transferred, a ghost of the original that would have to suffice.

“Casper.”

The voice was thin, weather‑worn, but unmistakable. Tomas turned, and for a heartbeat the yard seemed to narrow until only we two existed beneath the drizzling grey. He looked thinner than the last time I’d allowed myself to imagine him, the lines around his mouth deeper, the circles under his eyes the colour of bruised sky.

“You’re not supposed to be here,” he said, his voice a rasp. He glanced over his shoulder, checking the nearest sentry. “The quartermaster’s men. They’d—”

“They’d what?” I interrupted, keeping my tone light, the wry edge I’d cultivated over years of silence. “Send me back to the ledger? To translate more codes? To tell them how many divers can be spared for the pearl beds while the harbor deepens?” I shrugged, the motion stiff from my own indentured shoulders. “I’ve been many things. A mule. A scribe. A daughter who never speaks first.”

Tomas stared at me, searching, and I saw the same calculation I knew so well etched into his face—the same math of survival that had driven us apart. “Mother. She’s—”

“Sick.” I finished for him. “I know. I’ve been reading the margins for years, Tomas. The ink fades, but the silences don’t.” I stepped closer, lowering my voice. “I copied what I could. The ledger’s still in the company offices, but the army has the margins now. They know who’s eligible. Who can be sent away.”

He exhaled, a sound that broke somewhere between a laugh and a sob. “And you’re here. Why?”

“Because someone has to.” I met his gaze. “Because if I don’t, no one does. The quartermaster’s clerk fled when the army came, but the ledger’s still here. Somewhere. And someone has to keep it alive.” I tapped the paper in my pocket. “That’s why I’m here. Not to escape. To remember.”

We stood in the mud between the tents, the rain beginning to spit from a low, sullen sky. The yard hummed with the sounds of men shuffling, of shovels striking dirt, of a distant bugle calling for roll call. Somewhere beyond the wall, the harbor water lapped against the quay, indifferent and cold.

“They’re offering an evacuation berth,” Tomas said suddenly, the words tumbling out as if he hadn’t meant to speak them. “The armistice negotiations. One berth. Given to anyone listed in the ledger as eligible—families, conscripts with dependants, the sickly. The quartermaster said the selection’s already begun. He’s been checking names.”

I felt my throat tighten. “And?”

“Mother’s name’s on the list. Yours, too, by default—indenture status, the works. But the rules say only one per family. If one name’s taken, the other’s struck.” He looked at me, and for the first time in years, I saw the old fierce intelligence in his eyes, the one that had once gotten us both into trouble. “I’m listed as conscript labor. I could take it, maybe push through. But if I go, Mother’s berth goes to someone else. And if I don’t go, she might still lose it to another name, another family with more pull.”

“What about you?” I asked. “You’re not officially her child anymore, not on the books since the night the ledger was seized. They think you’re dead.”

“They think a lot of things.” Tomas’s jaw tightened. “But I know how the game’s played. I’ve watched you read those margins, Casper. You’ve kept us alive without saying a word. I won’t be the reason she goes.”

“And I won’t be the reason you stay.” I reached out, my fingers brushing his cheek, rough with grime and stubble. “Tomas, listen to me. The ledger’s a code, but the code’s only as strong as the silence behind it. If I forge a name, if I name a stranger’s child, the berth goes to someone who has no one. Someone who disappears into the paperwork and is forgotten. That’s what I’ve been trained for. That’s what I am.”

He pulled back slightly, searching my face. “You’d sacrifice yourself. Again. After everything.”

“After everything,” I agreed, “I’ve learned that silence is the only anchor we have. If I go, the ledger goes with me, or it gets burned, or it gets used against everyone I’ve ever loved. If I stay, I can keep copying. I can keep the true record, even if it means I’m the one left behind.” I smiled, but it felt like pulling a broken tooth. “I’m good at staying. I’ve had plenty of practice.”

“Then what about the berth?” he pressed. “What about the chance? What about—”

“What about the truth?” I interrupted gently. “The truth is, Tomas, that the ledger’s already a lie. The army’s already rewritten it. The only thing I can still control is what goes into the blank spaces after the war. What gets remembered. What gets buried.” I gestured vaguely at the yard, at the men, at the harbor beyond. “I’ve been indentured since I was old enough to hold a diving bell. I’ve had my name erased, my debts transferred, my mother’s illness coded as ‘complication of age’ so the quartermaster wouldn’t have to pay for her care. I’ve been useful, and I’ve been quiet, and I’ve survived by never speaking first. But I’ve also survived by knowing when to speak, and to whom.”

He was quiet for a long moment, the rain beginning to fall steadier, pattering on the canvas tents, blurring the edges of the yard. Somewhere above, a gull cried, and the sound seemed to carry the weight of the whole town, the whole sea, the whole history of silences and spoken words that had brought us to this moment.

“If I take the berth,” Tomas said finally, “I go. Mother stays. But she doesn’t have long. You know that. She’s been telling me for months that she’s tired, that she’s ready. And if I leave her here, alone, with the army and the ledger and all the names they’ve already marked…” He trailed off, but I understood. The fear wasn’t death. The fear was being left behind in a world that had already decided you were disposable.

“And if I don’t take it?” I asked. “Then Mother’s berth might go to someone else. A stranger’s child. Someone the quartermaster favors. And I stay. I keep copying. I keep the truth, even if it kills me slowly.”

“Slowly,” he repeated, as if testing the word. “As if there’s any other way.”

“As if there’s any other way,” I agreed. We stood there, two siblings who had been torn apart by circumstance and reunion, facing the same impossible arithmetic that the town had been forced to learn since the army came. The ledger was a map of who was valuable, who was disposable, who could be saved and who had to be sacrificed. And we had both spent our lives learning how to navigate its margins, how to read between the lines, how to keep the things that mattered alive without naming them outright.

“Mother’s lucid tonight,” Tomas said suddenly, as if reading my thoughts. “She asked after you. Said she wanted to see you before whatever happens happens. She’s been delirious for weeks, but tonight—” He swallowed. “She begged me to take the berth. Said I was the only one who’d ever stood a chance of getting out. Said I should save myself, that she’d already lived enough.”

I felt something shift inside my chest, a hollow space that had been slowly filling with grief and guilt and a fierce, stubborn love that refused to be shaped by anyone else’s expectations. “And what did you tell her?”

“I told her I couldn’t leave you. That you’d been the one keeping the ledger alive, that if anyone was going to survive, it should be you.” His voice cracked. “She cried, Casper. She cried, and she kept begging me to go, to take the berth, to promise her I’d live. But I couldn’t promise her that. I couldn’t promise her anything when the only thing I was certain of was that if I left, I’d be leaving you behind to carry everything we’ve both been hiding.”

I stood silent in the mud, the rain now coming down in earnest, mixing with the dirt, turning the yard to sludge. The ledger’s weight sat in my pocket, a physical presence, a reminder of all the names I’d copied, all the silences I’d kept, all the choices I’d made in the dark. And here was Tomas, my brother, my other half, asking me to be the sacrifice so he could live, and me asking him the same, and neither of us able to name the other as the one who deserved to go.

“She told me the same thing,” I said finally. “She begged me to take the berth. Said I was the one who could read the codes, who could keep the record alive, who had the best chance of getting out and remembering. She said I was the diver’s daughter, that I’d already learned how to survive under water, how to hold my breath when the world above became too heavy.” I laughed, but the sound came out wet. “She doesn’t know me at all, does she? After all these years, she still thinks I’m the one who survives.”
