## 9. The Last Berth

The armistice delegates arrived in a launch that cut the harbor's surface like a knife through cold cream. White flags snapped at the stern. Their oars moved in unison, the rhythm of men who had rowed away from too many battlefields to count. I watched from the upper window of the trading company's offices — my office now, by Rehn's decree — and felt the familiar tightening in my chest that came whenever the water bore strangers toward Vilna.

*They bring peace,* the town would say. *They bring the end.*

They brought a single berth on a hospital ship bound for Copenhagen. One berth. For a town of three thousand souls indentured to the harbor's bottom.

Rehn stood beside me, his uniform crisp despite the journey, the silver braid catching the weak April light. He smelled of tobacco and the particular soap officers used — sandalwood, I thought, or something equally absurd in a place where pearl divers coughed blood into handkerchiefs and mothers wrapped their dying in salt-stained shawls.

"Major Rehn," one of the delegates called from below, his voice carrying across the water. "We have the signed orders. The berth is yours to allocate per the ledger's eligibility."

Rehn didn't turn. His eyes stayed on the launch until it kissed the dock, until boots thudded on timber and the delegation's leader — a Colonel Voss, pale as a worm in plaster — climbed the stairs to the office.

"Quartermaster," Voss said, extending a hand. "The armistice takes effect at noon tomorrow. The ship departs at first light. Your ledger determines the passenger."

"My ledger," Rehn repeated, and there was something in his voice — a warning, or perhaps a confession.

Voss nodded, oblivious. "The criteria are clear. Conscripts with surviving dependents. Widows with children under twelve. The gravely ill with no local kin to tend them. One name. One signature. We leave the rest to God and the peace terms."

He produced a folder, thick with stamps and seals, and laid it on the desk where I had spent six months decoding the margins of three hundred lives.

Rehn didn't reach for it. His gaze slid to me.

"Fräulein Kavanagh," he said. "You have the ledger ready?"

My fingers, resting on the windowsill, went cold. *Ready.* As if I hadn't spent the last three nights with a lamp shaded by my skirt, a stolen nib scratching changes into margins written in a code only the divers knew. As if I hadn't moved Marta's youngest from the conscription column to the dependent column, shifted old Henrik's death date forward by a week so his grandson qualified as orphaned, erased the debt of the Kowalski girl so her mother could claim her.

*As if I hadn't forged my own brother's name into the eligible column, then forged it out again.*

"The ledger is complete, Quartermaster," I said. My voice held the flatness of a pearl dropped on a scale. "As you ordered."

"Good." Rehn turned to Voss. "Colonel, if you'll follow me. Fräulein Kavanagh will present the eligible names."

I didn't move. The window framed the harbor — the diving platforms like skeletal fingers, the warehouses huddled against the wind, the church spire where my mother had whispered prayers into her rosary until the beads wore smooth. Somewhere in the conscript barracks, Tomas waited. Somewhere in the room above the bakery, my mother waited, her breathing a rattle in the silence.

*One berth. Three Kavanaghs. Arithmetic, Rehn called it. Cruel arithmetic.*

"Fräulein."

Rehn's voice dropped. The courtesy vanished, leaving the officer who had watched me decode my neighbors' deaths with the detachment of a man reading a shipping manifest.

I turned.

He held a single sheet of paper. Not from the ledger. Thinner. The watermark of the trading company's old stationery — the clerk's private stock, the paper he'd given me years ago when he taught me to forge, *just in case, little diver, just in case.*

My forgery practice. The page where I had written Tomas's name in the eligible column, then crossed it out. My mother's name. My own. All in the coded margin script, all in my hand.

*Proof.*

The delegates murmured behind Voss. Rehn's face was impassive, but his eyes — those pale eyes that had measured me from the first day — held something I couldn't name. Not triumph. Not pity. Something closer to *respect*, twisted by duty.

"Explain," he said quietly.

My throat closed. The wry voice in my head — the one that had narrated every dive, every deception, every silence — went still. *Well, Casper. This is the part where you lie. You're good at it. Six months of practice.*

"I was verifying entries," I said. "The margins are... ambiguous. The old clerk's code varies by year. I made test notations to confirm my readings."

"Test notations." Rehn tilted the paper. The light caught the ink — iron gall, the same as the ledger. "Including your brother's name. Listed as alive. Listed as eligible. Then struck through."

*Tomas.* The name burned in my chest. The brother I had mourned for three years, the brother I had found in the conscript column two weeks ago, the brother who had refused the berth when I smuggled myself to the barracks fence last night.

*Let Mother go,* he had said, his face hollowed by hunger and the quarry dust. *She's dying. I'm not. The berth is hers.*

*And Mother, lucid for one evening, her fingers finding mine in the dimness: Save yourself, Casper. You're the one who remembers. You're the one who keeps the record.*

*Two sacrifices. One berth. No Kavanaghs.*

"Colonel Voss," Rehn said, not looking at me. "The ledger shows one eligible name from the Kavanagh household. Tomas Kavanagh, conscript, surviving dependent: mother, gravely ill. But the margin notes..." He tapped the forged sheet. "They suggest manipulation. Fräulein Kavanagh has been... creative."

Voss frowned. "Manipulation invalidates the entry. The berth passes to the next eligible name."

"Which is?" Rehn asked.

"Maria Kowalski. Widow. Two children under twelve. Her husband drowned last month." Voss checked his list. "Clean entry. No margin anomalies."

*Marta's friend. The woman who had pressed a pearl into my palm three nights ago, whispering, "My boys. Please. Just get my boys out."*

Rehn's gaze returned to me. "And if the ledger were corrected? If the true eligible name were restored?"

"Then the Kavanagh boy goes," Voss said. "Assuming his mother survives the journey. Which is unlikely."

*My mother would not survive the journey. She would not survive the night, perhaps. The doctor had given her days. The rattle in her chest had deepened this morning.*

"But you have proof of forgery," Voss continued. "That's a court-martial offense. Collaborator tampering with military records. The penalty is — "

"I know the penalty." My voice surprised me. Steady. Cold as the harbor at midnight. "Quartermaster Rehn knows it too."

Rehn's lip curled. The closest thing to a smile I'd seen on him. "Do I, Fräulein Kavanagh? Do I know what I'll do with a diver's daughter who rewrites the occupier's law?"

The delegates shifted. The harbor wind whistled through the window crack. Somewhere, a gull screamed.

"You'll use it," I said. "You'll offer me a choice. The berth for my silence. The ledger delivered intact for my life."

"And if I do?" Rehn asked. "What will you choose?"

* * *

The question followed me down the stairs, through the corridor, into the records room where the ledger lay open on its stand — the great leather-bound volume that held Vilna's soul in its pages. Names. Debts. Marriages. Deaths. The coded margins that only divers read: *this one dives deep, this one has weak lungs, this one's father was crushed by a falling spar in '09, this one's daughter is promised to the baker's son.*

My hands trembled as I lifted the nib. The inkwell was cold. The lamp sputtered.

*One final entry. One final forgery. The last time I will hold this pen.*

I thought of the old clerk, Herr Vogel, dead now these six months, his body carried from the office on a shutter door. He had taught me the code in the margins — *our code, the divers' code* — and he had taught me to forge it. *For when the ledger lies, little one. For when the truth is dangerous.*

He had not imagined this truth. This danger.

The eligible column. Page forty-seven. The Kowalski entry, clean and legal. Maria Kowalski. Widow. Two sons: Jan, eight. Piotr, five.

I dipped the nib. My hand found the rhythm — the angle, the pressure, the flow. Muscle memory from years of copying, years of lying.

*Strike through Kowalski. Enter new name.*

But whose name? Not Tomas. Not Mother. Not mine.

*A stranger's child. A drowned stranger's orphaned child.*

The thought came from somewhere deep, from the part of me that had learned to read the water's language — the currents, the pressure, the silence at thirty fathoms where the light dies and the pearls wait. The part that knew sacrifice was not a single act but a practice, a discipline, a way of breathing.

*Henrik's grandson. The one I already moved. No — too visible. Too connected.*

I turned to the back of the ledger. The supplement pages. The names of those lost at sea, recorded in the clerk's careful hand for insurance purposes, for widows' pensions that never came.

*Jonas Mikkelsen. Drowned October 1916. Pearl diver. Age thirty-four. Wife: deceased 1914. Child: Elsa, age four. Status: orphan, ward of the diving collective.*

Elsa Mikkelsen. Four years old. No living kin. Ward of the collective — which meant Marta, which meant the network of women who had passed me pearls and begged for alterations and called me savior and collaborator in the same breath.

*Elsa Mikkelsen. Eligible: orphan under twelve, no local kin to tend her.*

My hand moved. The coded script flowed — the diver's code, the margin language. *Elsa Mikkelsen. Ward of collective. Eligible. Priority: grave.*

I wrote it in the eligible column. Struck through Kowalski with a single line — *error in transcription* — and added the note: *Corrected per margin verification. Kavanagh, C.*

Then I wrote the second entry. The one that mattered more.

*True Record. Margin supplement. Page ninety-nine.*

I turned to the blank pages at the ledger's end. The pages Herr Vogel had left for *corrections*, he'd said. *For when the truth comes out.*

*Casper Kavanagh, daughter of Nils Kavanagh (drowned 1908) and Ingrid Kavanagh (dying). Sister of Tomas Kavanagh (conscript, alive). Diver. Ledger-reader. Forger. This ledger has been altered under duress. The true eligible names for the evacuation berth: Tomas Kavanagh, Ingrid Kavanagh. The berth is awarded to Elsa Mikkelsen, orphan, by deliberate forgery of the undersigned. The true record of Vilna's indentured families is held in memory and in these margins. Let no official stamp erase what the divers know.*

I signed it. *C. Kavanagh. 14 April 1917.*

The ink dried black and permanent.

* * *

The office was full when I returned. Voss. Two aides. Rehn by the window. The ledger closed on the desk.

"Fräulein Kavanagh," Voss said. "The corrected ledger?"

I placed my hands on the cover. Leather worn smooth by generations of clerks' palms. The brass clasp cold against my fingers.

"Corrected," I said. "Per your instructions."

Rehn moved closer. "The Kavanagh entry?"

"Struck through. Error in the margin code. The eligible name is Elsa Mikkelsen. Orphan. Ward of the diving collective. Four years old."

Voss checked his list. "Mikkelsen. Not on the original eligibility roll."

"Margin supplement, page ninety-nine," I said. "Verified this morning. The child has no kin. The collective cannot sustain her. She qualifies under the 'no local kin' clause."

A lie. The collective *was* her kin. Marta would have taken her. The divers would have raised her as their own, passing her from boat to boat, teaching her the water's language before she could read.

But the collective had no berth. The collective had only the ledger I had just forged.

Voss nodded, satisfied. "Very well. The child boards at dawn. Quartermaster, ensure the transfer."

"Of course." Rehn's voice was flat. His eyes stayed on me.

"And Fräulein Kavanagh?" Voss asked. "Your... irregularities. The forgery evidence."

Rehn was silent. The aides shifted. The harbor wind carried the sound of the ship's engines warming up — a low throb felt in the floorboards.

"Fräulein Kavanagh," Rehn said finally, "has provided the ledger intact. The corrections are verified. The berth is allocated. The army's obligation is fulfilled."

"Quartermaster — " Voss began.

"Colonel." Rehn turned, and his face was the face of a man signing a death warrant — his own, or mine, or both. "The armistice takes effect at noon. The ship departs at dawn. Fräulein Kavanagh is a civilian. Her... errors... are a matter for the civilian courts. If any remain."

Voss stared. The aides exchanged glances. The power dynamic shifted — the quartermaster protecting his ledger-reader, his collaborator, his *suspect* — and I understood then that Rehn had known all along. Not just the forgery. *Everything.* The night copying. The pearl bribes. The network. He had watched me build the true record in the margins, and he had let me.

*Why?*

*Because the true record was the only thing that mattered. Because when the army left, the ledger would remain. Because he knew — as I knew — that the occupiers' law was temporary, but the divers' memory was permanent.*

"Very well," Voss said. "Dawn, then. Quartermaster."

He left. The aides followed. The door closed.

Silence.

Rehn stood by the window, his back to me. The launch returned to the ship. The white flags snapped.

"You chose the stranger's child," he said. Not a question.

"I chose the record," I said. "The true one."

"The true record," he repeated, "would have sent your brother. Or your mother. Or you."

"The true record," I said, "is not written in the eligible column. It's written in the margins. In the names the ledger forgets. In the silences we keep."

He turned. In the failing light, his face was carved from shadow and bone. "And what happens now, Fräulein Kavanagh? The army withdraws. The company collapses. The indentures dissolve. You are... unindentured. Free. But you have no berth. No brother. No mother for much longer. What does a diver's daughter do with freedom?"

* * *

Dawn broke gray and wet. The hospital ship — *The White Dove*, painted with red crosses that looked like wounds — sat low in the water. A gangplank extended to the dock. Medical orderlies moved crates. A nurse checked a list.

Elsa Mikkelsen stood at the dock's edge, small in a coat three sizes too large, clutching a wooden doll. Marta knelt before her, adjusting the collar, whispering something that made the child nod solemnly. Other divers ringed them — a wall of salt-stained wool and calloused hands. No tears. Divers didn't cry at departures. The water took; the water gave. You breathed through it.

Tomas stood at the circle's edge. He wore the conscript's coarse uniform, his arm in a sling from the quarry accident. His eyes found mine across the distance. He didn't smile. Divers didn't smile at departures either.

*He knows. He knows what I did. He knows I gave away his berth, his mother's berth, his sister's berth.*

*He knows, and he will never forgive me. Or he will forgive me instantly. Divers forgive what the water demands.*

The gangplank rattled as the first passengers boarded. Wounded soldiers. A widow with twins. An officer's clerk.

Elsa Mikkelsen was lifted by an orderly. Her doll's wooden head knocked against his coat. She didn't cry. She watched the water.

*She will learn to read the water. She will learn the code. She will carry the true record in her bones.*

The ship's horn sounded. A low moan that vibrated in the harbor's ribs.

"Fräulein Kavanagh."

Rehn stood beside me on the office steps. He held a small package wrapped in oilcloth. "Your effects. The clerk's stationery. The nibs. The code key Herr Vogel left you."

I took it. The oilcloth was warm from his hand.

"You're not coming," I said.

"I have a train at noon. The armistice requires my presence in Riga." He paused. "The ledger stays. The true record stays. You stay."

"Yes."

"Colonel Voss asked why I didn't arrest you. Why I accepted the forged entry." His voice dropped. "I told him the ledger was accurate. That the margin code had been verified. That the orphan qualified."

*He lied for me. The quartermaster lied for the forger.*

"Why?"

Rehn's pale eyes met mine. "Because you gave the berth to a child who had no name in any official record. Because you wrote the truth in the margins where only divers read. Because..." He hesitated. "Because I have spent three years requisitioning ledgers in occupied towns. Burning the ones that told inconvenient truths. Promoting the ones that served the empire. And this is the first time I've seen someone forge a lie to tell the truth."

The horn sounded again. The gangplank was raised. The ship's engines throbbed deeper.

"Goodbye, Quartermaster Rehn."

"Goodbye, Fräulein Kavanagh. Casper."

He didn't salute. He didn't bow. He simply turned and walked toward the waiting staff car, his boots striking the dock in a rhythm that matched the ship's engines.

* * *

The White Dove pulled away. The white flags snapped. The harbor swallowed its wake.

Marta stood beside me on the dock, her face wind-scoured, her hands folded in her apron. The other divers drifted back toward the warehouses, the platforms, the boats. The network dissolving into its daily rhythm.

"She'll be safe," Marta said. "In Copenhagen. The Red Cross has families waiting. She'll learn Danish. She'll forget Vilna."

"She won't forget," I said. "The water doesn't let you forget. She'll carry it in her fingers. In her breath. In the way she reads the light on waves."

Marta glanced at me. "And you? What do you carry?"

*My mother's rattle. My brother's refusal. My forged signature. The ledger's true record burning in my memory. The oilcloth package against my chest.*

"I carry the margins," I said. "All of them."

The church bell tolled. Once. Twice. The hour of the armistice.

Somewhere in the room above the bakery, my mother took her last breath alone.

Somewhere in the conscript barracks, my brother stared at the empty berth and understood.

And I, Casper Kavanagh, diver's daughter, ledger-reader, forger, turned from the departing ship and walked toward the trading company's offices — toward the ledger waiting on its stand, its blank pages hungry for the names the water had taken, the names the army had erased, the names only the margins remembered.

*The true record begins now.*

* * *

The first entry I wrote in the blank pages was not a name.

It was a date: *14 April 1917. Armistice. The White Dove departs. One berth. Elsa Mikkelsen, age four, orphan. The Kavanagh berth. Forged by C. Kavanagh. Witnessed by M. Rehn. Known by the water.*

The second entry was a name.

*Ingrid Kavanagh. Mother. Died 14 April 1917, 11:47 hours. Last words: "Save yourself, Casper. You're the one who remembers." Buried 15 April, harbor cemetery, plot beside Nils Kavanagh (drowned 1908). No priest. Divers' rites. Pearls on the shroud.*

The third entry was a name I had not written in the ledger for three years.
