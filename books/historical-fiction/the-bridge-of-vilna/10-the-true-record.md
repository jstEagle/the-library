## 10. The True Record

The army left the way armies always leave: badly, and in a hurry.

They came through town for three days in a river of grey wagons, taking with them everything that hadn't been nailed down and several things that had been. Rehn went out on the second morning, in a staff car polished to an indecency that seemed almost apologetic. He did not say goodbye. He sent a corporal instead, who stood in my doorway holding an envelope like it might bite him and said, "The Quartermaster's compliments."

Inside was a single sheet of paper. My indenture entry, torn cleanly from some duplicate register, stamped *ANNULLED — ADMINISTRATIVE ERROR* across my name in red ink so fresh it smudged on my thumb.

An administrative error. Eleven years of my life, filed under mistake.

I laughed until the corporal shifted his weight, and then I thanked him with the grave courtesy we'd all learned to perform for men in uniform, and closed the door, and sat down on the floor with my back against it, holding the paper that made me free the way you'd hold a fish — carefully, and wondering what it cost somewhere else.

* * *

They buried my mother on a Thursday, beside the harbor, because she had asked for it in one of her lucid hours and because the churchyard was full of better-connected dead who would have complained.

Marta dug the first spadeful. Then her husband. Then old Piet, whose hands shook so badly he mostly fertilized the grave with his own effort rather than the shovel's. The diving families came the way they did everything — without being asked, without speaking much, each of them carrying something. A pearl. A stone from a bed only they knew. Marta laid a coil of new rope at the head of the grave, diver's rope, the kind that holds.

"She'd have hated the fuss," I said.

"She'd have hated it quietly," Marta said, "which is the same as loving it."

That was the whole eulogy, and it was more than enough. My mother had spent sixty-one years underwater and most of the rest of them silent, and silence was the only hymn she'd ever trusted. We gave her that. The harbor gave her the rest — gulls, tide-slap against the pilings, the far-off groan of a crane nobody owned anymore.

Afterward, people kept pressing things into my hands. Not condolences. Records. A scrap of paper with a date. A name remembered from a boat that went down in '09. Piet's wife's recipe card with three names written on the back in pencil, women lost to the bends before the war made losing people fashionable. They didn't explain. They just looked at me — at me, the ledger-reader, the forger, the girl who'd given away the last berth to a drowned stranger's child — and they handed over their dead like deposits at a bank they'd finally decided to trust.

I understood what they were doing about a day later, standing in the ruined company office with the ledger open in front of me.

* * *

The company had collapsed the way rotten pilings collapse — not all at once but in a long settling creak, directors resigning by post from cities they'd fled to months ago, the harbor office stripped of anything brass. The ledger itself they'd left behind. Of course they had. To them it had only ever been debt; the names were incidental. The army had wanted it for labor columns and conscription lists. Nobody, in eleven years of owning my life on paper, had ever wanted what was actually written in it.

Which left the blank pages at the back. Forty of them, maybe fifty, good rag paper, waiting.

I sat down at the clerk's old desk — my desk now, I supposed, in the way that ruins belong to whoever's willing to sit in them — and I sharpened a pen, and I thought about where to begin.

Not with the requisition. Not with the berth, or the forgery, or Rehn's courteous grey eyes watching me across the margin-notes. Anyone could write down what the army did; the army had written plenty of it themselves, in triplicate, in a language designed to make atrocity sound like inventory.

No. You begin where the record actually begins. With the water.

*Entry the first,* I wrote. *In the year 1906, in the month of the herring run, Anneli Kavanagh, aged nineteen, dove the north beds four hours past safe measure to cover her father's debt to the company, and brought up eleven pearls, and was not thanked, because thanking divers is not among the company's expenses.*

And then the next. And the next. I wrote the debts and who owed them and who had actually paid them, which were rarely the same people. I wrote the coded margins out plain at last — the little marks we divers had used for generations to warn each other: *this officer takes bribes, this one doesn't; this bed is poisoned, this family is marked.* I wrote the names of the drowned with their beds attached, because a diver isn't properly dead until someone knows where to find her. I wrote the silences, too — the ones I'd kept, the ones kept for me, the ones my mother had carried like stones in her apron pockets for sixty years. *Silence kept:* I made it a column. It filled faster than any other.

Some nights I wrote until my hand cramped and the lamp oil ran low. Some nights I couldn't write at all, and sat looking at the harbor through the broken window, doing the arithmetic I would do for the rest of my life: one berth, two eligible Kavanaghs, zero used. Tomas alive in a conscript column. My mother in the ground. A stranger's child on a boat to somewhere safer, alive because a dead man's daughter had learned to forge her own family out of existence.

People asked, sometimes. Gently. Whether I regretted it.

I told them the truth, which is that regret requires imagining a different choice, and I had never once been able to imagine one. I had held the pen over that page with every name in the world available to it, and my hand had known before I did. Memory over blood. It sounds noble when you say it that way. Mostly it was just that I had spent eleven years learning what happens to towns that let other people keep their records, and I could not bear to be the one who let it happen again, even to save the two people I loved best. Even especially then.

Noble. Wretched. Both, probably. The ledger doesn't care how it's written, only that it is.

* * *

Winter came early that year, the way it does after wars, as if the sky too wants the thing finished. The harbor froze in patches. The town learned to be poor in the new way instead of the occupied way, which was worse in some respects and infinitely better in the one that mattered: the hunger was ours now, unmanaged, unbureaucratic, ours.

I kept writing. By February the true record ran to ninety pages and the diving families had begun a custom I never sanctioned and never stopped: on Sunday evenings they came to the office, one or two at a time, and read. Old Piet brought his grandchildren and made them sound out the names. Marta came and read the column of silences and said nothing, which was its own kind of reading. Once, a woman I didn't know — a stranger, inland-refugee thin — stood over the page where I'd recorded her husband's drowning and wept without noise, the way divers' families learn to, and then touched the ink with one finger, very lightly, like checking whether a wound has closed.

That was when I understood what I'd built. Not a history. A harbor. Somewhere for the drowned to come ashore.

* * *

He came on foot, in March, at the hour when the light goes long and dishonest.

I was at the desk — I was always at the desk; the town had started calling it *the record office*, half in jest — copying out the winter deaths, of which there had been thirty-one, when the door opened without a knock and a man stood in it wearing a conscript's coat gone to rags and boots wrapped in sacking, and he was taller than my memory of him and thinner, and his hair had my mother's exact color, the brown of wet kelp, and I knew him the way you know water. Before thinking. In the body.

"Tomas," I said. My voice came out strange, as if borrowed.

"Cas." He looked around the room — at the ledger open on the desk, at the pages of names pinned to the walls where I'd run out of room, at the whole improbable archive of us — and something moved through his face that I couldn't read, grief and relief tangled past separating. "They said there was a woman here keeping the town's book. They didn't say it was you."

"Who said?"

"Everyone. Every town between here and the east. There's a story going around." He smiled, and it cracked me open, that smile, our mother's smile worn thin by three years of war. "About a diver's daughter who writes down the dead so they can't be stolen twice."

I stood up. My legs nearly declined the assignment. Three years I'd believed him dead outside a town whose name I'd refused to learn, because learning it would have made the grief specific, and specific grief, I'd reasoned, would kill me before the war got the chance. And here he was, smelling of road dust and woodsmoke, alive, walking, *mine.*

"Mama," I said, and stopped, because there was no gentle way to build that sentence.

"I know." He crossed the room and took my hands, and his were cracked and scarred and shaking slightly, and I held on anyway. "I saw the grave. Fresh earth, by the water. I've been standing out there for an hour trying to make myself come in." His voice broke on the next part. "I'm sorry, Cas. I'm sorry I wasn't—"

"You're here." I said it like a fact entered into evidence, because that's what I knew how to do. "You're here, and it's March, and the herring will run in six weeks, and I have ninety pages and forty blank ones, and you are going to sit down at that desk and tell me everything — every name, every date, every silence you kept — and I am going to write it down, and neither of us is ever going to be unrecorded again."

He laughed — wetly, badly, wonderfully — and pulled me into his arms, and above his shoulder the harbor lay grey and thawing, giving back the light.

Outside, the ice was breaking up. I could hear it all along the shore: the long groan and crack of a thing that had seemed permanent remembering, all at once, that it was water.
