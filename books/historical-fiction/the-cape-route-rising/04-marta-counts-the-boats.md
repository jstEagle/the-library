## 4. Marta Counts the Boats

I count boats the way other girls count stitches in a sampler or verses in a psalm. It is not a game. It is a ledger I keep in my head, and like any proper ledger, it balances only when the truth is told.

This morning, the balance is wrong.

I stand at the kitchen window, the porcelain bowl of porridge cooling between my hands, watching the harbor breathe beneath its shroud of mist. The tide is ebb, the water sucking back from the pilings with a sound like old men clearing their throats. Three fishing smacks ride at anchor where they always do — *The Providence*, *The Sainted Margaret*, *The Widow's Mite* — their names painted in peeling white on weathered sterns. I know their captains by the set of their shoulders, by the way Halvorsen spits over the rail before he speaks, by the way young Tomas Petersen crosses himself when the foghorn sounds.

But today there are two others.

I set down my bowl. The porridge forms a skin. Uncle Dominic — Father, I correct myself, though the word still catches sometimes — Father will say *eat before it's glue*, and I will say *it's already glue*, and he will ruffle my hair with hands that smell of whale oil and salt. But not yet. First, the numbers.

Two vessels I do not know. Three-masted, both, sitting low in the water. Not fishing lines. No nets drying on the rails. Their hulls are painted a dull black that drinks the light, and they fly no flags I recognize — not the Danish cross, not the Swedish triple-crown, not the red ensign of the British traders who come for timber in summer. Just gray canvas furled tight, and men moving on deck who do not move like fishermen.

Fishermen move with the sea. These men move against it.

*One. Two.* I tap my fingers against the windowsill. *Three. Four. Five known. Two unknown. Seven total at anchor. The ledger says six this time of year. The ledger says the *Widow's Mite* puts in at Christiansand for winter refit by Martinmas. The ledger says — *

"Marta."

Father's voice behind me. Not sharp. Never sharp. Just the low rumble of a man who has learned to speak softly because the light demands it, because the foghorn does the shouting for him.

I turn. He stands in the doorway of the keeper's cottage, dressed in his oilskins though the storm has not yet broken. His boots track wet sand across the scrubbed pine floor. At forty-three, he carries his grief like ballast — necessary, invisible, the only thing keeping him upright in certain weathers. Elena has been gone three winters now. I have counted them too.

"Porridge," he says. "Before it walks away."

"It's not the porridge that's walking." I nod toward the window. "It's the harbor. Two new ships. Black hulls. No flags. Came in on the midnight tide, by the look of their anchor chains — slack for high water, taut now. They didn't signal the light."

Father crosses the kitchen in three strides. He does not ask how I know the tide they arrived on. He does not ask how I see anchor chains from a kilometer away. He has long since stopped asking *how*. He only looks.

His face does not change. This is its own language. The slight tightening at the corner of his jaw. The way his eyes, the color of the sea in March, go distant — calculating. He was a ship's officer once, before the light, before Elena, before the letter that arrived with a drowned man two nights past. I did not see the letter. I heard the heavy tread of boots on the tower stairs at three in the morning, the muffled conversation in the study, the sound of paper burning in the stove. I heard Father say *not a word to Marta* and Halvorsen's rough reply *she'll know anyway, Dom. She always knows.*

I am eleven years old. I know the weight of things not said.

"Black hulls," Father repeats. Not a question. "No colors."

"None I could see. The mist — "

"Stay here. Finish your porridge. I'll walk the beach."

He turns to go, and I see it then — the paper folded tight in his coat pocket, pressing against the wool. A square of cream vellum, edges singed. Not the letter from the courier. That one burned. This one is new. Arrived yesterday with the post boat from the mainland, handed to him by the clerk with the ink-stained fingers who never meets my eyes.

*Another one,* I think. *Another piece of the sum I'm not supposed to add.*

"Father."

He pauses, hand on the latch.

"Will you tell me when you come back?"

He looks at me then. Really looks. The way he did when I was six and asked why Mama Elena wasn't waking up, and he knelt in the narrow bed and said *she's gone to a place without pain, little one, and I don't know the way there yet*.

"No," he says gently. "But I'll tell you what I can. When I can. That's the best promise I have."

He leaves. The door swings shut. The porridge is glue now, truly, and I eat it spoonful by gray spoonful, watching the harbor through steam-rimmed glass.

***

The beach walk takes him two hours. I know because I count the minutes by the kitchen clock — the one with the cracked face that loses three minutes a day, which Father winds every Sunday with the same reverence he gives the light's mechanism. Two hours, and the mist burns off to a pale winter sun that does nothing to warm the stones.

When he returns, his oilskins are stiff with salt ice. He hangs them by the stove without a word, accepts the mug of tea I've brewed — weak, the way he takes it since Elena died, because she was the one who remembered the proper measure — and sits at the table with his back to the window.

"Well?" I ask.

He blows across the tea. Steam fogs his spectacles. He is a man who reads by lamplight, who deciphers ciphers in his father's ledgers, who once navigated by stars and now navigates by secrets. The spectacles are new. His eyes are failing. He says it is age. I say it is the weight of what he reads.

"Two vessels," he says. "Dutch registry. *The Verkenner* and *De Zwaan*. The Swan and the Scout. Innocent names."

"And not innocent cargo?"

He sets down the mug. "Customs logs show them clearing Copenhagen for Stockholm. Ballast only. But their draft marks tell another story. They're riding light for ships supposedly empty."

"Smugglers."

"A word I'd rather not hear in this house."

"Then what word? *Merchants*? *Traders*? *Gentlemen of fortune*?" I stand, scraping my chair back. "Father, the magistrate's clerk was here yesterday. He asked for 'family papers.' He looked at the workshop door. He looked at *me*."

Father's hand closes around the mug. The knuckles whiten. "He asked after the ledgers?"

"He asked after *papers*. Plural. Vague. The way men ask when they know the answer but want to hear you lie."

"Marta — "

"Uncle Halvorsen says the magistrate has owned this harbor for twenty years. He says every keeper before you paid tribute in silence. He says — "

"Halvorsen says many things. Half of them rumored, half remembered wrong, all of them colored by whiskey and grudge."

"Then tell me the truth. So I don't have to listen to his version."

Father is silent. The clock ticks. Three minutes slow. The fire pops, a spruce knot spitting embers.

"I am the keeper of the Cape Route light," he says finally. "My duty is the lamp. The foghorn. The log. The lives saved on this coast. It is *not* my duty to police the harbor, to judge the magistrate, to — "

"To protect me?"

The words hang. He flinches, microscopically. A man struck without a blow.

"That is *exactly* my duty."

"Then tell me what the letter said. The one the drowned man carried. The one you burned."

"I burned nothing. I — " He stops. Removes his spectacles. Rubs the bridge of his nose. "I gave it to the provincial magistrate in Kristiansand. By the post boat. Yesterday."

"And?"

"And I have heard nothing back. The ice closes the roads. The post runs irregular. We wait."

"While the black ships unload? While the clerk returns? While — "

"Marta." His voice hardens. The keeper's voice. The officer's voice. "Go to your lessons. Latin. Arithmetic. The cipher exercises I set. That is your work. Mine is the light. The ledgers. The letters. The boundary between them exists for a reason."

I stare at him. At the man who taught me to read before I could reach the table, who showed me how to strip a lens and polish it blindfolded, who answers every question with a lesson and every fear with a task.

*The boundary exists because you drew it,* I think. *And you drew it because you're afraid of what I'll find on the other side.*

"Yes, Father," I say.

I go to the small room off the kitchen — my room, though it was a storage closet when we arrived. The desk is pine, scarred by my knife, ink-stained from the cipher practice. *Substitute each letter with the third following. A becomes D. B becomes E.* Child's play. Father's real ciphers are in the workshop below the cliff, in the false drawers he found last week, in the ledgers that name names and sums and dates stretching back to his father's father.

I open my Latin grammar. *Amo, amas, amat.* I love, you love, he loves.

*Amamus, amatis, amant.* We love, you love, they love.

The plural forms feel like a joke. *We love.* As if love were a conjugation, a rule, something that follows pattern. Elena loved me. Father loves me. Halvorsen loves me in his gruff, fish-scented way. But love does not tell me why the Dutch ships anchor at odd tides. Love does not explain the clerk's ink-stained fingers or the magistrate's interest in our workshop or the letter Father sent to Kristiansand that may never reach its destination because the ice breaks early some years and takes the road with it.

I push the grammar aside. Pull a blank sheet toward me. Dip the pen.

*Harbor Log, 14 February 1888. Weather: clearing, wind NW force 3. Temperature -4°C. Ice forming on inner harbor. Vessels at anchor: 7 total. 5 known (list attached). 2 unknown — Dutch registry claimed, black hulls, no flags displayed, draft inconsistent with declared ballast. Arrival: approx. 00:30 hrs, 13 Feb, per anchor chain observation. No signal to light. No entry in harbor master's book (checked 09:00 hrs). Magistrate's clerk visited keeper's cottage 13 Feb, 14:00 hrs, requesting 'family papers.' Keeper absent. Clerk questioned child re: workshop access. Child declined response. Clerk departed 14:22 hrs. Keeper returned 15:45 hrs. Keeper departed for beach walk 16:00 hrs. Returned 18:00 hrs. Keeper reports vessels as 'Dutch, innocent.' Child observes: vessels unloading after dark? Unconfirmed. Requires verification.*

I sign it *M. Iversen, Observer.* The initials feel deliberate. *Iversen.* Not *Lund*, my birth name. Not *Andersdatter*, my patronymic. *Iversen.* The name Father gave me when he took me to the parish priest and said *this is my daughter now*, and the priest blinked and wrote it down because Father Iversen kept the light and the light kept the coast, and no one argues with the keeper.

***

The afternoon brings Halvorsen.

He arrives like a storm front — sudden, inevitable, smelling of tar and tobacco and something sharper beneath, something like fear disguised as brine. His beard is white now, his shoulders bowed, but his eyes are the same flinty blue I've known since I was small enough to hide in his coat pockets during the winter festivals.

"Girl," he grunts, stomping snow from his boots in the entryway. "Your father about?"

"In the tower. Polishing the Fresnel." I appear in the kitchen doorway, drying my hands on a towel. "He sent me for lessons. Latin. Ciphers. The usual imprisonment."

Halvorsen snorts. A sound like gravel in a tin pail. "Latin. Ciphers. And what does an eleven-year-old need with ciphers, unless her father's keeping secrets worth coding?"

"He says it trains the mind."

"He says it keeps you busy. There's a difference." He leans against the doorframe, arms crossed. His oilskin coat is patched at the elbows, the cuffs frayed. A good man. A hard man. The closest thing to a grandfather I have, since Father's father died before I was born and Elena's parents live in a Copenhagen I've never seen. "He tell you about the ships?"

"He told me they're Dutch. He told me they're innocent. He told me to do my lessons."

"And you believe him?"

I consider the question. Halvorsen deserves honesty. He has never lied to me, not even about Elena, not even when the truth was a knife.

"I believe he believes it. Or needs to. There's a difference there too."

Halvorsen's gaze sharpens. He studies me the way he studies the barometer — reading pressure changes too subtle for instruments.

"Smart girl. Too smart. Your uncle Erik — God rest his fool soul — he was smart too. Smart enough to get himself drowned in a storm he should've waited out. Smart enough to leave you with Dom and a legacy of trouble he never spoke of."

"My father was not a fool."

"No. He was a brother. Dom's brother. And brothers keep each other's secrets, even the ones that kill them." Halvorsen pushes off the doorframe. "Where's the workshop key?"

"Around Father's neck. Always."

"Good. Keep it there. And you — " He jabs a callused finger toward me. "You stay away from the harbor after dark. You stay away from the Dutch ships. You stay away from the magistrate's clerk, who I hear was asking after you by name yesterday."

"I didn't speak to him."

"Good. Keep not speaking. Silence is a weapon, girl. The only one you've got."

He turns to go, then pauses. His hand rests on the latch, rough and scarred.

"Your father... he's carrying something heavy. Heavier than the light. Heavier than Elena." The words come slow, dragged from somewhere deep. "He won't set it down. Not for me. Not for the magistrate. Maybe not even for you. But if anyone can make him... it's you. Remember that."

He leaves. The door bangs shut. The cottage settles back into silence.

I stand in the kitchen, the towel forgotten in my hands, and think about weapons. Silence. Ciphers. Ledgers. The light itself — a beam that cuts through darkness, that says *here is land, here is danger, here is the way home*.

*What is my way home?* I wonder. *And who decides where it leads?*

***

Night falls hard and fast. The winter sun surrenders by three, and the light takes up its work — the great Fresnel lens throwing its signature across the water: *flash, flash, pause. Flash, flash, pause.* Two white, one red. The rhythm of this coast. The heartbeat of the Cape Route.

Father is in the tower. I hear the clockwork mechanism turning, the weight descending, the gears meshing with the precision of a heartbeat. He will wind it every four hours. He will trim the wicks. He will log the weather, the visibility, the ships passing. He will not sleep until dawn.

I am in my room, ostensibly asleep. Actually watching through the cracked window that faces the harbor.

The Dutch ships have not moved. But at twenty-two hundred hours, a small boat puts out from *The Verkenner* — a ship's boat, six oars, muffled with cloth. It rows not toward the harbor quay but toward the old smuggler's cove, the one concealed behind the basalt fingers at the base of the cliffs. The cove Father forbade me to visit. The cove Halvorsen once showed me on a dare, whispering *this is where the king's revenue men never look, and where the magistrate's men always do.*

The boat disappears into the shadow of the cliffs. Twenty minutes later, it returns, lower in the water. Heavier.

*Cargo,* I note. *Inbound. Not outbound. Smugglers usually run product out — spirits, tobacco, tea. This runs something in.*

At twenty-three hundred, a second boat. From *De Zwaan*. Same course. Same return. Heavier.

At midnight, a wagon creaks along the cliff-top road — the one that passes the keeper's cottage. I hear it because I am listening. Because I have trained myself to hear the differences: the light cart of the baker, the heavy dray of the fish merchant, the silent sled of the magistrate's men.

This wagon is heavy. Iron-shod wheels. Four horses, not two. It turns toward the workshop path — the narrow track that descends the cliff face to Father's workshop, the one carved from the rock by my great-grandfather's hands.

The workshop Father sealed when he became keeper. The workshop he reopened last week. The workshop with the false drawers.

I am out of bed before I decide to move. Down the narrow stair, past the study where the burned letter's ashes still linger in the stove, past the kitchen where the fire has banked to embers. Out the back door into the biting night.

The path is treacherous in daylight. In darkness, with ice glazing the rock steps, it is madness. I know every foothold. I have descended it a hundred times in summer, when the wild thyme bloomed and the gulls screamed and Father laughed at something Elena said. I have descended it twice in winter — once to bring Father his forgotten spectacles, once because Halvorsen dared me and I never refuse Halvorsen's dares.

Tonight I descend for a different reason.

*To see. To know. To add the numbers myself.*

The workshop looms ahead — a stone building huddled against the cliff base, its roof slate, its door oak banded with iron. The window is dark. But the door... the door is ajar. A finger's width. A slice of lamplight spills onto the snow.

Someone is inside.

I freeze on the path, my heart a trapped bird. The cold bites my face, my hands, the bare ankles where my nightgown meets my stockings. I should run. I should fetch Father. I should —

*Count,* I tell myself. *Count and know.*

One man visible through the crack. Large. Wearing the magistrate's clerk's coat — the one with the ink-stained cuffs. He stands at the desk where Father's ledgers sit open. He holds a page to the light. He is reading.

A second man appears in the slice of light. Smaller. Wiry. A knife glints at his belt.

"— not the full set," the clerk whispers. "Only the current year. The magistrate wants the current year. The rest can wait for spring."

"The rest is the proof," the second man hisses. "The rest hangs him. Hangs us all."

"Then we take the rest. But quietly. The keeper watches the light. He doesn't watch — "

The clerk's head turns. His eyes, pale in the lamplight, sweep the doorway.

They land on me.

I don't breathe. I don't move. I am a statue on the ice-slick path, an eleven-year-old girl in a nightgown, holding the sum of everything in my head.

The clerk smiles. It is not a nice smile. It is the smile of a man who has found a variable he didn't expect, and knows exactly how to solve for it.

"Well," he says softly. "Well, well. The keeper's little accountant."

He opens the door fully. The cold rushes in. The light rushes out. And I see, on the desk beside the ledgers, a single sheet of vellum — cream-colored, edges singed, bearing a seal I recognize.

*The seal from the drowned man's letter. The seal Father said he sent to Kristiansand.*

It never left this room.

"Marta," the clerk says, my name strange in his mouth. "Come in from the cold. We have much to discuss. You and I and the ledgers your father keeps."

Behind him, the wiry man's hand closes on his knife.

I look at the ledgers. At the false drawer Father showed me, the one with the cipher key. At the clerk's ink-stained fingers. At the seal that should be in Kristiansand.

*Two unknown ships. Two boats to the cove. One wagon. Two men in the workshop. One clerk who lies. One father who keeps secrets. One girl who counts.*

The numbers align.

I turn and run.

Not up the path — too slow, too exposed. I run *along* the cliff base, toward the smuggler's cove, toward the black water and the basalt fingers and the darkness that knows my name.

Behind me, the clerk shouts. Boots pound on stone. The wiry man laughs, a sharp bark.

*Run,* I tell myself. *Count the steps. Count the breaths. Count the heartbeats. The ledger balances only when the truth is told.*

And I am the only one keeping it.

***

The cove is a throat cut into the cliffs. The water here is black, the waves slapping against rock with a sound like muted applause. The Dutch ships' boat is gone. But tracks in the snow lead to a narrow fissure — a crack in the cliff face, barely wide enough for a man.

Father showed me this once. *A bolt-hole,* he called it. *Used by the original keeper. If the tower falls, if the light fails, you go here. You wait. You survive.*

I squeeze into the fissure. The rock tears my nightgown, scrapes my shoulders, bruises my hips. I worm upward, sideways, backward, following the crack as it spirals into the cliff's guts. My fingers bleed. My breath rasps. The cold is a living thing, pressing against me.

Above me, voices echo in the stone. The clerk. The wiry man. They're on the path now, searching.

"— find her. She's seen the ledger. She's seen the seal."

"She's a child. She'll freeze before she reaches the top."

"Then she freezes with the truth in her throat. Either way, the magistrate wins."

I climb. The fissure narrows. My ribs catch. I panic — a hot, clawing thing — and force it down. *Count. Count the handholds. Count the breaths. One. Two. Three.*

Ahead, a faint gray light. Not daylight. The light from the tower, refracted through quartz veins in the rock. The fissure opens onto a ledge — a natural shelf halfway up the cliff face, hidden from below, invisible from above.

I haul myself out, gasping, shivering, alive.

The ledge is wide enough to lie down. Narrow enough to fall from. The wind screams here, tearing at my hair, my nightgown, my skin. Below, the harbor spreads out — the Dutch ships, the fishing smacks, the quay where the wagon waits. Above, the tower's beam sweeps the water: *flash, flash, pause. Flash, flash, pause.*

Father is up there. Winding the weight. Trimming the wicks. Logging the weather.

He does not know I am gone. He does not know the clerk has the seal. He does not know the ledgers are compromised.

*I have to tell him. I have to reach him. I have to — *

A hand closes on my ankle.

I scream. I kick. My heel connects with something soft. A grunt. The hand releases.

I scramble backward, heart hammering, and look down.

The wiry man hangs from the fissure's mouth, one arm hooked around a rock protrusion, the other nursing his groin. His knife is gone — lost in the climb or dropped when I struck him. His face is a mask of pain and fury.

"Little bitch," he gasps. "I'll gut you. I'll — "

A second hand appears. The clerk. He hauls himself onto the ledge with terrifying ease, despite his bulk. No knife visible. But his eyes... his eyes are the coldest thing I have ever seen. Colder than the winter sea. Colder than the light's glass.

"Marta Iversen," he says, breathing hard. "You have made this remarkably difficult. The magistrate will be... displeased."

"She's made nothing difficult," a new voice says. Deep. Calm. Familiar. "She's made it *possible*."

Halvorsen steps from the shadow of the cliff face — from a second fissure I never knew existed. He holds a shotgun broken across his forearms, the barrels gleaming in the tower's sweeping beam. Behind him, two more figures emerge: Tomas Petersen, young and wide-eyed, clutching a boat hook; and a woman I don't know, broad-shouldered and grim, holding a lantern.

The clerk's face cycles through surprise, calculation, decision. "Halvorsen. You're a long way from your hut."

"I'm exactly where I need to be. As are we all." Halvorsen gestures with the shotgun. "Step away from the girl. Now. The magistrate's writ doesn't extend to this cliff. And if you think the provincial court in Kristiansand will back a magistrate who sends thieves to steal from a lighthouse keeper — "

"The magistrate *is* the law in this province."

"Then the law has come to a bad end." Halvorsen's voice drops. "Because I've seen the ledger. The real one. The one your master doesn't know we copied. And I've sent a copy to the King's Commissioner in Copenhagen by the last ice-boat — the one that left before the roads closed."

The clerk goes still. The mask slips. For a heartbeat, fear flashes across his face — raw and real.

"You're lying."

"Am I? Ask your master about the entry for 1762. The one about the *Crown Prince* wreck. The one where the magistrate's grandfather took the cargo and left the crew to drown. Ask him about the entry for 1809. The one where the keeper's great-grandfather was paid to extinguish the light. Ask him — "

"Enough." The clerk straightens. His hand moves inside his coat. "The girl comes with me. The ledgers stay. The rest... can be negotiated."

"No." Father's voice.

He stands at the top of the cliff path, silhouetted against the tower's glow. The light sweeps past him — *flash, flash, pause* — and in the red sector's glow, I see the rifle at his shoulder. Not the shotgun he uses for gulls. The service rifle from his officer days. The one he swore he'd never fire again.

"Dominic Iversen." The clerk's voice is smooth again. Controlled. "You'd shoot a king's officer? Over a child and some account books?"

"I'd shoot a thief. Over my daughter. Over the truth." Father doesn't raise the rifle. Doesn't need to. The threat is absolute in his stillness. "The seal you hold. The ledger you read. The wagons you sent. All of it ends tonight. The provincial magistrate has my deposition. The King's Commissioner has Halvorsen's copy. Your master's warrant was revoked three days ago. You're not a clerk. You're a criminal trespassing on Crown land."

The clerk's composure fractures. "That's impossible. The ice — the roads — the post — "

"The post boat you intercepted. The rider you turned back. The letter you burned." Father takes a step down the path. "I sent two letters. One by post. One by a fisherman who knows the winter currents better than any magistrate's man. Tomas?"

Young Petersen steps forward, grinning fiercely. "The *Widow's Mite* made Christiansand in two days, sir. Captain Halvorsen's cousin took the letter. He's fast when the cause is right."

The wiry man scrambles up onto the ledge, knife returned to hand. He lunges for me.

The shotgun cracks.

The report rolls off the cliffs like thunder. The wiry man drops, the knife spinning from his grasp, and slides back into the fissure with a wet sound. The clerk stares at the blood on the rock, at Halvorsen reloading with practiced ease, at Father descending the path like judgment given form.

"Take him," Father says quietly. "Bind him. The *Providence* leaves for Kristiansand at first light. He'll face the Commissioner there."

Tomas and the woman move — efficient, silent, the way fishermen move when the net is full and the storm is rising. They drag the clerk from the ledge, down the path, into the darkness toward the harbor.

Halvorsen slings the shotgun. Reaches for me. His hands are gentle as he lifts me, wraps me in his coat, presses my frozen face against his rough wool.

"Smart girl," he murmurs. "Too smart. Brave girl. Too brave. Foolish girl. Too foolish."
