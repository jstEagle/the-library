## 5. The Workshop Below the Cliff

The workshop sat where my father had built it, thirty years ago on land that the sea never quite forgave. The cliff face pressed close enough that the spray kissed the windowpanes when the morning tide came in, and tonight the roar of the Atlantic was the only conversation the rocks seemed willing to hold. I could hear it through the stone walls, a constant bass note beneath which the wind whispered its usual lies—always something different, always a new way to pretend the world beneath the waves didn't matter.

Marta was awake already. She slept best in the bunk near the lantern room floor, her head resting against the worn wood of her bedroll, eyes closed even though the lamp cast shadows across her face that made her look older than twenty-two. Twenty-two. Old enough to know that the winter would take its toll eventually, young enough that the thought did little to ease the knot in my stomach. She had been born in this house, taken from her cradle within a year of my arrival, and now here I was, forty-three years old and still holding onto a life that felt more like an obligation than anything else.

"You're late," she murmured, not opening her eyes. Her voice was thin in the cold air, but steady.

"I'm exactly on time." I pulled aside the curtain at the lower door, the one that had been reinforced after the third gale took the west wall three winters back. The workshop smelled of salt and old paper and the particular staleness of a place left too long untouched. My father's tools still hung in their places along the far wall—brass compass, a half-finished sextant, a leather-bound logbook my father had maintained since the light was lit. The work went on without him.

I stripped off my coat and tied my boots. The stone steps down to the level of the lantern room were slick, and I made sure my shoes were dry before committing to them. The descent took longer than it should have, the wind tugging at my collar, but the path was familiar. My mother would have told me so many times. She'd call my stubbornness a curse and my perseverance a virtue, and I'd always known those two things were the same thing wearing different clothes.

When the workshop doors opened behind me, Marta shifted and turned her face away toward the dark interior. She wasn't frightened—their conversations rarely ran that high—but something in her expression suggested she understood that I was about to do something that would bring trouble.

"The ledgers?" she asked.

"My father's ledgers." I moved deeper into the workshop, past the rows of wooden chests and the iron frame that held the windows. The main cabinet stood in the corner, oak heavy with age, its surface scarred by scratches and initials and the slow erosion of salt-stained hands. It was here, in these cabinets, that I would find whatever my father had left behind—or whatever he had buried there deliberately.

I pushed the cabinet open and leaned in to inspect it. The wood was swollen with moisture, the grain visible in strips now, the earlier pale finish darkened to brown at the edges. Someone had tried to preserve it. That much remained true. But the lock—

*Marta* appeared beside me, her hand reaching out to touch the handle.

"Careful," I said.

"I'm not touching anything." Her grip was gentle but firm. "Ask me if you want to see inside."

I wanted to argue. The truth was that something in me needed to understand why this place had been given to me, why it felt less like a treasure and more like a shackle. My father had built the workshop. He had kept it running. And he had left behind an inheritance that looked, from the outside, like prosperity—ledgers filled with numbers, accounts balanced in the careful handwriting of a man who believed in order. But orders weren't the same as freedom, and he had always been the kind of man who thought he could arrange the world to fit his understanding of it.

"Let me check myself," I said finally. "And then I'll let you come in if you still wish to observe."

She nodded and retreated, leaving me alone with the cabinet and the storm beyond.

I pulled out my pocket knife—a good one, my father's own—and began to examine the lock from every angle. The mechanism looked traditional, brass and steel, but I knew better than to assume. I examined the keyhole, the tumblers, the way the latch responded to pressure. Nothing obvious. No trick or deception that would reveal itself to a casual inspection.

Then I noticed it. A seam in the side panel, barely perceptible, running nearly the height of the cabinet. It was not the design of any standard filing system. If I pressed gently on it, just above the lock, the panel lifted slightly, revealing a narrow gap between the inner surface and the outer.

*Marta*

I stared at the gap. It had been there all along. Every man who had managed the workshops knew how to open this cabinet—the real one, the one that matched my father's handwriting on the ledgers, the one marked "Family Accounts" in faded ink. But my father hadn't locked it. Hadn't written it anywhere. How did someone get inside?

Unless—wherever this secret was kept, it wasn't inside the main cabinet at all.

I replaced the knife and tried again, this time focusing on the seam. There was a slight give when I pressed in just the right spot. The panel slid outward another fraction of an inch, and I saw, reflected in the steel interior, my own hand gripping my knife handle.

*Halfway.*

Not half of the cabinet, but half of something larger. A list of compartments, perhaps, or a series of drawings rendered on the exterior that wouldn't have shown themselves under ordinary lighting. The workshop was designed to hide things. The whole point of building a private reserve below the public light booth was protection—not from the elements, but from the magistrate, from the authorities, from anyone who might decide that the smuggling network running through these waters required a certain kind of vigilance.

But the question was simple: why my father had entrusted me with this knowledge? Why had he hidden it instead of documenting it, explaining it, making his intention transparently legible?

I thought of the last time I'd heard my father speak about the ledgers. Three months prior, he'd been sitting in this very workshop, reviewing the monthly tallies. The candlelight had flickered, casting long shadows across his weathered face, and he'd muttered something about "keeping the secrets that keep us safe." When I'd asked what secrets he meant, he'd looked at me with those gray eyes that had been my mother's in the mirror, and he'd said, "You don't need to understand. You just need to carry them."

Carry them. Not protect them, not explain them, not make them accessible. Carry them. Like a weight on the shoulder.

Now I reached deeper into the panel, and this time I found something that stirred my attention. Behind the false panel, nestled in a shallow recess, was a small leather pouch—worn soft at the corners, the type of item that belonged in a sailor's chest or a merchant's purse. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, were documents. Dozens of them. Correspondence, accounting records, maps of coves and channels that ran parallel to the main harbor, routes for goods too valuable to leave trace.

This wasn't a single ledger. This was a collection. My father had assembled pieces of something larger, stitched them together with care, and hidden them below the observation deck of the lighthouse that was also a fortress against the men in authority.

The implication settled over me like the cold that crept through the workshop walls. This wasn't a business ledger run by a legitimate trading operation. This was evidence—physical, tangible evidence. And whoever had left this in the workshop intended for me to find it.

*He's been building a case.* The thought arrived fully formed, and I hated myself for it. To understand the situation, I needed to grasp it clearly. Yes. My father had been preparing something. Not a defense, but an indictment. Evidence that the harbor magistrate's operation ran deeper than the current understanding—deeper than the smuggling rings that had been exposed in previous harvests, deeper than the corruption that had festered for generations in this port city.

But burying it. Hiding it below the very place that was supposed to serve as a sentinel. Why?

Because hiding it preserved the possibility that someday, the right person would find it. Because my father had been smart enough to think that by controlling the narrative, he could ensure that justice would arrive in a form amenable to his own interests. He had built a system, not a solution—as if the problem could be solved by keeping the evidence concealed until the right moment.

Or maybe—

Or maybe he was protecting me. From what?

By keeping the truth alive in a state of controlled dormancy, he had ensured that if he ever regained control of this location—if the magistrate fell, if the law could be restored—I would be able to continue the work. But that was a dangerous hope, and I had learned, over the course of these years, that the world had a habit of turning against the ones who survived long enough to witness its cruelty.

I turned back to the false panel and studied it more closely. The drawer slots were numbered, though none of the numbers corresponded to the labels inside the main cabinet. Slot seven. Slot twelve. These weren't random. Someone had organized them, perhaps for access by people who didn't belong. Or perhaps for accessibility, distributed throughout the structure so that multiple hands could reach the same files without disturbing one another.

A clue. Perhaps. Though my father had been meticulous about his secrecy, and he would have known that anyone searching for this information would quickly discover the system. So why leave it here? Why not simply tell me what he had done?

The answer, I realized, was simpler than I'd expected. He couldn't tell me because telling me would invite exposure. If he explained the contents of this collection, everyone would know. The magistrate's operation—revealed, confirmed, validated. And someone would need to act. Someone would either enforce the law or exploit the evidence for personal gain. Either outcome was worse than the alternative.

So he had chosen a different method: he had buried the keys in the only place someone might reasonably search by accident, and trusted that the seeds of justice would grow regardless of who found them. He had prepared the ground. He had planted the witnesses. Now it was up to me to tend the garden.

---

The workshop was silent except for the distant crash of waves against the cliffs. I returned to the main cabinet and examined it more thoroughly, looking for signs of further compartments, secondary locks, any indication of how much material my father had truly accumulated over the decades. The original inventory listed fourteen distinct ledger sections—trade ledgers, passenger manifests, repair logs, correspondence with suppliers. That accounted for the bulk of the documents hidden in the false panel. But there were other possibilities.

I moved to the windows, examining each pane for irregularities. The panes were thick oak, cut solid, with no glass in them. Standard practice for windowless storage rooms. But the frames themselves showed wear patterns—scratches and pits that suggested the frames had been moved or repaired more often than any official record would indicate.

And then I found it.

Behind the western window, partially obscured by a layer of frost that refused to retreat no matter how long the sun had been out, was a small brass plate. Etched into its surface, in a script that matched my father's handwriting, were the words:

**For the keeper who bears the weight — do not dig until the ice has broken.**

The date stamped beside the instruction was from eighteen months before today. The same month my father passed. The same month I learned, through other means, that he had been planning his death—some kind of poison, some premeditation that left no immediate trail. I had assumed it was natural causes, the slow decline of an unremarkable life. But now, sitting in this workshop alone, with my father's instructions hanging from a brass plate in a window that no one came to clean, I wondered what else he had been keeping in preparation.

Was this a final message? A last act of love, or the closing of a book of responsibility? The ambiguity was almost funny, given how the world had treated me these past few years. They'd seen my presence at the light, my dedication to the trade, my willingness to absorb risks and dangers. They had never considered what I might have inherited—or what I might owe.

I stepped back from the window, feeling the cold seep through the thin fabric of my shirt. The lamp in the upper chamber flared briefly on its timer, casting long shadows across the workbench. It was the same lamp I'd sworn by every day for years—the light that guided ships through darkness, the symbol of my purpose. But now it seemed heavier than it had ever been. Every flicker told me that this time, the light would keep burning, not because of some grand mission, but because I had no choice. Some debts could not be paid, only carried forward.

Marta had been asleep when I entered. She would wake soon, probably, drawn by the sound of the bell or the shifting of the tide. She would worry, in that way she always worried—quietly, persistently, the way a person does when they sense something wrong. And I would have to answer for that worry, however I could. This was the price of the inheritance I now understood.

*The workshop below the cliff.*
*False drawers.*
*Evidence hidden.*
*Responsibility passed.*

Perhaps the real lesson here was not about the smuggling ring or the magistrate or the ledgers that lined my shelves. Perhaps it was simpler than all of that. The weight my father had placed on me—that was the inheritance. Not wealth or title or influence, but the unrelenting burden of being the keeper of something that could hurt people, and the necessity of living alongside that harm.

It was a burden worth bearing. I knew this. Someday, in this lifetime, I would have to make decisions—hard ones, choices that would echo through the years to come. But until then, I would hold it. I would carry it. And if I ever failed—if I let it consume me or turn to bitterness or despair—the consequences were already written in the ledgers my father had left behind.

I finished my examination of the workshop and began the slow process of reassembling it. The false panel would stay open, as the original lock demanded. No one came to this place except those committed to the work, and I had made a vow—to my father, to Marta, to the memory of Elena—that this knowledge would not fall into the wrong hands. Not permanently. Maybe not forever. But for now, here, in this room with its cold stone walls and its impossible secrets, it would remain guarded by me alone.

The lamp continued its steady pulse. Outside, the storm raged, the Atlantic throwing itself against the cliffs in a perpetual struggle that had shaped the geography of this place for centuries. The water broke over the rocks somewhere below, sending up sprays that caught the lamplight and scattered it into fragments of gold.

I lit the stove in the adjacent cottage and turned it toward the workshop entrance. The heat would warm the wooden floors and discourage the damp that had been creeping into my bones all winter. I would spend this evening organizing the materials, creating an index for the hidden documents, determining how to authenticate and preserve them for future reference. The work was not mine to complete—my father had entrusted this to me, and the terms of that entrustment had been clear about what I was supposed to do. All that remained was to do it properly.

The thought of those documents spread before me—pages of transactions that could exonerate men, could protect innocent lives, could perhaps, in the right circumstances, expose a corrupt magistrate and dismantle an entrenched conspiracy—felt strange. So much at stake. Yet I had learned, from watching Marcus Thorne die in the hold of that ship three summers ago, that outcomes were rarely determined by merit alone. Power operated on other principles. Force. Patience. The willingness to wait for the right moment.

My father had waited. Had watched the law fail repeatedly, the magistrates take their bribes, the evidence disappear in cases that never reached trial. And he had kept digging anyway, accumulating this archive of proof like an acrid smoke that grew denser with time. The question now was whether he had done enough, or whether there was ever enough to do.

I returned to the false panel and opened it fully. The interior was cooler than the rest of the workshop, the temperature dropping noticeably where the false compartment had been removed. Frost crystallized on the edge of the metal casing, delicate lacework against the industrial strength of the iron. Someone had wanted this space to be forgotten. Someone had thought twice before sealing it away.

Above me, through the window, the storm continued its relentless assault. But here, in the workshop, there was a kind of stillness that contradicted the chaos visible from the lantern room. The evidence was safe. The story was preserved. The burden was acknowledged, even if the task ahead was daunting.

Tomorrow I would begin compiling the index. Tomorrow I would study the documents my father had hidden, learning their histories and their implications. Tomorrow I would confront the magistrate's men and the legal system that had allowed them to operate for so long. Tomorrow the work would start in earnest.

Tonight, I would sleep in the bunk beneath the lamp, my mind turning over what I had found, the meaning of what my father had risked his life to create. The light burned. The clock ticked. And somewhere in the depths of this workshop, waiting for the right moment, the evidence of my father's life waited to be used.

That was sufficient. That was enough. For now, the lighthouse stood, and the secret endured.

---

*Dust floated in the beam of light, catching on the pages of my father's ledger as I turned another entry. The numbers were precise—dates, weights, quantities, totals. Each line a small act of stewardship, a record kept for reasons no one could quite articulate. My father had loved this work. He had loved the responsibility, the solitude, the quiet satisfaction of a job well done. And he had entrusted me with the continuation of that devotion.*

*What I did not understand, then, was that the work had outlived him. But I understood this much: the ledgers were the foundation upon which any future action would rest. Without them, the evidence was merely speculation—possible truths that might never be proven. With them, I could build a case that might, sometime, change what had been done in his name.*

*He had prepared for this. Not hoping to be buried, but ensuring that if burial were necessary, the truth would survive.*

*The words on the brass plate still vibrated in my thoughts as I laid down my pen. Do not dig until the ice has broken. Let the winter clear the way. Wait for spring.*

*Spring always comes eventually. Even the hardest stones give way.*

*And with them, the weight would lift.*

---

*Lights off. Beds in. The storm continues its ancient rhythm. Somewhere below, the workshop waits.*

*The mantle remains lit. The watch turns. And the keeper rests, knowing that tomorrow, he will rise again—more burdened than before, but no longer alone in the bearing of it.*
