## 6. Old Halvorsen's Warning

The wind that night didn't howl; it whispered, a low, persistent susurrus through the floorboards of my hut, and I listened as I always do, half-awake, half-asleep, the sea's breath syncing with my own. Winter in 1888 had arrived early, a gray fist clamping down on the Cape, and the fishermen said this was the coldest season since the year the ice came downriver in November, trapping boats in crystal before their time. I’ve outlived most of the men who once sailed with me, outlived the magistrates who wore fresh faces every few years, and yet the pattern of it all remains stubbornly the same.

I dressed in the wool coat Marta had knitted for me last autumn, the stitches uneven but warm, and stepped out into the drifting snow that already coated the path to the lighthouse. The light itself burned steady, a vertical pillar of oil and glass cutting through the bluster, and Dominic was below, as he always is at this hour, checking the lens, polishing the brass, his hands moving with the quiet competence of a man who has made a life of mending what the storm breaks. Marta was with him, her small frame wrapped in a oversized coat, her eyes scanning the horizon as if she too could read the sea's moods the way the older men do.

“Halvorsen,” Dominic called up the stairs, his voice carrying over the wind. “Come in out of it. Marta’s reading the logbook; she says she’s looking for constellations, but I think she’s just avoiding the stew.”

I laughed, a raspy sound that the wind nearly swallowed, and stepped inside. The workshop smelled of pine oil, salt, and the faint, sweet scent of the ledger paper Dominic’s father left behind. Papers. Always the papers. I took a seat by the hearth, the fire casting long shadows across the shelves of jars, nets, and the heavy wooden desk where Dominic’s father spent his final years. Marta looked up, her face illuminated by the lamp’s glow, and offered me a spoonful of stew. I accepted, eating slowly, savoring the warmth.

“You look like a man carrying a secret that weighs more than the anchor of the *Lark*,” Dominic said, setting his tools down. He wiped his hands on a rag, and I noticed how the oil had stained the skin at his thumbs, dark and permanent.

“Secrets are the heaviest things the sea gives us,” I replied. “Some float, some sink. I’ve learned which is which.”

We spoke of nothing urgent, of the weather, of the way the ice had begun to form closer to the mouth of the river than anyone could remember. But the quiet between us was thick, the kind that comes when a man has lived long enough to know that something is coming, though he cannot name it. I looked at Dominic—younger than me by twenty years, yet older by a century in the things he’s had to carry since his brother died and his sister-in-law vanished into the deep. And I thought of Marta, sharp-eyed and quicker with numbers than most men I’ve known, who had already become the heart of this found family of ours.

Later that night, after the stew was put away and the lamp trimmed, I rose to leave. Dominic walked me to the door, Marta trailing behind, her boots sinking slightly in the fresh snow.

“Halvorsen,” he said as I reached for the latch. “If—if things go south, if the magistrate comes calling, you’d tell me. You’d not keep something from me that could hurt Marta.”

I paused, the cold air biting at my face. I turned back, and in the lamplight, I saw the genuine concern in his eyes. It reminded me of the way my own son looked at me before he sailed into a gulf he never returned from. I placed a hand on his shoulder, rough and warm.

“A man does what he must to protect what’s his, aye? Don’t you worry about me. I’ve lived long enough to know when to hold fast and when to let go.” I stepped out into the night, and the wind took my words and scattered them like salt on water.

***

The next morning, the storm had not abated. If anything, it had deepened, the sky a bruised slate, the sea heaving against the lighthouse base with a rhythm that felt almost intentional. I found Dominic in the workshop, surrounded by stacks of his father’s ledgers, the pages yellowed and brittle. Marta was perched on a stool nearby, her fingers tracing the lines of ink as if she could divine the secrets held within.

“What are you looking at?” I asked, setting my pack down on the workbench.

“Father’s accounts,” Dominic said, not looking up. “He never talked about them, not really. But there are gaps. False drawers. Hidden compartments. I found one yesterday, behind the cabinet for the ship’s manifests. It’s false front, Halvorsen. Someone went to great trouble to make it look like part of the original wood.”

I narrowed my eyes, the memory of decades flooding back. The magistrate, Alistair Vane, had been in office ten years now, since the year the herring run collapsed and the townspeople looked for someone to blame. Vane was a man who believed the sea existed to be taxed, and if the tax rolls didn’t add up, he’d make them add up by any means necessary. I’ve seen his type before. A young magistrate with a clean face and a hungry appetite for control, backed by the kind of money that comes from turning a blind eye to smuggling in exchange for a cut. And when the tide turns, when the pressure becomes too great, he burns the evidence and starts fresh under a new name.

“False drawers,” I said slowly. “In my younger days, I’d have thought them clever. Now I see them as signatures.”

“Signatures of what?” Marta asked, her voice small but clear.

“Of a man who plans to drown the truth along with anyone who knows it,” I replied. I stood, moving toward the ledgers. “Dominic, you shouldn’t be poking into this. Not yet. Not until you know who’s digging.”

But Dominic was already pulling at the false drawer’s seam. With a careful levering of a brass instrument he’d taken from his own toolkit, the wood gave way, revealing a compartment lined with oilcloth. Inside, sealed with wax stamped with the magistrate’s own crest—a gull in flight, its wings clipped—I found a stack of vellum pages, densely filled with handwriting that was not his father’s. The entries spoke of payments, of shipments timed with the tides, of names I recognized from the docks: men who’d vanished, men who’d been found dead in the shallows, their throats cut or their bodies weighted with stone.

“By the saints,” Dominic breathed, lifting a page. The ink was faded, but the words were legible enough: *Payment to Captain Hall for run to Norn’s Island, 12 barrels, due 15th of January. Magistrate’s share: 40%.* Another page: *Dead drop at Gull’s Rock. Vane to receive weekly update.*

“It’s a ledger of the smuggling ring,” I said, the weight of it settling in my chest. “And we’re named as guarantors. The letter you mentioned, Dominic? The one from the drowned courier? It wasn’t a random delivery. It was a warning, and a demand.”

Marta’s head snapped up, her eyes wide. “Guarantors? For what?”

“For the ring itself,” I said. “The Iversen name, used as surety. If the ledgers see the light of day, if the truth comes out, then the magistrate falls. And if he falls, he takes everyone who ever stood near him with him. That’s the pattern, Dominic. That’s always the pattern.”

I turned to leave, but Dominic’s hand on my arm stopped me. “What do we do? If we burn them, we destroy the proof, but he still has the power. If we give them to the courts—”

“—the courts are his, or they’re empty enough that he’ll make them his,” I interrupted. “The only way out is through, and that means making him look at the truth until he can no longer pretend it isn’t there. But you must be careful. Vane doesn’t do half-measures. He’ll burn the workshop, take Marta, and leave you with nothing but the memory of a light that once burned.”

I saw the fear flicker in Dominic’s eyes, the same fear that had been there the night his brother died, the same fear that had kept him awake some nights, staring at the ceiling while Marta slept beside him. And I felt the old pull between survival and conscience, the same tension that had kept me awake many a winter’s night, wondering whether to speak or to stay silent, whether to risk everything for a truth that might not change anything, or to keep the peace at the cost of our souls.

“Halvorsen,” Dominic said, his voice steady despite the tremor I could hear beneath it. “What would you do?”

I looked at him, at the man he was becoming, at the responsibility he carried for Marta, for the fishing families who looked to him as much as to the light. I thought of my own son, of the choices I’d made, of the men I’d lost and the ones I’d saved by keeping my mouth shut. And I thought of Marta, who had already lost so much and yet continued to count boats and stars with a clarity that put most adults to shame.

“If I told you to burn everything and say nothing,” I said slowly, “you’d do it. You’d protect Marta, you’d protect the light, and you’d carry the weight of the silence for the rest of your days. Is that what you want? To be the man who knew and said nothing?”

Dominic was quiet for a long moment. The fire crackled, throwing shadows across his face. When he spoke again, his voice was softer, the steel underneath it more apparent.

“I want Marta to have a future where she doesn’t have to look over her shoulder. I want the light to stay lit, not because of fear, but because it’s right. If burning the ledgers buys us a few years of quiet, then maybe that’s what we do. But if it just delays the inevitable… then I can’t. I can’t be the one who lets the truth sink without a fight.”

I placed a hand on his shoulder, squeezing gently. “Then we fight. But we fight with eyes open, aye? No reckless glory, no grand gestures that get good people killed. We bide, we gather, and when the time is right, we strike with the truth, not with anger.”

I turned to go, but before I reached the door, Dominic called me back.

“Halvorsen. Thank you. For not giving me an easy answer. For making me think.”

I smiled, a small thing, and stepped out into the snow. The wind had shifted, and for a moment, the blinding white gave way to a thin, watery sun that caught the frost on the railings and made them sparkle like scattered diamonds. I walked back to my hut, the ledgers heavy in my mind, the weight of Dominic’s resolve settling into the same space where my own conscience had lived for so long.
