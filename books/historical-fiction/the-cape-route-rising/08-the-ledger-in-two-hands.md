## 8. The Ledger in Two Hands

I have always been better with numbers than with people, though I suspect Dominic would dispute that last part, because he has a way of looking at me when I say something sharp that makes it sound as though I had offered him a gift rather than an observation. He calls it my clarity. The fishermen call it trouble. I call it being correct, which is the only kind of right I have ever been sure of, and so when he asked me to sit with the ledgers that winter, I sat, because sitting was something I knew how to do, and because the alternative — whatever he might have to face alone — was a thing I did not wish to picture.

The workshop smelled of pine resin and old iron, the same smell it had held since before I could remember, since before Dominic had carried me up the cliff path in his arms after my father died and set me down among the fishing families who decided without my consent that I would be their own. The ledgers lay on the table in two stacks, one the tidy set of business records Dominic had kept since taking over the lighthouse, the other the cabinet he had found sealed in his father's workshop — the one with the false drawers, the one Halvorsen had warned me not to open. I had opened it anyway, because I have never been very good at taking advice that sounded like a command, and because something in the wood grain had looked at me the way a locked door looks at a child who knows there is something inside worth seeing.

Dominic sat across from me with a candle that burned low enough to keep the shadows deep and his face half-hidden, the way he kept things — half-revealed, half-concealed, a man who had mastered the art of holding back just enough to make you lean closer. He was turning pages with a thumb and forefinger the way you would turn the pages of a prayer book, which told me he understood what we were looking at, even if neither of us said it aloud. The entries were written in a hand I recognized as my grandfather's, spidery and deliberate, every letter pressed into the paper as though the pen were angry. But alongside the entries, running perpendicular to the text in columns I had first mistaken for margins, there were numbers. Not prices, not quantities — something else. A rhythm, if you knew how to listen for it, like the rhythm of tides hidden inside the sound of the surf.

"It starts on the third page," Dominic said, without looking up. "Your grandfather had a way of marking the true figures with the false."

I had already found the third page, because by the time he spoke I had been studying the ink for twenty minutes and had noticed that on every third page the column of numbers changed its pattern — from what looked like random sequences into something that repeated, over and over, like a melody you could hum. "The third page," I said. "You could have just said the third page."

"And I could have just said the truth," he replied. "But I am your guardian, and guardians are not supposed to be efficient."

I smiled. It was not a wide smile, because wide smiles required feeling things I was not always certain I had, but it was there, small and crooked, the way my mouth made it when I was trying to be agreeable without promising to be cheerful. Dominic smiled back, and I noticed that his hand — the hand that had carried me up cliffs and held me during storms — trembled just slightly, the way hands do when a person is holding something fragile and does not want to admit it.

* * *

We worked until the candle burned down to its holder and the dark pressed against the windows like a patient animal waiting to be fed. I had laid out sheets of my own scrap paper, copying the number columns and rearranging them in sequences that made sense to my brain, which was not the same as making sense to anyone else's, but which had carried me through school when the other children were busy being confused and I was busy being certain. The cipher was not complicated, not in the way a grown-up's cipher would be complicated with multiple alphabets and keyword systems. It was simpler than that — a substitution based on intervals, the kind of code a person could break by patience alone if they understood that numbers, unlike people, always behaved the same way if you gave them the same conditions.

My grandfather had used the pages of his business — the names of ships, the dates of arrivals, the quantities of goods — as a cover, and buried the real information inside the number columns. What I found, page by patient page, was not just a record of transactions but a map: which ships came in, which left, what they carried, and where it went. The true ledger was a confession written in arithmetic.

"Here," I said, pointing to a sequence that had resolved itself into a pattern of dates and quantities that matched nothing I had seen in the column above. "This entry — it is not a quantity. It is a name, written as a number. Look at the intervals between the figures."

Dominic leaned closer. His breath smelled of pipe tobacco and cold, and his eyes — the gray eyes that had looked so steady when he carried me through the storm last autumn — were wide now, the way they got when something surprised him and he did not want to show it. "Show me," he said.

I laid my scrap paper over his ledger and traced the intervals with my fingertip. Three, seven, two, eleven, four — a sequence that, when translated through the alphabet my grandfather must have used, spelled out a word I had learned to associate with the harbor magistrate, because the word had appeared in the letter, in the sealed letter that had washed up below the light with its drowned courier and its demand for silence.

"There," I said. "His name."

Dominic did not speak for a long time. The candle guttered and threw shadows across the walls that danced like something alive. When he finally moved, it was to reach across the table and take my hand, which he held with a grip that was careful the way a man is careful with something he has broken once and does not want to break again.

"You have done more tonight than I have done in three months," he said.

"That is not a compliment," I told him.

"It was not meant to be."

* * *

The wind picked up after midnight, the way it does when a storm is deciding where to land, and the workshop shuddered against the cliff as though the sea below were trying to pull it into the water. I had wrapped my shawl tighter around my shoulders and was stacking the decoded pages into a neat pile when I noticed Dominic had stopped writing. He was staring at the last page of the ledger — the final entry, the one that my numbers had not yet been able to break, because it was written in a hand different from my grandfather's, newer, rushed, the ink slightly smudged as though the writer's hand had trembled.

"This one is recent," I said. "Different ink. Different hand."

"I know," Dominic said. "I have been wondering about it since I found the cabinet."

"What does it say?"

He looked at me, and I saw in his face the calculation he was making — the same calculation I made every time I stood at the edge of the cliff and looked down, weighing the distance against the certainty of what would happen if I jumped. "It names the magistrate," he said. "Directly. No cipher, no code. Just his name and what he has done and who he has paid to look away."

"Then why is it coded?"

"Because if it were not, anyone could find it. And anyone could be compelled to read it." He paused. "I did not want you to see that until I was ready to show you what it meant."

I looked at the page again. The smudged ink, the tremor in the writing — this was not my grandfather's careful hand. This was someone writing in fear, or in haste, or in the kind of certainty that comes from knowing you are about to do something that will change everything. The numbers on this page did not follow the old intervals. They were erratic, unpredictable, which meant the cipher had been broken by someone who did not want it broken, or by someone who wanted it broken only for the right eyes.

"Someone tried to destroy this page," I said, because that was what the smudging meant — not accident, but intention, the way a person crumples a letter before putting it in the fire and changes their mind. "Someone wrote it and then tried to erase it, but could not."

Dominic reached for it gently, the way you reach for a bird that has flown into a window. "I think," he said slowly, "that I know who."

The wind screamed outside, and somewhere below the cliff a wave hit the rocks with a sound like a shout that had been held too long. I pulled my shawl tighter and sat closer to the candle, because the light was good and the warmth was good and because sitting beside Dominic in this workshop, deciphering the truth of my grandfather's life with my own hands, was the closest thing I had ever known to feeling that I belonged somewhere — not by blood, not by accident, but by choice, the way a family chooses to stay together when the storm makes it easier to run.

* * *

We did not finish that night. The last page held its secret like a mouth held shut, and Dominic said we would need daylight and a different method, one that did not rely on numbers alone. But we had come far enough — I had cracked the rhythm of the cipher, had traced the map of ships and goods and destinations, had found the magistrate's name buried in the intervals between figures — and that was enough to know that what lay ahead was not a question of whether the truth would be proven, but of whether we would be alive and standing when it was.

Dominic gathered the papers and slid them into a oilskin pouch, which he tucked inside his coat against his chest, the way a man carries something that matters more than his own heartbeat. He looked at me and I saw that he was tired — not the tired of a body that had worked too long, but the tired of a mind that had carried too much and was now deciding, slowly and with great care, what to do with the weight.

"You should go back to the cottage," he said. "It is late, and the storm is coming in."

"I am not sleepy."

"No. I know that." He almost smiled. "But the storm is coming in, and I would rather you were not here when it does. The window near the door — the one with the iron latch — it does not hold well in wind like this."

I knew what he was really saying, which was that he did not want me to see what he would do when morning came, or what he would become. I knew this because I had been living with him for three years and had learned to read the silences the way I read numbers — for the pattern beneath the surface, for the truth that was written in what was left unsaid.

"I will go," I said. "But I will be back before dawn."

He nodded. That was his way of agreeing without promising, of leaving the door open for whatever I might choose to bring back. I gathered my shawl and my scrap papers — the ones with my calculations, the ones that showed the rhythm of the cipher the way a musical score shows the rhythm of a song — and I walked out into the wind that was already beginning to bite, carrying the salt smell of the sea and the faint, acrid scent of something burning somewhere down the cliff, though I could not say where.

The path up from the workshop was slick with frost, and I went slowly, because going slowly was the only way I had ever found of arriving where I meant to be. Behind me, the lighthouse beam turned once, twice, three times through the dark — steady, relentless, a light that refused to be extinguished by the weather or the years or the grief that had hollowed out the man who kept it. I did not look back, because I knew Dominic would still be there when I returned, the way the lighthouse is always there — standing, burning, waiting for the next ship to find its way home.

But I thought, as I climbed, about the last page of the ledger and the tremor in my grandfather's handwriting and the name of the magistrate buried inside numbers that only I had been patient enough to crack. I thought about what Dominic had said when we finished — that the truth was no longer a question of proof but of timing. And I thought about the storm that was coming in, not just the one above the sea but the one that waited in town, in the magistrate's office, in the clerk's polite visits and the folded papers and the promises kept hidden inside silences.

The night was wide and dark and full of sound, and I walked into it with my numbers in my head and my feet on the frost and a certainty growing inside me like a flame — small, but steady, and very far from being done.

* * *

When I reached the cottage, Marta was not in her bed. I knew this because the bed was made and the chair at the writing desk was pushed back and the window was open, which was not something Marta did unless she was waiting for someone or expecting trouble or both, because Marta had her father's instincts and her grandmother's caution, and she kept them both locked inside her the way a lighthouse keeps its light locked behind glass — visible when needed, protected always.

I went to the door and knocked, because knocking was what you did even when you already knew the answer, because knocking was a form of respect and also a form of warning, and I wanted whichever version of Marta was on the other side to know that I had come prepared to listen.

The door opened, and I saw her face in the candlelight — pale, sharp-eyed, the same face that had noticed the unfamiliar ships at odd tides and asked questions adults deflected. She was holding the oilskin pouch I had left on the table, which meant she had followed me back down the cliff without my knowing, which was either very brave or very foolish, and I suspected it was both.

"You decoded the last page," she said, before I could explain why I was standing on her doorstep at this hour in this weather.

"Yes," I said. "It names the magistrate."

She nodded, as though this were exactly what she had expected, which was her way of being correct without having to gloat. Then she held out the oilskin pouch toward me, and I saw that the true ledger was inside, all of it — the decoded pages and the final, trembling page and the map of everything my grandfather had hidden inside numbers.

"Then we finish it together," she said. "Before the storm takes it from us."

I took the pouch from her hands, and they were warm despite the cold, and I thought that this was what found family felt like — not the absence of fear, but the presence of someone beside you who was willing to be afraid with you, and to do the work that fear made necessary, the way a lighthouse stands in the storm not because it is unafraid of the sea but because it has decided that the light matters more than the dark.

"We will," I said. "And when we are done, I will teach you to keep the light."

She looked at me then with an expression I could not quite name — something between gratitude and defiance, something that belonged to the future and not to the past, and I knew that whatever came next, whether it was storm or arrest or fire or all three at once, we would face it with the ledger in our hands and the numbers in our heads and the stubborn, unreasonable certainty that truth was a kind of light worth keeping lit even when the wind tried to put it out.

The river ice groaned somewhere in the dark, low and deep, like a thing waking up, and I realized with a chill that was not entirely from the cold that the storm was closer than either of us had planned for. But the ledger was safe, and the numbers were cracked, and Marta was beside me — and that was enough to stand against tonight, the way a single flame is enough to stand against a long winter, if you know where to plant it and are willing to tend it until the thaw comes.
