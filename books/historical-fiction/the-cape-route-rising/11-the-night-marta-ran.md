## 11. The Night Marta Ran

The clerk came at dusk, when the light was neither day nor night but that bruised purple the fishermen call *the dog's hour*—when the sea turns the color of old bruises and the gulls go quiet, sensing something the rest of us haven't learned to name.

I was in the kitchen, grinding cinnamon for the spice cake Elena's recipe card promised would rise even in damp weather. *Two teaspoons. Not three. Three makes it bitter.* The mortar was cool against my palms, the pestle worn smooth by generations of Iversen hands. Dominic was at the tower, winding the weight that drove the lens rotation—*thirty-seven turns, exactly, I'd counted them once when he thought I wasn't watching*—and the house held that particular silence that settles when he's aloft: the silence of a man doing the only work he trusts himself to do.

The knock didn't rattle the door. It *asked* permission. Three taps, measured, polite as a prayer.

I didn't move. The cinnamon dust coated my fingers, sweet and sharp. *One thousand, two thousand, three thousand.* On four thousand, the knock came again. Same rhythm. Same restraint. A man who knows he'll be answered eventually.

When I opened the door, the wind didn't rush in. It *seeped*, carrying salt and something else—wet wool, pipe tobacco, the metallic tang of a storm still building its courage offshore.

The clerk stood on the threshold. Same man as last time: collar starched against the damp, ledger tucked beneath his arm like a shield, eyes the color of sea glass—pale, flat, revealing nothing. His boots were new. I noticed because Dominic's boots were never new; he wore them until the soles flapped like tongues, then patched them with tarred canvas and swore they were better broken in.

"Miss Iversen," he said. Not *Marta*. Never *Marta*. "Your guardian is... occupied?"

The question was a hook baited with courtesy. I felt it catch in my ribs.

"He's at the light," I said. My voice didn't shake. I'd practiced not shaking. *Count the freckles on his left cheek. Seven. Eight. The scar beside his jaw—knife? Hook? Count the buttons on his coat. Six. The top one strained.* "He doesn't receive visitors during winding."

"Of course not. Sacred work." The clerk's smile didn't reach his sea-glass eyes. "I'll wait. The magistrate sent me with a... clarification. Regarding his generous offer."

*Clarification. Generous offer.* Words dressed in silk to hide the knife.

"I'll tell him you called."

"Please do. And tell him..." He leaned fractionally forward, the ledger shifting. "Tell him the magistrate's patience is not infinite. The ice breaks early this year. He'd hate for the river to carry away something... valuable... before proper arrangements are made."

The threat wore manners like a coat. I nodded once, the way I'd seen Dominic nod to fishermen who'd lost nets to the reef—acknowledgment without agreement.

"Good evening, Miss Iversen."

He turned. Walked down the path. Didn't look back.

I counted his steps to the gate. *Fourteen.* Counted the seconds before the gate latch clicked. *Twenty-three.* Counted the heartbeats in my own throat before I moved.

*Thirty-seven. The same as the winding turns.*

---

The path to Halvorsen's hut wasn't a path at all, not really. A goat track, the fishermen called it, worn into the cliff face by generations of men who knew the tide tables better than the calendar. I'd walked it a hundred times in daylight, counting switchbacks (*twelve*), measuring the drop to the rocks below (*thirty-two paces at the widest ledge, nine at the narrowest*), memorizing the handholds where the rock crumbled (*loose shale at the third turn, solid basalt at the seventh*).

In darkness, with the wind screaming off the water and rain needling my face, the numbers blurred.

*One. Two. Three.* Breath counting steps. *Four. Five. Six.* Heart counting breath.

The cliff didn't care about my arithmetic. A gust caught my coat—unbuttoned, *stupid, stupid*—and flung me against the wet rock. My palms scraped, stone biting through skin. Blood welled, hot and sudden against the cold.

*Seven. Eight. Nine.*

Halvorsen's lantern showed yellow through the hut's single window, a bruise of light against the black. The door wasn't locked—Halvorsen said locks were for people with something to steal, and he'd spent eighty years proving he owned nothing worth taking—but it stuck in its frame, swollen by salt and time. I threw my shoulder against it, gasped as it gave, stumbled inside on the rush of warm air that smelled of tar, dried herring, and the particular dust that accumulates when a man lives alone with his memories.

"Girl?" Halvorsen's voice cracked from the corner where his bed was curtained off. "That you, Marta?"

"Yes." The word came out ragged. "Halvorsen. It's me."

He levered himself up on one elbow, the mattress sighing. In the lantern light, his face was a map of the sea: deep grooves carved by wind and squint, eyes clouded but sharp as the hooks he still tied by feel. Eighty-three years, Dominic said. Eighty-three years of reading water, reading men, reading the silence between words.

"What brings you at this hour, child? The light need tending?"

"No. The clerk. He came to the house. Looking for Dominic. Said the magistrate sent a... clarification."

The old fisherman was on his feet before the last word left me. No hesitation. No creaking joints, no pause for breath. One moment abed, the next standing in his wool trousers and shirt, lantern raised, shadow huge against the wall.

"Clarification." The word tasted of bile in his mouth. "That's what they called it in 'sixty-two, too. Right before they burned Larsen's shed and called it accident."

He moved to the hearth, threw on a handful of peat. Flames licked up, greedy and orange.

"Sit, child. Tell me exactly. Every word. Every look. The numbers matter."

*Numbers matter.* That was Halvorsen. He'd taught me to count the boats in the harbor before I could read the names on their transoms. *Three gillnetters, two longliners, one ketch—wait, the ketch is new, hasn't been here before, mark it down.*

I sat on the wooden stool, hands still gritty with cinnamon and blood. Told him everything. The knock. The steps. The seven freckles, six buttons, twenty-three seconds. The threat wrapped in courtesy.

Halvorsen listened the way the sea listens: completely, without interruption, storing it all in depths I couldn't fathom.

When I finished, he was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice.

"He know you ran?" Halvorsen asked.

"I don't know. He was gone when I left. But..." I swallowed. "There was a lantern. Down at the foot of the cliff. Moving. Coming up."

* * *

The hut had a back door. I hadn't known. Halvorsen pushed aside a hanging sackcloth—*flour sack, repurposed, stitching still visible*—revealing a narrow opening masked by shadow and the angle of the cliff.

"Go," he said, pressing something into my palm. Cold metal. A key. "The boat shed. Third plank from the left lifts. Under it, a lantern. Dry matches in the tin. Row to the far side of the cove—there's a path up the bluff behind the magistrate's summer house. No one watches it. They think the climb's too steep."

"Halvorsen—"

"Go. *Now.*"

His hand shoved me gently toward the opening. Not unkind. Urgent. The same urgency I'd seen in Dominic's face when the lamp failed during the storm of 'eighty-five—*move, think, move, don't stop to feel.*

I went.

The back path was a knife edge. Rock on one side, void on the other, wind hammering me sideways. Rain slashed horizontal. My boots slipped on wet grass, caught on roots, found purchase by luck or grace or the muscle memory of a hundred daylight crossings.

*Count. Breathe. Count. Breathe.*

Behind me, the hut door slammed. Not the wind. A boot heel. Heavy. Purposeful.

*Don't look. Don't count him. Count your steps.*

The boat shed loomed—a lean-to of driftwood and tarred canvas, barely darker than the night. Third plank. *One. Two. Three.* Fingers numb, hooking the edge. Lift. There. Lantern. Tin.

Matches struck on the third try. Flame caught. Light bloomed, small and defiant.

The cove opened below: black water, white foam, the vague hulk of Halvorsen's dory pulled up on shingle. I'd rowed it once, two summers past, Dominic at the oars teaching me the rhythm—*pull, feather, recover, breathe.* The bluff path was a darker smudge against the cliffs across the water.

*Row. Count strokes. Don't think about the lantern behind you. Don't think about the clerk's sea-glass eyes. Don't think about Dominic at the tower, winding thirty-seven turns, not knowing.*

The dory was heavy. Waterlogged. My arms burned by the third stroke, shoulders screaming by the tenth. *Twenty. Thirty. Forty.* The rhythm came back, muscle memory overriding exhaustion. *Fifty. Sixty.*

A shout carried across the water. Male. Angry. Cut short by wind.

*Seventy. Eighty. Ninety.*

Shingle scraped the keel. I scrambled out, ankles twisting in loose stone, lantern swinging wild shadows. The bluff path started as a goat track—*of course it did*—then steepened into a climb that needed hands as well as feet.

*One hundred. One hundred ten. One hundred twenty.*

My fingers bled again, different cuts. My lungs tasted copper. The wind tore at my hair, my coat, my resolve.

*One hundred fifty. One hundred sixty.*

A hand caught my wrist.

I screamed—short, strangled, swallowed by the gale—and swung the lantern like a weapon.

"Marta! *Marta, stop!*"

Dominic.

His face in the lantern light: rain-slicked, wild-eyed, the scar beside his jaw white in the flare. The scar from the hook. *Three stitches. He'd counted them aloud while Elena sewed him up. 'One for the fool who dropped the net, two for the sea that whipped it back, three for the wife who'll never let me forget.'*

"Dominic." The name came out a sob. "He followed me. The clerk. He was at Halvorsen's. He—"

"I know. Halvorsen sent word. A boy on a bicycle, riding like the devil chased him." Dominic's grip shifted from wrist to shoulders, steadying, grounding. "Are you hurt?"

"My hands. My side. I fell on the cliff path."

"Show me later. Now we move. The clerk's men—there were two with him, waiting at the base—Halvorsen said they're searching the bluff path. We go up the old smuggler's stair. Cut through the quarry."

"Smuggler's stair?"

"Your great-grandfather built it. For nights like this." A grim twist of mouth. "Come. Fast. Quiet. Count your breaths. Match mine."

We climbed.

The smuggler's stair was carved into the cliff face: shallow steps, handholds worn smooth by generations of contraband—brandy, tobacco, silk, once a crate of rifles that Dominic's father had refused to touch, *said the money wasn't worth the weight on his soul.* Elena had told me that story once, voice soft with something like pride. *Your father-in-law chose the light over the dark, Marta. Every time.*

Now the dark pursued us up the very stairs built to evade it.

*Match his breath. In. Out. In. Out. Step. Step. Step.*

Halfway up, a rock gave way beneath my boot. I slid—three feet, four—before Dominic's hand clamped my belt, hauling me back like a landed fish.

"Careful," he breathed. "The quarry edge crumbles. One misstep and it's a hundred-foot drop to the breakers."

*One hundred feet. Thirty-three meters. Three seconds to impact. Terminal velocity irrelevant at this height.*

"Okay," I whispered. "Okay. Careful."

We reached the quarry plateau—flat, moonlit, scattered with the bones of abandoned industry: rusted winches, splintered timbers, the flooded pit a black mirror reflecting nothing. Across it loomed the magistrate's summer house, pale and pillared, windows dark.

"Through the garden," Dominic muttered. "The wall's low at the southeast corner. Drop into the lane behind the cooperage. Then the back streets to the light."

"Someone will see us."

"Let them. A keeper and his niece walking home from a neighbor's. Nothing suspicious."

"Your coat's torn. You're limping."

"I'm fine."

"You're not."

A pause. The wind tore between us, carrying the scent of rain and stone and something else—smoke. Faint. Woodsmoke and something sharper. Tar? Oil?

"Dominic." I pointed. "The workshop. Smoke."

He followed my finger. The workshop—his father's workshop, the one built into the cliff base below the lighthouse, where the ledgers lived in false drawers—showed a thin plume against the stars.

"The clerk's men," he said flatly. "Or the magistrate's. Burning evidence. Or sending a message."

"My spice cake," I said stupidly. "The cinnamon. I left it on the table."

Dominic's laugh was a broken thing. "The cake can burn. The ledgers—"

"Are in the false bottom. Third drawer left. You showed me."

"I showed you the *decoy* ledger. The real one's in the wall safe behind the chimney bricks. Only Elena and I knew."

*Elena.* The name hung between us, heavier than the rain.

"She knew," I said. "About the smuggling. About the ledgers."

Dominic went still. The wind caught his coat, flapping it like a flag of truce.

"She knew I kept the light," he said carefully. "She knew I mended nets and counted boats and wound the weight thirty-seven turns. She knew I woke screaming sometimes from dreams of the reef. She didn't know about the ledgers. She *couldn't* have."

"But—"

"She died of fever, Marta. Three days. The doctor came from town, bled her, gave her laudanum, shook his head. She never left the bed. Never saw the workshop. Never—"

"Then how did she know to hide the page?"

The words hung. I hadn't meant to speak them. They'd been hiding in me since the workshop, since the false drawer, since the single sheet of vellum tucked behind the chimney brick—*a page Dominic hadn't known existed, written in Elena's precise hand, decoding the cipher's first key.*

Dominic's face went through changes: shock, denial, a terrible dawning.

"What page," he said quietly. "What are you talking about?"

* * *

We moved again. Faster now. The quarry's edge, the low wall, the drop into the lane—*six feet, roll, shoulder tuck, I'd practiced this off the hayloft at Halvorsen's barn.* Dominic landed harder, a grunt swallowed by wind, but he was up and pulling me toward the back streets before I'd caught my breath.

The town slept. Shutters closed. Chimneys breathing smoke into the storm. A dog barked once, somewhere, then subsided.

We took the alley behind the chandlery, the cobbles slick with slime and something oily. The lighthouse path opened ahead—the long climb up the cliff face, the lantern room a blind eye in the dark because Dominic hadn't lit it, *hadn't had time, had left it dark to chase me.*

"Wait." I stopped at the path's foot. "The light. It's not lit."

"Ships know this coast," Dominic said, hand on my shoulder, urging me upward. "They'll stand off. The bell buoy—"

"The bell buoy's been silent three days. The clapper froze. You said you'd fix it Thursday. Today's Tuesday."

"Wednesday. And I *will* fix it. After—"

"After what? After the clerk burns the workshop? After the magistrate arrests you? After—"

"After we get inside and I tell you everything. *Everything.* No more half-truths. No more 'when you're older.' You've earned the truth tonight, Marta. You ran into the storm for it. You bled for it. You *deserve* it."

His voice cracked on the last word. The keeper of the Cape Route light, who'd faced hurricanes and fevers and the slow erosion of grief, stood on rain-slick cobbles and trembled.

*Count his breaths. Six per minute. Elevated. Fear. Relief. Something else.*

"Okay," I said. "Then tell me. Here. Now. Before we climb."

"Here the walls have ears. The chandler's apprentice sleeps above us. The cooper's wife watches from her window. The magistrate has eyes everywhere."

"Then tell me on the climb. Thirty-seven turns to the top. You can tell me in thirty-seven turns."

A pause. Rain hammered his hat brim, ran down his cheeks like tears he wouldn't shed.

"Thirty-seven turns," he repeated. "And one more for luck. Elena always said the mechanism needed thirty-eight. Said the thirty-eighth was for the keeper's soul."

"She was wrong about the mechanism. I checked the manual. Thirty-seven is exact."

"She was never wrong about souls."

We climbed.

The path was steep, switchbacking up the cliff face—*twelve switchbacks, I knew them all.* Wind tore at us. Rain lashed. Dominic's hand never left my shoulder, a tether, a lifeline, a promise.

"Start at the beginning," I said into the gale. "The ledgers. The smuggling. The magistrate. All of it."

He breathed. Once. Twice. The lighthouse tower loomed above, a dark cylinder swallowing stars.

"My father," Dominic began, voice thin but steady, "was a smuggler. Not the romantic kind. Not the kind who ran brandy for gentlemen's cellars. He ran *people*. Deserters. Political fugitives. Once, a family of Jews from the east—he got them to the ship bound for America, but the magistrate's father caught the last boat. Three children. My father watched them hanged from the pier cranes."

*Switchback one. Two. Three.*

"He quit after that. Swore the light would be his penance. But the magistrate's father had the ledgers—records of every run, every name, every payment. He used them. Controlled the harbor. Controlled the town. When he died, the son—the current magistrate—inherited the leverage."

*Switchback four. Five. Six.*

"Your uncle—my brother, Lars—he found the ledgers in the workshop false drawer. He was going to burn them. The magistrate's clerk caught him. Offered a deal: the ledgers stay hidden, the Iversen name stays clean, the light stays in the family. In exchange... the workshop becomes a waystation. Certain cargoes. Certain nights. No questions."

*Switchback seven. Eight. Nine.*

"Lars refused. Said he'd go to the provincial governor. The magistrate said Lars's niece—*you*—would grow up in an orphanage if he did. Said the light would pass to a political appointee who'd let the harbor rot. Lars... Lars chose you. He took the deal. He ran the cargoes for two years. Then his boat found the reef in the storm of 'eighty-six. The magistrate said it was an accident. The ledger entries for those two years... they're in the false drawer. In my father's hand, but Lars's cargoes."

*Switchback ten. Eleven. Twelve. The tower base. The interior stair begins—stone, cold, spiraling into dark.*

"I took the light. I took you. I took the ledgers and hid them deeper. I thought... I *hoped*... the magistrate would forget. That the old man's death ended it. But the offer last week—half the ledgers for protection—he knows. He's always known. He's been waiting for the ice to break, for the storm season, for the moment he can crush us all at once."
