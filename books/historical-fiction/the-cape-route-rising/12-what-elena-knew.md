## 12. What Elena Knew

The lamp in the lantern room hissed like a kettle left too long on the fire, its flame guttering against the glass as the first squall of the season clawed at the tower. I stood there, as I had stood every night for the past three years, watching the light sweep across the black water. It was a ritual that had once been a comfort, a proof that something in the world still moved with purpose. Now it was only a habit, a way to keep the dark from settling in the cracks of my own mind. The wind carried the smell of salt and something else—something metallic, like old blood or rusted iron. I told myself it was just the sea, but the sea had a way of reminding you it kept secrets.

I had come up here to escape the silence of the cottage below, where Marta was already asleep, her small body curled beneath the quilt Elena had sewn from her old dresses. The girl had grown, but she still slept like a child, her breathing steady, her dreams no doubt of boats and numbers and the kind of safety that only the young can afford. I envied her, in a way. Not her innocence—I had long since learned that innocence was a luxury paid for in experience—but her ability to wake each morning without first remembering that the world had taken something from you.

I was about to turn the key and begin the descent when I noticed the envelope. It was wedged between the brass casing of the lamp and the railing, as if someone had slipped it there in a hurry. The paper was thick, cream-colored, and sealed with a drop of black wax bearing the impression of a falcon in flight. I did not recognize the seal, but the handwriting on the front was unmistakable. Elena’s. My wife’s hand, looping and elegant, the same script that had once filled our kitchen table with grocery lists and love notes. She had written my name—Dominic Iversen—in the center, and nothing else.

My first thought was that it was a mistake, a letter meant for someone else. But the tower had no other keeper, and the only people who came here were the fishermen who brought supplies, and they did not carry envelopes sealed with falcon wax. My second thought was that it had been there for years, perhaps since the winter she died, hidden away like a secret she had never meant for me to find. I broke the seal with my thumbnail, the wax cracking like thin ice.

Inside was a single sheet of paper, folded once. The ink had faded to a sepia brown, but the words were still legible. It was not a letter, not really. It was a list. Names. Dates. Amounts. And at the bottom, a single line written in Elena’s hand: *I found this in the false drawer of the cabinet. I have said nothing. I will not speak. For him.*

For him. For me.

I read it again, and again, and each time the words rearranged themselves into something sharper, more terrible. The list was a ledger page—one of the coded entries from my father’s workshop. The one I had spent weeks trying to decipher, the one that had led me to the truth of the smuggling ring. But here it was, in Elena’s hand, dated two months before her death. She had known. She had known, and she had chosen silence.

The wind outside seemed to pause, as if the storm itself was holding its breath. I felt the weight of the paper in my hand, heavier than it should have been. It was not just paper; it was a confession, a burden, a choice made by a woman who was already dying and yet had chosen to protect me from a truth that would have destroyed me sooner.

I thought of her last days, how she had grown quiet, how she had stopped asking about the ledgers, how she had looked at me with a kind of sorrow that I had mistaken for fatigue. I had thought she was retreating into herself, preparing for the end. But she had been preparing for this. She had known the magistrate’s reach, and she had chosen to stand between me and it, even if only by saying nothing.

The lamp flickered again, and for a moment the room was cast in shadows. I saw her then—not as she was in her final weeks, pale and thin, but as she had been when we first came to the Cape, laughing in the gale, her hair whipping about her face like a banner. She had always been braver than me. Not in the way of grand gestures, but in the quiet, stubborn way she held her ground when the world pressed in. She had known the cost of truth, and she had chosen to pay it in silence.

I folded the page and slipped it into my coat pocket, next to my heart. The storm outside began to howl again, and I realized I had been standing there for hours, the lamp still burning, the night stretching on without end. I descended the tower slowly, each step heavier than the last. The cottage was dark, the fire low. Marta’s breathing was the only sound. I sat at the kitchen table and laid the paper before me, as if it might speak again.

I did not sleep. I sat there until the sky began to pale, watching the sea turn from black to grey to a dull, bruised silver. The wind had died down, but the waves still crashed against the rocks below, relentless. I thought of Elena’s choice, and I thought of my own. I had been so certain that truth was a shield, that exposing the magistrate would set us free. But Elena had known better. She had known that some truths were knives, and that the hand that held them could bleed as much as the one they were meant to cut.

When Marta came down, rubbing her eyes and asking for breakfast, I did not tell her. I could not. Not yet. But I looked at her—really looked—and saw the way she watched me, the way she tried to read my face the way I had once tried to read the ledgers. She was Elena’s child in that, at least. She saw too much.

I made tea. I burned the bread. I watched the gulls wheel above the cove and thought of how they always seemed to know when a storm was coming, how they would rise and vanish into the clouds like ghosts. Elena had loved gulls. She had said they were the only creatures that understood the sea’s moods without needing to be told.

The day passed in a kind of haze. I went to the workshop, but I did not open the cabinets. I sat at the table where Elena used to mend nets, and I traced the grain of the wood with my finger, as if I might find her fingerprints still there. The letter was in my pocket, a weight I could not ignore.

It was nearly evening when Halvorsen came. He did not knock; he simply pushed the door open and stood there, his oilskin coat dripping onto the floorboards. His face was grim, but there was something else in his eyes—something I could not name.

“They’ve sent another clerk,” he said without greeting. “He’s asking about papers again. More insistent this time.”

I nodded, though I did not trust my voice. Halvorsen glanced at the table, at the letter I had not put away.

“You found something,” he said. It was not a question.

I hesitated. Then I reached into my pocket and handed him the page. He read it slowly, his lips moving, his weathered hands steady despite the wind outside. When he finished, he looked up at me.

“She was a wise woman,” he said. “Wiser than any of us.”

“Wise enough to let me walk into this blind?”

“Wise enough to know that some things you have to find out for yourself.”

He handed the page back. “The fishermen are meeting tonight. They’ll want to know what you’re going to do.”

I looked out the window. The sea was calm now, the storm spent, but the clouds were gathering again on the horizon. I thought of Elena, of her silence, of the way she had carried this alone.

“I don’t know what I’m going to do,” I said. “But I know I can’t keep it from them any longer.”

Halvorsen nodded once, as if that was answer enough. He left without another word, and the door creaked shut behind him. I stood there for a long time, the letter in my hand, the lamp burning low. Outside, the first star appeared, faint and distant, like a promise I was not sure I deserved.

* * *

I walked to the edge of the cliff after Halvorsen left, the letter still in my coat. The wind had turned sharp, cutting through my wool like knives. Below, the sea was black glass, reflecting the first sliver of moon. I thought of Elena’s last letter to me, the one she had written before the fever took her. She had said she was proud of me, that I was a good man in a hard world. I had thought she was trying to comfort me. Now I wondered if she had been trying to warn me.

I took the letter out again and read it once more. The names were all there, the sums, the dates. The magistrate’s name was not on this page, but I knew it was only a matter of time before I found it. Elena had not given me the whole truth, but she had given me the first piece, and that was more than anyone else had offered.

I thought of Marta, asleep in the cottage, her dreams untroubled by the weight of legacy. I thought of the fishermen, who had become my family not by blood but by necessity, by the shared understanding that the sea did not care whose boat went down. I thought of Elena, who had chosen silence not out of fear but out of love, and how that love had been a kind of courage I was only beginning to understand.

The moon climbed higher, and the tide began to turn. I could hear the waves beginning to swell again, a low rumble like distant thunder. I folded the letter and tucked it away, but I did not go back inside. I stood there until the stars began to fade, until the sky turned the color of old pewter. I knew what I had to do. It would not be easy. It would not be safe. But it would be right.

And then I heard it—a knock on the door. Not the wind, not the sea. A knock. Three times, deliberate, patient. I turned, my heart already knowing who it would be before I opened the door.

It was Halvorsen again, and with him was Marta, her face pale in the pre-dawn light, her eyes wide with something I could not read.

“They’ve taken the clerk,” Halvorsen said. “He’s gone. And the magistrate’s men are on the move.”

Marta stepped forward, her small hand finding mine. “They’re coming for the workshop,” she whispered. “They say they’ll burn it if you don’t give them the papers.”

I looked at her, then at Halvorsen, then out at the sea, where the first light of morning was breaking over the water like a wound.

“Then we’d better be quick,” I said, and I led them inside to begin.
