## 13. The Fishermen Vote

I’ve kept the lighthouse lamp trimmed through every gale since the winter came early, but I’ve learned that oil and glass alone don’t stop the sea from finding its way inside. The ledger my father left me sits in a tin box beneath the keeper’s floorboards, its pages a map of lies half-buried in honest toil. Marta’s been helping me sift through it when the light’s work’s done, her small hands surprisingly steady over the ink-stained paper. She says numbers have a rhythm, like the tide, and maybe she’s right. But the rhythm’s been broken lately, and somewhere out on the dark water, a magistrate’s men are counting on that silence.

The letter arrived three weeks past, brought by a courier who’d been found frozen half a league down the beach, his throat cut, the parchment clutched in a hand that would never write again. Dominic read it by the fire’s dying light, his face pale as the winter moon, and for a moment I thought the workshop would burn then and there, ledgers and all. But Dominic’s a man who bears weight well — or so I thought — and he folded the letter and put it away, saying nothing to the men who haul nets beside him, nothing to Marta, who’s begun to notice that the boats coming in aren’t always the same ones that went out.

So when I suggested we meet, I didn’t expect much resistance. The harbor’s always been a place of quiet deals and quieter grievances. But the frost’s been harder this year, and the ice’s been thinner, and men’ve been talking in taverns about what happens when the magistrate’s hand reaches too far. I’d mentioned a gathering at Halvorsen’s hut — not the old man’s hut, God rest him, but the one he’s lent us near the cove, sheltered by the dunes and far enough from the road that the magistrate’s clerk wouldn’t think to look twice. I’d said we needed to discuss the ledgers, the winter’s catch, maybe the price of herring. What I didn’t say was that the ledgers hold names. Names of men who’ve taken pay under the table, names of ships that’ve sailed with cargo that didn’t belong to them, and a name at the top: the magistrate himself.

The meeting was set for the night the new moon rose, thin and sharp as a blade against the sky. I arrived first, as usual, checking the planks of the hut, the stack of dried nets, the small iron stove that Marta’s taught me to light without smoking the room out. The wind howled beyond the dunes, sounding like a pack of wolves testing the fence, and I pulled my wool collar tight and wondered if we were all just rats in a sinking ship, each of us gnawing at the wood while the water rose.

First to arrive were the Halvorsens’ old mates — men like Bjorn and Svend, whose families have hauled nets from these shores since before the British crown cared whether a fisherman lived or died. They came in oilskins damp from the spray, boots caked with mud, and they brought with them the quiet certainty of men who’ve survived by keeping their heads down. Bjorn was the first to speak, his voice rough as sandpaper.

“You called us here, Halvorsen. For what? The herring’s run thin, and the magistrate’s been asking questions. Said he’s auditing the Iversen workshop, see if the accounts line up with the tide.”

“Aye,” I said, “and I’ve reason to think the audit’s a cover for something uglier. Got a letter, myself. Drowned courier, washed up below the light. Said the Iversen family’s been named as guarantors of a smuggling ring. And the magistrate wants the workshop’s ledgers. By spring thaw.”

Svend spat to the side, the snow on his moustache flashing white in the lantern’s glow. “Smuggling. I’ve heard whispers. But I’ve also heard the magistrate’s been squeezing the families hard, raising the licences, taking a cut of every catch that goes to market. Maybe he’s the smuggler, Halvorsen. Maybe we’ve been projecting our own guilt onto a convenient target.”

“Perhaps,” I said, “but the letter names Dominic and his late wife, Elena. And it names Marta. They’re asking for the ledgers as proof of silence. If Dominic gives them up, he hangs. If he doesn’t, he and Marta and anyone who’s stood with him’ll be targets until the ice melts and the spring takes the road away.”

That’s when the others began to filter in, one by one, until the hut’s single room was crowded with the faces of men and women who’ve known Dominic since he was a boy scrubbing lantern glass, and me since I was the one who taught him how to read the weather. There was Marta’s mother’s brother, Eirik, who’d come all the way from the next cove, his face set in a line of determination. There was Ingrid, Bjorn’s wife, who’d brought a basket of rye bread and a quiet sorrow in her eyes — her brother vanished last year, presumed drowned, and she’s been watching the magistrate’s men with a suspicion that’s grown sharper with every passing week. And there was young Lars, barely twenty, who’s only recently begun to go out in the boats, but who’s already learned that silence’s a currency more valuable than gold.

The room felt heavy, the kind of weight that settles in your bones when you realize you’re not alone in your guilt, or your innocence. I lit the stove, and the fire caught, casting long shadows across the faces gathered there. The scent of peat and salt and baking bread mixed with the faint smell of ink and paper that clings to Dominic’s workshop, and I felt a pang of something like homesickness for a home I’ve helped build from the ground up.

“So what now?” Eirik asked, setting his mug down on the rough-hewn table. “You’ve told us what the magistrate wants. What do we do about it?”

I looked around the circle, really looking, and I saw the flicker of hope and the deeper flicker of fear. Some of these men, I knew, had taken money from the magistrate’s clerks under the table, had looked the other way when a shipment came in under cover of darkness, had signed papers they didn’t understand in exchange for a few extra coins to keep their children fed. Others, like Ingrid and Eirik and Bjorn, had stood by Dominic since the day his brother died, since the day I took Marta in and made her my own, such as she is. The division was there, invisible but palpable, and I felt the old familiar twist of pragmatism warring with conscience in my chest.

“Some of you,” I said, “have taken the magistrate’s money. Some of you have turned a blind eye. And some of you —” I gestured toward the window, where the dunes blurred into the dark “— have stood by Dominic and his family when the world’ve given us no reason to. The letter names us all as guarantors. That means if he falls, we fall with him. But it also means if he stands, we stand with him.”

A murmur rippled through the room. Bjorn’s jaw tightened. Svend’s eyes darted to the floor. Ingrid’s hands wrapped tighter around her mug. And young Lars, who’d been quiet until then, spoke up, his voice small but firm.

“If the magistrate’s got our names in his books, then we’re all in the same boat, ain’t we? Even if some of us rowed a little differently. If Dominic’s telling the truth, and the ledgers’ll prove it, then we’ve got a choice. We can let him take the fall, and maybe the magistrate’ll leave the rest of us alone. Or we can stand with him, and take our chances together.”

The silence that followed was different this time. It wasn’t the silence of resignation. It was the silence of a decision being weighed, of hearts and minds aligning in a way that hadn’t quite happened before. I saw it then: the moment when a group of men and women, bound by circumstance and habit, chose to become something more deliberate. A covenant. Not because it was easy, but because it was necessary.

“Then it’s settled,” I said, and I felt the words leave my mouth before I could stop them, as if something deeper than my will had taken hold. “If Dominic stands, we stand with him. If the magistrate comes for one of us, he comes for all of us. We’ll hide the ledgers, we’ll mislead his clerks, we’ll tell the truth to anyone who’ll listen, and we’ll do it together. Not because we’ve got nothing to lose — God knows we’ve lost enough already — but because the alternative is a quiet kind of death, where we live with our heads down and our consciences empty.”

Bjorn nodded, slow and deliberate. Svend grunted, but he didn’t argue. Ingrid’s eyes glistened, but she lifted her chin. Eirik extended his hand across the table, and one by one, the others took it. Lars grabbed my forearm with a grip that was surprisingly strong for his age. By the time the circle was complete, the air in the hut had shifted. The weight of the secret was still there, but it was no longer a solitary burden. It was shared. It was covenanted.

We talked late into the night, past the point where the fire had dwindled to coals and the wind had begun to lull. We discussed where the ledgers might be hidden, who among us had the best memory for false passages and hidden drawers — Dominic’s father was clever, I’ll give him that, but cleverness can be unraveled if you know where to pull. We talked about the risks: the magistrate’s men are numerous, and they’re ruthless. We talked about the possibility that the ledgers themselves might be booby-trapped, or that the magistrate might have already planted false evidence to discredit us. And through it all, Marta’s presence lingered at the edges of our conversation, a quiet reminder of what we were fighting for. She’d fallen asleep in the corner, her head resting on Dominic’s shoulder, and the sight of her there — small, vulnerable, fiercely intelligent — grounded us in a way that no amount of strategizing could.

When the first light of dawn crept over the dunes, we rose silently, each of us promising to keep the covenant close to our chests. I shook Bjorn’s hand, Svend’s gruff “aye” echoing in the quiet, Ingrid’s whispered thanks that nearly broke my heart. Eirik hugged Marta before she’d fully woken, and for a moment, I thought the girl might cry, but she just blinked those large, clear eyes and asked if there were more biscuits. I gave her two, and she ate them with the seriousness of a child who understands that some things are worth more than a full belly.

We dispersed like shadows at dawn, each of us going back to our respective tasks, our respective lives. But the meeting had changed something irreducible. The harbor families, who’d once existed in a state of polite coexistence and hidden transactions, now moved with a subtle, unspoken coordination. I noticed it days later, when Bjorn’s boat came in with a catch that was a touch light on the paperwork, and yet the magistrate’s clerk didn’t press him. When Ingrid’s net tangled on a submerged log, and instead of waiting for the magistrate’s salvage team, Lars and two other young fishermen pulled it free without a word of expectation. When Eirik’s wife fell ill, and the families brought meals and firewood without being asked. The covenant was already taking root, quiet and persistent as a winter root breaking through stone.

Dominic didn’t know the full extent of what’d transpired that night. I kept the details from him, as I’d kept many things from him since Elena died — the small acts of defiance, the quiet solidarities, the way the families had begun to look at one another not just as neighbors or rivals, but as something closer to kin. I couldn’t burden him with the weight of our pact while he was still trying to navigate the immediate danger of the letter, the ledgers, the magistrate’s threats. But I saw the way he looked at the fishermen when he walked along the docks, a little more wary, a little more appreciative. And I saw the way Marta began to count the boats not just as a game, but as a way of keeping track of who was there, who wasn’t, and why.

The weeks that followed were a blur of storm watches, ledger deciphering, and the slow, tense preparation for whatever the magistrate would throw next. The winter wore on, ice forming thicker on the river, the days shortening, the light of the lighthouse becoming a more critical beacon than ever. Dominic threw himself into decoding his father’s accounts, his fingers stained with ink, his eyes burning into the false drawers and hidden compartments that my own aging hands had once overlooked. Marta, bless her, proved herself a natural with numbers, her young mind picking up the cipher’s rhythm faster than I’d ever expect. Together, they uncovered entries that made no sense on the surface — transactions with no dates, amounts that didn’t add up, names that were crossed out and replaced with initials. But beneath the confusion, a pattern emerged, a rhythm that spoke of money moving through hands that weren’t supposed to see each other.

The magistrate’s clerk, a thin man with a cravat that was always slightly askew and a smile that never quite reached his eyes, made several polite but menacing visits. He’d arrive in a carriage that creaked like a dying hinge, ask after “family papers” with a tone that suggested he already knew more than he was saying, and leave with a thinly veiled threat that the lighthouse’s light might “accidentally” go out if the ledgers didn’t surface. Marta, who’d been keeping watch from the stairwell one evening, heard the whole thing, her small hand gripping the banister until her knuckles went white. When she told Dominic, his jaw tightened, but he only said, “We’ll handle it. Together.”

And together we would. The covenant forged that night in Halvorsen’s hut had become a living thing, growing stronger with each threat, each near-miss, each small victory of withholding what the magistrate wanted while protecting what we held dear. The ledgers remained hidden, tucked away in a false bottom of a sea chest that Bjorn “found” in his attic and “passed along” to Dominic with a wink that said I know more than I’m telling. The magistrate’s men grew restless, their inquiries more frequent, their tempers shorter. But the families held fast, their silence not born of fear alone, but of a shared commitment to a cause that’d grown larger than any one of them.

It was on the eve of the second storm — the one that would come to be known as Storm Two, though none of us knew it yet — that the full weight of our covenant would be tested. But that’s a story for another night, and another chapter. For now, I’ll sit by the fire, the ledger’s tin box beside me, and listen to the wind outside, knowing that whatever comes, I won’t face it alone. The families of the harbor have chosen their covenant, and I, for one, am grateful for the company. Not because it makes the danger any less real, but because it makes the truth a little easier to carry, and the future a little more certain.

The fire crackles, and somewhere beyond the dunes, the sea grumbles in its sleep. I’ll light the lamp at dusk, as I always do, and I’ll say a quiet prayer for Dominic, for Marta, for all of us who’ve found a family in the most unlikely of places. And I’ll hope — against hope, perhaps — that the ledgers will see the light of day, not as evidence of crime, but as proof that some bonds are strong enough to withstand even the harshest winter.
