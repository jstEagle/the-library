## 15. A Theft in the Dark

The workshop was a cavern of soot and timber, its stone walls humming with the ghosts of generations of Iversen hands. It was a place where the past was kept in drawers, the future in ledgers, and the present in the perpetual smell of boiled linseed and salted fish. I’d spent more nights in this place than I cared to count, learning the rhythm of its creaks, the way the tide sang against the cliffs, and the subtle art of staying invisible to the world that watched us from the harbor. It was my sanctuary, my classroom, my refuge, and, lately, my battlefield.

The night the clerk came, the wind had taken on a persona of its own—an irascible old man rattling the shutters as if he’d been tasked with reminding us that the world outside never truly sleeps. I was the only one awake, perched on a stool near the ledger cabinets, a candle flickering in the draft, its light casting long shadows that danced like specters on the pine planks. The workshop was supposed to be a place of quiet labor, a place where the ledger’s secrets could be uncovered without the prying eyes of magistrates or the clumsy fingers of men who thought they owned the harbor. I’d learned to read the ledger’s code, to decipher the cryptic entries that seemed to be written in a language meant to protect the Iversen name as much as to imprison it.

When the clerk arrived, he was a tall man with a face as smooth as the river ice in early spring—pallid, expressionless, and dressed in the crisp uniform that made him look like a bureaucrat who’d forgotten how to breathe. He carried a lantern that threw a harsh circle of light on the floorboards, and his boots made a soft thudding as he moved, as if he were treading on something he didn’t want to disturb. He paused at the doorway, glanced at the candle, then at the ledger cabinets, as if measuring the distance between himself and the truth. I could feel his presence like a cold draft, a reminder that the magistrate’s reach was longer than the coastline, and that the ledger I’d helped Dominic keep safe was a prize worth more than any coin.

I watched him with a wry smile, because I’d learned early on that the most dangerous men are the ones who think they’re the most polite. He lifted a finger, tapped the side of the ledger cabinet, and whispered something to himself, his voice swallowed by the thick walls. “The ledger,” he murmured, as if the wood could hear him. “The ledger. The ledger.” He repeated it three times, as if incanting a spell to force the wood to surrender its secrets. I could have sworn the wood shivered, but maybe that was just the wind.

He moved with deliberate slowness, as if he were trying to savor every moment before the moment he’d finally claim the ledger. He lifted the top drawer of the cabinet, and my heart did a little jig. That drawer held the true ledger, the one with the cipher that Dominic had entrusted to me. It was the one that could bring down the magistrate, or protect the Iversen name, depending on who held it. He hesitated, looking around, as if checking for any sign of life. I was the only soul in the workshop, but I was hidden in the shadows, my breath shallow, my fingers hovering over the lever that controlled the alarm.

The alarm was a simple contraption—a wooden lever that, when pulled, would release a heavy iron bar that slammed shut over the ledger cabinets, trapping any intruder inside. It was a crude device, but it had saved us more times than I could count. I’d helped Dominic install it, testing it with my own trembling hands. The lever was a small thing, but it held the weight of our family’s secrets. I reached for it, my knuckles white, and thought of the times I’d pulled it in secret, the sound of the iron bar grinding into place echoing like a promise.

When the clerk finally reached for the drawer, I felt the world tilt. He yanked it open with a force that made the wooden frame groan. The ledger’s leather cover caught the lantern’s light, revealing a faint imprint of the Iversen crest. In that moment, time stretched, and the only sound was the clerk’s ragged breathing and the soft ticking of the candle. I could see the code—those cryptic symbols that looked like a child’s scribble but were actually a cipher that only I could read. I thought of Dominic’s words, of how the ledger was a shield as much as a sword. I also thought of the nights I’d spent with Elena, her gentle voice reminding me that sometimes the bravest thing you can do is protect the ones you love without needing a medal.

My fingers moved instinctively. I slipped my hand into the air, the lever within reach, and pulled it down with a sudden, decisive jerk. The iron bar slammed down with a thunderous clank that reverberated through the stone walls, echoing off the cliffs and back into the workshop like a warning shot. The clerk’s lantern sputtered, the flame guttering as if the sudden darkness had taken its breath away. The drawer I’d just opened slammed shut, catching the clerk’s fingers in the hinge. There was a sharp cry, a mix of pain and surprise, and the clerk’s hand flew up, clutching the metal with a wince that sounded like a cat being stepped on.

I felt the impact of the bar as it pressed against the back of the ledger cabinets, the weight of iron pressing into my chest, and I realized I was standing on a threshold—between protection and injury, between victory and sacrifice. The clerk’s struggle to free his hand made a horrible grinding noise as the drawer tried to stay shut. He scrabbled at the lever, his palm slick with sweat, his eyes wild with panic. I could have let him stay trapped, but the thought of him being crushed by the iron bar made my stomach twist. I wanted to be a hero, but I also wanted to be humane. So I reached down, my fingers trembling, and pulled the lever just enough to give him a chance to escape the drawer, but not enough to let the bar rise fully.

The clerk’s hand flew free, and he staggered back, his eyes flashing with fury. He lunged at me, his fingers splayed, his breath ragged. I tried to step back, but the floor was slick with oil and the weight of the iron bar pressing against my ribs made me feel as if I were being squeezed by a thousand tiny hands. He grabbed my arm, his grip like iron, and I felt a sharp pain in my wrist, a burning sensation that made my vision blur. “You… you little…” he hissed, his voice cracking like ice.

I tried to pull away, but his strength was unexpected, a brute force that seemed to come from somewhere deeper than his pale skin. My wrist throbbed, and the pain was a reminder that courage could be as fragile as a candle flame in a storm. I thought of the ledger, of Dominic’s trust, of Halvorsen’s warning, of the promise I’d made to protect the true ledger at any cost. I could have fought harder, but my body was betraying me, my mind foggy with pain and adrenaline. I could have screamed, but the sound got caught in my throat, a sob that turned into a guttural bark.

The clerk’s other hand reached for the ledger, his fingers trembling as he tried to grasp the cover. I lunged forward, my free hand slamming into his forearm, the impact sending a shock through my injured wrist. He yelped, his grip loosening, and the ledger slipped from his grasp, clattering onto the floorboards. I rolled onto my side, my body pressed against the cold stone, the iron bar still pressing against my ribs, the pain sharp enough to make me want to curl into a ball and disappear. The clerk tried to get up, his eyes narrowed, but the iron bar had already sealed the drawer, trapping him in a tiny cage of wood and metal.

The sound of his struggle faded as the workshop filled with the low rumble of the storm outside, the wind howling through the cracks like a choir of lost souls. The lantern sputtered out, leaving us in a darkness that was absolute and unforgiving. My wrist throbbed, and I could feel the blood pooling in my palm, staining the rough wood a dark crimson. I lay there, half-conscious, my mind racing through the possibilities. The ledger was safe—somewhere beneath the iron bar, protected by the very device I’d activated. But the cost? I had a broken wrist, a bruised ego, and the knowledge that the clerk would not go quietly.

I tried to get up, but the pain was too much. I could hear the clerk’s breathing, shallow and erratic, as he fought against the wooden barrier. He was not a man I wanted to see dead, but I also knew that if I let him go, he would return with more men, with more force, and the ledger would be gone forever. I thought of Halvorsen’s warning: “Burn everything and say nothing.” I thought of Dominic’s resolve to protect Marta and the family he’d built. I thought of the ledger’s promise—to expose the magistrate’s corruption while shielding the Iversen name. I was torn between mercy and duty.

The clerk’s hand beat against the wood, his knuckles white, his voice hoarse. “You… little… bitch,” he spat, his words slurring with desperation. I could have let him speak, but the sound of his words was a dagger to my pride. I wanted to prove to him that the Iversen family was not to be trifled with, that even a clerk from the magistrate’s office could be outwitted by a girl with a broken wrist and a fierce heart. I reached down, my fingers finding the lever again, and pulled it just enough to give the clerk a glimpse of hope, a chance to retreat, but not enough to free the ledger. The iron bar shifted slightly, making a soft groan, and the clerk’s grip loosened, his eyes widening as he realized he was not going to win.

He dropped his weapon—a rusted iron key he’d been using to jimmy the ledger’s lock—and slumped to the floor, his shoulders shaking. He looked at me, his face a mask of fury and defeat. I could have left him there, but the thought of him being left to the mercy of the storm, the cold, and the fishermen who might come hunting for him later made me hesitate. I was a child of the sea, raised on stories of mercy and retribution. I could have turned him over to the authorities, but that would have meant betraying the family we’d built, the secret pact we’d forged to protect each other. I could have left him to the mercy of the storm, but the thought of him being left to die made my stomach churn.

I stood, my wrist throbbing, my breath shallow, and I knelt beside him. “You’re lucky,” I said, my voice rough, “that I’m a merciful person. If you tell anyone what you saw tonight, the magistrate will come for you, and you’ll be gone.” I lifted the ledger, its leather cover now stained with my blood, and tucked it under my arm. The clerk’s eyes widened, a mixture of fear and grudging respect crossing his features. “You… you saved the ledger,” he managed, his voice hoarse. “You saved it because you’re… you’re a… a liar.” He laughed, a hollow sound, and I felt a pang of sadness. “Because you’re a… a coward,” I corrected, my tone sharp. “Because you’re a… a coward.” I thought of the many faces that had haunted the workshop—Dominic’s, Halvorsen’s, Marta’s—and I wanted to be something more than a coward. I wanted to be a protector, even if it cost me my wrist.

The clerk tried to stand again, but his legs gave way, and he fell back onto the floor, his hands clutching the spot where I’d struck him. I could have left him there, but I felt a strange kinship with him, a shared burden of being caught between duty and desire. I lifted the ledger, feeling the weight of its pages, the weight of the truth it held, and I thought of the night I’d first helped Dominic decode it, of the way the cipher had been a puzzle that had brought us together. I thought of the night I’d first seen the clerk’s face, of the way his eyes had seemed to hold the weight of the magistrate’s power. I thought of the night I’d finally understood that courage was not the absence of fear, but the act of moving forward despite it.

I stood, my wrist throbbing, and I whispered a promise to the ledger, to the workshop, to the family that had become my own. “I will protect you,” I said, my voice barely audible over the storm’s roar. “I will protect you, even if it costs me my wrist, even if it costs me my life.” I tucked the ledger under my arm, my hand trembling, and I felt the cold stone beneath my feet, the weight of the iron bar pressing against my ribs, the pain in my wrist, and the faint echo of the clerk’s defeat. I turned, my back to the workshop’s darkness, and stepped out into the storm, the wind howling like a chorus of lost souls, the lanterns flickering in the distance, the cliffs rising like guardians around me.

I walked to the harbor, the sand cold under my boots, the tide lapping against the shore like a metronome. I could hear the distant cries of gulls, the rustle of the reeds, and the low murmur of the fishermen returning from their night’s catch. I felt the weight of the ledger, the weight of the truth, and I felt the cost of my courage. My wrist throbbed, and I could feel the blood staining my glove, but I also felt a strange exhilaration, a fire burning inside me that was not the pain but the resolve. I thought of the next step—Dominic would need to testify, Halvorsen would need to speak, Marta would need to heal, and the magistrate would need to be brought down. I thought of the next chapter, the one that would begin with a partial deposition in town, a test of whether the courts could be trusted, and whether the voices of the harbor would finally be heard.

I paused, my breath shallow, and I looked out at the dark sea, the stars peeking through the storm clouds like distant eyes. The clerk’s silhouette was gone, swallowed by the night, but the ledger was safe, protected by a girl with a broken wrist and a fierce heart. I felt the weight of the ledger, the weight of the truth, and I felt the cost of my courage. I whispered to the night, “Next time, I will be ready.” The storm raged on, the wind howling, the waves crashing, but I stood there, my wrist throbbing, my heart beating, my resolve firm. The next chapter was about to begin, and I was ready, even if it meant paying a price I could barely feel yet.
