## 16. Half Truths Before a Justice

The summons came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Storms, fires, drowned couriers — those at least had the decency to arrive dramatically. But the law, I was learning, preferred Tuesdays.

The paper was delivered by a boy of perhaps twelve who wouldn't meet my eye and left before I could tip him, as though proximity to me were itself a contagion. *Dominic Iversen is requested to appear before His Worship's panel of inquiry at the provincial courthouse in Halden, Thursday next, to give deposition regarding matters of harbor commerce and customs irregularity.*

*Requested.* The word had been chosen with care. Not summoned. Not compelled. Requested, the way a man requests that you hand over your purse while his friends stand behind you with their hands in their pockets.

I read it three times by lamplight while Marta pretended to mend a net that needed no mending. She'd gotten good at pretending lately. We both had. The whole house had become an exercise in it — the ledgers wrapped in oilcloth under the floorboards of Elena's old writing room, the true cipher pages sewn into the lining of my winter coat, and over all of it, this careful theater of ordinary life: nets mended, lamps trimmed, meals cooked and eaten at the proper hours.

"Halden," Marta said, not looking up from her netting. "That's where the assizes sit."

"It is."

"And the panel of inquiry. That's the magistrate's own court."

I set down the letter. "You've been reading the shipping gazette again."

"Someone has to know things in this house." She pulled a knot tight with more force than the rope deserved. "Papa used to say the magistrate never asks a question he doesn't already own the answer to."

Your father, I did not say, said many wise things and followed almost none of them. "It's a deposition, not a trial. They ask questions. I answer some of them."

"*Some* of them?"

"The ones I choose."

She finally looked up, and there it was — that look she'd inherited from no one and everyone, the one that made her seem forty years old in a fourteen-year-old face. "And if they don't like the ones you choose?"

"Then they'll be disappointed," I said, "in a courtroom, in daylight, with witnesses. Which is more than they've offered anyone else."

This was not entirely true, and we both knew it. A deposition before a friendly panel could ruin a man as efficiently as a trial — quietly, officially, with paperwork. But Marta didn't need my doubts. She needed my spine, so I showed her that one and kept the other in the cellar with the ledgers.

Halvorsen came by that evening, ostensibly to return a borrowed auger, actually to stand in my doorway looking like a man watching a friend walk toward a cliff edge he himself had once fallen off.

"You're going," he said. Not a question.

"I'm going."

"To give them half the truth."

"To give them enough truth to see what they do with it." I leaned against the doorframe. "If the court is honest, they'll pull the thread themselves. If the court is bought, they'll show me whose hand is on the scale — and then I'll know exactly how much rope I need."

Halvorsen chewed on this, and on the inside of his cheek, which was his habit when thinking. "Forty years ago," he said finally, "a man named Eriksen went before a panel just like it. Gave honest testimony about cargo that moved at night. The panel thanked him for his service." He paused. "Eriksen's boat burned that spring. Eriksen moved inland. Last anyone heard, he was selling nails in Kristiansand and wouldn't say his own name twice."

"And yet here you are, telling me to go anyway."

"I'm telling you to go with your eyes open. There's a difference between courage and blindness, boy, and only one of them gets you buried with your boots on."

He left the auger on the step, which was his way of leaving something behind without admitting he cared. I watched him walk down the cliff path, small and crooked against the gray water, and thought: *he's right, and I'm going anyway, and isn't that always the shape of it.*

* * *

Halden was two hours by coastal packet, and I spent them standing at the rail watching the winter sea do its patient arithmetic against the hull. The town itself I remembered from Elena's funeral — the same narrow streets, the same smell of coal smoke and fish oil, the same courthouse squatting at the top of the hill like a toad that had decided architecture was beneath it.

Inside, it was warmer than it had any right to be. Coal stove burning generously. Fresh paper on the tables. Someone had money, and someone had spent it on comfort, and I noted both facts the way Halvorsen had taught me to note things: without expression, with permanent record.

The chamber was smaller than I expected. A long table for the panel, three chairs behind it. A chair for me, placed at a distance calculated to feel like a dock without legally being one. Two clerks. A stenographer with ink-stained fingers and eyes that never stopped moving.

And at the center of the panel, in the middle chair, Magistrate Voss himself.

I should have expected it. In my bones, I think I had. But seeing him there — not across a courtroom as an adversary but *on the bench*, presiding over the inquiry into his own affairs — did something to me that no amount of preparation had done. It wasn't anger. Anger would have been useful. It was colder than that: the particular chill of watching a lock turn from the wrong side.

Voss was a compact man, silvering at the temples, with the kind of face that juries trusted and accountants feared. He wore his authority the way some men wear good wool — fitted, unremarkable, expensive. When our eyes met, he inclined his head with perfect courtesy.

"Keeper Iversen. Thank you for making the crossing in this weather. Please, be seated."

I sat. The chair was hard. Everything else was soft.

The clerk read the formalities — my name, my station, my obligation to answer truly and fully. *Truly and fully.* I signed where indicated and felt the pen scratch like a small animal trying to escape.

Then Voss folded his hands and began.

"We'll begin with general matters, Keeper. You maintain the Cape Route light. How long has your family held that position?"

"Thirty-one years. My father before me, myself after."

"A long tenure. And your father kept — what, precisely? Beyond the light itself."

There it was. No circling, no warming up. The first real question, asked plainly, in front of the stenographer whose record would outlive us all.

"He kept the light," I said. "And he kept accounts. The workshop handled chandlery, repairs, provisioning for the harbor boats. Ordinary trade."

"Ordinary trade." Voss smiled faintly, as though tasting the phrase. "Your workshop was, I understand, quite prosperous for its size."

"The coast pays well for reliability."

"The coast pays well for *discretion*," Voss said mildly, and let the word sit there between us like a fish hook baited and waiting.

Behind him, the second panel member — a dry stick of a man named Brandt whom I recognized as a customs officer — made a note. The third member, a heavy, sweating justice-of-the-peace named Kold, looked at his fingernails. I marked them both. Brandt might be honest; Kold was already bought, or already frightened, and either way his eyes went to Voss every four seconds like a dog checking its master.

"I keep a reliable light," I said. "Ships come in safely. That's the whole of my expertise."

"Of course." Voss consulted a paper before him. "Then let us turn to recent matters. On the night of November the ninth, a body was recovered below your light. A courier. Do you recall the circumstances?"

"I recall recovering the body. I recall notifying the harbor watch."

"Do you recall whether the deceased carried anything?"

Here it was. The hinge. Everything turned on this question, and I could feel the room lean in — Brandt's pen hovering, Kold's fingernails forgotten, Voss's hands perfectly still on the table.

I had decided on the crossing, somewhere past the second headland, what I would give and what I would keep. Half truths, Halvorsen had called it, with contempt and approval in equal measure. But half truths told to a corrupt court are not lies; they are reconnaissance. You send a patrol forward to learn where the guns are.

"He carried a letter," I said. "Addressed to my family. I opened it, as was reasonable, being the addressee."

"And its contents?"

"An accusation. Anonymous. Unsubstantiated. It claimed the Iversen name had been used — without our knowledge — to guarantee certain shipments through the harbor. It demanded documents as proof of... cooperation." I let the pause stretch. "I judged it extortion. A dead man carrying a demand for papers is not a messenger of law, Magistrate. It's a hook with a corpse on it."

Brandt wrote steadily. Kold examined the ceiling. Voss watched me with an expression of such mild, reasonable interest that I understood suddenly why men trusted him on juries: he looked like the sort of person who would help you carry your trunk upstairs.

"Extortion," he repeated. "Interesting. And did you report this letter to any authority?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because the letter arrived by way of a drowned man, Magistrate, and the last authority I reported a death to sent back a clerk who asked more questions about my family's papers than about the dead." I kept my voice level, almost apologetic. "I formed the impression that certain offices were interested in the letter rather than the crime. I chose to wait until I could speak to a court I trusted."

The silence lasted three seconds. In those three seconds, Kold stopped looking at his fingernails, and Brandt's pen went still, and Voss's smile acquired — briefly, professionally — a hairline fracture.

"You doubted the integrity of local authorities," Voss said.

"I doubted the discretion of one clerk."

"And now? Do you trust this panel?"

I looked at him. At the coal stove warming a room paid for with smuggled cargo. At the stenographer recording every word I said and none of what mattered. At the machinery of justice arranged around a man investigating himself, wearing his own authority like a coat he'd stolen from its rightful owner.

"I trust courts, Magistrate," I said. "Courts are larger than panels. Panels can be seated; courts must be filled. When this matter reaches the assizes — when it's heard in open session, with counsel for both sides and a public gallery — I'll trust it completely. Until then, I'm giving this panel exactly what it has earned: my cooperation, proportioned accordingly."

Kold made a sound that might have been a cough or might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Voss's hands unfolded, refolded.

"You speak boldly for a man with no evidence."

"I speak carefully for a man with evidence," I corrected. "Boldness is for people who've run out of patience. I haven't. I'm a lighthouse keeper, Magistrate. Patience is the trade."

We went another hour. He probed at the workshop — closed these two years, why? My father's health, I said, and then mine. He probed at the ledger cabinets — sold, stored, destroyed? Stored, I said, awaiting probate. He probed at the fishermen, at Halvorsen, at the harbor families, and I gave him nothing but weather and prices and the names of men who owed me nothing and would say less.

Through all of it, Brandt wrote. And near the end, when Voss called a recess to confer with his fellow panelists, Brandt did something small and enormous: he turned his notebook toward me, just slightly, just for a moment, and tapped one line with his finger before covering it again.

I couldn't read it upside down. But I saw the shape of the question in his eyes, and the shape was: *How much do you have?*

And I let my own eyes answer: *Enough.*

* * *

The deposition concluded at four o'clock with signatures and seals and a final statement from Voss that thanked me for my candor and noted that the panel "reserved the right to recall the witness as inquiries proceed."

*Inquiries proceed.* As though it were a tide. As though it were weather. As though the thing moving through our harbor were a natural phenomenon and not a man in a good coat.

Outside, the afternoon had gone the color of pewter, and the first flakes of what promised to be a serious snow were coming down sideways off the water. I stood on the courthouse steps and let the cold hit my face and took inventory, the way I did after every storm:

What I'd learned. That Voss was frightened — not of me, but of the assizes, of open court, of the public gallery I'd named aloud. That Brandt was either honest or playing a longer game than mine. That Kold was worthless. That they knew about the courier, knew about the letter, and had been fishing for the ledgers with polite hooks because they did not yet know where the ledgers were.

What I'd given away. Almost nothing. Almost. But the mention of probate — that had been a mistake, a small one, the kind of mistake that clerks with lanterns and crowbars know how to use. *Stored, awaiting probate.* Stored where, Keeper? Stored how securely?

I started down the steps and stopped.

At the bottom, waiting under an umbrella held by a man too well-dressed for the weather, stood the clerk. My visitor from the autumn. He smiled up at me through the falling snow with the warmth of a well-lit window in a house you're not invited into.

"Keeper Iversen. Safe travels back, I hope. Nasty crossing, this time of year." He tilted his head toward the harbor, where the packet boat bobbed at its moorings. "Though I hear the ice will make crossings impossible soon enough. Perhaps a month. Perhaps less, with this cold coming."

"A month," I said.

"Or less." He adjusted his gloves. "The magistrate wishes you to know he found your testimony *thorough*. And that he looks forward to the assizes as much as you do. Spring is a fine season for settling accounts." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "All sorts of accounts."

They walked away together, umbrella tilting against the wind, and I stood on the courthouse steps doing the arithmetic that governed my life now, the same arithmetic Marta did with her boats and her tides:

One month. Possibly less. Enough time to finish the cipher, if the river ice held and the workshop stayed dry and no one came in the night with fire.

Not enough time to do it alone.

I turned toward the harbor, toward the packet boat, toward home — and found, standing at the foot of the steps with snow gathering on his shoulders and his hat in his hands, old Halvorsen, who was supposed to be fifty miles away mending his own nets.

"How—" I began.

"Followed the packet along the shore road," he said gruffly. "Been standing here two hours. My knees hate you." He put his hat back on and squinted up at me through the snowfall. "Well? Whose side is the law on?"

I looked back once at the courthouse, its windows glowing warm against the storm, its magistrate already climbing back into his seat at the center of things.

"Ask me in spring," I said. "If we're both still here to answer."

Halvorsen snorted. Then he turned up his collar, nodded toward the harbor, and said the words that followed me all the way home through the snow:

"Then we'd best make sure spring comes early, boy. The families meet tomorrow night. All of them. And this time, boy — this time they want to hear it from *you*."
