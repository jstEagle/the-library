## 10. Men in Good Coats

They came on a Thursday, which meant nothing to me then and everything to me now. Thursdays were the days I took the motor round to the back of the livery on Pollard Street and greased the axle while the yard cat supervised from atop a barrel of oats. It was the one hour of my week that belonged entirely to nobody — not to Mrs. Marlowe's schedule, not to the union men who flagged me down at corners, not to the district itself with its thousand small demands. I had the spanner in my hand and grease to the second knuckle when the two of them walked into the yard as though they owned the deed to it.

I knew at once they were Hargreave men. Not because I recognised their faces — I didn't, not then — but because of the coats. Ancoats has its own wardrobe: shawls gone grey at the edges, jackets shiny at the elbows, caps pulled low against soot and rain. These two wore good cloth. Not flashy, not the peacock finery of the dinner set, but the quiet expensive kind that doesn't shine because it doesn't need to. Wool the colour of wet slate, cut close, buttons of real horn. Men dressed by someone whose business was dressing men.

The taller one did the talking. He was perhaps fifty, with a face like a folded document — all creases pressed flat, nothing left open to read. His hair was silvered at the temples in a way that looked arranged rather than earned. The shorter one said nothing at all. He stood near the gate with his hands in his pockets and looked at the cat, at the barrels, at the door of the livery, at the street behind him, in that unhurried way of a man counting exits.

"Mr. Vasquez." The tall one pronounced it carefully, each syllable set down like china. "Yara Vasquez."

"That's me." I kept the spanner in my hand. There is a particular stupidity available to a working man when he wants it — a slackness of the mouth, a dullness behind the eyes — and I reached for it now the way Edith reached for her spectacles. "You'll be wanting the livery office. Round front."

"I'll be wanting you, Mr. Vasquez." He produced a card from an inner pocket and held it out between two fingers. *Mr. E. Calloway. Secretary.* No firm named beneath it, no address. Just the name, printed in type so restrained it was almost apologetic. "May we speak privately?"

"There's nobody here but me and the cat."

"The cat can stay."

So we spoke with the cat staying. Calloway declined the upturned crate I offered him — of course he did; men like him don't sit on crates — and instead stood with his hands clasped behind his back, surveying the yard as if taking inventory for a future auction.

"You drive for Mrs. Marlowe," he said. "Have done, what, eighteen months?"

"Nineteen."

"Nineteen. And before that?"

"Carter's yard. Before that, my father's cart. Before that, Spain, which I don't remember."

He smiled at that — a thin, correct smile, the smile of a man acknowledging a joke he has decided not to find funny. "A local boy, then, whatever the name suggests. That's useful to know. Roots." He let the word sit in the cold air between us. "Mrs. Marlowe speaks well of you. Loyal, she says. Discreet."

"Does she."

"She does. Which is why I've come to you rather than to her." He turned, slowly, taking in the livery wall, the ivy dying brown along its top, the gap in the cobbles where rainwater pooled even in dry weather. "There's a matter of some delicacy, Mr. Vasquez, and delicate matters are best handled through discreet channels. You understand channels."

I understood channels. I also understood that every word out of this man's mouth was a hook dressed as a courtesy, and that somewhere behind his folded face was the knowledge — or the suspicion — or merely the fishing line — of what I'd read in a letter sealed twice over. My heart was going like a loom at full speed, but I made my hands stay stupid. I wiped them on my rag. I scratched my jaw. I let a full three seconds pass before answering, the way a slow man would.

"Don't know about channels," I said. "I know about axles."

"Of course you do." Another smile. "Let me be plainer, then. Certain parties have observed that Mrs. Marlowe's correspondence passes through your hands. Certain parties are concerned that some of that correspondence might be — misdirected. Lost. Read by eyes it wasn't intended for." He paused, giving me room to confess, to flinch, to do anything at all. I gave him nothing but a blank look and a shrug, and after a moment he continued. "Certain parties would be prepared to make that concern worth your while. To make it go away entirely, in fact. Along with you."

"Go away where?"

"Spain," he said, and watched me say it back to myself. "Passage to Bilbao. Or Santander, if you prefer. First class — well, first class as the boats go these days. Five hundred pounds in an account at a bank of your choosing, drawn before you sail. Your father came from Oviedo way, I believe? Asturias. Green country. Better air than this."

Five hundred pounds. I want to be honest about what that number did to me, because honesty is the whole point of what came after. Five hundred pounds was more than I'd earn in fifteen years of driving. It was my mother's lungs seen to by a proper doctor instead of the dispensary. It was a house with a door that locked and windows that shut. For one whole second — I counted it later, that second, over and over — I was on the boat already, feeling the deck move under me, smelling salt instead of coal smoke.

Then I thought of Bess Whitaker standing in the mill yard with flour on her apron, telling me that generosity has a price. Then I thought of Edith's voice, level as a spirit gauge: *If they offer you something, Yara, it's because they're afraid of what you have.*

And then I thought: he knows my father's village. He knows the bank I'd choose. This man had opened me like a ledger and read down the columns, and the only thing missing from his arithmetic was the papers themselves.

*He doesn't know where they are,* I told myself. *If he knew, there'd be no offer. There'd be no conversation at all.*

"Never been," I said. "To Spain. Dad never went back. Said the mountains were too steep for carrying anything heavy." I bent, deliberately, and picked up the rag again, giving my hands work to hide their shaking. "What is it exactly I'd be going away from, Mr. Calloway?"

The question landed harder than I'd intended — or perhaps exactly as hard as I'd intended, since fear sometimes aims better than courage does. Something moved behind the folds of his face. Not anger. Recalculation.

"Nothing at all," he said smoothly. "That's rather the point. A man in your position carries things. Letters, parcels, messages. Sometimes he carries things without knowing their weight. And weight, Mr. Vasquez, is dangerous. Weight sinks men." He took a step closer, and this close I could smell him — bay rum and wool and underneath it something faintly chemical, like the inside of a new hatbox. "We're offering to take the weight off you. All of it. Whatever it may be. A clean ship sails faster."

"And if I don't feel heavy?"

Calloway looked at me for a long moment. Behind him, the silent man shifted his weight at the gate, and the cat, with the superb indifference of cats, stretched and yawned.

"Then think of it as a holiday," Calloway said. "The season's turning. Manchester in November is no place for anyone with sense. Think it over. A week. I'll call again." He drew another card from the same inner pocket and laid it on the oat barrel, weighting it with a farthing he produced from nowhere. "Any hour, day or night. Ask for me at that number and say only that you've thought about the weather in Santander."

He put out his hand. I looked at it. Grease to the knuckle, remember — I held it up and showed him, apologetic, stupid, sorry as any slow lad would be, and he withdrew the hand without a flicker and replaced it in his pocket as though it had never been extended.

"A practical man," he said. "Good. Practical men live longest." At the gate he stopped, half-turned, and delivered the last of it over his shoulder, mild as milk: "Mrs. Marlowe drives to Stockport on Saturday, I'm told. The roads are treacherous this time of year. Do take care of her, Mr. Vasquez. She's valuable to a great many people — in very different ways."

Then they were gone, two good coats dissolving into the grey of Pollard Street, and I stood in the yard with the spanner cold in my fist and the farthing glinting on the barrel like a coin laid on a dead man's eye.

* * *

I didn't touch the card for an hour. I circled it. I greased the axle. I fed the cat half my lunch and told it, in Spanish, things I couldn't say in English. When I finally picked the card up, the number was a private exchange, Ancoats central — the exchange that served the big houses north of the park. Of course. Even his telephone lived in a better neighbourhood than I did.

I drove to Ardwick that afternoon, to the narrow house behind the chapel where Edith kept her second office, and found her at the table with her reading glasses pushed up into her hair and three letters open before her like a hand of losing cards.

"They came to me," I said, before my coat was off. "Two of them. One talked. One watched the gates."

She went very still. Edith Marlowe could go stiller than any woman I ever met — still as the canal in January, still as the moment before a shell lands. "Tell me everything. In order. Leave nothing out because you think it small."

So I told it all: the coats, the card, the five hundred pounds, Bilbao, Santander, the farthing, and last — because I saved it, because saying it aloud would make it real — the bit about Saturday and the roads to Stockport being treacherous.

When I finished she sat looking at the middle distance for a long time. Then she took off her glasses entirely and cleaned them on her sleeve, which she only did when thinking hard, and said, "He quoted your father's village."

"Yes."

"He knows your bank preferences."

"He asked which I'd choose."

"He asked," she repeated softly, "which you'd *choose*. Clever. That way the knowledge looks like yours, given freely." She set the glasses down. "Yara. Listen to me. This is important, and I need you to hear it without arguing. You must appear to consider the offer."

"What?"

"You must not refuse. Refusal tells them you have something worth protecting. Acceptance — or the appearance of it — tells them you're a coward, and cowards are safe. They watch cowards loosely." Her eyes were steady on mine, and behind the steadiness I saw something I hadn't seen in her before: fear, old and well-managed, like furniture kept under sheets. "Take the week. Take two weeks. Be slow, be greedy, be frightened — you won't have to act much. But do not, under any circumstance, tell them no."

"And Stockport?"

"Stockport is cancelled." She said it flatly, and I understood that it had been cancelled the moment I'd walked through the door, that she was rearranging her life around the shape of that sentence the way water rearranges itself around a stone. "I'll write letters instead. Letters travel badly, you see. They get lost. They get read." A ghost of a smile. "By carriers."

"Edith—"

"Yara." She reached across the table and took my wrist, her grip dry and strong. "Whatever happens next, whatever you hear, whatever anyone offers you or threatens you — you hold to the appearance of the coward. Promise me. Promise me as my driver, as my reader, as the stubborn boy who reads my post against all propriety."

It was the closest thing to tenderness she ever allowed, and I hated it, because tenderness at that volume sounded exactly like farewell.

"I promise," I said.

* * *

For three days I performed. I performed at the livery, wiping my hands and looking at the middle distance whenever anyone mentioned the strike. I performed at my mother's kitchen table, letting her talk about Bilbao — she'd heard, somehow, word travels in the Spanish colony faster than any motor — and nodding slowly, noncommittally, a man weighing. I performed walking home past the corner of Great Ancoats Street, where a man I didn't know stood outside the public house two nights running, doing nothing, magnificently, the way watchers do.

And in performing, I learned. That's the thing nobody tells you about playing dull: people perform *around* you. Calloway's visit had marked me, and the mark worked both ways. The barman at the Crown, who'd never given me more than a nod in two years, suddenly wanted to talk about football. The clerk at the coal office asked after my health. Mrs. Ogden, who took in washing three doors from the livery, mentioned — unprompted, to the yard, to nobody — that she'd seen a motor car parked opposite two nights, engine off, a gentleman inside with a newspaper he never turned the pages of.

They weren't following me. They were *surrounding* me, gently, the way a hand closes around something it intends to lift. Every friend was a listening post. Every kindness had a return address. By Friday I had mapped it without meaning to: the watcher at the pub, the motor on our street, the new porter at the goods yard who asked my name twice as though forgetting it, the boy who tried to sell me newspapers I'd already bought. The family's reach wasn't a fist. It was a net, and nets are made of ordinary rope, and ordinary rope is made by ordinary people paid a little extra to tie knots.

Saturday night I sat in my room above the stable with the dossier — Edith's dossier, the letter sealed in wax, the copies I'd begun making in my own cramped hand — spread under the floorboard where the horse's warmth rose through the wood and kept the paper dry. I heard the motor pass below at eleven, slow, and stop, and start again. I lay in the dark and did the arithmetic Calloway had left me.

A week, he'd said. Six days spent. On Monday he would come again, or send, and my performance of greed and fear would have to buy me more than a week, because the story wasn't ready, the journalist wasn't found, the route wasn't mapped. Edith had promised me the name of a man who printed what others wouldn't, and promises like hers needed delivering in person, and every road out of the district now ran through somebody's knot.

Below, the motor passed again. Slower this time.

I lay still and listened to it fade toward the bridge at Beswick, and I thought: *they're timing me.* Learning my hours, my routes, the rhythm of a man they expected to buy. Let them learn it. Let them learn it perfectly.

Because a man who knows he is being timed can choose the minute he stops keeping their time — and starts keeping his own.
