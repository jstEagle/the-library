## 11. Checkpoint Two: The Goods Yard

The goods yard at Miles Platting smelled of coal smoke and cold iron, and by half past six in the evening it was already dark enough that the searchlights made their own weather. Yara Vasquez stood beside the Austin with his cap pulled low and his hands in his pockets, watching a corporal of the Manchester Regiment wave a cartload of tarpaulins through the gate as though he were waving away flies.

The plan was simple, which was to say it was the kind of plan that only seemed simple to a man who had never been shot at. Edith's dossier — her private papers, the corroborating letters, the note naming him courier — was wrapped in oilcloth inside the spare tyre bolted to the back of the motor. The tyre would go north on the nine o'clock goods train in the care of Tommy Ellery, a railwayman who owed Edith more than he could say aloud and had agreed to say nothing at all instead. From Leeds it would pass through hands Yara did not know, into a solicitor's office Yara could not name, and there it would wait until the world was ready for it.

Or until someone opened the wrong crate. Or until a soldier with a bayonet got curious about a spare tyre. There were a great many ways for the world not to be ready.

He checked his watch by the light of a lamp swinging on its bracket over the gatehouse: twenty to seven. The armistice rumours had been running all week like current through wet wire — this morning, said some; Friday, said others; any hour now — and every rumour made the checkpoints tighter and the soldiers jumpier. A man carrying papers out of Ancoats in November 1918 might as well be carrying dynamite, because in the eyes of the men with rifles, that was what truth had become.

"Vasquez." Tommy Ellery came out of the shadow of the signal box, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime before the war. He was a big man gone soft at the middle, with a moustache the colour of engine grease and eyes that moved constantly, cataloguing everything, committing nothing. "You're early."

"You're late."

"I'm exactly when I said I'd be." He glanced at the searchlight tower, then at the gate, then at Yara again. "Things have changed since Sunday. There's a new order from the Provost Marshal. Everything leaving the yard gets opened. Everything." He let the word sit between them like a dropped tool. "Crates, parcels, luggage. They've got a sergeant here now who used to do customs work at Liverpool. He doesn't wave anything through."

Yara felt the cold move up under his collar, though the night was no colder than it had been five minutes ago. "Then we don't send the tyre tonight."

"We don't send *anything* tonight," Tommy said. "That's what I'm telling you. Come back Thursday, maybe the order lapses—"

"There is no Thursday." Yara heard his own voice go flat and hard, a voice he barely recognised as his own. It was Edith's voice, or the voice she'd used on him once, the one that meant *listen*. "Tommy. The people who killed her are closing their hand. Calloway has been to my lodgings twice. If those papers are still in this district when the armistice comes, they'll be ash, and so will anyone connected to them. Tonight or never."

Tommy looked at him for a long moment. Somewhere across the yard a shunting engine coughed and clanked, dragging wagons into the long sidings where they'd sleep until morning. The searchlight swept past them both, painting them briefly white as two figures in a photograph, then released them back into the dark.

"The tyre stays on the motor," Tommy said finally. "I can't take it off you here — too many eyes. You drive in through the east gate like you've business with the parcels office. There's a wagon going north at nine-forty, the eight-twenty's been delayed. I'll be loading it. When I call for a hand with a crate, you come help me, and while your back's turned to the gate, I'll swap the tyre off the mount onto my barrow under the canvas. Thirty seconds. Then you drive out the west gate like a man with somewhere better to be, and whatever happens after that happens to me and no one else."

"And if they open the wagon?"

"Then I'm a railwayman moving a spare tyre for a customer, which is no crime in England yet." Tommy almost smiled. "And if they find papers in it, I never saw them go in, and you never handed me anything, and we're both strangers who happened to lift a heavy box together. That's the whole of my story and I'll stick to it."

It was a thin story. Yara thought of Sergeant Doyle's face outside the police station, the way courtesy had drained out of it like water out of a cracked jug, and how politely Doyle had told him to go home and stay there. Thin stories were all anyone had left.

"All right," Yara said. "Nine-forty."

* * *

Bess Whitaker had told herself she wasn't going to the goods yard.

She had told herself that at four o'clock, when Yara had come to the mill gates at the end of the shift and spoken to her in a low voice among the streaming crowd of shawl-wrapped women — *the goods yard, tonight, if anything goes wrong I need someone who knows where I am* — and she had told herself it again walking home along the canal, and again over her tea, and again while her mother complained about the price of dripping and her brother Danny sat silent in the corner with the particular silence of a man out on bail whose trial was coming.

Danny. That was the thing she kept circling back to, the stone in her shoe. Her brother was charged with the depot fire he hadn't set, in a cell he shouldn't be in, because men in good coats had decided a strike needed a lesson. And here was Yara Vasquez, the Spanish fellow, the driver, carrying the proof of it around in his coat pocket like a love letter, trying to post it north through a yard full of soldiers.

If it went wrong, they wouldn't just take the papers. They'd take him. And whoever they found standing near him.

She went anyway. She told herself it was because she owed Edith Marlowe, who had once stood up at a meeting in Ancoats and said that a mill girl's word was worth a magistrate's. She told herself it was because of Danny. Both things were true. Neither was the whole truth. The whole truth was simpler and worse: she could not stand in Ancoats one more night knowing the truth was sitting in a motor car fifty yards from the men who'd kill to burn it, and do nothing.

She reached the wall behind the signal box at ten past nine. The goods yard was floodlit in patches, pools of hard white light separated by lanes of darkness, and the soldiers moved between them like fish under ice. She counted six at the gate, two on the platform, one walking the fence line with a rifle slung and his breath smoking. Overhead the searchlight turned its slow wheel, and every time it passed over her stretch of wall she pressed herself flat against the cold brick and became part of the brick.

Yara's motor came through the east gate at twenty past nine. Even from the wall she knew the shape of it, the high bonnet, the spare tyre mounted at the back like a shield. He drove slowly, a man with nowhere to be, and stopped by the parcels office, and got out, and lit a cigarette he didn't smoke.

Bess had learned to read men across a bobbin floor — which ones would last the week, which ones would break, which ones were dangerous. Watching Yara lean against his own motor car in the searchlight's wash, she read a man performing calm the way she performed indifference when the overlooker walked the floor. Every inch of him was listening.

Then the sergeant came.

She saw him from two hundred yards and disliked him instantly, which was unfair, but there it was. He was a thickset man with a customs officer's walk, unhurried, thorough, the walk of a man paid to find things. He spoke to the sentry at the gate, and the sentry pointed — pointed at Yara's motor — and Bess felt her stomach drop through the wall, through the earth, through the whole rotten foundation of Manchester.

The sergeant walked a slow circle round the Austin. He stopped at the rear. He put out one gloved finger and touched the spare tyre.

* * *

Yara had known, in the abstract, that this moment might come. Knowing a thing in the abstract, he discovered, was worth precisely nothing. His heart was going like a loom, and he made himself draw on the cigarette, and blow the smoke out slow, and look bored.

"Sergeant." He touched his cap.

"This your vehicle?"

"It is. Vasquez. I do driving work — private parties, mostly. Been waiting on the parcels office; they close at half nine and I've a package to send my sister in Leeds." The lie came smoothly, which frightened him more than the sergeant did. He hadn't known he had this in him. Edith had. She'd have recognised it at once, and perhaps mourned it.

"Your sister." The sergeant's finger was still resting on the tyre. Through the rubber and the oilcloth and the canvas wrap, Yara could feel the weight of the dossier as though it were a heartbeat against the man's glove. "What's in the package?"

"Woollens. Socks. She writes that Leeds winter gets into your bones." Keep it small, he told himself. Small things are boring things. "Nothing worth your trouble, Sergeant, I promise you."

"Everything's worth my trouble. That's what they pay me for." The sergeant straightened. "New orders. All vehicles leaving the yard get inspected. Tyres off, panels tapped, hood up. Take the spare down and lay it flat, if you please."

There was a moment — Yara would remember it all his life, the length of a held breath — in which the entire future balanced on a gloved finger resting on a tyre. He calculated, with terrible clarity: run, and be shot in the back in a goods yard; refuse, and be searched where he stood; comply, and be finished in ninety seconds. None of the sums worked. He was reaching for the wheel brace anyway, because a man must do something with his hands—

"That's my fault, Sergeant, that is."

The voice came from the shadows by the parcels office steps, and Bess Whitaker walked out of them into the light with her shawl over her head and an expression of aggrieved apology that deserved a theatre all to itself.

"Beg pardon, Sergeant — beg pardon, sir — but that's my doing." She was at the motor now, breathless, hands wringing the shawl. "Mr. Vasquez brought my washing — I do laundry work, extra, for the quality — and the tyre's got my basket strapped under it, see, because the rack's the only place it won't get soaked, and I've been waiting near an hour for him to bring it round to me, haven't I, and now I'll have to repack the whole blessed thing in front of God and everybody—"

She was already unstrapping it. Her fingers flew at the buckles, quick and clumsy-seeming, chattering the whole time — *my mum says I'm too soft-hearted taking in washing, it's no wage for the labour, is it, Mr. Vasquez, you tell her* — and the sergeant stood with his mouth opening and closing like a landed fish, because there are few forces in nature more disorganising than a working woman mid-flow about laundry.

"The basket stays," the sergeant managed. "But the tyre comes down. Orders."

"Course it does, course." Bess hauled the tyre off its mount and staggered theatrically under it. "Lord, it's heavier than sin. Here—" and she swung it, all her mill-girl muscle behind the swing, directly into Tommy Ellery's path as he came trundling his barrow past with a crate of parcelled goods. "Oh — oh, I'm sorry, sir — here, give us a hand, would you, before I do my back in—"

And it happened exactly as Tommy had drawn it, except faster and with more noise: the tyre went from Bess's arms to the barrow, under the canvas, atop the crate, in one flowing motion that looked like nothing at all, while Tommy swore amiably about women and weights, and Bess took up the empty basket from the ground and held it out to the sergeant like evidence.

"There. Now you can see everything, can't you. Washing. Look — Mrs. Kowalski's sheets, and them's Mr. Ollerenshaw's collars, and I'll thank you not to breathe on them, they're clean." She thrust the basket at him. He stepped back from it as from a live grenade. Behind him, his own sentries were grinning and pretending not to.

The sergeant looked at the basket. He looked at Bess. He looked at Yara, and for one long second something moved behind his eyes — the customs man's instinct, sniffing at the shape of a story that didn't quite sit square — and Yara met the look with the blank, patient face of a driver who wanted his supper.

"Take the basket back," the sergeant said at last, heavily. "Put your tyre back on. Move along, all of you. And you—" this to Bess, with a customs man's parting shot, "—laundry work. Is that what you'd call it."

"What would you call it, Sergeant?" she asked, wide-eyed, innocent as milk, and held his gaze until he turned away first.

* * *

From the wall, Bess watched the nine-forty pull out northward with its cargo of crates and one spare tyre, and she did not allow herself to breathe properly until the red tail-lamp of the guard's van had shrunk to a spark and vanished past the gasworks.

Yara found her by the fence ten minutes later, out beyond the lamplight, where the canal smell mixed with coal. For a while neither of them spoke. The searchlight wheeled overhead, sweeping the yard, the wall, the dark water, and each time it passed they stood still as posts until it moved on.

"You shouldn't have come," he said at last.

"No," she agreed.

"If they'd taken you—"

"They'd have taken a laundress with a basket of other people's washing, and spent a very dull hour with it." But her voice wasn't steady, and he heard it. She wrapped her arms around herself. "Danny's up in Strangeways. Trial's set for the twelfth. They're saying transportation, maybe, for a fire he never lit, in a depot he's never been inside." She turned to look at him, and in the dark her face was all angles and exhaustion. "So don't tell me what I should and shouldn't risk, Yara Vasquez. My brother's already paying the price of that letter you're carrying. I'm only making sure it isn't paid twice."

He wanted to say something worthy of the moment. What he said was: "Thank you."

"Aye, well." She shrugged, and something in the shrug forgave him his poverty of words. "Get yourself home the long way. Don't take London Road. Don't take any road you've taken before." She pulled her shawl tighter and stepped back toward the lane. "And Yara — the sergeant. He'll think about us tonight. Men like that always do their thinking a day late, but they do it. Whatever your next plan is, make it one he can't catch up to."

She was gone into the dark before he could answer, and Yara stood alone by the canal with the empty tyre rack rattling faintly behind him whenever the wind came off the water, and understood that the papers were safe and he was not, and that the district had grown too small and too well-watched to hold either of them much longer.

He was halfway to the motor when he saw the figure leaning against the wall by the east gate, hat brim low, hands folded, waiting with the patience of a man on salary.

"Evening, Mr. Vasquez," said Mr. Calloway pleasantly. "Fancy a word? I hear you've been doing some shipping."
