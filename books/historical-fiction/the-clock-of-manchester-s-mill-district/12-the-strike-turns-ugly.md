## 12. The Strike Turns Ugly

The night the depot burned, Bess Whitaker was awake with her hands in a basin of lye water, scrubbing the mill's grease out from under her nails because her mother said a girl who went to bed filthy would dream filthy, and Bess had enough of that already without inviting it.

She heard the bells first — not church bells but the alarm at the canal, a hammering iron voice she'd known since childhood, since the year the tannery went up and half of Ancoats stood in the street in their nightclothes watching the sky turn the colour of an open wound. She knew that sound the way she knew her own heartbeat. She knew it before she understood it. And then came the second knowledge, arriving in her chest like a dropped bobbin: *the canal depot.*

The strike fund was kept there. Not money — there wasn't any money, that was the whole bitter joke of it — but the stores. The sacks of meal and sugar the Lancashire committees had sent down. The blankets. The printing press old Tom Ellery had smuggled in pieces inside a coffin, God rest his sense of theatre. Everything the strikers had to keep them through a winter that Mr. Hargreave intended them not to survive.

Bess dried her hands on her apron without looking at them and went out into the dark.

* * *

Ancoats at three in the morning in November is a colour: a grey so complete it seems less like light failing than like light having never been consulted. The gas lamps along the canal burned in little orange haloes, and the smoke moved through those haloes in ropes, and the whole district smelled of wet cinders and tar and the river's ancient sourness. Bess ran with her shawl clutched at her throat and her boots slapping the setts, past the shuttered pubs and the mission hall and the pawnshop whose three brass balls caught the lamplight like a saint's halo hung crooked, and all the while the alarm hammered and the smoke thickened until she could taste it, oily and sweet at once, the taste of other people's ruin.

The depot was a long brick shed behind the coal yard on Store Street, and when she came around the corner the fire had already eaten through its roof. Flames stood up out of the building like men rising from their seats. Heat rolled across the yard in waves she felt against her face from thirty yards off, and sparks went up into the dark and drifted, drifting, drifting, settling on the cobbles and the canal water and the shoulders of the crowd that had gathered the way crowds gather in Ancoats — fast, silent, knowing.

They stood in a wide crescent, forty or fifty of them, strikers and their wives and the old men who had nothing left to lose and the young ones who hadn't yet learned what losing cost. Nobody spoke. The fire spoke for all of them, roaring and cracking, and beneath its voice Bess could hear the smaller sounds: glass going in the heat, timber groaning, and once — she would remember this always, the way you remember a tooth breaking — once, a soft collapse from deep inside the shed, and a woman near her put her hand over her mouth as though something living had died in there.

"The press," someone said. Old Tom Ellery, in his shirtsleeves in November, his white hair lifting in the heat-draft. "The press were in the back room."

No one answered him. There was nothing to answer. The back room was a furnace now, and everything it held — the type, the rollers, the pamphlets waiting for ink, six months of somebody's careful smuggling — was becoming smoke and going up into a sky that didn't care.

And then the police arrived, and Bess understood, all at once and completely, what she was looking at.

They came not from the direction of the station but from Piccadilly, quick and organised, as if they'd been told where to come before the fire had even found its voice. A sergeant she didn't know walked the line of them with a lantern, and the lantern light went along the crowd's faces one by one, slow, deliberate, a man taking attendance. Two constables broke off toward the coal yard gate. Another pair went round the back of the burning shed, toward the towpath, where anyone fleeing would have gone.

"They're not fighting it," Bess said aloud, to no one. "Look at them. They're not here for the fire."

Old Tom heard her. His face in the firelight was the colour of the bricks, and he looked at her with an expression she'd never seen on him before — not fear exactly, but the recognition of fear, the way a man recognises in another man's face the country he himself has just crossed into.

"No," he said quietly. "They're here for us."

* * *

They took two men before dawn.

Bess saw the first arrest from across the yard: Danny Kavanagh, who worked the loading bay and had been on the picket line every day since September, hauled out of the crowd by three constables while he shouted that he'd been standing with everyone else, that he'd run *toward* the fire same as they all had, that he hadn't a match on him, search him, search him — and they did search him, theatrically, turning out his pockets under the lantern while the crowd pressed forward and the sergeant's voice cracked out like a whip: *Back! Back, or you'll all feel the batons!* In Danny's coat pocket they found, miraculously, a tin of matches. Bess watched Danny stare at it with genuine, naked bewilderment, watched him say *those aren't mine* in a voice that climbed and cracked, watched the constable smile — actually smile, a small professional curl of the lip — as he bagged the evidence.

The second arrest happened nearer to her, and it happened to her family.

Her brother Joe had come out when the bells rang, same as everyone; he'd been standing ten feet from her in the crowd, his arm around their cousin Nellie because the girl was crying. When the police pushed into the crescent a second time, they went straight to him. Not searching. Straight to him, as if following a list.

"Joseph Whitaker?" the sergeant said, though he must have known — everyone knew Joe, Joe with his loud laugh and his union badge sewn inside his cap where his mother wouldn't see it.

"What's he done?" Bess was moving before she decided to move, shouldering through, and a constable's arm came across her chest like a bar. "He's been stood here with the rest of us since the bells — ask anyone —"

"Witnesses place him at the towpath gate," the sergeant said, in the flat reciting voice of a man reading words he did not write. "Possession of paraffin was observed."

"*Paraffin.*" Joe laughed, high and disbelieving, his hands already being drawn behind him. "I'm a spinner, you great fool, I smell of oil and cotton and me mam's stew, when would I — Nellie, tell them, I've been stood—"

Nellie was sobbing too hard to tell them anything. Bess got one hand free and grabbed Joe's sleeve, and for a moment — one long, electric moment — she looked into the sergeant's face over her brother's shoulder, and she made herself look, made herself memorise it: the well-fed cheeks, the new boots, the eyes that slid away from hers with the practised ease of a man who had been paid not to see things before and expected to be paid again.

"You know he's innocent," she said. Low. Clear. "And you know who told you to say otherwise."

The sergeant's jaw tightened. That was all. But it was enough, and Bess held onto it the way you hold onto a rope over a drop.

They took Joe away in a Black Maria through streets still raining ash, and Bess stood in the road after the sound of it had gone, and the crowd melted around her by degrees, and the fire burned itself down to a red skeleton against the grey coming of morning, and she did not cry. She noted, distantly, that she did not cry, and that this frightened her more than crying would have.

* * *

By seven o'clock the *Manchester Evening Mail* was on the streets.

Bess bought one from the boy on Great Ancoats Street with coins still warm from her pocket, and she read it standing under the awning of the closed fishmonger's, and her blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

INCENDIARY OUTRAGE AT CANAL DEPOT, said the headline. STRIKE LEADERS SUSPECTED. And below, in the confident prose of a man writing what he'd been handed rather than what he'd seen: *It is understood that certain persons prominent in the present regrettable stoppage at Hargreave's Mill No. 3 have for some weeks advocated direct action... Police were on the scene with remarkable promptitude...*

*With remarkable promptitude.* Yes. Bess almost laughed. Someone had written that sentence and not heard how it sounded.

She read the piece twice, and the second time she read it differently — not as a worker reading news but as a woman who had spent eleven years on the bobbin floor learning to read machines, learning that a machine tells you what's wrong with it by how it sounds. This article sounded wrong in every bearing. It named no witnesses. It quoted only the police and "well-informed sources." It had been typeset, printed, distributed, and sold within four hours of a fire that was still smoking. Papers took days to be this sure of anything. Unless they had been sure of it beforehand.

Unless the story had been written before the fire was lit.

She thought of the letter Yara Vasquez had shown her — no, not shown; *described*, carefully, in the yard behind the mission hall, his voice low, his foreign-dark eyes flicking to the door between sentences. *A warehouse. Dates. The Hargreave name beside a plan for staged sabotage.* She had believed him — mostly because she had wanted to believe that the unease she'd carried since the fundraising dinner, since old Hargreave's fat white hand pressing banknotes into Edith Marlowe's glove like a man feeding a horse he meant to ride hard, had a name and a shape. Believing had been a comfort, in a way. A dark thing seen clearly is less frightening than a dark thing guessed at.

But belief was one thing. Standing in the ash-fall outside a burnt depot with your brother in a cell on a charge of paraffin was another thing entirely.

*It begins early,* Yara had said. Or had she said it? It didn't matter. It had begun early. The dates in the letter had named the middle of November, and here it was barely past the first week, and either the letter had been wrong or the plan had been hurried — and Bess, who knew machinery, knew what it meant when a machine starts before its time. It meant someone was afraid. It meant the hands on the controls were shaking.

Good, she thought, and then hated herself for thinking it, and then thought it again anyway, harder. *Let them shake.*

* * *

The cells under the town hall let visitors in on Thursdays, if the prisoner hadn't been remanded, and Joe hadn't — yet — so on Thursday morning Bess presented herself at the counter with a parcel of clean linen and a face like a locked door.

The matron was a tired woman with ink-stained fingers who had processed a thousand wives and mothers and sisters and had stopped seeing them as people somewhere around her third year of service. She looked at Bess's paper, looked at Bess, and said, "Ten minutes. No touching. He's in number four."

Number four was underground, lit by a gas jet behind wire, and Joe sat on the plank bed with his elbows on his knees looking older than his twenty-three years, and when he saw her he stood up too fast and hit his head on the low ceiling and swore, and that small stupid human gesture undid her more than anything the week had done.

"Bess." His voice came out cracked. "Bess, I never—"

"I know."

"I was stood right next to our Nellie, I never went near the towpath, I don't know where they think—"

"*I know.*" She said it firmly, the way she'd say it to a panicking new girl on the floor, because panic spreads and someone has to stand in front of it. "Sit down, Joe. Eat what I brought and listen."

She told him about the newspaper. She told him — carefully, in the flat uninflected voice of someone reciting a timetable, aware of the matron's shadow at the end of the corridor — about the letter, about the plan, about the name beside it. Joe listened the way workers listen, completely, without interrupting, and when she finished he was quiet a long moment, turning his cap in his hands.

"They'll hang me for it," he said at last. Not weeping. Just stating it, the way you'd state the price of bread. "Somebody's got to swing for that fire, and I'm the somebody they picked."

"Nobody's hanging anybody." Her voice surprised her with its hardness. "Because nobody's letting it get that far. There's papers, Joe. Real ones. Names and dates and the whole rotten scheme in black and white, and there's folk carrying them to people who can print them, and when they print—" She stopped. She had been about to say *when they print, you walk free*, and she had realised, saying it, that she didn't know it was true. Nothing was true yet except the fire and the cell and the sergeant's sliding eyes.

"When they print," Joe finished for her, softly, and gave her the ghost of his old grin, the one that had charmed every forewoman in the district and gotten him moved to three different rooms. "Our Bess. Always were the fighter."

"Someone had to be. You spent your youth being charming."

"Aye, and look where charm got me." He lifted the parcel. "Is there a bun in here?"

"There's a bun."

"Then civilization isn't dead entirely."

She laughed — a short, wet sound, more grief than laughter — and the ten minutes ran out, and she touched her fingertips to his through the bars because the rule said no touching and rules about touching had never once stopped a Whitaker, and she walked up out of the underground into the grey daylight feeling something in her chest that she finally had a word for.

Not despair. Despair was what the Hargreaves wanted from them; despair was the whole design, the fire and the arrests and the headlines, the great machine for manufacturing hopelessness at industrial scale. What Bess felt was the opposite of the design. It was rage, yes — rage with a temperature she could feel in her ears — but rage refined now, concentrated, drawn off clean the way the chemist drew spirits from the mash. Rage with a purpose attached, like a belt on a wheel.

They had made a mistake, she thought, climbing the steps into the cold. They had burned a building and stolen her brother and thought that would finish it. But they had never worked a mill floor. They didn't know what happens when you mistreat the women of a district like this one. You don't break them. You *organise* them.

* * *

She found Yara Vasquez that afternoon, because she had reasoned it out the way she reasoned everything: methodically, walking it through step by step on the long trudge home from the cells. If there were papers, and if the papers needed moving, and if the district was about to fill with soldiers and checkpoints the way a boiler fills with steam, then the man carrying them would need help from inside the mills — from the laundry carts, the coal deliveries, the hundred small unsearched arteries of Hargreave's own operation. And if he was half as clever as she thought he was, he would come looking for her.

So she saved him the trouble. She left a note in the crack in the mission hall wall where the strike committee left messages — a note written in pencil, unsigned, in the coded plainness mill girls used when the overlookers might read: *Your machine needs a new drive-belt. Ask for the bobbin girl at the No. 3 gate, Thursday, six o'clock.*

He was there. Of course he was there, standing in the shadow of the gate arch with his collar up and his cap low, looking for all the world like a delivery driver with time to kill, and when she came out with the last shift he fell into step beside her without a word, and they walked together into the streets, which were seething.

That was the only word for it. Ancoats seethed. On every corner, knots of strikers argued and gesticulated; outside the union office a crowd had gathered around a man reading the *Mail*'s article aloud in a voice gathering fury with each sentence; two women were chalking on the wall of the coal yard — PARAFFIN INDEED — and a constable stood twenty feet away studying the sky, deciding, visibly, whether he was paid enough to care. Somewhere a barrel organ played a music-hall tune, jauntily, absurdly, as though the world had not tilted.

"They took Joe Whitaker," Bess said, when they were clear of the corners. "My brother. Paraffin. Matches in his pocket that weren't his. The whole pantomime."

Yara stopped walking. In the gaslight his face was very still, and very pale, and she saw in it — plainly, unmistakably — the thing she had come to confirm. Guilt. Not the guilt of a guilty man; the guilt of a man who had read tomorrow's weather and watched it arrive on schedule.

"It's early," he said. "The letter said the middle of the month. This is days ahead of—"

"Then whoever's driving it is rattled." She watched him absorb that. "Something's spooked them, Vasquez. Something's made them hurry. And hurrying men make mistakes." She stepped closer, and lowered her voice to the register she used on the floor, under the noise, when what she said mattered. "You've got papers. I've got a laundry cart that goes through that mill twice a day and girls on every floor who owe the Hargreaves nothing but their twelve hours. My brother's in a cell for a crime they wrote the script for. So here's what happens now: you stop carrying this alone, and you start telling me where it goes next."

Yara looked at her for a long moment. Behind them the organ ground on; ahead, down the darkening street, the burnt depot smoked faintly against the last of the light, and beyond it the towers of Mill No. 3 rose black against a sky the colour of dirty wool.

"You understand what you're asking," he said quietly. "Calloway knows my face now. After the goods yard, he'll be watching everyone I've spoken to. You'd be putting yourself—"

"My brother's in number four," Bess said. "You think there's a version of this where I stay safe?"

He had no answer for that. She hadn't expected one.

"Thursday," she said instead. "The laundry cart goes out at five. Come to the yard gate and bring whatever you want to live longer than this winter." She turned to go, then paused, and looked back at him once, and gave him the only comfort she had to give, which was the truth: "They started this thinking fire would frighten us. Let them find out what they've actually started."

She walked home through the seething streets with her head up and her brother's bun-money still in her pocket, and behind her the district murmured and gathered and sharpened like a whetstone finding its edge, and above it all the chimneys of Hargreave's empire stood against the dark, breathing their smoke, unaware — she promised herself they were unaware — of what was coming up the road toward them, folded in linen, carried by women.
