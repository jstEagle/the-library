## 1. The Summons

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which was the day the post came to Ciutadella, and it arrived the way all important things arrive in Jude Vane's life — too late, and smelling faintly of salt.

He found it on the workshop floor when he opened the shutters at seven, a white rectangle lying face-down on the flagstones just inside the door, as though it had been slipped through the gap in the night and had drifted there on some current of air. The morning was still grey. Beyond the workshop's open front, the Plaça des Born stood empty, its obelisk a black finger against the sky, and somewhere down toward the old port a donkey was complaining to no one. Jude bent, picked the letter up, and turned it over.

The handwriting stopped him where he stood.

It was not the handwriting itself — he had never seen it before — but the character of it, the upright, unhurried, faintly ecclesiastical script that his master, old Tomàs Ferrer, had used on every work order, every invoice, every label pasted to a drawer of screws for the last forty years. Jude had learned his letters copying that hand, tracing it in the dust of the bench until Tomàs cuffed his ear and told him a clockmaker's hand should be as steady as his pulse. This letter might have been written by Tomàs. It was not signed by Tomàs. Tomàs had been dead for eleven weeks, buried up at the cemetery on the rise where the wind came off the plain and the cypress trees leaned like listeners, and the workshop had been Jude's alone ever since — a boy of twenty-one keeping a dead man's shop alive out of habit, because the alternative was to think about the war, and he had become very skilled at not thinking about the war.

The envelope was addressed to *Tomàs Ferrer, Rellotger, carrer del Seminari, Ciutadella*. The postmark was smudged, but the franking was British — he could make out the crown and the letters G.R. — and beneath it, in a second hand, smaller, clipped, the kind of hand that wrote orders: *To be delivered into the hands of the apprentice, J. Vane. Urgent.*

Jude stood in the doorway a long moment with the letter in his hand, feeling the morning cool against his neck, and understood that the island, which had let him hide for three years, had finally come looking for him.

* * *

He did not open it at once. This was not superstition — or not only superstition. It was training. Tomàs had taught him that a movement must be understood before it is touched: read the train, count the wheels, find where the power goes and where it is lost. A letter, too, was a mechanism. He set it on the bench beside the lamp, weighted it with the brass rule, and went about the morning's work as though nothing had changed.

The work was the clock in the case against the north wall, a tall eight-day regulator that had belonged to the Convent of Santa Clara until the convent sold it to pay a roofer, and which Jude was restoring on a promise made to a nun who had died before the promise was kept. Its escapement lay in pieces on a chamois cloth: anchor, pallets, the suspension spring he had been thinning for three days with files finer than needles. He took up the spring, held it to the light, and found his hands were not steady. He set it down again.

The workshop smelled as it always smelled — oil and old wood, the iron-and-honey scent of brass filings, the ghost of the pipe tobacco Tomàs had smoked for forty years and which had soaked so deeply into the beams that on damp days the room seemed to remember him. His tools still hung on their board in the order of his working day: the pliers first, because he was always breaking something; the screwdrivers in descending size; the loupe on its hook at eye level, so that reaching for it was a kind of greeting. Jude had not moved any of it. He dusted around the dead man's habits the way one dusts around a sleeping cat.

Through the wall he could hear the Seminari clock striking the quarter — a thin, cracked note, the third bell gone flat since before the war. He had offered twice to service it. Twice the rector had said there was no money, and both times Jude had done it anyway, and both times the rector had paid him in figs and absolution.

At half past eight he made coffee on the spirit burner, and while it boiled he finally broke the seal.

* * *

The letter was written in Spanish, formally, in the ecclesiastical hand, and it began with courtesies so elaborate they took up half the first page — *To the esteemed successor of the late and much-lamented Master Ferrer, whose skill was known wherever men care for the measurement of time* — before it arrived, with the suddenness of a dropped weight, at its purpose.

The Naval Observatory at Port Mahon. The master clock. It was failing — not stopped, which would be a mercy, but failing: losing time, gaining it, wandering through the days like a drunkard, and the observatory's time signals, on which the fleet's chronometers were rated, had become an embarrassment that the garrison could no longer ignore. The Royal Navy had occupied Menorca since the beginning of the war — the great harbour with its mile-deep ria was the fleet's southern anchor, its coaling station, its watching eye — and the observatory's time ball on the headland dropped each noon for the shipping, and lately it had been dropping on a lie.

*The clock is a Lepaute,* the letter continued, *of the type your master knew, for it was he who serviced it, in the years before the present war, and before that.* Jude read that sentence twice. Tomàs had never spoken of Port Mahon. Tomàs had never, in eleven years of apprenticeship, spoken of anything before Ciutadella — not of his youth in Palma, not of the years the workshop's ledgers went silent, the gap of a decade that Jude had once asked about and been told, with a look he had never forgotten, that some wheels were better left uncounted.

*The garrison requires a clockmaker of discretion,* the letter said. *Discretion, and the ability to be silent regarding what he observes in the course of his work. You will be lodged at the observatory. You will be paid from naval funds. You will present yourself to the Officer Commanding within the week. A boat is arranged for Thursday, the tide permitting.*

It was signed with a name and a title: *Lt. H. Corvane, R.N., Naval Intelligence, Port Mahon.* Naval Intelligence. Jude sat with the word a while, turning it like a wheel of uncertain function. What did intelligence want with a clock? What had Tomàs Ferrer wanted with Naval Intelligence, years ago, in the silent decade?

Outside, the morning had come fully up. Sun lay along the flagstones of the Plaça, and the first market carts were rattling through, and a woman was calling the price of sardines in the rising, falling way of the port. The world went on being ordinary, which was the world's great talent. Jude looked at the escapement pieces on their cloth, at the suspension spring he had not the steadiness to file, and understood that the letter had done what no letter should be able to do: it had stopped his train.

He thought about his brothers.

He thought about them the way he thought about everything now — briefly, and from a distance, and with the sensation of touching a hot iron. William had gone first, in '15, at Loos, a rifleman in a battalion raised from the Yorkshire mill towns where their father had been born before emigrating to the island to build boats. Edward had gone in '17, at Passchendaele, though *gone* was a generous word for what happened at Passchendaele; the letter from the War Office had used the word *missing*, and their mother had used no words at all, and had died in the spring of this year with Edward's photograph in her hand and the shutters closed against a Menorcan sun she no longer seemed to see. Jude had been exempted — one son remaining to a widowed mother, and then, after the spring, one son remaining to no one — and the exemption sat on him like a coat made for a bigger man. The other men of his street had gone. The ones who came back came back wrong, in pieces, or silent, and when they saw him in the street they looked at him with a pity that was worse than accusation. So he had made himself small. He had made himself a boy bent over a bench, all elbows and lamp-light, a person the war had overlooked. The workshop was his trench, and he had held it for three years.

And now a lieutenant of Naval Intelligence wanted him at Port Mahon, where the fleet lay at anchor in the loveliest harbour in the Mediterranean, where the war was not a word in a letter but a presence — grey ships, grey uniforms, the daily gun salutes, the wounded carried down gangways on litters with their faces toward the sky.

He should refuse. He knew he should refuse. He could write back — or not write back, which was simpler — and go on filing his spring, and finish the nun's clock, and grow old in the smell of brass and pipe tobacco, invisible, safe.

He looked at the letter again. *It was he who serviced it, in the years before the present war, and before that.*

The clockmaker's instinct, the one Tomàs had drilled into him — *find where the power goes* — would not leave him alone. There was a wheel here he could not see. A dead man's secret, a naval lieutenant's urgency, a master clock failing at the heart of a war harbour, and a summons addressed first to Tomàs Ferrer and delivered, by someone who knew he was dead, into the hands of his apprentice. Someone at the other end of this letter knew more about Tomàs Ferrer than Jude did. And Jude had loved Tomàs — had loved him the way one loves a difficult father, with grievance and gratitude in equal measure — and the dead man had left him nothing but the tools and the silence, and the silence had begun, lately, to feel less like peace and more like a room with a locked drawer.

He got up, took his coat from the peg, and went out into the Plaça.

* * *

He walked, because walking helped. He walked down through the old town, past the Seminari with its flat third bell, past the Cathedral where the beggar Miguel sat in his eternal place by the north door and nodded to him — *Jude, fill, you look like a man with a letter in his pocket* — and out along the ramparts to where the city fell away toward the sea. From the bastions he could see the whole southern water: the grey-green swell, the fishing boats coming in with the morning's catch, and far out, a smudge of smoke that might have been a steamer or might have been nothing.

Menorca was a strange island to be at war from. It had been British twice before — the eighteenth century was a long argument between the crowns over this rock — and the Menorcans had learned what occupied peoples learn: to be pleasant, to be patient, and to keep their true accounts elsewhere. The garrison in Mahon was polite and the islanders were politer, and beneath the politeness, everything was weighed. Jude had grown up in that weighing. His father had been English, his mother Menorcan, and he had spent his childhood translating between two kitchens, two prayers, two ways of being silent. He was fluent in silence. It was perhaps the only thing he was fluent in.

He stood at the parapet until the sun was high, and then he did what he always did when a mechanism defeated him. He went back to the bench.

* * *

The afternoon went to the regulator. He filed the suspension spring, and the filing steadied him, as it always did — the small hiss of steel, the blue curls of metal falling away, the spring growing thinner under the file until it held the light like a breath. By four o'clock the escapement was back in the movement, and by five the clock was running, and he sat and listened to it the way other men listened to music: the tick, and the tick, and the tick, each one a small promise kept.

It was while he listened that he noticed the sound was wrong. Not the clock — the clock was perfect. The sound beneath the clock. A scratching, faint, irregular, coming not from the movement in the case but from inside the case itself, behind the backboard, where no sound should be.

He opened the case door. He put his ear to the wood. The scratching came again — a tiny, rhythmic drag, like a paw, or a spring slipping its seat, or — he knew this sound — a piece of paper moving in a draught.

Tomàs's regulator had a false backboard. Jude had known this for years; every apprentice finds his master's hiding places eventually, and he had found this one at nineteen, and had looked inside once, and had seen only the shapes of papers wrapped in oilcloth, and had put the panel back and never touched it again, because a man's locked drawer is his own. But now the man was dead, and the drawer was scratching at him like something buried.

He did not open it. He stood before the case with his hand on the panel, and the clock ticked against his palm, and he thought: *Thursday. The tide permitting.*

If he went to Mahon, he would go into the war's front room, among the grey ships and the intelligence officers, to fix a clock that a dead man had once serviced in some uncounted year. He would be visible. Everything he had built his life on — the smallness, the bent back, the lamp-lit invisibility — would be spent in a week.

And if he did not go, he would spend the rest of his life listening to a scratching behind a panel, and wondering what Tomàs Ferrer had been keeping time for, all those silent years.

He took his hand off the panel. He banked the lamp. He ate bread and cheese and an orange standing up, the way Tomàs had always said a working man should eat, and at dusk he walked up through the town to the cemetery on the rise, where the wind comes off the plain.

* * *

Tomàs's grave was modest, granite, already going green at the base. The inscription was his name, his dates, and the words his sister in Palma had chosen: *RELLOTGER. MESURÀ EL TEMPS DELS HOMES.* Clockmaker. He measured the time of men. Below it, unauthorised, uncarved, Jude had set a small brass plate of his own making, no bigger than a watch dial, blank, because he had not known what to put on it and had thought perhaps one day he would.

The wind moved in the cypresses. Below, the whole plain lay in evening light, and beyond it the sea, and beyond the sea, everywhere, the war.

"I've had a letter," Jude said. He spoke in Menorquí, because one spoke to the dead in the language of home. "From Mahon. They want me to finish your work there. They say you worked for them." He crouched, and pulled a weed from the granite's edge, and was quiet a moment. "You never told me anything. Eleven years, and you told me how to true a wheel and how to blued steel and how to keep my mouth shut, and nothing else. And now a lieutenant with a crown on his paper is telling me your history in half a sentence, and I have to decide whether to go and find the rest of it."

The cypresses leaned and straightened. Somewhere below, the Seminari clock struck the hour — six strokes, the third flat, the rest true.

"I'm going," Jude said. "Not for them. For you. You owe me a decade, old man, and I've come to collect."

He walked down in the blue dusk, and the letter was in his breast pocket, over his heart, and by the time he reached the Plaça the lamps were lit and the decision had settled in him with the particular weight of a thing already in motion — a weight released, a wheel beginning to turn, the escapement letting go of one tooth and catching the next.

* * *

Thursday came grey and early. He closed the workshop properly — banked the burner, covered the regulator, locked the door and put the key on the ring with Tomàs's other keys, which he now wore — and left before dawn, walking the whole road to Mahon rather than wait for the carrier, because he wanted the hours to think, and because the road along the island's spine was beautiful at that hour, the dry-stone walls running like veins through the fields, the windmills turning their idle arms.

Mahon received him with smoke and noise. The harbour opened below the town like a held breath — that deep green ria, five miles of drowned river, crowded now with the grey shapes of the fleet: destroyers lying in tiers, a monitor with her absurd gun, store ships and colliers and the little launches stitching back and forth. Guns spoke somewhere out at sea, practice, the sound arriving late and flat across the water. Soldiers on the quay. Sailors. Nurses. The war, in the flesh, wearing its best harbour manners.

The observatory stood on the headland above the harbour mouth, a low stone building with a dome and, beside it, the mast and ball of the time signal. A naval sentry checked his papers at the road. A steward showed him along a corridor that smelled of paraffin and sea salt, to a door marked simply *TIME*, and knocked once, and opened it.

The room was cold and clean and full of instruments, and at a desk by the window sat a lieutenant with fair hair and a face like a closed account book, who rose, extended a hand, and said, in English flattened by long service abroad:

"Mr. Vane. You have your master's look about you. That is convenient, because what we require —" and here the lieutenant glanced at the door, and lowered his voice by a degree that made Jude's skin prickle, "— is not, strictly speaking, a clockmaker. It is a man who can open a Lepaute without the Navy knowing it has been opened. Your master built that clock a second movement, Mr. Vane. Forty years ago. And last week, for the first time since I have kept this office, it stopped keeping the time he set it to."

Behind the lieutenant's shoulder, through an inner doorway, Jude could see it: the master clock, tall and dark, its pendulum hanging dead-still, its hands frozen at a minute to eleven.

He heard himself say, "Then I'd best begin," and did not know, as he said it, whether he was answering the lieutenant or the clock.
