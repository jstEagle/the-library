## 1. What Can Be Carried

The letters came in bundles tied with butcher's twine, and Tamsin read them the way other people said grace.

Not because they had been asked to. Frau Voss had never once instructed them to open a sealed envelope — that was the point of her, the thing that made women trust her from Kaiserslautern to Zweibrücken. But every bundle she handed into the motorcar passed through Tamsin's hands first, and every envelope had a seam, and every seam yielded to a warm knife if you were patient and the road was long enough.

They read while the car idled outside the bakery in Alsenborn, morning fog still pooled in the street like spilled milk. A woman in Hochspeyer wrote that her husband's pension had come short again by eleven marks and she did not know whom to ask. A girl of perhaps sixteen wrote that she wanted to learn to keep accounts, and would it be true that a woman could sit the examination now, or was it another rumor like the last one. A widow wrote nothing at all but enclosed a photograph of three boys in sailor suits, two of them dead, and on the back, in pencil gone soft with handling: *so someone will know their faces*.

Tamsin folded each letter along its original creases, resealed the seams with a breath of steam and a flat thumb, and carried what they had read into themselves, where they kept it. They thought of it as the second ledger — the one no army could requisition, bound not in cloth boards but in the wet gray matter behind their eyes. Every debt unpaid. Every examination hoped for. Every face that needed knowing.

"You've stopped turning pages," Frau Voss said from the passenger seat, without looking up from her correspondence cards. She was addressing envelopes at an angle that suggested the motorcar was a desk and the Rhineland merely weather happening outside it. "Which means either the fog is thick or you are reading again."

"The fog is thick," Tamsin said.

"Mm." Frau Voss licked the flap of an envelope with the efficiency of a woman who considered adhesive a moral question. "Then I shall say nothing further about it, as I always say nothing further about it, and we will both continue in our comfortable hypocrisy."

She was sixty-one years old and built like a door, square and unyielding, with white hair pinned so tightly it seemed architectural. She wore mourning black for a husband dead nine years and had never once been seen to grieve him; when asked, she said only that he had been a patient man and marriage required patience, and let the sentence do its work. For eleven years she had walked and ridden and latterly been driven across the Palatinate, gathering women the way rivers gather rain — one at a time, in kitchens and churchyards and the backs of shops, until there was a current. The ledger lived in the headquarters house on Kirchstraße: a great bound volume, half a hand thick, in which every name was entered in Frau Voss's upright hand. Members. Contributors. Women who had signed petitions. Women who had merely attended, once, and might deny it later if their husbands asked.

Tamsin had watched her write those names for years. It had seemed then like a sacrament. A name written down meant *you exist, you count, you were here.*

It occurred to Tamsin, sitting in the fog with a widow's photograph warming in their coat pocket, that they had never before wondered what else a name written down might mean.

* * *

The army came on a Thursday, which felt like an insult; Thursdays were market days, and Alsenborn's one street was already crowded with carts when the lorries ground in from the west, mud to their axles, soldiers riding the tailboards with their rifles between their knees. Not combat troops. Worse — clerks. Tamsin could tell from the way the officers moved through the market without buying anything, taking notes, pointing. Occupation had settled into administration, and administration had begun to tidy up.

By noon the notices were pasted to the church door and the bakery window and the post office shutter, printed in that dense bureaucratic German that made even mercy sound like a tax assessment: the town's housing, livestock, fuel stocks, and records were subject to requisition for the purposes of the occupation authority. *Records.* The word sat there among the hay and the firewood as though it belonged.

Tamsin read the notice twice, then walked fast — too fast, a person with somewhere innocent to be — back to Kirchstraße.

Frau Voss was in the front room standing very still, which from her was the equivalent of another woman screaming. Through the window, four soldiers were carrying crates down the lane toward the schoolhouse, which had become a depot overnight. One crate held the movement's pamphlets. Another held the correspondence files, three years of them, sorted by district in Frau Voss's own system.

And the third — Tamsin saw it lifted onto the lorry bed with no more ceremony than a crate of turnips — bore a stenciled label. *AKTEN. PALATINAT. FRAUENVEREINE.* Files. Palatinate. Women's associations.

"They took the ledgers," Frau Voss said. Her voice was level. Her hands, clasped at her waist, were not; the knuckles had gone the color of candle wax. "All of them. The main volume and the district books. The officer in charge was perfectly polite. He showed me his orders." She turned, and her eyes had the flat brightness Tamsin had learned to fear, the brightness that preceded action. "He thanked me. He said our record-keeping was exemplary."

"Where are they going?"

"To the archives. The victors' archives." She said *victors* the way one says a diagnosis. "There is a history being written, Tamsin. It is being written now, this month, by men with access to everything we ever put our names to. And in that history, a list of organized women is not a movement. It is evidence."

Tamsin stood in the doorway of the room where they had addressed ten thousand envelopes and understood, all at once and bodily, the shape of the thing. Every name in that ledger had been given in trust. Given by women who believed a signature was a gift to the future. And the future had arrived wearing field-gray, and it had come for the signatures.

The widow's photograph shifted against Tamsin's chest. *So someone will know their faces.* That was what writing things down was. And that was also — they saw it now with terrible clarity — exactly how you made a list of people to be found.

"There were names in the district books," Tamsin said slowly. "Women who signed quietly. Some of them told me—" They stopped. Some of them had told *them*, in letters, things they'd never have said aloud: the schoolmistress whose brother was in the new government; the innkeeper's wife who'd hidden pamphlets inside hymnals. Things that read very differently depending on who was holding the paper.

"I know what was in them," Frau Voss said. "I wrote them."

Outside, the lorry engine caught with a cough of black smoke. Tamsin watched a young soldier climb onto the tailboard beside the crates and settle himself comfortably, one boot against the wood, a man guarding boxes he had no reason to think anyone wanted. The lorry pulled away north, toward the depot, and the fog closed behind it like water over a stone.

"How long," Tamsin heard themself ask, "before they're shipped?"

Frau Voss looked at them for a long moment. Something passed across her face that was not quite a smile — recognition, perhaps, of a pupil arriving unbidden at the correct answer.

"The officer mentioned a transport schedule," she said. "Clerks love schedules. Clerks write them down." She reached for her hat. "Fetch your coat, Tamsin. We are going to be charming."
