## 1. The Summons

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which August Dravenmoor would remember afterward because Tuesdays were the days her uncle let her work alone on the regulator clocks, and she was elbow-deep in the movement of one when the postman's knock came through the frosted glass of the shop door like a small, polite gunshot.

"August," her uncle called from the back room, where he was grinding a pivot with the patience of a man who had made peace with his own hands decades ago. "See to the door."

She wiped her fingers on the leather apron — a habit, since the apron was already more oil than leather — and went. The postman stood on the step with snow gathering on the brim of his cap, and he held out not an envelope but an envelope within an envelope, the outer one stamped with the seal of the city administration of Vilna, and beneath that, smaller, the crest of the university observatory.

"You're to sign for it," he said. "Registered."

"I'm only the apprentice," August said.

"You're the one whose name is inside, miss." He turned the envelope so she could see the direction written in a clerk's careful hand: *Fräulein A. Dravenmoor, Uhrmacherwerkstatt J. Dravenmoor, Gertrudenstraße.* Her name, spelled correctly, which almost never happened. People usually assumed the *Dravenmoor* belonged to her uncle and father both, and that the girl who answered the door must be a daughter or a niece or someone's wife. They were right about the niece part, but they were wrong about everything it implied.

She signed. The postman left footprints in the thin snow, going away, and she stood in the doorway holding the letter as if it might be hot.

It was heavy paper. That was the first thing she noticed — the weight of it, the good rag stock that officials used when they wanted you to understand that what they said mattered and what you said did not. The second thing she noticed was the wax seal, unbroken, crimson, pressed with the observatory's device: an armillary sphere crossed by a meridian line, and beneath it the motto in Latin that Professor Lelis had once translated for her uncle over brandy — *Tempus servitium est.* Time is service. Or possibly, her uncle had argued cheerfully, time is slavery, depending on your Latin and your mood.

"August." Her uncle appeared in the corridor, wiping brass filings from his fingers. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with a beard the color of old pewter and eyes that missed nothing, which was the occupational hazard of forty years at the bench. "Well? Open it, girl. It won't bite. Though if it's from the city it may chew."

She broke the seal.

*To Fräulein Auguste Dravenmoor,* it began, and she flinched inwardly at *Auguste*, which no one had called her since her christening, and which made her feel like a stranger wearing her own coat.

*You are hereby summoned, by authority of the University Commission of Instruments, to attend upon the master clock of the Vilna Observatory, there to effect such repairs as shall be found necessary to restore said clock to proper function. You will present yourself at the Observatory on the Hill of Three Crosses no later than the twentieth day of November. Travel and lodging expenses to be borne by the Commission. Failure to comply will be regarded—*

She stopped reading aloud in her head and read it again silently, twice, because the words refused to arrange themselves into sense.

"Uncle Josef," she said. Her voice came out strange, flattened. "They want me."

He took the letter from her hands — he did that sometimes, without asking, and she had never learned how to object — and read it through, his lips moving slightly at the Latin. Then he looked up at her over the top of the page, and his expression did something complicated that she could not entirely parse: pride, yes, but folded into something else, something like worry, the way a watchmaker folds a spring into its barrel, under tension, contained.

"The observatory clock," he said. "The great regulator. Lelis's clock." He whistled low, a sound like steam escaping. "That movement has four thousand parts, August. Some of them older than the university itself. Men have written books about that clock and been wrong."

"They've summoned *me*," she said again, as though repetition might make it less absurd. "Not you. Not Father. Me. I'm third-year. I'm—" She gestured vaguely at herself, at the whole of her, the oil-stained apron and the ink-stained cuffs and the twenty-two years of being the smallest person in every workshop she'd ever entered. "I'm nobody."

"You are the person whose name is inside the envelope," her uncle said, returning it to her with ceremony, "and outside it, apparently. Which makes you somebody to the University Commission, whatever you are to yourself."

* * *

That evening the family gathered in the kitchen above the shop, which was how all important business was conducted in the Dravenmoor household, because important business required bread and dripping and her aunt Marta's opinion, delivered from the stove with the authority of scripture.

Her father had come down from his own shop two streets over — the elder branch of the trade, the repair of church tower clocks, the work that took ladders and courage and a head for heights that August had inherited along with her father's colorless eyes and her mother's stubbornness, wherever her mother was now, which was Riga, which was another story, which was not told in this kitchen.

"So," her father said, spreading the letter flat on the scrubbed table between the bread board and the salt cellar, as though laying out a patient on an operating table. "Read it to us properly, Auguste."

"Don't call me that."

"It's your name."

"It's a name for a christening and a death certificate and nothing in between."

Her father smiled — he had her exact smile, narrow and quick, gone as soon as seen — and pushed the letter toward her. "Read."

She read. She had a good reading voice; the schoolmaster in Kaunas had said so before the war took the schoolmaster and gave him back different, quieter, but the compliment remained lodged in her like a splinter. She read the summons through, all of it, including the clauses about expenses and compliance and the final paragraph, which was the strangest:

*The Commission notes with regret the indisposition of Herr Professor E. Lelis, Keeper of Instruments, whose health prevents him from undertaking the adjustment himself. The Commission relies upon the competence of the named artisan to act in his stead and under his remote direction.*

"Indisposition," Aunt Marta repeated, from the stove. "That's a word men use when they mean dying and don't want to say so at dinner."

"Marta," her uncle said mildly.

"Well, it is. Lelis is seventy if he's a day. My cousin's husband saw him at the Philharmonic in October and said the man held the rail all the way up the stairs like it owed him money."

August looked at the word *indisposition* and felt the first cold thread of something that was not yet dread but was learning to be. Professor Emanuel Lelis had come to her uncle's workshop exactly twice in her three years there, both times in winter, both times smelling of cold air and pipe smoke and the particular dust of old libraries. He had watched her file a pallet stone for eleven minutes without speaking, and then he had said, to her uncle but looking at her: *This one listens to the metal. Most of them listen to themselves.* She had thought about that sentence for a week. She had never decided whether it was a compliment.

"And why you?" her father asked now, tapping the table. "That's what I want to know. There are masters in Vilna itself. There's Kowalski, there's the Brandt brothers, there's half a dozen men with guild papers and gray beards. Why does the Commission summon a third-year apprentice from—"

"From nowhere," August said.

"From here," her father corrected, gently, and she heard the difference even as she resented it. "From a town with two hundred souls and one church clock, which I keep running, thank you. Why you?"

Nobody had an answer. Uncle Josef turned his brandy glass by its base, around and around, a habit that meant he was thinking and would speak when the thinking was done, and not before. The stove ticked. Outside, the early dark of November pressed against the windows, and somewhere down the street the church clock — her father's patient, his burden — struck the hour with a sound like a coin dropped into a very deep well.

"Because I'm cheap," August said at last. "And because I'm available. And because a girl can be summoned and sent and blamed without anyone's guild getting upset about it. Because I'm convenient."

"That's one answer," her uncle said.

"It's the true one."

"It's *an* answer." He set the glass down. "Here's mine. Lelis asked for you by trade, or I'm a Dutchman. The Commission doesn't know apprentices from archbishops; some clerk wrote down whatever Lelis told him to write. And Lelis has met perhaps six clockmakers in this whole province who can touch that movement, and he's watched all six of them work, and he chose the one who listens to the metal." He shrugged, enormous and unhurried. "Or he chose the cheapest. Both things can be true. The world is generous that way."

* * *

She did not sleep well, which was unusual; August slept like a closed watch, wound and silent, and woke precisely. But that night she lay in her narrow bed under the eaves and listened to the house settle and thought about the observatory clock, which she had seen exactly once, at fourteen, on the one trip of her life to Vilna — a trip she remembered now with the strange vividness of things witnessed before one knows their weight.

Her father had taken her up into the tower. She remembered the climb, the smell of cold stone and machine oil, the way the sound changed as they rose, the city dropping away below them into a haze of chimney smoke and river fog. And then the clock itself, filling the room like a cathedral's organ: the great barrel, the weights hanging in their shafts like prisoners serving long sentences, the pendulum — nine feet if it was an inch, iron rod and gilded bob — swinging with a patience that had frightened her a little, that had seemed less like machinery than like breathing. Something alive that did not care whether she lived or died, that counted anyway, that would count after.

Her father had put her hand flat against the case, and the vibration had come up through her palm, through her wrist, into her chest, and she had understood — not as a thought but as a fact of the body — that this thing was the heartbeat of the city, that every train and court session and factory whistle and midnight curfew in Vilna was set by this swing, this tick, this iron patience.

"What happens if it stops?" she had asked.

Her father had considered the question seriously, because he considered all questions seriously; it was his best quality and his worst. "Then everyone's watch drifts apart from everyone else's," he'd said. "The trains miss each other. The courts sit late or not at all. Nobody agrees on when anything happened." He'd wiped his nose on his sleeve, high up there in the cold. "Time isn't real, August. Agreement is real. That clock is where the city agrees."

She had thought about that for years without knowing she was thinking about it.

Now, lying awake, she thought: *they are asking me to go and mend the place where the city agrees.* And beneath the fear, beneath the impostor's certainty that a mistake had been made somewhere in a clerk's office, there was a small, warm, shameful coal of feeling that she recognized, with discomfort, as pride.

She smothered it the way she always did, with the practiced hand of long habit. Pride was for other people — for masters with guild papers, for men whose names went on plaques. For her there was the bench, and the work, and the pleasure of a mechanism coming true under her hands, and that had to be enough, because wanting more got people laughed at, and she had been laughed at enough for one lifetime already, thank you, beginning with the foreman in Kaunas who had told her that girls' fingers were too soft for pivots and then watched her cut and finish forty of them better than his own men, and had never once said so out loud.

History, she told herself, turning onto her side, pulling the quilt to her chin, history happened elsewhere. History happened to ambassadors and generals and men whose names were printed in the gazette. History did not happen to apprentice clockmakers in towns with one church clock. Whatever was happening in Vilna — the flags, the soldiers, the notices her uncle read aloud at breakfast in a voice carefully stripped of opinion — that was weather. It passed over. One kept one's head down and one's hands busy and one waited for it to blow through, the way her grandmother had waited out three governments in the same kitchen, knitting through all of them.

She was almost asleep when the last thought arrived, unbidden, and it was this: *the clock is where the city agrees.*

And the city, just now, agreed on nothing at all.

* * *

The twentieth of November fell on a Thursday, and it snowed.

Uncle Josef drove her to the station in the cart, wrapped in horse blankets among the tool cases, because the train from the junction connected only twice a week now and the schedule was, in the stationmaster's phrase, *indicative*. Her father walked alongside as far as the bridge over the millrace, and there he stopped, because stopping at the bridge was a ritual between them neither had ever explained nor needed to.

"You'll write," he said.

"Every week."

"You'll send measurements, not opinions. If Lelis tells you something about that movement that contradicts what I taught you, you'll assume he's right until you've proven otherwise, and then you'll write to me anyway, because I'll want to know."

"Yes, Papa."

He looked at her a moment longer. Snow was collecting in the fur of his collar. He was not a demonstrative man; the last time he had embraced her was at her mother's leaving, seven years ago, and it had been brief and bone-crushing and neither of them had mentioned it since. Now he put out his hand instead, and she took it, and they shook like colleagues, which was its own kind of tenderness.

"That clock," he said. "When you're inside it, remember: it doesn't care about you. Don't take it personally. And don't trust any gear you haven't counted twice."

"Counted twice," she agreed. "Trusted never."

He smiled his narrow smile, gone as soon as seen, and turned back toward the town, and she watched him go, small against the white fields, a man shaped by forty years of bending over other people's time.

At the station, Uncle Josef bought her coffee from the kiosk — real coffee, or the approximation the war had left behind, chicory and roasted acorn and one sacrificial bean — and they drank it standing on the platform while the snow came down in fat, unhurried flakes.

"You could refuse," he said suddenly.

She looked at him.

"The summons. It's not conscription, August. It's a commission. Commissions can be declined. Write back, say the workshop can't spare you, say your health — Marta will swear to anything in writing, she enjoys it. Someone else will go. Kowalski will go. The clock will get mended by somebody." He blew on his coffee. "I'm not saying decline it. I'm saying know that you can. There's a difference between choosing a road and being thrown onto it. You should always know which one you're doing."

August looked down the line, at the rails vanishing into the snowfall, at the signal arm hanging motionless, at the small red lamp of the distant semaphore burning through the weather like a drop of blood on white cloth. She thought about the tower room, the pendulum's iron patience, the vibration in her fourteen-year-old palm. She thought about the word *summoned*, and how it was a word used for kings' courts and for spirits raised unwillingly from graves, and how it had nonetheless been addressed to her, spelled correctly, *Fräulein A. Dravenmoor*, as though the world had finally, accidentally, looked directly at her.

"If I decline," she said slowly, "I'll wonder my whole life what was wrong with that clock."

"There'll be something wrong with every clock you ever meet," her uncle said. "That's the trade."

"No." She shook her head. "Wrong in a way only I could find. That's vanity."

"That," said Uncle Josef, "is the first honest thing you've said all week. Vanity's allowed, August. It's fuel. Just don't let it drive." He drained his coffee and grimaced at it on principle. "Besides. If Lelis is dying, someone should be there who listens to the metal."

The train came out of the west like a rumor, black and steaming, shedding snow. They loaded her cases — the good ones, with the fitted trays, the tools her uncle had begun buying for her the day she arrived, one per month, quietly, never discussed — and then he stood on the platform with his big hands in his pockets and his beard whitening further by the minute, and he did not embrace her either, because that was not the family way, but he said:

"Third year. Remember what I told you your first week? When you cried in the back room over the escapement?"

"I didn't cry."

"You absolutely cried. I told you: the work is bigger than you, and that's a mercy. If it were smaller, you'd break it. Go be small next to something large, and pay attention." He stepped back as the carriage shuddered. "And August —"

The wheels began to move. She leaned out.

"— count everything twice!"

The platform slid away. The snow thickened. The town with its one church clock dissolved behind her, and ahead the rails ran straight through the white fields toward the river, toward the hills, toward Vilna, where the flags had changed twice in a year and the newspapers spoke in careful passive voices about *events* and *measures* and *the situation*, words designed, August had always assumed, for other people's emergencies, other people's history, none of it hers.

She settled into her seat, opened the summons, and read it through once more, counting the clauses like teeth in a wheel. Fourteen clauses. All accounted for. She folded the heavy paper along its original creases and put it in the inner pocket of her coat, over her heart, where important documents go because the body keeps them warmer there.

Outside, the snow went on falling, patient as a pendulum, erasing the road behind her.

She did not yet know that the road ahead was being erased too — that at that very hour, upriver, military engineers were already stacking timber and cable beside the great bridge at Vilna, and that the season's last crossing would close behind her like a trap with a silken click, and that the hours she was traveling toward had already been stolen from the record by someone's careful hand.

She only knew that the train was moving, that the letter sat warm against her ribs, and that for the first time in her life, history — vast, indifferent, previously so very elsewhere — seemed to have turned, slightly, in her direction.

She told herself it was nothing. She was very good at telling herself things.

The train ran north into the weather, carrying her toward the hill, the tower, and the clock that did not care, and the snow fell on all of it equally, on the living and the dead and the not-yet-written-down.
