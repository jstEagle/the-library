## 2. Rumors in the Fish Market

The fish market woke before the sun did, and August Dravenmoor had learned to move through it the way one moves through a set galley — knowing where each body belonged, which hands would reach for what, and how to slip between the rows without disturbing the arrangement. She went at first light for the ink order, because Mr. Takeda the chandler kept his lamp-oil and his lampblack in the same shed behind the drying racks, and because by mid-morning the smell of the place — brine and blood and charcoal smoke — would cling to her sleeves in a way her father always claimed he could read like a ledger. *You went to the market first, not the chandlery. You lingered.* He was right about such things. He was usually right about such things, which was one of the small tyrannies of being his daughter.

She carried the order slip in her coat's inner pocket, folded twice, and she carried, as she always carried, the habit of measuring. It was her mother's habit first, inherited the way one inherits a crooked finger or a way of holding a spoon: everything in the world could be sorted into what could be carried and what could not. A stick of ink could be carried. A printing press could not — not really, not far, though her father would argue otherwise with the fervor of a man who had once moved a full Columbian across three provinces. A house could not. A harbor could not. A friend — August paused at this, as she paused every time, her thumb worrying the edge of the folded slip — a friend was a thing she had never quite decided how to weigh.

"August-san!" The voice came from behind the octopus stall, where Kenji Watanabe stood with a stack of manifests under one arm and a look on his face that was trying very hard to be casual and failing in a manner that would have been endearing if it weren't so obviously a warning. "You're early."

"You're later." She stopped beside him, tucking a loose strand of hair back under her hood. The market noise rose around them — the wet slap of fish on split boards, the criers calling prices in the rising cadence that always reminded her of a press building speed, the gulls screaming their endless editorial above the roofs. "The chandler will sell his lampblack to the bathhouse if I'm not quick. Your people have been hoarding it for winter."

"My people hoard everything for winter. It's a national virtue." But Kenji wasn't smiling the way he usually smiled, with the crooked ease of a man who spent his days among sailors and had learned that most disasters could be laughed at in advance. He glanced over his shoulder — at the octopus seller, at the two men in plain dark cotton standing near the drying racks who were not buying anything, who had not been buying anything for the better part of an hour. August noticed them because she noticed everything; it was the trade she'd been raised in. Noticing was to a printer's daughter what a steady hand was to a surgeon.

"Kenji." She lowered her voice. "What is it?"

"Nothing. I wanted to see you. Is that a crime?" He shifted the manifests. "How is your father?"

"Cutting a new face. A condensed gothic, for a merchant house in the foreign quarter. He's been at it nine days. He talks to the punches like they're disobedient children." She smiled. "He'll have it perfect by the twelfth day. He always does."

"Nine days." Kenji said it strangely, as if the number meant something to him that it didn't mean to her. "And what else is he printing?"

"Ledgers. Bills of lading. A wedding announcement for the Nakashima girl — the type is terrible, bought secondhand, half the sorts are worn round as pebbles. Why?"

"Why does a dock clerk ask about printing?" He tried the smile again. It didn't take. "No reason. Curiosity. It's a national virtue too."

August looked at him. Kenji Watanabe was twenty-three, three years her senior, a clerk of the harbor customs with a clerk's memory and a fisherman's son's shoulders, and she had known him since she was fourteen and had dropped an entire case of twelve-point brevier into the harbor mud and he had helped her wash it sort by sort in a tide pool, laughing so hard he cried, saying she had the worst luck of any foreigner in Nagasaki and the dirtiest vocabulary. He was her friend in the way that things in Nagasaki were friends: across a border, in a shared language that was neither his nor hers entirely, in the small commerce of favors and gossip that made a harbor a community rather than a frontier. She did not like the look on his face now. It was the look of a man doing arithmetic he didn't want to finish.

"Kenji," she said, "you've asked about my father's work twice this month."

"And you've asked about the villages three times." It came out of him fast, low, almost angry, and then he seemed to hear himself and pressed his lips together. Around them the market went on — the octopus seller haggling, a cart wheel grinding through wet sand, the gulls — and the two men by the drying racks had not moved, and August felt something shift in her chest, a small cold settling, like the first frost forming on a window she hadn't known was open.

"What villages?" she said, which was a lie, because she knew, or half knew, or had been letting herself half know for weeks.

"August." He stepped closer. The smell of him was ink and salt and the cheap tobacco of the customs house. "Don't. Please. Not here."

"Then somewhere. Tonight, after your shift—"

"No. Not anywhere. That's what I'm trying to tell you." His hand twitched toward her sleeve and stopped, the way a man's hand stops at a fence he knows is electrified. "You've been asking the chandlers where the lampblack goes. You've been asking the boatmen what they saw off the coast past Hirado. You asked the paper merchant why the demand for cheap stock has doubled in Shimabara. Do you think nobody hears you? Do you think a foreign girl with her father's accent asking questions in a fish market is a secret?"

"I ask about ink," August said. "Ink and paper. That's my trade. That's my *father's* trade—"

"Your father's trade is knowing things," Kenji said quietly, "and putting them where everyone can read them. I know. I've always admired it. I admired it when he printed the tide tables for free so the fishermen wouldn't be cheated by the brokers. I admired it when he ran the Dutch shipping lists even after the magistrate's man told him to stop. But August — there are things moving through this harbor right now that don't want to be known. And the people moving them are not the kind who write letters of complaint."

The cold in her chest settled deeper. She made herself look at the two men by the drying racks again. Plain cotton. Sandals, not tabi — travelers, or men dressed as travelers. One of them had the callused palms and the hip-stance of a swordsman pretending to be a porter, and he was looking at the fish without seeing it, the way her father looked at a page of bad typography, cataloguing everything wrong.

"Tell me," she said. "Or tell me nothing and I'll ask someone else, and I'll ask them worse questions, because I won't have you deciding what I can bear."

"That's exactly what I'm afraid of." Kenji exhaled through his teeth. He looked out at the harbor, where the morning fog was burning off the water in long gray rags, where the Dutch ship *Vryheid* sat at her moorings with her sails furled like a sleeping animal, where the bridge — the single bridge, the one bridge, the thread that stitched Nagasaki to the rest of Japan — arched over the river with its usual morning traffic of palanquins and porters and priests. "There's a village," he said at last, so quietly she had to lean in, so quietly the words barely survived the gulls. "Up the coast. Past the peninsula. Three weeks ago, maybe four. It burned."

"Fires happen. It's a dry autumn."

"The whole village, August. In one night. And the people in it." His jaw worked. "The fishermen who came through last week — the ones who won't take that stretch of coast anymore, who'd rather row the long way around and lose a day's pay — they talk when they're paid in sake and I'm the one pouring. They say there were soldiers. They say the village had been sheltering men from the loyalist cells, ronin, the kind that move through the mountains collecting money and leaving promises. They say when the soldiers came, they didn't distinguish. They say—" He stopped. He picked up a fish from the stall beside them, turned it over as if inspecting it, put it down. His hands were not steady. "They say there were children. They say the well was full."

August said nothing. The market roared on around her and she heard none of it. She was aware of absurd, irrelevant details — the precise blue of a mackerel's back, the way the octopus seller's knife caught the light, the folded order slip against her ribs going soft with her own heat — because her mind, her father's mind, her mother's mind, reached for the particular when the general became unbearable. That was the training. That was the inheritance. The world is made of particulars, her father said; a lie is always vague, and the truth is always specific, and that is how you tell them apart.

"They say," Kenji went on, "that when the magistrate's men came to look, the story was already written for them. Foreign sailors. Drunken Dutchmen from a shore leave, they said. A fight over a woman. The Dutch consul is furious — Vosse's people have been filing protests for a week — but the papers are signed and the story is in every register from here to Edo, and a story in a register is a story in stone." He looked at her. "You understand what I'm telling you. Somebody printed that story. Somebody set it, and signed it, and filed it, and now it's true. That's the power your father plays with every day of his life. And somebody, somewhere up that coast, did what they did, and then reached for a man with a press to make it permanent."

"Foreign sailors." August's voice came out flat. "There were no foreign sailors."

"How would you know?"

"Because the Dutch ships' logs are kept in duplicate and the harbor office holds the second copy and *you* hold the third, Kenji, and if any shore leave had turned into a massacre the *Vryheid* would have been impounded and every man aboard questioned, and instead she's sitting out there taking on water like a fat merchant on holiday." She heard her own voice rising and dragged it down. "Because I've grown up on this harbor and I know what a shore brawl costs and what it looks like, and it doesn't look like a burned village and a full well."

"Then you understand." Kenji's voice was barely above the market noise now. "You understand, and you're clever enough to understand it in the space of one conversation, which is exactly why you frighten me. Because the men who did this — the ones who gave the order and the ones who carried it and the ones who wrote the lie afterward — they are not finished. A thing like this isn't a single fire. It's a policy. And the people who are asking questions — the fishermen, the priests, the ones who won't let it lie — they're being noticed. There's a list, August. There's always a list. And a Dutch printer's daughter who asks about lampblack and cheap paper and the coast past Hirado is a name that goes on a list very easily."

"I'm not Dutch. I'm half. And I'm nobody."

"You're the daughter of the best type cutter in western Japan, and you're a woman who walks into a fish market and reads it like a broadsheet, and you think nobody notices?" He shook his head, and for a moment the anger went out of him and left only the fear, and the fear was worse, because Kenji Watanabe did not frighten easily; she had seen him stay calm through a dock fire and a customs raid and the time a whale had washed up half-rotten and the whole waterfront had nearly rioted over the claim. "Please. For me. Stop asking. Buy your ink. Print your wedding announcements. Let the well stay full and the story stay in stone, because stone is heavy but it doesn't bleed, and the people who move it will move it over anyone who gets in the road."

August looked at him for a long moment. The gulls screamed. The cart wheel ground past. Somewhere a bell buoy clanged its dull note off the breakwater, the harbor's oldest voice, saying the same thing it always said: *here, here, here.*

"You think I can un-know it," she said finally. "Now that I know it."

"I think you can stop *collecting* it." He held her eyes. "There's a difference between carrying a thing and going out looking for more weight. Your mother knew that. Everyone says she was the sensible one."

The name landed harder than he could have known — harder than he *should* have known, and August saw by the flicker in his face that he knew even that much, that he had reached for it deliberately, the one argument she couldn't answer. Her mother, who had fled something once, across a border, with a typecase and a half-told story; her mother, who had taught her to measure the world in what could be carried; her mother, who was three years in the ground now and whose silences August was only beginning, piece by piece, to read as warnings.

"The chandler," August said at last. Her voice was steady. She was proud of that and ashamed of it in the same breath. "I have to buy my ink."

"August—"

"Two sticks of lampblack, one of vermilion if he has it, and the order slip is in my pocket, and I will smile at Mr. Takeda and ask him nothing about anything except whether his wife's cough is better." She took the slip out and held it up between two fingers like a pass. "There. You see? Harmless. Paper is harmless. That's what my father always says. Ink is harmless. It's just a trade, Kenji. It's just a trade."

Kenji looked at her the way a man looks at a boat whose seams have begun to weep — not yet sinking, not yet worth panicking over, but no longer something to stand on with both feet. "Yes," he said. "That's what worries me. Your father says paper is harmless the way a gunsmith says steel is harmless. You're both right. That's the whole problem." He tucked the manifests under his arm. "Go buy your ink. And August — if your father asks you to help him with anything this winter, anything at all, anything that prints faster than a wedding announcement — come find me first. Before you help him. Promise me."

"Why would he ask—"

"*Promise me.*"

And because it was Kenji, and because the two men by the drying racks had finally, unhurriedly, begun to move — not toward her, not toward Kenji, but along the row, in the unhurried way of men who had seen what they came to see — August said, "I promise," and watched him go, and stood a moment longer in the roar and stink of the market with the cold settled in her chest where her certainty used to be.

* * *

Mr. Takeda's wife's cough was better, he said; the sea air did that, or the pickled plums, he couldn't say which, and he sold August her lampblack and her vermilion and threw in, unasked, a third stick wrapped in oiled paper. "For your father," he said, not meeting her eyes. "Tell him — tell him it's the good stock. The batch I keep back." And when she asked, lightly, why he was giving it, he said, "He'll know," and turned to the next customer, and would not look at her again.

August walked home along the harbor road with the ink against her ribs where the order slip had been, and the weight of it was wrong — too light for what it now carried. The *Vryheid* sat at her moorings. The bridge arched over its traffic. The gulls went on with their editorial. And she found, as she walked, that she was doing what she always did, what she could not stop doing, what her father had built into her the way he built a letterform into a punch: she was setting the morning in type. *Kenji's hands not steady. The well. Two men in travelers' cotton. Takeda's gift, and his turned back. He'll know.*

*He'll know.* Her father would know what? That the good lampblack was a message? That the chandler was paying a debt, or a bribe, or a warning? That the quiet printer on the harbor's edge, the man who talked to his punches like disobedient children, the man who said *paper is harmless* with a straight face for twenty years —

She stopped at the top of the shop's steps, the harbor at her back, the door before her, and she stood there in the cold morning with her hand on the latch, and she thought of the locked drawer in the back of the composing room that her father had told her, years ago, held spoiled stock — mis-cast type, ruined plates, the failures every shop accumulates. She had never once opened it. She had never once wanted to. It had simply been part of the shop's furniture, the way a scar is part of a body.

The key hung on the board by the press, on its own hook, the only key on its own hook, and it had hung there all her life, in plain sight, the way her father hid things: not concealed, only unremarked, trusting the world never to look at what it had no reason to see.

August went inside. The shop smelled of lead and oil and the ghost of last night's lamp. Her father's stool stood empty by the cutting bench; he had gone out early, before she woke, to the paper merchant's, the note on the bench said, back by noon. The morning light came gray through the harbor-facing window and lay across the composing stone like a sheet over something sleeping.

August stood in the doorway a long time. Then she took the key from its hook, and her hand was steady, and she was ashamed of that too.
