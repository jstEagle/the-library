## 12. The Brother's Ledger

The rain had been falling since dawn, a fine gray drizzle that turned the harbor into something soft-edged and half-erased, as though the whole city were a woodblock print left out in weather it was never meant to survive. August watched it from the loft window above the shop, her breath fogging the glass, waiting for Kenji the way she had learned to wait for everything now — with her hands busy and her mind counting.

She was sorting type. It was what her hands did when they could not be still: twelve-point Minchō, worn at the shoulders, each letter dropping into its compartment with a small click like a clock keeping time in a house where no one trusted the hour. *A* into its cell. *B* into its cell. The alphabet was the only order left in Nagasaki, and even it was a lie of arrangement — meaning lived only in what you chose to set beside what.

Kenji came up the outside stair just past noon, hooded against the rain, and he climbed slowly, which was not like him. Kenji moved through the world the way water moved through the fish market channels — quick, finding every gap, never lingering where lingering cost anything. Tonight he lingered on every step.

She opened the door before he knocked.

"You have them," she said.

"I have them." He stepped inside, dripping, and stood in the middle of the room without removing his hood, which was also not like him. The loft smelled of ink and lamp oil and the cold coming off his coat. "August." He said her name strangely, as if testing whether it would hold weight. "Before I give them to you, I want you to understand what you're asking me to have done."

"I'm not asking anymore. I asked once. You named your price."

"And you paid it. Three nights copying manifests by candlelight while your father slept two rooms away, not knowing, because if he knew he'd stop you, and if he stopped you—" Kenji pulled back his hood at last. His face was older than it had been a month ago. Fear does that; it carves. "You don't know what's in these papers. You copied them, but you copied them the way a bird copies song. Sounds without knowing what they mean."

"Then tell me what they mean."

He unwrapped the bundle from inside his coat — oilcloth, tied with cord, the manifests she had spent three nights bent over, her wrist aching, transcribing figures and names and cargo weights in her smallest hand so they would fit on fewer pages. She knew those pages. She had made them. But when Kenji set them on the worktable between them, they seemed to belong to someone else entirely, some other woman who had believed that paper was harmless.

"The night your father printed his proof," Kenji said, "the boats that burned Kaminoseki didn't come from nowhere. Someone provisioned them. Rice, lamp oil, powder. Someone signed for it through the harbor office so the shipment would look like ordinary coastal trade." He tapped the top page with one finger, once, twice, and stopped himself from tapping a third time. "That someone is listed here. Provisioning officer, third district. The signature appears eleven times across four months."

August looked down at the page. She had copied that column herself. She remembered the brushstrokes of the original, the confident downward strokes of a man who signed his name the way he did everything — without hesitation.

She read the name.

*Okabe Tomonori.*

The room went very quiet. Even the rain seemed to draw back from the window.

"That's your brother," she said. Not a question. She had known Kenji had a brother in the harbor administration — had met him once, years ago, a broad-shouldered young man who had laughed too loudly and bought her sweet chestnuts off a brazier near the customs house because she'd been staring at them and pretending not to be hungry. Okabe Tomonori, who called her *little Dutch girl* without malice, who had shown her how the harbor cranes worked, who had ruffled her hair with a hand that smelled of rope and salt.

"My brother," Kenji said. "Who fed me when my mother couldn't. Who paid for my clerk's position. Who taught me every trick I know about reading a manifest, which is how I found his name in this one, because he thought no one would ever look." He laughed, and it was an awful sound, dry as type dust. "He taught me to find him."

August sat down slowly on the sorting stool. Her legs felt unreliable, as though the floor beneath the loft had become the deck of a ship.

"Take it out," Kenji said.

"What?"

"His name. Take it out of your copy. The rest stands — dates, quantities, the route the supplies took. It damns the faction either way. One name removed changes nothing about the truth of the massacre. But one name kept—" His voice cracked and he pressed his lips together until it passed. "One name kept means they take him. And they won't just take him, August. They'll take him apart to see who else he touches, and everyone he touches, and everyone those people touch. My mother. Me. Possibly your father, if they trace the copy back through me, which they will, because I am the only person in Nagasaki stupid enough to steal from my own office for a foreign printer's daughter."

"He helped burn a village." Her voice came out flat, and she hated the flatness, hated that grief and principle had flattened her into something that sounded like her father. "There were children at Kaminoseki. Your pamphlet—my father's proof—it lists them. Eleven under the age of six."

"I know what it lists. I've read it. Do you think I haven't read it?" He was leaning over the table now, both hands flat on the wood, and she saw that his fingers were trembling. "I've read it every night since I found the name. I've read it and I've seen his face and I've tried to make the two things sit in the same room in my head, and they will not sit together. That is why I brought this to you instead of burning it. Because I cannot decide. So I've decided to let you decide, and then I can hate you instead of myself, which will be easier."

The honesty of it struck her harder than pleading would have. She looked at him — really looked — and understood that he had spent the walk across the quarter in the rain hoping she would refuse. Hoping she would say *no, we keep the truth whole*, so that the sin would be hers and he could go home clean to his mother's house and hate her forever, comfortably, the way one hates a storm.

And she almost gave it to him. That was the terrible thing. She felt the temptation rise in her like warm water: *one line drawn through one name, and Kenji sleeps, and his mother keeps her son, and the truth survives minus one letterform.* What was one character among thousands? She was a compositor. She knew better than anyone that a page was never perfect — there was always a turned letter, a bad impression, a flaw the reader's eye forgave. Readers forgave everything. That was the mercy of readers, and the ruin of them.

But her father's voice was in her head, the voice from the night she'd found the locked drawer, the night the betrayal had bloomed into dread: *A truth with a piece cut out isn't a smaller truth. It's a different lie.*

"If I strike his name," she said carefully, "and the pamphlet comes out, and someday someone finds the original manifests — and someone always finds the originals, Kenji, paper outlives all of us, that's the entire religion of my family — then the evidence is broken. Every name in it becomes doubtful. They'll say we protected our friends. They'll say the whole document is a faction's forgery, and they'll be right to say it, because it will be. A forgery by omission."

"So you'll hang him."

"I'll *tell the truth about him*. There's a difference, and I know it doesn't feel like a difference tonight, but it's the only difference that matters." She stood. Her knees were steady now, which surprised her. "Your brother signed for the rice and the oil and the powder. He knew what boats they were feeding. Whatever else he is — the chestnuts, the cranes, the man who carried your mother up the hill when her lungs failed — he did that thing. And the families of Kaminoseki are dead, and dead people cannot decide whose names to spare."

Kenji stared at her. Rain ran off the hem of his coat onto the floorboards, pooling in the grain of the wood, darkening it the way ink darkens paper.

"You sound like your father," he said finally.

"No," August said. "My father would have decided already and never told me there was a decision. I'm worse than my father. I'm making you watch."

* * *

They argued for another hour. She gave him that much — she owed him that much, and more than an hour, probably more than a lifetime, for the manifests and the pass and the three nights of candlelight and the terrible gift of his indecision. He paced the loft between the cases of type, and every few turns he stopped and read the name again, as though it might have changed, as though love might have edited it.

At some point he began to talk, and she understood that he needed to talk, needed to fill the room with his brother the way you fill a grave.

"He carried me to the doctor when I broke my arm. Four miles, uphill, in summer. I was seven." He said it to the type wall, not to her. "When Father died, he took the examinations because someone had to feed us, and he hated the examinations, he hated the paperwork, he used to say a man should smell of honest sweat, not of other men's signatures." A pause. "And then he learned that signatures were the most powerful thing in Japan. Stronger than swords. You sign a boat's provisioning and the boat burns a village, and your hand never smells of smoke at all."

"That's why he did it," August said quietly. "Not cruelty. Efficiency. He found the weapon that fit his hand."

"Don't explain him to me." But there was no anger in it, only exhaustion. "I've been explaining him to myself for a month. I don't need help."

Near midnight the rain thickened and the lamp burned low, and Kenji stopped pacing. He stood at the table with the manifests spread before him, and August watched him take out a knife — a small one, a clerk's paper knife, bone-handled — and hold it above the page where his brother's name sat in her careful transcription.

He held it there a long time. Long enough that the blade trembled visibly in the lamplight. Long enough that August's heart climbed into her throat and stayed there, because she realized she did not know what she wanted him to do. Cut the name out, and betray the dead of Kaminoseki, and save the last piece of his family. Leave it, and betray his blood, and keep the truth whole enough to survive the scrutiny it was sailing toward. There was no clean side of the blade. There was only the choice of which wound to carry forward, and every person in Nagasaki was carrying one now — the sergeant at the paper gate who had lingered over her papers and let her pass, Vosse with his debts and his appetites, her father with his locked drawer and his silence. Everyone smuggling something. Everyone paying.

Kenji put the knife down.

"It stays," he said. His voice was very small. "It stays, and you print it, and when they come for him — if they come for him — I'll be the one who told them where to look. Do you understand what you're making me? I'm becoming the informer. For the right reasons, maybe. Maybe. But my mother won't know the reasons. She'll only know her elder son is in a cell and her younger son signed the paper."

August crossed the room and did the only thing she could think of. She took the manifests, folded them along their existing creases, and wrapped them again in the oilcloth — but she did not tie it. She held the bundle out to him.

"Then don't give them to me," she said. "Walk out with them. Drop them in the harbor. No one will ever know but the two of us, and I'll swear I never saw them."

He looked at the bundle. She watched him want it — watched the wanting move through his face like weather. And then he laughed again, that dry awful laugh, and pushed her hands back toward her.

"No. Because you'd swear it and mean it, and then you'd dig the proofs out of the harbor yourself, swimming, in winter, because you're your father's daughter and you measure your life in what can be carried and you'd carry this if it killed you." He tied the cord himself, roughly, his fingers clumsy. "Give it to Vosse. Put it on the ship. Let it damn whoever it damns, including my brother, including me if it comes to that." He pulled up his hood. At the door he stopped, his back to her, rain hammering the stair beyond. "August. If he hangs — if they take him apart — I need you to remember that I chose this with my eyes open. Don't you dare pity me later. Pity is for people who didn't know."

"I won't pity you," she said. "I'll remember you. It's worth more."

He went down the stairs into the rain, and she listened to his footsteps fade toward the harbor, and then she sat alone in the lamplight with the bundle in her lap and her hands around it, feeling its weight change — not in ounces, but in something scales couldn't measure. This morning it had been evidence. Now it was a brother. Now it was a mother who would not understand. Now it was Kenji's open eyes, chosen and unrescuable.

She thought of her mother's typecase, the story half-told, the habit of measuring the world in what could be carried. Some things could be carried only because you refused to weigh them first. She suspected her mother had known that too.

Tomorrow she would take the manifests to Hendrik Vosse, and Vosse would smile his merchant's smile and find a way to profit from her grief, and the papers would go aboard a Dutch hull and out into the wide indifferent world. Tonight she banked the lamp, and lay down in the type loft among the smell of lead and ink, and did not sleep.

Somewhere below, her father turned in his bed, not knowing what his daughter now carried. Somewhere across the dark quarter, a broad-shouldered man who had once bought a hungry girl sweet chestnuts slept beside his wife, his signature drying in an office ledger, his name already sewn into the lining of history.

And somewhere south of the bridge, in a house she would not see again for a long time, Kenji sat awake with a bone-handled knife he had not used, sharpening nothing, waiting for a dawn he had purchased with his own hand.

Three days remained before the tide turned and the Dutch ship took on cargo. Three days, and one checkpoint left between the truth and the sea.

August closed her eyes and began, in the dark, to count what could be carried.
