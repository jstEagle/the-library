## 13. What My Mother Carried

The night the city burned, August could not sleep in the type loft, and so she did what she had always done when sleep refused her: she opened her mother's typecase.

It sat in the corner of the loft where it had sat for eleven years, a shallow wooden box the size of a bread loaf, its corners worn round by handling, its lacquer cracked along one side like a river delta seen from a great height. Her father never touched it. August had learned early that the case was hers alone — not by instruction, never by instruction, her father did not speak of such things — but by the simple fact that when she was six years old and had reached for it, his hand had hovered near hers and then withdrawn, the way a man withdraws his hand from a flame he has already been burned by.

Outside, the fires had moved east along the harbor road. The glow came through the loft's small window in a slow pulse, orange and then dark and then orange again, like the breathing of some enormous animal lying down to die. Smoke threaded the gaps in the shutters. Somewhere below, in the street, men were shouting in the clipped, frightened way of men who are not yet sure what they are shouting about.

August set the case on her knees and opened the lid.

Inside, the type was arranged as her mother had left it. Not sorted into the proper compartments of a printer's case — not the neat geography of capitals and lowercase, figures and spaces — but gathered in small deliberate clusters, as if the woman who had packed it had been choosing letters the way one chooses jewels before a flight: not everything, only what could not be borne to lose. August had counted them a hundred times as a child. Forty-one pieces. She knew each one by the shape of its nick, by the particular wear of its face, though she had never printed with them. They were her mother's hand in metal, and printing with them would have been like speaking with a dead woman's voice, and August had not been brave enough for that at six, and was not brave enough for it now.

She lifted the top cluster into the firelight. An *A*. A *v*. An *e*. The letters of a word she had never been able to complete, because the case held no *s* and no *t* and no second *r* — she had checked, at eight, at twelve, at sixteen, kneeling on this same floor with the patience of a child who believes that if she looks carefully enough the missing pieces will have arrived, the way missing people arrive, the way her mother had never arrived.

Her mother's name had been Liesel. August knew this the way she knew the harbor tides — as a fact of the world, constant, unexplained. Liesel Dravenmoor, who had come to Nagasaki with a Dutch trading family as a girl of nineteen, who had married a typeface cutter with ink in the creases of his knuckles, who had given birth to a daughter in a year of bad rice and then, seven years later, walked out of the shop on a morning in the plum rain season carrying one typecase and a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and had not come back.

*She went to Edo,* her father had said, in the first weeks, when August asked. *There is work there.*

*When will she return?*

*When the work is finished.*

He had said it the way he set a line of type — firmly, each word in its place, no room for the line to shift. August had believed him at seven, and at nine, and at eleven, and then one day — she could not remember which day, only that it was winter and the harbor had frozen at its edges and the shop had been very quiet — she had understood, in the way children understand such things, all at once and forever, that her father did not know where the work was, or whether it was finished, or whether it would ever be finished, and that he had not known for years.

She had never asked him again. It was the only cruelty they had ever practiced on each other: the cruelty of not asking.

* * *

But she had asked her mother. Once.

She was five years old — no, six, the winter before — and she had climbed onto her mother's lap in the room behind the shop while Liesel mended a hem by lamplight, and August had asked the question children ask, the terrible simple one: *Where are you from?*

Her mother's needle had stopped.

August remembered the silence the way she remembered the weight of type — in her hands, in her bones. The lamp had hissed. Outside, a boat bell had rung twice, and the water had carried the sound away. And then her mother had said, in her accented Japanese, the accent that the neighbors smiled at and the merchants did not, the accent that marked her forever as a woman from somewhere else:

*From a place with winter. A real winter. The canals freeze and the whole city walks on them.*

*Can we go?*

*No.* And then, softer, the needle moving again, the thread pulling through the cloth: *No, little one. That door is closed.*

*Why?*

Her mother had looked at her then — August could still summon the look, could still feel it, the full weight of those gray eyes, so pale in a face the harbor sun had browned — and she had said something August had spent her whole life turning over like a stone, looking for what was underneath.

*Because I carried something out of that country that they wanted back. And a door that is closed behind you cannot be opened from the far side.*

*What did you carry?*

Her mother had smiled. It had not been a sad smile, exactly. It had been the smile of a woman standing at a window, looking at weather. *Letters,* she had said. *I carried letters.*

And August, six years old, had laughed, because her mother was a printer's wife and the shop was full of letters, cases and cases of them, and it was the funniest answer in the world. *Silly. You can't carry letters. They're too heavy.*

*Not those letters,* her mother had said, and pulled the hem tight, and bitten the thread, and the conversation was over, and it was the only conversation they ever had about it, and August had carried it — the half of it, the broken half — for eleven years.

* * *

Now she sat in the loft with the fires breathing outside the window and the case open on her knees, and she was seventeen, and the city she had been born in was burning, and her father was downstairs asleep — or not asleep, she could hear him, the small sounds of a man lying awake, the creak of the bedframe when he turned, the cough he tried to swallow — and she understood her mother for the first time.

Not everything. Not the country, not the canals, not the thing that had been wanted back. But the *shape* of it. The shape of a woman standing in a room at night, deciding what to take.

Because that was what her mother had done. August saw it now with a clarity that felt almost physical, almost painful, the way a key turns and you feel the lock's resistance give. Her mother had stood in a room — in this room, perhaps, this loft, or the room below where the presses stood — and she had known that she was leaving, and that she could carry one case, and she had opened every case in the shop and she had chosen.

Not the most useful letters. A printer fleeing to Edo, to set type in some other shop, would have taken a full case, or the matrices, or the money hidden — August had found the money hidden years ago, behind a loose board, and it was still there, untouched, which told its own story. Her mother had not taken what was useful. She had taken what was *hers*. Forty-one pieces, in clusters, arranged by a hand that knew it was arranging them for the last time. Letters that spelled, perhaps, a name. A word. A sentence. Something that could be carried in a box the size of a loaf of bread and could not be carried any other way at all.

And August understood — sitting in the smoke-glow, the case warm under her hands as if the metal remembered skin — that she had inherited this. Not the case. The *measuring*. All her life she had measured the world in what could be carried. When she walked through the fish market she counted exits. When she entered a room she noted what she would take if she had one minute, if she had ten. When Kenji talked about the ships in the harbor she found herself calculating not their cargo but their capacity — how many people, how many cases, how much of a life could be folded into a hold and carried out past the point where the water changed color.

She had thought it was caution. She had thought it was the ordinary sense of a shopkeeper's daughter in a city where inspectors came without knocking. But it was not caution. It was inheritance. It was her mother's last night in this house, transmitted through the case like a current through wire, arriving in August's hands every time she counted what could be carried, and it had never stopped arriving, not for one day in eleven years.

Her mother had known she would not come back. That was the thing August had never let herself know, and now she knew it, and it did not break her the way she had always feared it would. It *settled* her, the way type settles into a form when the quoins are tightened — everything suddenly fixed, suddenly bearing weight, suddenly capable of taking an impression.

Her mother had packed the case knowing. She had kissed her daughter's hair — August remembered that, the last morning, the smell of lamp oil and camellia oil in her mother's hair, the pressure of lips at her crown — and she had walked into the plum rain carrying letters, and she had known.

* * *

The fires shifted. Through the shutter-gaps the light turned from orange to a deeper red, and the shouting in the street changed key — not fear now, but something with rhythm to it, something almost like exultation, and August's hands went still on the case. The loyalists were moving east. The storehouses would go next, then the granaries, then — her stomach tightened — the quarter where the inspectors kept their offices, and after the offices came the searches, and after the searches came the arrests, and her father's shop held a hidden galley of set type that named names, that proved a massacre, that could hang every man who had touched it.

*Some truths must outlive their printers.* He had said it three days ago, standing in the doorway with the inspectors' cart still rattling in his ears, and he had not explained, and she had hated him for not explaining, and she had lain awake hating him, and now, holding her mother's case in the firelight, she understood that too.

Her father had done what her mother had done. He had stood in a room and decided what could be carried and what must be left, and what he had chosen to carry was not metal and not paper but a fact — a massacre, a lie, the names of the men who had ordered it — and he had set it in type because type was the only vessel he had, the only case big enough, and he had known, when he locked the drawer, that he was packing for a journey he might not survive. He had measured his own life in what could be carried, and he had found that the truth weighed less than his safety, and he had carried it.

And he had not told her. Because that was the other inheritance, the one that ran underneath: you did not tell. You packed in silence. You kissed the child's hair and you walked into the rain and you let them believe, as long as belief could last, that the door was not closed behind you, that the work was not finished, that you would come back.

August closed the case. Her fingers rested on the lid a moment, on the cracked lacquer, on the delta of the break.

Then she did something she had never done. She opened it again, and she took out the letters, all forty-one, and she arranged them on the floorboards in the light that came through the shutter, moving them with the tips of her fingers the way her father moved type, and she tried — as she had tried at eight, at twelve, at sixteen — to make them speak.

*A*. *v*. *e*. *M*. *a*. *r*. *i*. *a*. *N*. *o*. *o*. *r*. *d*. *e*. *r*. *k*. *o*. *o*. *k*.

She stopped.

She looked at the letters. She looked at them for a long time, the firelight pulsing on the floor, the shouting rising and falling outside, her father's bedframe creaking below.

*Ave Maria Noorderkook.* No. *Noorderk—*

*Noorderkwartier.* The north quarter. A district — she had heard the name in her mother's stories, in the fragments that slipped out over the years, the canals, the winter, the church bells — a district of a Dutch town, a town whose name she had never been told, because the name was the one thing her mother had not carried, the one thing too dangerous to bring through the closed door.

Her mother had left her a place and not a name. A direction and not a destination. A compass bearing and no map.

August gathered the letters up, all forty-one, and put them back in their clusters, exactly as they had lain, and closed the case, and set it in its corner. And she knelt there in the dark with her hands flat on the floorboards, and she said aloud, very quietly, to a woman eleven years gone, in a city on fire:

*I understand now. I understand what you carried.*

And what she meant was not the letters. What she meant was: I understand that you carried *me*. That everything you left behind was left so that something could be carried forward. That the measuring — the counting, the exits, the one case, the one truth — was not fear. It was love, wearing the only clothes it had.

Below her, the bedframe creaked. Her father was awake. She could hear him now, moving, the soft tread of bare feet on the stairs, and then his voice, low, at the foot of the ladder:

*August. Come down. We have to talk about the harbor.*

She rose. She took one last look at the case in the corner — her mother's forty-one letters, her father's locked drawer, her own name that she had never chosen and might soon have to leave behind like everything else — and she went down the ladder into the smoke-smelling dark, toward the voice, toward the harbor, toward the thing that could be carried and the thing that could not.

Outside, the fires reached the granary row, and the sky over Nagasaki turned the color of a forge.
