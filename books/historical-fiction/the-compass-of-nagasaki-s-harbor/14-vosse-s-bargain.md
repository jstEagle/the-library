## 14. Vosse's Bargain

The trading house of Hendrik Vosse sat on Dejima's edge like a fat man leaning over a fence, all shuttered windows and speculative angles, its foundations planted in the harbor mud as though the building itself were waiting for a ship. August had passed it a hundred times in her life — the Dutch factor's clerks buying ink from her father, the smell of tallow and salted herring drifting from its loading doors — but she had never been inside until the morning she came carrying her father's ruin sewn into her coat.

Now she came again, three days later, and the door was opened by the same clerk with the same incurious eyes, and she understood that she had crossed some invisible line between visitor and possession. The house had taken her measure. Houses like this one did that; they were built for it.

Vosse received her in a room above the water. The floorboards were scarred oak, imported whole from Rotterdam forty years before, and they creaked underfoot with a sound like an old man clearing his throat before delivering bad news. He sat behind a desk of black lacquer — Japanese work, August noted, and good work, bought cheap no doubt from a family that had needed rice more than heirlooms — and he did not rise when she entered. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with a merchant's face: nothing cruel in it, nothing kind either, just a landscape of calculations that had long ago worn away whatever features had come before.

"Fräulein Dravenmoor." He spoke English, as he always did with her, his vowels rounded and slightly too careful, a man who had learned languages the way other men learned card games. "Sit. You'll take coffee? It's real. Not the chicory the Chinese quarter sells."

"I don't want coffee."

"Nobody ever wants coffee. They drink it anyway, because refusing small comforts is how people convince themselves they're still making large decisions." He poured two cups regardless, and pushed one across the desk toward her, and the smell of it — dark, bitter, honest — filled the room like an accusation. "You've thought about my offer."

"You said you'd carry the documents aboard the *Oosterschelde*. That was the offer."

"That was the opening bid." He smiled without warmth, the way a scale tips without opinion. "I'm a trader, Miss Dravenmoor. I deal in goods. What you brought me is the most valuable cargo to enter this harbor since the treaty ports opened, and you brought it to me wrapped in linen like a dead cat. I've read it. Twice. My clerk has translated it properly. A village called Minamisawa. Forty-one souls. Blamed on foreign sailors — on *my* countrymen, incidentally, which gives me a personal interest. And the proof is set in your father's type, which any printer in Nagasaki could identify blindfolded."

August kept her hands still in her lap. She had learned that from watching her father across the composing stone: hands that moved were hands that confessed.

"The documents leave on your ship," she said. "That was the arrangement."

"The arrangement," Vosse agreed pleasantly, "was made by a frightened girl who came to me because she had nowhere else. Frightened girls have poor leverage. But you're not frightened anymore, are you? I can see it. Something happened to you in the last three days. You've stopped asking whether the documents will survive and started asking what they're worth."

She hated him then, suddenly and completely, the way one hates a mirror.

* * *

She had spent those three days in the type loft above the burned quarter, sleeping in the narrow space between the cases of ten-point Caslon and the window that faced the harbor, listening to the city cough smoke. The shop still stood — her father had been released after questioning, or rather returned, hollow-eyed and silent, and had said only that the inspectors would come back — but the government storehouses along the canal were charred skeletons, and patrols doubled each night, and Kenji had stopped meeting her at the fish market altogether. When she'd finally cornered him near the customs sheds, he had pressed a folded paper into her hand without looking at her and walked away, and inside the paper was a single line in his careful clerk's hand: *They know about the manifests. My brother's name is in them. Decide fast.*

Decide fast. As if truth were a fish that spoiled.

And now Vosse leaned back in his chair with his fingers laced over his belly and named his price, and August understood that she had sailed all this way only to arrive at a second bridge, and this one was guarded not by soldiers but by arithmetic.

"Exclusive publication rights," Vosse said. "The pamphlet prints first in Batavia, under my imprint. Then the treaty papers — Shanghai, Hong Kong, eventually London if the story holds. No other edition. No other translation authorized. And your silence, Miss Dravenmoor, on a related matter."

"Which matter?"

He drew a paper from the drawer — not the proofs, something else, a shipping manifest, and even upside down she recognized the format, because she had grown up among paper the way farmers' children grow up among soil. "My house carries certain cargoes for certain parties in Satsuma and Chōshū. Weapons, mostly. Some coin. The same loyalist gentlemen whose massacre your father documented also buy their powder through my warehouse. I profit from the men your pamphlet condemns, and I will continue to profit from them, because a factor who refuses business on moral grounds is a factor who starves morally and otherwise. If the story breaks and anyone traces the arms trade through my house — if your name surfaces connected to mine in a way that invites questions — I lose everything. So you will sign this." He turned the manifest around and slid it toward her. "An affidavit, witnessed by my clerk, stating that the documents you delivered were found abandoned in the harbor district, provenance unknown. Your father's name appears nowhere. Your name appears nowhere. The truth arrives in the world orphaned, and I am merely its foster father."

"And the type blocks?"

"Still wanted. Still part of the price. The original matrices are worth more than the pamphlet — every future printing pays a toll to whoever holds the cuts. Your father cut that face himself, didn't he? The 'Nagasaki Dravenmoor'? There isn't another like it in Asia." He said it with genuine admiration, which made it worse. "A beautiful face. Honest letterforms. It deserves to keep working."

"It deserves to belong to him."

"He belongs to the shogunate's jailers as of last week's questioning, and to worse than that when the next round comes. Sentiment is a luxury good, Miss Dravenmoor, and you can't afford it. Neither can I, which is why I'm offering you terms instead of simply taking the documents. I could, you know. You handed them to me. Legally speaking—" he smiled again, that tipping-scale smile, "—there is no legally speaking. We are both beyond law in this room. The only thing holding us together is that we each need the other's silence. That's not trust. That's ballast."

August looked at the affidavit. The clerk's translation was precise, bloodless, professional. *Found materials, provenance unknown.* Her father's four months of night work — the cutting, the setting, the proofing by lamplight while she slept ten feet away believing he was merely perfecting a new italic — reduced to flotsam. Washed up. Ownerless.

"Some truths must outlive their printers," her father had said, standing in the shop with the lamp between them like a wall. He had refused to explain, refused to repent, refused even to argue. She had thought it was principle. Now, sitting in Vosse's office, she wondered if it had been prophecy — because here was the outliving, and it looked like this: a fat Dutchman's ledger, a lie of provenance, a signature at the bottom in her own hand.

"If I refuse?" she asked.

"Then the *Oosterschelde* sails Thursday without your documents, and you may try the bridge, which is sealed, or the mountain roads, where the patrols shoot first and file reports later. Or you may keep the proofs hidden in your coat until the inspectors find them, and they will find them, because they found your father once already and men who break once break easier the second time." He said it gently, almost kindly, and that gentleness was the cruelest instrument in the room. "You have until the tide turns. Roughly two hours. The coffee will keep."

* * *

She walked to the window while he pretended to review correspondence, and looked down at the harbor, and did the arithmetic she would spend the rest of her life doing.

The truth, unaltered, stayed in Nagasaki and died there — burned with the shop, buried with her father, a footnote in some inspector's report marked *destroyed*. The truth, altered, left on Thursday. It would reach Batavia bent — stripped of its maker, stripped of her, wearing Vosse's imprint like a borrowed coat — but it would reach. Forty-one names would be spoken aloud in foreign parliaments. The faction that butchered Minamisawa and hung the crime around innocent sailors' necks would find its account published in letters her father had cut with his own hands, and no power in Edo could unprint it.

Bent truth or buried truth. Those were the choices. Nobody had told her, growing up among the lead and the ink, that truth was a commodity with freight charges.

Her mother had fled with a typecase and half a story. August had sworn, in the secret ledger of her heart where she recorded such vows, that she would be the one who carried things whole. And now she stood at a Dutchman's window with the tide turning, about to sign her father out of his own history, and the vow lay in pieces around her feet like pied type spilled across a stone floor.

There was one thing, she realized. One small thing Vosse had not thought to price.

The affidavit said *provenance unknown*. But paper could be made to say many things, and August Dravenmoor had been raised in a print shop — she knew better than anyone alive that what mattered was never what the page said, but what was hidden in its margins, in the kerning, in the spaces between words where only a compositor would think to look. Vosse wanted her silence. He had never specified its texture.

She turned from the window.

"I'll sign," she said. "On one condition. The first printing uses my father's typeface exactly as cut — no recutting, no substitution. If a single letterform is altered, I'll write the true provenance myself and send copies to every consulate in the treaty ports. You'll have sold one edition and poisoned the well for all the rest."

Vosse studied her for a long moment, and she watched the calculations move behind his eyes like fish under ice, weighing threat against profit, and finding — barely, narrowly — that the scales held.

"A printer's daughter to the bone," he said at last, almost fondly. "Very well. The face stays intact. Sign."

The pen was heavy in her hand, English steel, and the ink was her own shop's ink — she recognized the smell, iron-gall and lampblack, bought from her father at a fair price for years — and she wrote her name in the space provided, and the letters came out steady, which she would remember afterward with a strange shame, that her hand had not shaken at all.

Vosse blotted the page, folded it into his drawer, and rang for the clerk. "The documents go aboard tonight. You'll want to be elsewhere when they do. And Miss Dravenmoor—" he paused at the door himself, holding it open for her, a courtesy performed with the precision of a man closing a transaction, "—you should know that I don't consider this settled. Factors never do. But I think you and I understand each other: we are both going to live with what we signed, and neither of us is going to enjoy the taste."

Outside, the wind had shifted, and it carried the smell of the burned storehouses across the water, and beneath it, fainter, the smell of fresh-cut pine from the shipwrights' yards — the harbor building and burning in the same breath. August pulled her collar up and turned toward the city, toward the loft, toward whatever remained of the hours before the tide.

She had signed. The truth would sail on Thursday, orphaned and bent and alive.

And as she crossed the canal bridge — the small one, the footbridge, still open, still unguarded — she saw them at the far end: two men in shogunal colors, walking slowly, unhurried, asking questions of the fishwives, moving upstream against the crowd like blood flowing the wrong way through a vein.

One of them held up a sheet of paper. Even at fifty paces, even in the flat gray light, August knew the shape of a printed notice when she saw one.

She knew, too, whose typeface it was set in.
