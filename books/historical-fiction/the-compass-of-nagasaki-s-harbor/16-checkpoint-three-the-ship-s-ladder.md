## 16. Checkpoint Three: The Ship's Ladder

The linen had to be wet. That was the whole of it — the small, absurd physics on which everything now balanced. Dry laundry could be opened. Dry laundry could be shaken out over a plank table while a bored soldier ran his hands along the seams of a bundle looking for paper, for type, for the weight of truth folded into a woman's burden. But wet linen was heavy, and cold, and no man wanted to put his fingers into it twice.

August had learned this from watching the laundresses themselves.

For three days she had sat on the seawall below the Dutch factor's warehouse with a basket at her feet that did not belong to her, watching the women who served the foreign ships come down to the water's edge in the gray hour before dawn. They came in twos and threes, heads wrapped against the harbor wind, carrying bundles of soiled shirts and tablecloths down to the washing stones, and they came back up an hour later with the same bundles wrung out and dripping, and they climbed the ship's ladder without anyone stopping them, because everyone stopped them once and only once, and after that they were furniture. Part of the harbor's daily machinery. A woman with wet linen was as unremarkable as a gull.

She had counted the guards at the third checkpoint eleven times. There were never fewer than four and never more than six. Two stood at the foot of the ladder where it met the stone quay, two patrolled the length of the customs shed beside it, and one — always one, always the same man, broad through the shoulders with a scar that split his left eyebrow like a badly mended seam — walked the quay itself, slow, unhurried, his eyes doing the work his feet were too lazy for. He was the one who had turned back the rice merchant on Tuesday. He was the one who had made the young laundress with the crooked teeth open her bundle on Wednesday, though he had waved her through afterward with something almost like apology.

He was the one August would have to pass.

* * *

She prepared in the type loft above what had been the shop, by candlelight, with the patience her father had taught her and none of the joy he would have brought to it.

The proofs came out of their hiding place behind the loose board — twelve sheets, set in his own hand, each letterform cut and cast in the foundry he had built with his father before her, each line a small accusation in metal made flesh on paper. She had read them so many times now that she could recite them the way other people recited prayers: the village name, the number of the dead, the dates, the names of the officers whose seals appeared on orders that had never been meant to survive. Her father's masterpiece. The thing he had known would cost him everything, printed anyway, because some truths must outlive their printers.

She had not let herself think those words since the night the soldiers took him. Now she let them come, and they hurt going down like swallowed glass.

Twelve sheets. She divided them into three packets and wrapped each in oilcloth, and then she took the oldest, coarsest linen she owned — a bedsheet gone gray with years, a shirt of her father's that she had never been able to throw away — and she soaked them in the rain barrel until they hung from her hands like dead things. She wrapped the packets inside the wet cloth, deep in the center, where a hand thrust carelessly into a bundle would find nothing but cold and sopping weight. Then she wound the whole of it tight, tied it with the rough cord the laundresses used, and set it in a willow basket that smelled of lye and river mud, which she had bought from an actual laundress for three mon and a compliment about her son.

Over it all she laid two genuinely wet garments, so that the first thing any searching hand touched would be honest.

The disguise was simpler than she had expected it to be, and this frightened her more than complexity would have. She bound her breasts flat. She rubbed ash and lye-water into her hands until they looked scoured raw, which was not entirely a performance; they had been raw for days. She dressed in the padded jacket and dark trousers of a working woman, wrapped her head low, and practiced, alone in the loft, walking like someone whose back ached and whose day had started before the birds. It was astonishing, she thought, how little separated one person from another. A posture. A smell. The willingness to be overlooked.

At the bottom of the basket, beneath the false bottom she had built herself from slats and canvas, she placed the last thing: a single piece of type. Not from the pamphlet. Something older. An ornamental initial, a compass rose her father had cut when she was nine years old, the first face she had ever helped him polish. She told herself it was a talisman. She knew it was a promise — that whatever happened on that ladder, on that quay, in the hold of that ship, the hand that had made her would make it across the water too.

She blew out the candle and sat in the dark for a long time, listening to the city breathe below her.

Somewhere out there, her father was in a cell. Somewhere out there, the shop's sign hung charred above a doorway filled with ash. And somewhere out there, Kenji was sitting in a room with a copy of a shipping manifest that named his brother among the butchers of Minamikata, waiting to learn whether the sister of the printer would include him or spare him. She had not yet decided. She carried both versions of the truth in her chest like twin blades, and she did not know which one would be in her hand when the moment came.

That was tomorrow's arithmetic. Tonight there was only the ladder.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of pewter, and Nagasaki's harbor wore its fog like a woman wearing a veil at a funeral she did not wish to be seen weeping at.

August joined the stream of laundresses at the washing stones below the customs shed, and for one hour she was exactly what she appeared to be. She scrubbed. She wrung. Her knuckles burned in the cold water and she welcomed the pain, because pain kept her in her body, kept her a laundress with aching hands instead of a fugitive with a hammering heart. Around her the women talked in the low, rolling cadence of the working morning — a husband drunk again, a daughter's betrothal, the price of charcoal — and one of them, the old one with the laugh like a cart on gravel, teased August about scrubbing so hard she'd wear holes in a shirt that had already given up on life.

"First week?" the old woman asked, not unkindly.

"Something like that," August said, and discovered that her voice, pitched low and flattened of all its edges, held.

They went up together when the light was right — that narrow window, she had learned, between the changing of the patrol and the moment the harbor watch grew bored enough to start looking closely at anything. The fog was thinning but not gone. The Dutch ship rode at her moorings forty yards off the quay, the *Vrouwe Anna*, three masts and a hull the color of old tar, her rails lined with idle sailors who watched the city the way men watch a fire they are glad is not theirs.

The ladder was a rope-and-plank affair fixed to the quay's edge, sagging under decades of use, descending to a line of sampans and lighters that ferried goods and servants out to the anchored ships. At its head stood the checkpoint. Two soldiers. The customs shed. And coming along the quay, unhurried as tide, the sergeant with the split eyebrow.

August picked up her basket.

It weighed what truth weighs. Perhaps eight pounds of paper and oilcloth and wet linen, perhaps the whole of her father's life, perhaps her own. She settled it on her hip the way she had seen a hundred women settle a hundred baskets, and she walked toward the ladder with her head down and her shoulders rounded and her breath measured in counts of four, the way her mother had taught her to breathe through fever.

"Name and business." The soldier at the head of the ladder was young. His voice had the flat boredom of a boy repeating a formula.

"Laundry," August said. "For the Dutch ship."

He glanced at the basket. Wet cloth showed at the top, gray and dripping. His nose wrinkled. "Go."

One step. That was all it took, sometimes. One step past the point where turning around became suspicious, and you were committed, and the world narrowed to the width of a plank.

Her foot found the first rung.

"Oi."

The voice came from behind her, and August knew it before she turned, the way you know thunder belongs to the cloud you have been watching all morning. The sergeant with the split eyebrow stood ten paces away, and he was not looking at the other laundresses, and he was not looking at the ship, and he was looking at her.

"You," he said. "The new one. Set it down."

* * *

There is a moment — August would remember this all her life, would return to it in the years after like a tongue returning to a broken tooth — when the body understands it is about to be undone and offers, absurdly, every escape at once. Run. Drop the basket in the harbor. Scream. Faint. In the space of a single heartbeat her mind laid all of these before her like a merchant laying out cloth, and her hands, her father's hands, trained in precision, trained in patience, trained to do the correct thing a thousand times without thinking — her hands simply bent her knees and set the basket on the stones.

"Gently," the sergeant said. "You'll spill the wash."

"Yes, honored one." She kept her eyes down. Down was safe. Down was where laundresses looked.

He crouched beside the basket. She watched his shadow fall across it, watched his hand hover, and she understood with terrible clarity that he was in no hurry at all. This was a man who enjoyed the pause. Who had learned, somewhere in the long school of petty authority, that the moment before the search was worth more than the search itself.

"Wet linen," he said. "For the foreigners. They can't wash their own?"

"They pay," August said. "Better than I can refuse."

Someone behind her laughed — one of the soldiers, or maybe one of the laundresses still in line. The sound broke something loose in the morning, loosened the sergeant's shoulders by a degree. He reached into the basket.

His hand closed on the top garment. Her father's shirt. Soaked through, heavy with water, honest as anything in the world. He lifted it, wrung it once with a twist of the wrist that spoke of a mother who had made him do his own washing once upon a time, and dropped it back.

"The whole bundle's soaked through," he said. "What do you wrap it in, river water?"

"Lye and cold spring," August said. "The whites don't come clean otherwise. The Dutch are particular."

"The Dutch are particular," he repeated, and there was something in his voice she couldn't read — not suspicion exactly, not quite belief either. He was looking at the basket the way her father looked at a page of type that had been set by an unfamiliar hand: reading it for errors, for tells, for the small wrongnesses that betrayed a stranger's work.

His hand went in again.

Deeper this time. Past the first layer. She felt rather than saw his fingers find the second garment, the gray sheet, and begin to work through it, and she stood absolutely still with her eyes fixed on a crack between two paving stones and her heart beating so hard she was certain he must hear it, certain the whole quay must hear it, certain the fog itself would tremble with it.

Under the gray sheet: the corded bundle. Under the corded bundle: oilcloth. Under the oilcloth: her father's handwriting, twelve pages of it, enough to hang a faction and free a truth and bury her before noon.

The sergeant's fingers stopped.

"Haraguchi," he said to the younger soldier, without turning his head. "The boat from Hirado. Is it in yet?"

"Not due till midday, Sergeant."

"Then we've time." He withdrew his hand slowly, and August watched it emerge empty, dripping, holding nothing but water and the ghost of her hope. He rose to his feet with the care of a man whose knees had opinions, and he looked at her — actually looked, for the first time, not at the basket but at the bowed head and the ash-gray hands and the shape of a woman trying very hard to be no shape at all.

"Your face," he said quietly. "Show me your face."

* * *

Later — much later, in a hold that smelled of hemp and pepper, pressed against crates with her arms around a bundle of proofs — August would try to reconstruct why she lifted her head. Obedience was the obvious answer: refusal was the privilege of people with power, and she had none. Fear was another. But neither was true, not entirely. What she felt in that moment, standing on the quay with the fog lifting and the *Vrouwe Anna*'s bell ringing the half hour across the water, was something closer to recognition. He had asked. And some part of her — the part her father had built letter by letter, the part that believed a thing hidden was a thing already lost — decided that if she was to be taken, she would be taken as herself.

She raised her head.

The fog had thinned to lace. Gray light fell on her face, and she watched it land there, watched him see her: young, hollow-eyed, unmistakably a woman who had not slept, and — she saw it register in him, a flicker like a lamp guttering — unmistakably not merely a laundress. He saw it. She knew he saw it. Whatever it was he saw — grief, or gentility, or the specific desperation of someone carrying something heavier than linen — it moved across his scarred face like weather.

For a long moment neither of them moved. On the water, a sampan knocked against the pilings. Somewhere in the city a dog barked itself hoarse at nothing.

"My daughter does washing for the trading houses," the sergeant said at last, and his voice had changed, gone quieter, gone somewhere private. "She stands in that water until her hands crack. For four mon a bundle." He looked past her, out at the harbor, at the Dutch ship riding her anchor with her sails furled and her boats lowered, ready within the hour to take on the last of her cargo and go. "Everyone in this city is carrying something for someone else. Most of it isn't ours to carry."

"Honored one—"

"I didn't see your face," he said. "Fog. Bad light. Pick up your basket."

August's hands obeyed before her mind caught up. She lifted the basket — lighter now, impossibly, though nothing had been removed — and settled it on her hip, and turned toward the ladder, and her legs carried her the three steps to the first rung on pure borrowed momentum.

"Wait."

She stopped. Every muscle locked. Behind her she heard him shift his weight, heard the leather of his gear creak, and then his voice came again, low, pitched for her alone:

"The ship leaves on the morning tide tomorrow. After that, the harbor closes for the season — official order, posted tonight. Whatever you're carrying, whoever you are—" a pause, long enough for her heart to stop and start again, "—don't be on this quay when it closes. Understood?"

"Understood," she whispered.

"And tell whoever taught you to walk like that," he added, and there was, unbelievably, something at the edge of his voice that might have been the ghost of a smile, "that they taught you well. But not well enough for me."

She climbed.

The rungs were slick with spray and her palms screamed where the lye had cracked them, and she did not look back, because looking back was the one thing she could not afford — not at the sergeant, not at the quay, not at the city stacked up its hillside in the clearing fog with its smoke and its bridges and its cell where her father sat refusing to say her name. She climbed with the basket banging gently against her hip, twelve pages of the dead village pressing cold and damp against her side, and halfway up, where the ladder swayed with the wake of a passing junk, a hand shot out of nowhere and closed on her sleeve.

She nearly fell. The basket lurched. Below her, the water of the harbor turned slow and green and patient, forty feet of it, deep enough to drown a woman and swallow a bundle of oiled paper forever.

"Steady, miss." A sailor's voice, above her this time, accented, amused. A lean Dutchman in a salt-stained jersey leaned down from the ship's rail, gripping her sleeve with one brown hand, hauling her the last few feet as easily as hauling a net. "You looked like you were climbing to heaven and reconsidering."

"Only to the deck," August managed.

"Heaven's cheaper," he said, and pulled her over the rail, and the solid wood of the *Vrouwe Anna*'s deck came up under her feet like the answer to a question she had been asking all her life.

Behind her, on the quay, the sergeant had already turned away. Ahead of her, down the deck and toward the shadow of the mainmast, a figure in a fine wool coat stood with his hands clasped behind his back, watching her arrive with the expression of a man checking a delivery against an invoice.

Hendrik Vosse smiled.

"Right on time," he said. "And just in time, Miss Dravenmoor. We have a great deal to discuss before the tide turns — beginning with what else you're willing to carry for me."
