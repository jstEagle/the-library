## 1. The Weight of Water

The tide at Port Mahon did not turn so much as it changed its mind.

Coral Sterling had learned this before he learned his letters, standing knee-deep in the shallows while his father pointed to the water and said, *Watch. It lies first, then it moves.* The sea would go still — a stillness like held breath, like a room where someone has just said something unforgivable — and then, without warning, the whole grey-green body of it would begin to slide sideways, dragging its cold weight back toward the deep beds, and every man on the shore would know that the working day was over whether the company's clock agreed or not.

Fifteen years later, Coral could read that stillness from inside a building. He stood now at the high window of the Aldous Trading Company's counting house, a ledger open on the desk before him, and he knew — without looking, without being told — that the morning tide had begun its retreat, because the light on the water had gone flat and pewter-coloured, and because old Tammaso down on the jetty had stopped coiling rope and started watching the horizon instead. The water lied first. Then it moved. Everything else on this island had learned the trick from the sea.

Behind him, the counting house smelled of ink and camphor and the sweet rot of paper stored too long against a damp wall. Shelves rose to the ceiling beams, groaning under thirty years of volumes: dive logs, wage books, cargo manifests, correspondence copies, each one bound in green baize with the company's stamp pressed into the cover in gold leaf that had dulled to the colour of old brass. Somewhere in those shelves was a record of every pearl ever pulled from the beds east of the causeway, every shell, every wage, every debt. Somewhere in those shelves, if a man knew how to look, was a record of every silence too.

Coral dipped his pen.

*October the 9th, 1899. Bed Seven, outer shoal. Divers deployed: eleven. Weather fair, water clear, current moderate. Yield: four hundredweight shell, pearls of merchant grade as itemized. No incidents.*

He wrote *no incidents* the way a man might write his own name — smoothly, without hesitation, the letters small and even, because unevenness invited questions and questions were how men drowned on dry land.

The entry was false. He had known it would be false before he sat down, and he would know tomorrow's would be false before he wrote it, and the knowing had long ago stopped feeling like anything at all — the way a scar stops itching, the way a bad tooth settles into the jaw and becomes part of the architecture of the face. Bed Seven had yielded barely two hundredweight. The water had been murky past the third fathom, stirred up by the spring tides, and the divers had come up empty-handed more often than not, their baskets light, their faces bloodless behind the glass of their masks when they broke the surface gasping through the air-hose. And one of them — Danio, who was twenty-six and broad as a door and laughed like a landslide — had found, in the last hour of the last working day, a cluster of shells so rich that the overseer himself had waded out to look at them, whistling through his teeth.

Fourteen pearls of the first water. Coral had seen them laid out on the sorting cloth, wet and luminous, glowing with that soft inner light that made grown men go quiet. They were worth more than Danio would earn in six years of diving, and under the terms of the bed-share — the terms printed on the notice board in three languages, none of them the language Danio dreamed in — a find like that earned a diver a bonus. A real one. Cash. Enough to buy his daughter shoes. Enough to pay down a little of the boat-debt that hung around the necks of every diving family on the eastern shore like an anchor no one could see.

But the bonus required certification. The certification required the log. And the log, by the time it reached Mr. Quillan's desk, required a yield figure consistent with the quarterly projections that Mr. Quillan had already sent to the mainland office, projections that assumed Bed Seven was nearly worked out and due for closure. A fourteen-pearl find in a bed scheduled for abandonment raised questions. Questions raised inquiries. Inquiries, in Coral's experience, ended with names in ledgers and men in the water.

So he wrote *four hundredweight* when the true figure was two, and he entered the pearls as *merchant grade, assorted* rather than *first water, clustered*, and he left the column for *special finds* blank, and somewhere between his pen and Mr. Quillan's initials, Danio's bonus would evaporate like spray off the jetty. Danio would never be told there had been a bonus. He would simply work another season, and another, and the shoes would wait, and the debt would keep its slow patient arithmetic, and no one would ever say aloud that anything had been taken, because nothing had been taken — it simply failed to appear, the way the tide fails to arrive when the moon has other plans.

That was the genius of it. That was what Coral had understood at fifteen, copying fair sheets in this same room while the factor's clerk dictated figures over his shoulder: the ledger did not need to lie loudly. It only needed to lie quietly, in columns and carry-forwards, in roundings and reclassifications, until the lie was indistinguishable from weather. Men did not drown here the way they drowned in stories, dragged under screaming. They drowned in margins. Their lives were adjusted. Their deaths were written down as *misadventure*, initialled, filed, and the file grew fat and green-bound and respectable on its shelf, and the island went on breathing.

Coral blotted the page. His hand was steady. He noticed that his hand was steady, and hated it a little, the way you hate a callus — proof of the work, proof of the wearing.

* * *

He carried the log down to the factor's office at half past ten, as he did every working day, and he climbed the stairs slowly because the stairs gave him time to become the version of himself that Mr. Quillan expected. There were several versions of Coral Sterling, and he kept them the way a diver keeps knives: the clerk-diver, competent and incurious, for the counting house; the quiet island boy, respectful and slightly dull, for the magistrate's clerks; the trusted one, careful and grateful, for Quillan alone. Fifteen years of practice. He could put any of them on in the time it took to climb twelve steps.

Quillan's office occupied the front corner of the building, with windows on two sides — one facing the harbour, one facing the bridge. The causeway bridge ran out from the island toward the mainland like a stone arm, a mile and a half of pilings and planking that carried carts and cable and, twice a week, the mail steamer's lighter. In October the monsoon would close it. Everyone knew the date; the tides kept it; the bridge company posted it. But this year, Coral had heard, the closure would come early. *For security,* the word was. Nobody had asked whose.

Quillan looked up when Coral entered, and smiled, and the smile was genuine, which was the worst thing about it. He was a spare man of fifty with a clerk's stoop and a fisherman's hands, and he wore his kindness the way other men wore coats — easily, in all weathers, and always over something.

"Bed Seven," Coral said, laying the log on the blotter.

Quillan read it the way he read everything: quickly, and twice. His eyes went to the yield line, then to the special-finds column, and Coral watched him register the blank space there, and watch him understand that Coral had understood, and watch him be pleased by it. It happened in perhaps two seconds. It happened every day. It was, Coral sometimes thought, the true business of the Aldous Trading Company — not pearls, but this: the daily silent confirmation, between one man and another, of what would not be said.

"Clean work," Quillan said. "You've a tidy hand, Sterling. Better than Hargate ever had." Hargate had been the previous clerk-diver, promoted off-island two years ago, and no one spoke of what had become of him, which meant everyone knew.

"Thank you, sir."

"The beds are thinning." Quillan leaned back, lacing his fingers over his waistcoat. "You know that better than anyone. You've dived them since you were a boy. A company can't pay bonuses on a dying bed — it wouldn't be honest, would it? Money has to follow truth." He said this without a flicker, looking Coral full in the face, and Coral held the look because holding looks was the first thing the counting house had taught him. "Danio worked hard today. I'll see he gets a cask of oil for his household. Oil's practical."

"Yes, sir."

"That's all." And then, as Coral reached the door, pleasantly, almost as an afterthought: "Your father dove Bed Seven, didn't he. Back in the old days."

Coral turned. Quillan was smiling again, mildly, a man making conversation about weather.

"He did, sir."

"They were richer beds then." Quillan picked up his pen. "Everything thins. That's the century teaching us its lesson, I expect. Off you go."

Coral went. On the stairs his heart was doing something complicated that he refused to name, and he made himself breathe the way his father had taught him for the descent — slow in, slower out, because panic spent air faster than work did — and by the bottom step he was level again, and the thing Quillan had said was folded away in the place where he kept things, the place that was getting fuller every year, the place that had no shelf or stamp or green baize binding but was, he suspected, the truest ledger on the island.

* * *

In the afternoon the light came low and golden across the eastern flats, and Coral walked out to the shallows where the boats were drawn up, because the log was done and the counting house released him to the beds for the evening sort, and because — though he would have denied it under oath — he needed to stand among the divers and feel the size of what he'd done that morning.

They were mending and sorting and arguing, a dozen men on the shingle with their suits hanging from a rack like flayed skins, and the smell was kelp and tar and the particular salt-sweet stink of opened shell. Children ran between the boats. Someone was frying fish over a driftwood fire, and the smoke went sideways in the wind, flat along the water, pointing at the bridge the way smoke always did here, as if even it wanted to leave.

Danio saw him coming and raised a hand — easy, unguarded, the wave of a man to a friend. That was the cruelty of it. Danio liked him. They had dived together in the training years, before Coral's lungs proved better suited to ledgers than depths, and Danio used to haul him out of the shallows laughing, calling him *little clerk-fish*. Danio's daughter came running now and hung off Coral's arm, demanding to know if he could write her name, and he knelt and wrote it in the wet sand with a stick — *Lissi*, the letters big and crooked — and she demanded he make it permanent, and he said the tide was the island's penman and took whatever it liked, and she laughed and ran off, shoeless, her feet slapping the shingle.

Shoeless. He had looked straight at it and written *no incidents*.

"Clerk-fish." Danio dropped down beside him on the boat's gunwale, offering a share of fried fish wrapped in leaf. "They tell me Bed Seven closes after this season. True?"

"So the projections say."

"And the pearls?" Danio chewed, squinting out at the water, which had gone the colour of hammered tin. "A bed that thin doesn't give up what we pulled today. Fourteen in one basket, Coral. First water. My luck, eh? Lissi's winter solved, one good hour on the bottom." He laughed, warm and certain, a man dividing a fortune he had already spent. "The factor will certify it, yes? Fair is fair, even for a company."

The fish tasted of ash. Coral looked out at the tide, which was lying again — that flat, breath-held stillness — and he performed the oldest trick he owned: he said nothing, and let the silence do the lying for him.

"You'll want to ask Quillan yourself," he said at last. "Certification comes from his office."

"You're his man." Danio nudged him, friendly. "Put a word in. One word, clerk-fish. You write the numbers; you know what we brought up."

"I write what's measured," Coral said, and heard, as he said it, exactly how it sounded — clean, official, hollow as a shell with the animal gone out of it.

Danio studied him a moment longer than was comfortable. Then he shrugged, the great shoulders rolling, and clapped Coral's knee hard enough to sting. "Well. The sea gives and the company takes. Old story. My father sang it." He stood, stretched, and went back to his nets, and the moment closed over the two of them like water over a dropped coin.

Coral stayed on the gunwale until the light failed. Behind him the fires burned down; ahead, far out along the causeway, the bridge lamps were being lit early, one by one, a string of small yellow eyes staring at the island through the dusk. He thought about the crates he'd seen yesterday on the magistrate's jetty, stencilled *BALLAST*, stacked too high and guarded too well for ballast. He thought about the closure coming early, *for security*, and whose security, and against whom. He thought about the green-bound shelves upstairs, thirty years of them, and about the fact that he had just added one more quiet lie to their number, and that Danio's daughter would go another winter without shoes, and that the sea would come in tonight and erase her name from the sand without anyone filing a report.

The tide turned. He felt it before he saw it — the stillness breaking, the whole grey body of the water beginning to slide — and he got up and walked back along the shingle toward the lights of the counting house, a trusted man going home to his trusted bed, carrying nothing anyone could weigh.

It was three days later, in the grey of a rising tide, that the shout went up from the northern shallows, and the men came running with ropes and grapnels, and the sea gave back the first thing it had been holding all season.
