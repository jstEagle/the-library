## 7. The Divers' Grudge

The tide went out at dawn the way it always did, dragging its hem of foam across the flats, and Sari went with it, because the flats were where the men gathered when the boats were beached and the beds were closed and there was nothing left to a man but his voice.

She had mended nets beside these men her whole life. She had salted their catch, buried their dead, sewn the black bands on their sleeves when the sea took them. She knew the shape of their grief the way she knew the shape of a torn mesh — where it gaped, where it held, where a knot would slip if you pulled it wrong. And she knew, before she reached them, that this morning her hands would be pulling the wrong knot. There are mends that hold and mends that only make the tear tidy.

The brotherhood met on the flats below the drying racks, eleven men in a half-circle facing the water, because the brotherhood had always faced the water even when the water was the thing that ate them. Old Tuan Bima sat on an upturned hull, his legs stiff with the cold that had settled into them thirty years ago and never left. The others stood or squatted, their faces turned toward the grey line where the sea ended and the monsoon sky began. Nobody spoke. The silence of the brotherhood was not an empty silence. It was a silence with weight, like a net full of wet sand, and Sari had learned long ago that you did not break it from outside. You waited to be given a place inside it.

She did not wait this morning.

"I need you to hear me," she said.

Eleven faces turned. Eleven pairs of eyes, and not one of them soft. She had expected that. She had dressed for it, in a manner of speaking — she had left her widow's white at home and put on the plain working grey, the net-mender's grey, the color of a woman with a task and no time for mourning. Mourning would come later, or it would not. First the task.

"Eka Surya did not die by accident," she said. "The company killed him. I have proof, and I need you to swear testimony."

The silence held for the length of three waves. Then Tuan Bima laughed — not the laugh of a man amused, but the dry cough of a man who has heard a joke so old it has fossilized.

"Proof," he said. "The wife brings proof. Where did a net-mender find proof, eh? Washed up on the tide with her husband?"

"Eka copied the ledgers," Sari said. "For months. He was carrying them across the bridge when they drowned him for it. I have seen the pages with my own eyes. Names. Dates. Payouts. Every diver the company insured who died in the beds — and how the company collected on every one."

She had rehearsed this. She had rehearsed it in the dark of her house, with Eka's cold pages spread on the floor before her like a spread of fish, and in her rehearsal the men had gone quiet, then angry, then risen together the way the brotherhood rose when a boat was lost — one grief, one voice. In her rehearsal the anger had a direction.

Here on the flats, the anger had no direction at all. It turned on her like a fish turning on the hook.

"Ledgers." A younger man, Rafa, whose father had drowned the same year as hers. "You bring us paper. Paper is theirs. Paper has always been theirs. My father couldn't write his own name and the company wrote it for him — wrote it in a book, wrote a debt under it, wrote *deceased* under that. You bring us more of their paper and call it proof."

"It is *his* hand," Sari said. "Eka's own hand. He copied—"

"I know whose hand." Rafa stood. He was not tall but he stood like the mast stands, and his voice carried down the flats to the drying racks where the women had stopped working to listen, because everything on this island stopped to listen when the brotherhood raised its voice. "I know Eka's hand. I also know whose hand taught Eka to read a ledger in the first place. Whose hand signed the dive logs. Whose hand sat in the counting house while our fathers drowned and wrote *misadventure* in a fair round script."

The name came before she could stop it, the way the tide comes before you can stop it.

"Sterling," Rafa said. "The clerk-diver. The company's own. You come here wearing his errand, widow, and you think we don't smell him on you?"

* * *

She had known this would come. She had known it the way you know a storm is coming — by the pressure in your ears, by the way the birds went silent. But knowing a storm and standing in it are different weathers entirely.

"Coral Sterling brought me Eka's coat," she said. "He was sent to inventory the body and he found what Eka hid in the lining, and instead of burning it or burying it or handing it to Quillan, he brought it to me. He has risked—"

"He has risked *nothing*," said Tuan Bima, and his voice was worse than Rafa's because it was old, and old grief has deeper water under it. "A man risks something when he has something to lose. What has that boy ever lost? He eats from the company's table. He sleeps under the company's roof. Fifteen years, girl. Fifteen years he has been the firm's bright-eyed little pen, and in fifteen years how many of ours went into the beds and did not come up? And every time, whose signature was on the log?"

Sari's hands closed at her sides. Her hands were her pride — a net-mender's hands, strong and patient — and they wanted to close around someone's throat.

"He was eight years old," she said. "He was sold the same as any of them. Sold younger. His father's debt—"

"His *father*," Rafa said, and the word came out of him like a splinter coming out, slow and bleeding.

The half-circle shifted. Men looked at the sand. Men looked at the water. One or two looked at Sari with something that was almost pity, which was worse than the anger, because pity was what you gave to someone standing in the wrong place.

"You were not here," Rafa said, "when the north bed went down. You were not here when six ropes went slack in one morning. My uncle was on that rope. Tuan Bima's brother was on that rope. And when we dug them out of the collapse — what was left of them — the company sent a man to count the bodies and write the report, and the man wrote that the bed was worked too deep, that the ropes were set wrong, that the *divers* were at fault for greed." He stepped closer. He smelled of tar and salt and the inside of grief, which smells like all of those. "And the man who signed that report — the man who wrote *diver error* over my uncle's body — was Sterling. The father. The one whose debt the company so generously took the boy for."

"That is not what—"

"You *know*," Tuan Bima said softly, and softness from that old man was the most frightening thing on the flats. "You know what the story is. Everyone on this island knows the story. The Sterlings were the company's family before the company had a family. Old Sterling kept their books, dove their beds, set their ropes, and when the north bed killed six of ours, the company needed a name to hang it on, and they hung it on him — and then, oh, the beautiful arithmetic of it — they took his son for the debt the scandal cost them. A dead man can't argue a ledger. And his orphan grows up in the counting house, learning the same hand, signing the same logs, and we are asked — *asked*, widow — to believe that the son is different from the father because the son brings us a dead man's coat."

"He found the pages," Sari said, and her voice cracked and she hated it. "He could have destroyed them. He gave them to me."

"He gave them to *you*," Rafa said. "Not to us. Not to the brotherhood. To one widow with no boat and no voice. Why do you think that is? Because he trusts us? Or because he needs us and does not trust us, which is the company's own arithmetic, learned at the company's own table?" He spat into the sand. "A man who has spent fifteen years being the knife does not become the hand by handing the knife to a widow."

* * *

She stayed. That was the thing she would remember afterward, in the nights when she turned this morning over like a stone looking for what was underneath: she stayed. She did not gather her dignity and walk back up the flats. She stood in the half-circle's cold center with the tide sighing out beyond them and she took what they gave her, because Eka had died carrying pages and she would not make his death cheap by flinching from the men those pages were meant to save.

"Tell me what you want," she said, when they had finished. The wind came off the water and moved through the drying racks behind her with a sound like the whole island whispering.

"Want?" Tuan Bima laughed his dry cough again.

"You want him to suffer. You want him to have been the traitor you decided he was when he was eight years old and took the company's bread while your fathers drowned. Fine. Say it plainly. But then tell me what you want *after*." She looked at each of them, one by one, the way she looked at a net, finding the weak places. "Because Quillan does not want anything after. Quillan wants this morning to end with you turning me away and the pages going into a stove and Eka's name going into the same column as all the others — *misadventure*, *misadventure*, *misadventure* — until the column is so long it stops meaning anything. That is what the company wants. And everything you have said to me this morning, every true and bitter word, serves the company better than any of Quillan's own men could."

"Careful," Rafa said.

"No," Sari said. "You have had fifteen years to be careful and it bought you nothing but the right to stand on a beach and count your dead. I have had three days of being a widow and I am already done with it." She pulled the folded pages from inside her jacket — not the master copy, she was not a fool, but three pages, three names, three dates — and held them out. "Eka died for these. He learned to read their numbers because he said a man who can read the trap can walk out of it. He believed the brotherhood would stand when the time came. He believed it the way he believed in the tide. And I am telling you the time is coming, and when it comes you can stand or you can stand on this beach counting your dead until you die of it, and the company will write *misadventure* over that too."

Nobody took the pages. She had not expected them to. She laid them on the upturned hull beside Tuan Bima's stiff legs, weighted them with a stone from the flats, and turned to go.

"The father," Tuan Bima said behind her.

She stopped. The wind pushed at her back like a hand.

"You want to know what the story is," the old man said. "Here is what the story is, from a man who was on the water that day. The north bed was worked too deep because the company's factor ordered it worked too deep, and every man on the rope knew it, and we dove anyway because the debt-books gave us no choice. And when the bed came down, the company needed the factor's order to disappear. So they put it in old Sterling's hand. Made him sign the report against his own log. Made him write *diver error* with his own fair round script, and the man did it — do you know why? Because they held his son's indenture over him. Sign, or the boy goes to the mainland, to the ships, to the places company boys go and don't come back from." The old man's voice had gone quiet as the tide's far edge. "He signed. And six men stayed dead, and he lived two more years with what he'd signed, and then the sea took him too, and everyone called it even. Even! As if the sea keeps accounts." He was silent a moment. "I have hated that man fifteen years in the ground. And lately, widow, I have begun to hate the company more for what it made him sign than for what it made him pay. But hate is not testimony, and I am too old to be wrong twice."

Sari turned. The old man was looking at the water, not at her.

"The boy," Tuan Bima said. "If he is his father's son, he will do what his father could not. He will put his own name where the company's name should be. Not the widow's errand. Not the dead man's coat. His own name, his own hand, his own count — spoken to our faces, on this beach, where we can see whether it is the father's script or the son's." He picked up the stone from the pages and weighed it in his palm. "Until then, take your paper home, widow. Paper keeps. Grudges keep better."

* * *

She walked back up the flats with the wind full against her, and the women at the drying racks watched her come and asked nothing, because the women of the shore asked nothing of each other that the face could answer.

In her house she sat down among Eka's things — his oilskin, his weight-belt, the little brass compass he had worn on a cord and that she had not yet been able to put away — and she let herself be angry, briefly, cleanly, the way a squall comes off the water and is gone. Angry at Rafa's splinter-voice. Angry at Tuan Bima's terrible softness. Angry most of all at the arithmetic of the island, which turned every grief into a debt and every debt into a chain and taught the chained to hate each other for the length of their links.

And then, underneath the anger, the other thing. The thing she had heard in the old man's story and knew he had not meant to give her.

*He will put his own name where the company's name should be.*

Tuan Bima thought he was setting a condition the boy could never meet. But Sari had seen Coral Sterling's face when he laid Eka's coat on her table. She had seen a man open a door he had spent fifteen years nailing shut. And she had seen, in the oldest of Eka's copied pages, in the margins of the earliest volumes, a fair round hand making small private marks — counts, corrections, a name underlined twice — that were not Eka's hand at all, and not the company's either.

She did not know yet what those marks meant. But she knew the boy was not sleeping. She knew the counting house kept its lamp burning past midnight, past the hour when any honest clerk had any honest reason for light.

And she thought: *he is already doing it. He is already putting his own hand where theirs should be. He just has not yet learned he must do it where we can see.*

She rose, and took down Eka's compass, and held it in her palm until the brass warmed. The needle swung, settled, held — not toward the bridge, not toward the counting house, but north, toward the water, toward the beds.

"Very well," she said to the needle, to the dead man, to the island. "Then I will make them look."

Outside, the tide had begun to turn, and somewhere across the darkening water a single lamp burned late in the counting house window, patient as a held breath.
