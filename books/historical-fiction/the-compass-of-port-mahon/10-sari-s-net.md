## 10. Sari's Net

The nets taught Sari everything she knew about holding things together.

A net was not rope. Rope was strength, and strength broke. A net was a thousand small agreements — knot after knot after knot, each one weak alone, each one useless alone, and together they held a living weight that fought against them all night long. You did not mend a net by being strong. You mended it by knowing where the holes were.

She had learned this at her mother's knee, on this same stretch of shore, with the same wooden needle her mother had used before her, its handle worn to the shape of a woman's grip. Eka used to laugh at it. *You could buy ten new needles for what your time is worth,* he'd say, and she would tell him: *A needle is worth nothing until it's in a hand that knows the water.* He stopped laughing somewhere around the third year of their marriage, when his own boat came home light two seasons running and hers — the women's nets, the mended ones, the ones everyone said were past saving — brought in fish while the men's new-purchased gear rotted at the moorings.

Now she sat in the doorway of her house with the needle in her hand and a net across her lap that belonged to no one, because its owner was dead, and the company had written him off as weather.

The pages lay beneath the netting. That had been Coral Sterling's idea, or perhaps it had been hers and he had only been clever enough to see it — afterward, she could never remember whose thought it had been first, which was, she supposed, what trust looked like from the inside. The copied ledger leaves, thin as fish skin, folded into eighths and worked into the mesh where a repair patch would sit. From three feet away, a mended net was a mended net. From three feet away, everything on this island was what it pretended to be.

*You read the water better than any clerk,* Coral had told her, and she had wanted to strike him for the flattery, because flattery was the company's coin and she knew its weight. But he hadn't been flattering. He'd been frightened, and asking for help, and there was no word for that kind of asking except *trust*, and so she had given it, the way you give blood to family — without being asked twice, without expecting it back.

The tide was going out. In an hour the boats would come up the shallows and the women would go down to meet them, as they had every evening of every season since before the company had a name, and the nets would change hands, and nobody would look too closely at anything, because looking closely was men's work and men's work was done in counting rooms with doors that shut.

Sari began to knot.

* * *

It took nine days to build the web, and she built it the way she built anything: one knot at a time, each one tested before the next was tied.

She started with Ibu Rani, who salted fish at the eastern racks and had buried two sons in the beds and had been saying *accident* through her teeth for eleven years. Sari did not bring her pages. She brought her a length of twine and sat beside her at the racks and asked about the sons, and listened, and let the silence do what silence does when two women have both counted their dead alone. On the third day Ibu Rani said, without being asked, *My sister's husband runs the ferry to the salt pans. Nobody searches a ferry that smells of fish.* And that was the first knot.

Then Mara, the youngest of the menders, seventeen and sharp-eyed, whose father had drowned the year Coral Sterling went into the company house. Mara could fold a page small enough to hide inside a knot's eye and tie it so the mesh hung true. She asked no questions for four days. On the fifth day she asked one: *How many?* And Sari told her the truth — *We don't know yet; that's why the pages have to leave* — and Mara nodded and went back to work, and that was the second knot.

Then the salter widows, then the two old women who dried squid on the north beach and saw every boat that passed, then the wife of the customs skiff-man, who owed Sari's mother a debt from a famine year and paid it now without ever naming it. Nine knots. Ten. Each woman told one other woman, never two. Each knew her own stretch of the line and nothing else — where the pages entered her hands and where they left them, and nothing between. Sari had watched the company work for fifteen years. The company kept records. Records were how they owned people. So the web would keep no records at all. If one strand broke, the rest would hold, and no one broken strand could name the others, because there was nothing to name.

By the twelfth day, pages were moving. Inside mended nets bound for the fishing boats, inside bundles of repaired gear sent out to the lighthouse keeper's wife, inside the false bottoms of fish baskets that went to the salt pans and from there, Ibu Rani promised, onto the mainland ferries when the weather allowed. Not to anyone in particular yet — Coral was still working on that end, on names and addresses and the dangerous business of finding someone outside the island who would listen — but moving. Out of the house. Off the shore. Into the world's hands a leaf at a time, the way water takes a cliff apart.

Sari slept four hours a night and mended nets sixteen, and had never in her life felt less tired.

* * *

The divers' brotherhood met on Thursdays, in the long shed behind the drying racks, and their voices carried, because men who spend their lives underwater learn to speak loudly on land.

Sari stood outside that shed on the fourth Thursday with a bundle under her arm and let herself listen to them refuse her again. She had gone to them once already, politely, with Coral's name left out, and they had said what she knew they would say: *The sea takes men; that is the sea.* Old Tomas had said it, Tomas who had dived beside Eka for twenty years, and the others had looked at their hands, and the meeting had ended the way meetings ended when the truth was too heavy to lift.

But politeness was the company's language, and she was done speaking it.

She walked in. The talk died. Forty faces turned toward her, and forty bodies shifted, because a widow walking into the brotherhood's shed uninvited was a thing that happened perhaps once in a generation, and everyone understood that something was ending or beginning.

"I brought your dead," she said, and set the bundle on the trestle table.

She unwrapped it slowly. Not the pages — those were too precious, and besides, these men could not read a ledger. She brought instead the other count, the one every diving family kept without knowing it was a count: the mourning cloths. White cloth squares, one per dead man, stitched with his name by his widow or his mother, kept in the house until grief grew old enough to be private. She had spent six evenings collecting them. Women did not refuse a request made over a dead husband's name.

Forty-one cloths. She laid them out across the table in rows, and the rows reached the table's end and went over the edge, and still there were more, and the men looked at them the way men look at water when it has gone suddenly quiet.

"Eka Surya," she said, touching the first cloth. "Pulled from the shallows in the closing month. The company wrote *misadventure*." Her finger moved down the rows. "Danu Wirya. Two years ago. Misadventure. His widow got a payout she never asked for and a debt she couldn't refuse." Another. "Bayu Aji. Misadventure. Ketut Sari — no kin of mine, whatever the name says — misadventure. Six men in the bed collapse eight years ago. One accident, six times written."

"Those are the sea's accounts," Tomas said, but his voice had lost its floor.

"The sea doesn't pay insurance." She let that stand in the shed's silence until it finished growing. "Your brothers die at a rate no water explains, and every time one dies, money moves in the company's books. I've seen the pages. There is a man on this island who can show you — the clerk, Sterling, the one you hate — and the pages name your fathers and your sons, not as debtors but as *claims*. As profit. Your deaths are a crop, and someone harvests them."

"You come here with his name?" A younger diver stood, one of the ones who remembered the bread, the child Sterling climbing into the masters' house while the beds closed over his father. "His father's collapse killed six of ours—"

"His father's name is in those pages too," Sari said, "and not where you think. Not as the man who broke the bed. As the man who found out what the beds were for, and died for it, and had his death called a debt." She looked around the shed, at the faces hard and wet and unwilling. "You hate the boy for taking their bread. Fine. Hate him. But ask yourselves who baked it, and who decided which children ate and which fathers drowned, and whether your hatred has ever once kept one of your brothers above water."

Nobody answered. The mourning cloths lay in their rows like a fleet becalmed.

"I'm not asking you to love him," she said. "I'm asking you to count. It's the only thing this island has ever asked of women, counting our dead quietly, and we've done it so well that men built a trade on top of it. So count. Out loud. With your names attached. Because paper burns" — she touched the bundle, the hidden pages beneath the cloths — "but forty-one voices in one room, saying forty-one names, that burns slower. That might not burn at all."

She rolled up the cloths, one by one, unhurried, and tied the bundle, and walked out of the shed with her back straight and her heart hammering against her ribs like a fish in a trap.

Behind her, no one spoke. But no one had told her to leave, either, and in the brotherhood's grammar, that was almost a sentence.

* * *

That night the wind turned, and the first real storm of the season came down off the open water, and Sari sat up listening to it test the roof beams and thought: *the boats will be confined to harbor now, week upon week, and the nets will stop moving.*

The web would go slack. The pages would pile up in hiding places that Quillan's men were, even now, learning to sniff out — because one of her knots had frayed, she was nearly sure of it, one of the young salters who had gone quiet and careful in a way that quiet and careful should never look on a girl of nineteen.

She sat with her mother's needle and a net across her lap and no pages inside it, just the work itself, knot after knot, and let the storm say what it had to say.

Mending, she thought, was the oldest power on this island, and it had always been invisible, because invisible was what made it safe. But invisible things could also be moved. A net that could not go to sea could still be sold, traded, lent, bartered for rice. Cargo moved in storm season or the island starved. And cargo, she thought, her needle pausing, cargo went through one place before it left Port Mahon.

The company's own warehouse. The company's own export bales. The company's own manifest, signed by the company's own clerk — the boy they trusted, the traitor, the son of the man who had counted before him.

Sari looked down at the knot in her hands and laughed, low, once, into the wind.

If the truth had to travel, let it travel first class — packed in the thief's own goods, sealed with the thief's own seal, carried out under the thief's own flag. Let them search every fishing boat on the island and never once think to open their own property.

She put down the needle. Tomorrow she would find Coral Sterling, and she would watch his face when she told him where his ledgers were going to ride.

Outside, the storm leaned on the house, and somewhere down the shore a shutter banged like a signal, and Sari smiled at the dark, because the dark had finally given her something back.
