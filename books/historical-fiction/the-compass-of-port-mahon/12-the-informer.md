## 12. The Informer

The rain came in off the strait that morning like a debt collector — no warning, no negotiation, just the grey weight of it settling over Port Mahon and refusing to leave. Coral stood at the counting room window with a pen behind his ear and watched the water climb the sea wall, and he thought about nets.

Sari's nets were moving again. Three boats had gone out before the squall closed the harbor mouth, their holds full of mended gear bound for the fishing camps on the windward shore, and folded into the tarred twine of those nets were pages from his own fair hand — copies of copies of the company's arithmetic of death. He had written them by lamplight in a hand not his own, disguised as shipping tallies, and every time he finished one he felt the strange double sensation of a man both burying and exhuming a body.

He was still watching the rain when the boy came through the door soaked to the skin, hatless, breath ragged.

"Clerk Sterling." The boy was one of Orense's runners, fourteen maybe, with the flat eyes children acquire early on this island if they work for the magistrate. "Mr. Quillan wants you at the salt house. Now."

"Is something wrong?"

"He said now." The boy did not repeat himself twice. Nobody on Port Mahon repeated themselves for clerks.

Coral took his oilskin and his ledger satchel because a clerk without a satchel was a man without a reason to exist, and followed the runner out into the drowning light. The streets ran with water the color of pewter. Women pulled shutters closed against the squall and did not meet his eye, which was normal, which had always been normal, but today it made the back of his neck prickle like the air before lightning.

The salt house stood at the harbor's edge, a long low building where fish were packed in coarse crystals for the mainland trade. Even from thirty paces Coral could tell something was wrong, because there were men outside it who should not have been — two of Orense's constables under an awning, smoking against the rain, and beside them a figure hunched on an upturned crate with a blanket around his shoulders that was not a kindness but a costume.

Tomas Reyes. Sixteen years old. A salter's son, third generation, with salt scars on his forearms like pale rivers branching. He had carried pages twice in November, tucked inside bundles of dried mackerel, and each time he had come back grinning with the particular joy of a boy who has discovered he matters.

Now he sat on the crate and stared at the ground between his feet, and when Coral passed close enough to see his face, Tomas flinched as though struck.

*They know,* Coral thought, with the terrible clarity of a diver who feels the line go slack above him. *God forgive us, they know.*

* * *

Inside, the salt house smelled of brine and iodine and old wood, and Mr. Quillan stood at the packing table with his sleeves rolled, sorting fish with his own hands as though he were a common factor and not the most powerful man on the island. That was Quillan's way: he performed labor the way other men performed piety, publicly, and expected it to be noted.

"Coral." He did not look up. "Come look at this catch. Mackerel's running small this year. The Chinese buyers will complain."

Coral came to the table. His heart was beating somewhere up near his collarbone. "The monsoon shifts the schools," he said. "It'll right itself after the new moon."

"Mm." Quillan selected a fish, held it to the lamplight, set it down. Around the room, four constables pretended to inspect barrels. "You know what I admire about you, Coral? You always know the reason for things. Tides, prices, weather. A man who understands causes is worth ten men who only understand effects." He looked up then, and his eyes were mild and gray and bottomless. "Take our young friend Tomas. Cause and effect. Do you know what caused him to be sitting out front wrapped in a blanket like an invalid?"

"I couldn't say, sir."

"No?" Quillan smiled faintly. "A patrol found him on the windward road at dawn. Carrying dried fish to the camps. Nothing wrong with that — boys carry fish all the time. Except this fish was heavy. Too heavy. And when the sergeant opened a bundle to see why a boy would pack his mackerel so tight—" Quillan turned another fish over, delicately, as if checking its gills. "Well. Paper keeps better than you'd think, inside tarred twine. Someone taught him that. Someone with neat hands."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, the rain redoubled against the roof like applause from a hostile crowd.

"What happened to the papers?" Coral asked. His voice came out level. Fifteen years of practice went into keeping it level.

"Burned, naturally. Along with everything else in the bundle." Quillan set down the fish and wiped his hands slowly on his apron, taking his time. "And the boy? The boy is the interesting part. Do you know what he told Sergeant Voss?"

"No, sir."

"Nothing. At first. Sat there like a stone for six hours." Quillan shook his head with something that might almost have been admiration. "Then Voss mentioned his mother. Mentioned that salt licenses are renewed in January, and that renewals depend on good conduct, and that widows have such a hard time keeping licenses on their own. And the boy wept like a tap turned on and told us about the nets. Not names — he swears he never knew names, and I believe him, because whoever organized this isn't careless. But routes. Boats. Which nets, which camps, how often." He paused. "Since October."

October. Before Eka Surya drowned. The network had been moving pages since before Coral even knew it existed. Sari had built it while her husband was still alive, or Eka had begun it himself — either way, months of work, months of pages, and now the whole map of it lay open on some sergeant's table like a gutted fish.

"You've been very quiet, Coral," Quillan said pleasantly.

"I'm trying to understand what this has to do with me, sir. I keep the dive ledgers. Nets and fish are the harbormaster's province."

"Of course they are." Quillan came around the table, unhurried, and stopped an arm's length away. Up close he smelled of camphor and wet wool, and his mildness was worse than any anger Coral had ever been shown. "That's exactly my point. It has nothing to do with you. A clerk-diver, promoted above his station, trusted with certification of deaths — what would he want with smuggled paper? Nothing. Nothing at all." He let the silence stretch until Coral could hear his own pulse in it. "I'm telling you this because you're useful, and because I dislike surprises, and because I want you to hear it from me rather than from the walls, which talk too much on this island. The boy gave us the roads. He didn't give us the river. When I find the river, Coral — and I will find it — I intend to be very precise about who swims in it and who merely lives on the bank."

"And the boy?"

Quillan's face softened into something that looked, from a distance, like mercy. "Released. This evening. With a caution." He picked up another mackerel, examined it, discarded it. "His mother's license will be reviewed in January, of course. Reviewed — not revoked. There's a difference, and I expect the family will spend the next two months being extremely clear that they understand the difference. Fear is a poor teacher, Coral, but it's thorough."

Outside, thunder rolled along the strait like furniture dragged across a ceiling.

"Thank you for informing me, sir," Coral said.

"Don't thank me." For just a moment — half a heartbeat, no longer — Quillan's eyes lost their mildness entirely, and Coral saw the thing underneath, the thing that had signed payouts for drowned men for fifteen years and slept soundly. "Gratitude implies a transaction. This wasn't a transaction. This was courtesy between men who understand each other." He rolled his sleeves back down, buttoned them, became once more the reasonable factor. "Go home, Coral. It's filthy weather for walking."

* * *

Coral walked home through the rain and did not go home.

He cut instead through the alley behind the chandlery, past the cooperage where the barrel staves sweated in the damp, and stood in the lee of a doorway watching the salt house across the lane. Within the hour, as promised, Tomas Reyes came out. The constables did not escort him; they simply watched him go, the way cats watch a mouse they've already decided not to eat yet. The boy walked fast, shoulders up around his ears, and turned toward the salters' row, and Coral let him pass without showing himself, because there was nothing left to say to him that wouldn't endanger them both.

But the image stayed. Sixteen years old, wrapped in a blanket that was a leash, weeping for his mother's license. Coral had been eight when the company first showed him that fear could be administered in installments — a little here, a little there, never enough to kill, always enough to steer. They had done it to him with bread and bed and the slow arithmetic of a debt that never shrank. They had done it to Tomas with a widow's livelihood. It was the same instrument. It had simply gotten more refined.

*I did this.* The thought arrived without drama, the way water arrives in a hull — not all at once but steadily, finding every seam. *I wrote the pages. I chose the couriers' routes with Sari. I looked at a sixteen-year-old salt boy with quick legs and a hungry grin and I put paper in his hands because he was available and eager and I did not ask myself what the company does to eager boys.*

He pressed his forehead against the cold wet planks of the doorway and breathed.

There was no version of this work without risk to someone. He knew that. Sari knew it better — she had said so plainly, standing in her doorway with the tide going out behind her: *Every net has a mesh size, Coral. Whatever you weave, something small slips through and something big gets kept. You choose what you can afford to lose.* He had agreed. He had believed he understood.

He had not understood until he saw the blanket around Tomas's shoulders.

* * *

That night the storm settled in for a long siege, and Coral sat at his table with the shuttered lamp burning low and tried to think like a clerk, because thinking like a clerk was the only discipline he had that could hold against panic.

Inventory. What did they have? What did they know?

The company had the routes — three of them, maybe four. The windward road was dead; the fishing camps would be watched now, every bundle opened, every net shaken out. Whatever moved from tonight onward had to move differently or not at all. The company had a confession — partial, nameless, but confirming that a network existed, which meant Quillan no longer had to wonder whether he was hunting a ghost. And the company had time. Five months of closed bridge, five months of sealed room, and all the patience in the world, because patience was what power could afford and the powerless could not.

What did they have? The master copy — wherever Sari had hidden it, she had not told him, and he understood now with a fresh chill that this had been wisdom and not distrust. They had his father's tin, still lying in the dark of the wreck where no one living but Coral knew to look. They had the divers, sour and unconvinced. They had the women of the shore, who mended and salted and carried and were invisible precisely because everyone had always agreed not to see them.

And they had a leak-shaped hole where trust used to be.

Because here was the question that would not stop circling: how had the patrol known to weigh a salt boy's fish? Random inspections happened, yes, but this was monsoon season, when even Orense's men preferred their fires to the windward road. Someone had pointed. Someone had seen Tomas take a bundle from a net-mender's shed, or seen the shed itself, or heard something in a tavern, and carried it to the sergeant, and the sergeant had known exactly where to apply pressure.

Or — and Coral made himself hold the thought up to the lamp and examine it, ugly side out — someone had wanted the network found. Someone who knew enough to sacrifice a route and a boy in order to make Quillan feel victorious, feel thorough, feel finished — while the real current ran somewhere else entirely.

He hated himself for the thought even as he tested it. That was the true cost of the day, he realized. Not the burned pages, not the route, not even the boy. The cost was that he could no longer verify anything. Every friendly face on this island now came with a question mark stitched into it like a maker's mark. The baker who saved him the end of the loaf. The diver who nodded to him on the seawall. Sari herself — no. No, not Sari. He refused that door, slammed it, barred it. A man needed one unexamined thing or he would go mad in the sealed room.

But everyone else. Everyone else was now a currency he could not count.

A knock at the shutter, soft, three taps, pause, two taps. The signal they'd agreed on weeks ago, back when signals had seemed like safety.

Coral killed the lamp. He crossed the room and lifted the shutter a finger's width. Rain blew in against his knuckles. In the dark beyond, hooded and streaming, stood Old Marta — the oldest net-mender on the shore, a woman with hands like driftwood who had buried a husband and two sons to the pearl beds and never once been heard to complain aloud.

"They're asking for you," she said, barely above the rain. "Down at the drying racks. She says come alone, and she says—" the old woman hesitated, and in the hesitation Coral's stomach dropped through the floorboards, "—she says to come by the long way. And Coral—" Marta's ancient eyes caught what little light the storm allowed. "She says the boy talked more than they think he did. She says you should know that before you decide anything."

The shutter closed. Coral stood in the dark with the rain ticking against the boards and the taste of salt in his mouth, and understood that whatever waited for him at the drying racks, the island he had woken up on this morning no longer existed.

He took the long way.
