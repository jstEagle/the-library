## 4. Mother's Salt Garden

The tide was going out when Noor came up the path to the drying sheds, and his mother was sitting in the doorway of their quarters with her hands full of nothing, sorting it.

He knew the look of her these days—the way her eyes went past him as if he were a window she'd grown used to seeing through. The company doctor had a word for it. *The sea takes the mind the way it takes the shore,* Noor had thought, watching her, *grain by grain, and no one marks the loss until the garden is gone.* But there were mornings—rare, sudden mornings—when the fog lifted off her entirely and she was sharp as a gutting knife, and those mornings frightened him more than the empty ones, because they reminded him what was being taken.

Today she looked up at him and smiled, and said, in the old tongue, soft as tide over shell:

"You're late, Yusuf. The boats are already out."

Noor stopped on the path.

His father had been dead eleven years. Drowned off the third reef in a season of bad water, his body never recovered, his name struck from the company rolls within the month because dead divers cost nothing and living ones cost wages. Noor had been nine years old. He had learned then that grief was a luxury the ledgers did not carry.

"I'm sorry," he said carefully, in her language. "I came as fast as I could."

She patted the step beside her. "Sit. Your supper's cold but your mother kept it warm." She laughed at her own joke, the same joke she'd made for forty years, and Noor sat, and the wood was warm from her body, and for a moment he let himself be Yusuf's son instead of Noor Montrose, which was not even his name, which was a name like a coat someone else had bought for him, cut wrong at the shoulders.

"You've been diving," she said, touching his wrist where the salt had dried white in the creases. "Your hands smell of the deep water. You always come back smelling of the deep water, Yusuf, even when you swear you only went to the shallows."

"The deep water pays better," he said, because that was what his father would have said.

"It pays nothing," she said, and her voice changed—not confused anymore, something older than confusion, something that had been waiting under the fog all along. "It pays nothing and it takes everything, and still you go. Your father's father went. His father before him. In our country we did not dive for pearls, you know."

"No?" He kept his voice level. He had learned to keep his voice level the way other men learned to swim.

"We farmed the salt." Her hands began moving again, sorting the nothing, shaping it into rows. "Not from the sea—from the earth. There is a place where the ground itself weeps brine, north of the city, past the fig orchards. My grandmother tended pans there. You rake the earth flat, so flat, and flood it shallow, and then you wait. That is the whole art of it. Waiting. The sun does the work if you let it. By evening the pan is white as a bride's sheet, and you lift the crust with wooden tools—never iron, iron spoils the taste—and carry it to the storehouse on your head."

She laughed again, softer. "You should have seen me, Yusuf. Sixteen years old with a hundred pounds of salt on my head, walking the levee road like a queen. Every man on the road looked. And I looked at none of them except one."

"Which one?" Noor asked, though he knew. He had heard this story in fragments a dozen times, each time a little more worn, like a coin passed through too many hands.

But tonight she didn't answer right away. Tonight the fog rolled back in behind her eyes, and she frowned at the path, at the drying racks, at the compound wall with its company stamp stenciled in fading black.

"This isn't our house," she said.

* * *

In her country—it was not called by its own name anymore; the maps had renamed it twice in her lifetime, and she refused both names—in her country the houses were built around courtyards, and the courtyards held a single bitter-orange tree, and beneath the tree there was always a well, and the well rope sang when it turned.

She was young in the courtyard. She was always young in the courtyard now; the years between had thinned to paper and blown away. Her mother was grinding grain inside, and the sound of it was the sound of home, a slow stone heartbeat, and outside the men were arguing about rain the way men argue about rain everywhere, which is to say loudly and without effect.

Her name, in that country, had meant *morning*. She had forgotten this until just now, sitting on a step in a place of corrugated tin and company rules, and the forgetting-then-remembering hurt like a cramp.

A boy came down the levee road with salt on his head. He walked straight-backed under the weight the way her grandmother had taught her to walk, spine long, chin level, because the salt must not spill and the bearer must not bow. He was brown as a nut and thin as a reed and he did not look at her, which of course meant she looked at him.

That was Yusuf. That was always Yusuf. Even here, even now, when the world had shrunk to a doorway and a path and a wall with a stamp on it, he came walking toward her out of the sun with the whole harvest balanced on his head, and she loved him the way you love a language: without deciding to, completely, and forever unable to stop.

"Yusuf," she said, and the boy on the step beside her turned, and he had his father's wrists and his father's habit of listening with his whole body, and for a moment—only a moment—she saw that it was not Yusuf at all, that Yusuf was thirty years drowned, that this was the child, grown tall, wearing his father's patience like a borrowed shirt.

The moment passed. Or she let it pass. It was kinder, some days, to let it pass.

"The salt needs turning," she told him. "You've let the pans go dry. A dry pan cracks, and a cracked pan holds nothing."

* * *

Noor listened to her talk about salt for an hour.

He understood, dimly, that she was not talking about salt. She was handing him things—one by one, carefully, the way you pass breakables across a crowded room. The fig orchards. The singing well rope. The wooden tools, never iron. The name that meant *morning*. Each thing was small. Each thing was true. And together they were the only inheritance his family had left, because the company had taken the papers, and the papers had taken the names, and the names had taken the country, and all that remained was a woman on a doorstep describing white fields to a son who had never seen them.

"Tell me about the city," he said. "The one near the orchards."

She was quiet so long he thought the fog had taken her again. Then:

"There was a gate in the old wall with a lion carved above it, but the lion had lost its face. Weather, or war—I don't know. Children used to dare each other to kiss the blank stone. I kissed it once." She smiled. "Yusuf dared me. He stood below and swore he'd carry my salt home if I did. So I kissed the faceless lion in front of everyone, and he carried my salt home, and my mother beat us both with a wet cloth, and it was worth it."

She turned to him suddenly, gripping his arm, and her eyes were clear—terribly clear, the clarity of deep water.

"Noor," she said. His real name. The name that existed on no ledger, that he had not heard spoken aloud since Brand's visit, since the census man's polite pen had begun scratching at the seam of their invented lives. "Listen to me. When I go, you will want to put me in the ground here, quick and quiet, so no one asks questions. Don't. Whatever else you do—don't let them write me down as no one. I was born in a house with a bitter-orange tree. Say it. Say it to whoever comes asking, even if it costs you. Especially if it costs you."

"Mother—"

"They're making lists." Her grip tightened. "I hear things. The walls here are thin and the clerks are careless. They're making lists of people whose papers don't sing true, and they'll put us on boats, and boats have berths, and berths run out. I'm old, Noor. I'm heavy cargo. But you—" her voice cracked, and she pressed his hand against her cheek, and her skin was salt-dry and warm, "you find Amara a place. Do you hear me? The girl has tomorrow in her. I only have yesterday, and yesterday travels light."

He could not speak. He nodded, once, and she seemed satisfied, and the fog came down over her face again like a curtain drawn by a gentle hand.

"Your supper's cold," she murmured, patting his knee. "But your mother kept it warm."

* * *

That night Noor did not sleep. He sat in the dark with a stub of pencil and the back of a discarded manifest, and he wrote down everything she had given him, while it was still warm from her hands.

*Bitter-orange tree. Singing well rope. Wooden tools, never iron. The gate with the faceless lion. A name that means morning.*

He wrote until the pencil wore to the wood, and then he looked at the list—a page of small true things, the first honest inventory his family had ever kept—and he understood that his mother had handed him something heavier than any salt harvest, and lighter than anything the company would ever weigh.

Across the compound, a lamp burned late in the records office. Noor watched it through the slats, thinking of lists, of berths, of arithmetic.

Then the lamp went out, and a figure crossed the yard toward the director's quarters, moving fast, carrying papers.
