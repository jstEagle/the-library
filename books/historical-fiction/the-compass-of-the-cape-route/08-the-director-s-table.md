## 8. The Director's Table

The summons came at dusk, folded into the hand of a boy who wouldn't meet my eyes.

*Mr. Montrose will see you after the office closes. Come alone.*

Montrose. Even the name was a forgery—a Dutchman's name worn by a man who had been born on a different continent under a different sky, the way I wore *Noor*, the way my mother wore her borrowed maiden name like a coat two sizes too large. The whole Cape Route Trading Company was built out of names that didn't belong to anyone.

I told Amara I had business with the paymaster. She looked at me the way she'd been looking at me all week—since Brand's second visit, since the word *repatriation* entered our house and sat down at the table like a guest who intended to stay. She knew I was lying. She let me lie anyway, which is its own kind of mercy.

The compound emptied by degrees. The clerks left in twos and threes, their voices thinning down the shell road. The lamplighter made his rounds. By the time I crossed the yard to the administration house, the only light burning was on the top floor, behind shutters that threw bars of gold across the veranda like the ribs of something enormous.

The houseboy took my hat and said nothing. That was the first strange thing—the silence of servants who have been instructed to be deaf. The stairs creaked under me with the particular loudness of stairs in an empty house.

He was waiting at the far end of a long room, seated at a table set for dinner for two. Not his usual desk—that was upstairs, behind the door with the brass plate. This table was lower, older, dark wood scarred by decades of elbows and decanters. A table where men settled things that never went into ledgers.

"Sit," he said. "You'll take wine."

It wasn't a question. He poured it himself, which was also not normal, and I understood then that everything about this evening would be slightly wrong, deliberately wrong, arranged to make me feel that I had stepped out of the world where rules applied.

"I'll be brief," he said, "because you strike me as a man who prefers brevity." He smiled. He had a good smile. It was one of the things people said about him, that he could smile at a man while signing away the man's wages, and the man would walk away feeling honored. "You've been in my archives."

My hands stayed flat on the table. I had decided, walking up the stairs, that I would not flinch. Flinching is a currency too, and I had spent my last of it years ago.

"The archives are open to company staff," I said.

"The archives are open to company staff," he agreed pleasantly, "at hours when there is someone to watch them. You came at night. Three times in the past fortnight—I checked the gate log, which you were careful enough to alter, but not careful enough to alter *well*. You changed the hour but kept the guard's initials. Okafor signs nothing after eight o'clock. He can't write." He turned his glass by its stem. "So. Either you're stealing from me, or you're reading from me. And if you were stealing, you'd have run by now. So you were reading."

I said nothing. In the silence I could hear the sea, faint and constant, the way you hear your own blood.

"Do you know what I did before I ran this post?" he asked. "I audited. Twenty-two years. I sat in rooms in Amsterdam and Batavia and Zanzibar and read other men's numbers until I could smell a false entry through three inches of paper. It's why they gave me this coast. Because this coast is made entirely of false entries." He leaned back. "Every trading post on it keeps two books. One for the tax man, one for the truth. And every family on it keeps two names. Yours is no different, Noor. Your mother arrived here as—what was it?—Saida bint Rashid, and became *Sarah Montrose* because some clerk forty years ago couldn't be bothered with the spelling. Your father dove for pearls under a name he learned the week he arrived. You were born into a fiction so old it has grown roots. I'm not angry about it. I'm telling you: I know exactly what you are. Which means I know exactly what you've been doing with my manifests."

The wine sat untouched in front of me. My mouth had gone dry as paper.

"You altered the berth allocations," he said. "Cleverly. You created a fifth berth on the *Meridian Star* by reclassifying a cargo hold as passenger space—fire regulations permit it, barely, if the inspector can be persuaded, and Inspector Voss owes me money, so he can be persuaded. You did this without asking anyone. Without leaving your own name anywhere near it. Do you understand what that tells me?"

"That I'm careful," I said.

"It tells me you're wasted here." He set down his glass. "A clerk who can forge a berth into existence, who can move families across an ocean on paper alone, who does it all without being caught until I caught him—do you know how rare that is? I have men who can count. I have men who can dive. I do not have men who can *make things true by writing them*. That is not literacy, Noor. That is power. And power is the one commodity on this coast that never spoils."

There it was. I had walked up the stairs knowing the shape of the offer before I heard it—you don't summon a man to dinner unless you want something, and you don't pour his wine yourself unless the thing you want is dirty—and still, hearing it laid out so plainly, I felt the floor tilt a degree beneath my chair.

"What do you want?" I asked.

"Names." He said it simply, the way a man says *bread* or *water*. "Brand's list has eleven families flagged for irregular papers. Eleven. But Brand is a census man—he counts what walks in the door. He doesn't know what hides behind it. There are others. Families who've been passing for thirty years. Men whose grandfathers jumped ship and married into the harbor community. Women whose papers burned in the warehouse fire of '81 and were rebuilt by hand, badly. I know some of them exist. I don't know *who* they are." He refilled his own glass. "You grew up among them. You read the archives; you know the seams. Give me the names, and the *Meridian Star* sails with every berth filled—including yours, including your mother's, including your sister's. First class, if you like. And afterward—" he spread his hands, encompassing the room, the compound, the coast—"a position. Real papers. A real name, if you want one, or keep the one you have, it makes no difference to me. You'd be a free man, Noor. Free, and paid, and *counted*."

Counted. He had chosen the word carefully. He must have known—Brand must have reported it—how the counting had gone at our door, how my mother had stood with her hands folded while a stranger wrote down what she was. To be counted. To exist in ink. It was the thing I had spent my whole life avoiding and my whole life wanting, and here it was, offered over wine, priced at the lives of everyone else who lived the way we lived.

"How many berths does the ship have now?" I asked.

"With your little adjustment? Enough." He smiled again. "For your family. For no one else. That's the arithmetic, and you already understand arithmetic better than most men I employ. Eleven families, one ship. Someone stays. The only question is whether it's you."

I thought of my mother that morning, standing in the doorway with salt drying white on her wrists, calling me by my father's name. I thought of Amara under the lamp with her stolen alphabet, writing *MERIDIAN STAR* in letters that trembled with hope. I thought of the archive at night, the erased men, page after page where a life had been and then wasn't—Rashid al-— struck through, Fatima struck through, whole families reduced to a marginal note: *removed from rolls, destination unknown.* Removed by whom? By men sitting at tables like this one, pouring wine for themselves, deciding.

Invisibility had always been someone else's decision. That was what I'd learned in the archives, and it rose in me now not as fear but as fury, clean and cold, the way the sea goes calm before it takes a diver.

"No," I said.

He didn't move. "Think carefully. If you refuse, I reopen the allocation. Your forged berth disappears. The list stands at eleven families and one short ship, and Brand reads the names at the dock on Thursday, and whatever happens at that dock happens because you chose it. Your mother is old, Noor. Ships are not kind to the old. Is that what you want on your conscience?"

"My conscience," I said, "already holds the names of every man your company erased. It has room."

Something moved behind his eyes—not anger, not yet. Recalculation. He set down his glass and laced his fingers.

"You misunderstand your position. You think refusing protects them. Refusing only removes you from the transaction. I will find those names with you or without you—it will simply take longer, and cost more, and the cost will be paid by people who cannot afford it. You are not buying their safety tonight. You are only declining to sell it cheaply."

"Then I decline," I said, "expensively."

Now the anger came, briefly, like a squall—his jaw tightened, the lamplight caught the sweat at his temple—and then it passed, and what remained was worse: patience. The patience of a man who audits. Who knows that every ledger balances eventually, one way or another.

"Very well," he said softly. "Thursday, then. The dock. Bring your family early—Brand reads the list at seven, and the tide waits for no one's sentiment." He stood, and I stood, and for a moment we faced each other across the scarred wood, two men wearing names that belonged to neither of us.

"One more thing," he said, as I reached the door. "Your sister. Amara. She's been teaching herself to read off my discarded manifests. Theft of company property, technically. At her age, with her—background—an irregularity like that could put her on a list of her own." He smiled his good smile. "Do give her my regards."

I walked down the stairs with my blood roaring so loudly I couldn't hear the sea anymore. Outside, the night was thick and warm, and the harbor lights lay scattered on the black water like a spilled ledger, every one of them a name, every one of them counted.

Thursday. Two days. He thought he had taken my choice away by threatening Amara. He thought fear would finish what greed started.

But fear had made me careful for twenty years, and careful men work fast. There was a sympathetic clerk named Dawit who wanted testimony against this very man, and there was ink in my room, and there was one night left before the lists were sealed.

I turned toward the archive, and began to run.
