## 10. Betrayal at the Mill

The mill at Văduva had been dead for six years, but its bones still remembered work. Ilka felt it every time she came — the way the great waterwheel stood half-sunk in its own reflection, the way the millstones sat in the dark like two fat moons that had fallen out of the sky and given up. She had chosen it herself, three winters ago, as a rendezvous for the network's eastern line. Far enough from the Silistra road that a lantern would not be seen from the causeway. Close enough to the water that Danilo's barges could lie against the ruined loading dock without their ropes showing. A place where the empire's eyes, so busy counting the living, had forgotten to count the dead.

She had been proud of it. That was the thing she would remember afterward, the thing that would sit in her chest like a swallowed stone: she had been *proud*.

Now she stood in the doorway of the miller's old counting room and watched the water carry away the last of the blood.

* * *

It had begun, as most of her days did, with paper.

Ilka worked in the back room of a shuttered dyer's shop on the edge of the tanners' quarter, where the smell of old lye covered the smell of fresh ink, and no one asked a woman with stained fingers what she was staining them with. Her trade was names. She made them the way a seamstress made collars — cut to fit, stitched to hold, invisible against the skin of a document. A Hungarian baptismal record for a boy who had never been baptized. A Wallachian travel pass for a man who had been born on the wrong side of a border that had moved twice in his lifetime. A marriage certificate, a death certificate, sometimes both for the same person, because the empire's arithmetic only balanced if the living were subtracted somewhere.

She was good. She had been good for a long time, and she had learned her goodness in a bad school — the debtors' cells of Galatz, where she had forged her first document at nineteen to buy her mother out of a cell that was slowly drowning her. The document had worked. Her mother had walked out into the snow and lived four more years on dry land, and Ilka had never quite forgiven the world for the fact that it could be bought with nothing but well-made paper.

Old Andrei — Mirabel's master, the cartographer, the man whose name Ilka still would not say aloud in a room with thin walls — had found her a year after her mother died. He had come to her with a map folded into his coat and a request that was really a test: *copy this border, but make it wrong. Make it wrong the way an official would make it wrong.* She had studied the map for an hour and then asked him which surveyor's hand he wanted her to imitate, because every surveyor erred differently, and he had smiled like a man finding water in a desert.

For eleven years he had paid her in coin when he had it and in silence when he didn't, and she had never asked what the maps were for. That was the courtesy between them. He made lines on paper; she made names on paper; neither of them asked what the paper was carrying.

Then Andrei had died — murdered, though the gendarmes wrote *burglary* in their ledger with the smooth indifference of men writing weather — and the maps had passed to the girl. And the girl had come to Ilka in the first week, still smelling of the workshop's ink and of something else, something rawer, and had said: *You knew what he was doing. You knew, and you helped, and I need you to keep helping.*

Ilka had looked at Mirabel Sandoval — twenty-three years old, ink under her nails, grief worn like a coat two sizes too large — and had seen herself at nineteen, standing outside a debtors' cell. She had said yes. She had not known yet what the yes would cost.

* * *

The convoy was to move on the Thursday. Twelve souls out of the eastern villages — a family of five from near Hirşova whose names had been struck from the parish rolls when the priest who'd baptized them was arrested; a widow and her two grown sons; a shepherd who had never existed on paper at all, born in a hut above the treeline where the census had never bothered to climb; two brothers who had fled conscription with the army's description of them nailed to every customs house from Brăila to the delta.

Twelve souls, and Ilka had spent three weeks making them into thirteen documents, because one always spoiled, the ink feathering or the seal cracking or her own hand, some nights, refusing to obey her. She worked by a single lamp with a green shade, and by the end of the third week she knew their faces better than their own mothers did — the widow's mole, the shepherd's crooked thumb, the scar on the younger brother's jaw shaped like a question mark. She had drawn them all into existence with a pen. She had begun, without meaning to, to love them.

The rendezvous was the mill at Văduva. They would come in twos and threes over two days, by the drover's path, and lie in the mill's dry loft until Danilo's barge came down on the Thursday night with a grain manifest that Ilka had made so beautiful it hurt her to surrender it. Then twelve souls would become twelve sacks of wheat, and the wheat would float down to the delta, and somewhere past the last imperial buoy it would turn back into people.

She had told exactly four people the place and the hour.

Danilo. Mirabel. Petru, the drover who guided the fugitives overland. And Sava.

Sava.

* * *

The first two days went the way the good days went, which is to say they went like water under ice — everything moving, nothing showing. The widow came in on the Tuesday with her sons, walking separately, reuniting in the loft with the wordless efficiency of people who had practiced being strangers. The family of five arrived on the Wednesday morning, the youngest child asleep in a basket of onions, and Petru met them at the causeway and walked them in talking loudly about the price of pigs.

Ilka came out on the Wednesday afternoon with the last documents sewn into a false binding of a psalter, because no soldier on earth searches a psalter. She meant to stay the night, count heads at dawn, and hand the convoy to Danilo on Thursday.

She was still a mile from the mill when she saw the smoke.

Not chimney smoke. Chimney smoke rises. This smoke lay down along the river valley the way a beaten dog lies down, low and gray and spreading, and Ilka stood on the causeway with the psalter against her chest and understood, in the way one understands a fall only after the ground has hit, that something had already happened and her arriving faster would only mean arriving sooner at the end of it.

She left the road. She went through the reeds the way she had gone through them a hundred times, crouched, the cold water finding every seam in her boots, and she came up behind the mill's loading dock with her heart going like the old waterwheel.

The gendarmes were already gone. That was the worst of it — the tidiness. Three wagons stood in the yard, and mounted men were riding off toward the Silistra road at a walk, unhurried, men whose work was done. Two local constables remained, bored, kicking through the miller's old chaff, and near the loading dock, where the water was black and slow, one of them was washing something off his hands.

Ilka did not go in. She made herself not go in. She lay in the reeds with the psalter pressed against her mouth to keep her own breath quiet and she counted, the way she counted everything — strokes, seals, seconds, sins.

They had taken three. She learned it that night from Petru, who found her at midnight in the dyer's shop, gray-faced, his drover's coat torn at the shoulder. The widow. Her elder son. The shepherd from above the treeline — the man who had never existed on paper, who had been so careful for fifty years to be no one, and who had been made into a number in a ledger in a single morning.

The rest had scattered into the marsh and come out the far side by ones and twos; Petru had shepherded them to the reed camp Danilo kept below the fisher islands, and they were alive, and alive was not the same as safe, and everyone knew it.

The younger conscription brother had fought. That was how Petru told it, flat and fast, the way you pull a bandage. He had fought, and a gendarme had broken his arm with a carbine, and they had taken him anyway with his mother and the shepherd, down the Silistra road, into the machinery.

And the question, the question that Ilka could feel already growing in the dark of the shop like mold in a damp wall, was not *how*. She knew how. Wagons, carbines, the empire's endless appetite. The question was *how did they know*.

* * *

She sat up all night with the lamp's green shade pulled low and did what she always did with a thing she could not fix: she took it apart.

The rendezvous had been set three weeks ago, in a conversation between her and Mirabel in the dyer's shop, in Romanian, both windows shuttered. Four people had been told the place and the hour, each separately, each told that the others knew. Danilo, who had run the river for thirty years and whose own son had been taken by the army before he ever turned to this work. Mirabel, who had finished her master's atlas with her own hands and had nothing in the world but it and the people it carried. Petru, who had walked more fugitives over the drover's paths than he could count and had never lost one — until now.

And Sava.

Sava, who had joined the network eighteen months ago, a customs clerk from Brăila dismissed for drinking, who had wept the first time Ilka put a finished passport in his hands and said, *My brother died in a cell like the one you bought your mother out of. Let me carry the paper.* Sava, who was the one who moved between the river line and the eastern line, who knew the rendezvous points because someone had to carry the word from her to Petru, and who had been told the mill at Văduva on the Monday, three days before the convoy.

Sava, who had been drinking again lately. Sava, whose sister and her children lived in Brăila, four streets from the gendarmerie.

Ilka laid it all out on her worktable like the parts of a document — the facts in their separate piles, the inks sorted, the evidence of her own reasoning spread under the lamp — and she found that her hands, which never shook over a forged seal, were shaking now.

Because the other thing she knew, the thing she kept trying to push to the bottom of the pile and that kept rising to the top like a body in a river, was this: the gendarmes had come at dawn on Thursday morning, to the hour. They had come *before* the barge, before the loading, at the one moment when three of the twelve were in the open yard. That was not a raid on a rumor. That was a schedule. Someone had given them the place *and the hour*, and of the four who knew both, three were people whose bones Ilka felt she knew like the bones of the mill.

And the fourth was Sava.

She put out the lamp and sat in the dark and let herself think the unforgivable thought, the one she had been refusing since the reeds: *if it was him, then I chose him.* She had vouched for Sava. She had brought him in. She had told Mirabel he was sound — *he's a drunk, but he's ours*, she had said, with the fat confidence of a woman who believed that grief was a reliable character reference. If Sava had sold the mill, then every name Ilka had ever put on paper was a name she had handed to a man who might sell that too.

And there were names on her table right now. Eleven documents for the scattered nine, plus Danilo's people, plus the reed camp's coordinates, plus — she looked at the false psalter binding, still sewn shut, still holding the widow's papers, papers for a woman who was at this moment in a wagon on the Silistra road — plus everything.

* * *

Mirabel came at first light.

Ilka heard her before she saw her — the particular step on the tanners' stair, light but deliberate, a step that had learned caution without learning fear. She opened the door before the knock, and the two women looked at each other in the gray morning, and Ilka saw that Mirabel already knew. Word traveled the network's veins faster than blood.

"They took Ana Văduva," Mirabel said. The widow's name. Not *the widow*. Ilka noticed that, and it went through her like a splinter — that the girl had learned the names, all of them, had insisted on learning them, against every rule of a trade that ran on anonymity.

"Ana, and her son Mihai, and old Costin the shepherd." Ilka stepped back to let her in. "You'd better sit."

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit down, Mirabel." Her voice came out harder than she meant. She softened it with an effort. "Please."

Mirabel sat on the work stool, hands in her lap, and waited, and Ilka realized with a small cold shock that the girl was waiting the way one waits for a verdict — that she had already done her own arithmetic in the night and had arrived at the same four names, and was waiting to see if Ilka's list matched hers.

"Four people knew the mill and the hour," Ilka said. "You. Me. Danilo. Sava."

"I know."

"I'm not saying it's one of us because it's comfortable. It isn't. I'd sooner cut off my own hand than—"

"I know." Mirabel's voice was quiet and did not waver, and Ilka hated how much that steadiness frightened her. "I've been awake since midnight. I've been through it the way you go through a plate looking for a flaw." She looked up, and her eyes in the gray light were the color of the water at the mill. "It isn't you. It isn't me. I'd stake my life on Danilo — I have staked my life on Danilo, every night since the first convoy. That leaves Sava."

"That leaves Sava," Ilka said. "And Sava has a sister in Brăila with three children, four streets from the gendarmerie. And Sava has been drinking. And Sava knew the mill on Monday, and the gendarmes came on Thursday at the exact hour, which means they had the schedule, not the rumor."

Mirabel was silent for a long moment. Then she said, "Or Sava talked without meaning to. Drunk, in a tavern, boasting or grieving or just loose — and someone patient sat next to him and drew it out of him the way you'd draw a wire through a die. There's a man who does that for the census office. Vogel's people. They don't always buy. Sometimes they just *listen*."

"Is that mercy, what you're doing?" Ilka asked. "Making him smaller in the story?"

"It's accuracy," Mirabel said. "And accuracy is all we have. If we're wrong about him, and we treat him as a traitor, then we do the empire's work for it — we break our own circle, and the circle is the only thing that's kept any of us alive." She turned her hands over, looked at the ink in the creases of her fingers. "But if we're wrong the other way — if we forgive him and he sells the reed camp next — then it's Ana and Mihai and Costin all over again, and it will be our doing. Ours, Ilka. Not his. Ours."

The lamp was still cold on the table between them, and Ilka looked at it and thought about ink — how a name, once written, could not be unwritten, only blotted, and blotting always left a ghost. That was the whole of her trade and the whole of her terror. You could not unmake what you had made. You could only decide what you would make next.

"Then we don't decide tonight," she said at last. "We watch him. I'll set a meeting — a small one, nothing real, a test with a false center. If the soldiers come to the false center, we'll know. If they don't—" She stopped. She found she did not know how to finish the sentence, because the other half of it was *if they don't, we still won't know, not truly, not the way we need to*, and saying that aloud would let the question mark into the room permanently, and once it was in the room it would sit down at every table and sleep in every loft and ride on every barge, and nothing would ever be simple again.

Mirabel seemed to hear the unsaid half anyway. She stood, and for a moment she put her hand over Ilka's hand on the table — the first time either of them had touched the other in all the months of their working — and her hand was cold and steady and ink-stained, and Ilka thought: *she is twenty-three years old and her hand is steadier than mine, and that is the most frightening thing in this room.*

"Petru is moving the nine tonight, downstream to Danilo's camp," Mirabel said. "The documents?"

Ilka unsewed the psalter binding with fingers that had finally gone still, and laid the eleven papers on the table between them like a hand of cards, like an accusation, like a prayer. "For the nine," she said. "And three I made anyway. For Ana, Mihai, and Costin." She heard her own voice crack on the last name, the shepherd who had never existed on paper and had been erased anyway. "In case they get out. In case someone finds them. In case there's a border somewhere they can still be walked across."

Mirabel gathered the papers slowly, one by one, and held them against her chest the way Ilka had held the psalter in the reeds.

"There's something else," Mirabel said at the door. "Danilo sent word this morning. Vogel's office has requested the grain manifests from the Brăila customs house. All of them. Going back two years." She paused. "Someone is reading our paper, Ilka. Someone patient."

After she was gone, Ilka sat down at her worktable and pulled a clean sheet toward her and did not write on it for a long time. Outside, the tanners' quarter was waking — carts, water, the first shouts — the ordinary world going on with its ordinary counting, and somewhere on the Silistra road three people were being carried into that counting, and somewhere in Brăila a man with a sister and three children and a drinking problem was waking up too, and Ilka did not know yet whether she was going to save him or bury him, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like the mill's dead wheel, huge and still and half-drowned, waiting for the water to move.

She dipped her pen at last. But what she wrote was not a name. It was a single line, in her smallest hand, at the top of the sheet, where a title would go:

*Everything can be forged except trust.*

And beneath it, because she was who she was, because the trade was the trade, she began to design the test.
