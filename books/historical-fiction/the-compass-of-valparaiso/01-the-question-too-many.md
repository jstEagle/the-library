## 1. The Question Too Many

The census taker arrived on a Tuesday, which Kestrel would later think of as the first cruelty of it, because Tuesdays were ledger days, and ledger days were the only hours of the week when Kestrel Winterbourne — or rather, when Corin Ashe, archivist, second-class, of the Aldermoor Provincial Records Office — allowed himself to be entirely predictable.

He had been Corin Ashe for eleven years now. Long enough that the name had grown a callus, the way a oar handle grows one against the palm. Long enough that when the neighbors called it across the lane he answered without the half-beat of hesitation that had plagued him in the early years, that terrible quarter-second in which a man must remember who he is pretending to be. He was Corin Ashe, forty-six years of age, born in the mill town of Harrow's Bend upcountry, orphaned at fourteen in the fever years, educated by a parish charity, employed in the cataloguing of drainage easements and grain-tithe records for a government he did not love and did not, on the surface, hate. He was a thin man with a stoop he had practiced until it stopped being practice, ink-stained fingers, a cough he could produce on demand for the rare occasions when a stranger's curiosity pressed too near. He was nobody. He had built nobody the way a mason builds a wall, stone by patient stone, and for eleven years the wall had held.

The census taker's knock came at ten minutes past the hour, and Kestrel knew before he opened the door that the wall had a crack in it somewhere, because the man on the step was not the usual kind.

The usual kind wore the grey sash of the Provincial Enumeration Service and asked their eleven questions in the flat recitation of men reading from cards: names, ages, trade, years at present address, livestock, hearths, dependents. They came in autumn, they smelled of cheap tobacco, and they wanted to be finished before the light went. Kestrel had been counted four times since coming to Aldermoor and had perfected the art of being counted — the modest answers, the slight deafness in the left ear that justified asking a man to repeat himself, the offer of tea that was always declined.

This one wore no sash. He wore a plain dark coat of good wool, and he carried not the standard enumeration board but a ledger bound in black leather, and he stood on the step with the stillness of a man who has been taught that stillness is a form of authority.

"Corin Ashe?" he said. "Archivist, provincial records?"

"That's me." Kestrel let the door swing wide in the easy way of a man with nothing behind it worth hiding. "You'll want the back room, I expect. It's warmest. The front one gets the river wind."

The man smiled — a thin, correct smile — and did not move. "The step is fine. This won't take long."

*The step is fine.* Kestrel catalogued the sentence and filed it in the drawer of his mind reserved for things that would keep him awake. Census men came inside. Census men wanted chairs and warmth and the small humiliations of hospitality that made the counted feel like hosts rather than subjects. A man who refused comfort was a man who had not come to count.

"Suit yourself. It's bitter out."

"It is." The man opened the black ledger across his forearm. "Ambrose Fell," he said, as though the name were a credential. "Special enumeration. You'll not have seen the form before."

"I haven't."

"No. Most haven't. It's a new instrument. The Concordat is — modernizing its records." He said the word *modernizing* the way a priest says a saint's name, with the particular reverence of the recently converted. "Names, ages, trade — you know these. But there are new questions this year. Questions of provenance."

"Provenance." Kestrel coughed, gently, the practiced cough. "Sounds like something you'd ask a horse."

Fell's smile did not waver. "Everything in the Concordat is asked of everything, Mr. Ashe. That's rather the point of it."

* * *

He worked through the eleven questions quickly, and Kestrel answered them quickly, and the whole familiar liturgy took less than five minutes. Corin Ashe, forty-six. Born Harrow's Bend. Trade: archivist, second-class. Years at present address: eleven. Livestock: two goats, a shared interest in a neighbor's pig. Hearth: one. Dependents: none.

At *none* Fell looked up for the first time, and his eyes — pale, patient, the eyes of a man who reads everything twice — rested on Kestrel's face a moment too long.

"No family?"

"None living."

"That's a common answer this year," Fell said. "In the river towns especially. The fever years took a generation, they say."

"They took what they took." Kestrel kept his voice level and mildly bored. Behind his sternum his heart had begun a slow, deliberate work, the way it always did, like a pump being brought up from a cellar. *Steady. Breathe from the floor of it. A man with nothing to hide breathes from his chest.*

Fell made a small mark in the ledger — not a word, Kestrel noted, but a mark, a stroke or a symbol, the kind of thing a man does when the answer itself is not what interests him — and turned the page.

"Now the new questions." He cleared his throat. "Question twelve. Religious observance. Do you attend the Concordat chapel at Aldermoor?"

"Twice a year," Kestrel said. "Founding Day and the Feast of Unification. I find the sermons long but the singing is good."

"Question thirteen. Membership in any guild, fraternal order, or benevolent society."

"The Archivists' Benevolent Fund. It's a burial society, mostly. We pay in and complain about the rates."

"Question fourteen." Fell's voice did not change at all, and that was the thing about it, that was the thing Kestrel would turn over for the rest of the night and the rest of his life: the question came out of him with no more weight than the eleven before it, smooth as a stone passed hand to hand. "Where were you during the Unification?"

The river wind came up the lane and pushed at the door Kestrel was holding, and for one full second — one long, ringing second — Kestrel Winterbourne stood in a doorway in the town of Aldermoor and was nowhere at all.

He was, in fact, in the Chancellery, twenty years and four hundred miles and one lifetime away, with a wax-sealed dispatch pressed against his ribs and the sound of the gates coming down, and a woman's voice — *you'll get it through, Kestrel, you're the only one left who can* — and the taste of smoke that had never entirely left the back of his throat. He was in the water under the bridge with the current pulling him toward the delta and the dark. He was in a hay loft in a country that no longer appeared on maps under a name that no longer appeared in print. He was twenty-six years old and running.

And then he was Corin Ashe, forty-six, of Harrow's Bend, and he coughed.

"The fever years, you mean? The Unification was the fever years, wasn't it — the winter they swept through the river valley. I was in Harrow's Bend. I told you — orphaned at fourteen. That was the winter. My mother went in the first month, my father in the second. The parish charity took me after. Reverend Halloran — he'd be dead now, I expect — he taught me letters and ledgers, and here I am." He shrugged, the shrug of a man whose history is small and worn smooth as a river pebble. "It's not a story. Half the river towns could tell it."

"No," Fell agreed. "Half of them could."

He was writing. Kestrel watched him write — watched the pen move in quick, economical strokes, recording the answer, and then, after the answer was done, recording something else. A second mark. A second line, shorter, made lower on the page, the way a man annotates. The pen paused. Fell's eyes came up, and they were not unkind, and that was somehow worse.

"Reverend Halloran," Fell said. "Of the Harrow's Bend parish charity."

"That's right."

"Curious." Fell turned back a page, running one finger down a column of entries. "I interviewed a cooper in Harrow's Bend last week. And a ferryman. And a widow who keeps bees." He looked up. "None of them remember a Reverend Halloran."

The wind came again. Somewhere down the lane a cart rattled past, and a dog barked at it, ordinary sounds, the sounds of a town going about its morning with no idea that a man was standing in a doorway deciding, in the space of a breath, whether to live or die.

"Well," Kestrel said, and heard his own voice come out rueful and slightly hoarse, the voice of a man embarrassed by the failures of other people's memories, "it was twenty years ago, and he was an old man then. And Harrow's Bend is not a place people stay. You could stand in the market square there and ask after anyone and get six different answers and a fight." He smiled. "You might try the parish register. If it survived. Churches burn, in my experience."

"Churches do burn," Fell said softly. He wrote something else. Another mark, lower still. Then he closed the ledger with the gentle finality of a lid coming down on a box. "That's all, Mr. Ashe. You've been very helpful."

"Have I?" Kestrel laughed, and the laugh was perfect, self-deprecating, the laugh of a small man amused to be told his small answers were of use to a great state. "I'd hate to think I wasted a special enumeration."

"You didn't." Fell tucked the ledger under his arm. He hesitated on the step — one beat, two — and then said, without looking back: "The river will be closing soon. Ice. It always closes early above the lock. A man with business downriver would want to see to it before the equinox."

He walked away down the lane at an unhurried pace, a plain man in a good coat, and turned at the corner toward the ferry landing, and was gone.

Kestrel stood in the doorway for a long time. The wind moved the bare branches along the lane. The river, grey and heavy with the first pans of ice, slid past the town below with the patience of a thing that had all the time in the world.

*The river will be closing soon.*

* * *

He did not go to the archive that morning. For the first time in eleven years, Corin Ashe was absent from his desk, and the drainage easements of three parishes went uncatalogued, and the world did not end.

Instead Kestrel sat in the back room of his narrow house above the chandlery, with the shutters closed and a single lamp lit against the grey, and took inventory the way he had been trained to take inventory twenty years ago, in another life, by people whose names he still said to himself sometimes at night like a litany: *Aldous, who taught me locks. Merren, who taught me faces. Ilsa, who taught me everything else.*

The wall had a crack. He could feel it now, could trace its whole length. A census taker had asked a question too many, and the answer had been good — it had been excellent, it had been one of the best lies of a life built on them — and none of it mattered, because the man had written something extra in his ledger. Twice. Three times. Little marks, lower on the page, the annotations of a man who was not recording answers but building a case. And then the parting words, delivered to the lane and the wind rather than to Kestrel's face, because a careful man did not put warnings in writing or in the direct address: *a man with business downriver would want to see to it before the equinox.*

Either Ambrose Fell was a fool playing games with a stranger, or Ambrose Fell was not a fool, and Kestrel had survived twenty years by never once betting on the fool.

He thought of the four previous censuses. Grey sashes, tobacco, eleven questions, gone by dusk. He thought of the new chapel in the market square with its Unification banners, and the wardens who had begun, these last two years, to stand at the ferry landing checking papers — a formality, everyone said, a formality — and the way the word *provenance* had crept into the provincial gazette like a weed into a wall. He thought of the notice he had read last month, nailed by the customs house, about the reward for information regarding persons of unverified origin, and how the whole town had read it and shrugged, because everyone in a river town was of unverified origin if you pressed hard enough.

Everyone but one.

The lamp hissed. Down in the chandlery, Marta was arguing with a delivery boy about the price of pitch, her voice carrying up through the floorboards, indomitable, seventy years old and built like the dock cranes she'd grown up among. Marta who asked no questions. Marta who had once, in Kestrel's third year in Aldermoor, found him standing in the yard at three in the morning staring at the river with a look on his face she had plainly recognized, because she had said only, "Whatever you buried, you buried it deep, and it's none of my business, but you'll want a coat," and had gone inside and come back with a coat. He had never asked what she had recognized. She had never asked what he buried. That was the treaty between them, unwritten and inviolable, and he thought now — with a cold that had nothing to do with the season — of what it would cost her if wardens came asking the neighbors about Corin Ashe of Harrow's Bend, and what they would say, and what she would say, and whether her saying it would save her or damn her.

*The river will be closing soon.*

He got up. He knelt at the corner of the back room where the floorboard sat loose beneath the rug, and for the first time in eleven years he put his fingers to the edge of it.

The wood came up with a sound like a held breath released. Below it, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, lay the courier's case: flat, iron-banded, no larger than a prayer book, and sealed — he could see the edge of it even in the dark of the cavity — with a bead of wax that had been red once and had gone the color of old blood. He did not take it out. He had not taken it out in eleven years, and he did not take it out now. He only knelt there with his hand resting on the oilcloth, feeling through the fabric the hard little shape of the thing that had cost a republic its last night and a courier the rest of her life, and he said, aloud, to the empty room, in a language he had not spoken in twenty years:

"Still here."

The lamp hissed. The river slid past below, carrying its first ice down toward the lock, down toward the delta, down toward the sea, patient as a promise.

Kestrel put the floorboard back.

Then he went to the window, and moved the shutter a finger's width, and looked out at the lane where Ambrose Fell had stood, and at the grey water beyond it, and began — with the terrible, clarifying calm of a man who has finally admitted the truth he has been dodging for two decades — to plan how to be gone before the equinox.

He did not yet know that the ledger of Ambrose Fell was already traveling. He did not yet know that in a provincial office upriver, a report with three small annotations in its margin was being copied, sealed, and forwarded to a woman who would read the name *Ashe, Corin, Harrow's Bend, provenance unverified* and go very still, because she had trained that courier herself, and had buried him once already.

He only knew that the wall had cracked, and that the ice was coming down the river, and that the thing beneath the floorboard had waited twenty years, and could not wait for spring.
