## 9. Rain on the Rooftops

The rain came down the way it always did in Vilna in October—not falling so much as settling, a gray wool wrapped around the spires and chimney pots, beading on the tin roofs until the whole city shone like the back of a spoon.

Tomas Vaitkus had walked this stretch of the river road a hundred times since the checkpoints went up, and he had learned to see it the way his orders demanded: as terrain. Sightlines. Choke points. The fish market's covered stalls to the east, where crowds thickened and papers changed hands. The footbridge where the guards could funnel pedestrians into a single file. The warehouse district with its blind alleys, useful for smuggling and for arrests in equal measure.

But today the rain had emptied the riverbank of everyone except one figure hunched against the parapet wall, sketching.

He knew her before he saw her face. There was a particular stillness to Casper Galloway when she worked—the way she held her shoulders, the slight forward tilt of her head, as if the world existed only to be recorded and could not touch her. He had seen that posture once before, three years ago, in the third row of Professor Brazauskas's lecture hall, when she'd raised her hand and asked whether a border drawn by men who would never live along it could be called anything but fiction. The professor had laughed. Half the room had. She hadn't smiled at all.

She should have been easy to forget. She was not.

Tomas stood under the arch of the old customs house and watched her work. Her coat was too thin for the weather. Rain darkened the paper pinned to her board, and she angled her body over it like a mother bending over a child, shielding it from the sky itself.

*Sketching rooftops,* he thought. *In the rain. In a city where sketching anything can get you questioned.*

He told himself he was doing his duty. He told himself that a lone person drawing the river approaches—drawing the checkpoints' sightlines, if that was what she was drawing—was precisely the sort of thing his orders required him to investigate. His brother's voice was in his head as he crossed the wet cobbles: *You're too soft, Tomas. Softness is how they get in.*

"Miss Galloway."

She didn't startle. That alone was remarkable. Most people flinched at a uniform behind them these days; she simply turned her head, rain dripping from the brim of her hat, and looked at him with an expression he couldn't parse—wariness, yes, but underneath it something almost like recognition settling into place.

"Officer." She closed the portfolio with deliberate slowness. "Is there a law against drawing weather?"

"There's a law against everything now. It saves time." He stopped beside her, close enough to see the charcoal smudged along the edge of her right hand, silver-gray to the wrist. "May I?"

He nodded at the portfolio. She hesitated—a heartbeat, no more—and then handed it to him as though it were nothing, as though her hands weren't careful in the way hands are careful when they are handing over something that matters enormously.

He opened it. Rooftops. That was all. The jumble of red-tiled slopes above Užupis, the crooked spine of the castle hill, the onion dome of the church rendered in quick confident strokes. No checkpoints. No guard posts. No sightlines. Just the city as it looked from above, beautiful and indifferent, the way it must have looked to the gulls.

"You draw the parts of Vilna people can't reach," he said.

"People can reach them. They've just forgotten to look up." She took the portfolio back. "It's the only direction left where nobody's built a wall."

He should have asked for her permit. He should have noted her name, her address, the fact that she was loitering near the river checkpoint on a Tuesday morning with a clear view of the guard rotations. He had done exactly that to a photographer two weeks earlier, a nervous man whose camera case had earned him three hours in a back room and a file that would follow him for the rest of his life.

Instead Tomas heard himself say: "The rooflines are wrong."

Her eyes narrowed. "I beg your pardon?"

"The church on the corner." He pointed across the water to where St. Anne's spire dissolved into the mist. "From here, the buttresses hide behind the nave. You've drawn them visible. It's the kind of mistake someone makes drawing from memory instead of from life."

Something shifted in her face—the smallest movement, a door opening and closing somewhere behind her eyes. "And what does that make me, Officer? A liar?"

"It makes you someone who knows this city better than from street level." He kept his voice even. "Which is either very innocent or very interesting."

The rain filled the silence between them. On the far bank, a barge groaned against its moorings, and somewhere upstream a whistle sounded, thin and mournful.

"And which do you decide I am?" she asked quietly.

"That depends," Tomas said, "on what you're really drawing."

For a moment he thought she would lie to him. He braced for it—the smooth deflection, the artist's excuse, the little performance everyone performed now because their lives depended on performance. He had heard a thousand of them and had learned to hear the seams.

Instead Casper Galloway turned back to the parapet and looked out at the drowned city, and said, in a voice stripped of all performance:

"I'm drawing the way it used to look. Before anyone decided it needed fences."

* * *

They ended up walking. Neither of them suggested it; it simply happened, the way water finds the low ground, the two of them drifting east along the river road beneath her umbrella—he refused it, then accepted it, because his uniform was already soaked through and pride was a luxury he was beginning to suspect he could no longer afford.

"This is madness," he said, after a while. Not to her. To the rain, to himself. "Walking together. Anyone sees us—"

"Anyone sees a citizen talking to a guard," she said. "That's not madness. That's Tuesday."

"It isn't the same for you."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't." And she did not pretend otherwise, and he found, strangely, that he trusted her more for it.

They passed the fish market, shuttered now, its stalls empty, the smell of brine and ice lingering under the awnings. Two of his colleagues stood at the checkpoint ahead, checking papers with the mechanical boredom of men who had learned that boredom was safer than attention. Casper's pace didn't change. She didn't lower her head or pull her collar up or do any of the hundred small things that guilty people did. She walked past the checkpoint the way she might have walked past a fruit stand.

*Either she's innocent,* Tomas thought, *or she's the best actor in Lithuania.*

"Tell me something true," he said abruptly.

She glanced at him. "That's a strange request from a man with the power to arrest me."

"Then it shouldn't be hard to grant."

A pause. Rain drummed on the umbrella between them. Then, looking straight ahead at the gray ribbon of the river, she said: "My master taught me that a map is never the territory. It's a promise about the territory. Every line says: *this is here, this will still be here tomorrow.*" Her voice caught, just slightly, on the last word. "Lately I find myself drawing promises I'm not sure I can keep."

Tomas stopped walking.

Because he knew what she was. Of course he knew—had known since the university, had confirmed it a dozen times since in small ways, the Yiddish he'd once heard murmured in her sleep-adjacent exhaustion at the library, the name Galloway worn like a coat buttoned to the chin. Everyone in this city knew things about everyone now; knowledge was the only currency that hadn't been devalued. And knowing what he knew, he understood what she had just given him: a truth with a trapdoor under it, offered to see what he would do.

She was testing him. She had every reason to. And the terrible thing—the thing that sat in his chest like swallowed stone—was that he wanted to pass the test. Wanted it more than he wanted to be safe.

"My father used to say Lithuania would be a country where no one had to carry papers," he said. "When I was a boy, I believed him. We were going to be a nation of poets and farmers. Steponas used to—" He stopped. Corrected himself. "My brother and I used to lie on the roof of our house in the summer and argue about what the capital would look like. He wanted marble. I wanted bookshops."

"And now?" Casper's voice was gentle in a way that undid him more than suspicion would have. "What does the capital look like now?"

"Now it looks like checkpoints." He heard the bitterness come out of him raw, unpolished, and hated how much of it there was, how long it had been fermenting. "Now my brother compiles lists and I check papers and we're building the opposite of what we were promised, and we call it order. We call it safety." He shook his head. "I wanted bookshops, Miss Galloway."

"Casper," she said.

He looked at her.

"My name," she said. "Not Miss Galloway. Not today, standing in the rain, when no one's listening." A faint, wry curve touched her mouth—the first he'd ever seen there. "Casper."

"Casper," he repeated, and the name felt like a door he hadn't known was open.

* * *

They walked as far as the bridge before sense reasserted itself. Below them the Vilnia ran swollen and brown with the rain, carrying leaves and twigs and one abandoned hat toward the Neris, toward the sea, toward everywhere none of them could go.

"I have to ask," Tomas said. "And you don't have to answer. But if I don't ask, and someone else asks for me—"

"You want to know what I was really doing on the riverbank." She set the umbrella handle against her shoulder, watching the water. "All right. A half-truth, officer, freely given. I'm completing a survey. An atlas of the city. Roofs, lanes, courtyards—the places that don't appear on official maps because official maps only record what matters to officials." She turned to look at him fully, and her eyes were steady, and terrified, and brave in a way that made his throat tighten. "Whether there's anything else in it—I suppose that depends on whether the man reading it believes maps are promises, or believes maps are evidence."

The rain came harder. Downstream, a siren started up and died away again, some routine alarm, some ordinary cruelty.

He thought about the form in his breast pocket. The incident report he was required to file by evening. *Subject observed sketching river approaches. Subject identified as C. Galloway, cartographer, residence—* He thought about his brother's pen scratching lists. He thought about marble capitals and bookshops and a country that had been promised to children who were now adults guarding its gates against its own people.

He thought: *If I write her name, I am choosing a side. If I don't write her name, I am also choosing a side.* There was no blank space anymore. There had not been for years. The rain made that simple and undeniable, washing the city down to its essentials: which side of the paper you stood on.

"An atlas of courtyards," he said at last. "Roofs and lanes." He exhaled slowly. "Vilna has always had too many of both for any map to hold. Any honest mapmaker would need help."

Something flickered across her face—surprise first, then caution, then a careful, guarded light, like a lamp shaded against discovery.

"Would they," she said.

"New guard rotations went up at the harbor last spring. The old surveys are useless there. Anyone completing such an atlas would be working blind." He met her eyes. "Hypothetically."

"Hypothetically." Her voice was very quiet.

They stood on the bridge in the rain, two people on a narrow span over deep water, each of them having just stepped past a line that could not be unstepped, neither willing to say so aloud. A patrol passed on the far bank. They watched it go, shoulder to shoulder, and did not move apart.

"I should go," Casper said finally.

"You should." He reached out and, without letting himself think about it, adjusted the strap of her portfolio where the rain had loosened it—an absurd, domestic gesture, the kind of thing done between people who had shared a table for years. His fingers brushed hers. Cold, ink-stained, steady. "Go home by the long way. Past the cathedral. Not the harbor."

"Why the long way?"

"Because the short way is being watched," he said, "and because the cathedral is worth drawing, and your buttresses are always wrong."

The almost-smile again, there and gone. "Good afternoon, Officer Vaitkus."

"Good afternoon, Casper."

He watched her go, the umbrella tilting against the wind, the thin coat disappearing into the gray wash of the streets. Then he took out the incident form, and the pencil stub he carried for exactly this purpose, and stood on the bridge in the rain for a long moment, the paper darkening in his hand.

*Subject observed sketching rooftops. Nothing of interest.*

His pen lied easily. That was the part that frightened him—not the risk, but how naturally the lie came, how little friction there was between his conscience and his hand. He filed the form at the end of his shift, and walked home through the wet streets rehearsing what he would say if anyone asked why a certain mapmaker's name was missing from his day's report.

He did not know that his brother was already asking questions. He did not know that the name *Galloway* had appeared, that very afternoon, in a ledger on a census clerk's desk, circled twice in red.

And he did not know that four days remained until the morning tide.
