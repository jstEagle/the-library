## 12. Confessions by Candlelight

He came at the hour when the city stopped pretending.

Not a knock—she would have bolted at a knock—but the scrape of a boot on the stair, unhurried, familiar, and then his shadow through the frosted pane of the studio door, broad-shouldered, capped, wrong. Casper stood very still among the drying inks and let the shadow lengthen. She had rehearsed a dozen versions of this evening. In none of them had she lit candles.

"Casper." His voice through the wood, low. "It's Tomas. I know you're there. The lamp's been burning since dusk and you never burn it before dark."

She opened the door.

He stood in the corridor with rain on his greatcoat and something in his hands he didn't know he was holding—nothing, just his own fingers, curled as if around a pen he'd been ordered to write with and couldn't. His eyes went past her shoulder to the worktable, to the sheets pinned there under weights, and she saw him understand, in the slow way men understand a thing they have been refusing to see, what a mapmaker's apprentice kept in a locked studio at night.

"May I come in," he said, "or would you rather I arrest you in the hallway? It's colder out here."

She stepped aside. It was not a decision. It was a surrender she had been walking toward for three years, and her feet simply finished the journey.

* * *

He took off his cap and set it on the corner of the worktable, and the gesture undid her more than any words could have. A guard's cap on a mapmaker's table. The two halves of the city touching.

"You followed me," she said.

"I walked behind you. There's a difference, and I've been telling myself it's a large one." He turned his hands toward the candle flame, warming them, or hiding them. "The rooftop by the river. The fish market. The way you watch checkpoints the way other people watch weather. I'm not a clever man, Casper, but I count things for a living, and I have counted you."

"Then you know you shouldn't be here."

"I know it better than you do." He looked at the pinned sheets again. "Show me."

"No."

"Casper—"

"No." Her voice cracked on it, and she hated that. She made herself go on, quiet, level. "You'll ask me to show you, and I'll show you, and then one of us will have to become someone else. Either you'll become the man who arrests me, or I'll become the woman who was fool enough to trust a uniform. I can't survive becoming either one tonight."

The candle guttered. Rain worked at the window in long silver threads.

Tomas pulled out the stool at the drafting table—the stool where Master Levin had sat for thirty years, where Casper still caught herself leaving room for a shoulder that was no longer there—and sat down in it slowly, like a man entering a church he'd been taught to fear.

"Then don't show me," he said. "Tell me. I'll keep my hands in my pockets. I'll keep my eyes on the floor. Just talk, and I'll listen, and whatever I am when you finish, that's what I'll be. I'd rather be damned for what I choose than promoted for what I don't."

So she told him.

* * *

She began with the fire, because everything began with the fire.

"Three years ago, in November. Soldiers came to the studio at dusk—two of them, then four, then a captain with a list. Master Levin heard them on the stairs the way you hear weather. He didn't panic. He took down every map on the walls and fed them to the stove, one by one, while I stood there not understanding. The river charts. The tenement surveys. Maps he'd spent a decade making, and he burned them like letters from a woman he'd stopped loving." Her hands had found a pen somewhere along the way; she was turning it, not drawing. "Then he put this in my arms."

She unlocked the drawer beneath the drafting table and lifted the atlas out—wrapped in oilcloth, heavy as a sleeping child—and set it between them without opening it.

"He said, *A map is a confession, Casper. Everything else is just ink.* He said the men coming up the stairs wanted the city to be one city, legible, countable, owned. And this—" she touched the oilcloth "—this was the other city. The one underneath. The safe houses. The false-paper routes. The cellars and couriers and back doors of every family the census has been erasing since before either of us could read. He said silences keep people safe, and then he told me to go down the coal chute and not come back up until the studio was quiet."

The candle flame stood very straight, as if listening.

"They shot him in the doorway," she said. "I heard it from inside the chute. Two shots. They never found me because he'd moved the coal scuttle over the hatch years ago, in case of exactly that night. He'd been preparing for it the way a cartographer prepares for anything—slowly, in advance, without telling anyone." She looked up. "He died with his hand on the atlas case. The soldiers took his papers and his ledgers and his name off the door, and they never found this, because he'd already given it to a girl who officially didn't exist."

Tomas had kept his eyes on the floor the way he'd promised. Now he looked up, and his face in the candlelight was not the face from the fish market, not the careful, countable face of a border guard. It was younger. It was the face she remembered from the lecture hall, three winters ago, when a professor had drawn the old boundaries of the Commonwealth on a slate and Tomas Vaitkus had asked, in front of everyone, *And where do the people go who live on the lines?*

"The ship," he said quietly. "You're taking it out."

"Sunday. The morning tide. There's a diplomatic pouch—there's a man at the harbor who owes Levin a debt older than either of us—and from there to London. It has to be printed. It has to be *out*, Tomas, because a map in a drawer is just paper, but a map in the world is a road, and there are people—" Her voice failed and she let it, briefly, and then finished anyway. "There are people who need roads."

He was silent for a long time. She watched him the way she watched a river in spring, looking for the crack where the ice would give.

"Show me the harbor section," he said at last.

Her stomach dropped. "Why?"

"Because I've been standing at those gates for two years, and I know what you don't." He leaned forward, and now his voice had the flat, precise cadence of the man who counted things. "The harbor district was redrawn in April. New checkpoint line along the canal, new rotation, a third gate where the fish market used to back onto the water. If your atlas shows the old routes—and it does, I saw the plate the night you asked me about guard schedules at the market, don't look surprised, you were about as subtle as a fire alarm—then your atlas is wrong. And a wrong map doesn't save people, Casper. A wrong map *kills* them. They follow it into a gate that isn't there anymore, into a patrol that is."

She stared at him. The candle flame doubled, then steadied. "You memorized the rotation."

"Tuesday's. I copied it out for myself three weeks ago because I already suspected what you were, and I wanted to know whether knowing would make me braver or more afraid." He smiled, and it was a terrible, tender smile. "It made me both."

* * *

She opened the atlas.

She had never shown it to a living soul. Her hands shook so badly getting the oilcloth off that he reached across and stilled them—just his fingers over hers, just for a moment, warm and ink-stained and steady—and then she turned the cover back and let him see.

Page after page. The hidden city in Levin's fine hand and her own newer, harder one: the cellar beneath the synagogue on Zydu Street with its second exit through the well shaft; the printer on Subaciaus who kept a false wall behind his paper stock; the courier route along the rooftops from the Gate of Dawn to the river, marked with the little compass roses that meant *safe by night*. And the harbor section, the last chapter, half-finished—Levin's coastlines, her unfinished interior, and the April changes absent, a white wound in the middle of the page.

Tomas bent over it for a long time without touching it. When he finally did touch it, he touched it the way one touches a relic.

"This is what he died for."

"This is what everyone dies for, eventually. He just got to choose what for." She closed the cover gently. "I have four days to redraw the harbor from memory and get it past three gates. I have one forged transit pass, one map tube, and no idea whether the man at the second gate has been transferred since spring. That's the whole of my plan. You wanted to know what you'd be when I finished talking. Now you know what I am. A woman with four days and a drawer."

Tomas stood. He walked to the window, looked out at the rain, and stood there with his back to her for what felt like the length of an entire war.

Then he turned around and recited.

"Harbor gate, primary, six men, rotation change at midnight and four. Canal checkpoint, secondary, four men and a dog—the dog retires in February, they haven't replaced it. River gate, tertiary, three men, and the sergeant there is Karosas, who drinks, and whose drinking I have covered twice because covering it bought me a favor, and I have just now decided to spend that favor on you." He said it all without notes, without hesitation, the numbers falling out of him like coins from a torn pocket. "The new gate at the fish market backs onto a loading yard with a blind corner at the north wall. The blind corner is not on any official map. It is on mine, because I walk that wall every morning and I have nothing else in my head worth keeping."

Casper had not realized she was crying until the ink on the atlas cover blurred.

"Why," she said. It was the only question left. "You could walk out of here and be a hero by morning. A promotion. A commendation. Why are you standing in a traitor's studio telling me the blind corners of your own city?"

He came back to the table. He did not touch her. He sat down across from her, and the candlelight made one small nation of the space between their hands.

"Because when I was nineteen a professor drew a line on a slate and I asked where the people go, and everyone laughed, and one girl in the third row didn't laugh. She just looked at me like I'd asked the only question in the room." He turned his hand over on the table, palm up. An offering, or a surrender. "I've spent six years being the man who guards the line. I'd like, before they shoot me or promote me or both, to be the man who asks where the people go. Just once. Just with my whole life behind the asking."

She put her hand in his.

It was not a declaration. There were no words for what they were—two people on opposite sides of a line that had been drawn through the middle of each of them—and they were both too honest, and too afraid, to pretend there were. They sat with their hands joined over the closed atlas, and the candles burned down, and outside the rain kept erasing the city and the city kept refusing to be erased.

"I walk you through the first gate myself," Tomas said finally. "Sunday. The paper gate. I'll have transit seals on your tube, real ones, from my own kit. If they stop us, I'm escorting an evidence transfer and you're my prisoner, and you'll have to look at me like I'm the worst thing that ever happened to you."

"I've had practice looking at uniforms that way."

"Good." He almost smiled. Then it faded, and he said, very quietly, "Casper. If this goes wrong—if they take the tube, if they take you—promise me you'll burn the atlas rather than let it be read by the wrong hands. He burned his maps. You have to be able to burn yours."

She thought of Levin in the doorway, his hand on the case, two shots, the quiet after. She thought of the coal chute, and the cold, and the three years of ink she could not sign.

"No," she said. "He didn't burn this one. He gave it to me. That was his whole answer to the soldiers, Tomas—he kept one map out of the fire. I'll burn every map in the world before this one, and this one never." She tightened her grip on his hand. "You guard the line. I *am* the line. Learn the difference."

He looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw made him nod—slowly, the way men nod at verdicts.

* * *

He left before the fourth bell, because a guard's absence is a signature and his had to stay forged. At the door he stopped, cap in hand, rain waiting behind him.

"Tuesday's rotation changes Friday," he said. "I'll memorize Friday's. Come to the fish market at dusk and buy herring, and I'll be counting soldiers nearby, and between the two of us we'll know everything the city knows about its own gates."

"And after Sunday?" she asked. "After the tide?"

He was quiet. The candle between them had burned to a thumb's width, and its light barely reached his face.

"After Sunday," he said, "I'll be either in a cell or in a uniform I can't wear honestly. So let me say it now, while I'm still a free man with a bad future: whatever they call this—" he touched, briefly, the space between their hands, the air where their joined fingers had been "—I'm not sorry for it. Whatever it's called. Whatever it costs."

Then he was gone, boots on the stair, and the door closed, and Casper stood alone in the guttering light with the atlas in her arms and four days on the clock.

She carried it back to the drawer. Then she stopped, and set it on the drafting table instead, open to the wounded harbor page, and trimmed a fresh wick, and filled a pen.

Four days. One chapter. She dipped the pen.

Outside, far off toward the river, a whistle blew—the changing of some guard, some gate, some line—and Casper Galloway bent over the blank paper and began, stroke by stroke, to draw the way out.
