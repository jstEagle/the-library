## 13. Checkpoint One: The Paper Gate

The glove was the first thing to betray her.

Not the tube—Tomas had seen to that, wrapping it in brown paper and wax until it looked like nothing more than a surveyor's commission from the municipal works office. Not the pass—Rivke's press had turned out a transit document so clean that even the watermark held up to lamplight. Not the clothes: a courier's grey coat, a man's flat cap pulled low over her cropped hair, boots two sizes too large stuffed with newspaper so they wouldn't slap the cobbles like a woman's heels.

It was the glove. Her right one. She had taken it off in the alley behind the tannery to check the seal on the tube one last time, and now, standing in the queue outside the Paper Gate with forty other souls shuffling toward the clerk's table, she realized she was holding it in her left hand like something she'd stolen.

*Put it on,* she told herself. *Slowly. Couriers fidget. Couriers are cold. No one remembers a cold courier.*

But her fingers wouldn't obey. They had been refusing obedience all morning, since before dawn, since Tomas had pressed the tube into her hands in the doorway of Rivke's basement and said, in a voice stripped of everything but function: *"You don't look at the clerk. You look past him. A man carrying orders looks through people; a man carrying contraband looks at faces."*

She put the glove on. It caught at the second knuckle because her hand was sweating, and for three full seconds—a lifetime, an era, the span in which a guard could cross a courtyard and demand to see what made her palms wet—she stood in the open air of Vokiečių Street wrestling with a single kid-leather glove while the queue moved forward without her.

Then it slid home, and she breathed, and the line carried her another four meters toward the gate.

---

The Paper Gate had been a customs house once, back when Vilna's borders were lines on maps instead of men with rifles. Its arched entrance still bore the faded crest of some dead administration, a double-headed eagle worn smooth by weather and indifference. Now sandbags framed the archway in a low breastwork, and a machine gun sat atop them under an oiled tarpaulin like a sleeping animal no one wanted to wake. Two guards flanked the clerk's table. A third lounged against the wall by the records office, smoking, his rifle slung with the careless ease of a man who had never needed it.

Casper catalogued all of it the way Master Levin had taught her, years ago, when she was still a girl who thought maps were about mountains.

*Every checkpoint is a map of itself. The gun is the fixed point. The clerk is the road everyone must travel. The smoker is your variable—he can move, so he is the danger. Count the exits. Count the eyes.*

Three exits. Four eyes on the queue, plus the smoker's, which drifted anywhere and nowhere. She fixed the geometry in her mind—not for today, today there was only forward—but because recording it was the only prayer she knew anymore. Somewhere in the atlas there would be a page for this. Somewhere, if the atlas ever reached London, some other fugitive would stand where she stood and know exactly what waited inside the arch.

The queue shortened. A farmer argued about a grain permit and lost. An old woman with a wicker basket was waved through after the clerk lifted out her cabbage and looked beneath it with the weary thoroughness of a man who had found worse than contraband in baskets. A family—mother, father, a boy of perhaps ten—approached together, and Casper watched the clerk's manner change, watched the paper in his hands become suddenly fascinating, watched the guards shift their weight in that particular way that meant *not yet, not here, wait for the courtyard to empty.*

The family was turned away. The mother did not cry out. That was the worst part—the silence of it, the practiced way the father took the boy's hand and the mother took the papers back as though reclaiming a borrowed umbrella. They walked back down Vokiečių Street the way water flows downhill, without decision, without hope of any other direction.

Casper's stomach turned to iron.

*Four ahead of you. Three. Two.*

She ran the litany one final time, the one she'd composed at Rivke's table by lamplight, each clause a rung on a ladder over an abyss:

*I am Jurgis Balodis, courier for the municipal survey office. I carry sealed instruments to the harbor engineer. I have a transit pass countersigned Tuesday. I am tired, I am cold, I want my supper. I am not afraid, because Jurgis Balodis has nothing in this world worth being afraid for.*

The lie fit her like the glove had—snug everywhere except the knuckles.

*"Next."*

---

The clerk was younger than she'd expected. That unsettled her more than cruelty would have. He had a long face, ink-stained fingers, and the squint of a man who read small print all day, and when he extended his hand for her papers he did not look up from the ledger in front of him.

Casper laid the transit pass on the table. She kept her grip light—Tomas had warned her about that too, about the desperation of hands that hold documents too tightly, as though the paper might be snatched back—and stepped back half a pace, into the posture of a man waiting out a delay he resents.

The clerk picked up the pass. Squinted. Held it closer to the lamp.

Somewhere behind her, in the city she was leaving, a church bell rang the quarter hour. Nine-fifteen. The tide turned at noon. The ship—the *Aurora*, a Swedish freighter that took on timber and, when the money was right, human cargo—would clear the breakwater by half past, and every meter between her and the gangway was measured now in minutes she could feel draining out of her like heat from a stone.

The clerk turned the pass over. Turned it back.

"Balodis," he said. Testing the name the way a man tests ice.

"Sir." Her voice came out lower than she'd planned, roughened deliberately. She cleared her throat. "Jurgis Balodis."

"Courier." His eyes came up at last, pale and unhurried, and fixed on her face. "For the survey office."

"For six years." This was true of someone, somewhere; the real Balodis existed, or had, a minor functionary whose identity Rivke's press had resurrected with a death certificate and a clean coat. "Since before the reorganization."

"The reorganization." The clerk set the pass down and laced his ink-stained fingers together. "And what does the survey office send to the harbor engineer that requires a sealed tube?"

"Instruments." She let boredom flatten the word. "Sighting levels. A theodolite lens. Paper seals cost less than replacing broken glass, sir. Ask anyone in the office."

"I intend to."

He reached for the tube.

Everything in Casper's body went silent—the way a forest goes silent, the way her own heartbeat seemed to stop and hang suspended in the cold air of the customs house. She watched his ink-stained fingers close around the brown paper wrapping, watched the wax seal catch the lamplight, the municipal crest pressed deep and crisp by Tomas's own hand two nights ago in a room that smelled of candle smoke and fear.

*If he breaks it,* she thought, with a clarity that felt almost calm, *it is finished. If he breaks it, I will never see the tide, and Tomas will have burned his life for a tube of paper, and Master Levin's three years of dying will end at a clerk's table under a lamp.*

The clerk did not break the seal. He weighed the tube in his hand instead, turning it slowly, and frowned at something.

"This seal," he said. "It's dated Thursday."

Her blood stopped.

"It is dated Thursday," she agreed, because denial was a door and agreement was a wall.

"The survey office seals its dispatches on Fridays. Standing order. I process their couriers twice a month." He set the tube down on the table between them, gently, the way a chess player sets down a piece he intends to make you look at. "Why is yours dated Thursday?"

The smoker by the wall shifted his weight. One of the guards at the archway turned his head.

And Casper Galloway—who had spent three years learning to be no one, who had memorized the grammar of survival the way other women memorized recipes, who had recited false grandmothers to census takers and forged baptisms to boards of verification—felt the whole architecture of her disguise tremble like a bridge under too much weight.

Then she remembered Master Levin's voice, quiet as ash falling: *When the map fails you, cartographer, draw a new one. There is always another route. There is always another page.*

She sighed. She let the sigh carry six years of bureaucratic grievance.

"Because the office sealed it Thursday," she said, "and then the engineer's assistant telephoned Friday morning to say the specifications had changed, and rather than waste a new seal on the same instruments, the chief told me to go with the old one and take the amendment separately." She patted her breast pocket, where a folded sheet of blank paper lay. "Which I have here. You're welcome to read it, though I warn you it's duller than the seal question."

The clerk stared at her.

The pause stretched—one second, two, three—long enough for Casper to notice absurd things: a fly walking across the ledger's margin; the smell of lamp oil and wet wool; the boy's family long gone now, swallowed by the street, and no one in the queue behind her daring to cough.

Then the clerk huffed—a short exhalation through the nose that might, in a different life, in a different country, have been a laugh.

"Survey office," he muttered, with the weary contempt of one bureaucrat for another's chaos. He stamped the transit pass. He pushed it and the tube across the table together. "Amendment goes to the records office, not me. Next time tell your chief some of us have to account for dates."

"Some of us account for nothing," Casper said, "that's why we're couriers."

She picked up the tube. Her hands were steady. Later she would understand that they had gone steady precisely because the terror had burned through its fuel all at once, leaving behind that strange ringing calm she'd felt only once before—standing in Master Levin's studio while smoke rose from the grate and his hands pressed the atlas against her chest.

"Move along," the clerk said, already reaching for the next set of papers.

---

She walked through the arch.

Ten meters of shadow under the vaulted stone, the machine gun looming above her left shoulder, the guard at the far side glancing at her stamped pass and jerking his chin: *through.* Ten meters that took a year apiece. And then she was through, out the other side, onto the quay road where the wind came off the river smelling of tar and fish and diesel, and the harbor spread out below her in the grey morning light—cranes and masts and the brown water sliding seaward, and there, at the timber dock, the *Aurora*, black-hulled, patient, flying a flag she couldn't yet read at this distance.

Casper made herself walk. Not run. Couriers didn't run. She walked down the quay road with the tube under her arm and her heart slamming against her ribs like something trying to get out, and she did not look back at the Paper Gate, and she did not let her knees fold until she reached the shelter of the fish market's awning, where she stood among the gutted herring and the shouting wives and shook so violently that a fishwife handed her a mug of chicory without being asked and said, "Cold morning, love. Drink it slow."

She drank it slow.

Under the awning, hidden by the crowd and the steam, she allowed herself thirty seconds of being a person again—thirty seconds in which her eyes stung and her breath came ragged and the glove on her right hand felt like a shackle she'd earned. Then she folded the feeling away, the way she folded the atlas's pages, flat and precise and ready for use.

One gate down. The river gate next, at eleven, where Tomas would be waiting in his uniform with escort authority that would be logged against his name forever. And after that—

After that, the harbor. After that, the tide.

She set down the mug, thanked the fishwife with a nod, and turned toward the river road—and stopped.

At the mouth of the market lane, half-hidden behind a stack of crates, a man in civilian clothes was watching the quay. Not the boats, not the fish stalls. The quay. The road from the Paper Gate. He held a notebook, and though he was too far for her to see his face clearly, something in the set of his shoulders, the tilt of his head as he scanned each traveler coming through—

She knew that silhouette. She had seen it leaning over her master's drying maps, pen poised, voice soft as a blade going in.

The census taker.

He hadn't followed her. He couldn't have—no one had known her route except Tomas and Rivke, and neither of them would have given it up alive. Which meant he wasn't watching for her at all. He was watching the road itself, the flow of bodies, keeping his lists, doing his patient work—and she was simply one more figure in his field of view, a courier in a man's cap walking toward the river with a tube under her arm.

Unless he looked harder. Unless he saw what he had seen that day in the studio, behind her eyes, in the way she stood.

Casper pulled the cap lower, turned her shoulder to the lane, and began to walk—unhurried, bored, invisible—along the front of the fish stalls, putting crates and bodies and the reek of brine between herself and that watching silhouette, while behind her the church bells rang the half hour, and the tide, two hours out, began its long pull toward the sea.
