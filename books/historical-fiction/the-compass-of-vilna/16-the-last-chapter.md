## 16. The Last Chapter

The lamp burned low by the time Casper spread the blank plate across the drafting table, and she sat for a long while without touching it, the way one sits before a grave.

The paper was good stock—Rivke had procured it from somewhere, along with a fresh cake of ink and three new nibs, and had asked no questions beyond *will this be enough?* It would have to be enough. Four days until the tide. Three checkpoints between here and the gangway. And in her head, if her head held, the whole drowned geography of the harbor district that Master Levin had spent his final spring walking, measuring, and committing to memory because he no longer dared to draw it.

Now she would draw it. From memory. In one night.

She trimmed the wick so the flame stood tall and steady, and she began where he had always taught her to begin: not with the lines, but with the water.

*The river first,* he used to say, hands folded behind his back as he paced the studio, *because the river does not lie and does not negotiate. Everything else is argument. The water is simply true.* So she inked the bend of the Viliya as it swung wide past the timber yards, the shallow shelf below the old granary where the current slowed enough for a rowboat to cross at night without an engine's sound carrying, the stone embankment with its iron rings worn smooth by a century of mooring ropes. Her hand knew these lines even when her mind doubted them. Three years of apprenticeship lived in her wrist. She felt him standing at her shoulder the way she always did at the start of a difficult plate—felt the ghost-weight of his breath, the creak of his floorboards—and she let the feeling steady her rather than grieve her. There would be time to grieve after the ship sailed. If there was time at all.

The harbor district came next, and here the work turned from cartography into testimony.

She drew the fish market square with its crooked colonnade, marking in Levin's cipher—the small hooked symbol they had devised together in her second year, meaning *watched: do not linger*—beside the corner where the new guard post stood. She drew the customs house and its blind side, the alley behind the chandler's shop too narrow for two soldiers abreast, the cellar door beneath the cooperage that opened onto the smugglers' stair down to the waterline. Every line was a life. She understood that now in a way she had not fully understood when the atlas was only her master's obsession and she was only his hands. The woman who sold herring at the third stall kept a false wall behind her ice chest; the ice chest was on the plate. The ferryman who asked no questions but counted coins twice; the ferryman was on the plate. The blind man who heard footsteps better than any sentry saw them; the blind man was on the plate, marked with the small circle-and-cross that meant *trusted absolutely.*

And then the names.

This was the part she had been dreading, though she had not admitted it to herself until her nib hovered over the margin. The atlas did not carry names—not in plain script, never—but it carried initials, dates, tiny marginalia in Levin's cramped hand that she had memorized the way other people memorized prayers. *M.R., widow, north wall, takes children only. S.B., baker, will hide papers in flour sacks, unreliable after dark. D.K., doctor, charges nothing, asks nothing.* Each one a person she might be delivering to the gallows if this plate were seized. Each one a person who would be lost entirely if it were not drawn at all.

Her hand shook on the first initial. She set down the nib, pressed her palms flat against the table, and breathed until the shaking stopped.

*Rivke took the original plate,* she reminded herself. *Rivke returned it. Whatever else the network is, it has held for years under worse pressure than one frightened girl at a lamp.* She picked up the nib again. She wrote the initials. She wrote them all.

* * *

Midnight came and went. The candle beside the lamp guttered and she replaced it without thinking, the way her body performed its small offices while her mind walked the streets she was drawing. Twice she stopped to test herself—closed her eyes, turned away from the plate, and recited aloud the sequence of turns from the cooperage stair to the safe house above the sailmaker's loft. Left at the arch. Right past the shrine with the broken saint. Through the yard where the dog was friendly and would bark at strangers but not at those who smelled of the river. Both times the sequence came back whole. Both times she opened her eyes half expecting the room to be gone, expecting to find herself somehow already in those streets, so completely had the map become the territory in her mind.

Somewhere in the small hours, the exhaustion broke over her like cold water, and with it came the memories she had been holding at arm's length all night.

Levin, in the last autumn, standing at this same table with his sleeves rolled, showing her how to lay a wash so faint it read as blank paper unless you held it to a flame at the correct angle. *The best hiding place,* he said, *is a thing everyone looks at and no one sees.* He had laughed then—a dry, surprised sound, as if laughter had caught him unawares. She could count on one hand the times she had heard it.

Levin, three days before the soldiers came, tracing the unfinished harbor section with one finger and frowning. *The city changes faster than I can draw it,* he said. *That is the trouble with maps of living things. They are confessions of ignorance.* She had thought he was speaking of coastlines and guard posts. She had been slow. She had been so slow, and slowness had cost him everything.

She put the memory down the way she would put down a weight she intended to pick up again later, and she worked.

Toward three o'clock the details thinned and the great shapes stood complete: river, quay, market, alleys, stairs, cellars, the checkpoint positions marked in red-umber with their sightlines indicated by the fine dotted arcs Levin had invented for exactly this purpose. She checked it against the fragments of the stolen plate that remained in her memory—the hour Rivke's courier had possessed it, Casper had studied it desperately, greedily, imprinting what she could—and found them agreeing in all but two small particulars. One was a courtyard gate she believed had been bricked up since the plate was drawn; she amended it. The other was a guard rotation arrow near the customs house that she could not verify and dared not guess at. She left it blank and wrote in the margin, in cipher: *verify before use.*

Honesty, even on a forged map. Especially on a forged map. People would die of her vanity if she allowed herself any.

* * *

Dawn found her with the plate finished and drying, propped at a slight angle so the ink would set without pooling, and Casper standing before it like a witness before a coffin.

It was good work. She knew it was good work, and knowing gave her no pleasure—only a fierce, exhausted gratitude that her hands had not failed her master when his hands could no longer serve. The harbor section looked as though Levin himself had drawn it. That was the inheritance, she realized. Not the atlas, not the routes, not even the knowledge. The inheritance was this: that when the moment came, her hand would move as his hand had moved, and the work would continue as though death were merely a change of draftsman.

But a plate unsigned was a plate orphaned. Levin had been explicit about that, once, in one of his rare discourses on the philosophy of their trade. *An unsigned map is a rumor,* he said. *A signed map is a promise. Sign your work, Casper, even when signing is dangerous. Especially then. A promise made anonymously is no promise at all.*

She had not signed anything in three years. She had signed permits with false names, receipts with false names, letters with no name at all. Her own name had become a contraband she carried folded small against her heart.

She took a clean nib. She dipped it. And at the lower right corner of the plate, in the formal cartouche style Levin had favored, she wrote:

**I. LEVIN**

His name first. Because the knowledge was his, bought with his blood, and she was only its custodian. Because the dead deserved the credit of the living's courage. Because some promises must be made in another's name before they can be made in one's own.

Then, beneath it, smaller but no less firm:

**C. GALLOWAY**

Her hand did not shake. She watched it write the name she had hidden for three years, and she felt neither fear nor triumph—only a quiet click, deep inside, like a lock turning home. Let them come for a Galloway. Let the census taker find his summons answered by a name on a traitor's map. She was done being invisible. Invisibility had saved her skin and cost her everything else.

And then, because the atlas would carry two names to London or none at all, because there was a man in a uniform who had copied her address off a permit and burned his own letters and lied with his pen for her sake and would stand at a gate for her tomorrow—

**T. VAITKUS**

Three names. A dead man who taught her, a living woman who chose, and a living man who would pay. She blew gently across the wet ink, and the signatures dried together, inseparable.

Outside, the sky over the rooftops went from ash to rose. Somewhere below, Rivke's press clanked into its morning rhythm, printing lies that told the truth. Somewhere across the city, Tomas was putting on his uniform and folding falsified orders against his breast, and the clock was running, and the tide would not wait.

Casper rolled the plate with hands that finally, finally trembled—afterward, never during—and slid it into the map tube with the others. She sealed it. She weighed it in her palm: lighter than a loaf of bread, heavier than anything she had ever carried.

One chapter finished. Two gates remaining. And somewhere in the city, a census taker who had seen her face was asking questions that had not yet stopped being asked.

She shouldered the tube, pinched out the lamp, and went down the stairs toward the last day.
