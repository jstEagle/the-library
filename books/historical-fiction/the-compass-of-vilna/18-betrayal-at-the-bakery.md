## 18. Betrayal at the Bakery

The morning came in gray and smelling of rye.

Casper had walked this street a hundred times without thinking of it—Didžioji, where the cobbles wore their winter polish of wet stone and the shopfronts leaned together like conspirators sharing a secret over a fence. But today every window was an eye. Today the bread smell rising from Kagan's bakery felt like a hand laid flat against her chest, gentle and immovable, saying: *stop here.*

She stopped at the corner by the shuttered bookshop and let herself look ordinary. A woman with a market basket, a scarf pulled low, cheeks reddened by cold that wasn't entirely the weather's doing. Under her coat, flat against her ribs, the atlas rode in its oilcloth wrapping, thin as a psalter and heavy as sin. The tube was too long to hide; the oilcloth made it merely a bundle of drawings, a student's work, nothing. She had told herself that four times since leaving Rivke's basement, and four times the words had come out sounding like someone else's rehearsed alibi.

*The bakery. Nine o'clock. Ask for the dark loaves, the ones with caraway. I'll be at the back table.* That was what Rivke had said last night, lamplight carving hollows under her eyes, her hands still floury from the press room even though she hadn't touched flour in years—ink did that, Casper had learned; ink got into the creases of the knuckles and stayed there like a second set of fingerprints.

Rivke had been late arriving last night. Casper hadn't thought anything of it then. People were late; the city made people late; soldiers made people later. But now, standing at the corner with the atlas pressed to her side like a second heartbeat, she found herself turning the lateness over the way she'd turn a coin whose edge felt wrong.

Through the bakery's fogged window she could see the tables, the brass rail along the counter, the woman behind it pulling loaves with a wooden peel. And at the back table—

Not Rivke.

A man sat alone with a cup of coffee he was not drinking. He wore a civilian coat, brown, unremarkable, buttoned to the throat, but he sat the way officials sit when they are pretending not to be officials: spine too straight, hands arranged, eyes fixed on the middle distance with the studied blankness of a man listening through his own ears for something else. Casper knew him. Of course she knew him. Three years of annual visits had burned his face into her memory more thoroughly than any plate she'd ever etched—the census taker, the one with the soft voice and the questions that arrived sideways, the one who had stood in Master Levin's studio and looked too long at pages drying on the rack.

His name was Gudaitis. She had never learned it until the summons card, which had carried it in small clerk's letters: *J. Gudaitis, Office of Population Verification.*

He was at the back table. Rivke's table.

Casper did not go in.

She turned her head slowly, the way a person turns who has simply remembered an errand, and looked down the street toward the river. Two men stood beneath the awning of the tobacco shop across the way, talking, or shaped like talking—one held a cigarette he never lit, the other kept his hands in his pockets and his weight on the balls of his feet. They were positioned, she realized with a cartographer's cold clarity, at the two sightlines of the intersection. Anyone approaching the bakery from the north would pass them. Anyone coming from the south would be seen by the man at the window table inside, who was not drinking his coffee.

It was a map. They had drawn a map of the trap, and she was standing on its margin, and the only reason she was not already inside it was that Rivke had taught her, months ago, always to look first from a corner, always to count the men who weren't doing anything.

*Rivke.* The name went through her like a nail. Where was Rivke? Had she been taken already, on her way here, in the night, in the basement with the press still warm? Or was she walking toward this corner right now, basket on her arm, about to round the same corner Casper occupied?

Casper stepped back into the shadow of the bookshop doorway and waited, counting seconds the way her master had taught her to count scale bars—steadily, without hope, because numbers didn't care what you wanted. At forty seconds, a woman came up the street from the south with a covered basket, quick-stepping, head down. Gray shawl. The particular roll of the shoulders that Casper would have known in a crowd of thousands.

Rivke reached the bakery door.

And the two men under the tobacco awning stopped pretending to talk.

Casper's body moved before her mind finished the sentence. Later she would try to reconstruct the decision and find there wasn't one—only the fact of her legs carrying her down the side alley beside the bookshop, the atlas clutched flat, her breath tearing white in the cold. Behind her she heard the bakery door open, the little bell above it giving its cheerful domestic ring, and then a voice she knew saying, in tones of elaborate mildness:

"Frau Shapira. How good of you to come."

Gudaitis. He'd said *Shapira*, not the false name Rivke used on her papers, and the wrongness of it rang down the street like a dropped glass.

Casper reached the end of the alley. It opened onto a courtyard stacked with firewood and ringed by the backs of buildings—a dead end except for the low door of the bakery's cellar, sunk into the ground at the courtyard's edge, its hatch propped open on a cinderblock because the morning's delivery had come early. She could hear, muffled through stone and timber, the sounds from inside: a chair scraping, Rivke's voice pitched high and steady, performing indignation, and Gudaitis answering softly, softly, the way he'd asked *which family do you belong to* three years ago while his eyes crawled over drying maps.

She should run. North, south, anywhere. Every instinct bred into three years of hiding said *away.*

But the cellar door was open, and beyond it lay the catacombs, and beyond the catacombs lay the harbor, and the harbor gate, and Tomas, and the tide—and between her and all of it now stood two men in a courtyard she'd have to cross anyway if she ran. The cellar was not away. The cellar was *through.*

Master Levin's voice, three years old and worn smooth as a river stone: *A map is not the lines, Casper. It is the choosing of what to leave out. Remember what you leave out, and you can always find your way back in.*

She left out the running. She became the delivery girl.

There was a canvas apron folded over the cinderblock holding the hatch—left by the baker's boy, presumably, mid-errand. Casper shrugged it on over her coat in three seconds, took up the empty wicker tray leaning against the wall, and descended the cellar steps with the tray balanced on her hip like she'd been born carrying other people's bread.

* * *

The cellar was warm and yeast-dark, all brick arches and hanging ropes of dried onions, sacks of flour slumped against the walls like sleeping animals. A single bulb burned at the far end near the stairs up into the shop. Casper crossed to it, set down the tray, and began moving sacks with brisk, bored efficiency—rearranging nothing, accomplishing nothing, being furniture.

Above her, the floorboards spoke.

She couldn't make out words, only the grammar of them: Gudaitis's low even cadence, unhurried, a man who had all morning and knew it. Rivke's voice climbing once, cut off. Then silence of a particular quality—the silence of people standing up.

Casper's hands went still on a flour sack. Flour dust hung in the bulb light, turning slowly, beautiful and indifferent.

Then footsteps on the cellar stairs—not Gudaitis's measured tread but the quick hard heels of the men from the awning, coming down two at a time, and a voice barking upward, "Clear down here!" followed by Gudaitis from above, mild as milk: "Check the yard. She may have come early. Check everyone leaving the square."

They hadn't seen her yet. The bulb light ended at the archway; she was standing in the black past it, pressed between flour sacks and the cold outer wall, and the two men were scanning the lit half of the cellar, their backs to her.

*The map becomes the territory.* Her master had said that too, once, laughing, drunk on Passover wine: *One day, girl, the lines will save your life, because you'll be standing inside yourself.*

Behind the onion ropes, at the cellar's north wall, there was a door. Not a hidden door—nothing so romantic—an old service door, painted shut, that the baker used for coal deliveries in winter. Casper had mapped this building eighteen months ago for the network, one of forty structures she'd surveyed, and she had noted in her field book, in her own private shorthand: *N wall, coal door, hinge rusted, opens inward, drops to culvert level — old channel runs W under Didžioji toward river gate district.*

She had drawn it. Now she would walk it.

The men turned. One of them saw the apron, the tray, the rearranged sacks, and dismissed her in a single flick of the eyes—"You. Out front, stay where the officer can see you"—and Casper nodded dumbly, picked up her tray, and walked toward the stairs. Toward the light. Toward Gudaitis.

Because the worst thing, the truly unbearable thing, was that she wanted to see Rivke's face. Wanted to read it. *Was it you? Was it the network after all, trading me to buy time, or did they take you and make you talk?* Three days ago Rivke had confessed to stealing the harbor plate and returned it with a place on the ship for two, and Casper had forgiven her—had believed she'd forgiven her—but forgiveness is a door that swings both ways, and standing now at the foot of the stairs she understood that some part of her had never closed it.

She climbed six steps. Through the doorway at the top she could see the back table, and Rivke standing beside it with two men flanking her, and Gudaitis seated again, comfortable again, turning something over in his fingers.

A pencil. Her pencil—the one she'd left, weeks ago, in the cup on Rivke's press table. Soft graphite, the kind cartographers used, the kind no fishwife's cousin would own.

"Interesting thing to find," Gudaitis was saying gently, "in the home of a woman who cannot read a transit schedule." He looked up then, past Rivke's shoulder, toward the cellar door—and Casper saw the exact moment his gaze slid over the delivery girl in the apron and snagged, caught, held.

Time did something strange. The bakery sounds—the bell, the peel scraping the oven, a customer complaining about prices—all of it receded as if a door had closed on it. Gudaitis's eyes narrowed by perhaps a millimeter. His head began to tilt, the way it had tilted three years ago over the drying maps.

Casper dropped the tray.

It was pure theater and she knew it—clumsy girl, clumsy tray, loaves rolling everywhere—and it bought her exactly the two seconds she needed, because every eye in the room went to the bouncing bread, human nature being what it is, even in a room full of hunters. She was already moving, backward, down the stairs, and by the time the first shout went up she had shouldered through the onion ropes and hit the coal door with her whole body.

It groaned. Paint cracked. It did not open.

Behind her, boots on the stairs. In front of her, a door that eighteen months ago had been rusted shut, and she had written *hinge rusted* and gone on to the next building because it hadn't mattered then.

It mattered now.

She hit it again, lower, at the lock plate, and felt something give—a shriek of metal, a crack of splitting wood—and then hands were on her collar, hauling her back, and she twisted, dropped, drove her elbow into whatever part of the man she could reach, and heard him grunt and loosen, and threw herself at the door a third time.

It burst inward. Cold air, wet stone smell, darkness. She fell through onto gravel and rolled, and the atlas dug into her ribs hard enough to leave a bruise she would carry to London without ever knowing its date.

Someone fired. The shot went wide—too narrow a doorway, too much fear of hitting their own man—and the sound of it in the confined culvert was apocalyptic, a hammer blow inside a bell. Then she was up and running, bent double, into the black.

* * *

The old channel ran west under Didžioji exactly as she had drawn it, because channels do not care about empires. Water seeped somewhere below the gravel bed, invisible, audible, a whispering companion. Overhead, at intervals, iron grates let down blades of morning light, and Casper moved between them like a swimmer surfacing between waves—never lingering in the light, never quite losing it.

Her hands were shaking so badly she had to press them flat against her thighs to stop them. The atlas. Feel the atlas. It was there, whole, dry, wrapped in oilcloth, riding against her ribs. Whatever happened now, whatever they did to Rivke, whatever Gudaitis reported to whichever ministry had purchased his patience—the atlas was here, and the ship sailed tomorrow morning, and between her and the harbor lay perhaps two kilometers of city that existed, for the next few hours, only inside her own skull.

She stopped under the fourth grate, where the light fell in a dusty column, and allowed herself ten seconds of grief.

Ten seconds for Rivke, standing in that bakery with her chin up, lying beautifully or telling the truth, Casper would never know which. Ten seconds for the pencil in Gudaitis's fingers, which meant they had searched the basement, which meant the press was found or soon would be, which meant every courier route on the plates she'd copied was burning right now or soon would be. Ten seconds for the woman she'd been yesterday, who had a plan.

Then she took out her compass, opened it, and did what her master had trained her to do.

North-northwest. The channel would fork ahead—she remembered the fork, she had *drawn* the fork, a junction where a medieval drain met a cistern gallery, marked in her field book with a symbol meaning *passable, low ceiling, wet*. Take the left branch. Four hundred paces. Then a shaft with iron rungs, disused, emerging—she closed her eyes and saw her own handwriting—*behind the fish market, under the ice house, grate loose.*

The smugglers' marks would begin after that. Master Levin's marks, chalk and knife-cut, the little language of arrows and crescents he'd taught her to read on the assumption that someday one of his students would need to read them in the dark, running.

"Someday" had been patient. Someday was now.

She moved. The dark took her measurements step by step, correcting her, teaching her the difference between a line on paper and the thing itself—the culvert was narrower than she'd drawn it, the ceiling lower, the water higher after autumn rains, and twice she had to stop and admit, aloud, in a whisper that embarrassed her, *I was wrong here,* and stand still until her memory surrendered the correction. This was the discipline no apprentice ever believes matters: not drawing well, but *remembering honestly*. A mapmaker who flatters her lines kills the traveler who trusts them.

At the fork she paused. From somewhere far above, filtered through stone and street, came a sound she knew: whistles. Many of them, overlapping, spreading. They had found the coal door. They were putting dogs on the streets, probably, and men at the grates, and Gudaitis—he would be thinking like her now, she realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the cold. He had seen her maps. He knew how her mind worked. He would not search the tunnels randomly; he would ask himself *where does she surface*, and he would go to the answer.

The fish market. He would go to the fish market.

Unless. Unless he thought she'd expect him to think that, and—

No. Stop. Paralysis dressed as cleverness. Master Levin again, sharper now: *Draw the truth you know, not the lie you fear.*

She took the left branch.

Four hundred paces, counted in her head, the water rising past her ankles, the ceiling pressing down until she walked bent like a question mark. Twice the passage narrowed around fallen brick and she squeezed through with the atlas held above the water, arms trembling, whispering to it like a child: *not you, not today.* Once she passed a smuggler's mark already—knife-cut crescent at shoulder height on the left wall, exactly where the master's notes had promised—and she touched it as she went by, the way you touch a doorpost, the way you touch a grave.

The shaft came at last, exactly where she'd drawn it, its iron rungs furred with rust and old rope fibers. Above it, faint but unmistakable, drifted the smell she had been walking toward all night and all morning: fish, ice, tar, the harbor.

She climbed. Each rung rang dully under her boots. At the top, the loose grate she'd noted eighteen months ago swung aside with a grinding whisper, and gray dawn light poured down over her hands, and she hauled herself out into the alley behind the ice house, bleeding, soaked to the knee, alive.

The fish market spread out before her, waking. Carts. Gulls. The great salt-smelling bustle of a city that did not yet know what it would become.

And at the mouth of the alley, checking papers at the market gate with his cap pulled low and his jaw set like a man chewing on his own future, stood a border guard she knew better than any line she had ever drawn.

Tomas looked up. Their eyes met across fifty feet of cobblestone and catastrophe, and behind her, deep under the earth, she heard the whistles still searching, and she started toward him.
