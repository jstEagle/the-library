## 19. Underground

The dark beneath Vilna was not empty. It had a texture, a grain, like the inside of a violin—old brick and older stone, the smell of wet lime and candle smoke gone cold decades before Casper was born. She moved through it with one hand on the wall and the other pressed flat against her chest, where the map tube rode against her spine like a second heartbeat.

Behind her, muffled by six feet of earth and the bakery's collapsed cellar door, the shouting had stopped. That was worse than the shouting. Shouting meant they were still looking where she wasn't.

She counted her steps because counting was something to do with her mind that wasn't screaming. Rivke's face when they took her—the way she hadn't struggled, the way she'd looked at Casper over the census taker's shoulder and said nothing, gave nothing away, only a small sharp shake of the head: *go*. Casper had gone. She hated herself for going. She would hate herself more if she didn't survive to make the going mean something.

*Left at the third arch,* Master Levin's voice said in her memory, calm as ever, as if he were walking beside her with his pipe unlit between his teeth. *You'll know it by the keystone with the chipped star. The Jesuits built these galleries in the old century; the smugglers cut their own after. Where the two meet, the mortar changes color. Trust the mortar, not your eyes.*

Casper stopped. Her match flared, guttered, showed her a wall of rough-hewn blocks—and there, above her head, a keystone carved with a six-pointed star, its lower point broken away by some mason's carelessness or some mason's kindness, three hundred years ago or thirty. She put her palm against the stone as if it were a hand she could hold.

"I'm here," she whispered. "I'm doing what you told me."

The dark did not answer. It never had. But it had never lied to her either, and that was more than she could say for the city above it.

* * *

She had entered the tunnels knowing the first mile only from the atlas—from plates she had inked herself, copying Levin's survey lines, his notations in his cramped leftward-slanting hand: *low arch, duck; water to the knee after rain; do not trust the eastern branch, it floods.* Knowing a thing on paper and knowing it under the earth with sirens bleeding down through the stone were different countries entirely.

The compass helped. Levin's brass compass, the one he'd pressed into her hands along with the atlas, its needle trembling now in the light of her second-to-last match. Northeast. Everything was northeast. The harbor lay northeast of the old city, past the river gate, past the fish market with its stink of herring and ice, and if the tide waited for no one then neither could she afford to wait for courage. She had to spend what she had now, in installments, one gallery at a time.

The water came sooner than she'd hoped. After twenty minutes the floor tilted downward and the dark ahead took on a sound—a soft, patient lapping, like something breathing in its sleep. She rolled her trousers to the knee, tied them with cord, and stepped in.

Cold climbed her legs like a living thing. The water was black and still and smelled of iron, and somewhere in it her boots found things that shifted—broken crockery, perhaps, or bones, or only stones; she made herself believe only stones. The atlas tube she carried slung across her back now, high between her shoulder blades, sealed with wax at both ends. If the water rose to her neck she would hold it above her head. If the tunnel flooded completely she would drown before she let it drown.

*Sentimental,* Levin would have said, watching her wade with her jaw set. *The atlas is paper. You are flesh. Paper can be redrawn.*

*It cannot,* she answered him silently. *Not this one. Not again.*

Because that was the truth she had learned in Rivke's basement, inking duplicates by lamplight while the press clattered upstairs like a mechanical heart: the atlas was not paper. It was people. Every line she'd drawn was a door someone had slipped through at night; every shaded square was a room where a family had breathed quietly for a season; every name in the margin, coded in Levin's private cipher, was a person who had trusted him with the coordinates of their survival. To lose it was not to lose a document. It was to lose all of them at once, a whole city's worth of second chances, folded into a tube no wider than her arm.

So she walked. And when the water reached her thighs, she walked. And when the ceiling dropped so low she had to turn sideways and drag the tube ahead of her through the gap, scraping her knuckles raw on stone, she went sideways, and dragged, and cursed once, quietly, in Yiddish, which was the language her fear spoke even though her papers said otherwise.

* * *

The marks began an hour in—or what she judged was an hour; time underground was a well without a bottom.

A match flare caught it first: a small triangle scratched into the brick at shoulder height, its point aimed down the passage to her right. She stood very still, letting the memory come up out of her like water rising in a lock.

Levin, three years ago, tracing the same triangle with a cracked fingernail. *"The Bessarabian runners used these before the war. A triangle pointing true means the way is clear. Pointing false—" he'd rotated his finger "—means the route has been watched. Two triangles crossed means do not pass at all."*

This one pointed true. Clear. Someone had walked this gallery recently enough that the scratch still held dust in its grooves rather than being filled with it. Someone alive, moving cargo or people through the dark beneath the occupation, keeping the old grammar alive.

Casper found the next mark forty paces on—an arrow like a bird's foot, then a circle with a slash, meaning *no shelter here*, then, blessedly, a spiral coiled tight as a snail shell: *safe house above. Rest if needed.*

She did not rest. She wanted to—her legs had begun to tremble in a way that had nothing to do with the cold—but the sirens above had changed pitch twice since the bakery, and changing sirens meant changing searches, and changing searches meant the net was drawing tighter around streets she could no longer see. Every minute underground was a minute the harbor gate might be closing. She touched the spiral once, the way you'd touch a sleeping child, and walked on.

But she added her own mark as she passed. A small thing, learned from Levin: a single horizontal stroke beneath the spiral, meaning *one passed through, heading outward, unharmed.* So that whoever came behind her—Rivke's couriers, some fleeing family, some stranger with a compass and no map—would know the way was lived-in. That the dark had traffic. That they were not alone down here, and never had been.

That was the atlas's real secret, she thought, and Levin's, and maybe the whole stubborn history of her people compressed into a sentence: *you are not alone, and never have been.* Every mark on every wall was a hand reaching back through time to say so.

* * *

The third hour nearly killed her.

The eastern branch. She had meant to avoid it—Levin's note was explicit, *do not trust the eastern branch, it floods*—but the western gallery ended in rubble, a collapse she found with her forehead before her matches found it with their light, and the only way forward was the way the atlas forbade.

She sat in the dark for a long moment, breathing, listening to the sirens thin out above her into something distant and mechanical, and made the calculation every fugitive eventually makes: the known danger behind versus the unknown one ahead. The rubble might be passable with hours of digging she didn't have. The flood might be ankle-deep or chest-deep or a drowned throat of water with no air at the end of it.

"Trust the mortar, not your eyes," she said aloud, and her voice came back off the stone sounding smaller than she felt, and that steadied her somehow. Fear shrank when it was given words. Levin had taught her that too, though he'd never said it outright—he'd simply narrated his own dread in that dry, unhurried voice until it became just another item on the day's inventory, to be worked alongside the inking and the tea.

She went east.

The water started at her ankles and rose with every hundred steps, and the compass needle swung once, wildly, near a seam of iron in the rock, and she had to stop and breathe and hold the compass level and wait for the needle to remember itself. When it settled, it pointed her into a stretch where the ceiling rose and the water shallowed, and she understood suddenly that the flood note in the atlas was years old—that the runners had since cut a bypass, or cleared one, because here were fresh marks: the bird's-foot arrow, twice, and then a new symbol she didn't know, a simple vertical line with a dot above it.

She stood before the unknown symbol for a full minute, matches burning down between her fingers.

Then she laughed—a short, cracked sound that the tunnel swallowed whole. A dot above a line. A point above a horizon. She didn't know the code, but she knew cartography, and cartography said: *here is a place marked. Here is something worth noting.* And whatever it marked, the arrows led through it, and the arrows had not lied yet.

She stepped past the symbol and the floor dropped away.

Not far—two feet, maybe three, into a lower channel where the water ran faster, and she went down hard onto hands and knees in the icy current, the tube wrenching against her shoulder strap, one matchbox spinning off into the blackness forever. For a moment she knelt there in the rushing dark, gasping, palms stinging, and felt the full weight of everything she was carrying—not the atlas, which weighed almost nothing, but the dead master and the arrested friend and the man in uniform who had handed her forged orders with his future folded inside them, all of it pressing down on her shoulders like the whole city overhead.

Then she got up. There was no drama in it. That was the thing no one told you about endurance—it wasn't a blaze, it was a chore. You got up because staying down accomplished nothing, and you kept walking because stopping accomplished less, and somewhere in the repetition the fear burned down to embers and the embers gave a steadier light than any flame.

Her hands were bleeding. She noted it the way Levin would have noted a smudge on a plate: a fact, to be dealt with when dealing was possible. She tore a strip from her already-ruined hem, bound the worst of it around her right palm, and kept the compass in her left.

Northeast. Always northeast. Toward the water that moved, toward the ships, toward the morning tide that would not wait and must not be missed.

* * *

Dawn found her before she found the dawn.

There was no way to mark the hour underground except by her body's exhaustion, and her body had stopped reporting honestly hours ago. But the air changed—she noticed it first as a loosening, a faint movement where there had been none, and then as a taste: salt, fish, tar, rope. Harbor air, seeping down through grates and crevices, threading through the last galleries like a promise.

And then, scratched into the stone beside a rusted iron ladder she remembered from the plates—the final mark, the one Levin himself had cut into the atlas as a marginal sketch: a triangle, a wave, and a rising sun, together meaning *the way ends in open air and open water.*

She climbed.

The hatch at the top was wood and rot and thirty years of paint, and it fought her with everything its hinges had left. She put her bleeding shoulder against it, thought absurdly of Tomas opening doors for her—the lecture hall, the fish market stall, the river gate—and pushed with all the strength she had not spent, and the hatch gave with a crack that sounded, in the quiet of the alley behind the fish market, like a gunshot.

She froze in the dark below it, counting heartbeats. Ten. Twenty. No boots. No voices. Only gulls, and the slap of water against pilings, and somewhere a cart wheel squeaking its early rounds.

Casper pulled herself up into the gray light.

The world above was washed pale and new—the sky the color of dishwater going gold at the edges, the fish market stalls shuttered and dripping, the harbor beyond them glittering with that cold early light that makes even imprisonment look briefly beautiful. Sirens had gone silent sometime in the night; the search had moved on, or narrowed, or given her up. The tide was coming in. She could see it in the way the moored boats swung their heads toward the sea, restless, ready.

She stood in the alley with the hatch gaping at her feet and took inventory, as Levin had taught her. Atlas: intact, dry, sealed. Body: soaked, shaking, half-frozen, hands opened up along both palms, one knee bloodied where the channel floor had taken its tax. Time: enough. Barely, but enough.

From her coat pocket she took the last dry match and struck it against the stone—not for light, but for the ritual. She watched the flame stand up straight in the harbor breeze, and she cupped it until it warmed her ruined fingers, and then she shook it out and saved the box, because a cartographer wastes nothing, least of all fire.

"You'd have liked this part," she told Levin, looking east, where the water met the brightening sky. "The ugly walk. The part that doesn't go in the book."

Somewhere beyond the fish market, between her and the gangway, stood the harbor gate. And the harbor gate, as of yesterday, was locked down.

She tightened the strap across her chest, turned the compass one last time—though she no longer needed it; the sea was north enough—and started walking.

Two hours. One gate. One uniform she was betting everything on, praying she'd find standing on the right side of it.

The tide would not wait. Neither would she.
