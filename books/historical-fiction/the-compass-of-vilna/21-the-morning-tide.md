## 21. The Morning Tide

The harbor gate was a wall of gray uniforms and bayonets, and behind it, the sea.

Casper saw it first as a strip of light between two customs sheds—a cold silver line that meant everything. The *Vilnius Star* sat at her moorings with steam already climbing from her stacks in slow white columns, and every column of it said *soon*. Two hours. Perhaps less. The tide did not negotiate.

She stood at the edge of the crowd by the fish market's eastern archway, the map tube slung across her back beneath a courier's oilskin coat, and counted what stood between her and that silver line: a double barricade of timber and barbed wire; a document table manned by three clerks under an awning; a squad of six soldiers with rifles sloped but hands near triggers; and at the center of it all, a gate officer she recognized from the river checkpoint yesterday—the one whose stare had followed them like a debt.

The gate was locked down. She had known it would be. Tomas had told her last night, his voice low against her hair in the candlelit studio: *"They'll close the harbor after the bakery. They'll be looking for a woman with a tube."* He had promised to come. He had not promised to succeed.

The morning smelled of brine and diesel and fear. Gulls screamed over the masts. A fishwife argued with a soldier about a confiscated crate, her voice cracking on the word *children*, and Casper made herself listen, made herself stay small and ordinary, a courier waiting for a manifest, nothing more. Her hands, still raw from the catacombs, throbbed inside her gloves. Under her ribs her heart kept its own time—faster than the tide, more patient than the sirens that had hunted her all night through the dark above the tunnels.

She had emerged from the underground an hour before dawn, bleeding, filthy, the atlas pressed against her chest like a second heartbeat, and she had thought, climbing the ladder into the fish market's gray light: *I have carried you this far. You will not die in a cellar.*

Now she watched the gate and waited for a uniform.

He came at half past six, walking from the direction of the barracks with the stride of a man who had decided something and would not un-decide it. Tomas Vaitkus in full dress—the collar high, the belt cinched, the insignia polished so that he caught the early light like a blade being drawn. He carried a leather folio in his left hand. His right hand hung loose at his side, and Casper, who had learned to read him the way she read coastlines, saw that the fingers were curled slightly inward, holding nothing, holding himself.

He did not look at her. That was the discipline of it, the terrible tenderness of it—he walked past the archway where she stood without a flicker, past the woman he had walked rooftops with, past the girl from the lecture hall, and presented himself at the document table with the folio and a voice pitched to carry.

"Vaitkus. Second District Command." He set the folio down. "Evidence transfer. Sealed material for the diplomatic pouch aboard the *Vilnius Star*. Authorization here." He tapped a paper. "Countersigned."

The gate officer—Kazlauskas, his name tag said, though Casper had never needed the name—did not reach for the paper. He looked at Tomas the way a man looks at weather he does not trust.

"Evidence transfer," Kazlauskas repeated. "At this hour. Through my gate. Which has been locked since four o'clock on orders from the commandant himself."

"The order supersedes the lockout. Read it."

"I intend to." Kazlauskas opened the folio slowly, savoring the obstruction. Behind him the clerks pretended to work and listened with their whole bodies. The fishwife had gone silent. Even the gulls seemed to circle lower.

Casper moved—not toward the gate, that would be death—but along the shed wall, closing distance by inches, positioning herself where she could hear and, when the moment came, walk. The tube shifted against her spine. She thought of Master Levin's hands pressing the unfinished atlas into hers while the smoke of burning maps rose around them both. *Silences keep people safe.* She had spent three years learning that silence. Today she would spend it all at once.

Kazlauskas read. Casper watched his eyes move down the page and stop, and stop again, and she understood that the forgery was good—Tomas had prepared it days ago, had spent its collateral at the river checkpoint yesterday and been stared at for his trouble—and that goodness might not be enough.

"This signature," Kazlauskas said. "Colonel Rimgaila signed this?"

"You may telephone him."

"At this hour I may wake him. And when he tells me he signed no such thing—"

"Then arrest me." Tomas said it flatly, without theater, and something in the plainness of it landed harder than any shout. "But open the gate first. The transfer is time-bound to the tide. If the pouch misses the ship, the delay is yours to explain. I'd think about which explanation survives longer."

For a moment no one breathed. Steam climbed. Water slapped the pilings. Somewhere beyond the breakwater the sea waited, indifferent and enormous, the only country left that asked no papers.

Then Kazlauskas smiled, thin as a razor's edge, and Casper felt the world tilt.

"No," he said. "I don't think I'll open anything. I think—" he lifted the folio, turned it in his gloved fingers, "—I'll search the material instead. Right here. Evidence transfers get verified. That's the regulation, Vaitkus. You'd know that if you'd ever actually done your job instead of letting fishwives and mapmakers slide through your checkpoints like gifts."

The words were a key turning in a lock Casper had feared for days. *He knows. Not everything—maybe not even much—but enough.*

Tomas did not move. But Casper saw the change in him, the way a coastline changes before a storm—not visibly, not yet, but in the pressure of the air. His hand came up and rested, almost gently, on the map tube's strap across his own shoulder—because he was wearing it now, had taken it from her in the shadow of the sheds thirty seconds before presenting himself, their only exchange a pressure of fingers and a look that had contained everything they had never said aloud.

"That material is sealed under authority," Tomas said quietly. "Break the seal and the authority breaks with it. Break it without cause and you'll answer for what's inside going unrecorded."

"What's inside, Vaitkus?" Kazlauskas stepped closer. "Let's find out together."

And then Casper understood what Tomas had known since the barracks, perhaps since the candle went out: there would be no clean passage. The forged orders had been a bridge, and the bridge was out. What remained was the truth, deployed like a weapon, at the cost of everything.

Tomas straightened. He looked at Kazlauskas, and then—deliberately, unmistakably—he looked past him, down the length of the quay, to the archway where a courier in an oilskin coat stood very still among the crates.

Their eyes met. Neither looked away.

"My name is Tomas Vaitkus," he said, and his voice carried across the water, across the years, across every checkpoint between duty and love. "Second District Command. And I am telling you that the material in this tube leaves on this ship, because I am a traitor to the state that issued this uniform. Search me if you want. Court-martial me. But the seal stays intact, and the ship sails on time, and somewhere in London, in a few months, people will read what I died carrying and know exactly which of us was on the right side of history."

The silence afterward was total. Even the sea seemed to hold its breath.

Then the world broke open.

"Seize him!" Kazlauskas's voice cracked like ice. "Both of them—the woman by the archway! *Seize them!*"

What happened next happened in pieces, each one sharp and separate as shards of glass.

Soldiers moving. Rifles coming off shoulders. The fishwife screaming and hurling her crate of herring so that silver fish skidded across the cobbles and men slipped and cursed. Casper running—not away, *toward*, because the tube was on Tomas's shoulder and the tube was the atlas and the atlas was everyone the master had loved and everyone she had promised to save—

But Tomas was faster. He tore the strap free and hurled the tube in a long flat arc over the heads of the scrambling soldiers, and Casper caught it against her chest the way you catch a child falling, the way you catch the last thing left of someone, and she was running again, through the gap the fishwife's riot had torn in the line, past a clerk who reached for her and got only oilskin, past the barricade's open leaf where a soldier hesitated—one heartbeat of hesitation, one human heart deciding—and then she was through.

The gangway rose before her, plank and rope and salt wind. Behind her: shouting, a whistle, boots. Ahead: the deck, the rail, the smoke, the sea.

A sailor grabbed her arm. "Papers!"

She thrust the transit pass at him with shaking hands. He glanced at it, glanced at the chaos boiling at the gate, glanced at her face—and shoved her up the gangway. "Go, go, *go*—"

*

From the stern rail, with the quay falling away beneath her foot by foot, Casper watched them take him.

They had not shot him. She would learn later why—orders about prisoners, about trials, about examples—but in that moment all she knew was that they had put his hands behind his back and he had let them, standing straighter than any man she had ever seen stand, while Kazlauskas circled him shouting questions he did not answer.

The gap of water widened. Ten meters. Twenty. The *Vilnius Star*'s engines turned over with a shudder Casper felt in her teeth, in her bones, in the tube clutched against her chest.

Tomas raised his head. Found her at the rail. And smiled—not the smile of a man defeated, not the grimace of a martyr, but the small crooked smile of the rooftop, of the fish market, of the lecture hall three years and a lifetime ago, when the world had still been a place they were promised rather than a place they were losing.

Neither of them looked away. Not when the soldiers wrenched his arms. Not when the quay slid backward and the gulls wheeled and cried. Not when the distance grew too great for anything but the shape of him, straight and alone on the gray stones, becoming smaller and becoming permanent, fixed in her memory the way she fixed coastlines: precisely, devotedly, forever.

*I will come back,* she thought, pouring the thought across the water as if thought could cross it. *Do you hear me? Whatever they do—I will come back, and I will find you.*

His lips moved. Too far now to read. But she knew. She had always known. It was the same word she was saying, soundless, into the wind.

The harbor opened. The breakwater fell astern. Vilna shrank to a smudge of roofs and steeples and smoke, a city drawn in fading ink, and Casper Galloway stood at the rail of a departing ship with the whole persecuted geography of that city sealed in a tube against her heart, and she wept without sound, the way the master had taught her, the way silences keep people safe.

*

Below decks, in a cabin she shared with three sleepless refugees, she opened the tube by lamplight and unrolled the atlas one final time.

Every route. Every safe house. Every false name and hidden door and blind alley the master had died completing and she had finished by candlelight with bleeding hands. The last chapter, inked at dawn two days ago, bore three signatures in a descending line: *Levin. Galloway. Vaitkus.* She touched the third name with one finger, lightly, the way you touch a wound to prove it still hurts.

A knock. A steward, middle-aged, tired-eyed, with the diplomatic pouch under his arm and a question in his face he was too careful to ask.

"The material for London," he said.

"Yes." She rolled the atlas, sleeved it, sealed it with wax and the official stamps Tomas had given her—stamps that would hang him, stamps that had bought these hours. "It goes in your pouch. It goes to London. And when it gets there—" her voice held, barely, "—it goes to print. Both names. Promise me it's both names."

The steward looked at her for a long moment, at the bloodied gloves, the salt-stiff coat, the eyes that had watched a man be taken and had not looked away.

"It'll be both names," he said.

She handed the atlas across the space between them, and the weight of it leaving her hands was the strangest feeling of her life—lightness and loss in the same breath, the master's purpose passing out of her keeping and into the world.

The pouch closed. The latch clicked. Somewhere beneath the waterline the engines drove her west, toward England, toward print, toward the hundreds of strangers she would never meet who would live because two people had loved each other more than they feared.

Casper went back up on deck. The sun was clearing the horizon now, laying a road of gold across the water, and she stood at the rail with the wind pulling her hair and looked east, back along that golden road, back toward the smudge on the horizon where a city held a man in a cell and a neighbor in a cellar and a thousand names in hiding.

London would publish the atlas. London would save the routes. And the routes led *here*—every one of them, every safe house and smuggler's path and hidden door, all of them pointed home like needles finding north.

She had inherited a purpose. She had spent a love to keep it. And when the ship passed the last buoy and turned its bow to the open sea, Astrid—no. *Casper.* Casper Galloway made herself a promise with the tide as her witness:

*This is not farewell. This is a map. And I am coming back to read it.*

Behind her, the pouch slept in the hold, carrying Vilna's secret geography toward the world. Ahead of her, the sea. And somewhere in a cell above the gray stones of the harbor, a man who had chosen her over his uniform sat in the dark—alive, she told herself. Alive, and waiting, and worth every mile she was about to travel back through fire to reach him.

The tide ran west. Her heart ran east.

And the compass in her chest—true north, home, *him*—never once wavered.
