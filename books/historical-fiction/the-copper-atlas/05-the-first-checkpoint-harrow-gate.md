## 5. The First Checkpoint: Harrow Gate

There is a particular quality to fear that arrives too late at night and stays for breakfast. It sits in the chest like a swallowed stone, and it does not care what you tell it. I had carried such stones before—across three coastlines, two prisons, and one marriage I don't discuss in polite company—and I can report with some authority that they never get lighter. You only get stronger shoulders.

Mine were about to be tested against Inspector Vane of Her Majesty's Customs at Harrow Gate.

* * *

We left the lighthouse an hour before dawn, while the gale was still deciding whether it meant business or merely wished to be taken seriously. Lena Blackwood walked ahead of me down the cliff path with a lantern she never once let swing, and I followed, cradling my broken wrist inside my coat like a stolen hen, watching her boots find the safe stones as though the path had been sewn for her personally.

The oilskin packet rode against my ribs, strapped there with a bandage Lena had wound herself—tight enough to hurt, which I took as a professional courtesy. To any searching eye it would read as a man's injured arm bound up by a woman who knew knots. Which was true. The letter inside was also true, though truth was precisely the cargo we could not declare.

"You're limping," she said, without turning around.

"I'm favoring my dignity."

"Your dignity drowned two nights ago. Walk properly or walk slower; either way, stop performing."

I have been shouted at by captains, threatened by revenue men, and once lectured on morality by a nun who was, I later learned, fencing stolen lace out of her convent. None of them had Lena's economy. She wasted nothing—not words, not movement, not grief. Two years of erasing yourself from the world teaches you a kind of thrift the rest of us spend lifetimes failing to learn.

"Harrow Gate opens at seven," she said. "Vane takes his tea at half past. He will not be pleasant before it."

"Is he pleasant after?"

"He was never pleasant. He was thorough. There's a difference, and you'll feel it."

* * *

Harrow Gate is not a gate. It is a grey stone customs house squatting at the neck of the coastal road where the cliffs pinch the land down to a cart's width, so that everything moving north or south along this stretch of coast must pass its door like a coin through a miser's fingers. A stable yard, a weighbridge, a lamp over the lintel that hadn't been trimmed since the Jubilee. Above the door, carved in letters the weather was patiently eating: *HONI SOIT QUI MAL Y PENSE*. Shame to him who thinks evil of it. On a customs house, that's not a motto. It's a confession.

Lena had dressed me in her late husband's spare coat—a good coat, navy wool, cut for a broader man than me but respectable in the way that matters to inspectors—and she had transformed herself in twenty minutes flat into something I found genuinely unsettling: a widow going about lawful business. Grey dress, black shawl, hair pinned with a severity that would make a schoolmistress weep. The woman who had pulled me from the surf and set my wrist with a fisherman's brutality had folded herself away somewhere, and in her place stood Mrs. Edmund Blackwood, deceased's wife, carrying a basket of mended sails to her sister in Millbrook, and would the inspector kindly not keep her standing in the cold.

It was the finest piece of smuggling I ever witnessed, and she wasn't even smuggling anything yet.

"The packet," she murmured as we joined the queue of carts and fishwives at the weighbridge. "If he asks to search you—"

"He won't ask."

"He'll ask."

"Then we give him something better to look at." I nodded toward the basket. "What's under the sails?"

"Mending thread and a tin of shortbread I baked at four this morning because lying is easier when your hands are tired." She said it without a flicker of humor, which somehow made it funnier. "You will stand slightly behind me and to the left. You will cough if you must speak. And Tomas—" she turned then, and her eyes did the thing they do, the thing where the sea looks back at you and takes notes, "—whatever he says to you, whatever he shows you, you are a sailmaker's man with a bad wrist and no opinions. Can you be nobody?"

"I've been nobody most of my life. I'm rather good at it."

"Not today," she said. "Today you're nobody *on purpose*. There's a difference, and Vane will feel it."

* * *

Inspector Vane received us in a room that smelled of ink, damp wool, and authority left too long in a barrel. He was younger than I'd imagined from Lena's careful silences—forty perhaps, tall without being graceful, with the kind of face that has decided early what it thinks of everyone and merely waits for confirmation. His uniform fit him the way conviction fits a zealot: perfectly, and to the discomfort of all present.

He was stamping parcels. That was the whole theater of it—a stack of sealed packages on his desk, and between each signature he would press the stamp down with a small, deliberate violence, as though the paper had personally offended the Crown. Thump. Sign. Thump. Sign. I have watched men count contraband with less ceremony.

"Mrs. Blackwood." He did not look up until the word was fully out, and then he looked, and I understood several things at once. I understood why she had warned me. I understood the phrase *once courted*, and how much history can be packed into a single syllable of someone's name. And I understood that this man had spent two years rehearsing this meeting in front of whatever mirror he kept his conscience in, and had still not settled on an expression.

"Inspector." Lena's voice was level as a spirit gauge. "You know my sister's been poorly. I'm taking her some mended canvas and—"

"I heard you'd come down in the world." He gestured at the basket. "Set it here."

She set it down. He unpacked it himself, item by item, with the unhurried hands of a man who wants you to watch. Mended sails. Thread. The tin of shortbread, which he opened, examined, and—God help me—actually ate one of, chewing slowly while looking at her.

"Lemon," he said. "Edmund liked lemon."

"He did," Lena said, and gave him nothing else. Not a tremor. Not a blink. I stood behind her left shoulder being furniture, and I will confess that my heart was conducting business at a rate I found undignified.

Vane turned to me at last, and his gaze went where gazes go on men like me: the hands first, then the eyes, then the coat. "Your man."

"Sailmaker. From Millbrook. He mends for me since—" she produced a small hesitation, expertly measured, "—since I couldn't manage the heavy work alone."

"What happened to his arm?"

"Gaff swung wild in Thursday's blow," I said, before she could answer. First rule of lying: take the boring questions yourself, and make them duller. "Rope's quicker than the doctor out our way."

Vane looked at me for a long moment. Then he said, pleasantly, "Hold out your hand."

I held out the right one—the good one. He shook it, which was not what he'd asked for, and we both knew it, and neither of us said so. His grip was dry and strong and stayed a half-second past courtesy.

"Millbrook," he said. "Which yard?"

"Prynn's. Though Prynn would tell you it's his yard the way a barn tells you it's the horse's."

Something moved behind his eyes—not amusement; Vane struck me as a man who filed amusement under luxuries—but close kin. He let go of my hand and returned to the basket, and here is where I learned the difference between a search and an inspection. An inspection looks for what's hidden. A search decides beforehand that it's there, and hunts accordingly. Vane ran his fingers along every seam of those sails, checking the hems, weighing each fold in his palm, and I stood there with a letter that could hang three officials strapped to my forearm, watching him work, and felt fear sharpen itself into something with edges.

Because he wasn't hunting randomly. He knew what shape he was looking for. Flat. Oilskin-wrapped. About so long.

And then I understood something worse: he wasn't searching the basket at all. The basket was the performance. He was watching *us*—watching Lena's breathing, watching my weight shift, watching for the small animal flinch that guilt makes in even the steadiest body. The parcel-stacking, the shortbread, the slow unpacking—all of it was bait for a reaction.

So I gave him one. I yawned.

A wide, honest, workingman's yawn, jaw-cracking, the yawn of a man who'd been up since four mending canvas and wanted his tea. Beside me, I felt Lena go very still—which was, I realized too late, exactly the wrong thing for her to do—and Vane's attention slid off me and onto her like a gull onto a dropped fish.

"You've not slept, Mrs. Blackwood."

"The storm kept me awake. It keeps everyone awake. That's rather its profession."

"Two years," he said, "and you still live out there alone." He said it gently, which was the most frightening thing he'd done all morning. "Most widows come into town by now. Take rooms. Company."

"I had company enough of the sea, Inspector. It writes often."

Thump went the stamp on some poor merchant's declaration, though nothing new lay before him—he stamped the desk itself, the way other men crack their knuckles. "There are letters," he said, "going missing along this coast. Naval correspondence. Official seals opened and resealed. You'd know if anything washed ashore near your light. Anything at all. Flotsam. Jetsam. Papers."

"The sea sends me rope and dead gulls," Lena said. "Its correspondence has declined."

He smiled. It reached his mouth and stopped at the border like a traveler without papers. Then he repacked her basket—sails, thread, shortbread—stamped a chit for the canvas duty, which he waived with a flourish I'm sure he believed was kindness, and said:

"Give my regards to the light."

"It hasn't got any," said Lena. "That's rather the point of it."

* * *

We walked out through the yard, past the weighbridge, past a boy soldier picking his teeth with his bayonet scabbard, and we did not speak until the road bent and Harrow Gate sank behind the hill like a stone into water. Even then Lena spoke first, quietly, facing forward.

"He ate the shortbread."

"He did."

"That was a message. Everything with him is a message." Her hands were white-knuckled on the basket handle. "He remembers Edmund liked lemon. He's telling me he remembers *everything*. Every visit, every conversation, every year he came to the lighthouse before—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "Before."

"Before he died," I supplied, uselessly.

"Before anyone says he died," she corrected, and there it was again—that precision of hers, that terrible bookkeeping. Then, after ten paces: "You yawned."

"I did."

"In front of Vane, you yawned."

"It's called misdirection. The conjuror drops the scarf so you don't watch the hand."

"The conjuror," said Lena, "does not usually drop the scarf while his accomplice stops breathing." But something in her voice had shifted a degree—warmer, or at least less armed. "It worked. He looked at me instead of your arm. Don't do it again."

"No promises. Fear makes me drowsy."

"Fear," she said, "should make you useful. There's a difference, and Vane will feel it." And then—this is the part I turn over still, when the nights get long—she almost smiled. A crack no wider than a coin's edge, gone as soon as it came, but I saw it. Two years of solitude, and the first thing that cracked her was not grief and not terror but the sheer absurdity of a smuggler yawning at the law.

We made Millbrook by noon, or the hamlet that passes for it—a chapel, a smokehouse, and Mrs. Quill's office leaning against the harbor wall like a drunk against a lamppost. Lena stopped at the top of the lane and looked down at it for a long moment, longer than a woman looks at a clerk's office.

"There's someone there," she said, "who owed Edmund a debt. The kind that doesn't get spoken of."

"And you mean to call it in."

"I mean to ask politely," she said. "The calling-in is hers to decide." She started down the lane, then paused. "Tomas. If she refuses—if the debt's gone stale—we'll need another way through Miller's Reach by Friday, and I don't have one. So whatever you think of blackmail, and however qualified you are to think it, today we are polite."

"Politeness," I said, "is just strategy wearing its Sunday clothes."

"Then wear yours," said Lena Blackwood, and pushed open the door of the harbormaster's office, where a small grey woman sat behind a desk piled with ledgers, and looked up at us with the expression of someone who had been expecting this visit for two years and had hoped, daily, to be wrong.
