## 7. Storm Signals

The storm had spent itself by morning, the way storms do on this coast — all that fury and thunder through the night, and then by dawn it simply loses interest in us and goes off to ruin someone else's day. I stood at the gallery rail with my tea going cold and counted what the sea had left us: three herring barrels with their staves sprung, half a fisherman's punt, enough rope to keep me mending for a week, and one dead gull wearing an expression of profound betrayal.

I had learned not to count men among the sea's leavings. Two years of practice will teach you anything.

The knock came at nine.

Nobody knocks at a lighthouse at nine on a Thursday. The supply boat comes Tuesdays; the vicar comes never, having decided some years ago that my soul was a lost cause not worth the climb; and the customs men come when they please, which is precisely the problem, because they had pleased to come now, while Tomas Ashby lay asleep in my lamp room with his wrist splinted in lath and linen and his boots drying by my stove like a pair of faithful dogs.

I set down the tea. I took one breath — the long kind, the kind you learn when you're holding a lamp steady against a gale and your hands want very much to shake — and I went down the stairs counting them as I always do, one hundred and six, because a mind occupied with numbers has no room for panic.

Through the iron door's viewing slit I saw him before I opened it. Inspector Ambrose Vane of His Majesty's Customs, standing on my shingle path in a coat too good for the weather and a hat that had never once been rained on, because rain knows better.

"Mrs. Blackwood," he said, when I let him in. "Routine inspection."

"Of course," I said. "Come in out of the routine."

* * *

Here is what you must understand about Ambrose Vane: he is not a stupid man, which would have been a mercy. A stupid man I could have managed with a dull face and duller answers. But Vane had courted me once, briefly and without conviction, in the year before Edmund came home from his survey posting and asked me to marry him inside a fortnight. Vane had taken the loss the way certain men take losses — quietly, and filed away, and kept warm like a coal in a pocket, waiting for a wind.

He also had eyes like a customs officer, which is to say they went over everything in the room and left receipts.

He did not sit. He walked my ground floor slowly, hands clasped behind him, pausing at the window to remark on the state of the sea wall, at the stove to note that I burned peat now instead of coal, at the shelf where Edmund's charts used to hang before I took them down and put up a calendar from a flour merchant instead.

"You've changed the place," he said.

"Widows do," I said. "We find we don't need so much."

"And yet the light burns every night. Faithful as ever." He turned and gave me the smile he probably practiced in front of a looking-glass, the one meant to say *we are old acquaintances, we may speak freely.* "They tell me yours is the steadiest light between here and Harrow Gate. No missed nights in two years. Remarkable, for a woman alone."

"I sleep badly," I said. "It seemed sensible to be awake anyway."

He laughed — a short, pleasant sound with nothing behind it, like a door knocked on in an empty house. "And how do you manage the oil? The supply runs are heavy work. The casks alone."

"The supply boat's crew carries them to the door. I carry them the rest of the way. Oil casks are heavier than grief, Inspector, but only just, and grief doesn't roll."

Another laugh. He was enjoying himself. That was the worst of it — he was genuinely enjoying himself, strolling through my widowhood like a man through a garden he'd once been denied entry to, touching things, taking the measure of what had grown there.

"May I see the lamp room?"

My heart performed a small, cold maneuver, the sort of thing a fish does when the hook goes in. Somewhere above us — forty feet up, one hundred and six stairs, behind a door I'd locked with the key in my pocket — a smuggler with a broken wrist was sleeping off half the North Sea.

"Naturally," I said. "Though I warn you the stairs are steep. If you die on my staircase I'll have paperwork."

* * *

We climbed. I went first, which was either courtesy or strategy, and I have decided since that it was both. Behind me I heard Vane's breathing change on the fourth flight — the man was fit but he was a desk man at heart, and desks forgive everything except the truth.

At the twentieth landing I said, "You didn't come about the light."

"No?" Footsteps, even and patient.

"Customs inspections are conducted at the harbor. The stores. The boats. You've never once climbed my stairs in two years of 'routine' visits, because there is nothing routine about a lighthouse and you know it. So either you've developed a sudden passion for Fresnel lenses, or you came to ask me something and wanted to do it standing where I couldn't walk away."

Silence, then, for three steps. When he spoke again his voice had shed its pleasantness the way a snake sheds skin — same shape underneath, newer and more honest.

"They pulled a boat out of the surf at Miller's Reach yesterday morning," he said. "Smuggler's craft. Half-swamped. And no body aboard, though the tide ran toward your beach, and the tide always tells the truth even when men won't."

"Does it," I said. My voice came out level. I have never been prouder of any achievement, including the light itself.

"A man was seen on the cliff road Tuesday night. Big fellow, limping. Headed north." A pause calibrated to the inch. "Toward the last lit structure on the coast."

"Then he'll have seen a fine view of it," I said, "and carried on to Scotland, where sensible criminals go."

We reached the lamp room. I unlocked the door with the key from my pocket, and I confess my hand hesitated on it for less than a heartbeat — but Vane was watching my back, not the key, and a heartbeat is a small coin to spend.

The room was clean. It was clean because I had spent the hour after dawn making it clean: Tomas's boots gone from the stove, his coat folded into the oil locker below, the blanket he'd slept under hung out the vent window to air and looking, if anyone asked, like a curtain I was fond of. The man himself was not in the lamp room. He was in the oil cellar beneath it, behind a hatch under the storage bench, in a space four feet high that smelled of paraffin and rust, and I had put him there twenty minutes before the knock with a whispered *not a sound, not a breath, not if the house falls down* — and God help me, he'd looked at me with those bruised, amused eyes and mouthed back, *yes, ma'am,* like a schoolboy, and I'd had no time to decide whether I wanted to strike him or laugh.

Vane circled the lamp. He admired the lens. He remarked on the clockwork rotation, the brasswork, the state of the wicks. He opened the storage bench — my stomach dropped through the floor and kept going — and examined the spare mantles and the logbook, running one finger down the columns of my handwriting.

"Your husband kept this log before you," he said. Not a question.

"He did."

"His hand was very fine. Schoolmaster's hand." He closed the book gently, almost tenderly. "I remember it from the shipping manifests. Edmund Blackwood signed for cargo twice a season, coming through Harrow Gate. Neatest signatures on the whole coast. A man could forge that hand, of course. With practice. Most hands can be forged with practice."

"Most things can," I said, "with practice. Marriage. Grief. Conversation."

He looked at me then — really looked, the pleasantness entirely gone, and what remained was not cruelty exactly. It was appetite. The look of a man who has found a locked box and believes, with growing certainty, that it contains something valuable.

"Lena," he said. We were not on Christian-terms and he knew it; using the name was a move, like laying down a card. "Two years ago I stamped the report on the *Cormorant*. Lost with all hands. I read every page of it. And I have spent those two years reading other pages — manifests, ledgers, letters of credit — and I will tell you what I told no one: that wreck was not an accident, and the report I stamped was written before the ship ever sailed. Someone in London needed a drowned crew, and got one, and someone along this coast helped arrange it." He stepped closer. The lamp threw our shadows huge against the glass. "If Edmund Blackwood were alive — if he'd seen what was in those holds — he'd be a dead man walking, and whoever sheltered him would be standing in a very deep hole. So I am asking you, as the man who once —"

"Don't," I said.

"— as an officer of the customs," he finished smoothly, "whether you have seen or heard anything that bears on the matter."

The wind worried at the glass. Below us the sea worked at the rocks with its patient criminal hands. And somewhere under the bench, four feet of dark and paraffin-smelling air held a living man who was, at that moment, obeying me absolutely for the first time since I'd known him.

"No, Inspector," I said. "I have seen nothing. I hear the sea, and the wind, and on Sundays the bell buoy. Nothing in any of it bears on London's business." I met his eyes and gave him the whole weight of what I had — two years of it, the loneliness and the ledger lines and the name scratched out of every record that would hold still for a pen. "My husband is at the bottom of the channel. I signed the paper myself. If you've brought me hope, take it back down those stairs, because I haven't the strength to carry it and I won't thank you for leaving it here."

Something moved behind his face. I could not read it, and that frightened me more than anything else he'd done, because I had always been able to read Vane the way one reads weather.

"That," he said quietly, "was very well done."

"Inspector —"

"It was." He put on his hat. "Almost good enough to make me sorry I came." At the stair door he paused, one hand on the iron frame, and did not turn around. "There's a packet missing from the *Cormorant*'s recovered effects, Mrs. Blackwood. An oilskin wallet, listed in the salvage inventory, absent from the strongbox at Harrow Gate. Small thing. Papers, most likely. Worthless, most likely." Now he turned, and smiled, and it was the smile of a man setting a trap he intended to watch for weeks. "But inventories are like consciences. One notices what's missing from them. Good day."

His footsteps went down and down, one hundred and six, unhurried, each one landing in my chest.

* * *

I waited until the front door closed. I waited until his boots crossed the shingle. I waited until the path was empty and the headland road had given him up, and then some minutes more, because terror is mostly patience.

Then I lifted the hatch.

Tomas Ashby lay curled in the dark with his splinted wrist cradled against his chest and sweat shining on his face, and when the light hit him he blinked up at me and said, hoarse as a crow:

"I heard every word. For the record, ma'am — you're a terrifying liar, and I mean that as the highest compliment I know." He worked himself upright, wincing. "He knows about the packet."

"He suspects. There's a difference, and it's the width of a rope bridge." I sat back on my heels and made myself look at him straight. "He thinks my husband may be alive. He thinks the wreck was murder dressed as mischance. And he thinks the proof of it passed through this house." I heard my own voice go flat and strange. "So it's time you showed me what's in that oilskin, Mr. Ashby, and you'll show me now, because I have just lied to a customs inspector with a man's life folded under my storage bench, and I will not do it again blind."

Tomas looked at me for a long moment. Then he reached into his shirt — slow, careful, the way a man moves when he's decided the person holding the lantern deserves honesty whether she wants it or not — and drew out the packet, salt-stiff and swollen, tied with cord gone green from the sea.

"There's a letter in here," he said. "Written in a hand I'd know anywhere, because I've signed receipts against it for six years. Neatest hand on the coast, they used to say." He held it out between us, and his eyes, which had been amused since the moment I dragged him out of the surf, were not amused at all.

"Edmund Blackwood's," he said. "Whoever you are, ma'am — I think you'd best sit down."
