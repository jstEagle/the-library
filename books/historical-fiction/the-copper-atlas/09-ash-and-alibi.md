## 9. Ash and Alibi

The smell reached the lighthouse before the news did.

I was trimming the wicks at first light—Edmund's old habit, which I had adopted the way one adopts a limp, out of necessity and spite—when the wind swung round from the southeast and carried with it a charred sweetness, like toast left too long in a house where nobody is hungry. I stood at the gallery rail and looked down the coast toward Harrow Gate, and there it was: a smudge of grey standing up against the morning, too thick for sea fog, too stubborn to be anything but smoke.

The coastal records office sat between the customs house and the chandlery, a squat stone building that held every birth, death, marriage, wreck, and debt registered on this stretch of shore for the last hundred years. I knew its contents intimately, because I had spent two years making certain of my absence from them. No census entry. No tax roll. The lighthouse itself was still logged under Edmund's name, dead men being wonderfully convenient tenants—they ask no questions and pay no rates.

I watched the smoke for a long time, doing arithmetic I did not like.

* * *

Tomas was awake when I came down, sitting at my table with his splinted wrist cradled against his chest like something he'd stolen and didn't want to admit to. He'd slept in the oil cellar again, on the cot I kept for deliveries, and he had the particular look of a man who has been awake since before dawn listening to a house decide whether it trusts him.

"You smell it," I said. Not a question.

"Smoke." He nodded toward the window. "Southeast wind. That'll be Harrow Gate."

"The records office."

He went very still, the way men do when they realize the ground beneath them has opinions. "You're sure?"

"I've been watching that coastline for six years, Mr. Ashby. I know what fog looks like, what gannets look like, and what a government building looks like when someone has fed it to itself." I set the kettle on the stove because my hands wanted occupation. "Someone burned every scrap of paper between here and the county line."

"Or someone burned a *selection*," Tomas said quietly. "Offices like that don't burn clean. They burn convenient."

I poured the water and let myself think the thought I'd been refusing since the gallery rail. If you wanted to destroy a single document—a wreck report, say, or a falsified cargo manifest—you would not burn an entire records office. That was like burning the church to erase one sin. Unless, of course, you wanted everyone to believe the document was gone. Unless you wanted the people who *held* copies to relax their grip.

Or unless you were Inspector Vane, and what you actually wanted was an excuse to knock on doors.

"He knows," I said.

"You can't know that."

"I can know that. It's the one thing I'm qualified to know." I sat down across from him. Up close, in daylight, Tomas Ashby was younger than his voice suggested—thirty perhaps, with the kind of face that had been handsome once and had then been used hard by weather and bad decisions. His broken wrist gave him the air of a man perpetually about to apologize. "A man doesn't burn a records office unless he believes something survives outside it. And the only thing that has survived outside it, in two years, washed up on my beach four days ago in your coat."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he said, with the flat honesty I was beginning to suspect was his only real currency: "I never told you whose letter it was. You knew before I said it."

"I knew the handwriting."

"That's not a thing most wives forget."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

We spent the morning doing what frightened people do best: pretending to be ordinary. I lit the lamp early against the low cloud, checked the mechanism, wound the clockwork. Tomas swept the gallery steps, badly, holding the broom in his left hand like a man fencing with it. To any passing boat we were exactly what we appeared to be—a keeper and her odd assistant, going about the dull machinery of a dull life.

It was Mrs. Quill who brought the truth up the cliff path just after noon, walking fast in a way she almost certainly called *brisk* and her doctor would have called *suicidal*. She arrived at my door breathing like a kettle and carrying a basket of mackerel as cover, which fooled no one, least of all the gulls trailing her hopefully.

"They're saying a lamp exploded," she announced, before she was properly through the door. "Oil store. Spontaneous, they're saying. In a stone building. With no lamp in the records room."

"How thorough of the fire," I said.

"There were men asking questions before the ashes were cold. Vane's men. They had a list." She set the basket down with more force than fish deserve. "Names, Lena. Every keeper, every clerk, every widow within twenty miles who signed anything in the last three years. And your name was on it. I saw it. Third from the top."

The room got very quiet. Outside, the wind worried at the eaves, patient as a debt collector.

"Third," I repeated.

"After the harbormaster and the chaplain. Before the coroner." She wrung her hands inside her apron. "They're working downward. The harbormaster's already given his statement—said he remembers nothing, which for Mr. Prosser is nearly true. But they'll reach you by tomorrow. Maybe tonight if the weather holds fair enough for rowing."

Tomas had come up from the cellar at the sound of voices and now stood at the foot of the stairs, watching us both with the expression of a man reading a chart in a language he half knows. "What list?" he asked. "Who keeps a list of signatories?"

"A falsified wreck report needs witnesses," I said. "It needs a chaplain to bury a coffin, a harbormaster to log debris, a coroner to sign a finding, and—" my voice did something I hadn't authorized, a small crack, quickly mortared over, "—a widow to identify effects and accept the finding. Four signatures. Four names. Three of whom are now being interviewed by the man who wrote the report."

"And the fourth," Tomas said slowly, "is standing in this kitchen."

Mrs. Quill looked between us, and I watched her arrive at the shape of it—the packet, the letter, the reason a smuggler with a ruined wrist had been living in my oil cellar for four days—and I watched her decide, visibly, deliberately, not to ask. That is a kind of loyalty too. Perhaps the only kind that lasts.

"Then you need to move it," she said simply. "Tonight. Whatever it is. Because Vane won't come with a warrant, Lena. Warrants leave records, and records are what he's currently burning. He'll come with a boat and two quiet men, and by morning you'll have drowned tragically, and no one will ever check, because the office that would have checked is ash."

* * *

After she left—I sent her home with half the mackerel, because even conspiracies run on courtesy—I stood at the window a long while, watching the grey smudge over Harrow Gate thin into the afternoon.

Two years ago, I had made myself into a ghost with great care. I had let Edmund stay listed as the keeper of record. I had paid no taxes, answered no censuses, signed nothing, joined nothing. When the vicar pressed me to join the Ladies' Benevolent Society, I pleaded my nerves. When the tax assessor came, I told him the lighthouse fell under Trinity House jurisdiction and watched him decide not to climb the hill to verify it. I had built my invisibility plank by plank, and I had told myself it was prudence. Wisdom, even. A woman alone on a coast full of dangerous men does well to be nobody.

But standing there, watching the smoke of everything I'd hidden behind, I understood the truth with the clarity of cold water: I hadn't been hiding. I had been buried. There is a difference, and the difference is that the buried cannot choose when to dig themselves out.

"You're smiling," Tomas said from the table. "It's unsettling, given the circumstances."

"I'm deciding something."

"Should I be worried?"

"Probably." I turned around. "Here is where we stand, Mr. Ashby. Vane knows the packet exists—that's why the fire, that's why the list. He can't search openly without explaining *why*, and explaining why means admitting the wreck report was false, which is the one sentence in the world he cannot say aloud. So he'll come quietly. And when he comes, he will find either me or the packet, and if he finds me without the packet, he will find me anyway, because I am the last signature on his list and dead widows make excellent endings."

"So we run."

"We deliver. Running is what guilty people do, and I have spent two years being innocent in every way except the one that matters." I crossed to the table and laid my hand flat on the wood between us. "Your letter says the atlas proves what they did. Fine. Then the atlas goes to London, to whoever reads mail ships' postbags with honest eyes, and it goes soon, and it goes through checkpoints that are about to grow teeth. Which means you and I stop being hostess and castaway and become what we should have been from the start."

"Which is?"

"Smugglers," I said. "Both of us. Proper ones. You have the trade routes and the contacts; I have a coast I could navigate blindfolded and a talent for being underestimated. We have perhaps two days before Vane works down his list to my name. Between here and the London mail there are three places a parcel can be stopped—Harrow Gate, Miller's Reach, and the quay at Greywater. Three doors, and Vane owns keys to all of them."

Tomas looked at my hand on the table, then up at my face, and something in his expression shifted—the wariness settling into something steadier, the look of a man who has finally located dry ground. "You understand what you're saying. Once we move it, there's no ghosting back. Your name goes on things. Real things, in real ledgers, in ink."

"I've been a ghost for two years, Mr. Ashby. Do you know what I've learned?" I picked up the kettle and refilled our cups, because even declarations go better with tea. "Ghosts don't drown. Ghosts just fade. And I find, at my age, that I'd rather be hanged for who I am than evaporate for who I pretended to be."

"That's a terrible plan for staying alive."

"It's an excellent plan for mattering. We'll take alive as it comes."

He laughed then—short, surprised, rusty, as though the laugh had been stored somewhere damp—and winced and held his wrist, and laughed again anyway. "All right, Mrs. Blackwood. Smugglers, then. Where do we start?"

* * *

We started, as all good crimes do, with a funeral.

Not a real one—though God knew the coast had supplied enough raw material lately—but the idea of one. It was Tomas who saw it, pacing the lamp room that evening with his good hand gesturing like a conductor, laying out the geography between us and London. "Three checkpoints," he said. "Customs, toll bridge, quay. Every one of them searches cargo. Crates, barrels, mail sacks, luggage. Everything gets opened."

"Except?"

"Except the one thing no man with a soul will open, and no man without one will dare to." He stopped pacing. "A coffin going to burial. Sealed, witnessed, blessed. You'd need a corpse, a grieving family, and paperwork."

"Paperwork," I said, "is currently a buyer's market."

He stared at me. Then he grinned, and it transformed his whole battered face. "Mrs. Blackwood, I begin to see why your husband married you."

"My husband married me because I could gut a mackerel faster than his mother. The rest was luck." I looked out the lantern glass at the darkening water, at the beam sweeping its slow white arm across the swell, marking the rocks for strangers the way it always had, the way it would tomorrow and the day after, regardless of what men burned or forged or buried. Somewhere out past that light lay the channel, and past the channel the world, full of offices and archives and men with lists. Somewhere in it, presumably, was whatever remained of Edmund—not the handwriting, not the memory, but the fact of him, the grave or the hulk or the truth I had signed away.

I had spent two years believing the lie was the safest thing I owned. Now the lie was burning in a public square and taking my name up with the smoke, and the only thing left worth saving was the truth—which meant the only thing left worth saving was everything.

"Tomas," I said. "How soon can you get word to whoever owes you favors at Miller's Reach?"

His grin faded into something more businesslike, which on him amounted to the same expression. "By tomorrow night, if the wind holds. Why?"

"Because we're going to need a coffin," I said, "and a body to put in it, and a family to mourn it—and I think I know where we can find all three."

Below us, far down the dark coast, the smudge over Harrow Gate still glowed faintly against the stars, like a wound that hadn't decided whether to close. By tomorrow, Vane's list would reach my name. By tomorrow night, I intended for there to be nothing left at the lighthouse to find.

The lamp turned overhead, steady as a heartbeat. I trimmed the wick, and began to plan a funeral.
