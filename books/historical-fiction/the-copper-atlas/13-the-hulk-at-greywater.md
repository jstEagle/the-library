## 13. The Hulk at Greywater

The boat was called the *Patience*, which struck me as either a joke or a prayer, depending on how the wind behaved.

She belonged to Mrs. Quill, or rather to her late husband, who had won her in a card game from a man who then drowned under circumstances nobody investigated too closely. The Quills had a talent for acquiring things that came with questions attached. I had known the *Patience* since I was a girl, back when this coast had still been mine by right of birth instead of by erasure, and she handled like a woman with strong opinions and no interest in hearing yours.

"She leaks," Tomas said, hauling on the jib sheet with his splinted wrist held carefully against his chest like something he'd been issued and couldn't return.

"Everything on this coast leaks. The lighthouse leaks. My boots leak. Grief leaks." I took the tiller. "You get so you don't notice."

"I notice."

"You would. You're new."

Greywater lay eleven miles down the coast, past the headland where the cliffs gave up pretending to be solid and started shedding themselves into the sea in great slabs that could be heard for miles on a quiet night. It wasn't a port so much as an apology for one—a deep mud channel, a hulk yard, and a chapel whose congregation had dwindled to the minister, his dog, and whatever the tide brought in. Nobody went to Greywater who didn't have to. Which was, of course, precisely why the Navy kept its prison hulks there.

The sky had been building all morning, clouds stacking themselves like crockery before a fall. By noon the wind had teeth in it. By one o'clock it was worrying at the sail the way a dog worries a bone it means to keep.

Tomas stood at the bow with his good hand shading his eyes, and when he turned back to me his face had gone careful in the way faces do when they're carrying news they'd rather drop overboard.

"There," he said, and pointed.

I saw it before he finished pointing. The hulk.

They moor them out in the channel, the old ships, stripped of masts and dignity, their hulls furred with weed to the waterline, windows barred, smoke rising from a stovepipe like the ship itself had been reduced to cooking its own ghost. This one had been the *HMS Resolute* once—I knew the name from Edmund's letters, from the way he said it, half affection and half contempt, the way you speak of a beautiful animal that would eat you given the chance. Now she sat in the grey water with her ribs showing through rot, and men lived inside her. Men the Crown preferred not to count.

"Edmund's there," I said. "Or was."

"Lena." Tomas came aft, ducking under the boom, and put his good hand over mine on the tiller—not steering, just holding, the way you'd steady someone on ice. "Whatever we find—"

"Don't." I said it quietly, because the wind was loud enough that quiet was all I had left. "Don't do the thing where you prepare me. I've had two years of preparing myself. It doesn't work. It's like bailing with a fork."

He almost smiled. That was the thing about Tomas Ashby—he carried catastrophe around with him like luggage and still found room for a smile. "A fork," he said. "What's wrong with a bucket?"

"A bucket implies hope."

* * *

We anchored in the lee of a derelict coal barge and rowed the last stretch, because no boat that looked purposeful got within a quarter mile of a prison hulk without being boarded and questioned, and I had spent two years perfecting the art of looking like nothing at all. A widow in a borrowed boat, come to beg medicine for a sick sister from the hulk surgeon—that was my story, and it had the advantage of being nearly true, if you counted Tomas as the sister and grief as the sickness.

The guard who met us at the accommodation ladder was young enough that his uniform fit him like a promise he hadn't yet learned to break. He looked at my papers—Mrs. Quill had provided papers, because Mrs. Quill could provide anything short of absolution—and at my face, and something flickered behind his eyes.

"You're Blackwood," he said. "Lena Blackwood."

The world did a small, slow revolution around me. I kept my hands folded and my voice level. "I'm afraid you have me confused with—"

"He talked about you." The boy-guard lowered his voice, glancing at his mate up on the deck above. "Blackwood. The officer. He talked about you every day. Said his wife lit the finest light on the coast and one day he'd see it home again." His throat worked. "I'm sorry, ma'am. I'm sorry to be the one."

I heard the words arrive one at a time, each with its own little detonation. *Was.* *Said.* Past tense laid out like a row of coffins.

"When?" I asked. My voice sounded like someone else's, someone sensible, someone standing slightly outside herself taking notes.

"February gone. Fever. Half the lower deck had it." The boy's eyes were wet and furious about it. "They buried him ashore. There's a plot behind the chapel. I can show you where, if—" He stopped, because I had reached out and gripped the rail, and perhaps my knuckles were white, or perhaps it was simply that some things cannot be performed away.

"Show me," I said.

* * *

The grave was small and mean, a mound of grey clay with a wooden marker, the kind of marker that rots before the mourning does. Someone had carved into it with a knife, badly, letters going off at angles like drunks leaving a tavern:

E. BLACKWOOD — OFFICER — HE KEPT HIS WATCH

I read it four times. The wind read it over my shoulder. Somewhere behind us Tomas stood very still, hat in hand, and I was aware of him the way you're aware of weather—present, enormous, unaddressed.

"He carved it himself," the boy said. "Before the end. Asked me to see it done proper when he was gone. I didn't know what 'proper' meant for a man like that, so I just... left it as he made it."

"Did he say anything else?" I heard my own voice from a great distance. "At the end?"

The boy hesitated. Then: "There's a man in the infirmary. Corrigan. He nursed your husband through the fever—caught it himself doing it, and it's in his lungs now, eating him slow. He's been asking after anyone from the coast. Anyone at all." He glanced up at the hulk, at the barred light in its portholes. "He says he has something to give you. Says he's been keeping it alive two years waiting for someone to ask."

* * *

The infirmary of a prison hulk is not a place I will describe fully, because some rooms deserve the mercy of summary. Suffice it to say it smelled of carbolic losing its argument against everything else, and that the man in the corner cot had been handsome once, in the way of portraits that have been left too near a fire.

Corrigan. Forty, maybe, or sixty—the fever and the damp had conspired to erase the difference. His chest moved in shallow, whistling pulls, and when he saw me his eyes went bright with something that might have been relief or might have been the last flare of a lamp running dry.

"You're her," he rasped. "The wife. He said you'd have sea-grey eyes. Said you'd come in a boat called Patience, because he told everyone aboard about that boat, told us she was the only honest vessel on the coast." A sound that was meant to be a laugh. "Madman. Talking about a dinghy while the fever cooked him."

I knelt by the cot. The floor was wet. Everything was wet. "You knew him."

"Two years, off and on. Cell across from mine, then hammock beside mine when they moved us." His hand came out from under the blanket—yellowed nails, a tremor—and pressed something into mine. A key. Small, brass, worn smooth. "His effects. They let me hold them, thinking I'd die before I could use them. Joke's on the Crown."

"What does it open?"

"The chapel vestry. Greywater chapel. He hid papers there—his own hand, sealed in oilcloth, same as the packet Ashby carries. He made two, see. One to go out with a smuggler, one to stay hidden, in case the first never sailed." Corrigan coughed, a long terrible business, and I waited through it with my hand on his shoulder feeling the architecture of him coming apart. "But listen. Listen, because I haven't got the breath to say it twice."

"I'm listening."

"The wreck report. The one they made you sign." His eyes fixed on mine, fierce as a gull's. "It wasn't just falsified. It was *authored*. Not by some clerk covering his ledgers. Vane wrote it. Customs Inspector Vane—he came aboard the hulk twice, in plain clothes, to check that Blackwood's letter hadn't been copied. And the second time, your husband saw the signature page of the original report tucked in his folio. Saw his own death warrant with Vane's name at the bottom of it." Another cough tore through him. "Vane didn't just cover the scandal, missus. He built it. The contraband, the naval flags, the crew burned in the wreck—they were witnesses, all of them, and Vane signed the order that made them cargo. Your husband survived because he was below decks when they scuttled her, and Vane has spent two years making sure the one loose thread stayed on this hulk."

The world went very quiet, the way it does inside a wave.

"And Edmund?" I asked, though I already knew, had known since the boy at the ladder, had perhaps known since the shingle beach two weeks ago when a dying man washed up carrying a dead man's handwriting. "Did Vane—"

"No." Corrigan's voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "Fever took him honest. God's own mercy, though I doubt it felt like mercy at the time. He went easy, for a hulk. Talking about you. About the light." His grip tightened on my wrist with the last of his strength. "He made me swear. Tell her the truth found me, and tell her—" The breath caught. Held. Failed.

Tomas stepped forward and finished the sentence Corrigan couldn't, softly, from memory, from the oilskin packet that had started all of it:

"*Tell her the light was always hers.*"

Corrigan smiled. Then he closed his eyes, and whether he slept or slipped further down the long dark slope toward February, none of us in that room could say. The boy-guard appeared at my elbow with a lantern and said nothing, because there was nothing left to say that the room hadn't already said.

* * *

We rowed back to the *Patience* in a silence broken only by the oars and the wind, which had risen from worrying to outright theft—it took our words as fast as we could form them and scattered them over the grey water.

I let Tomas get the anchor up before I spoke.

"Vane," I said. "He signed it. He authored it. Two years I've hated a shapeless thing, a system, a *they*—and it turns out it had a name and a face and it drank tea in my kitchen last week and asked after my health."

Tomas coiled the line with his one working hand, unhurried, giving me the courtesy of not looking at me directly. "And now?"

"Now it's not grief anymore." I took the tiller. The rain arrived then, sudden and sideways, and I welcomed it, because it gave me an excuse for the state of my face. "Grief is a passenger. This is ballast. It goes where I point the boat."

"We still have to get the atlas to the *Meridian*," Tomas said. "Morning tide. And Vane will be watching the quay like a hawk with a grudge."

"Yes."

"And now he knows you know. If Corrigan talks—if that boy talks—"

"Then we've lost the luxury of caution, and gained the advantage of a man who thinks he's hunting a widow and doesn't know he's hunting a widow with ballast." I brought her head round toward the open water, toward home, toward the lamp I would light tonight for a husband who was beyond needing it and a coast that needed it more than ever. "Hold on, Mr. Ashby. The tide waits for no one—but neither, as of tonight, do I."

Behind us Greywater sank into the rain, its hulk, its chapel, its small mean grave. Ahead the storm opened like a door.

I sailed through it.
