## 4. The Bookbinder's Hands

The child slept through the first hour of the night, which Sable took as a small mercy and a large problem.

Mercy, because a crying infant in Copper Harbor after curfew was a death sentence delivered by its own lungs. Problem, because he could not stop looking at her. He had laid her in the shallow wooden trough where he soaked his binding leathers—lined now with a folded apron, the closest thing to a cradle his shop owned—and every time he turned from the bench he found his eyes going back to her face, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

*Forget the face.* The note had said that. He had burned it within a minute of reading it, feeding it to the glue-pot flame, watching the ink curl and go gray and then nothing. But burning a note did not burn an instruction out of the mind. It sat there instead, the way a splinter sits under skin, working deeper with every movement.

He knew Kellin Valley weave when he saw it. Everyone from the eastern provinces did—the indigo warp shot through with rust-red thread, a pattern like water running over stones, woven on the looms around Kellin town since before the Republic had a name. His grandmother had worn it. Half the dead of the valley had been wrapped in it. And someone had wrapped this child in it, carried her across a city strangled by checkpoints, and set her down on the step of a man whose trade was making things disappear into other things.

Someone wanted the cloth preserved. Or wanted it found and destroyed, and Sable was simply the nearest fire. He could not tell which, and the not-knowing was worse than either.

He made himself turn back to the bench.

The documents lay in three stacks, sorted by weight of consequence: the diplomatic correspondence in cipher, thin and brittle; the garrison manifests, dense with columns; and beneath everything, wrapped in oilcloth inside a false bottom of his ledger drawer, the Kellin Valley record itself—forty-one pages in a clerk's careful hand, numbers and names and dates, the last true account of what the Republic's army had done in the valley in the autumn before the collapse. Maren's voice was still in his ear from two nights ago, calm as a woman discussing weather: *Velmark, before the armistice signs it away. If you can't carry it, destroy it. Never let it be taken.*

He had spent eleven years being unremarkable. A bookbinder with ink-stained fingers and a limp he exaggerated on icy days, who charged fair prices and complained about paper costs and had no opinions anyone had ever thought worth reporting. Invisibility was not a gift. It was a discipline, practiced daily, the way a musician practices scales. You did not look at soldiers. You did not linger at windows. You did not remember aloud.

And now there was a child breathing in his leather trough, and forty-one pages that weighed more than any book he had ever bound.

He began to work.

* * *

The shop smelled of hide glue and beeswax and the faint sourness of old leather, and Sable had always found the smell comforting, but tonight it reminded him of another room, another lifetime, and he let it, because there was no stopping it anyway.

There had been a house once, outside Vessany City, in the years of the first purges—before the Republic was collapsing, when it was merely rotting, which was somehow worse because no one would say so. He had been twenty-six. He had had a wife named Liora who sang off-key while she cooked, and a daughter, Iselle, who had been two years old and afraid of geese. He had been a junior archivist then, a man of files and indexes, and he had believed, with the serene stupidity of the young, that accuracy was protection. That if your papers were in order, you were in order. That the state, whatever else it was, respected its own records.

They came at four in the morning. He remembered the light afterward—not the men, not even their faces, though he had looked at them, he had tried to fix them—but the light: the blue-gray of very early winter, the color of steel about to be tested. Liora had time to press Iselle into his arms and say one word, which was *run*, and he had run, out the kitchen door and over the frozen garden and into the drainage culvert beyond the wall, holding his daughter so tightly against his chest that afterward, when he finally stopped in the reeds of the river marsh, she was not breathing. Whether it was the cold or the pressure or something before that night—a sickness coming on, a small heart simply tired—he never learned. There had been no inquiry. There were no records. The state did not respect its own records; it wrote them, and what it wrote was that the family of Sable Montrose, archivist, third class, had been relocated.

Relocated. He had learned to love that word the way you learn to love a scar: not because it is beautiful but because it is yours.

He had walked south for a season. He had learned to bind books from a deaf old master in a river town who asked no questions because he could not hear them, and Sable had understood, somewhere in those months, that forgetting was not a failure of love. It was a skill. It could be practiced like scales. You put the memory in a cover and you sewed it shut and you shelved it spine-inward, and you did not take it down, and if your hands shook when they passed certain shelves, well—hands shook. Paper dust got into everyone's lungs.

For eleven years it had worked. He had almost believed, some nights, that it was true—that the man called Sable Montrose had died in the marshes with his daughter, and the bookbinder of Copper Harbor had been born from the reeds, weightless, unattached, free.

Then tonight a child in Kellin weave had been left on his step, and the shelf had come down.

* * *

He worked the way grief works: methodically, without mercy toward himself.

First the child. He could not leave her in the trough all night; the shop was cold, and cold killed infants faster than patrols killed couriers. He cleared the lower cabinet beneath the finishing bench—the one with the false back he used for storing contraband tools—and lined it with felt scraps and his own coat rolled into a ring. She woke while he moved her, regarded him with the grave, unfocused stare of the very young, and did not cry. Her eyes were dark. Almost black in the lamplight. He looked away first.

"You are not my business," he told her quietly. "Understand that. I am a binder. I bind books. I do not bind children."

She yawned, which he chose to interpret as agreement.

Then the documents. The principle was old and simple: the best hiding place for a page is inside a book, and the best book is one no one wants to open. Not a rare volume—rare volumes invite hands. Not scripture—scripture invites reverence, and reverence means reading. What he needed were books that invited nothing: municipal registers, tax assessments, almanacs of river depths, the gray sediment of bureaucracy that no soldier, no censor, no curious officer had ever opened voluntarily in the history of printing.

He pulled candidates from the shelving: a fifteen-year run of harbor tonnage reports, a directory of licensed ferrymen, the collected drainage ordinances of the northern provinces. He split each spine along its joints with a bone folder, lifted the text block free, and built a cavity in the heart of it—cutting pages with a razor in thin shaves until a recess formed, deep enough for a folded quire, shallow enough that the book still closed naturally and sat on a shelf with the honest slouch of boredom.

Then he sewed.

This was the part his hands loved, and he hated them a little for it. Even now—even with a child sleeping in a cabinet six feet away and the armistice delegations already booking rooms in Velmark—even now, when needle met thread and thread met linen and linen met board, something in him went quiet and orderly. Two-on, two-off, the kettle stitch locking at the head. The rhythm of it was older than the Republic, older than any regime that would replace it. Regimes came and went; the sewing frame endured. There was a bitter comfort in that, and he took it.

Into the harbor tonnage reports, 1909 through 1913, went the diplomatic ciphers, folded to the width of a finger. Into the ferryman's directory went the garrison manifests, page by page, each one interleaved between listings of licensed boatmen who had mostly drowned or fled. And the Kellin record—the record itself—he saved for last, because he could not decide where it belonged, and because he kept reading it.

He had read it twice already, in Maren's back room, by the light of a lamp shielded with a tin plate. He had told himself he was verifying the cipher key. He read it a third time now, standing at the bench with the pages trembling slightly in his grip, and verified nothing except that his hands were shaking.

It was not polemic. That was what made it terrible. It was clerical. Rations issued, rations diverted, rations sold—signed, countersigned, dated. Requisitions for rope in quantities that had only one civilian use. A column headed *persons processed*, with weekly totals, and beside the totals, in a different hand, corrections: *actual*, followed by larger numbers. And then, in the final section, the lists. Names. Whole families, entered the way one enters livestock or freight, with ages in a narrow column, and against many of the entries a clerk's abbreviation he had not seen before and had needed Maren to translate: *n.v.r.* — *no further record.*

No further record. Relocated. The language never changed. Only the scale did.

He found himself searching the lists—for what, he did not admit to himself until he admitted it: a surname. Not his own; his family had died too early and too obscurely to be in any valley ledger. But names like Liora's. Names like the ones from the village where he'd convalesced that first winter, people who had fed him and asked nothing. He found three. He made himself memorize none of them, and failed, and would carry one of them—*Vessik, Marta, age 34*—for the rest of his life without ever knowing why that one and not the others.

The child stirred in her cabinet. He closed the record gently, as if the pages were sleeping too.

* * *

Sometime past three, with the record sewn into the drainage ordinances and the ordinances rebound and pressed under weights, he allowed himself to sit, and the sitting undid him.

It happened the way it always happened—not as memory but as weather. The smell of the glue pot shifted and became the smell of Liora's kitchen, onions and bay and woodsmoke, and he was in the blue-gray light again, running, and the weight in his arms was not forty-one pages but thirty pounds of warm, terrified child, and the word *run* was still hanging in the air, unfinished, because he had never gone back to hear how the sentence ended. Had she said anything else? Had she called his name? For eleven years he had kept that door shut, and tonight it stood open, and everything he had shelved came off the shelves at once: Iselle's fist closing around his finger in the marsh reeds, the terrible lightness of her afterward; the deaf master's hands guiding his own along a spine, patient as water; the first book he had ever finished alone, and how he had wept over it, and how he had decided the weeping was about craft.

It had not been about craft.

Sable Montrose, courier of the dying Republic, put his head down on his bench among the bone folders and the beeswax and the shavings of cut pages, and shook, silently, the way a man shakes when he has taught his body that noise is fatal. He wept for a family relocated. He wept for a valley he had never seen, full of people reduced to an abbreviation. He wept, most shamefully, for himself—for the eleven comfortable years of the lie, the weightless man born from the reeds, who had believed that forgetting was survival and had never asked what survived, exactly, at the end of it. A name? A body? Some walking arrangement of habits that could bind a straight spine and look at nothing?

The child cried.

Not loudly—a thin, rising complaint from the cabinet, the sound of hunger or cold or the simple animal refusal to be alone. He lifted his head. Wiped his face with a rag that smelled of glue. Went to her.

She quieted the moment the cabinet opened, as if the draft had been the whole grievance. He fed her the goat's milk and honey mixture Odile—the midwife, if it was Odile, if the stitching meant what he thought it meant—had described to the neighbor woman in fragments he had pretended not to overhear. He held her upright and patted her back until she produced a burp of magnificent dignity. He changed the swaddling cloth with the clumsy competence of a man who has bandaged wounds and understands that the principles transfer.

And when she was clean and fed and drowsy again, he made the mistake of looking at her face.

Dark eyes. A crease between the brows that would become, in twenty years, the expression of a skeptical woman. Kellin weave at her throat, water-over-stones, indigo and rust. Someone had loved her enough to cross a city under curfew. Someone had written *forget the face* and chosen a bookbinder's step, which meant someone knew what a bookbinder could hide—and that knowledge was a thread, and threads could be followed, and he would follow it tomorrow to the midwife, because the alternative was to sit here forever with a question sewn shut inside him.

"I'm bad at forgetting," he told her. "I used to be good at it. I've gotten worse."

She slept. He laid her back in the cabinet, left the door open a hand's width for air, and stood a moment listening to her breathe—small, even, absurdly confident, as though the century had no intentions toward her.

Then he cleaned up. This too was discipline. Razor and bone folder wiped and racked. Cut shavings swept and burned. The rebound volumes arranged on the shelves in their proper places, spines out, gray and dull and honest-looking, forty-one pages of the Republic's worst truth sitting between drainage schedules where no one would ever willingly look. He stepped back and surveyed the shelving the way a general surveys a field, and found it good, and found no comfort in it whatsoever, because he knew now what he had known at twenty-six and unlearned for eleven years: records in order protect no one. They only make sure that later—much later, if anyone dares—someone can know.

Outside, the curfew sky was turning the color of steel about to be tested. He banked the stove, trimmed the lamp, and was reaching for the shutter bar when he heard wheels.

Not a patrol cart—too slow, too deliberate, the unhurried roll of official business arriving at an official hour. He stood motionless in the dark of his shop, one hand on the bar, and watched through the gap in the shutters as a staff car drew up before his door, gray paint, brass fittings, a pennant on the fender bearing the new regime's device. A driver remained at the wheel. A figure descended—an officer's greatcoat, a cap, and beneath the cap, in the first gray light, a face he had not seen in fourteen years and would have known in the dark, in death, anywhere.

Elsira Vant stood on the cobbles of Bindery Lane, consulting a paper clipboard as though the shop were already hers, and raised one gloved hand to knock.
