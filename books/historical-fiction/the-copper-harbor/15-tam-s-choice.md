## 15. Tam's Choice

Tam Voss had sold worse things than a man's name. He had sold his mother's silver thimble in the second winter of the war, and his father's watch the winter after that, and by the third winter he had learned that everything a man owns is only waiting to become bread. But those sales had been clean. A thing left his hands; bread entered them; his stomach stopped its complaining for a day or two. What he had done with the bookbinder's name was not like that. It sat in him like a swallowed hook.

He had told them on a Tuesday, in the ration office on Quay Street, where the queues began before dawn and the clerk behind the grille never looked up. A lieutenant with a soft voice and a ledger full of blank lines had asked the queue, casually, as if asking about the weather, whether anyone knew the comings and goings of the tradesmen on Tanner's Row. And Tam — who had been standing there since four in the morning with his hands blue from cold and his belly flat as a board — had heard himself say, *the bookbinder keeps odd hours*, and then he had heard himself say more, because once such words start they do not stop until they are finished: the light behind the shutters at all hours, the woman who came and went, the smell of glue and something else, something chemical, the night a cart had come and gone again before dawn. He had not meant to say any of it. His mouth had simply discovered that hunger had been negotiating on his behalf for months, and had closed the deal without consulting him.

They had given him a double ration card. Bread, oil, a tin of meat with a label in the occupier's language. He had eaten half the tin sitting on the quay with the gulls screaming at him, and it had tasted of nothing at all, and then it had tasted of everything, and then he had been sick into the harbor water.

Now it was Thursday, and the frost had come early, and Tam stood across the street from the bookbinding shop with his collar up and his heart going like a fist against a door, trying to make himself walk away instead of knock.

*They want him alive.* That was what the lieutenant's man had said, the one who had come to Tam's room to confirm details, the one whose boots were too good for this quarter. *Alive and whole. The officer wants to question him personally.* Tam had understood, in the way a man understands a thing he does not want to understand, that *alive* was not mercy. Alive meant the rooms below the customs house, where the lights burned past midnight and the neighbors crossed the street rather than pass the door. Alive meant a long time. Dead would have been quicker, and somehow that made it worse — that they wanted the bookbinder kept, like a jar of preserves, for someone else's table.

And there was the child. Everyone on Tanner's Row knew about the child by now; a baby could not be hidden in a city of hungry ears. Tam had seen it once, carried against the bookbinder's chest in a sling of grey cloth, its head lolling with sleep, one small fist escaped from the swaddling and opening and closing at the cold air like something reaching for a branch. Tam had looked at that fist for longer than was safe.

He crossed the street. His legs did it without his permission, which was how most honest things got done in Copper Harbor that year.

The shop bell gave its small dry note when he pushed the door. Inside, the smell was the same as always — paste and leather and lamp oil — but underneath it now there was milk, faintly, and something else, something like fear that had lived indoors so long it had become furniture.

Sable Montrose looked up from his bench. He did not look surprised. That was the first terrible thing. He set down his bone folder with the unhurried care of a man who had already decided how the next minute would go, whatever it contained.

"Tam."

"I need to talk." Tam's voice came out wrong, too high. He glanced at the back room, where a shadow moved behind the curtain — the child, awake, or the woman Odile, or both. "Not here. Or here. I don't — I don't know where's safe anymore, that's the whole—"

"Here is fine." Sable rose and turned the sign on the door to *closed* and shot the bolt, and the small domestic click of it sounded louder than anything Tam had ever heard. "Sit down. There's tea. It's mostly chicory, but it's hot."

"I don't want tea." Tam stayed standing. His hands wouldn't stay still; he put them under his arms like a boy caught out in the cold. "I want you to take your kid and your books and go, tonight, because they're coming for you, and I'm the reason, and if you stay you'll die, and it'll be my—"

"Stop." Sable said it quietly, and Tam stopped, because there was a kind of quiet that was louder than shouting and the bookbinder had always owned it. "Start at the beginning. Who did you tell, and what exactly did you say?"

So Tam told it. All of it, standing there among the presses and the stacks of unbound signatures, the whole shabby architecture of his betrayal laid out between them like goods on a market blanket: the ration office, the soft-voiced lieutenant, the words that had come out of his mouth in the queue, the double card, the visit from the man with the good boots. *Taken alive,* he repeated, and watched the bookbinder's face do nothing at all, which was somehow worse than watching it break.

"They said alive," Tam finished. "They were very clear about alive. And they said soon. Maybe tomorrow. Maybe tonight. They've been waiting for something — orders, I think, from above. The officer herself. The one who came to inventory your shop." He couldn't say her name. He had seen her once through the window, drinking tea across from Sable, both of them smiling the way people smile over an open grave.

Sable nodded slowly. He walked to the window and stood beside the curtain, not touching it, looking out at the street through the gap where the fabric didn't quite meet. Frost furred the edges of the glass. Somewhere down the row a dog was barking at nothing, or at something.

"How long have you known?" Sable asked.

"Since yesterday morning."

"A day." Sable turned around. There was no anger in his face. That was the second terrible thing. Tam had prepared himself for anger the way a man prepares for a blow — braced, eyes narrowed, ready to take it and perhaps deserve it — and the absence of it left him off balance, staggering inwardly. "You sat on it for a day."

"I tried not to come at all." The honesty came out of him now in a flood, ugly and relieving, like pus from a wound. "I told myself it wasn't certain, that maybe I'd remembered wrong, that maybe they'd lose the paper, that maybe — God, I told myself every lie there is, and then last night I dreamed about the baby's hand, just the hand, opening and closing, and I couldn't eat, and I can always eat, that's the one thing I can always—" His voice cracked. He pressed the heel of his hand against his mouth.

"You came anyway," Sable said. "Late, but you came."

"Don't forgive me." Tam said it fiercely, surprising himself. "Don't you dare forgive me. I don't want it. I want you to hate me enough to leave, that's all. Hate me and go. Take the child and go north, or wherever it is people like you go, and let them find this shop empty, and let them trace it back to me and shoot me, because I'd deserve—"

"Nobody's shooting anybody today." Sable crossed to the stove and poured the chicory water over the grounds anyway, two cups, out of habit or stubbornness. He set one on the bench near Tam. Steam rose between them, smelling of burnt root and better days. "Sit down, Tam. Please."

Tam sat. The bench creaked under him. On the wall above the press hung the tools of the trade in their neat rows — awls, folders, the great shears — and Tam thought, absurdly, of his father's workshop, dead ten years now, and of how a man's tools outlived his hands and hung there patient and useless, waiting.

"Why aren't you angry?" Tam asked. "I'd be angry. I'd put you through that window."

"Because anger costs energy I don't have, and because hating you changes nothing except making two men smaller instead of one." Sable sat down across from him, cup in both hands, and for a moment he looked very tired, tired in the bones, tired the way the whole republic was tired. "You sold my name for bread. I know. I've watched half this city do the same arithmetic since the armistice. Hunger doesn't make you a bad man, Tam. It makes you a hungry man. The occupation counts on that. It's cheaper than soldiers."

"That's kind of you and I wish you'd stop."

"It isn't kindness. It's strategy." Sable drank, set down the cup. "Here is what happens now. Tonight or tomorrow, a detail comes to this shop. They will find it empty or they will find it occupied, and either way they will find *something*, because I'm not going to run blind into the street and be taken in the first square. Which means I need a cart. And a driver. Someone with a license, a route pass, a face the checkpoints know and wave through because he's delivered to them a hundred times and always complained about the weight."

Tam went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the frost on the glass. "No."

"Yes."

"I'm not — I can't drive you anywhere. Do you know what they'd do to me? They already suspect everyone on this row. If I'm the one driving—"

"Then they'll hang you with the rest of us, and you'll die having driven a cart instead of having sold a friend, and I promise you the second death sits easier in the ground than the first." Sable said it without heat, almost gently, and that gentleness was the sharpest thing in the room. "I'm not asking you to love me, Tam. I'm not even asking you to be sorry, though you clearly are, loudly and at inconvenient hours. I'm giving you work. It's the only currency I have left, and it happens to be worth more than their ration card. Their card buys you bread until spring. This buys you the chance to look at yourself in a mirror for the rest of your life without flinching. I've priced both. Take whichever you can live with."

Silence. The stove ticked. Behind the curtain, faintly, a baby made a sound — not crying, just the small conversational noise infants make to reassure themselves the world is still there — and Tam closed his eyes as if struck.

"The mother," he said hoarsely. "The one from Kellin Valley. She's dead, isn't she. That's why the baby's here."

Sable didn't answer, and the not-answer was an answer, and Tam understood that the bookbinder's silence was itself a kind of discipline, a wall built brick by brick over years, and that behind it were rooms Tam would never see and would not survive seeing.

"I have a cart," Tam said finally. His voice was flat, emptied out, the voice of a man stepping off a ledge. "Fish cart. Hauled herring for eleven years before the fleet was requisitioned. The horse is lame in the near foreleg but she's honest, and the checkpoint at the weir gate knows me — I've cursed at that sergeant every Friday for three years and he curses back, we're practically married." He laughed, a horrible little sound with no floor under it. "Route pass runs out at the north gate. Past that I'd need papers I don't have."

"Papers I may be able to arrange." Something moved behind Sable's eyes — plans being weighed, doors being counted. "North first. We'll worry about past-north when we're past-north. Can you load tonight?"

"After dark. The stable lane backs onto the tannery; nobody walks there after the lamps go out, the smell alone keeps people off." Tam stood, and his knees felt strange, lighter and heavier at once, the way they say a condemned man feels when the pardon comes — except no pardon had come, only a job, only a road, only the chance to trade one kind of fear for another and call the trade redemption. "Sable." He stopped at the door, hand on the bolt. "Why? Why give me this? You should spit on me. Anyone sane would spit on me."

Sable considered him for a long moment. In the lamplight his face was all planes and shadows, a face built for being forgotten, and yet Tam thought he had never seen anyone less forgettable than the bookbinder in that moment, holding his chicory tea in a doomed shop with a dead woman's child sleeping behind a curtain.

"Because I need a driver more than I need your remorse," Sable said. "And because a man who has sold a name once will guard the next one with his life, if only to prove the first time was a lie. That's not forgiveness, Tam. That's arithmetic. Go home. Eat something. Come back after dark with the cart."

* * *

Tam drove home through streets gone blue with early dusk, and the double ration card in his pocket weighed more than any cargo he had ever hauled. He ate — of course he ate; he was who he was — but he ate standing up, quickly, without pleasure, like a man taking medicine, and afterward he sat by his cold hearth and took out the tin of meat with the foreign label and looked at it for a long time. Then he wrapped it in cloth and put it in his coat pocket. He didn't examine why. Some gifts you deliver without letting yourself think, or thinking would stop your hands.

At the stable he fed the mare extra oats, all of them, the reserve he'd been hoarding against a worse winter, and talked to her in the low nonsense voice drivers use, and she bumped his shoulder with her blunt warm head as if she already knew they were going somewhere and approved.

After dark he brought the cart around through the tannery lane, wheels muffled with sacking, breath smoking, heart hammering at every footstep that wasn't his own. The bookbinder was waiting in the doorway with a load already stacked: crates marked as binding stock, false-bottomed, heavy in the wrong way. And against his chest, inside his coat, the bundle that was not cargo at all, its small face turned into the wool, asleep or pretending to be, learning already the first lesson of the occupied city — how to be silent.

Odile came last, moving like a woman twenty years older than her papers said, carrying a carpet bag and a face that had finished grieving and started working. She looked at Tam once, and he flinched under it, expecting contempt, and found instead only assessment — a midwife weighing whether a man would hold, the way she'd weigh a rope.

"He'll hold," she said to Sable. "Men who hate themselves hold best. It's the comfortable ones you have to watch."

"Thank you," Tam said. She didn't answer. He hadn't earned an answer. He climbed onto the bench and gathered the reins, and the mare leaned into the collar, and the cart rolled north through streets where every shuttered window might be a witness and every lamppost a gallows in waiting.

Behind them, Copper Harbor lay under its curfew dark, and somewhere in it a telephone was ringing, or would ring soon, and a soft-voiced lieutenant was writing a name in a ledger. Ahead, the north gate, and beyond the gate the long black fields where the war had burned everything down to the color of ash.

Tam clicked his tongue and the mare picked up her pace, lame leg and all, toward the dark.
