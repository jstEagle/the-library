# 1. The Weight of Small Names

The tide went out before the sun did its work, and Beatrix Underhill went out with it.

She waded into the grey shallows with her skirts knotted at her knees and the salvage basket dragging behind her on a length of frayed rope, and she did not look back at the workshop. She knew it too well to need looking. Forty years of Meridian Trading Company timber and tar, squatting on the bay's edge like a patient animal that had learned it would always be fed. The drying racks. The net-lofts. The long shed where forty indentured workers bent over their tasks from bell to bell, and where Beatrix had spent nine of her twenty-two years learning how to be a shadow among them.

The water was cold enough to make her gasp, as it always was, as her mother had once told her it always would be. *Cold is the sea reminding you it is alive,* Sera Underhill used to say, kneeling in these same shallows with her hands full of shell and kelp. *A dead thing wouldn't bother.*

Beatrix dove.

Down into the green-brown murk where the light came apart in ribbons, down past the pilings furred with barnacles, fingers working along the silted bottom for whatever the company counted as hers to find: dropped tools, lost fittings, a coil of copper sheeting gone overboard in last month's storm. Her lungs burned. Her hands moved without her. This was the part of the day she loved, and she hated herself a little for loving it, because the water was the one place the company could not follow—not really, not into the dark where a girl became only a shape, only breath held and released, belonging to no ledger at all.

She surfaced with a fistful of verdigrised fittings and swam for shore.

* * *

By lamplight that evening, she copied figures.

This was her other work, the work almost no one knew she did. In the corner of the counting room, at a desk too large for her, under a lamp the foreman pretended not to refill, Beatrix transcribed the company's numbers into fair hand: pearl weights, wage deductions, rations issued against wages earned, the slow arithmetic by which forty human beings remained forever in debt to their own keepers. She wrote small and neat and fast. Nine years ago she had not been able to write at all. Nine years ago she had been a half-drowned child hauled off a boat after the storm took her mother, and the company had signed her name—*her* name, letters she could not yet read—into a book, and that was that.

It was Mr. Calloway who had taught her otherwise. The foreman. A dry, spare man with ink-stained cuffs and a face like a closed door, who had caught her studying the ledgers upside down across his desk one evening when she was thirteen and said nothing for three days—and then set a slate and a stub of chalk beside her supper and walked away. He never spoke of it. Neither did she. But every letter she owned, every figure she could summon like a spell, had come through him, passed hand to hand in silence like contraband.

Tonight the lamp burned low and yellow, and her pen scratched, and around her the workshop settled into its evening sounds: Marta in the cookhouse rattling pots she could not hear but could feel through the floorboards, humming tunelessly to herself; Tam the sailmaker somewhere above, needle and palm whispering through canvas; the creak of the building breathing.

Over her cot in the women's bunkroom hung a lantern of hammered copper, unlit, polished bright. Her mother's. It had come ashore with the wreckage of the dive boat, dented but whole, and Beatrix had carried it through nine years of bunks and barracks and never once lit it. She told herself there was no oil to waste. That was a lie she kept the way you keep a stone in your pocket—to remember the weight of something.

Her mother had died free. That was the strange arithmetic of it, the sum Beatrix could never make come even. Sera Underhill had owned nothing, owed nothing, belonged to no book—and the sea had taken her anyway. Whereas Beatrix, safe and fed and owned outright, lay each night beneath an unlit lantern and tried not to notice which of them had been richer.

She blew out the lamp and slept.

* * *

The soldiers came on a Tuesday, marching up the coast road with dust rising behind them like smoke that hadn't decided yet.

Beatrix was mending nets on the seawall when they entered the town—forty men in colonial blue, rifles slung, boots striking cobbles in a rhythm that made the gulls lift screaming off the rooftops. An officer rode ahead and dismounted at the customs house, and men with paste and paper began posting notices on every wall and doorpost: requisition orders, printed in a hand too official to be hurried.

The workshop emptied onto the steps to watch. Nobody spoke. Indentured workers learned early that crowds were read like weather, and this weather meant change, and change was never good for people whose names lived in someone else's book.

Beatrix edged closer along the wall, close enough to read.

*By order of the Provisional Command, all property, records, and holdings within the township of [the name blurred by paste] are hereby requisitioned for the conduct of the war. All commercial papers, registers, and accounts to be surrendered for inventory and disposal.*

*Disposal.* She read the word twice. It sat on the page the way a stone sits on a net.

That night, Mr. Calloway found her at the copying desk, though there was nothing left to copy. He stood with his hands behind his back, looking at the lamp rather than at her, and when he finally spoke it was in the low, flat voice he used for things that must never be overheard.

"You saw the notices."

"Yes, sir."

"The officers were in my office today. Counting my shelves." He turned a page of the great master ledger—the oldest book in the room, its spine cracked to white, its corners soft as cloth—without appearing to touch it. "They asked me which documents were essential. I told them none. They seemed pleased by that."

"Sir?"

"They'll burn the archives, girl." He said it the way he announced storms. "Company papers coming due. Debts, contracts, indentures—all of it. Easier than honouring any of it, once the war gives them an excuse." His eyes came up at last, and there was something in them she had never seen in nine years: fear, carefully folded small. "Every name in this room lives in that book. Every debt. Everything anyone here is, on paper, ends when the paper does."

Beatrix looked at the master ledger. It lay closed on its shelf like a sleeping animal, and she thought of the thousands of entries inside, the columns of small names, hers among them, written in a hand she now recognized as her own—for she had copied from it often enough—and felt something turn over in her chest, slow and cold, like a hull righting itself.

"And if the book burns," she said quietly, "what are we?"

Mr. Calloway picked up the lamp. For a moment his shadow loomed huge across the shelves, across the whole room, across everything the company had ever written down.

"Exactly," he said, and carried the light away, leaving her in the dark with the ledger and the question.

Upstairs, in the bunkroom, the copper lantern hung over her cot, catching what little moonlight came through the shutters and holding it, dully, patiently, the way it had held it for nine years.

Beatrix lay awake until the bells, listening to the soldiers' boots in the street below, and thinking about names, and fire, and what the sea keeps that no one can burn.
