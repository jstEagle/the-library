## 4. The Burning and the Keeping

They came at dawn, three days early.

Beatrix knew it first by the gulls—the way they lifted off the warehouse roof in a single ragged sheet, screaming, as though the sky itself had been torn. Then the drums. Not the workshop's morning bell, which Marta struck with a wooden mallet in three soft strokes, but the army's double-beat, hollow and hungry, rolling up the coast road like a tide that had come to take back everything the sea had ever lent.

She was at the copying table with the last eleven pages of the ledger still wet under her fingers. Nine nights of copper light had gone into them—nine nights of Tam's borrowed ink, of Marta's flour-sack hiding places, of the foreman Odell scratching false dates into the burn schedule with his steady clerk's hand. Nine nights of her mother's name moving under her pen, *Marisol Underhill, née Vasquez*, over and over, until Beatrix could have written it blind, drowning, in the dark.

"Torches," Tam said from the doorway. His empty sleeve was pinned up neatly, the way he always pinned it when he wanted to look respectable, which meant he was frightened. "They're lighting them at the gate. Odell's gone out to argue. You know what arguing buys us."

"Minutes," Beatrix said.

"Minutes," he agreed. "Maybe less."

She looked down at the finished copy—two hundred and six names in her small, careful hand, each one a person the company had counted as cargo. Then she looked at the original, the great iron-bound ledger that had sat on Odell's shelf for thirty years, heavy as a coffin lid. The army wanted it burned so the debts would die with it. But the debts weren't the only thing written there. In the oldest pages, in faded brown ink, her mother's entry waited: *pearl rights, unclaimed*, and beside it, *debt discharged—one dive*. One dive. The dive that killed her, though the ledger didn't say so. The ledger only said *paid*.

"Take the copy," Beatrix said. "I'll take the original."

Tam stared at her. "The original's worth nothing to the magistrate if—"

"It's worth everything to *me*." She heard her own voice come out low and strange, her mother's voice almost. "They're not burning my mother twice."

* * *

The smoke reached the workshop before the soldiers did.

It came under the door in grey fingers, tasting of pitch and palm thatch, and Marta was already moving—she had moved the moment the drums began, deaf to them but not to the floor's trembling, not to the gulls. She pressed a flour sack into Beatrix's arms, heavy with the first ninety pages, and pointed: the skiff, the north slip, the water. Then she went back for the second sack, and Beatrix saw that the old woman meant to make three trips if the roof fell on her head, and there was no arguing with Marta, there had never been any arguing with Marta.

The young officer was waiting at the slip.

Beatrix's heart stopped, then started again, wrong. Lieutenant Ashe—she had learned his name on the sixth night, when he stood in the doorway with her lantern-light all over his face and asked, not for her arrest, but for a name to be added: *Elena Ashe, sold to the Meridian Company, aged fourteen*. His sister. His sister's name was now written in her hand on page one hundred and ninety-one, and he was standing between her and the boat with a pistol on his hip and the burning town reflected in his eyes.

"They moved it up," he said. "I couldn't stop it. I tried."

"I know." She shifted the ledger against her chest. The iron corners bit through her shirt. "Are you here to take it from me?"

He looked at the ledger a long moment. Then he pulled his coat off and wrapped it around the book, iron corners and all, and took the flour sack from her shoulder onto his own. "I'm here to carry," he said. "My sister spent six years being carried by that company. I'd like to carry something back."

* * *

The bay was the color of hammered tin, and the smoke lay on it like a second sky.

Tam rowed. Of course Tam rowed—one hand on the oar, his body knowing the water the way Beatrix's knew the ledger, every pull timed to the swell. Marta sat in the bow with the sacks between her knees, her hands moving constantly, shaping words none of them could read but all of them understood. Beatrix sat in the stern with the original ledger wrapped in the officer's coat, and behind them the workshop burned.

She made herself watch.

The rafters went up like the ribs of a great animal, black against the orange, and the copper lantern—the one thing she had not been able to carry, the one thing that had hung unlit over her cot for nine years—she had taken it down at the last moment and it was in her lap now, cool and dented, her mother's lantern, her mother's mother's before that. The workshop that had owned her was dying, and she found she was grieving it anyway, because it had also been the place where Odell taught her letters with a wet finger on the tabletop, where Marta fed her the first meal anyone had ever made specially for her, where Tam had sung—badly, with one hand drumming on the sailcloth—songs about women who outswam their drownings.

"Look away if it hurts," Tam said, not turning around.

"No." She watched the roof fall. "I want to remember what they thought a fire could do."

* * *

The magistrate's house stood on the hill above the far shore, white-shuttered and absurdly calm, with a garden of frangipani that had never once been requisitioned. Lieutenant Ashe went up the steps first, because a uniform opened doors that a copyist's smudged hands could not, and Beatrix followed with the ledger held out in front of her like an offering, like a child, like a torch of her own.

Magistrate Okonjo was an old man with a reading cloth over his knees and a reputation, Odell had said, for being honest in the way that was most inconvenient to everyone. He unwrapped the officer's coat. He opened the iron-bound cover. He read the first page, and the tenth, and the oldest page of all, and when he reached her mother's entry his finger stopped on the brown ink and did not move for a long time.

"Marisol Vasquez," he said. "Pearl rights, unclaimed. Debt discharged—one dive." He looked up. "You are the daughter."

"I am the daughter."

"Then these rights pass to you." He closed the ledger with a gentleness that undid her more than any fire had. "And these names—" his hand rested on the two hundred and six, "—are not debts. They are people. The company burned its own records, and a burned record proves nothing against a living copy in the hand of a free woman." He looked at Tam, at Marta, at Lieutenant Ashe, at Beatrix. "You are all free as of this hour. I will sign it. I will sign it twice, in case they burn my office too."

Marta made a sound then, a great raw sound from deep in her chest, and pulled all three of them into her arms at once, flour dust and salt and smoke and the officer's stiff uniform crushed together, and Tam was laughing the way he laughed at his own bad singing, and Beatrix stood in the middle of it with her mother's name ringing in her ears like a bell that had waited nine years to be struck.

* * *

They rebuilt the workshop that same season.

Not as the Meridian Company's property—the company's charter was revoked, its agents scattered, its debts voided to the last entry—but as a cooperage and net-mending house owned jointly by the forty-odd hands who had once been counted in its ledgers. Odell kept the accounts now in a plain book with no iron corners. Marta's kitchen fed everyone who came through the door, paying or not. Tam taught the children to sail on Saturdays, one-handed, shouting the wrong words and letting Marta correct him with furious gestures.

On the first evening of the first month, Beatrix climbed the ladder to the new doorframe with the copper lantern in her hands.

She had polished it for a week. The dents were still there—the dents would always be there—but the metal had come up warm and glowing, and inside it she set a fresh wick and oil, and struck the match her mother had never lived to strike.

The light caught. It was small and gold and it did not waver.

She hung it above the door, where it would burn every night from now until her hands were too old to trim the wick—not as a company's mark, not as a warning, but as what a lantern is for: a light left on for whoever is still coming home across the water.

Below her, Tam was arguing with a sail, Marta was singing to the bread, and children who had been born free were chasing each other through the yard, their names written nowhere but in the mouths of people who loved them.

Beatrix watched the flame a moment longer. Then she went down the ladder to join them.
