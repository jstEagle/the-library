## 1. The Weight of Small Names

The fever came in with the tide, the way it always did in autumn. By midnight the infirmary smelled of camphor and wet wool and the particular sweetness of men burning from the inside, and Orsen Winterbourne moved down the rows of cots with a basin in one hand and a rag in the other, wringing cool water over foreheads like a woman tending a field she could not save.

There were thirty-one beds. Twenty-nine were full.

She had learned to count them the way other people counted money — quickly, without sentiment, because sentiment slowed you down and a man dying of fever did not have time for your feelings about it. Bed four: Corporal Dunmore, twenty-two, shivering so hard his teeth chattered like dice in a cup. Bed nine: Private Aldous, who had stopped speaking an hour ago and now only hummed, a low tuneless note that was worse than screaming. Bed seventeen: a boy named Fitch who kept calling for his mother in a voice that hadn't broken yet.

Orsen gave them all water. She gave them all her hands, cool from the basin. What she could not give them — quinine, beef tea, a doctor who stayed past ten o'clock — she did not waste breath mourning. The garrison surgeon, Dr. Pell, had gone home to his wife at half past nine, leaving instructions that amounted to *let them sweat*, and Orsen had watched him go with the same flat expression she wore for most things, because the alternative was to say something she'd regret, and regret was a luxury this town no longer stocked.

"Miss Winterbourne." A voice from bed twelve, thin as thread. "Miss Winterbourne, is it morning?"

"Not yet," she said, and pressed the rag to his brow. "Sleep while it's dark."

"It's better in the morning," he said. "Everything's better in the morning."

It was not, but she let him believe it. She had learned that too — that hope was a medicine you administered in small doses whether or not it worked, because the cost of withholding it was higher than the cost of the lie.

* * *

At two o'clock the worst of the sweating passed, or the men simply grew too tired to fight, and the infirmary went quiet in the way Orsen liked best: not silent, but hushed, breathing, alive. She sat at the little table by the stove, trimmed the lamp, and took out the ledger.

It lived in the bottom drawer of her supply cabinet, beneath a false layer of bandages, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp. It was not a beautiful book. It had been a shipping manifest once, salvaged from the harbor office when they cleared out the old clerk's desk, and its cover was scarred and its pages were soft at the edges. But it had copper clasps, green with age, and Orsen had come to love those clasps the way some women loved jewelry — because they held something precious shut.

She opened it and turned past the earlier entries, past the winter dead and the spring dead, to a fresh page. Then she dipped her pen and wrote, in her own shorthand — a compressed script of her own devising, half symbols, half memory-tricks, illegible to anyone but herself:

*Pvt. J. Aldous, 9th Co., d. 1:40 a.m., fever. Mother in Ashcombe. No effects beyond kit.*

*Cpl. R. Dunmore, 9th Co., living, grave doubt.*

*Boy Fitch, given name unknown, enlisted under age, d. 2:15 a.m., fever. Called for mother.*

The official morning report would say otherwise. It always did. The report was written by Lieutenant Sasson, a young man with good handwriting and no imagination, and it recorded deaths as *febrile casualties, three* — numbers without names, names without mothers, mothers without addresses. The army, Orsen had learned, did not lose men. It lost arithmetic. And arithmetic could be corrected; men could not.

So she kept the ledger. Not out of rebellion — rebellion required energy she did not have — but out of a conviction so old it felt less like belief than like bone: *a name written down is a name that happened.* The officers could round their numbers down all they liked. Somewhere in a drawer in a garrison infirmary, James Aldous had a mother in Ashcombe, and someday, if she ever asked, someone might be able to tell her the truth.

It was a small thing. Orsen knew it was a small thing. History, she understood, happened elsewhere — in capital cities, in war ministries, in rooms where men moved regiments across maps with brass pointers. Nothing that happened in this coastal town would ever be more than a footnote, and footnotes were exactly what her ledger contained. But she had decided, years ago, that if history would not trouble itself to remember the small names, then remembering them would be her work. Someone had to keep the accounts. The world was a business, and business required a ledger, and the ledger required a hand that did not tremble.

Hers did not tremble. She checked it sometimes, after writing, and it never did.

* * *

Near dawn, Tom came.

He came the way he always came on bad nights, letting himself in through the side door with the soft apologetic tread of a large man trying to be quiet, carrying two cups of chicory coffee he'd brewed himself because the mess wouldn't miss it. He was nineteen and built like his father had been, all shoulders and long bones, with their mother's dark hair falling into his eyes no matter how often he pushed it back.

"You look terrible," he said.

"You flatterer."

He set the cups down and looked at the cots, counting the way she'd taught him to count, and she saw his jaw tighten at the stillness of beds nine and seventeen. He was getting better at reading a room of the sick. She wished he weren't. She wished he were still the boy who'd run errands along the seawall and thought the soldiers were an adventure.

"Two?" he said quietly.

"Two."

He nodded and sat, and they drank their terrible coffee together while the stove ticked and the lamp burned low. This was the part of the night Orsen loved — not loved, perhaps; *valued* — this hour when the work was done and the only thing left was to sit with the one person in the world whose name she'd never had to write down, because he was still here, still breathing, still hers.

"The harbor master says there's troopships coming," Tom said. "More of them. Halloway's regiment, maybe. They're saying the whole coast's going to be garrisoned."

"They say a lot of things."

"Mum used to say that meant something was coming."

Their mother had said a great many things, most of them true, all of them gone now along with her and their father, taken by the same sea that fed the town and taxed it. Orsen turned her cup in her hands.

"If they come, they'll requisition everything," Tom went on. "Billets. Stores. The harbor. You've seen it happen up the coast. They take what they want and write receipts nobody can cash."

"Then we'll manage," Orsen said. "We always manage."

"I know we manage. I just—" He stopped, jaw working. "I don't like the idea of strangers going through things. Our things."

Something cold touched the back of Orsen's neck, though the stove was warm. She thought of the drawer, the oilcloth, the copper clasps. She thought of Lieutenant Sasson's neat columns and Major Halloway's reputation, which had arrived ahead of him like weather: a man who believed that disorder was treason and that paper was how you found it.

"It'll be fine," she said, and heard how much that sounded like *everything's better in the morning*.

Tom left before first light, taking the cups. Orsen washed her hands, banked the stove, and stood a moment at the window watching the harbor turn from black to iron-grey. Out on the water, faint in the mist, a ship was standing in toward the roads — one ship, then another behind it, then a third, riding low and heavy with men.

History, happening elsewhere, coming closer.

She went back to the cabinet, took out the ledger, and added one last line before the morning report could make its corrections:

*Night of the third: two dead, both omitted. Town filling with soldiers. If anything happens to this book, the fault is theirs, not mine.*

Then she wrapped it in oilcloth, slid it beneath the bandages, and went to open the shutters, because the day was starting whether the world remembered anyone or not.
