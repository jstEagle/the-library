## 2. Requisition

They came at dawn, as armies do, because dawn is when a town is weakest and the light flatters their banners.

I was still in the infirmary, wringing out a cloth for Private Aldous's forehead, when the first boots hit the cobbles outside. Not the loose, tired shuffle of the garrison men I'd nursed all winter — these were drilled boots, hundreds of them, striking in time like a single enormous heart. The water in my basin trembled with each step.

"Sister." Aldous's voice was a rasp. His fever had broken in the night and left him hollow-eyed and frightened of everything, including me. "What is it?"

"Reinforcements," I said, which was true, and "Nothing to trouble you," which was not.

Through the window I watched them come down from the ridge road in columns of four, grey coats darkened with sea-damp, bayonets catching the early sun so that the whole road glittered like a fish spine. Behind them came the wagons, and behind the wagons came the officers on horseback, and behind the officers — this was the part that made my hands go still on the cloth — came the clerks, with their ledgers and their inkhorns and their little brass-bound boxes, riding into my town the way locusts ride into a field.

Major Halloway rode at the head. I knew his face from the gazettes: long jaw, cold eye, a reputation for doing precisely what he was ordered to do and nothing more generous. He did not look at the town as he entered it. He looked through it, the way a man looks through a room he intends to strip.

* * *

By noon they had taken the church hall, the granary, both warehouses, and the harbor master's office. By two o'clock they were taking houses.

The requisition order was nailed to the market cross and read aloud by a lieutenant with a voice like a slammed door. Every dwelling to be assessed. Every spare room billeted. Every store of flour, salt meat, lamp oil, linen, and medicine inventoried and confiscated at rates the army considered fair, which meant rates no one else would have considered fair at all. Householders were to present themselves for registration. Doors that remained locked would be opened.

Mrs. Penhaligon stood beside me in the square, her knuckles white around her marketing basket. "Assessed," she repeated. "Like a ewe at market."

"Like less than a ewe," I said. "A ewe gets driven gently."

Tom found me there, breathless from the docks, where he'd been helping the fishermen argue with a corporal over their boats. The argument had gone the way arguments go with corporals. "They're taking the *Swift*," he said. "Impressed for transport. Da's boat, Orsen. They nailed a paper to her mast and called her government property."

"The army calls everything government property now. The air, likely, by evening."

His face was grey under its freckles. Tom was nineteen and had our father's shoulders and none of his patience, and watching him stand in the square with his fists opening and closing, I understood that the war we'd only ever heard about — the one that happened elsewhere, in other people's newspapers, to other people's sons — had just walked up our street and begun knocking on doors.

"Listen to me," I said, taking his arm. "You will not argue with them. You will not give them a reason. You'll smile and say yes sir and no sir, and you'll keep your name off every list they write. Do you hear me? Off every list."

He heard me. Whether he'd obey was another matter; Tom had never once in his life kept his name off anything.

* * *

They reached the infirmary on the third day.

I had expected it sooner. We were, after all, full of exactly what an army wants: beds, bandages, laudanum, and a building with good walls. Captain Vane — I did not know his name yet, then; I knew only that he was not Halloway — arrived with two clerks and a sergeant and a printed inventory sheet, and he walked the ward slowly while the clerks counted.

He counted differently than they did. They counted bottles; he counted men. I watched his eyes move down the rows of cots, pausing on faces, and something in the way he paused made me revise my first opinion of him downward from *enemy* to merely *problem*.

"You are the matron here?" he asked. He was younger than his rank suggested, thirty perhaps, with a voice that stayed level even when the clerks dropped a crate of instruments behind him and swore.

"Nurse. There's no matron. There's me."

"There's you," he agreed, and wrote something down. "How many patients?"

"Thirty-one. Two will die before the week is out regardless of what your quartermasters take or leave."

Something flickered across his face — not quite offense; recognition, maybe. "We'll leave the medicine," he said. "Mark it reserved."

"How generous. Reserved for whom?"

"For whoever needs it." He said it flatly, without warmth, as though generosity embarrassed him and he wished to get the transaction over with quickly. Then the clerks wanted the storeroom, and the storeroom was locked, and the key was on the ring at my belt, and for one absurd moment I stood there weighing whether I could refuse the army of the realm access to a cupboard of lint.

I unlocked it.

* * *

It was the clerk who found the ledger. That is the thing I cannot stop turning over since — not malice, not design, just a bored young man with a checklist and a talent for opening drawers.

My quarters were two rooms above the infirmary, and by the terms of the requisition they were subject to assessment like any other. The clerk went through them with the brisk piety of a man searching someone else's sins: trunk, mattress, floorboards, desk. My desk has a shallow drawer beneath the writing slope, and in that drawer, wrapped in a patient's old nightshirt, lay the copper-clasped book.

Four years of names.

I had been in the ward when he found it. I know this because I remember exactly where I was standing — at Aldous's cot, feeding him broth — when the boy came down the stairs holding it in both hands the way you'd carry something dead.

"Captain," he called. "There's writing here. Shorthand, looks like. And lists."

Vane took it from him at the foot of the stairs.

I want to say I moved. I want to tell you I crossed that ward and took it back out of his hands and told him what it was and what it wasn't. That is what I should have done, and what I did instead was stand very still with a spoon in my hand and watch a stranger open four years of my private defiance in the doorway of my own infirmary.

He turned the pages. His face gave away nothing at all, which I later learned was his habit and his armor both. The copper clasps caught the lamplight. I could see from twenty feet away which page he'd stopped on — March of last year, the entries after the point action off the shoals, nine names in my tight hand where the morning report recorded three.

"What is this?" he asked, without looking up.

"A record," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Of the dead."

"The official returns—"

"Are incomplete."

Now he looked up. And I saw him weigh it — the book in his hands, the woman with the spoon, the word *incomplete* hanging between us like smoke. A kinder officer might have handed it back. A crueler one might have smiled. Elias Vane did neither; he closed the book carefully, keeping his thumb in the page like a man marking scripture, and said to the clerk:

"Add it to the inventory. Item forty-one. One ledger, copper-bound, contents unverified."

"Unverified," I repeated.

"It goes to Major Halloway for assessment." He said it as though reciting regulation, and perhaps he was. Perhaps that was the whole truth of him then: a man for whom everything, including my four years of grief and stubbornness, resolved neatly into an item number. "If it's yours, madam, you may petition for its return through proper channels."

Proper channels. I have thought often since about how much cruelty can be packed into two ordinary words, delivered without a flicker.

* * *

They carried it out at dusk.

I followed it as far as the door. I watched the clerk load it into a canvas satchel with the rest of the day's confiscations — a minister's sermons, a widow's silver spoons, a merchant's account books — and I watched the wagon pull away toward the harbor offices where Halloway had established himself, and I felt something I had no ready name for.

Not grief, exactly. Grief is for what's lost. The ledger wasn't lost; worse, it was *found*. Every name in it — the drowned boys written off as deserters, the fevered poor buried in the pauper trench without rank or stone, Wren, Wren whose existence no document in this world confirms but mine — every one of them had just changed categories. For four years those pages had been memory. Now they were evidence. Evidence against whom, evidence of what, evidence for what purpose — that would be decided by Major Halloway, a man I had never spoken to, who looked through towns as though they were already rubble.

A name in the wrong hands stops being a name. It becomes a lever. I understood that standing on my own doorstep with the evening coming down and the gulls screaming over the harbor, and what I felt was fear, plain and cold, the first real fear of the war for me — not for Tom, not even for myself, but for the dead, who cannot be conscripted twice but can certainly be betrayed again.

Mrs. Penhaligon passed with her basket and asked if I'd lost something, I looked so stricken.

"My book," I said.

"Bought a new one, did you? There's a stationer still trading, near the—"

"No." I watched the wagon disappear down the hill. "Not that kind."

She clucked and moved on, and I went back inside, and I climbed the stairs to my ransacked rooms, and I sat down at the emptied drawer and did the only sensible thing left to a woman whose memory has been confiscated.

I began to reconstruct it. From habit, from the ward books, from the graveyard registers, from the inside of my own skull, which the army had not yet thought to inventory — I began to write the names back in, entry by entry, my hand shaking so badly the letters leaned like drunks.

I got eleven names down before the knock came at the infirmary door below, and a voice I didn't know asked for Nurse Winterbourne by name.

"Captain Vane's compliments," said the soldier on the step, holding out a folded paper. "He requests your attendance at the harbor offices tomorrow morning. Nine o'clock. Regarding item forty-one."

Item forty-one. My dead, reduced to a line in a quartermaster's book, and now summoning me like a debtor.

I stood in the doorway a long moment, the paper in my hand, the reconstructed ledger hidden upstairs behind a loose brick, and I understood that whatever waited for me at the harbor offices at nine o'clock, my life had already divided into before and after.

They had taken the book. Now they wanted the woman who wrote it.
