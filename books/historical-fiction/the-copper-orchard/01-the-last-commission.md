## 1. The Last Commission

The tallow candle had burned down to a thumb's width, and Wren Eastwick did not replace it.

There was a discipline to this — Master Halvard had taught it on the first day of the apprenticeship, six years ago now, when Wren had been all elbows and ink-stained cuffs and an eagerness so naked it embarrassed them both. *A cartographer works to the light he has,* Halvard had said, tapping the candlestick with one long finger. *Not to the light he wishes for. The land does not wait for better weather, and neither shall you.* So Wren worked in the last of the light, hunched over the copper plate with the burin held at the angle that had taken two years to find and would take a lifetime to perfect, cutting the final line of the final plate of the atlas of Master Halvard's life.

Outside, Copperbridge was settling into its November throat-clearing. The river muttered under the pilings. Somewhere up the hill a dog was complaining about the wind, and somewhere farther off, the foundry stamped out its slow iron heartbeat — the sound Wren had slept inside since childhood, the sound that meant home, though they had never once thought of it as music until this autumn, when everything began to seem like something that could end.

The plate before them was the delta country south of the marshes, forty miles of water braided into silver threads. It was good work. Wren could say that without false modesty, because false modesty was another thing Halvard had forbidden: *If it is well done, say so. If it is badly done, do it again.* The marsh channels ran true. The hatching on the high ground fell away like combed hair. In the lower corner, where the scale bar sat, Wren had cut a heron standing in the shallows — a small liberty, an unsanctioned ornament, the kind of thing that would have earned a lecture a year ago and only a raised eyebrow in the last months, when the eyebrow itself had grown rare.

That was the grief of it, Wren thought, and set the burin down to flex their aching hand. Grief was not the tearing of garments. Grief was reaching, eleven weeks after the funeral, for the second cup of tea out of habit, and then remembering there was no one to drink it. Grief was the workshop being exactly as he had left it — the apron on its hook, the spectacles folded on the shelf above the acid bath, the half-eaten apple gone to leather beside the window — because Wren could not bear to move any of it, and so lived inside a shrine without admitting it was one.

Eleven weeks. The lawyers had come and gone with their papers. The guild had sent a stiff letter of condolence and a stiffer inquiry about outstanding commissions. And the atlas — the great atlas, the work of thirty years, the thing Halvard had called *the only child I ever finished* — sat in its portfolio on the drafting table, twenty-nine plates complete, waiting on the thirtieth.

Wren picked up the burin.

*Finish it,* everyone said. Finish it and present it to the Guild, and let them stamp their seal on the corner, and let Master Halvard's name go into the catalogue with the dates of his birth and death beneath it, tidy as a grave marker. Finish it, and then decide what became of an apprentice whose master was gone. There were positions in the capital. There was the survey office in Marrowfield. There was, if Wren wanted it, the whole wide world that Halvard's maps made look so patient and so near.

They cut the last channel line. They cut the last reed. They cleaned the plate with spirits and held it up to the dying candle, turning it, checking the light across the surface the way Halvard had taught them — *tilt it until the lines catch; the metal will tell you its sins* — and the metal told them nothing but the truth: it was done.

Twenty-nine plates and one. Thirty years and six. Finished.

Wren sat back on the stool and felt the strangest sensation, which was not satisfaction and not sorrow but some third thing with no name, like the pause between the lightning and the thunder. The atlas was done. The workshop was silent. Their whole life had been arranged around this table like iron filings around a magnet, and now the magnet was lifted away, and nothing knew yet which way to fall.

It was in reaching under the table for the rag tin — a mindless motion, tidying up, the body doing what the heart could not — that Wren's knuckles struck something that should not have been there.

* * *

The underside of the drafting table was a country Wren knew intimately. Every apprentice knows the underside of the master's table; it is where gummed rags are stuck, where dropped gravers roll and hide, where a boy of fourteen once carved his initials and spent a week sanding them out before Halvard noticed. Wren knew the scarred oak of it, the iron bracket at the left rear leg, the shallow drawer built into the frame that held spare points and wax.

But the thing their knuckles had struck was none of these.

They got down on their knees on the cold flagstones and looked. Fastened flat against the underside of the tabletop, directly beneath the place where the portfolio lay above, was a board — a thin panel of walnut, no longer than a folio, screwed down at four corners with screws so old and so precisely seated that the slots had been filled with wax and dust and painted over. Painted over. Someone had not merely hidden this; someone had disguised it as part of the table's own anatomy, and the someone could only have been Halvard, who had built the table himself, who had known every screw of it, who had sat above this secret through six years of Wren's apprenticeship and never once, in illness or drink or tenderness, let slip that it existed.

Wren fetched the screwdriver from the tool chest. Their hands were steady, which surprised them. It was the steadiness of shock, they understood later — the body going calm when the mind has not yet caught up.

The first screw fought. The second surrendered. The third stripped, and Wren had to grip its head with pliers and coax it, whispering an apology to the table, to the dead man, to the wood itself. The fourth came free like it had been waiting.

The panel dropped into their hands. Behind it, in a recess routed into the oak, lay a folio wrapped in oilcloth and tied with cord, and the cord was sealed — a drop of red wax, pressed with Halvard's signet, the compass-rose with the broken north point. Wren knew that seal. They had watched him press it a hundred times on letters to clients. He had worn the signet ring into the ground, or rather his sister had buried it with him, and here was its ghost, its last impression, unbroken.

*He meant it for after,* Wren thought, kneeling on the floor with the folio in their lap like something rescued from a fire. *He sealed it knowing I would be the one cleaning this table.*

Or knowing no one would. Or hoping. The dead leave such ambiguous instructions. Wren turned the folio over twice, feeling the weight of it, the faint shift of paper within, and thought about how a man might spend thirty years drawing the world honestly and still keep one drawer of it locked.

They broke the seal.

* * *

Inside were maps. Eleven of them, drawn on Halvard's own stock, in Halvard's own hand — Wren would have sworn to both on any scripture, for they knew that hand the way they knew their own pulse, the confident verticals, the little hooked serifs, the way he crossed his sevens. Eleven maps, each titled in his neat capitals along the top margin.

And every one of them was impossible.

The first showed the valley of the Ashwater, sixty miles east, rendered in detail no traveling surveyor would bother with: not the roads and towns but the *farms*, each holding named, each farmhouse marked with a small square and, beside several of them, a small cross. The date in the corner was written in Halvard's numerals: *March 1847*. March. Halvard had died in August, in this room, in the bed Wren had helped carry down from the loft, and in the last year of his life he had not traveled farther than the churchyard gate. Wren had seen to his meals. Wren had mixed his medicines. Wren would have known.

The second map was of the copper orchard.

Wren went very still. Everyone in Copperbridge knew the copper orchard — the terraced slopes on the far bank where the mining company had planted fruit trees over the old diggings, a gesture of civic beauty that had won the company a commendation and a plaque. You could see the orchard from the bridge on a clear day, rows of apple and quince marching down to the water, picturesque as a print. Children stole apples there in September. It was, as far as Wren had ever known, simply a pretty place with a strange name.

Halvard had mapped it as it had been. The title read: *THE ORCHARD SETTLEMENT AS IT STOOD, MICHAELMAS 1837*, and beneath the terraces, where the company's plaque would someday say ORCHARD, the map said *village* — streets, a chapel, a schoolhouse, a smithy, a row of cottages along the spring line, each cottage bearing a family name in Halvard's small sure capitals. Ostrand. Vey. Kettleby. Nystrom. Forty-one names, and beside nine of them, the small cross Wren had first noticed on the Ashwater map, repeated here with a terrible regularity, clustered along the eastern terrace as though the crosses themselves had been arranged by some patient administrative hand.

There was marginalia. Of course there was marginalia; Halvard's margins were famous, dense with notes in his private shorthand, the cipher he used for working drafts — Wren recognized it and could not read it, not quickly, not tonight. But one note near the bottom border was written plainly, in the larger script he'd used for anything he wanted an apprentice to find:

*Ask Maren about the well.*

Wren read it three times. *Maren.* The name meant nothing. No client of the workshop bore it; no guildsman; no one in the ledger of commissions, which Wren had inventoried down to the farthing in the first raw week after the funeral.

The third map was of the mine offices themselves — the company compound, drawn from memory or from some document Wren could not imagine, with rooms labeled: counting house, assay room, the manager's quarters, and a small structure behind the assay room marked only *records*. The fourth through the ninth were more valleys, more villages, more crosses, spanning years Wren had thought uneventful. The tenth was a chart of payments — not a map at all but a table, sums and dates and initials, running down the page like water finding its level, and beside certain entries the same shorthand that filled the margins elsewhere, coiling and dense as nests of snakes.

The eleventh map Wren did not understand at all. It appeared to be blank — a clean sheet with a title block and border and nothing else, except that when Wren tilted it to the candlelight, as Halvard had taught them, tilting until the lines catch, they saw the faintest impressions in the paper, as though something had once been written upon it in a dried-out ink, or pressed through from a page above. Ghost-writing. A palimpsest of nothing.

Wren sat back on their heels among the scattered maps and listened to the workshop tick and settle around them, the river muttering, the foundry beating its slow heart, and felt the quiet dread arrive — not loudly, not dramatically, but the way floodwater arrives in a cellar: silently, from below, already ankle-deep before anyone thinks to look.

Because here was the arithmetic that would not balance. These maps were dated after the work stopped. Dated through the last year of Halvard's life, through the very months Wren had ground his pigments and trimmed his pens and watched him grow thin as a winter branch — dated, some of them, *after his death*, if the hand could be trusted, and the hand was his, and the latest date was the fourteenth of September, three weeks after they had nailed shut his coffin. Either someone else had drawn these and signed them with a dead man's skill, which was impossible; or Halvard had drawn them before his death and backdated them, which was strange; or —

Or the dates were true, and the world contained something Wren did not have a word for yet.

The candle guttered. Wren looked at the small cross beside the name *Kettleby*, and at *ask Maren about the well*, and at the payment table with its initials and sums, and understood, in the way one understands a change in the weather before the clouds arrive, that the atlas on the table above — the beautiful, honest, commissioned atlas, thirty years of rivers and coastlines and careful hatched hills — had perhaps never been the real work at all. Perhaps it had been the cover. Perhaps the last commission, the true one, had been hidden underneath it all along, screwed to the underside of the table where an apprentice's knees would one day find it.

The candle died. Wren did not move for a long time in the dark, the folio open across their knees, the river talking to itself below the window.

Then, carefully, they gathered the eleven maps, wrapped them in the oilcloth, retied the cord — the broken seal they tucked into their shirt pocket, against the skin, where it lay cold as a coin from a drowned man's purse — and slid the bundle into the false recess, and screwed the walnut panel back down over it, and sat in the black workshop listening to the wind come off the water.

Tomorrow, they told themselves. Tomorrow they would ask about Maren. Tomorrow they would begin to read the shorthand. Tonight there was only the dark, and the small hard shape of the seal against their chest, and the knowledge, arriving slowly as cold through a coat, that the bridge across the river closed for the season in three days' time — and that whatever this was, it had been waiting ten years, and could certainly wait three days more.

Could it not?

Out on the far bank, almost too distant to see, a lantern moved along the terraces of the copper orchard, paused, and went out.
