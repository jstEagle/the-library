## 7. Sef's River

The river taught Sef everything he knew about lying still.

He had learned it first as a boy of nine, flat on his belly in the reeds below the orchard while a company patrol walked the bank with lanterns, hunting men who had fled the clearing of 1837. His mother's hand had pressed the back of his neck into the mud — *don't breathe, don't blink* — and he hadn't. The lantern light had passed over him like weather. He had lain there long after the boots faded, listening to the river talk to itself under its first skin of ice, and understood something that had never left him: the world did not see you unless it wanted to. And most of the time, nobody wanted to.

He was thinking of that now, crouched in the willow scrub above the eastern bank, watching the Copperbridge water move black and slow between shelves of new ice.

The crossing was possible. He could see it — not easily, not safely, but possible. The ice held fast along both shores where the current slackened, white and ridged like old scar tissue, and out in the middle channel the water still ran open, a hundred feet of black silk sliding between the floes. A man couldn't walk it. But a man could go around, upstream past the old quarry bend where the river widened and shallowed and froze edge to edge — if the ice there bore weight, and if nobody was watching, and if the night stayed dark.

Three ifs. In Sef's experience, three ifs was two more than a sensible man liked, and one fewer than hope required.

Behind him, up the slope through the dead orchard trees, the house windows glowed amber. Maren would be at her table grinding something bitter-smelling. Ilse would be reading aloud to Piet, who pretended he wasn't listening. And Wren Eastwick — the mapmaker, the inheritor, the one who had come to them carrying a dead man's secrets like a bundle of lit kindling — Wren would be bent over the drafting table with a pen in hand, turning testimony into lines that would outlive them all.

They needed this letter to reach the outside. That was what Maren had said last night, her hands flat on the table, her voice level in the way that meant she had already argued herself past fear: *Before we commit everything to paper, we need to know word can travel. One letter. A test.*

And so here he was, the orchard's smuggler, its ferryman of small illegal things — tobacco, letters, once a whole crate of company-stamped medicine that he preferred not to think about — chosen because he was the only one among them who knew the river the way a thief knows a lock.

The letter sat against his chest, wrapped in oilcloth inside his coat, warm from his body. It was addressed to a shipping clerk in Marrow's Landing, a man named Dossel who owed Sef favors and asked no questions, three miles downriver on the far side. From Dossel's wharf, any letter could ride anywhere. That was the whole architecture of the plan: get one envelope across the ice, prove the artery open, and then the atlas — when Wren finished it, when they dared print it — could flow out of Copperbridge like blood from a wound.

Sef checked the sky. Clouds moving in from the northwest, low and fat, promising snow by midnight. Good. Snow covered tracks and muffled sound and gave every honest reason for a man to be abroad wrapped to the eyes. He waited for full dark anyway. Patience was the first tool of the trade; impatience got you drowned or shot, usually drowned, because the river always collected.

When the last grey drained out of the west, he moved.

* * *

The quarry bend lay an hour's hard walk upstream, and Sef made it in fifty minutes, keeping off the towpath, following deer trails he'd cut years ago with a knife and memory. The cold had teeth now. It found the gap between his collar and his jaw and worried at it, and his breath came out in plumes that he tried to time with his steps, exhaling downwind, though there was no one to see. Habit. The river taught habits too.

At the bend, the river spread wide and shallow over a gravel shelf, and the ice had taken it completely — a great pale plain, wind-scoured, groaning faintly to itself in the way ice does when the temperature drops and the water beneath contracts. Sef stood at the edge and listened the way other men prayed.

Ice spoke honestly if you knew its grammar. A high singing note meant thin and stretched. A dull knock meant water moving underneath, eating at the underside. A deep boom rolling across the whole sheet was only the ice settling, growing, complaining — that sound you could trust. He heard all three as he stood there, and none of them in the proportions he'd have liked.

He stepped out.

The first twenty yards were firm, packed snow over solid freeze, and he allowed himself the thought that this might be simple. Then the character of the surface changed. Wind had scoured a stretch bare, and beneath it the ice went glassy and dark, and through that darkness he could see the gravel shelf below, and the water sliding over it, and his own reflection looking back up at him with the expression of a man doing something stupid.

He crouched. Slid his knife from his boot and tapped.

*Tock. Tock.* Dull. Water underneath, close.

He rose and angled toward the near shore, where a line of boulders broke the current and the ice built thicker in their lee. It cost him distance — the crossing widened from a quarter mile to nearly half — but distance was cheap and drowning wasn't. He worked along the boulder line, testing each stride with his weight rolled onto the ball of his foot before committing, the way his mother had taught him, the way he'd taught himself falling through once at fourteen and climbing out with his clothes freezing to him in sheets, laughing so hard he couldn't swear.

Halfway across, the river reminded him who owned it.

It came without warning — a crack like a rifle shot directly beneath him, and the whole plain shuddered, a wave traveling away through the ice in both directions faster than a man could run. Sef dropped flat, arms spread, distributing himself across the surface the way a beggar spreads a coat, heart slamming. The crack ran on and on, out into the dark, diminishing. Somewhere far off, the ice boomed its low answer.

He lay there counting his own heartbeats. At forty he got up. At sixty he was walking again.

That was the thing about the river that he'd never told anyone, not even Ilse: it didn't frighten him anymore. It should have. It had every right. But somewhere in the years of small runs and small thefts, fear had worn down into something else — a kind of attention, total and clean, that he got nowhere else. On the river he was entirely alive, entirely present, entirely himself. There were worse addictions. He'd seen them.

The far shore came up slow, a black massing of alder and dead grass, and then he was on it, knees soft, breath ragged, standing on ground that didn't move. He allowed himself thirty seconds of standing there grinning like a fool at nothing.

Then he turned south and began the walk to Marrow's Landing.

* * *

Dossel's wharf smelled of tar and dead fish and, beneath it, the sweet rot of the pilings. The shipping office was a shack with one lamp burning behind fogged glass, and Dossel himself was a slab of a man with a beard going to rust, who opened the door before Sef finished knocking because he'd seen him coming down the lane — everyone saw Sef coming; that was the joke of being a smuggler with an honest face.

"You're either desperate or rich," Dossel said, looking him over. "Crossed tonight? You're mad."

"Both, probably. Can you post this?"

Sef unwrapped the oilcloth and handed over the letter — Wren's careful hand on the face of it, addressed to a magistrate's clerk in the capital, innocuous as a bill of lading. Inside, three sentences. *We hold materials concerning the copper orchard. We can produce testimony and documents. Reply by the hand of your correspondent.* Signed with a name that wasn't anyone's. It said nothing and everything, which was the point. If it arrived, the road was open. If it arrived and an answer came back, the road was proven.

Dossel weighed it in his palm like a man judging a fish. "Post it where?"

"The capital coach. Thursday run."

"Thursday's four days." Dossel set the letter on his desk, then looked up, and his face had changed — not hostile, but careful, the care of a man who has survived trouble by seeing it early. "Sef. There are men asking after crossings. Company men. Came through yesterday, two of them, well-dressed, well-paid, buying drinks and asking who moves freight on the river these nights. I told them nothing, because I know nothing, because that's how I stay alive. But they'll ask at the tollhouse too, and Tolliver sells anything to anybody."

Sef felt the cold come off him in a new way. "Which direction were they headed?"

"Back toward the bridge, last I saw. But they hired horses." Dossel pushed the letter across the desk again. "Take it back. Post it yourself in the spring, when the ferry runs and nobody cares."

"It has to be now. Before spring, there's nothing left to testify about."

Dossel stared at him for a long moment. Whatever he read in Sef's face made him sigh, a big bellows sound, and he took the letter back and slid it into a drawer beneath a ledger. "Thursday coach. If I'm still breathing Thursday. Now get off my wharf before my luck notices you're standing in it."

Sef grinned, clasped his forearm, and let himself out into the dark.

The walk back to the bend took longer than the walk out, because now he was listening behind himself as much as ahead. Twice he stopped in the alder shadow and counted to two hundred, hearing nothing but the wind working the dead stalks and, far off, the river booming its slow iron music. The clouds had thickened. Snow would start within the hour, and part of him welcomed it, and part of him remembered that snow that covers your tracks also erases theirs, and a man could be ten yards behind you in a whiteout and you'd never know until the knife.

He was forty yards out onto the ice at the quarry bend when the lanterns appeared on the far shore.

Two of them, bobbing, coming down the deer trail he'd used — his trail, cut with his own knife — and behind the lanterns, voices, low and carrying strangely across the frozen plain. Not patrol voices. Patrols were bored. These voices were interested.

Sef did the arithmetic in half a second and hated the answer. They hadn't followed him from Dossel's; they'd been waiting at the bend, or near it, watching the crossing — which meant someone had talked, which meant Tolliver or worse, which meant the company already suspected the orchard sent things across the river, which meant—

No. Later. Think later.

He dropped to his belly behind a pressure ridge of ice and killed his lantern, which wasn't lit anyway, and became the boy in the reeds again. *Don't breathe. Don't blink.* The lanterns came down to the ice edge and stopped there, and the voices resolved: two men, arguing about whether the ice would bear.

"Bear for what? We're not crossing tonight, you idiot. We watch it. Vell said watch the crossings and report anything that moves."

"He crossed already, maybe. Tracks come out, tracks go back."

"Tracks going back means he's coming back across. We wait."

A pause. The wind filled it. Then, closer, almost conversational, pitched across the ice as if the speaker knew exactly how sound traveled on a frozen river:

"Whoever you are, friend — the company pays better than whatever's paying you."

Sef lay with his cheek against the ice and felt, absurdly, like laughing. The river boomed beneath him, vast and indifferent, and he thought: *you have no idea what's paying me. What's paying me is a dead man's atlas and a woman who remembers 1837 and a girl who hasn't yet said out loud what happened to her, and you couldn't afford it.*

The lanterns withdrew up the trail — but slowly, reluctantly, the way a fisherman leaves a pool he believes holds fish. Sef counted to five hundred before he moved, and then he didn't take the straight line back. He swung wide, north along the shore, through scrub that clawed at his coat, adding an hour to the journey because the hour was cheaper than the alternative.

The snow started as he climbed the bluff below the orchard, fat flakes spiraling down through the dark, already laying a clean sheet over the ice, over the tracks, over the lantern-trampled snow at the bend. By morning there would be no evidence he'd ever crossed. By morning there would also be no evidence the watchers had ever waited — but they'd remember, and they'd be back, and next time they might not argue about whether the ice would bear.

In the kitchen, firelight and faces turning toward him. Maren rising. Ilse's eyes going from his face to his soaked boots to the snow melting in his hair. Piet in the corner, very still.

"Well?" Maren said.

Sef unwound his scarf, sat, and held his hands to the stove before he answered, making them wait, making himself say it plainly.

"It's done. The letter goes Thursday, on the capital coach." He looked up. "But they know about the crossing now. Two of Vell's men watched me come back across. They didn't see my face. Yet." He flexed his stiff fingers, feeling the river still in them, cold and patient. "So whatever we send next time — we'd better send everything at once. Because I don't think we get a third crossing."

The fire ticked. Outside, the snow thickened against the windows, and somewhere down in the dark the river went on talking to itself under the ice, saying the only thing it ever said: *everything breaks, eventually. The question is what you've put in motion before it does.*
