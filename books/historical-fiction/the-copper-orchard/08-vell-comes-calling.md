## 8. Vell Comes Calling

The knock came on a morning of hard blue light, the kind that made the frost on the orchard windows look like engraving — fine white lines, deliberate, as though some patient hand had been at work all night. Wren was grinding ink when they heard it: three measured raps on Maren's door, spaced like a man who had never once in his life been made to wait.

They had known, of course, that this day would come. Vell had been across the river for eleven days, lodged at the Ferryman's Rest, and the orchard folk had counted his days the way farmers counted frost warnings. Sef had seen him twice at the tollhouse, hatless in the cold, talking with the bridge foreman. Ilse had watched him measure the ruined company chapel with a little brass instrument, writing in a book whose pages were too white, too new, too clean for this shore. He was settling in. He was making himself a fixture. And a fixture, Piet had said grimly, is only a nail that hasn't been hammered yet.

But knowing a thing and hearing its knock are different animals entirely.

Wren set down the muller. Their hands were steady — that surprised them. Ten years of training under Halvard had taught their hands a discipline their heart had never managed, and now, absurdly, in the moment when everything was at stake, the hands simply did what hands do: wiped themselves on the apron, flexed, waited.

Maren appeared in the doorway of the stillroom, a sprig of dried yarrow still in her fingers. Her eyes went to Wren's, and something passed between them that had no words in it — or rather, had too many. *He's come for you. He's come for the atlas. He will be charming. Do not be charmed.*

"I'll answer it," Wren said, and heard their own voice come out level, and marveled at it.

"No," Maren said. "I answer my own door. But he'll want to see you — you're the apprentice, the legitimate heir to Halvard's effects. That's the door he'll knock on next." She crushed the yarrow absently between finger and thumb. "So let him see you here first. Let him see you as part of the household, not a guest to be peeled away. And Wren—" She hesitated, and the hesitation cost her; Maren was not a woman who hesitated. "He will offer you a version of yourself that is easier than the truth. A young artist, an orphan of the profession, a person with no stake in old quarrels. It will be flattering. That is the whole of his weapon. Flattery is cheap to make and expensive to refuse."

Wren thought of Halvard's last lesson, given not in words but in the way he'd held a burnisher over a plate, letting the apprentice watch the metal take a shine it could not keep. *Everything that gleams is not finished,* he'd said. *Some things only gleam because they're about to be sold.*

"I understand," Wren said.

"You don't," Maren said, not unkindly. "But you will, in about an hour."

She opened the door.

Ambrose Vell stood on the frozen mud of the orchard path like a portrait that had been hung in the wrong gallery. He was younger than Wren had expected — forty, perhaps, with the kind of face that made age difficult to guess because it had been arranged rather than lived. A good grey coat, good boots, a hat held correctly in one gloved hand. Behind him, at a respectful distance, stood a company man with the build of a door, who did not remove his hat and did not pretend he had come for anything but what he had come for.

"Madam," Vell said, bowing with a smoothness that had clearly been practiced in front of a mirror until it stopped looking practiced. "Forgive the intrusion. My name is Ambrose Vell. I'm a historian — by trade, by temperament, and, I confess, by affliction." The smile arrived on schedule. "I'm told the late Master Halvard lived out his final years in this vicinity, and that his apprentice — a Wren Eastwick — may still be on this side of the water. I've crossed the river on a fool's errand if that's not so."

"You haven't crossed it on a fool's errand," Maren said. "Wren is here. Come in out of the cold, Mr. Vell, though I'll warn you the hospitality of this house is honest rather than comfortable."

"Honest hospitality," Vell said, stepping over the threshold, "is the only kind worth having. Comfort can be bought. Honesty, these days, must be inherited."

*And there it is,* Wren thought, watching from the shadows of the passage. *The first coin. He pays in aphorisms.*

* * *

He was seated at Maren's table with a cup of nettle tea he praised more warmly than the tea deserved, and the door-man stood outside, and the frost on the window threw its white engraving over everything, and Wren came and sat where they were told to sit — at the end of the table, in the apprentice's place, hands folded, eyes modest. All of it performance now. All of it chosen. That was the strangest part: how quickly obedience became a costume one could put on and take off.

"Master Eastwick." Vell turned the full lamp of his attention upon them, and Wren felt the heat of it and understood why moths died. "You must forgive me. I've spent the last six months in the company's archives in the capital, and Halvard's name appears there — oh, forty times, fifty. Survey plats, municipal commissions, that magnificent river chart of '39 that they still use at the pilot house. A giant of the craft. And to find his last apprentice sitting in a copper orchard like a pearl in a pigsty—" He caught himself, glanced at Maren, and had the grace to color slightly. "My apologies, madam. I speak in drafts."

"You speak in sales," Maren said pleasantly, and poured him more tea.

Vell laughed — a real laugh, Wren thought, or a very good copy of one — and turned back to the business at hand with the air of a man delighted to be interrupted on his way to it.

"The truth is, I'm here on the company's behalf, but I'm here on my own behalf more. I've been engaged to write the centenary history of the Copperbridge Mining and Reduction Company. A great work. A work of record." He said *record* the way a priest says *sacrament*. "And no history of this company can be written without Halvard. His maps are the skeleton of the whole body. Which brings me to an awkward matter, and I'll be awkward about it plainly, because I respect plainness."

He leaned forward.

"The company has had difficulty locating his effects. His town workshop stands empty. His papers, his plates, his correspondence — scattered, presumably, among debts and drawers. If they exist, they are a national treasure sitting in a woodshed. If they were lost, then a chapter of this region's history is simply *gone*, Mr. Eastwick, and I cannot print a chapter that doesn't exist." He spread his gloved hands. "So I've taken the liberty — forgive me, it's what historians do — of asking around. And everyone, everywhere, says the same thing: *ask the apprentice. If anyone has Halvard's papers, it's Wren Eastwick.*"

The silence that followed was the silence of a held breath, and Wren realized with a small cold shock that it was *their* breath being held, and that Vell was watching them hold it, and that he was reading it — but reading it wrong, or so Wren prayed, filing it under grief rather than guilt.

"You must think I've come to take things from you," Vell said, softer now. "I haven't. I've come to *buy* them. Fairly. Generously." He named a figure.

It was an enormous figure. Wren's mind, traitorously, did arithmetic with it: paper by the ream, a proper press, engraving tools that didn't have Halvard's wear on them, a room with a lock. The figure was designed to do exactly that arithmetic. Vell had probably done it himself, sitting in the Ferryman's Rest, working out what a poor apprentice dreams of.

"I'm honored," Wren said. "Truly. But I don't have them."

"No?"

"No. Master Halvard's effects went to his creditors when he died. The workshop was cleared in the spring of '46. What I kept was — personal things. His brushes. A few letters. His spectacles." Wren looked down at their folded hands and found, to their private astonishment, that grief was available on demand; the performance had opened a door and the real feeling walked through it. "I was his apprentice, Mr. Vell, not his archivist. He didn't share his work with me. He barely shared his *opinions*."

It was a good lie. It was a *great* lie, Wren thought, dizzy with it — because it was built on a true foundation. Halvard *had* been secretive. Halvard *had* worked behind a closed door. Every word in the lie was a coin from the true mint, only stamped with a false face.

Vell studied them. And here was the terrible thing, the thing Wren would remember afterward with a kind of horror: he was *good*. Not the door-man's blunt menace, not the company's blunt money — Vell himself had a mind like a surveyor's instrument, and it was taking bearings.

"His spectacles," Vell said, with a small wondering smile. "You know, that's exactly the detail I'd have invented, if I were inventing. The apprentice who kept the spectacles." He sipped his tea. "Tell me — and forgive the question, it's an archivist's disease — when he died, was he working on anything? A last commission? Men like Halvard don't stop. The pencil outlives the man by weeks sometimes. There's always a last plate on the bench."

"There was a river chart," Wren said. "Unfinished. The lower bends. It went with the rest."

"Mm." Vell turned his cup slowly on its saucer, a quarter-turn, another. "The lower bends. Yes. He'd have known those bends better than any living man." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "You were fond of him."

"He was the only family I had."

"Then you know what it is to lose a whole country at once." And for one unguarded instant — Wren could not have sworn to it later, but in the moment it seemed unguarded — something moved behind Vell's pleasant face, some sediment of real loss, real grief, real *knowledge*, before the pleasantness closed over it like water over a dropped stone. "I lost a master too, when I was young. Not a craftsman. A professor. He taught me that history is not what happened; it is what we can prove happened, and everything else is weather." He smiled. "I've made a career of his lesson. I sometimes wonder if he'd be proud or appalled."

"I think," Wren said, "he'd know."

Vell laughed again, and this time the laugh had a wire in it.

* * *

He did not leave immediately. He was too skilled for that; a man who leaves directly after the matter is settled might as well hang a sign on himself. He stayed another half hour, admiring Maren's stillroom, asking after the orchard's history with the idle curiosity of a gentleman naturalist — *and who owned this land before the blight, and were there dwellings here once, and how many souls?* — and Maren answered him in the round, smooth, uncuttable voice of a woman who had spent ten years answering exactly such questions and had learned to give nothing away but soup.

"Company land, once," she said. "Before the blight. Before the clearing. Ask your own archives, Mr. Vell. I'm sure they're thorough."

"Oh, archives are thorough the way cemeteries are thorough," Vell said lightly. "Everything's in them, and nothing speaks."

He rose at last, hat in hand, and turned to Wren with his final coin already shining between his fingers.

"Master Eastwick. A last thought, and then I'll torment you no longer. If anything of Halvard's should surface — a plate in a drawer, a folio behind a shelf, memory itself — my offer stands, and it stands *generously*, because I believe in paying for greatness and I believe you are owed a share of his. More than that." He paused, and let his voice drop into something almost intimate. "There are people — I say this as a friend, though we met an hour ago — there are people who collect Halvard's kind of work for reasons that have nothing to do with history. If papers of his exist, they are worth money to the wrong hands and worth *more* money to mine, and worth most of all to the truth, which is what I serve, in my imperfect fashion. Think of me as a friend you haven't decided on yet."

"I'll do that," Wren said, and found that they could smile, and did.

Vell bowed to Maren, nodded to Wren, and let himself out. Through the frost-furred window Wren watched him cross the frozen yard, unhurried, the door-man falling in behind him, two figures going small and dark along the white path until the bare copper trees took them.

* * *

For a long moment nobody moved. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere in the orchard a crow said one thing, twice.

"He knows," Maren said at last. It was not a question.

"He suspects," Wren said. "There's a difference, and it's the whole width of the river." Their own voice sounded strange to them — older, lower, a voice that had been kept in a drawer and was now out on the table. "He has nothing. If he had anything he'd have brought the door-man inside and a warrant instead of tea. He came to *look* at me. He wanted to see what kind of person holds Halvard's secret." Wren looked down at their hands again, and this time the hands were not steady at all, and the trembling felt like a truth finally allowed. "And I sat there and lied to his face and named a figure for his spectacles and smiled at him, Maren. I smiled. Halvard raised me to be honest the way other men raise children to be clean, and I sat in your kitchen and was dishonest *beautifully*, and I don't know yet whether I've become a liar or something else."

Maren crossed the room and took Wren's face in her two rough hands, the way she might steady a sapling in wind.

"You've become a witness," she said. "And witnesses lie to the wolf. That's not a sin, child. That's the job." She let go, and her eyes went to the window, to the path, to the empty place where Vell had stood. "But hear me. He'll be back. Men like that always come back, because they cannot bear an unfinished account. Next time he won't knock politely. Next time he'll bring paper with a seal on it."

Wren looked at the frost on the glass — all those fine white lines, that patient overnight engraving, already beginning, in the weak sun, to run and blur and disappear.

"Then we'd better be finished before he is," Wren said.

And upstairs, under the floorboard, beneath the loose stone, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp — the folio waited, and the plates, and the names, and the night that no one had yet been permitted to forget.
