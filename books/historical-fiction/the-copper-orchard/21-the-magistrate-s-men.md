## 21. The Magistrate's Men

The ice went out of the Copper on a Tuesday in late March, and the magistrate's men came up the river road on the Thursday after.

Maren heard them before she saw them — the particular rhythm of hooves that did not belong to farmers or drovers, four horses moving in step, unhurried, official. She was in the drying shed with her hands full of coltsfoot, and she stood very still among the hanging bundles and listened to that rhythm come closer, the way she had once listened for the company's riders in another spring, twenty years gone, when listening had been the only defense anyone in the orchard possessed.

But this was not 1837. This was not even last autumn. These men came under a warrant, with papers, and the sound of their arrival was only the sound of a thing long delayed finally arriving — which was its own strange music, neither triumphant nor terrible, just heavy, like a door that had been stuck for a decade swinging open at last.

She brushed the herb dust from her apron and went out to meet them.

There were four: two clerks in good gray wool with ink-stained fingers and the apologetic posture of men who spent their lives writing down what other people suffered; a sergeant with a face like a closed ledger; and a thin young man with a magistrate's seal on a chain around his neck who introduced himself as Under-Investigator Corren and looked, Maren thought, as though he had expected to find a den of revolutionaries and had instead found a woman holding coltsfoot.

"Mistress Nystrom?" he said.

"Herbalist," she said. "Midwife. Orchard resident since before you were weaned, I'd guess. You'll want Piet Halvard's house — the big one by the south wall. And you'll want tea, because what you've ridden here to hear will take all day and go better warm."

Corren blinked. Behind him, one of the clerks made a small noise that might have been a laugh suppressed by discipline.

They took the tea. They took the whole day. And Maren gave them what they had ridden through the mud to collect: names, dates, sums, the shape of the night in October 1837 when the Copperbridge Mining Company had decided that its model village at the orchard was worth more empty than inhabited, and had emptied it.

She told it plainly. That was the discipline she had learned from Wren, over those winter weeks of engraving and proofing — that plainness was the hardest register and the truest, that horror spoken without ornament could not be dismissed as art. She told them about the evictions that began as notices and ended as fire. About old Tomas Vask whose leg had been broken by a bailiff's bar and who had died of the fever in a barn because no doctor would cross the company to treat him. About the eleven who had stood in the gate of the counting-house to refuse the final order, and about what the company's men had done to them, and how the record of it had been bought, page by page, witness by witness, until the orchard existed nowhere but in the memories of the people the company called squatters on their own ground.

Corren wrote it all down. His hand shook twice — once at Tomas Vask, once at the name of the foreman who had given the order at the gate — and Maren watched the shaking and understood that this young man had come believing in files and was leaving carrying something heavier than files.

"You understand," he said at last, carefully, "that justice in these matters is — that the court will move as the court moves."

"I have waited eighteen years," Maren said. "I can wait out your court. But I'll tell you what I won't wait out, Under-Investigator: I won't wait out being treated as a suspect in my own testimony. We've had enough of that arithmetic from the company."

He had the grace to look down at his notes. "No one here is a suspect," he said. "You are witnesses. That is — that is why you're summoned, not arrested. There's a difference. I know it may not feel like one."

It didn't. It felt like being handled again, like being a document in someone else's case. But Maren poured him a second cup of tea anyway, because the difference was real even if it was thin, and thin things were still things, and she had learned in the orchard to be grateful for thin things.

* * *

The arrests began the following week, and the town talked of nothing else.

Maren went into Copperbridge on market day and heard it everywhere — in the fish queue, at the cooper's, over the fence of the tannery yard where women traded news the way they traded yeast. Foreman Aldous Rennick, taken from his own doorstep in his nightshirt, so shocked he could barely dress himself. The company's paymaster, Halloran, gone quiet from his office in irons, his ledgers carried out in crates — the same ledgers, Maren knew, whose missing pages Halvard had spent his last years reconstructing from memory and marginalia. Two bailiffs. A clerk of the county court who had lost certain documents in 1838 and gained a house in town. Even old Governor Strayle himself, the company's founding director, summoned — not yet arrested, but summoned — to answer questions in the capital, his portrait already taken down from the assay office wall by persons unknown, leaving a paler rectangle on the paneling that everyone in Copperbridge now walked past and looked at and said nothing about, loudly.

"They're saying it's the atlas," the cooper's wife told her, leaning close across a basket of early greens. "The newspaper. My sister's boy works the press in Harrowgate — two towns over, where your young man took the sheets? He says they printed three thousand copies and sold out in a day, and the magistrate's clerk read it aloud in the assize hall with the whole room standing. He says there's a name in the margins of every plate. *Every* plate, mistress. Like a psalm."

Maren bought her greens and thanked her and walked home along the river with her heart doing something complicated in her chest — pride and grief and an old, stubborn anger, all braided together, none of them clean.

Because this was the truth of it, the truth she had promised herself she would not flinch from: the printing had not raised the dead. Tomas Vask was still dead. The eleven at the gate were still dead. Ilse's childhood was still stolen, Sef's mother still a name on a plate instead of a voice in a kitchen, Piet still carrying his winter's betrayal like a stone he had learned to walk bent beneath. No number of copies sold out in a day would give any of that back. Justice, arriving now, could only ever be a monument built beside a grave — and monuments, however necessary, however hard-won, do not warm the ground they stand on.

And yet.

She stopped at the riverbank, where the water ran high and brown and loud with melt, carrying the last of the ice downstream in grinding white rafts, and she let herself feel the other truth too, the one underneath: that it had *worked*. That the plates Wren had cut by lamplight, that Ilse's voice taken down verbatim in a cold room while the whole family held her like a net, that Sef's frozen crossing and Piet's confession mid-river and the proof sheets run across rotting ice while the Copper tried to drown them — that all of it had crossed the distance between this forgotten bend of river and the halls where power kept its records, and had *changed the records*. History happened to everyone, everywhere — Halvard had believed that, had died believing it — and for eighteen years the history that had happened to the orchard had been whatever the company paid to have written. Now it was written otherwise. Now it was written in three thousand copies, in a margin like a psalm.

That was not nothing. Maren had buried too much to call anything nothing.

* * *

The summons came for each of them by name, delivered by a bored constable who read them off a list: Maren Nystrom, Piet Halvard, Sef and Ilse Vask-Halvard, Wren Eastwick — to appear before the investigating commission sitting at the courthouse in Copperbridge, to give evidence touching the matter of the Copperbridge Mining Company and the events of October 1837 and thereafter.

Piet read his summons four times and then set it down on the kitchen table with hands that were not quite steady.

"Eighteen years I kept my mouth shut," he said. "Kept it shut when they cleared the village, kept it shut when Halvard started asking his questions, kept it shut last winter right up until the middle of a frozen river. And now they summon me like I'm — like I'm part of it all along." He laughed, short and bitter. "Maybe I am. Maybe that's what the paper says I am."

"The paper says you're a witness," Ilse said. She sat across from him, mending by the window, and did not look up from her needle. "I've read it. Everyone's read it. It says the man who turned back mid-river to mislead Vell is named in the testimony as having done exactly that. Sef wrote it down the night it happened. It's in the record, Piet. Your worst winter is in the record, and the record is kind to you, because the record is only interested in what's true."

Piet looked at her then, this woman who had been a child at the gate in 1837 and had spoken the whole of it aloud in January with her voice never breaking more than a river breaks, and something in his face eased and broke at once.

"And if they ask me what I offered Vell?" he asked quietly. "For the folio. For amnesty. If they ask me straight?"

"Then you tell them straight," Maren said. "That's the whole price of the record, Piet. It doesn't keep secrets, not even ours. That's what makes it worth trusting."

He nodded slowly, and Maren watched him fold the summons along its crease, once, precisely, the way a man folds a thing he intends to carry against his body for a long time.

* * *

Wren gave their evidence on the third day of the commission's sitting, and Maren went to hear it, taking a seat at the back of the courtroom among farmwives and clerks and a scattering of faces she recognized from the orchard's cleared days — old people come down out of the hills to watch, drawn by something older than curiosity.

Wren stood in the witness box in their good coat with Halvard's folio on the table before them, open, and Maren watched the apprentice who had arrived in the orchard in autumn half-broken by grief become, over the course of a single morning, something else entirely. Wren spoke of Master Halvard's method — the coded marginalia, the payments recorded in cipher, the way he had dated everything twice, once in ink and once in a private notation, so that no single forgery could erase him. Spoke of the sealed folio found beneath the drafting table, maps of places Halvard had never visited, dated after his death, the work continued by hands he had trained before he died. Spoke of the fire in the map room, and of the plates that survived because an apprentice had learned, somewhere along the way, to move the originals first.

And then the commission's chairman, a dry man with a bishop's voice, asked the question Maren had been waiting for: "Why? Why did you finish it? You might have burned the folio and lived quietly. Many would have."

The room went very still. Wren looked out over the benches — Maren was almost sure their eyes found hers — and answered.

"Because Master Halvard taught me that a map is a promise," Wren said. "That whoever draws the ground decides what exists on it. For eighteen years the company drew the ground, and the orchard didn't exist on it, and so what happened here didn't happen anywhere. Someone had to draw it otherwise. I was the one holding the pen. That's the whole of it. Anyone holding the pen should have done the same."

Maren sat with her hands folded in her lap and did not trust herself to move. Beside her, an old woman she half-recognized — Janna Ostrer, who had been a girl at the clearing, who had not spoken of it in eighteen years — was crying silently, without shame, into a square of linen, and around her other old faces were wet, and the chairman's clerk had stopped writing for a moment and was looking out the window at the river.

* * *

Ambrose Vell left Copperbridge on a bright cold morning in the second week of April.

Maren made a point of seeing it. She told herself it was not pettiness; she mostly believed herself. She stood at the corner by the ferry landing with a basket of nothing in particular and watched the company's historian supervise the loading of his trunks onto the morning packet — the confiscated "official" plates among them, crated and labeled, the version of history he had been paid to write riding east under seal to a publisher who would never know how nearly it had been the only version.

He had not been arrested. That was the bitter marrow of it, and everyone in town chewed on it all week. Vell had committed no massacre, signed no eviction order, broken no bones. He had only arranged the forgetting — and arranging the forgetting, the law of 1847 had not yet learned to name as a crime. The commission wanted his correspondence. He had given them a portion of it, elegantly selected, and retained counsel, and departed with his collar crisp and his hat at the correct angle, diminished but unbowed, a man who had lost a battle and remained entirely convinced of the war.

He saw her at the corner. Of course he did; he missed nothing, that was the whole of his profession. He paused on the gangplank, and for a moment the charm came up in his face like a lamp behind glass — and then, unexpectedly, it went out again, and what was underneath was tired.

"Mistress Nystrom," he said, inclining his head. "I listened to the recording of Ilse Vask's testimony. All of it. Twice." He said it quietly, so that only she could hear over the water. "I want you to know that. Not as apology — I'm past pretending I have one to offer. As information. There is a man under the historian, and the man heard her voice, and the man has not slept properly since January. Whether that man changes anything, anywhere, ever —" he shrugged, a small precise gesture, "— is beyond my professional guarantee. But he exists. I thought you should have that in your ledger too."

"We keep honest books," Maren said. "Even of enemies. Especially of enemies."

"I know," Vell said. "It's what defeated me." And he touched his hat brim, and boarded, and the packet pulled away eastward with the current, growing small, carrying its crates of the other history toward a world that had already, by hours and by copies, been told better.

Maren stood a long time after the boat was gone, watching the water.

* * *

Spring came on properly after that, the way it does in the valley — all at once, as if it had been waiting politely for the courts to sit. The orchard trees, the ones that had given the place its name before the company ever dug its first shaft, broke into blossom along the south terraces, white and reckless, and the whole settlement smelled of it, and Sef rigged a line between two trees and hung the wash, and Ilse planted her seedbeds with the fierce competence of a woman reclaiming ground row by row, and Piet — Piet walked the rows every evening with his hands behind his back like a man inspecting a garrison, except that what he was inspecting, Maren thought, was his own forgiveness, acre by acre, and finding it slow to take root, and patient with its slowness.

The commission sat through April and into May. Officers were committed for trial. Documents were subpoenaed from three counties. Old witnesses came down out of the hills, some of whom Maren had not seen since the clearing, and gave their evidence in voices that cracked and steadied and cracked again, and each time a voice cracked, the clerk wrote it down, and the writing-down was the whole miracle, the small daily resurrection of the record.

And the summons came again — not for evidence this time, but for trial, in the autumn, in the capital, where the company's directors would answer for 1837 with the whole country reading over the court's shoulder. The orchard folk would travel. The orchard folk would testify. The orchard folk would stand in a room far from home and say the names aloud one more time, and this time the names would be entered into a book no one could buy.

Maren read the new summons standing in her doorway with the blossom coming down around her like weather, and she felt the bittersweetness of it settle into her bones and make itself at home, because she was old enough to know that this was what vindication actually tasted like — not sweet, not clean, but real, like bread baked with grief in the flour. The dead would stay dead. The guilty would be punished partially, slowly, some of them hardly at all. Vell would write his book somewhere else, softened, and some of it would even be believed.

But the record stood. Three thousand copies, and the plates, and the testimonies verbatim, and a margin like a psalm. History happened to everyone, everywhere — and now, at last, everyone's history was written down.

She pinned the summons inside the door of her drying shed, next to the bunches of coltsfoot, and went to tell the others.

Inside the kitchen, Wren was already at the table with the atlas open, a fresh sheet of paper squared before them, pen uncapped — and Maren saw at once, from the set of their shoulders, that they had received their own letter too, and that it was not from the commission.
