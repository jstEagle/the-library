## 3. What the Satchel Holds

The satchel slept beneath his hammock like a sleeping dog he could not trust.

Silas had learned, in eleven days aboard the *Meridian*, to measure time in small repairs. A frayed halyard here, a seized block there, a hinge on the surgeon's medicine chest that had squeaked until he filed it smooth and Madame Voss had looked at him with an expression that might have been gratitude or might have been indigestion — with her, the two were difficult to distinguish. He kept his hands busy because busy hands asked no questions, and because a man who mended things was furniture, was ballast, was nothing worth counting.

But at night, when the watch changed and the lanterns swung low, his hands went still, and the satchel spoke to him from the deck below his hammock. It did not speak in words. It spoke in weight. Four pounds of oilcloth and wax and paper, and underneath them, a colony.

He had not opened it. That was the discipline of it, the whole architecture of the thing Edmund had built: a courier who did not know what he carried could not be made to tell it. *You are a ghost, Silas,* his brother had said, pressing the satchel into his hands with fingers already going gray. *Ghosts don't read. Ghosts don't speak. They pass through walls and are gone.*

Edmund had been dying when he said it. That was the obscenity of it — that the words had come out of a man whose blood was filling his lungs, who gripped Silas's wrist with the last of his strength, and whose eyes were not pleading but *instructing*. Even at the end, Edmund Lockridge had never once asked his brother for anything. He had only ever given orders wrapped in love.

* * *

Port Royal, six weeks past. The rain had come down in ropes, and the safehouse smelled of wet plaster and laudanum.

Silas had found Edmund the way one finds a thing long feared: too easily. A name whispered by a smuggler, a door in a wall of identical doors, and then his brother — thinner than memory, coughing the deep wet cough that Silas had heard once before, the summer their father died, as though death were a family heirloom passed hand to hand along the line of Lockridge men.

"You look well," Edmund said, and laughed, and the laugh cost him. "That's a cruelty, isn't it. I look like a drowned letter."

"What are you doing here?" Silas had asked, though he'd known. You always knew, with Edmund. There was no version of any story in which Edmund occupied the middle ground. "They say you took the King's commission."

"They say a great many things." He waved Silas into a chair with the economy of a man conserving breath. On the table between them lay the satchel, plain as bread, its wax seals unbroken, its oilcloth dry despite the season. "I'm dying, Silas. The physician gives me a month, and the physician is an optimist. Which leaves me a problem of logistics."

"Logistics."

"There is a document." Edmund's hand came to rest on the satchel the way a man rests his hand on a child's head. "Surrender terms. Articles of capitulation for the colonial assembly — the rebels, if you like that word better. Names, positions, the fortifications they hold, the terms under which they'll lay down arms and receive pardon. Or not receive it. There are clauses." His mouth did something that was almost a smile. "There are always clauses."

"And you want me to carry it."

"I want you to *be* it. A ghost. No name in the ship's books, no letters of passage, no commission. You board at Calloway, you ride her to the governor's station, you walk off with the tide and vanish. No one will ever know you existed. Isn't that what you've always wanted? To be no one?" He coughed then, long and terrible, and Silas sat very still and hated himself for the fear that moved through him — not fear of losing Edmund, but fear of the silence after, the silence where the only voice that had ever told him who to be would stop.

"Why me?"

"Because you're good at disappearing. Because you owe me." Edmund said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "And because I trust no one else to keep it sealed. Do you understand what's in there, Silas? If those terms reach the governor, the war ends. The colony kneels, the assembly hangs or scatters, and ten thousand people go back to being subjects instead of corpses. Order. Peace. *Mercy.*" He said the word like a man laying a card face up. "Or it stays here, and the fighting goes on, and everyone in that ledger dies anyway — slower, and louder, and in pieces. I'm not asking you to kill anyone. I'm asking you to let a piece of paper do what paper does best: end things."

Silas had looked at the satchel for a long time. Rain on the shutters. The candle guttering.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I'll find someone else, and you'll spend the rest of your life wondering whether the man who carried it was half as careful as you'd have been." Edmund leaned back. "That's not a threat, brother. It's arithmetic."

Arithmetic. Everyone in this war was doing arithmetic. Silas had taken the satchel because refusing meant staying, and staying meant watching Edmund die by inches in a wet room, and he had never learned how to be present for a thing he could not fix.

He had kissed his brother's forehead. Edmund's skin had been cool as coin.

"Ghosts don't read," Edmund said against his hair. "Promise me."

"I promise," Silas had lied, gently, the way you lie to the dying.

* * *

Now, eleven days out of Calloway, lying in his hammock with the ship breathing around him — the creak of her copper-sheathed hull working against the sea, the snore of Tam in the next berth, Rill talking in his sleep about a dog he must have owned once, some lost animal invoked nightly like a prayer — Silas turned the promise over and over, and it wore thinner every night.

He had not opened the satchel. But he had begun, without quite deciding to, to learn everything else.

He knew that Bosun Adeyemi had been pressed aboard a King's ship fifteen years ago and had never signed so much as his own name since, keeping his accounts in knots and notches. He knew Madame Voss had left her practice in the colonies under circumstances involving a magistrate's son and a grave that was either empty or ought to have been. He knew Tam owed money to men who collected in fingers, and that Rill was running from a conscription notice with his mother's handwriting on the back of it, and that Wren —

Wren. Thirteen years old, maybe fourteen, all elbows and quick eyes, who had appointed himself Silas's shadow within three days of his boarding. Who had watched him splice a line and then done it better by the following week, because that was Wren's whole method of loving things: he studied them until he could reproduce them. Who had asked Silas, on the fourth night, with the terrible directness of children, *"Where's home, then?"*

And Silas had opened his mouth to give the answer he'd given customs men and harbormasters for six years — *nowhere, wherever the work is* — and found that the lie wouldn't come out. Not to this boy. So he'd said, "I haven't got one yet," and Wren had nodded solemnly, as though this were a perfectly ordinary condition, and said, "Me neither. We'll manage," and gone back to coiling line.

*We'll manage.* As if it were settled. As if the two of them were a fact now, a small nation of two, requiring no papers.

That was the trouble. That was the rot in the design. Edmund had built a machine with one moving part — a ghost, a wall, a sealed packet — and had failed to account for the single variable that had ruined every plan Silas had ever made: he could be *taught to care*. It took him weeks, usually. This crew had managed it in days. They fed him without asking why he ate alone at first. They taught him the ship's particular curses, her moods, the way she handled heavy weather with a stubbornness that Adeyemi called *her mother's temper*. They saved him a place without ever saying they'd saved it, and a place, Silas was learning, was the seed of a home.

And somewhere south of them, past the trade winds, sat a governor's desk waiting for four pounds of oilcloth and wax, and under that wax, clauses. Clauses that would decide whether Rill's conscription notice became a noose, whether Tam's debts were forgiven or collected in full, whether a boy who mended rigging better than anyone aboard would grow up free or numbered — counted, catalogued, conscripted. The dispatches weren't paper. They were the future, folded twice and tied with string, and Silas carried it against his heart like a stone he'd agreed to swallow.

*Ghosts don't care,* he thought. *That's the other half of the discipline. Nobody told me because nobody thought it needed saying.*

He rolled over in the hammock. Below him, the satchel waited. Above, through the hatch, a slice of sky with stars in it, the same stars that hung over Port Royal and over the colony and over every road between, indifferent as arithmetic.

One question had begun to follow him through the days, quiet as Quill's pen scratch, patient as water finding a crack:

*What is in the satchel worth more than what is on the ship?*

He did not have an answer. He suspected, with a dread that felt almost like certainty, that he was about to get one — because tomorrow the *Meridian* would make landfall at the mid-voyage port, and ports meant officials, and officials meant questions, and somewhere in the world a census taker named Mr. Quill was still writing things down.
