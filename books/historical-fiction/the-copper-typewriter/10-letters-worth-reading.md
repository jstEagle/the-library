## 10. Letters Worth Reading

The bridge opened on the third of April, and the first thing to cross it was a cartload of lambs, bleating like the whole world had been held underwater all winter and had finally come up for air.

I stood at the north end with my hands in my pockets and watched them pass, and I will confess something here that I have confessed to no one else: I cried. Not much. A little. Enough that the lambs' driver, a boy of maybe fourteen, looked at me with the frank alarm of youth and asked if I was ill.

"Spring," I said.

He nodded as though that explained everything, which perhaps it did.

The ice on the river had gone rotten weeks before, dark and honeycombed, and then one morning it was simply gone, as if the water had decided to forgive us. The ferry landing below the bridge showed its pilings again. The road to town, which had been a rumor under snow since November, reappeared — rutted, brown, entirely ordinary. And along it, that morning, came the mail boat's tender ahead of schedule, because there is nothing so punctual as news that everyone has been waiting for.

Halvard Nilsen walked free on the second of April.

* * *

I read about it in the paper before Maren did, which felt strange and right at once. Twenty years I had been the one who knew things first — who steamed the envelopes, who read the words before their intended reader, who decided what lived and what burned. Now the newspaper sat open on the kitchen table between us, and we read it together, aloud, in turns, like children sharing a book.

Maren read the sentence about the mill foreman twice. *Released after nineteen years following new evidence in the death of a worker at the Brandt Mill, 1893.* Her voice caught on *nineteen*. Nineteen years Halvard Nilsen had spent behind walls for a death Anders caused and letters buried, and I watched my mistress — no, I will not call her that anymore; I watched my friend — press her thumb flat against the page as if she could hold the man's lost years down like specimens in a book.

"He'll be old," she said. "He was thirty when they took him."

"Fifty," I said. "Same as you."

"Yes." She folded the paper with great care, the way she once folded the letters I brought her sealed and left her to open alone. That habit was finished now. We had agreed on it in March, in the worst hour of our lives, standing over the scorched corner of a carpet where Anders had tried to feed twenty years of truth into the stove: no more sealed paper between us. If a letter came, it came open. If a secret existed, it existed in daylight or not at all.

It has cost her. I want to be honest about that, because the papers have not been. They print her name beside his — *suffragist leader implicated in cover-up* — and they print it with a certain relish, the relish of people who enjoy watching tall figures fall. The movement has not cast her out, exactly. It has done something worse. It has made her careful. Committees that once begged her to speak now write to ask whether her presence might prove *distracting*. She answers every letter herself, in her own hand, unflinching, and I watch her do it and understand that this too is a kind of courage — the smaller, harder kind, the kind that doesn't get banners.

Anders waits for trial in the county jail. He asked to see her once, through the visiting grate, and she went, and she came back and told me only this: "He said he'd done it all for the family. I said the family never asked him to." Then she washed her face and wrote her statement for the prosecutors, eleven pages, every word true.

* * *

Petra's baby came in the last week of March, a girl, born safely in the house because the bridge was still shut and there was nowhere else. Petra named her Ilse, which made the real Ilse — recovered now, her shoulder healed crooked but working, her laugh restored to its full unreasonable volume — go red as a brick and pretend to find something urgent to do in the scullery.

Ilse stayed through the winter, of course. The berth on the mail boat went unused after all; the constable, faced with a household of women who would not yield an inch and a warrant he could not serve across closed ice, contented himself with taking statements in February, when a thaw gave him three days of passage. By then Ilse's testimony was already in the hands of the prosecutor, already typed — I typed it myself, on the copper machine, and there is a justice in that I cannot fully explain even to myself. The same keys that struck the lie in 1893 struck the truth in 1914. The machine does not choose. It only remembers what fingers tell it.

The foreman's release came from those pages. Ilse had seen everything from the loft above the mill floor, a child of nine hidden among the flour sacks: the jammed gate, the belt that should have been stopped, Anders's face in the aftermath, and later — later, the letters, dictated in her father's study while she listened through the door, the copper typewriter clacking out a version of events that put the blame on a dead man's carelessness and a living man's neglect. She had spent thirty years collecting corroboration the way other people collect grievances: patiently, methodically, against the day someone would listen.

Someone finally listened. It took a wounded organizer stranded by a bridge, a driver who broke a twenty-year silence, and a leader who chose to fall rather than stand on a lie. I do not flatter myself that any of us were noble. We were cornered, all of us, and corners make honest people of the best of us.

But I will say this: when the verdict came, Ilse stood in our kitchen and said, "You could have kept me in the cellar until spring and put me on that boat and let the whole thing rot another twenty years. Nobody would ever have known."

"I would have known," Maren said.

That was all. But Ilse took her hand, and Maren let her, and I turned away to mind the kettle because some moments are not meant to be witnessed by employees, and I am not an employee anymore either, though we have not yet found the word for what I am.

* * *

On a bright cold morning in mid-April, Maren came to the back room — my room, the room where I had spent two decades bent over envelopes with the kettle's steam, the room with the copper typewriter on its table like an idol that had finally been knocked off its altar and turned out to be only a machine.

She carried a shoebox. Inside it were letters — her own letters, written across forty years, to organizers and rivals and ministers and her brother, the whole correspondence of a life given to a cause. Some of them I had read without her knowledge. Some I had destroyed without her knowledge. She knew that now. We had been through all of it, every ash and every confession, in the long terrible honesty of March.

"Tamsin," she said. "Read them to me."

I looked up. "Read them—"

"Aloud. All of them. The ones I sent, the ones I received. I want to hear my own life in someone else's voice. I want to know what it sounds like from outside." She set the box on the table beside the typewriter. "You've read everything I've ever written anyway. Half of it before I did. You may as well have the rest honestly."

"And then?"

"Then we decide what goes to the archive, what goes to the biographers — there will be biographers, God help us — and what goes in the stove. But we decide together, and we decide in the open, and afterward—" she paused, and for a moment she was not the fallen leader or the sister of a accused man but simply a woman of fifty with tired eyes, "—afterward, I want you to drive me somewhere."

"Where?"

"The coast. I haven't seen the sea since September. I'm told it's still there." Almost a smile. "You'll drive. I'll sit. We'll take the long road, the one past the mill, and I'll look at it straight on for the first time in twenty years, and then we'll keep driving."

I took the first letter out of the box. Unsealed, of course. Everything unsealed now. My hands, which had learned every trick of steam and blade and silence, felt strangely unemployed — and strangely light, the way a shoulder feels after a weight it had forgotten it was carrying is finally set down.

"'My dear Fru Holm,'" I began, and my voice filled the back room where so many secrets had been born and buried, "'I write to you tonight with a proposal that will seem madness...'"

Outside, the lambs were still crossing the bridge, one cart after another, the whole spring moving south toward town. Inside, I read, and Maren listened, and the copper typewriter sat quiet between us — not forgotten, not feared. Just a machine, waiting to be told the truth.

We got through eleven letters before noon. At the twelfth, Maren raised her hand.

"That one," she said quietly. "That one goes in the stove. But this time, Tamsin — this time I burn it myself, and you watch me do it."

I handed her the page, and she held it to the light a moment, reading her own young words one last time — and then, together, we walked to the fire.
