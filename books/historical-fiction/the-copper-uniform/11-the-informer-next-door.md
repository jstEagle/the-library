## 11. The Informer Next Door

Janek had learned to measure danger in small sounds.

In the hills above the city, where he and Tomas had spent that last bitter winter, danger announced itself plainly—a snapped branch, a dog barking at nothing, the sudden absence of birdsong. There you could hear it coming. Here, in the city, danger wore slippers. It shuffled past windows. It stopped to light a pipe. It was a boy of perhaps twelve, standing in the doorway of the chandler's shop across the lane, ostensibly sweeping the step, actually doing nothing of the kind.

Janek saw him from the loading door, where he'd gone to shake the dust from his coat before anyone could ask why a stranger's coat carried the smell of the workshop—ink and lamp oil and the particular metallic sweetness of hot type metal. The boy swept the same square foot of flagstone three times. Then he looked up. Not at the street. At the window of the composing room, where a candle burned behind the curtain Freya had hung for exactly this purpose.

The boy looked away too quickly when their eyes nearly met.

Janek stayed in the shadow of the doorway and did not move for a long time. His ribs ached where the soldiers' boots had found him in autumn—not Tomas's arrest, but his own, smaller, forgotten beating, the one nobody wrote pamphlets about—and the ache sharpened whenever he stood still too long, as if his body objected to idleness more than pain. He counted. The boy swept another four strokes, gave up the pretense entirely, leaned on his broom, and watched the window until a woman called him inside from somewhere deep in the chandler's shop.

*The Vondráček boy,* Janek thought. *The chandler's nephew.*

He knew the name because Alois Kavanagh knew every name on the lane the way other men knew psalms. Karel Vondráček, the chandler, was a careful man, a man who paid his taxes early and crossed himself when the garrison band passed and had not, in twenty years, said one word louder than necessary within his own walls. But careful men kept careless nephews, and careless nephews were the raw material from which informers were made. A few kreuzers. A kind word from a sergeant. The delicious, terrible feeling of mattering.

Janek went back inside and barred the loading door behind him.

* * *

Freya was at the stone, setting type by feel more than sight, her fingers moving through the case with a fluency that made something tighten in his chest. She didn't look up when he entered. "You're back late. I've finished the second page. Listen—" and she read aloud, her voice low and quick, stumbling only once over a phrase she'd clearly rewritten herself: *"A man who may not speak is not silent; he is being silenced, and these are different conditions, requiring different remedies."*

"It's good," Janek said. "It's better than good."

"Then say so with your face and not like a priest giving absolution." She set the stick down and finally looked at him, and whatever she saw there made her stop smiling. "What is it?"

"The boy across the lane. The chandler's nephew."

She was quiet a moment. "Karel's sister's son. Miloš." She said the name the way one identifies a familiar dog. "He sweeps their step every morning. He's swept it since I was a girl."

"He swept it this afternoon for an hour without moving two feet from your window. And then he watched the candlelight until someone called him in."

"You can't know he was watching us specifically. A boy looks at windows. Windows are interesting."

"A boy who is interested looks once," Janek said. "A boy who has been asked to look counts. He was counting."

Freya turned toward the curtained window as though she could see through the cloth, through the glass, through the grey evening and the lane and the boy's careful face. When she spoke again her voice had changed—the playfulness drained out of it, leaving something harder underneath. "My father will say we stop."

"I know."

"And what do you say?"

Janek took off his coat and hung it on the peg by the door, buying himself the seconds to answer honestly. This was the thing about Freya Kavanagh that had unsettled him from the first night, when he'd collapsed bleeding at her loading door with a dead man's satchel in his hands: she did not ask questions to be comforted. She asked them to be answered.

"I say we don't stop," he said. "I say we move."

* * *

They went down to the cellar while Alois was still upstairs, doing the slow, deliberate arithmetic of his ledger with the door open, performing normalcy for any ear that might be pressed to the wall. Janek had scouted the cellar on his second day in the house—old habit, the habit that had kept him alive in the hills—and had noted what it offered: no windows, thick walls older than the empire itself, a coal chute that opened onto the alley behind the tanner's yard, and enough ambient noise from the cistern pump to swallow the small clicks of a composing stick.

Now he carried down the type cases two at a time while Freya followed with candles, the galley, the stone, and the manuscript wrapped in oilcloth against damp.

"It smells like my grandmother's house," Freya said, looking around at the vaulted dark. "Root vegetables and old stone."

"Your grandmother kept revolutionaries in her cellar?"

"My grandmother kept everything in her cellar. I think she believed the Austrians would take the house but not the sauerkraut." She set the candle on a barrel head and the flame steadied, throwing their shadows huge up the curved wall. For a moment neither of them moved. The cellar held its breath around them, and Janek felt the strangeness of what they were doing settle over the space: they were building a printing house underground, in the dark, beneath the feet of a city that was learning how to listen.

"We'll need more light," he said. "Two candles minimum, shielded. And we work in shifts—if the house is searched, there should never be a moment when both of us are down here with ink on our hands."

"You've done this before." It wasn't quite a question.

"Tomas and I printed eleven hundred copies of a student address in Prague in a cellar half this size. We were never caught. We were only ever betrayed." He heard his own voice go flat and made himself continue. "There's a difference, and it matters. Locks keep out searches. Only silence keeps out betrayal."

Freya sat down on the barrel beside the candle. In the low light her face was all planes and shadows, and he was struck again by how much she resembled her father and how completely, in some essential way, she did not. "Miloš Vondráček," she said slowly. "I used to give him bread ends off the back step when he was small. He'd stand there in winter with his hands cracked from the cold and I'd hand him yesterday's loaf like the blessed Virgin distributing grace, and now you're telling me he's selling us."

"I'm telling you he might be. Or he might simply have been asked to notice things, by someone who noticed him noticing." Janek crouched to open the first type case, checking the compartments, running a finger along the rows of letters like a man checking a rosary. "In my experience, most informers don't begin as villains. They begin as people who want to be seen. The police understand that better than we do. They don't recruit the loyal; they recruit the lonely."

"That's almost kind."

"It's not kind. It's tactical. If you understand what a man wants, you understand what he'll trade." He glanced up at her. "You should sleep upstairs tonight. Your father expects you at table. Absence is evidence."

"So is presence, lately." But she rose, brushing the damp from her skirt, and paused at the foot of the stairs. The candlelight caught her from below, and for a moment she looked down at him with an expression he couldn't name—something between gratitude and grief and a third thing he was afraid to name correctly. "Janek. If they come for us—for the shop—I want you to take the manuscript and go. Out the chute, over the tanner's wall, gone. Don't argue. Don't be noble. The words matter more than either of us and you're the only one who knows the whole text by heart."

"I don't know it by heart."

"You do. You recited page three in your sleep two nights ago. All of it. Including the part where Tomas argues with himself in the margin notes." Her mouth twitched. "You talk in your sleep like a man defending a dissertation."

Heat climbed his neck, and he was grateful for the dark. "Go up," he managed. "Your father will wonder."

She went. But at the top of the stairs she stopped once more, silhouetted against the thin line of lamplight from the kitchen, and said, softly, "Goodnight, Janek," in a voice that made the two words sound like a promise neither of them had agreed to yet.

* * *

Over the following days the cellar became a country of its own.

They worked in the hours after midnight, when even the garrison patrols grew bored and the lane outside slept under a skin of frost. Freya came down with candles hidden in a market basket under cabbage leaves; Janek had the press frame disassembled and reassembled below within three nights, working by touch, cursing the low ceiling that forced him to print seated. The rhythm of the work built its own intimacy: her voice reading copy in the dark, his hands correcting, the soft percussion of type meeting stick, the smell of ink rising in the cold air like a second breath.

And always, above them, the boy.

Janek made a study of Miloš Vondráček the way he had once made a study of patrol routes in the hills. The boy swept the step each morning. On Tuesday he lingered. On Wednesday he was joined by a lanky youth Janek didn't recognize, and the two of them stood talking for a quarter hour with their faces angled, just slightly, toward the Kavanagh loading door. On Thursday the boy did not appear at all—which frightened Janek more than anything, because absence meant the watching had been handed upward, delegated, professionalized. On Friday morning a sergeant of the civic guard walked the length of the lane at a pace too slow for any errand, glancing at doorways, counting chimneys, and stopped for a full minute in front of the chandler's shop, where Miloš stood holding his broom like a soldier presenting arms.

That afternoon Janek told Freya, in the cellar, with the press between them like a table at which they negotiated.

"They know something is happening in this street. They don't know what yet. That's the only mercy left to us."

"Then we finish faster." She was already reaching for the galley of page four.

"Or we stop and let it cool."

"No." The word came out flat and immediate, and then she seemed to surprise herself with it, because she set the galley down and pressed her palms flat against the cold stone of the press bed, as if steadying herself against her own certainty. "No. I'm sorry. I know what stopping means. Stopping means it goes back in the satchel, and the satchel goes back under the floorboards, and in ten years I open the floorboards and find it and spend the rest of my life knowing I was the girl who almost." She looked up at him. "Tomas died carrying this. You crossed mountains carrying this. I am not going to let a twelve-year-old with a broom decide how it ends."

Janek looked at her for a long moment. Somewhere overhead, footsteps crossed the kitchen—Alois, pacing, the way he had begun to pace at night, a man walking the perimeter of his own conscience.

"You sound like him," Janek said quietly.

"Don't." Her voice cracked on it, just barely. "Everyone says that. My father says it when he wants me to be careful. You say it when—"

"When I mean it as a compliment," he said. "Which apparently makes me worse."

Something loosened in her face then—the first real smile he'd seen from her in days, tired and crooked and real. She picked the galley back up. "Page four. Read me the next paragraph. And if you fall asleep mid-sentence again, I'm adding your marginalia to the pamphlet."

* * *

It happened on Sunday, the way disasters prefer.

Sunday was the one day the workshop legitimately rested, the one day smoke from the chimney meant cooking rather than printing, the one day Alois permitted himself brandy and the newspaper and the long, elaborate pretense that his household was an ordinary household. Janek was upstairs helping Freya carry the dinner things when he saw it through the front window: Miloš Vondráček crossing the lane, not sweeping, not lingering, but walking directly, purposefully, toward the corner where the civic guard kept its post by the gate.

The boy stopped at the guard post. Spoke to the sentry. The sentry disappeared inside; returned with a corporal; the corporal listened, looked down the lane at the Kavanagh facade, and nodded.

"Freya." Janek kept his voice level, kept his hands moving, stacking plates as though the world were ordinary. "Don't look up. Don't turn around. The boy is at the guard post. He's pointing."

Her hands did not falter. He would remember that afterward—how her hands did not falter, how she set down the last plate with a small ceramic click and said, "How long do we have?"

"An hour. Maybe less. Maybe they wait for nightfall, maybe they don't."

"Then we move everything now. While he's still standing there deciding whether he's done a great thing or a terrible one." She untied her apron with steady fingers. "Father is at the tavern with the guild men. Good. Let him come home to a miracle instead of a decision."

They worked like people possessed, which was its own kind of prayer. The type cases went into the flour chests. The galley went into the false bottom of the paper cabinet. The press—too heavy, too slow—they covered with sacking and stacked with empty crates and a season's worth of undelivered broadsheets, burying it in the mundane. Janek scrubbed the cellar stones with lye while Freya scattered onion skins and cabbage leaves over the drying floor, and the room that had been their secret country for six nights became, convincingly, a root cellar again.

By the time Alois came home smelling of beer and guild politics, the house was clean. Too clean. He stood in the doorway of the workshop, reading the emptiness of it the way he read a misprinted page, and his eyes went from the buried press to his daughter's face to Janek's ink-stained fingers, which no amount of lye had fully surrendered.

"What," Alois said, "have you two been doing?"

"Miloš Vondráček talked to the guards today," Freya said. No preamble, no softening. "He pointed at our door."

The color left the printer's face in stages, like a proof fading. He set down his hat very carefully on the bench, the way a man does when his hands are not to be trusted. Then he looked at Janek, and Janek saw in the old man's eyes not anger but something older and sadder—the look of a man who has seen this exact moment before, in another year, with another young person full of words, and knows precisely what comes next.

"Then it's already too late to be innocent," Alois said quietly. "We are only deciding, now, what kind of guilty we intend to be."

From the lane outside, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of boots—more boots than a Sunday evening required—stopping, somewhere near, in the gathering dark.
