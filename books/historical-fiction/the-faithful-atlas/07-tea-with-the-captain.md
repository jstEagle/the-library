## 7. Tea with the Captain

Aldous Vey had been in Vhalen eleven days before he allowed himself to walk past the print shop on Corver Lane, and even then he did not go in. He crossed to the far side of the street, adjusted his cap to shadow his face — a soldier's habit, though no one in this city would know him yet — and looked at the window the way a man looks at a house he once lived in. The glass was older. The paint on the frame had gone from green to a tired grey. But the type specimens were still pinned inside, fanned out like a hand of cards: Garamond, Caslon, a heavy slab serif the father used for shipping manifests. And there, third from the left, a small copperplate alphabet with flourishes that curled like water finding its way downhill.

*Li Wen's alphabet,* he thought, and the thought arrived with the weight of a stone set down on a table.

He had come to Vhalen for the province, officially — the tax reassessment, the garrison rotations, the growing stink of sedition in the river districts. He had come unofficially because a man he had once served under had said, over brandy in the capital, *You knew the river country once, Vey. You'll know how to read it.* Both statements were true. Neither was the whole truth. The whole truth was that he had requested the posting, and that he had requested it because of a portrait in a window on Corver Lane, and because of a handwriting he had not seen in nineteen years and could still reproduce from memory, stroke for stroke, down to the way she crossed her *t*s with a single impatient slash, as though time were something she refused to grant the letter.

He made himself wait three more days. Then, on a grey Thursday with rain coming down in fine needles, he put on his dress uniform — not the field coat, the dress uniform, because the visit had to be official, had to be a tax matter, had to be the Empire at its most tedious — and went to the shop with a ledger under his arm and a lie in his mouth that he had rehearsed until it sat comfortably, like a coin worn smooth.

* * *

The bell over the door had the same cracked note. He remembered it. He remembered everything, which was the trouble with him; other men could forget, could let the past silt over, but Vey's memory was a river with a strong current that returned everything thrown into it, eventually, to his feet.

The shop smelled of ink and oil and the faint metallic sweetness of lead type, and beneath that, faintly, of tea — a cheap black tea, oversteeped, the kind printers drank because it could sit cold for an hour and still do its work. The presses had multiplied. There had been two when he was last here, a jobbing platen and the great iron hand-press the father was so proud of; now there were three, and a composing stone had been added by the window where the light was best. The shop had grown the way a body grows around an old wound: functional, careful, hiding the scar in its architecture.

A girl was at the stone. She was setting type, and she did not look up — which was itself a kind of answer, because every other soul in Vhalen looked up when a captain's boots crossed a threshold. She kept her hands moving among the type cases, selecting letters with the economy of someone who had done it ten thousand times, and her profile was — he searched for the word and did not find it, and then found it and wished he hadn't — *familiar*. Not identical. The mother had been rounder in the cheek, softer at the jaw, with the particular warmth of a face that expected to be liked. This girl's face had been planed down by something. Vigilance, perhaps. He had seen that planing before, in the faces of young officers after their first bad winter.

"Captain Vey," said a voice from the back, and the father came through the curtain that divided shop from living quarters, wiping his hands on a leather apron. Wen Kao — no, he used the imperial style here, Zhou Kao, had used it for twenty years — was greyer than Vey remembered, and thinner, but his hands were the same: broad, stained to the second knuckle with ink that no soap would ever fully surrender, moving with the deliberate calm of a man who had decided long ago exactly how fast he would ever do anything.

"Master Zhou." Vey inclined his head. "I won't take much of your time. It's a matter of the reassessment. Your stamp duty for the last quarter was filed under the old schedule."

"Was it." The father's face did nothing at all. "I'll correct it."

"You'll want to correct it in writing, on the new form. I've brought one." He set the ledger on the counter and produced the form with a flourish of bureaucratic tedium, and the father took it, and for a moment the two men stood with the paper between them like a fence both were pretending was a wall.

It was the girl who broke the silence, though she did not turn from her stone. "We don't have tea for officers."

"Kestrel," her father said, and the single word carried an entire household's constitution.

"I don't require tea," Vey said.

"Then you'll want the back room, where the accounts are." She still hadn't looked at him. Her hands kept moving among the type. He watched her select a letter, hold it a half-second longer than the work required — an *e*, he noted, from the copperplate case, the mother's case — and then set it down and choose another. *She knows who I am,* he thought. *Or she knows what I might be. Either way, she has decided to make me wait for her eyes, and she is right to.*

The father led him back past the curtain, past a narrow stair, into a room with a table, two chairs, and a window fogged with river damp. There was a portrait on the wall.

He had known it would be there. He had told himself he would not look at it, and then he looked at it, the way a man looks.

Li Wen sat in the painted frame at perhaps thirty years of age, in the blue-grey dress she had worn the autumn he knew her, her hands folded over a book — of course a book; she had always been holding a book the way other women held fans — and her mouth caught between a smile and a correction. The painter had flattered her eyes, made them larger, but had gotten the mouth exactly, and the mouth undid him for the length of one long breath. Nineteen years. He had buried men he loved with less ceremony than this.

"You knew my wife," Zhou Kao said. It was not a question, and it was not an accusation, and it was delivered with the flat care of a man laying a card face down.

"I did." Vey took his eyes from the portrait with an effort that cost him something visible; he saw the father see it, and catalogued the error. "In the capital. Before her marriage. She worked as a translator for the Customs Bureau — classical texts, trade contracts. I was a junior clerk in the same office. She corrected my provincial grammar for two years and never once let me forget it."

"She spoke of the capital sometimes." The father poured tea — the cheap black, oversteeped, into two cups, because hospitality was a reflex deeper than politics. "Not often."

"No," Vey said. "She wouldn't have." He accepted the cup. Their fingers did not touch. He had planned for them not to touch. "She was the finest hand I ever saw with a contract. There was a clause in the grain tariffs — Article Forty, the one everyone misread — and she read it correctly the first time, and saved the bureau a lawsuit, and was thanked in a memorandum that misspelled her name." He heard himself going on and made himself stop. "I'm here about the stamp duty."

"Yes," said Zhou Kao. "The stamp duty."

They discussed it for eleven minutes. Vey kept count without meaning to; his mind did that, kept count of things while the rest of him attended to something else. The father's answers were correct, complete, and empty, each one a door closed politely. Vey had interrogated smugglers, deserters, and one provincial governor, and he recognized the technique from the inside, because it was his own: give the questioner nothing to hold, and give him nothing to resent. He signed the corrected form, blotted it, and stood.

At the curtain, he paused.

"May I ask an impertinent question, Captain?" Zhou Kao said behind him.

"You may ask it. I may not answer."

"The Empire reassesses taxes in a province when it intends to stay. It sends officers who knew the families when it intends to watch." The father's voice did not rise. "Which is this?"

Vey stood with his hand on the curtain for a moment. Rain ticked against the window glass. Somewhere in the shop beyond, the girl was still setting type, the small iron music of it coming through the wall.

"Both," he said. "It is usually both." And he went out into the rain, and made himself walk, not hurry, to the corner, and only there did he let himself breathe.

* * *

He returned in nine days. This, too, was calculated: soon enough to seem like diligence, late enough to seem like coincidence. The pretext was thinner — a discrepancy of four silver fen in the paper levy, an amount so small that its pursuit was either an insult or a message, and Vey wanted it read as a message, though he wanted the message to be *I am watching you gently*, and he was not at all certain the message would be received that way.

This time the girl looked at him.

She was behind the counter, and when the bell cracked its broken note she raised her eyes and gave him the full weight of them, and Vey understood in that instant that the first visit had been a test he had unknowingly sat, and that he had passed or failed in some manner he would spend the rest of the occupation deciphering. Her eyes were her mother's brown, but the expression in them was not. Li Wen had looked at the world as though it were a text slightly beneath her, affectionately flawed, worth editing. This girl looked at him as though he were a type case she was sorting: this letter goes here, this one goes in the discard.

"Captain." Her voice was even. "The paper levy."

"Four fen," he said. "I've brought the reconciliation."

"Four fen." She wrote it in the shop ledger without being asked, her hand quick and small. He watched the hand. He could not help watching the hand. "My father is at the warehouse. I can settle it."

"Then I'm grateful to you." He set the paper down. "Miss — "

"Zhou Kestrel."

"Miss Zhou." He let the silence after her name sit for exactly as long as courtesy permitted, and then he said, quietly, because the shop was empty and the rain was loud and some doors only open once: "Your mother had a way of writing the numeral four. With the crossbar extending past the stem on the left, only the left. I have never seen anyone else do it. I have been reading documents for nineteen years, and I have never seen it since."

The pen stopped.

It stopped for perhaps a second and a half — he counted, his mind keeping count — and then it resumed, and she finished the entry, and blotted it, and looked up at him with a face that had gone very carefully still, the way water goes still over something deep.

"That's a strange thing to remember," she said.

"It's a strange thing to carry," he agreed. "I've found most memories are. The strange ones are simply the ones that refuse to be reasonable."

"My mother has been dead nine years, Captain."

"I know. I wrote to the Bureau of Records in the spring of the year after. There was a form for it. There is a form for everything." He heard the bitterness arrive in his own voice, uninvited, and was annoyed at it. He had not come to grieve at this girl. He had come — and here he made himself look at the motive squarely, the way he made junior officers look at their maps — he had come because the past was the only lever he had in this city, and because he had convinced himself that using it was a kind of loyalty. He was not sure he believed himself. He was no longer sure he believed himself about very much.

"She never mentioned you," Kestrel Zhou said. "In your letters, or — she didn't write much. But my father would have said."

"Your father and I were not acquainted. I met her before her marriage. I was posted east the year after. That is the whole of it." He took out the reconciliation paper and slid it across the counter. "Four fen. The Empire is thorough."

"The Empire is thorough," she repeated, and there was something in the repetition — not mockery, exactly; a testing, the way one tests a knot — that made him think of her mother again, and he took his hat because if he stayed another minute he was going to say something unforgivable, something true.

At the door he turned. "The copperplate in your window," he said. "Third case from the left. That was hers. She chose it in the capital, from a founder on Vellum Street, and argued the price down by a third because the *w*s were cast light." He put on his hat. "You're cutting the *e*s well. She'd have approved of the *e*s."

And he went out, and this time he heard the pen stop and not resume.

* * *

That evening he sat in his quarters above the garrison annex with the window open to the rain and wrote his report, which was two lines long and said nothing, because reports were the language in which he had learned to say nothing fluently. Then he sat for a long time with a sheet of blank paper before him and the pen dry in his hand.

He had told the girl the truth and it had functioned, exactly as he had intended, as a weapon. That was what disturbed him. He had watched her stillness and known that the memory had gone into her like a blade into a seam, and he had felt — satisfaction. Not tenderness. Satisfaction. The cold professional pleasure of a man who has found the lever and felt the structure shift.

*You knew the river country once, Vey. You'll know how to read it.*

He turned the paper over and, because no one would ever see it and because his hand demanded it the way a locked joint demands movement, he wrote in an imitation of a handwriting nineteen years dead: *Article Forty is misread on purpose. The ones who benefit want you to believe the error is innocent.* He looked at it a long time. The crossbars of the *t*s slashed, impatient. The numeral four with its crossbar reaching left, reaching back, reaching for something on the other side of the page that was no longer there.

Then he fed the sheet to the stove and watched the four burn last, the crossbar glowing longest, and he thought: *The girl is printing something. I don't know what. But a shop that still, a daughter that careful, a father that smooth — the river is running under the ice in that house, and when it breaks, it will break fast.*

He thought also, and this thought he did not feed to the stove, could not: *She looked at me the way her mother looked at a bad contract. As though I were a clause that had been inserted by someone with an interest I hadn't yet found.*

He was right. He would be right about the shop for a long time, and wrong about nearly everything else, and the distance between those two facts would be the story of what he became.

In the shop on Corver Lane, long after the lamps of the garrison were dark, a girl stood at a composing stone by the best light in the room, holding a copperplate *e* in her ink-stained fingers, and did not put it back in its case.
