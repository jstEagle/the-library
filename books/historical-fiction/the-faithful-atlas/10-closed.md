## 10. Closed

The chains went up on a Tuesday, in the rain.

Kestrel heard them before she saw them — the groan of the winch drums from the bridgehead, a sound like some enormous animal being hauled against its will. She was at the shop window with a rag in her hand, wiping condensation from the glass so she could see down Vhalen's long crooked street to where the river showed itself between the rooftops. The whole city seemed to be doing the same thing. Doors stood open along the lane. Old men on their stoops. Children perched on railings. A fishwife with her baskets set down forgotten at her feet, hands still dripping.

They had known it was coming for a fortnight. The proclamation had been nailed to the customs house in letters tall enough to read from across the square: *By order of the Imperial Provost, the Great Bridge shall close to all traffic upon the first day of the month, and shall remain closed until such time as the season permits safe passage.* There had been the usual language about ice floes and spring thaws, about the danger to life and property, as though the river were the enemy and not the Empire. Everyone in Vhalen could do that arithmetic. The river rose every autumn. It had risen every autumn for four hundred years, and the bridge had never once been chained shut before.

Now the soldiers came out onto the span in their grey coats, dark with rain, and began winding the great iron links around the stanchions. Kestrel watched a sergeant direct two privates at the near tower, watched them feed the chain through the iron rings her grandfather had helped cast when he was young and the Empire had seemed permanent. Each link caught the grey light and let it go again. When the last link dropped into its socket there was a sound she felt in her teeth rather than heard — a boom rolling up through the stone of the city, through the floors, through the soles of her boots.

Then the crowd made a noise. Not a shout. Nothing so useful as a shout. It was a long exhalation, hundreds of people breathing out at once, and then the silence after it, which was worse.

Her father appeared beside her at the window. He did not speak. He stood with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, ink to the second knuckle as always, and watched the soldiers march back off the bridge in file, and his face was the face he wore when he was setting type — attentive, blank, giving nothing away. That face had taught Kestrel more than any lesson he'd ever spoken aloud.

"Back to work," he said finally. "The Aldermoor invoices won't set themselves."

She went back to the case. Her fingers found the letters without her eyes, because that was the first thing her father had ever taught her: *the hand must know the way even when the mind is elsewhere.* She sorted lead into words into lines, and all the while part of her stayed at the window, listening to the city breathe.

* * *

By evening the rumors had already begun to breed.

That was the thing about Vhalen with the bridge closed — Kestrel understood it within three days, the way water understands a dammed channel. News came to the city by two roads only: the bridge and the river barges, and now both were shut or soon would be, because the supply traffic was suspended too, pending "inspection." What remained was rumor, and rumor in a trapped city grows like mold in a wet cellar, fast and blind and in the dark.

At the baker's on Thursday morning, the queue spoke in the low voices of people who have decided that everything is worth saying but nothing is worth being overheard saying. The armistice talks had collapsed. No — they hadn't even begun; the envoy's ship had turned back at Sarnholt. No — the envoy was already in the city, lodged incognito at the Salt House, and the whole negotiation was being conducted by candlelight because neither side dared be seen wanting peace. A woman ahead of Kestrel swore her cousin worked in the customs office and had seen the amnesty lists with her own eyes: eleven names, forty names, a hundred and sixty. A man behind her said the lists were blank, that the Empire intended no amnesty at all, that the garrisons had orders to make an example of Vhalen precisely because the bridge was closed and no one could flee the lesson.

Kestrel paid for her bread and said nothing, and carried the silence home like a parcel.

Tam was waiting in the shop's back room, and she knew before he opened his mouth that he had news of his own, because he had the particular brightness of someone carrying something dangerous and pleased about it. He was nineteen and wore his convictions the way other young men wore new boots — proudly, and with a limp developing.

"The cell met last night," he said, too quietly for the front room. "Under the chandlery. Marta says the river's due to crest within the week. If it takes the lower warehouses—"

"I know what the river does."

"Then you know what it means. The garrison can't be resupplied properly until spring. They're short already — Rennick saw the requisition ledgers. Half rations for the rank and file by month's end. And the talks—" He stopped himself, glanced toward the front room where their father's composing stick clicked and clacked against the morning quiet. "There are going to be talks. Real ones. And when they happen, the province needs leverage, and leverage means the printers keep printing."

"Keep your voice down."

"Nobody can hear anything over the river."

But that wasn't true anymore, and Tam knew it wasn't true, and Kestrel knew he knew. With the bridge closed the city had gone quiet in a way that made every sound carry. The usual traffic noise was gone — no carts, no drovers, no barge horns. What remained was a city holding its breath, and in a held breath, a whisper travels.

Their father chose that moment to come through the doorway, and whatever he read in their faces — he read everything, always, and filed it somewhere behind his eyes — he only said, "Kestrel, the Aldermoor job wants a proof pulled," and went back to his press, and the morning continued as mornings do, one small task at a time, while underneath it the whole city strained at its moorings.

* * *

On the sixth day of the closure, the newspapers stopped.

Not all at once. First the provincial papers ceased arriving — naturally, since they came over the bridge. Then the imperial gazette, which had come by military courier, simply stopped coming, and no announcement explained why. The reading room off Cordwainer Square put a notice in its window: *No papers received this week. We regret the inconvenience.* Someone had written beneath it, in pencil, in a fine educated hand: *We regret more than that.*

Kestrel felt the absence physically. She had grown up believing that print was the bloodstream of the world — that what happened anywhere arrived everywhere, eventually, in ink. Now the city was cut off from its own circulation, and she could feel the extremities going numb. People began asking her father, of all people, whether he'd heard anything. Him! As though the trade itself conferred knowledge. He would shake his head gravely and say the river kept its secrets, and they would nod, satisfied, and walk away knowing exactly as little as before but feeling better for having asked.

What frightened her was how quickly people filled the vacuum. Within a week she had collected, without trying, a dozen mutually exclusive versions of the world beyond the bridge. In one, the provincial armies had taken Sarnholt and were marching south. In another, Sarnholt had burned and the Empire was massing troops at the fords. In a third, there were no armies at all anymore; plague had broken out in the capital; the Emperor was dead; the Emperor had abdicated; the Emperor had never really mattered and the whole war was a quarrel between ministers fought with provinces as counters. Each version had its adherents, and each adherent believed with the fervent certainty of a person who has chosen a story because the alternative is unbearable.

And threaded through all of it, thin as wire: the names. Names of strikers arrested. Names of houses searched. Names of people who had left their lodgings one night and not returned. These traveled differently than the grand rumors — not shouted in queues but passed hand to hand, mouth to ear, in the particular hush reserved for the dead and the disappeared. Kestrel noticed that these whispers were never wrong. The grand rumors contradicted each other daily; the whispered names accumulated steadily, and no one ever retracted one.

She started keeping a list. Not writing it down — she was not yet fool enough for that — but keeping it, in the place where she kept type sizes and customer debts and the location of every loose floorboard in the shop. Eleven names by the end of the second week. She recited them to herself at night the way other people said prayers.

* * *

It rained for nine days straight, and the river climbed the stairs of the city.

Kestrel walked down to the water steps below the chandlery on the tenth day, just to look, the way people look at a fire or a wound. The Vhalen had swallowed its own banks entirely. Brown water moved under the bridge arches with a slow muscular power, carrying whole trees, a drowned cart, a dead ewe turning gently. The lower streets were shin-deep and the shopkeepers were moving their stock upstairs with the grim efficiency of people who had done this many times and expected to do it many more. But this year there was a difference, and everyone could feel it: usually the flood was an inconvenience that ended when the bridge reopened. This year the flood was a wall, and behind the wall was winter, and behind winter was whoever held the city when the thaw came.

A soldier stood at the top of the water steps, young, rain running off his helmet brim, watching the same brown water. Their eyes met. He looked away first. She thought: *he is as trapped as I am,* and then thought: *no, he has a chain of command, and I have a family, and we are trapped in different shapes.*

Marta found her there. The widow came down the steps with her skirts kilted up, unbothered by the wet, and stood beside her as though they had arranged to meet, which they had not, which was itself a kind of craft Kestrel was only beginning to learn — how conspirators appear to be strangers who happened to stop at the same railing.

"They say the crest comes tomorrow," Marta said, eyes on the river. "Two meters above the high mark of my grandmother's year."

"Will it take the chandlery?"

"The cellar floods every year. We've raised the floor of the meeting room." Marta's voice was level, unhurried, the voice of a woman who had buried a husband and learned that most catastrophes are survivable if you refuse to dramatize them. "The schoolteacher worries. He wants to move the type out of the city entirely, bury it somewhere until spring. Oren says burying the type is burying the cause. They argued half the night."

"And you?"

"I listened." Marta smiled faintly. "That's mostly what I do. Listen. You'd be surprised what people say in front of a widow. They think grief makes us deaf." She turned her head slightly, and her voice dropped into the register beneath speech. "Your brother argues well. Too well. He wants to print while the garrison is hungry and the officers are distracted. He may even be right. But right is a dangerous country, girl. Most people who go there don't come back the same."

"He's careful."

"No one is careful," Marta said. "Some people are only lucky, and luck is a loan, not a gift." She gathered her skirts. "Tell him the river is rising faster than the arguments. One of those things we can survive."

She went back up the steps, and Kestrel stayed a while longer, watching the water carry the city's debris seaward past the chained bridge, past the soldiers, past the whole apparatus of the Empire, as though none of it weighed anything at all.

* * *

That night she dreamed of type.

In the dream the shop was flooded to the ceiling and every letter she had ever set floated free of its case, ten thousand pieces of lead drifting in the brown water like fish, and she was diving among them trying to gather a sentence, any sentence, and the letters kept slipping through her fingers and rearranging themselves into words she couldn't read — words in her mother's handwriting, words in Captain Vey's voice, words in the whispered names of the disappeared. She woke with her heart hammering and lay listening to the rain on the roof tiles, and to the river, which she could hear now even from the shop, a deep continuous sound beneath everything, like the city murmuring in its sleep.

She got up and lit the lamp and went down to the shop floor, telling herself it was to check the sandbags at the door, and it was, partly. But once there she stood a long time in front of the type cabinets, her hand resting on the cool brass handle of the upper case, and she understood that what she was really doing was taking inventory — not of lead, but of herself. Of what she knew. Of what she owed. Of how much longer she could stand in this shop sorting other people's words while her own stayed locked in her chest like type in a tied-up case.

Through the shutter slats she could see a slice of the street, and in the street, lamplight: a patrol passing, four soldiers abreast, their boots ringing on wet cobbles with a rhythm that had changed in two weeks. Faster now. Harder. The garrison was nervous, and nervous garrisons march loudly.

The patrol passed. The sound faded. The river went on speaking under everything.

Eleven names, she thought. By spring it would be fifty. By spring the armistice would be signed or abandoned, the city would belong to someone, and every person in Vhalen would find out which side of the ledger their name had been written on. Unless someone wrote first.

She stood in the dark of her father's shop with her hand on the case of forbidden letters — for she knew now which drawers held them, she had known for years, she had dusted around them and said nothing — and outside, the rain kept falling on a city that had become one held breath, waiting to find out who would exhale first.
