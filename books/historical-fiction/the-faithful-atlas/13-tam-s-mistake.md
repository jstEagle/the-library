## 13. Tam's Mistake

The pamphlets were warm from the press when Tam took them, and he held the bundle against his chest like something newborn, something that might still be spoiled by cold air. Two hundred copies. He had watched his sister set every letter herself, her hands steady in a way his never were, and he had carried the finished sheets home wrapped in oilcloth beneath a delivery of jobbing work — invoices for a chandler, a funeral notice for old Mrs. Halloway, nothing that would interest a soldier at a checkpoint. He had been careful. He had been so careful for three weeks that the care had begun to feel like cowardice.

That was the thought he could not shake, walking through the Salt Quarter in the gray light of a November afternoon: that caution was just fear wearing a respectable coat. The cell met and argued and met again, and every meeting ended the same way — with the schoolteacher, Master Ellery, tapping the table and saying, *Patience. The river does its work. The city will come to us,* and with the dockhand, Bren, saying nothing at all, only looking at Tam with those flat harbor eyes that seemed to ask what a printer's son knew about hunger. Even Marta, gentle Marta, who had wept over the death-notices, had touched his wrist and said, *Not yet, boy. Not yet.* Everyone wanted to wait. Everyone wanted the perfect moment, as if the Empire would kindly schedule its cruelty for their convenience.

But the boy — the boy had not waited. The boy had died.

His name was Oskar Vell. He was seventeen. He had worked the rope-ferries since he was twelve, and when the garrison conscripted the ferry crews to haul artillery barges against their will, Oskar had stood on the dock and said no in front of forty people, and they had taken him to the barracks yard and broken his hands with a truncheon — not killed him, only broken his hands, the hands of a boy who lived by rope and water — and turned him loose with a warning. The city had heard the story by evening. The city had done nothing. The city had pulled its shutters closed and lit its lamps and pretended the screaming had been gulls.

Tam had heard the story and felt something crack open in him, some seam he had not known was load-bearing. He had gone home and sat in the dark of the print shop among the sleeping type and thought: *They broke a boy's hands and the river did its work and nobody came.* Patience was what the Empire fed on. Patience was the leash. Every day of waiting was a day the garrison grew fatter, a day the amnesty lists grew shorter, a day some other Oskar Vell learned that no one would stand beside him.

So he had not waited. He had taken the bundle Marta had asked for — fifty copies, to be left quietly in the market district, in doorways, under market stalls, places where hands would find them and pockets would carry them onward — and he had added fifty of his own from the stack in his room, and then he had kept walking. Past the market district. Past the chandlery. Across the Weavers' Bridge footpath where the fog came off the river in ropes, all the way to Garrison Hill, because that was where the ferry crews lived, that was where Oskar Vell's neighbors slept behind their thin walls, and what was the point of a call to strike that never reached the people with the strongest reason to strike?

He told himself this all the way up the hill, and the telling had the smooth, worn feel of a coin passed too many times. He was not distributing seditious print within a hundred paces of a garrison post. He was honoring a boy with broken hands. He was doing what his sister had been too careful to do, what his father had never once done in his life, what the whole cowed city had failed to do. He was brave. He was the brave one.

The fog was thick on Garrison Hill, thick enough that the lamp at the post gate was only a smudge of orange, and Tam worked the street quickly, sliding a folded pamphlet under the door of the ropewalk boarding house, tucking another into the crack of the ferry office's shuttered window, leaving a third weighted with a stone on the well cap where the ferry families drew their morning water. The paper was cheap and the ink had come out strong — his sister's work, always strong — and each folded sheet bore the same block-printed headline in letters like hammer strokes: *THE HANDS THAT HAUL THE EMPIRE'S GUNS CAN ALSO LET GO.* Below it, the facts, plain as a ledger: Oskar Vell, seventeen, his hands. Below that, the word every pamphlet carried now, the word Kestrel had set alone at night with shaking hands and had never once misspelled: *STRIKE.*

He had eleven left when he heard the boots.

Not the fog-muffled shuffle of a patrol walking its rounds — he knew that sound, had catalogued it the way his sister catalogued type, the way their whole family catalogued dangers — but the quick, deliberate strike of boots that had heard something. He pressed the remaining pamphlets flat against his stomach inside his coat and turned, casually, a young man out walking in the fog, and saw them at the end of the street: two soldiers and between them a small figure, thin, coat too big, arms held stiff at the sides.

He knew the coat. It was a ferryman's coat, rope-burned at the cuffs, and he knew the way the figure walked, shoulders up around the ears, trying to make himself small — every boy in the ferry quarter walked that way past soldiers, it was nothing, it was survival — but the soldiers were not walking past. They had stopped under the smudge of the gate lamp, and one of them had taken something from the boy's hand, and the lamp-light caught the white of unfolded paper.

Tam stood in the fog and did not breathe.

He had seen the boy before. That was the thing that would not stop repeating in him, later, like a line of type set twice by mistake. He had seen him at the ferry office two days ago, hauling a skiff up the slip, whistling — actually whistling, some tuneless made-up thing — and Tam had thought, *there, that's the spirit of it, that's who we print for,* and had felt the warm righteous glow of a man who loves humanity in the abstract. The boy had picked up one of Tam's pamphlets off the well cap, or out of a doorway, had read it with those broken-handed ferry families all around him, and had done the bravest, stupidest, most faithful thing in the world: he had taken it with him. He had carried it. He had believed the paper meant what it said.

The soldier turned the sheet over. Tam could see it even at that distance, could read the headline in his memory if not in the fog: *THE HANDS THAT HAUL THE EMPIRE'S GUNS CAN ALSO LET GO.* He had set that line himself. He had argued with Kestrel about it — she had wanted something gentler, something about wages and conscription, and he had said no, let it be a hammer, let it be plain — and she had looked at him a long moment and then set his line, because she always set his lines, because she was his sister and she trusted him, and now the line was in a soldier's fist under a garrison lamp with a seventeen-year-old whistler standing very small beside it.

The boy said something. Tam could not hear it. The soldier said something back, short, and then took the boy's arm — not gently — and the three of them moved toward the post gate, and the gate opened, a rectangle of deeper dark in the fog, and closed.

Tam walked. He did not run. Some cold, clear, horrified part of him understood that running was the one thing he must not do, and so he walked, down the hill, through the ropes of fog, past the ropewalk, past the ferry office, his eleven pamphlets a flat dead weight against his stomach like a second heart that had stopped. He walked all the way to the Weavers' Bridge footpath before his legs began to shake, and then he stood at the rail over the black water and was sick, quietly, thoroughly, like a man paying a debt.

They had the boy. They had the boy and the paper, and the paper had his sister's ink in it, his sister's setting, his sister's hours, and it had his line, his hammer, his plain brave words, and somewhere behind that gate there was a desk where a sergeant would smooth the sheet flat and read it twice and understand exactly what it was, and then there would be questions, and questions traveled. Questions traveled downhill. Questions traveled toward print shops.

* * *

He did not go home. He went to the chandlery.

The cellar beneath it was dry where the streets were not — old salt stores, brick-vaulted, smelling of tallow and brine — and the cell was already gathered when he came down the stairs, lamplit, low-voiced, the river audible somewhere above them as a long wet pressure against the world. Master Ellery stood at the table with a map of the city weighted at the corners. Bren sat with his arms crossed. Marta was mending a coat that did not belong to her, because her hands could not be idle. And Kestrel was there, ink to the second knuckle as always, and when she looked up at Tam her face did the thing it always did — the small brightening, the sister's face — and then it did another thing, slower, as she read him, because she had always been able to read him the way she read proof, catching errors no one else could see.

"Tam," she said. "What is it."

He meant to be careful. He meant to give them the shape of it without the shame of it, to say *a boy was taken on Garrison Hill carrying one of ours* and let them draw their own conclusions, and perhaps they would have, perhaps they would have blamed the fog or the boy or the war. But he was twenty years old and he had been carrying the guilt up the hill and down again and it was heavier than any bundle of type, and when he opened his mouth what came out was the truth, whole and ugly.

"I went to Garrison Hill."

The lamplight did not move. Bren's arms uncrossed, slowly, like a door opening onto something worse.

"I took the ferry-quarter pamphlets there," Tam said. "Fifty of them. The boy — there was a boy who works the ropes, Oskar Vell, they broke his hands last week for refusing conscription, and I thought —" His voice did something he hated, a thin crack, and he pushed through it. "I thought they had the most reason. I thought patience was just fear. I walked right up the hill in the fog and left them at the well, at the boarding house, at the ferry office, and there was a boy, I saw him take one, I saw him — he was whistling, two days ago he was hauling a skiff and whistling, and tonight they took him through the gate with the paper in his fist and I stood in the fog and did nothing. I could have called out. I could have — I don't know what. I could have taken his place. And I walked away."

He had not known he was going to say that last part. It came out of him like blood from under a door, and once it was out the room was very quiet, and the river pressed its long weight against the world above, and Marta had stopped mending, the needle hanging from the cloth.

Master Ellery spoke first, and his voice was the teacher's voice, the patient, disappointed voice that Tam had hated in schoolrooms and hated more now. "The garrison has the pamphlet. Do you understand what that means? Not a rumor of pamphlets — the pamphlet itself. Our setting. Our ink. Our phrasing. There are perhaps four presses in Vhalen capable of that impression, and the captain who runs that post is not a fool. He will compare. He will narrow. Every hour that boy is in a cell, he is being asked where he found it, and a seventeen-year-old with broken hands, after one night in a cell —" Ellery stopped himself, which was its own kind of mercy. "How many did you leave on the hill?"

"Fifty. Thirty-nine placed. I have eleven."

"Burn them."

Tam's hand went, without thought, to his coat, and Bren saw the gesture and something like contempt moved through his flat harbor eyes. "He carried them *here,*" Bren said. "Down through the streets. Past the market. Past the watch post on Tanner's Row, if he came the way I'd have come." He looked at Tam the way he looked at a fouled line. "You walked the evidence through the whole city to bring it to the one room we cannot afford to burn."

"I came to warn you."

"You came to be forgiven." Bren stood. He was not a tall man but he was made entirely of work, and when he stood the room seemed to rearrange itself around him. "You want us to say it wasn't your fault, that the boy was brave, that the fog was thick, that the cause is worth a cell. And maybe it is. Maybe he'd have been taken anyway, for his hands, for his mouth, for the shape of his hat. But you don't get to decide that for him. That's what you did tonight. You decided for a boy who trusted a piece of paper, because the paper said *strike* and you wanted to feel the strike in your own chest before the river closes and it's too late for you." He picked up his cap from the bench. "I've buried strikers. I've buried friends. I've never once buried one because a printer's son wanted to be a hero in his own story."

"Bren," Marta said softly.

"No." He settled the cap on his head. "Say it plain or don't say it. The boy is in a cell and the garrison has our ink, and one of us walked it up the hill on purpose." He looked around the lamplit room, at each of them, last at Tam. "I'll be at the docks. When the garrison starts kicking in doors, someone should warn the rope-walkers who aren't already in one."

He went up the stairs, and the door closed, and the silence he left was a physical thing, like the pressure of deep water.

Marta rose and did the thing she always did, which was to move toward the hurt. She put her hand flat on Tam's chest, over the coat, over the eleven pamphlets, over the stopped second heart. "The boy," she said. "Tell me about the boy. Not the mistake. The boy."

"He whistled," Tam said, and his voice went to pieces on it. "He was strong, and fast, and he laughed at his own jokes before he finished them. He had rope burns on both palms and he was proud of them. He took the pamphlet because he believed it. That's — Marta, that's the whole of it. He believed a piece of paper I made, and it's going to kill him."

"No," Marta said, with the terrible gentleness of a woman who had already lost a son to a different war. "What they do to him will kill him, or not. What you do next is the only part that is yours. So think, Tam. Think past your guilt. Guilt is a luxury now. What does the garrison do next, and what do we do before they do it?"

And it was Kestrel who answered, because Kestrel always answered, and Tam watched his sister's face as she spoke and saw there something he had never seen in it before — not fear exactly, and not blame, but a kind of recalculation, the look of a compositor discovering a cracked letter deep in a form, a flaw that had been there all along and would now print itself on every page.

"They'll work him tonight," she said quietly. "A boy like that, they'll work him tonight, because they'll want names before the ink dries on their report. And the first name a frightened boy gives won't be a name at all — it'll be a place. *I found it near the well. I found it on Garrison Hill.* They'll sweep the hill. And when the hill gives them nothing, they'll start comparing paper." She looked at Tam, and her voice did not waver, and that was the worst of it, that she would not let it waver, not even for him. "Our ink is milled by Voss. Our paper comes through two factors. Our impression is a Zhou impression — Father's press has a lifted shoulder on the K, everyone who knows print knows it. Tam. They won't need the boy to talk. They'll only need the paper to speak, and it will, because I set it, and I set it well."

The lamplight hissed. Somewhere above them the river rose another finger's width against the world, patient as the Empire, patient as everything that meant to drown them.

"Then we move the press," Tam said. "Tonight. Now. Before —"

"Before nothing," Kestrel said. "You go home. You walk in the front door, you eat supper, you are a printer's son who has never been on Garrison Hill in his life. If they come to the shop, they find a father, a daughter, a son, and a ledger of honest work. That is all they find." She was already gathering her coat, already turning to Marta and Ellery, already the courier, the fixer, the one who moved through locked doors. "I'll get word to Voss to change our ink stock. I'll get the type cases covered. And Tam —"

She stopped at the foot of the stairs and looked back at him, and for one moment the sister's face came through the courier's face, and it was worse than blame would have been.

"The boy," she said. "Oskar Vell. Whatever happens to him — whatever he gives them, whatever they do to him — you will carry that. Not as punishment. As weight. The way Father carries the ledger. The way I carry the type. Everyone in this room carries something they set with their own hands. Yours is a boy who whistled."

She went up the stairs, and Tam stood alone in the salt-smelling dark with the eleven pamphlets against his chest, and he understood that he would not burn them after all. He would keep them. He would keep them the way a man keeps the knife he was cut with — not to use, but to remember the edge.

He was almost at the top of the stairs when the knock came at the chandlery's street door — three slow knocks, the knock of a man with authority and all night — and through the boards, muffled by fog and brick, a voice Tam had heard only twice before, a voice like a drawn blade being sheathed:

"Open in the name of Captain Vey. We're looking for a boy."
