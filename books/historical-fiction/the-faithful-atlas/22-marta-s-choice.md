## 22. Marta's Choice

The cellar beneath the chandlery smelled of tallow and river rot, and by the third hour of the meeting Tam had stopped noticing either. What he could not stop noticing was the water. It came through the floor in a slow black seep that no one spoke of but everyone watched, pooling around the legs of the crates they sat on, rising a finger's width each day, as if the river itself had opinions about what was decided down here.

Eleven of them tonight. The schoolteacher, Ovid Rell, at the head of the upturned barrel he used for a table, his spectacles fogged from the single lamp. Dockhands Bren and Sallow, their shoulders filling the low corners of the room. The widow's boy, Joss, too young to be here and too stubborn to be sent home. And Marta Voss, sitting nearest the lamp because her eyes were going and she would not admit it, her hands folded on her knees like a woman waiting out a sermon.

Tam stood where he always stood — against the wall, half in shadow, his back to the shelves of candle stock. He had learned early that in this room it paid to be the one who saw everything and was seen least. His sister had taught him that without ever meaning to. *Which silences keep people alive,* Kestrel said, and he had laughed at her once, months ago, when silence still seemed like cowardice dressed in careful clothes.

He did not laugh anymore.

"The amnesty lists are dead," Ovid was saying. "The envoy won't move on them, and the provincial delegates won't sign without them. Which means every name we've ever put on paper is still worth a cell in the Saltworks, or worse." He took off his spectacles and rubbed the bridge of his nose, and for a moment he looked exactly what he was: a man who had spent thirty years teaching children their letters and now spent his nights teaching grown men how to die politely. "The garrisons know there's a press in this quarter. They don't know where. That knowledge is the only coin we have left, and we are spending it faster than we're earning."

"Then we stop spending," Bren said. "We go quiet until the armistice lands, one way or the other."

"And if it lands badly?" Sallow asked. "If the Empire takes the city back street by street? You want to be hiding under this floor when they come door to door?"

"I want to be alive," Bren said flatly. "I've got a wife who thinks I'm at cards on Thursdays. I'd like her to go on thinking it."

The argument circled, as it had circled for three nights now. Tam let it wash over him. He had heard all of it before — the strike, the quiet, the flight across the closed bridge that was not a flight because the bridge was chained shut, the appeal to the delegates, the appeal to the mob, the appeal to God, though none of them believed in Him except perhaps Marta, who crossed herself when she thought no one was looking, an old habit from a province far away and a life even farther.

It was Marta who ended it.

"You're all talking about saving the press," she said, "as though the press were a thing you could hide. It isn't. It's a thing you can only move. And moving it means someone has to be found not moving it."

The room went still the way water goes still before it freezes.

Ovid turned toward her slowly. "Marta—"

"There's a press in the chandlery," she said, in the reasonable voice she used for explaining things to people who ought already to understand them. "Everyone in this quarter knows I come here. Everyone knows I've quarreled with the garrison since they flogged my lodger's boy for stealing bread — which he did steal, mind, so they weren't wrong about the crime, only about the punishment. If soldiers find printing type in my rooms—" she shrugged, a small, terrible shrug, "—no one will be surprised. An old woman with a grudge and a smuggled press. It explains everything. It explains it so neatly that no one will look past it."

"That's not—" Joss started, and she silenced him with a look that had ended better arguments than his.

"It's not what? Not fair?" Marta smiled, and Tam felt something twist behind his ribs, because it was not a bitter smile. It was almost gentle. "Child, I buried a husband and two sons before the first pamphlet was ever printed in this city. My sons died for nothing — for a landlord's lawsuit and a fever, which is how most men die, meaninglessly, unmourned by anyone but their mothers. I have been given a chance to die for something, and you want to tell me it isn't fair. Fair is a word for children dividing cake."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere above them, the chandlery's shutters banged in the wind off the river, and everyone flinched, and everyone pretended they hadn't.

Tam pushed himself off the wall. "No."

He hadn't meant to speak. The word came out of him the way the water came through the floor — slow, then all at once. Eleven faces turned to him. He was twenty-two years old and the youngest full member of the cell besides Joss, and he knew what they saw when they looked at him: Kestrel Zhou's brother, the printer's son, the one whose zeal had already cost them. The boy arrested outside the garrison post had been carrying Tam's pamphlets. Tam had chosen that route. Tam had walked away whistling while a child was dragged into the Saltworks. He carried it in his chest every day now, a coal he had learned to breathe around.

"No," he said again, steadier. "You're describing yourself being taken so we believe the press is yours. But they don't just take printers, Marta. They interrogate them. And you'd give them nothing, I know you would, but it doesn't matter what you'd give. It matters what they'd take. They'll take your hands first. You know that. Everyone knows that."

"My hands," Marta said, and held them up into the lamplight, turning them over, palms and backs, as though inspecting goods at market. They were broad, cracked hands, wax-burned from decades of chandlery work, knuckles swollen like the roots of an old tree. "These hands set type twice a week for four months. These hands folded two hundred death-notices for men whose own families didn't dare mourn them. Do you know what I thought, the first time I held one of your sister's pamphlets?" She looked at Tam directly, and he could not look away. "I thought: at last, something these hands are for."

"They're for living," Tam said. "They're for the years after. That's the whole point. We print so there *is* an after—"

"We print so there's a *before* that someone remembers honestly," Marta said. "The after will take care of itself, or it won't. Boy, sit down. You've argued enough causes this season to last us all."

But Ovid had gone very quiet, and Tam knew that quiet. It was the quiet of a man doing arithmetic he hated. Ovid looked at Marta for a long moment, then at the water seeping through the floor, then at the ceiling, as if he could see through it to the press itself — Kestrel's press, disassembled crate by crate, waiting in the dark for the night they moved it to the salt warehouse.

"If the garrison believes they've found the press," Ovid said carefully, "they'll stand down the house-to-house searches. They'll report upward that the seditious printing in this quarter has been suppressed. And while they're congratulating themselves, the real press moves." He pressed his lips together. "It would work. God forgive me, it would work."

"Then it's settled," Marta said.

"It is not settled," Tam said. "You need a vote. You need—"

"I need nothing of the kind. This isn't the assembly hall, Tam Zhou. There are eleven of us in a flooded cellar; we don't vote, we decide, and I've decided." She rose, unhurried, brushing wax dust from her skirt. Up close she was smaller than she seemed seated, a thin woman with a spine like a boat's keel. "Type goes into my rooms tomorrow night. Two days after, I'll send Joss's cousin to buy ink from me in front of the window where the patrol can see. Three days after that, they come for me. By then the press should be under salt."

"And you?" Tam heard his own voice crack on the word and hated it. "What happens to you three days after that?"

"What happens to any of us," Marta said. "Whatever the armistice decides. Whatever the Empire decides. Whatever the river decides." She reached out and took his chin in those ruined hands, the way his mother had done when he was small enough to be scolded, firm and brief and unbearably kind. "You'll print it right, won't you? When they read the charges against me in the square. You'll print my name spelled properly. V-o-s-s. My husband's family spelled it with a double-s and I never corrected them, and I've regretted it forty years."

He could not answer. He nodded, because nodding was all his body would do, and her hand fell away, and she gathered her shawl, and she left the way she always left — first, unafraid, her boots steady on the wet stairs.

* * *

He walked home the long way, along the flood wall, because the short way passed the garrison post and he could not bear the sight of it tonight. The rain had stopped but the air was thick with it, and the river ran high and brown and silent beside him, carrying things he chose not to identify. Lamplighters were making their rounds early; the curfew bell would ring within the hour. Above the rooftops the bridge loomed, chained and garrisoned, its towers black against a sky the color of wet slate.

His father would be at the shop still, setting honest type for honest customers, pretending — Tam no longer knew what he pretended. His father had become a country Tam could not map: paying both sides, betraying no one or everyone, smiling at Captain Vey across the counter while the city burned down around his ledgers. And Kestrel — Kestrel was somewhere between the shop and the salt warehouse with ink to her elbows, moving the press piece by piece, and she did not yet know what Marta had decided tonight.

That was the thought that stopped him at the foot of the flood wall, his hand on the cold stone, the river breathing below him.

Kestrel would blame herself. That was the thing about his sister that Tam understood better than anyone, better perhaps than she understood herself: she kept accounts. Not money — consequences. Every name printed, every name withheld, every silence chosen or failed, she entered into some ledger behind her eyes, and Marta's sacrifice would go in under *debts unpaid*, and she would spend the rest of her life trying to balance it. She would print Marta's name ten thousand times if it came to that. She would burn the city down to get it onto an amnesty list.

And Marta knew it. That was the devastating part, the part Tam kept turning over as he climbed the wall stairs to their street. Marta had not volunteered despite knowing what it would do to them. She had volunteered *because* of it — because grief, freely given, becomes a weapon in the hands of people who loved you. Because a cell that has received a gift like that does not scatter. It hardens. It prints.

*She's using us,* he thought, climbing. *The kindest thing anyone has ever done to me, and she's using us.*

He let himself into the shop through the alley door. The press frame stood empty in the back room, a skeleton of iron and wood, and beside it the crates, sealed and stenciled as soap stock, waiting for the cart. His father was asleep upstairs; he could hear the particular rhythm of the old man's snoring through the floorboards, and beneath it, fainter, the scratch of a pen — Kestrel, awake, always awake, working at something by candlelight.

He found her at the worktable, and she looked up, and whatever was in his face made her set down her pen.

"Tam?"

"Marta's giving herself to them," he said. No preamble. He had rehearsed nothing. "Tomorrow night. Type in her rooms, a decoy press, so the searches stop and we can move ours. She decided it in the cellar and Ovid let her and I couldn't—" His voice failed and he stood there, twenty-two years old, his hands hanging at his sides like tools whose purpose had been forgotten.

Kestrel was quiet for a long moment. The candle flame leaned and straightened. When she spoke, her voice was very level, which he knew was the sound of her holding something down.

"When?"

"Three days. Maybe four. They'll want to make an example slowly."

Kestrel looked at the empty press frame. Then at the crates. Then at her own hands, ink-stained to the second knuckle, and Tam watched his sister do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic Ovid had done, the same arithmetic the whole drowning city was doing now, lives weighed against names, names against paper, paper against the chained bridge and the stalled talks and the snow coming on.

"Then we have three days," she said, "to make sure her sacrifice buys everything it can."

And Tam, who had wanted her to say *no*, to say *we'll stop her*, to say anything but that — Tam heard in her voice the exact note Marta's had carried in the cellar, calm and terrible and free, and understood that he was the only person left in Vhalen who still believed any of them should be saved.

Outside, the curfew bell began to ring.
