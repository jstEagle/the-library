## 24. The Last Berth Announced

The order came down from the envoy's staff on a Tuesday, folded inside a dispatch case sealed with the double eagle of the Imperial Provost, and Aldous Vey read it three times before he understood that it was not a clerical error.

*One civilian berth,* the dispatch said, *to be allocated at the commanding officer's discretion, aboard the supply barge* Meridian *on her final crossing of the Vhalen span prior to armistice conclusion. Recipient to be vetted for political reliability. Documentation to be filed under seal.*

One berth. One person. Out of a city of forty thousand souls standing hip-deep in rumor and rising water, one person would be permitted to cross the chained bridge before the negotiators finished their arithmetic of amnesty and surrender, and the choice of who that person would be had been left — deliberately, he suspected, the way a knife is left on a table within reach of a quarrelsome family — to him.

Vey set the dispatch down on his desk in the customs house and looked out the window at the river. It had come up over the lower quays again in the night, brown and muscular, carrying fence posts, a drowned sheep, half a rowboat with its name painted over. The bridge stood above all of it like a held breath, its iron chains strung gate to gate, soldiers posted at either end with orders that had not changed in six weeks: nothing crosses but what the army sends.

He had been in Vhalen for eleven months. He had arrived believing the province was a problem to be administered, and he would leave — whenever leaving came — knowing it was a wound to be cauterized or healed, and that he had done neither well. He thought often now of the print shop on Cordwainer's Lane, of the portrait of Lian Zhou that hung beside the press, of the daughter who had her mother's way of looking at a man as though she were reading the fine print of his soul. He thought of the safe conduct papers he had drafted and then, in a moment of weakness he still could not fully account for, offered to her — *name the leader of your cell, and your family walks free* — and of how she had looked at him then. Not afraid. Not tempted. *Weighing* him, the way she weighed everything, and finding the price printed somewhere below where he could see it.

She had never named anyone. He had checked. In the weeks since, through channels both official and otherwise, he had confirmed what he had already guessed: Kestrel Zhou had kept her silence so completely that even her silence had a craft to it, a typesetter's precision. And yet the cell had moved its press. The pamphlets had stopped coming from Cordwainer's Lane and started appearing from nowhere at all, which meant someone had smuggled two hundredweight of cast iron and lead through flooded streets under the noses of four garrisons, and Vey knew — the way an old soldier knows weather in his knee — exactly whose hands had done it.

Now the Empire, in its vast indifference, had handed him a single seat on the last boat and called it mercy.

* * *

He spent the morning doing what he always did when a decision frightened him: he made lists.

It was a habit from the frontier campaigns, from the retreat across the Salt Marches when he had been a lieutenant with frostbitten feet and forty men to feed and wagons enough for twenty. You wrote the names down. You counted what you could carry. You chose, and you lived with the choosing, and you did not sleep, but you lived.

He sat in the customs house with the shutters open to the cold and wrote:

*Zhou, Edran.* The father. A printer of thirty years' standing, a man who had paid protection money to both sides of a war with the meticulous bookkeeping of a parish treasurer. Vey had read the ledger — or rather, he had been shown it by an informant whose information he no longer entirely trusted, and he had chosen not to act on it, which was its own kind of transaction. Edran Zhou was a survivor. Survivors made good use of berths. But survivors also, sometimes, bought them with other people's names, and Vey could not shake the suspicion — unproven, perhaps unprovable — that the raid on the chandlery cellar had been purchased in part with coin that passed through a printer's careful hands.

*Zhou, Tam.* The son. Nineteen, zealous, guilty. Vey had the garrison report on the boy arrested outside the barracks with a strike pamphlet in his coat; he had read the interrogation transcript; he knew the thread that ran from that boy's arrest back through the widow Marta Halloway to the cell, and he knew — because he had delayed his own report by three days to let the trail go cold — that the only reason Tam Zhou was not already in the provost's cells was that Aldous Vey had committed a small treason with ink and dates. The boy had courage. Courage of the loud kind, the kind that got schoolteachers hanged. If anyone deserved a berth as reward for service to the cause, it was Tam — and if anyone would refuse it on those grounds, it would be Tam also, loudly, in front of witnesses, making the refusal itself into a pamphlet.

*Zhou, Kestrel.*

He wrote the name and then sat looking at it for a long time.

Here was the difficulty, and he was honest enough with himself to name it: he could not separate what was just from what he wanted. That was the rot at the center of every officer's discretion, the reason discretion was supposed to be exercised by men without attachments, and he had failed that test before he ever opened the dispatch case. He wanted to put her on that barge. He wanted it with a specificity that alarmed him — he could see it, the gangplank, the gray water, her dark coat, her turning once at the rail — and wanting it so plainly made the offer obscene. Because the berth was not a gift. It was a door with one handle, and behind it a family, and whatever hand he guided through that door would spend the rest of its life hearing the others knock.

And there was the further matter — the one he circled and could not land on — of what she had done with the manifest question already forming in the city's mind. Rumor traveled faster than water in Vhalen that autumn. By the time the supply barge's crossing became known, half the city would know of the berth, and every household with anything to fear would begin its own arithmetic. Fathers would argue with sons. Mothers would lie to daughters. The berth would not be a mercy dropped into the city; it would be a stone dropped into a well, and Vey would stand at the lip and watch the ripples take people he knew.

He burned the list in the tin basin by his desk and watched the ink curl off the paper. Then he put on his greatcoat and went to make the announcement in person, because some things should not be delivered by sergeant.

* * *

Cordwainer's Lane smelled of wet plaster and lamp oil, as half the city did now. The print shop's windows were shuttered — they had been since the chandlery burned — but smoke rose thin and ordinary from the chimney, the smoke of a household boiling its rationed barley and keeping its head down, and Vey knocked the way an inspector knocks, three measured raps, audible to any neighbor inclined to count them.

Edran Zhou opened the door himself. He had aged a year in a season; his hands, which Vey remembered as quick and sure among the type cases, now hung at his sides with the wariness of a man who has learned that his hands are evidence. Behind him, the shop interior was dim, the press shrouded in canvas like furniture in a dead man's house.

"Captain." Nothing in the voice invited him in, and nothing refused him. A craftsman's neutrality, maintained at ruinous cost.

"Master Zhou. I'll keep it short, because short is safer for us both." Vey did not ask to enter. "There is a supply barge making a final crossing before the armistice concludes. There is one civilian berth aboard it. The allocation is mine."

Behind Edran, in the dimness, something shifted — a shadow detaching from the stair, and then Kestrel was there, standing in the doorway's depth with a candle stub in her hand, and Vey felt the whole architecture of his prepared speech sag under the weight of her attention. She had been listening, of course. She had probably been listening since his boots hit the lane.

"One berth," she said. Not a question. She was testing the weight of the words.

"One person," Vey said. "Out. Before the chains go back up and the signing happens on the far side of the river without us. After that—" He let it hang. After that, the city's fate belonged to the negotiators, and the negotiators had spent three weeks failing to agree on whose names went on the amnesty lists, and everyone in Vhalen knew what happened to printers when amnesty lists stalled.

"And the terms?" Edran asked quietly. "There are always terms."

"The term is that I choose, and the choice is final, and the name goes under imperial seal tonight." Vey met the old man's eyes. "No names given up. No testimony. No conditions beyond the crossing itself. I want that understood, because I know what offers have been made from my side of this war, and I know why you'd distrust this one."

"You offered papers before," Kestrel said from the shadows. "For a name."

"I did." He did not flinch from it. "This is not that. This is worse, if you can believe it. This is one seat instead of three, and no price except the one you'll pay yourselves."

Silence filled the doorway. Somewhere down the lane a dog was barking at the river, a thin exhausted sound, the sound the whole city made at night.

Tam appeared behind his sister — Vey saw him materialize at the top of the stair, drawn by voices, still dressed for the flooded streets in waders rolled to the knee — and Vey watched the boy understand the announcement in real time, watched hope and calculation arrive together in his face like two messengers with contradictory letters. Tam opened his mouth. Kestrel, without turning, without even moving, said one word: "*Later.*" And the boy closed his mouth, and Vey understood that whatever authority operated in this family, it did not sit with the father anymore, and possibly it never had.

"When does the barge cross?" Kestrel asked.

"Friday. Dawn tide. The manifest is sealed at midnight Thursday."

"Then we have until midnight Thursday." She said it the way she must have said *two hundred copies*, evenly, converting terror into task. "You'll want our answer before then."

"I'll want your answer," Vey said carefully, "as a family. Not as a committee of the cell, Mistress Zhou — I know the difference, and I'm telling you I know it, because I want you to understand that this berth is dirty. It comes from the same hand that burns chandleries. Whatever purity you can preserve, preserve it by refusing me if refusing is right. I mean that."

"Do you?" she said. "Because I think you've brought this to us precisely because you couldn't bear to bring it anywhere else, and you're hoping we'll do your weeping for you."

The words landed harder than any blow he had taken in eleven months, chiefly because they were true. He had walked past the widow Halloway's ruined street to get here. He had walked past the schoolteacher's empty house. Any of them might have been given the berth — the widow whose son faced the provost, the teacher's wife teaching orphans letters in a borrowed room — and he had come to Cordwainer's Lane instead, to the shop with the portrait of Lian Zhou behind the canvas, because his discretion had rotted through and what remained was not justice but longing, dressed in a dispatch case.

"Yes," he said. "I believe I am. Choose accordingly."

* * *

He did not leave immediately. He stood a moment longer in the rain that had begun to fall, fine and needling, and looked past Edran Zhou's shoulder into the dim shop, to where the corner of the framed atlas showed behind its covering cloth — he had seen it once, weeks ago, before he was meant to see it: a hand-inked map of the city, every sympathetic household marked with a small faithful star. They said copies of it were in a hundred houses now. They said soldiers had begun to pocket the stars' addresses rather than report them. An empire could survive losing a province, Vey thought. It could not survive learning that its own map had been redrawn by a girl with a pen.

"Captain," Edran said, low, as Vey turned to go. The old man's voice had changed — stripped of neutrality, down to the bare wood. "Why us? Truly."

Vey considered lying, and found he had used up his allowance of lies for one lifetime, or nearly.

"Because your daughter's mother once told me that a man shows what he is by what he counts," he said. "I have spent eleven months counting everything wrong. This is the last thing on my ledger I can still count right. Whether I succeed — that isn't in my hands. Whether you're given the chance — that was."

He walked back down Cordwainer's Lane through the needle-rain, and behind him he heard the door close, softly, the way doors close in houses where shouting would be fatal.

* * *

That night he sat in the customs house until the lamps burned low, drafting the manifest form he would be required to file, and found himself unable to write a single name in the blank space reserved for *recipient*. He tried each of them in turn, speaking them aloud to the empty room like a man rehearsing a confession.

*"Edran Zhou."* And the old man crosses, and spends his exile paying protection money to his own conscience, and his children stay behind in a city that will remember what he may have sold.

*"Tam Zhou."* And the boy crosses waving to the crowd like a departing general, and the cause loses nothing it cannot spare, and the boy loses the only thing that could have saved him, which is the chance to stay and atone.

*"Kestrel Zhou."* And the sentence would not complete itself. His pen hovered over the line and his hand — a hand that had signed execution orders, requisitions, reports that ended lives with bureaucratic calm — that hand would not move.

Outside, the river rose another hand's width against the quay stairs. Friday was two days off. Midnight Thursday was one night off after that. Somewhere across the dark water the negotiators were sharpening their pens on the same whetstone as the reprisal squads, and here in Vhalen a family was sitting around a guttering candle performing the oldest arithmetic there is — the arithmetic where the sum is always one, and every remainder is a person.

Vey blew out his lamp and sat in the dark, listening to the rain, and waited to learn what mercy cost when it was measured out one soul at a time.

He would learn sooner than he expected. The manifest, when it finally reached his desk, would not bear any name he had imagined.
