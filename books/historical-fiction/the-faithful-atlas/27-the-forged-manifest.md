## 27. The Forged Manifest

The house had gone quiet the way it only went quiet after arguments — not peaceful, but emptied, as though every voice in it had been spent and nothing remained but the settling of dust. Kestrel lay in her narrow bed above the shop and listened to her father's tread cross the floor below, once, twice, then stop. Tam's door had closed an hour ago and had not opened since. Through the wall she could hear him turning, the creak of the frame, the small animal sounds of a young man trying to sleep inside his own guilt.

The berth. One berth.

She turned the words over the way she'd once turned pieces of type, feeling their weight, their edges, the way they caught the light differently at different angles. Her father wanted Tam to take it. Tam wanted their father to take it. And each of them, with that particular cruelty that love perfected, had looked at her last — not because they'd forgotten her, but because they could not bear to remember her, could not bear to let the arithmetic include a daughter who had already given so much of herself away in ink and silence.

They think I don't see it, she thought. They think I'm the one who stays behind because staying is what I do.

Well. They were right about one thing. Staying was what she did. She stayed with the type. She stayed with the secrets. She stayed when Marta was taken, when the chandlery burned, when the schoolteacher's name went into Vey's reports like a coin into a till. Staying was her craft now, as much as setting type, as much as reading which silences kept people alive.

But there was one more thing she knew how to do, something neither her father nor her brother had ever fully understood, though both of them had benefited from it all their lives.

She knew how to make paper say anything she wished.

* * *

She rose past midnight, dressing in the dark by touch, and stood a moment at the window. Snow had begun again — thin, dry flakes that made no sound as they fell, sifting down through the light of the watch-lamps along the quay until the whole city seemed to be dissolving gently into itself. The river ran black beneath the bridge, swollen with the season, carrying ice from the upper provinces in plates large enough to shatter against the pilings. Somewhere out on that water, moored in the shadow of the customs house, lay the supply barge that would carry one person across the closed bridge before the armistice concluded. One person. The Empire's mercy measured out in exact, insufficient portions.

Below, the shop smelled of ink and cold metal and the tallow stub her father still burned when he worked late, though he hadn't worked late in weeks. Not since the confrontation. Not since she had laid the ledger open between them like a wound and demanded to know which side of him had sold names. He had denied it — denied it with tears, even, or something close to tears — but denial was a thing that could be typeset too, and she had learned from the best how a man might set a false line and believe it true by the time the ink dried.

She lit no lamp. She didn't need one. Twenty years of this room had taught her hands the geography of everything in it: the composing stick on its hook, the galley trays, the drawer where the good knives lived beside the burnishers. She moved through the dark the way a fish moves through its own water.

The manifest lay in the drawer beneath the cash box, where Vey had left it three days ago with a look she was still learning to read. *One berth,* he had said. *The barge crosses the night after next. The name must be entered before then, and once entered, it cannot be altered.* He had said it formally, in his officer's voice, but his eyes had gone to the portrait of her mother hanging crooked above the press, and Kestrel had understood that whatever else Aldous Vey was — occupier, interrogator, the man who quoted her mother's handwriting back to her like a knife drawn slowly — he was also a man performing a mercy he could not afford to perform, and hoping she would not look too closely at the seams of it.

She did not look at the seams. She looked at the document itself.

She carried it to the workbench and unshuttered the small lamp there, turning the wick low, so that the light fell in a circle no wider than her two hands. The manifest was a printed form — imperial stock, heavy rag paper, watermarked with the double-headed eagle that the provinces had spent a year trying to pull apart. Someone in the quartermaster's office had filled it out in a fair round hand: the barge's designation, the date of crossing, the number of berths authorized (four; three already assigned to supply officers), and the fourth line, waiting.

**Passenger: ______________**

Beneath it, the certification: *Entered by order of Capt. A. Vey, garrison command, Vhalen.*

Kestrel sat down before it and did not touch it for a long time.

This was the moment, she understood, where a person became what they would be for the rest of their life. Not at the signing of the armistice, not at the burning of the chandlery, not even at the press in the deep hours printing death-notices under false names. All of those had been rehearsals. This was the performance. Because everything she had done before had been done *for* someone — for the cell, for the dead strikers, for Tam's carelessness, for her mother's memory. What she was about to do had no audience, no cause, no glory. It was theft of the purest kind: she meant to steal her family's grief from them, to rob her father of his sacrifice and her brother of his redemption, and to leave them nothing in exchange but a life without her in it.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She had expected them to shake as they had shaken the first night at the press, setting forbidden type alone, terror transfigured line by line into purpose. But terror, she was discovering, was a finite substance. You spent it. And what remained afterward was something colder and more durable — the thing her father must have felt all those years, paying protection to both sides, keeping his accounts in two hands. Not courage. Arithmetic.

Except that her arithmetic came out differently than his.

* * *

She began, as she always began, with the materials.

The manifest's hand was round and upright, a clerk's hand, disciplined but not beautiful — the writing of a man who wrote numbers all day and names only occasionally. Kestrel studied it under the low lamp, tilting the page, watching how the ink sat on the rag paper. Iron gall, recently applied; it had browned only slightly at the strokes' edges. She would need to match that. Fresh ink would shine wrong under any examining eye, and there would be an examining eye — Vey's, if no one else's, and possibly a harbor clerk's besides.

From the cabinet of jobbing supplies she took her father's old ink cake, the one ground soft with age, and mixed it thin with a drop of vinegar and rainwater caught from the sill, working it with the muller until it matched the browned tone of the original. She tested it on a scrap of the same stock — she had reams of imperial forms bought honestly over the years, forms her father had printed for the customs house back when the customs house still trusted him — and held the scrap beside the manifest until the difference disappeared. It did not disappear entirely. Nothing ever did, for a careful eye. But it disappeared enough.

Then the hand. She practiced on scraps for the better part of an hour, her breath fogging faintly in the cold, writing the same phrase over and over: *Kestrel Zhou, printer's daughter, Vhalen.* No — not that. Just the name. Names traveled lighter. *Kestrel Zhou.* She learned the clerk's habits: the way he crossed his t's with a single flat stroke, the slight rightward lean he fell into at the ends of lines, the loop he made of a capital K that was really just a vertical stroke with pretensions. She learned his hesitations, too — the places where his pen had pressed harder, correcting a wobble, and she reproduced the wobbles, because a forgery without flaws was its own kind of confession.

It occurred to her, somewhere in the middle of this, that she was doing the most honest work of her life. Every other piece she had ever set had been someone else's truth — the cell's proclamations, Marta's grief, the atlas of safe houses drawn in her own careful hand but full of other people's courage. This was hers. Hers alone, from conception to composition. The first document in the world that said exactly and only what Kestrel Zhou intended, with no one's survival but her own conscience bound up in it.

*The ultimate act of authorship,* she thought, and almost laughed, and did not, because the sound would have gone up through the floorboards and woken the men who slept above the very press that had raised her.

* * *

Before she wrote, she erased.

That was the part no one warned you about, she thought — that forgery was mostly destruction. The manifest had been left blank in the passenger line, yes, but blankness was never truly blank. There was the sizing of the paper, disturbed wherever a pen had hovered. There was the faintest ghosting where Vey himself might have begun a name and lifted the nib — she found such a ghost under the lamp, angled just so: the beginning of a capital letter, abandoned. A *T*, she thought. Or perhaps a *Z*. He had started to write one of their names and stopped himself, leaving the decision to her, and the ghost of his hesitation lay on the page like a fingerprint.

She erased it. Pumice dust, applied with a feather, worked into the fibers until the ghost dissolved. Then she burnished the spot smooth with the back of a spoon, patiently, the way her father had taught her to smooth a sheet before printing fine work. *Paper remembers,* he used to say, holding a spoiled impression up to the light. *Everything you do to it, it keeps. Your job is to give it something worth remembering.*

She wondered, with a distant, floating sadness, whether he would ever say such things to her again. Whether anything would ever again pass between them except the necessary courtesies of people sharing a roof over a fault line.

Then she dipped the pen.

*Kestrel Zhou.*

Three words. Eleven letters. She wrote them in the clerk's borrowed hand, in the clerk's borrowed ink, with the clerk's borrowed hesitations, and when she finished she sat looking at them for a long time while the lamp gutted and the snow ticked softly against the shutters.

It was done. It was undone. Both at once. Somewhere across the city, her father slept believing his son would cross the bridge; somewhere above her head, Tam slept believing his father would. And here on this bench, in a circle of light no wider than her hands, their daughter had quietly subtracted herself from every future they had planned and written herself into one they would have died to prevent.

She thought about her mother. She thought about how her mother had died — not dramatically, not for a cause, but stupidly, in the ordinary way, of fever, in a city that could not get medicine across a river. The closed bridge had killed her mother as surely as any soldier. And now the bridge would take her daughter instead, in the opposite direction, toward whatever the provinces held for a girl whose name would soon appear on no amnesty list, whose face would hang in no square among the honored dead, who would simply be *gone*, the way the flood took things — completely, without ceremony, without even the courtesy of a body to mourn.

No. They would mourn. That was the cruelty of it. They would mourn her and blame themselves, and her father would find the manifest eventually — he would have to, when the barge crossed and she did not come home — and he would read her name in that borrowed hand and know exactly what she had done, because he, of all people, would recognize the craftsmanship. He had taught her everything she knew about making paper lie.

Let him know, she thought fiercely, pressing her palms flat against the bench. Let him spend the rest of his life knowing. It was a terrible wish and she let herself have it, because she was learning that sacrifice which is never resented is never real.

* * *

There remained the matter of erasing her family entirely — not just from the manifest, but from the record. If she crossed and they stayed, questions would come. Questions always came; Vey had taught her that much. Who remained in the Zhou household? What printers operated at that address? Whose type had produced the pamphlets, the death-notices, the atlas? A family that surrendered one member to exile while keeping two in a city under reprisal was a family still half-caught. But a household with no children at all — a widower and his press, plain, declared, harmless — that was a story the record could hold without straining.

So she took a fresh sheet and drafted, in her own hand this time, the documents she would leave behind. A bill of sale, dated two years past, recording the sale of her apprenticeship papers to a printer in the lower provinces — a printer who had, conveniently, drowned in the spring floods, taking his records with him. A letter of farewell, undated, to be "found" later: a daughter grown restless, gone south before the bridge closed, seeking work. Tam's name she left untouched everywhere she could reach; her father's she wrapped in the dull anonymity of a man too old and too broken to matter to either side.

It was, she reflected, the strangest edition she would ever produce: an edition of one, print run of zero, distributed nowhere, its entire purpose to unmake its author. Every other thing she had printed had added her to the world. This work existed solely to remove her.

When the documents were done she blotted them, stacked them, and hid them where her father would find them in the natural course of his morning — tucked into the ledger, that ledger of double payments and quiet corruptions, so that the discovery would carry its own bitter symmetry. He would understand immediately. That was the worst gift she could give him and the only one he would believe.

The lamp was dying. Outside, the snow had thickened, and through the gap in the shutters she could see the bridge lamps strung across the black water like a rosary of small fires, each one counting off another hour of the city's held breath. Tomorrow night the barge would cross. Tomorrow night a name would be called at the gate, and a soldier would check it against a list, and the list would say *Kestrel Zhou*, and no one — not the soldier, not the clerk, not even the man who had left the manifest in her keeping — would know that the name had been written by the woman it named.

She cleaned the pen. She ground the remaining ink into the stone and wiped the stone clean. She returned the pumice and the spoon and the scrap sheets to their places, and burned the practice pages one by one in the cold stove, feeding them in slowly so they flared and curled and left almost no ash. Paper remembers, her father said. But paper also burns, and what burns cannot testify.

Last of all she picked up the manifest itself, holding it at arm's length in the guttering light, reading her own forged name the way she had read ten thousand documents in this shop — suspiciously, professionally, hunting for the flaw that would undo it.

She found none. Only the ghost of Vey's abandoned capital, smoothed away. Only eleven letters in a stranger's faithful hand. Only a daughter, erased from her family and entered into the record of her own departure, signed by no one, witnessed by no one, authored by the only person in Vhalen who would ever know the whole truth of it.

She slid the manifest back into the drawer beneath the cash box and shuttered the lamp, and stood a moment in the absolute dark, listening to the river tear at the pilings below.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked.

Not her father's tread — too light, too hesitant. Tam, sleepless, moving toward the stair. Toward the shop. Toward the drawer where, if he went looking — and he would, tonight of all nights, unable to bear the waiting — he would find the manifest, and the name upon it, and everything she had built in the dark would begin coming apart hours before the barge ever reached the bridge.

Kestrel pressed herself flat against the cold wall beside the press and held her breath as his foot found the first step.
