## 16. Hunted Through the Warrens

The warning note was still crumpled in her fist when the first lantern swung into Salt Row.

*They know. The bench. Not the loft. Run.*

Tadao's handwriting — she'd have known it blind, that cramped, apologetic slant, the same hand that had signed her apprenticeship papers and pressed a coin into her palm on market days. He had sold her, and then he had tried to buy her back with six words and a shutter left unlatched. She hadn't decided yet whether she could forgive him for either.

No time to decide anything. The knock at the workshop door had come three minutes after the note slid through the gap beneath it, and Felix had gone out the back over the coal bin with her coat still half-buttoned, the letter flat against her ribs where it had lived for two days now, sewn into the lining by her own hands in stitches so small they'd made her eyes water.

Salt Row emptied into the warrens — the tangle of lanes behind the fish market where the buildings leaned together like conspirators and the drainage ran black even in daylight. At night it was worse. At night it was perfect.

She went in low and fast, shoulders hunched, boots finding the dry line along the wall where the cobbles rose above the muck. Behind her, voices. Not Voss's — he wouldn't shout; shouting wasn't his trade — but his men had learned their trade from him, and they swept the district the way he did everything: methodically, patiently, in overlapping lines.

*Left at the cooper's. Right through the passage behind the smokehouse.* Her father had walked her these streets when she was nine, teaching her the city the way he taught her movements — *every mechanism has its slack, little bird, find the slack and you own the machine.* The warrens were all slack. Doors that didn't latch. Alleys that dead-ended but for a window nobody watched. A city built by dockworkers who'd needed to move things without asking permission, and the permission-seekers had never bothered to learn it.

A whistle cut the air behind her — two short, one long. Answered from ahead. They were folding the net closed.

Felix stopped in the black throat of an alley between a rope-walk and a derelict sail loft, pressed herself against wet brick, and did the arithmetic. Four men minimum, probably six, working a pincer from the harbor end. The warrens had three ways out: the fish-market steps, which would be watched; the culvert under Customs Row, which flooded on the tide and was flooding now; and the chandler's yard off Gull Lane, whose owner, a woman named Petra Voss— no relation, thank God — owed the Ishikawa workshop eleven years of clock repairs paid late and never once complained.

The slack. Find the slack.

She moved.

* * *

Gull Lane smelled of tallow and brine, and the chandler's yard was a canyon of stacked barrels with a rope ladder hanging down the side of the drying shed like something forgotten mid-escape. Felix climbed it with her heart slamming so hard she could feel it in her teeth, and rolled onto the loft floor among the nets.

Nets everywhere. Hung from beams, coiled in drifts, draped over frames like the shed skins of enormous animals. The loft was a single long room under a roof of tarred canvas, moonless-dark except for one seam of lamplight leaking up through the floorboards from the workroom below. Somewhere below, a dog shifted in its sleep and settled again.

Felix crawled deep into the nets until she found the pocket between two great hung masses where the mesh crossed and knotted, and she folded herself into it, knees to chest, and waited for her breathing to become something a person could survive inside of.

It didn't cooperate. Her breath came in shreds. Her hands, when she looked at them, were shaking with a fine fast tremor, and she thought absurdly of a balance wheel out of beat — *amplitude too high, regulate, regulate* — as if she could adjust herself with a pin and a steady eye.

They knew about the bench. Which meant Tadao had told them where she'd be, which meant they knew her face, or thought they did — the apprentice boy, the Ishikawa boy, the warrant Voss had been building since the observatory gate. She had spent four years making herself invisible, sanding away every soft edge of her voice and walk, binding and cutting and standing wrong on purpose, and now the invisibility was the thing hunting her. A boy was missing from a workshop. Boys didn't vanish in this city without every lantern in the district swinging toward the dark.

And underneath the fear, colder and quieter, sat the other thing. The thing she kept having to look at straight-on before it grew teeth.

Her father had done it. Or been part of it. The manifest in her coat named Ishikawa & Sons in cargo fraud, and the handwriting was his, and Uncle Tadao had sworn an oath to a dying man rather than tell her, and somewhere in the last two days the ground under her whole life had stopped being ground and started being another mechanism — one she couldn't see into, full of parts she hadn't known existed, running on motives she'd never been shown.

She held the letter through the coat fabric, palm flat against it, against her heartbeat. It felt less like paper every hour and more like a bone. Something structural. Something that would not heal right if she broke it.

Boots in Gull Lane. She went absolutely still.

Two men, walking slow, talking in the low carrying murmur of men who want to be heard by each other and no one else.

"—checked the sail loft, checked the cooperage—"

"Voss says the yards. Says a boy on the run goes to ground where there's work. Thinks he'll try for the harbor before the tide."

"Thinks? Corven thinks. Voss just stands there looking at you like you're a watch he's deciding whether to open."

A grunt. Boots receding. Then, fainter: "Check the chandler's."

Felix's blood turned to seawater.

Below her, through the floorboards, a bolt drew back. Lamplight bloomed up through the seams. Petra's voice, rough with sleep and irritation: "At this hour? Whatever you're selling, I'm not buying till dawn."

"Looking for a boy," said one of the men. "Apprentice age. Might've come over your wall."

"Half the boys in this district have come over my wall at one time or another. None of them tonight." A pause, deliberate, the pause of a woman who had spent thirty years selling to magistrates' men and knew exactly how much truth each purchase required. "You want to search my loft, you can bring a writ. You want to wake me again, bring coffee."

The door shut. The bolt went home. The lamplight died.

Felix lay in the nets and discovered she had been holding the letter so hard the stitching had printed itself into her palm.

* * *

Time behaved strangely in the loft. Without a clock — and there was no clock, the chandler kept time by tide and habit — the hours lost their edges and ran together like wet ink. Felix counted instead. Heartbeats, breaths, the intervals of the dog shifting below, the far-off bells of Saint Aldric's marking quarters with a third bell that dragged flat because nobody had serviced the striking train since winter and the thought of it itched in her like a splinter.

*Even now,* she thought bitterly. *Hiding from men who want to arrest me, and I'm diagnosing a stranger's bell.*

But it steadied her, the counting. That was the thing her father had given her that no one could take back, whatever else he'd done — the discipline of the mechanism. When the work went wrong, when a mainspring snapped or a client raged, he'd set his tools down, close his eyes, and count the beat. *The machine doesn't care about your feelings, little bird. Find the beat and hold it.*

So she found the beat.

Fact: they believed the letter might be aboard the morning ship, or that she might try to reach the harbor master before it sailed. Either way, everything converged on the pier at dawn. Fact: Voss was patient, but patience has a budget, and Corven's budget was the tide. Fact: Maren Ostrow had promised — had *insisted*, with that tower-keeper's stubbornness that treated promises like load-bearing walls — to meet her at the pier watch with the synchronization order, the one document in this city that could put a clockmaker on a boat legitimately.

Which meant Felix had to be at the pier watch before dawn. Through the third checkpoint. With the letter against her ribs and her face on every lantern-swinging list in the district.

Fear came back then, raw and total, a cold flood up from the floorboards, and for a long moment she let it — let it pour through her the way the tide poured through the culvert, because fighting it took strength she needed elsewhere. She was twenty-two years old and she had never been so frightened in her life, not the night her father died, not the first time she'd bound her chest and walked into the guild hall wearing his name like armor two sizes too big. This was different. This was being hunted. This was knowing that if they caught her, the letter would burn and her mother would hang beside her uncle and the word *Ishikawa* would mean *thief* for a hundred years, and it would all be tidy, all be official, all be stamped and filed in Corven's immaculate hand.

And under the fear, growing up through it like a spring through rubble:

Stubbornness.

Not courage. She distrusted the word. Courage was what other people claimed afterward. This was simpler and uglier — the sheer refusal to be handled. Corven wanted her documents destroyed and her family erased and the record made clean, and every instinct in her body, every gear-train her father had ever wound up in her, said *no*. Said it without drama. Said it the way a pallet stone says no to a wheel it isn't ready to release.

She thought of her mother, sitting up last night with the household ledgers spread across the kitchen table, forty years of entries in a hand as precise as engraving — *flour, oil, lamp-black, 2d, honest weight confirmed* — and how her mother had said, in that flat unimpressed voice she used for bad weather and bad news: "They think we keep books because we're careful. We keep books because we are *right*." 

Felix pressed her palm flat against the letter one more time, feeling her pulse knock against it, and made herself a promise with the shape of a mechanism: *I will get to the pier. I will put this paper in front of witnesses. And then I will sleep for a week.*

* * *

The sky over the warehouse roofs went from black to iron to the color of old pewter, and the bells of Saint Aldric's struck four — the flat third bell dragging its note like a lame foot, and Felix thought, *someone should fix that,* and almost laughed, and the laugh came out as a sob, and she let both happen quietly in the nets.

She climbed down the ladder into a yard silvered with early fog. The chandler's dog raised its head, considered her, and decided she was furniture. Petra stood in the workroom doorway with a lamp, arms crossed, hair bound up in a scarf, looking exactly as unsurprised as she had sounded through the floorboards.

"Eleven years of late payments," Felix whispered. "I'm calling in the debt."

Petra studied her for a long moment — the boy's coat, the shaking hands, the eyes. Whatever she saw, she'd apparently seen enough of it in thirty years on Gull Lane to price it instantly. "Culvert's passable for another hour, till the tide turns. Comes out by the net-drying racks, fifty yards from the pier watch." She jerked her chin at Felix's coat. "There's a fish crate by the gate. Smell like cargo. Walk like cargo. Cargo doesn't run."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Fix my kitchen clock while you're at it, it's been slow since Christmas." She paused, and something almost gentle crossed the weathered face. "Your father fixed my mother's clock once. Wouldn't take payment. Said some things you do for the record." Petra spat neatly into the tallow-barrel shadow. "Go on, then. Be for the record."

Felix went.

The culvert was waist-deep and freezing and smelled like the underside of the city, and she waded it with one hand raised holding her coat collar closed over the letter as if she could keep the water off the paper by will alone. The fog thickened near the harbor until the world shrank to ten feet in any direction — masts appearing overhead like drowned trees, rigging dripping somewhere unseen, the gulls already awake and arguing.

And then the fog thinned, and there it was: the pier, the ship riding at her moorings with her cargo lights burning yellow, the watch-post lantern blazing at the gangplank head, and figures moving behind it. Checkpoint three. The last door.

Beside the net-drying racks, exactly where Petra said, a slight figure in a keeper's heavy coat stood waiting in the gray light, a leather document case under one arm, scanning the fog with the fierce patience of someone who had decided, once and for all, whom to trust.

Maren saw her and lifted the case slightly, and even at fifty yards Felix could read the shape of the words forming silently on her lips:

*Papers. Show them papers. Walk.*
