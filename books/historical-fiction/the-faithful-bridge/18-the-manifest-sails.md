## 18. The Manifest Sails

The lanterns along the pier burned low and orange against a sky that had not yet decided to be morning. Felix stood in the crowd at the harbor gate with her collar up and her cap pulled down and the letter flat against her ribs, sewn there two nights ago by her own hands with stitches so small they might have been made by a machine — or by a mother who kept books to the half-penny.

The *Meridian Star* rode at anchor beyond the breakwater, waiting for the tide. From this distance she was all rigging and patience, a black lattice of masts against the graying east. The manifest that named Ishikawa & Sons in a fraud her father may or may not have committed was somewhere aboard her — in the captain's strongbox, Felix assumed, or folded into the harbor master's duplicate file, or already in Corven's coat pocket, ready to be produced at the right moment like a knife at a dinner party.

But the letter was here. Against her ribs. Warm from her own body.

She had learned something in the chandler's loft yesterday, crouched among nets that smelled of tar and old fish while boots hammered past on the street below: fear does not stay fear. Held long enough, pressed tight enough against the chest, it changes state. It becomes something harder. Not courage exactly — she would not flatter herself with that word — but a kind of stubborn mineral, cold and dense, that a person can carry the way a mason carries stone.

Voss's men were everywhere.

She counted six of them without moving her eyes: two at the customs house doors, one lounging against the bollard nearest the gangway, two walking the quay in slow opposite circuits, and one — young, nervous, his uniform still creased from its folding — stationed at the harbor gate itself, checking papers with the excessive thoroughness of a man who has been told that his career depends on finding something.

They were not looking for her. That was the exquisite joke of it. They were looking for an apprentice boy named Felix Ishikawa, and Felix Ishikawa was a fiction she had been wearing for three years, tailored so well that even she sometimes forgot the seams. Voss had described her to his men, no doubt — a slight youth, quick hands, ink-stained fingers — and every such description in this city fit a hundred boys, and none of them fit the woman standing ten feet away, breathing carefully, watching the tide come in.

*Invisible and hunted,* Maren had said. *The best place to hide is inside the thing they're chasing.*

Maren. Felix scanned the tower road above the customs house. Nothing yet. The observatory stood black against the lightening sky, its dome catching the first gray like a held breath. If Maren's plan worked — if the synchronization order arrived, official seal and all, demanding a clockmaker's presence aboard for the chronometer check before departure — then Felix would walk onto that pier not as a fugitive but as a tradesman summoned by paperwork. Bureaucracy was a language, and she had spent her life learning to speak it fluently. Her father had taught her that too, though he'd never known he was teaching it: *Every institution trusts its own forms more than it trusts any man. Learn the forms.*

*Papa,* she thought, and stopped herself. There would be time for grief later. Grief was a luxury that required both hands, and hers were full.

* * *

The trouble began, as trouble did, with Corven arriving early.

He came down the tower road in a closed carriage at half past five, when the sky was still the color of unpolished pewter, and stepped out onto the quay with the unhurried grace of a man attending the opening of an opera he had personally commissioned. He wore a dark coat and gray gloves. He did not look at the crowd. He looked at the ship.

Felix felt the mineral in her chest go colder.

Beside him walked Voss, and even at forty paces Felix could read the change in the inspector's posture. He moved like a man who had been given orders he did not entirely believe in. His head was slightly bowed, his hands clasped behind his back, and when Corven spoke to him he answered with nods rather than words. Something had happened between them. Something had cracked, or was about to.

*Not now,* Felix told herself. *Whatever it is, it isn't yours yet.*

Corven raised one gloved hand, and the search began.

It was not a quiet thing. He had brought twelve men from the magistrate's guard, and they went up the gangway two abreast with the confidence of people who expected to find what they had been sent to find. Felix watched them disappear into the ship's throat and imagined what they imagined: a sealed letter hidden in a cabin wall, a duplicate manifest tucked into a chart drawer, the whole conspiracy of paper waiting to be plucked out and carried ashore in triumph.

They believed the letter was aboard because Corven needed it to be aboard. That was the arithmetic she had finally understood, lying awake in the loft with the nets dripping above her: Corven had planted the frame, and the frame required originals. A forgery exposed was merely a scandal; a forgery *destroyed* was a verdict. He could not let the manifest sail with its twin still loose in the city, because a loose original meant a trial, and a trial meant witnesses, and witnesses meant Voss, and Voss — Felix watched him now, standing a careful half-step behind his patron — Voss was beginning to look like a man doing sums of his own.

So Corven had arranged for the letter to be "discovered" aboard the *Meridian Star*, seized in front of the harbor master's clerks, and burned or buried where no court would ever see it. Clean. Procedural. The Ishikawa name convicted by absence.

Except the letter was not aboard. The letter was standing in the crowd with its heart going like a flywheel.

A gull screamed somewhere overhead, absurd and indifferent. The tide turned. Felix felt it turn in the water below the pier, the long slow exhale of the harbor beginning to run out toward the sea, and she knew the ship would sail within the hour whether or not twelve guardsmen tore her apart plank by plank.

*Let them search,* she thought. *Search everything. You'll find nothing but your own reflection in the bilge water.*

* * *

They found plenty. Just none of it was anything.

Felix drifted closer along the fence line, close enough now to hear the reports coming back down the gangway. The captain of the *Meridian Star*, a broad Swede with a beard like winter brush, met each party of searchers at his rail with mounting indignation. She heard fragments — *"my cargo holds are not a magistrate's pantry"* — and *"I have a tide window and you are spending it"* — and once, gloriously, the captain's voice rising over the whole quay: *"Then arrest the ship, gentlemen, and explain to her owners why their freight missed the London market!"*

Corven did not flinch. He stood at the foot of the gangway consulting a paper — the warrant, presumably, or perhaps a list of hiding places he had invented himself — while his men emerged one by one empty-handed. A strongbox opened and inventoried: bills of lading, coin, the captain's private correspondence. A chart room turned out shelf by shelf. The crew mustered on deck and questioned in a bunch, sullen and uncomprehending. Two sailors' sea chests dumped out onto wet decking, shirts and souvenirs scattering in the wind, and the captain's roar at that was heard clear across the harbor.

Nothing. No letter. No manifest beyond the legitimate ones, which proved only what legitimate manifests prove.

Felix watched Corven's gloved hand tighten on the paper he held. It was a small motion, a fraction of an inch, but she had spent her life reading mechanisms, and a man under strain moves exactly like a clock under strain — some gear slipping somewhere deep in the train, invisible to anyone who hadn't been taught to look.

*You built a trap for a letter,* she thought, *and the letter is watching you from thirty feet away.*

And then — because triumph, she was learning, always arrived wearing exhaustion's clothes — the thought curdled. Because if the letter wasn't aboard, Corven would conclude, sooner or later, that it had never left the city. And every hour it remained in the city was an hour it remained near her, near her mother, near Tadao, near the workshop with its little brass bell above the door. The ship sailing empty-handed was not the end of anything. It was Corven discovering that his quarry had never been where he was looking, which meant that by tonight he would be looking everywhere else.

The tide waited for no man's conspiracy. At six-forty the harbor master's clerk came down the pier with the sailing papers, and Corven, after one last glance up at the rigging — one last hope that the thing he needed might drop out of the sky into his glove — waved the search off with a gesture so controlled it looked almost casual.

The *Meridian Star* warped off the pier into the ebbing tide.

Felix stood in the crowd and watched her go. The tugs took her bow, the great sails woke one by one, and the ship leaned out past the breakwater into the open gray, carrying her honest cargo and her innocent papers toward London, carrying nothing of the Ishikawas at all except a name on a manifest that would now sit in some foreign customs house, unread, unweaponized, slowly becoming just paper.

It should have felt like victory. It felt like the moment after a long fever breaks — the sweat cooling, the trembling starting, the strange grief of being well again. Around her the crowd was dispersing, disappointed in the way crowds are disappointed by searches that find nothing, already turning the morning into anecdote. The gulls resumed their arguments. Somewhere a bell rang the quarter hour, and Felix's ear, trained since childhood, noted absently that it rang four seconds slow.

Four seconds slow. She almost laughed. Even the harbor bells were drifting, and no one had fixed them, and someone would have to, and that someone, apparently, was her.

"Miss Ostrow said you'd be here."

Felix went rigid. Then she placed the voice — low, dry, coming from beside the fish stall where a figure in an observatory coat stood pretending to examine smoked herring. Maren Ostrow did not look at her. Maren Ostrow continued to look at herring with the focused interest of a genuine customer.

"You didn't need the order," Maren murmured. "I watched from the tower road. You never moved toward the gangway." A pause. "Good. Moving toward the gangway would have been stupid."

"It nearly killed me not to."

"I know. I could see it from here. You have a very loud face, apprentice." Maren selected a herring, paid for it, and thereby acquired the perfect prop for walking beside Felix out of the crowd. "Corven will know by noon that the ship was clean. By evening he'll tear the district apart. Where is it?"

Felix touched her ribs, once, through the coat. Maren nodded as though Felix had recited coordinates.

"The harbor master's office opens at eight," Maren said. "Ostrow the keeper keeps irregular hours, but the office keeps regular ones. If you mean to do this, do it today, before he decides the letter might be anywhere and starts deciding it might be *here*." She handed Felix the wrapped herring, absurdly, like a gift between friends. "Eat something. You look like a mechanism running on no oil at all."

"Maren." Felix caught her sleeve. "The tower. After. Whatever happens — the clock needs winding, and the strike train is still—"

"I know how to wind my own clock," Maren said. But her hand covered Felix's for a moment, brief and hard, and then she walked away toward the tower road with her herring, and Felix stood alone on the quay with the crowd thinning around her and the sun finally clearing the eastern warehouses, laying long gold stripes across the wet stones.

Eight o'clock. Ninety minutes.

She ate the herring standing up, because her body demanded it and because a courier or an apprentice loitering with food drew no eyes at all. The letter pressed warm against her side with every breath. Her father's handwriting was in there, folded around her father's crime or her father's sacrifice — she still didn't know which, wouldn't know until she read it again, and she had promised herself she would not read it again until it was in the hands of someone whose job was truth.

The harbor master's office. Witnesses. The record. Voss, if Voss could be turned — and something in the inspector's bowed shoulders this morning suggested the turning had already begun, quietly, in whatever room Corven kept for breaking his instruments.

Felix turned toward the customs house square, and that was when she saw the boy.

He was maybe fourteen, workshop-shy, running along the quay with the particular desperate economy of a messenger who has been threatened. He weaved through the fish carts, scanned faces, and stopped dead when he saw her — saw the cap, the coat, the build — and Felix knew, in the space of one heartbeat, that the note in his fist had her name on it.

"Felix Ishikawa?" the boy called out. Loudly. In front of everyone.

The mineral in her chest turned to ice water. Down the quay, one of Voss's lingering men lifted his head.

The boy thrust the note forward, breathless. "From the gentleman at the clock shop. He said — he said run, and don't stop for anyone, and give it to—" He squinted at the note's address line, then at her. "To the apprentice. Are you the apprentice?"

Felix took the note. The paper was cheap and the writing on it was Tadao's, hurried, the characters leaning like drunks:

*HE KNOWS WHERE YOU WILL BE. NOT THE OFFICE. HE HAS SENT FOR YOU TO THE OBSERVATORY — HE WANTS NO WITNESSES. VOSS IS COMING WITH HIM. DO NOT GO ALONE. DO NOT GO AT ALL. I AM SORRY. I WAS ALWAYS SORRY. — T.*

She read it twice. The quay seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a deck tilts.

*He knows.* Of course he knew. Tadao had told Corven everything days ago — the bench, the betrayal, the warning note through the shutter; her uncle had been playing both sides of a losing game and had finally, in terror or love or both, told her the truth. Corven wanted no witnesses. Corven wanted the clock room, where the master clock struck the hours and no clerk ever climbed the stairs, where a magistrate could demand a letter from a frightened apprentice with no one to hear the demand.

He wanted her alone in the room where time was kept.

Felix folded the note small and put it in her pocket next to her courage. Down the quay, the guardsman had begun to drift in her direction, unhurried, curious. Above the rooftops the observatory tower stood in the new sun, its dome bright, its clock face pale and patient, keeping its imperfect time over a harbor full of lies.

She had ninety minutes to reach the harbor master. Corven intended for her to spend them climbing a tower instead.

Felix pulled her cap lower, turned her back on the tower, and started walking — fast, purposeful, a tradesman late for a delivery — toward the customs house square, while behind her, faint on the morning air, the observatory bell began to strike the hour, four seconds slow.
