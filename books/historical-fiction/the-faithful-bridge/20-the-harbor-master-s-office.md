## 20. The Harbor Master's Office

Voss had attended forty-one arrests in his eleven years as an inspector of the port constabulary, and he could not remember a single one that had gone quietly.

There was always shouting. There was always a wife or a partner or a creditor who had to be pulled back by the arms, always someone weeping into their hands or spitting on the floorboards, always the small theater of it — the way a room rearranged itself around the moment the irons came out, everyone suddenly aware of where they stood in relation to power and how quickly that relation might change. He had learned to read rooms the way other men read weather. He had thought himself good at it.

The harbor master's office at half past seven on the morning after the equinox taught him otherwise.

It was a plain room, and it had been made plainer on purpose. Harbor Master Ilsa Brandt had cleared her own desk to the wall and set up a long trestle table in its place, bare except for a lamp, an inkwell, and a ledger opened to a fresh page. Three chairs behind the table — hers, the port solicitor's, and one left empty, which Voss understood only when Felix Ishikawa was shown in and directed to sit in it. Not across from them. Beside them. A witness's chair, arranged like a colleague's.

Brandt was sixty, gray at the temples, with the unhurried hands of a woman who had spent thirty years signing documents that decided whether ships lived or drowned. She did not look up when Corven was escorted in. That was the first sign, Voss thought, of how the morning would go. Magistrate Aldous Corven had entered a hundred rooms in this city and every one of them had turned toward him like a compass finding north. Brandt kept writing. Her pen moved with the sound of a small clock.

Corven noticed. Voss watched him notice — the fractional pause at the threshold, the recalibration behind the eyes. The magistrate recovered fast; he always recovered fast. He came into the room as though it were his, nodded pleasantly to the solicitor, acknowledged Voss with the particular half-smile he reserved for useful men, and took the single chair that had been placed facing the table. Alone. Facing three people and a stack of paper.

"Harbor Master," Corven said. "I confess I'm puzzled by the summons. If this concerns the equinox inspection, I'd have expected—"

"It concerns the manifest of the *Meridian Rose*," Brandt said. "Cargo declared at nine hundred and forty tons. Actual tonnage, per the tally clerk's second count and the stevedore foreman's sworn statement: eleven hundred and twelve." She turned a page without hurry. "The discrepancy was entered into the general ledger on the twenty-ninth of last month. The entry is initialed. Would you like to see whose initials?"

Voss had spent the night deciding what kind of man he would be in this room, and he had not finished deciding until roughly four o'clock in the morning, sitting at his own kitchen table with a cup of tea he never drank, turning the thing over the way he turned over any problem — not whether it was right or wrong, but whether it would hold weight.

He was a man of records. That was the whole of him, if you wanted the truth of it, and he had never minded the smallness of that truth. Other men had convictions, ambitions, loyalties worth dying for. Voss had files. He had spent eleven years building the habit of believing that the paper trail, honestly kept, was the closest thing justice had to a spine. Every case he had ever closed had closed on paper. Confessions were nice; witnesses were useful; but paper did not flinch, did not forget, did not change its story when the questioning got long.

And for eleven years he had served a magistrate who understood that about him, and had used it. Corven had never asked Voss to lie. That was the elegance of the man, the thing Voss had admired even while some quiet chamber below his ribs registered unease. Corven only ever asked him to look at certain papers and not others. To interview certain witnesses before they could be interviewed by anyone else. To lose, once, a deposition that had been inconveniently thorough. Each omission had been small. Each had been deniable. And each had been a brick, and Voss had watched the wall go up around the port's commerce one brick at a time, and had told himself that a wall was not his business. His business was bricks.

Then the apprentice boy had come out of the observatory tower carrying a letter sewn into his coat lining, and the wall had acquired a name on it, and the name was Voss's.

Not literally. Nothing in the letter implicated him. But he had understood, standing in the chandler's loft yesterday with the boy — the girl, he corrected himself, though the correction still sat strangely — watching her hold that folded paper against her chest like something alive, that the letter was a mirror. It showed a man what he had chosen not to see. And Voss had looked into it and seen eleven years of looking away, done neatly, professionally, one omitted page at a time.

So he had come to a decision at four in the morning, and the decision was this: he would not make a speech. He would not perform remorse. He would do the only thing he knew how to do, the only thing that had ever been worth anything in him. He would put the record straight.

"Whose initials," Corven said lightly, "I'm sure I couldn't say. Tally clerks are notoriously careless."

"The initials are A.C.," Brandt said. "Countersigned by your private secretary, Mr. Ellery, who has been in my custody since six o'clock and has given a full statement." She slid a paper down the table. "He says the arrangement was yours. He says it has been yours for two years. He says, and I quote, 'the magistrate told me the harbor books were a formality and that formality was beneath men of consequence.'"

The solicitor, a dry man named Pell who had the face of an unpaid bill, read the statement through and initialed it. The pen scratch was the loudest sound in the room.

Corven laughed. It was a good laugh — warm, rounded, the laugh of a man delighted by an absurdity. Voss had heard it a hundred times, usually just before Corven destroyed someone.

"Ilsa," Corven said, "you've been sold a fiction, and I'm sorry for it. Yes, I've audited irregularities in this port — that is precisely my office. And yes, I've pursued the Ishikawa matter with particular attention, because the Ishikawa firm has been bleeding this harbor white for years, and because—" he spread his hands, wounded magnanimity, "—because I believed the family deserved the dignity of a quiet resolution rather than a public scandal. I extended charity. This is my thanks?"

"That is one account," Brandt said. "Here is another." She lifted a sealed envelope from the tray beside her and broke it open. "A letter, found in the housing of the observatory master clock, in the hand of the late Kenji Ishikawa, describing a scheme of falsified manifests in which he was coerced to participate under threat of license revocation, naming the magistrate of this port as its author. Accompanied by a duplicate manifest in the same hand, marked against the original cargo declarations of the *Meridian Rose*, dated fourteen months ago."

She laid the letter on the table, flat, facing no one in particular, the way evidence is laid — owned by no one now, belonging to the record.

"And here," she said, "are the household ledgers of Sana Ishikawa, covering nineteen years of the firm's accounts, kept independently of the firm's official books, which will demonstrate that the discrepancies in the manifests originate not with the Ishikawas' receipts but with the port's declarations. The arithmetic has been checked twice. Once by me. Once by the guild assessor, who is waiting outside and would like very much to go home."

Corven's smile stayed exactly where it was. That was the discipline of the man, Voss thought, and hated that he still admired it. Only his eyes moved — a quick inventory of the room, the exits, the faces, calculating which lever existed and which had rusted shut. His gaze passed over Voss and paused there, half a heartbeat longer than it should have.

That pause was a question. *Are you mine?*

Voss had imagined this moment all night, and in every imagining his mouth went dry and his conviction wavered and he heard his own voice come out smaller than he intended. In the actual room, none of that happened. The actual room was quiet and well-lit and smelled of ink and cold coffee, and the great fact of it was that nothing dramatic was happening at all. Papers were being read. Statements were being initialed. Justice, it turned out, was not a thunderclap. It was a clerk's morning.

"Inspector Voss," Brandt said, without looking up from the ledger. "You conducted the investigation into the Ishikawa firm at the magistrate's direction. Your report of the eleventh of March concluded that the firm's books showed no actionable irregularity. Is that correct?"

"It is," Voss said.

His voice sounded like someone else's. Steady. Almost bored. Good, he thought. Boredom was the register of truth; only liars needed emphasis.

"And yet the magistrate has since represented publicly that your investigation established fraud by the Ishikawa firm. Which is it?"

"The report found no fraud by the firm," Voss said. "It found irregularities in the port's own declarations, which I flagged in a memorandum attached to the report. The memorandum does not appear in the file as it currently stands." He let that sit for exactly as long as it needed to. "I can produce my draft copy. I keep drafts. It's a habit."

"Produce it," said Pell, and wrote it down.

Corven turned fully then, and gave Voss the whole of his attention, and it was like being looked at by a lighthouse. "Edmund," he said gently, using the Christian name he deployed perhaps four times a year, each time like a coin pressed into a palm. "Whatever you think you're doing, understand what you're actually doing. You are a career man. I am your career. There is no version of this morning in which you walk out of this room better than you walked into it — unless you walk out of it having said nothing worth remembering. Think about the man you'll be tomorrow."

Voss considered the question seriously, because Corven had earned that much, and because Voss was a man who took questions seriously — it was most of what he was.

"The man I'll be tomorrow," Voss said, "keeps drafts."

He put the memorandum on the table. Two pages, in his own cramped hand, dated the tenth of March, flagging the tonnage discrepancy, noting the pattern across six vessels, recommending — he had forgotten he had written this, or had let himself forget — *further inquiry into the source of the declaration errors, which do not appear to originate with the shipping firms concerned.* Signed. Dated. Filed in his own cabinet for eleven months, waiting like a seed in dry ground.

Pell read it aloud. All of it. Slowly. The room listened the way a courtroom listens, which is to say completely and without mercy.

Corven sat very still. Voss had seen him cornered twice before — once by a rival magistrate, once by a journalist who had gotten too close to the dredging contracts — and both times Corven had fought, had spun, had produced allies and precedents and a fog of procedure thick enough to hide a cathedral. Now he did none of those things, and Voss understood why. The room had no levers. Brandt could not be flattered; Pell could not be bought; the girl at the end of the table had already survived everything Corven had sent at her, including Voss himself, and sat there with her bandaged hands folded and her face pale and absolutely level, watching the magistrate the way she had probably watched a broken escapement — diagnosing, without hatred, what had failed.

"You," Corven said finally, to Felix. It was almost conversational. "You understand what you've done. The firm's name will be dragged through every court on the coast. Your father's clients, your uncle's contracts, the guild license — all of it will be examined, reexamined, litigated for years. I offered you a clean name. I am the only man in this city who was offering you a clean name."

Felix Ishikawa looked at him for a long moment.

"You offered us a clean lie," she said. "My father wrote that letter so the truth would outlive him. I'm just delivering his mail."

It was not clever. It was not a performance. It landed in the room like a weight onto a scale pan, and the scale tipped, and everyone felt it tip.

Brandt turned to the last page of her ledger. "Magistrate Corven. On the evidence of the letter, the duplicate manifest, the household ledgers, the statement of Mr. Ellery, the tally records of the *Meridian Rose*, and the suppressed memorandum of Inspector Voss, you are remanded to the custody of the port constabulary pending formal charges of forgery, extortion, conspiracy to defraud, and obstruction of the harbor commission. Inspector—" she glanced at Voss, and there was something in her face that was not quite warmth, but was adjacent to it, the respect of one keeper of records for another, "—he's yours."

Voss stood. So did the constables by the door.

Corven rose too, and for a moment — one strange, suspended moment — he seemed about to say something enormous, some final speech, the summing-up of a career. Voss braced for it.

What Corven said was: "The clock in your office runs slow, Ilsa. It always has."

"Yes," Brandt said, signing the commitment order. "But it runs slow honestly."

They took him out through the front office, past the clerks, past the window with its view of the harbor where the *Meridian Rose* sat at anchor, searched and emptied and sailing nowhere. No crowd had gathered. No one shouted. Somewhere outside a gull screamed once and went silent. Corven walked between the constables with his coat buttoned and his chin level, and the only sound he made was the sound of any man's boots on any floor.

Voss followed as far as the door and stopped there. Procedure required him to accompany the prisoner; something else, unnamed, required him to stay in the room a moment longer. He looked back at the table — the letter, the ledgers, the memorandum, the fresh ink drying on the commitment order — and felt the strangest sensation, which was lightness. Eleven years of omissions, and it had taken one morning of ordinary paperwork to begin setting them down. He had imagined this would feel like falling. It felt like putting a tool back where it belonged.

Felix Ishikawa was gathering her mother's ledgers into their canvas wrap, careful as a surgeon. Voss crossed the room and stood beside her chair, awkwardly, a man who had chased her through two nights of her life and did not know what posture apology took.

"My memorandum," he said. "If I'd filed it properly. Followed it up. None of this—" he gestured at everything and nothing, "—would have been necessary. You'd have been a girl at a workbench."

Felix looked up at him. Up close, in honest lamplight, the disguise fell away entirely; there was nothing boyish left in the face at all, only tiredness and a kind of settled clarity.

"If you'd filed it properly," she said, "I'd never have found the letter. My father hid it where only a clockmaker would look." She tied the canvas cord. "Maybe things get found when they're ready to be found. I don't know. I fix clocks. I don't explain them."

She stood, and hesitated, and then held out her hand — the bandaged one, the one that had been cut sewing a letter into a coat lining. Voss shook it. Her grip was stronger than he expected, callused at the fingertips, a craftsman's grip.

"There's one more thing," she said, and now she did look away, toward the window, toward the harbor. "Tomorrow I go to the guild. To register the workshop transfer. Under my own name. My own — " a breath, " — myself. They'll likely revoke the license. They'll likely ruin what's left of us." Her jaw set. "I'm going to do it anyway. I wanted you to hear it from me first. Since you'll be reading the file."

Voss thought about the guild board — five men, four of whom owed Corven favors that had just evaporated, all of whom had never in living memory licensed a woman to a workshop. He thought about the word *likely*. He thought about the memorandum in his drawer, and the drafts he kept, and the habit of honesty that had lain dormant in him for eleven years and woken this morning feeling, apparently, ambitious.

"The guild board convenes at ten," he said. "I'm required to attend, as reporting inspector on the Ishikawa file." He picked up his hat. "I keep drafts, Miss Ishikawa. It's a habit. I imagine I'll want to take notes."

* * *

Outside, the morning was doing what mornings did — barges, gulls, the smell of tar and bread from the pier ovens. Voss walked to the observatory to return Maren Ostrow's key ring, confiscated during the search, and found the keeper on the tower steps with a broom, sweeping the night's dust off the stones as though the events of the past week had been merely weather.

"They took him, then," Ostrow said. Not a question. News traveled down the tower faster than it traveled the streets.

"They took him."

Ostrow leaned on the broom and looked out at the water. "You know what she asked me? Last night, before the pier. She asked me if the clock would forgive her for what she was about to do to it." A short laugh. "I told her clocks don't forgive. They just keep the time of whatever people do. And then she went down the stairs like she was late for an appointment with history."

Voss looked up the stairwell, into the dark throat of the tower, where the master clock ticked on above them both, keeping the time of everything.

At ten o'clock, he would walk into the guild hall. At ten o'clock, a young woman would stand in front of five men and say her own name out loud for the first time in three years, and the room would turn on her like a compass finding north, and Voss would open his notebook, and write down what happened next.

He found he was, for the first time in eleven years, genuinely curious what the record would say.
