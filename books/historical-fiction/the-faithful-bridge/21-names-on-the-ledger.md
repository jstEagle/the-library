## 21. Names on the Ledger

The guild hall smelled of beeswax and old varnish, and Felix stood in it with her hands at her sides, palms damp against the seams of her trousers.

Three days had passed since Corven was taken into custody. Three days of quiet in the workshop — the good kind of quiet, the kind that came after a storm had spent itself. Her mother had reopened the ledgers on the front table and begun copying figures into a fresh book, line by line, as if the household itself were being wound forward tick by tick. Tadao had come once, stood in the doorway without entering, and left a small paper sack of rice on the step like an offering at a shrine. He hadn't knocked. She hadn't asked him to.

But the guild could not be put off forever.

The summons had come that morning, delivered by a boy who wouldn't meet her eyes: *The Worshipful Company of Clockmakers requests the attendance of F. Ishikawa, apprentice, regarding matters arising from the harbor inquiry.* Regarding matters. As if the whole affair were a loose screw rattling somewhere inside the city's mechanism, and she was the one who'd have to open the case.

She had dressed carefully. The binding under her shirt, flat and familiar after two years of wearing it. Her father's coat, brushed clean. Her hair pinned up beneath the cap, every strand accounted for. She had done this ritual so many mornings that it had become invisible to her — and now, walking through the guild hall doors, she felt every layer of it at once, like standing naked in winter clothes.

The hall was smaller than she'd expected. A long table, seven men behind it, ledgers stacked before them. Guildmaster Aldous Renner sat at the center, a heavy man with a jeweler's loupe hanging from his neck on a cord, as if he might at any moment need to examine something more closely — perhaps even her.

"F. Ishikawa," Renner said, consulting a paper. "Apprentice to the late Master Ishikawa. You are here because your name appears in testimony given before the harbor master's office."

"I know why I'm here."

A murmur along the table. One of the younger guildsmen — Brandt, she thought, a fussy man who repaired music boxes — leaned toward his neighbor and whispered something that ended in a dry little laugh.

Renner raised a hand. "The testimony concerns documents recovered from the observatory clock. Documents bearing your father's hand. It also concerns the arrest of Magistrate Corven, and certain irregularities in the licensing of this workshop." He looked up. "You understand that a license issued to a deceased master cannot pass to an apprentice. Not lawfully. Not without examination."

Here it was, then. Not the trap she'd feared for two years — the discovery, the scandal, the mob — but something quieter and no less final. Procedure. The guild's own kind of clockwork, and just as indifferent.

"The workshop has operated continuously," Felix said. "Repairs logged, fees paid, standards kept. My father trained me himself. Every piece that left our bench—"

"Every piece that left your bench." Brandt again, unable to help himself. "Signed *F. Ishikawa*, is it? Which F. Ishikawa? There seems to be some confusion in the record about who exactly has been holding the file."

The silence stretched. Felix felt it the way she felt a mainspring reach full tension — everything coiled, waiting for release or breakage.

And she understood, suddenly, that there was only one honest weight left to set down.

She reached up and removed her cap.

Her hair came down past her shoulders, dark and unpinned, and she let it. She stood in the guild hall of the Worshipful Company of Clockmakers in her dead father's coat, with the binding still pressing flat against her ribs, and said:

"My name is Felicity Ishikawa. I am my father's daughter. I have kept his bench for two years, and every repair in those logs is mine."

* * *

She had rehearsed ruin so many times that its arrival felt almost anticlimactic.

No one shouted. No one called the watch. Brandt's face went through a sequence of expressions — disbelief, disgust, and then a kind of greedy delight, the look of a man watching a competitor's shop catch fire. Two of the older guildsmen exchanged glances over their ledgers. Renner took off his loupe and set it down very deliberately, the way her father used to set down a tool when a job had gone wrong beyond words.

"A woman," Brandt said, savoring it. "Practicing under a false identity. Forging the master's signature on guild work. Gentlemen, the *harbor inquiry* is the least of this workshop's problems."

"The signature isn't forged," Felix said. "It's inherited. And the work stands on its own. You've seen it — the merchant watches, the tower repairs, the regulator I rebuilt for the customs house last spring. Judge the pieces if you want to judge me."

"Judge the *law*," Brandt snapped. "The statutes are clear. No woman holds a journeyman's ticket in this company. No woman holds a license. What she's done is fraud, whatever else came out of the harbor business, and the penalty—"

"Is what?" said a voice from the back of the hall.

Maren Ostrow stood just inside the door, still in her keeper's gray, salt wind still on her coat. She must have climbed the whole way from the observatory without stopping. Behind her, filling the doorway almost entirely, came Harbor Master Quill — a broad, slow-moving man whose voice carried the weight of a man unused to repeating himself.

"Forgive the interruption, Guildmaster," Maren said, walking forward without waiting to be invited. "I'm told testimony is permitted. I have some."

"This is a guild matter—"

"And the observatory clock is a guild matter," Maren said. "Its master movement was sabotaged — deliberately, professionally, by someone who knew escapements. It failed three days before the equinox inspection, which would have cost this city its certification and this company its contract. The person who diagnosed the sabotage, corrected it, and restored the clock to accuracy within a single night was standing right where you're standing now." She turned to face the table fully. "I have kept that clock for eleven years. I know what it sounds like when it's running true. I signed the log myself. If you strike her name from anything, strike mine too, because I vouch for the work with my post."

Brandt opened his mouth. Quill got there first.

"Harbor master's office," he said, as though announcing a ship's arrival. "The corruption case against Magistrate Corven rests on documents authenticated by handwriting analysis, ledger cross-reference, and the sworn testimony of Inspector Voss. The ledger cross-reference" — he produced a thick volume from under his arm and set it on the table with a thump that made the inkwells jump — "was compiled by the same person. Line-item accounting, thirty years continuous, matching household expenditures against shipping manifests to the day. The prosecuting magistrate called it the most precise documentation he'd seen in twenty years on the bench." He looked around the table slowly. "Your statutes say no woman holds a ticket. Fine. But I'd ask you gentlemen to tell me plainly which you value more — the shape of the hands doing the work, or the work."

Nobody answered him. Nobody wanted to be the one to answer him.

Renner rubbed his eyes. "There will have to be an examination," he said finally, wearily. "Whatever we decide, whatever exceptions we consider — she sits the journeyman trials like anyone else. Full board. Full scrutiny. If the work is what's claimed, the work will speak."

"Then schedule it," Felix said.

Renner almost smiled. "You'll be examined by Brandt among others. He'll take apart your finest piece looking for flaws."

"Good," Felix said. "I'd rather be judged by a man who looks closely than one who doesn't."

* * *

The walk home took longer than usual, because people looked.

Word traveled fast in a port city; by the time she reached the merchant quarter, the story had already been bent into three shapes — the girl who caught the corrupt magistrate, the girl who disgraced her father's name, the girl who'd dressed as a boy to steal a dead man's trade. All three were half true, which was how the best lies worked. She kept her head down and her pace even, the way she'd learned to walk through checkpoints, and told herself that being seen was not the same as being known.

Her mother was in the workshop when she came in, not at the house table but at the bench itself, sitting in her father's old stool with a loupe pressed to her eye, examining a carriage clock someone had left for repair. She looked up when Felix entered, and lowered the loupe, and for a moment neither of them spoke.

"Well?" her mother said.

"They want me to sit the trials. Full examination. In six weeks."

Her mother considered this, turning the carriage clock over in her small, precise hands. "Six weeks is enough time to practice the complications," she said. "Repeaters are your weakness. You rush the strike train."

Felix laughed — a short, startled sound, half relief. Of all the responses in the world, she hadn't predicted *your repeaters are weak.*

"That's it? That's all you have to say?"

"What would you like me to say? That I'm proud?" Her mother set the clock down and looked at her directly, and Felix saw that her eyes were red-rimmed, though her voice was level as a balance wheel. "I have been proud of you since you were nine years old and filed your first pivot true. Everything since then has been confirmation. Sit your trials. Fix your repeaters. And Felix—" she paused, choosing the word the way she chose a gear, testing the fit before committing to it, "—your father would not have been ashamed. He would have been afraid for you, because he loved you, and then he would have been proud, because you are better at this than he ever was. Both things can be true. I have thirty years of ledgers proving that two things can be true at once."

Felix crossed the room and hugged her mother, who endured it stiffly for three seconds and then held on hard, her chin hooked over Felix's shoulder, her hands — those exact, patient hands — resting flat against her back like weights keeping a mechanism steady.

* * *

That evening, Maren came by.

Not to the workshop — to the little yard behind it, where Felix sat on an overturned crate cleaning parts in a bath of naphtha, because sitting still had become impossible. Maren leaned in the doorway with her arms crossed, watching the gears turn in the liquid like slow silver fish.

"You knew they'd make you sit the trials," she said.

"I hoped. Hoped and dreaded it. Same feeling, different directions."

"Eleven years I've climbed that tower alone," Maren said. "Do you know what I thought would happen if I ever told anyone what I suspected about that clock? That I'd lose the post. That I'd be the madwoman who heard sabotage in the ticking." She crouched beside the crate, close enough that Felix could see the oil stains ground permanently into her knuckles. "Then a boy shows up who listens to the clock the way I do. Who notices the third wheel's been shimmed. And I thought — ah. There's another one of us. Another person who lives inside the mechanism because the outside of the world doesn't fit."

"Maren—"

"I'm not finished." She said it gently, but firmly, the way she wound the clock. "When you took off that cap in there today, I watched their faces. Half of them saw a scandal. I saw the same person I've always seen. The hands don't change the work." She stood, brushing off her knees. "Trials are in six weeks?"

"Five and a half."

"Then climb the tower tomorrow. The great clock needs its quarterly service, and I'm putting your name in the log for it. Let the whole company see who services their master clock while they decide whether you're allowed to exist." She headed for the gate, then stopped. "Oh — and Voss sends word. The prosecution wants your mother's ledgers copied for the trial. Officially, this time. With receipts."

"With receipts," Felix repeated, and found herself smiling. "He's learning."

"He's a man who discovered the record matters more than the patron," Maren said. "Give him time. He'll learn to knock, too."

Felix watched her go, then looked down at the parts bath, at the small bright machinery turning in the light. Somewhere across the city, the observatory clock was striking the hour — she counted the strokes out of habit, out of love, out of the bone-deep certainty that a thing built honestly would keep honest time.

Five and a half weeks. Repeaters. Strike trains. Escapements she'd have to explain aloud to men who wanted her to fail.

She picked up the next part and began to clean it.
