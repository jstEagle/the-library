## 5. The Clock That Must Outlive Us

The summons was a single sheet of cream paper, folded twice and sealed with the Guild's crest—a wheel within a wheel—and it smelled of wax and authority. Mirabel read it in the carriage before she'd even turned the mare onto Fetter Lane, because she read everything now; that habit had grown roots in her the way ivy grows through brick, quietly, inevitably, until you could not pull it out without bringing down the wall.

*Mr. Elias Thorne is hereby required to attend the Guildhall on the fourteenth instant to present proof of progress upon the Commissioned Work.*

Proof of progress. She knew what that meant. The Guild had paid its deposit two years ago for an astronomical clock to mark the turn of the century, and every quarter since, some clerk had sent a letter demanding evidence that Elias Thorne had not simply taken their money and vanished into his gears. And every quarter, Elias sent back drawings so beautiful that the clerks presumably framed them instead of filing them.

She tucked the paper into her coat rather than the document case. Some deliveries felt different in the hand, and this one—she could not have said why—felt like carrying a live coal.

Fog had come up off the river overnight and lay along Fetter Lane like something poured. It softened the corners of the houses, turned the gas lamps into smudged halos, and made the workshop at number nine seem less like a building than a suggestion of one: a narrow frontage wedged between a stationer's and a shuttered pie shop, its window crowded with pendulums and regulator clocks all ticking at their own private tempos, none of them agreeing.

Mirabel tied the mare to the rail, climbed the three worn steps, and let herself in without knocking, because Adeline Hartwell's driver did not knock at tradesmen's doors—it wasted time, Adeline said, and time was the one currency the campaign never had enough of.

The bell above the door gave its small bright complaint anyway.

Inside, the fog followed her in a thin grey tongue and then seemed to think better of it, retreating. The workshop was warm. That was the first thing she always noticed—the warmth, and then the smell: oil and brass shavings and old wood, and beneath it something faintly sweet she had never identified, like the ghost of glue or varnish or patience itself. Clocks covered every surface. They hung on the walls in tiers, stood on shelves, clustered on the long bench under the north-facing window where the light came in even and grey and perfect for fine work. And they were all ticking, dozens of them, out of step with one another, so that the room did not sound like ticking at all but like rain on a roof, like breathing, like a city heard from very far away.

Elias Thorne stood at the bench with his back to her, a loupe screwed into his right eye, both hands buried to the wrist in the open case of a tall regulator. He was a tall man built like the things he made—all upright lines and concealed complexity—with dark hair going iron at the temples and forearms bare below rolled shirtsleeves, spattered with the bright dust of brass.

"You're early," he said, without turning around. "Or the mail coach is late. One of those."

"The Guild," Mirabel said, "does not use the mail coach for summonses. They send a boy who runs as though the hounds are after him."

That got him to turn. He took the loupe out of his eye, and the eye underneath was always slightly comical for a moment—enlarged by habit, blinking at the ordinary scale of things—and she had learned, over these past months of deliveries, to wait out that blink before she looked at him properly. It was a small vanity of hers, that waiting. A private ritual she would have denied under oath.

"Let me see it, then." He held out his hand, wiping the other on his apron first, an automatic courtesy that undid her more than any deliberate charm could have. She passed him the summons and watched him read it, watched his jaw tighten in the way it did when he was keeping words behind his teeth.

"Fourteenth," he said. "They want proof of progress by the fourteenth."

"Is there proof?"

He looked at her then, really looked, and something in his face shifted—the wariness that lived there permanently, the guardedness of a man whose trade was precision and whose life had lately been full of imprecise men, softening into something almost like confession.

"Come and see," he said. "Since you're here. Since—" He stopped himself, the way he always stopped himself, and gestured instead toward the back of the workshop, toward the door she had never been invited through.

* * *

The room beyond was longer than the front shop and darker, lit by two lamps and the pale rectangle of a skylight furred with city grime. And in the middle of it, on a great trestled frame like a ship in dry dock, stood the clock.

Not a clock yet. A cathedral of a clock, half-born. Wheels the size of cartwheels leaned against the wall in ranks; a forest of arbors and pinions lay sorted in drawers labelled in Elias's small severe hand; and rising from the frame itself, the movement's skeleton already stood taller than a man—a lattice of brass plates and steel posts, gear trains threading upward through it like climbing plants trained to geometry. At its crown, where the dial would go, a wooden disc waited blank as a moon not yet lit.

Mirabel forgot, for a moment, that she was a woman standing in a room with a man, and became simply a creature looking at a thing of terrible beauty.

"It's the astronomical train," Elias said behind her. His voice had changed—quieter, the way voices change in churches. "The whole works. Time-of-day, day-of-week, month, year. The moon's phase and node. The sun's position in the ecliptic. Precession of the equinoxes, if I can manage the differential." He came to stand beside her, close enough that she could smell the oil on his hands. "It will show the sky over London as it actually is. Not approximately. Actually. When it's done, a child standing under that dial will be able to point at the clock and say *that* is where the moon is right now, behind the clouds, and be right."

"And when is it done?"

"Midnight, December the thirty-first." He said it lightly, but she heard the weight underneath, the way you hear water under ice. "Nineteen hundred. The Guild wants it striking the century in. There'll be a crowd, speeches, the Lord Mayor if he can tear himself away from his dinner. Fifteen months."

"Fifteen months," Mirabel repeated. She walked slowly along the frame, reading the work the way she read letters—for what it said and for what it was trying not to say. She had learned schematics from him, in a fashion; he had caught her studying a drawing once, months ago, and instead of being offended had handed her another, and then another, testing her, teaching her without admitting to either. She could see now which trains were finished and which were phantom. The going train: nearly complete, gorgeous. The strike train: begun. The astronomical works themselves: a promise wearing the clothes of a machine.

"Elias." She touched nothing. You touched nothing in a clockmaker's shop unless invited; it was the first law of the place. "How much of it is real?"

The silence went on long enough that one of the regulators in the front room finished its hour and struck, muffled through the wall, five small silver notes.

"About half," he said finally. "A bit more than half. Which would be enough, if I had fifteen months." He pulled a paper from the bench drawer—not a drawing this time but a printed thing, official, bearing a crest she knew too well. "But I don't. Look."

She took it. *Metropolitan Railway Trust. Notice of Compulsory Purchase.* The address was number nine, Fetter Lane. The date of possession was printed in bold: the first of November.

"November," she said. "That's—that's barely eight months."

"They've been buying up the terrace all year. The stationer sold in the spring. The pie shop last month." He took the notice back and folded it with the crisp economy of a man folding away something that hurt to look at. "They want the corner. Something about the curve of the line needing the frontage. So they serve papers, and the law says I can appeal, and the appeal takes six months if you're rich and eighteen if you're poor, and meanwhile the commission says fifteen months, and the two dates pass each other in the night like strangers." He laughed, one note, entirely without humour. "The clock will be half-finished when they come with the sledgehammers. Or finished and homeless. I haven't decided which failure to schedule."

Mirabel stood very still. She thought of the window display, the patient tickings, the warmth. She thought of him at this bench at all hours—she knew the hours; she delivered at all hours, and the lamp in this window burned later than any other on the street. Eight months. Men had spent lifetimes on less than what remained undone in that frame.

"What will you do?"

"Work faster." He said it flatly, and then, catching something in her face, gentler: "I don't know. Appeal. Beg. Find a patron with more money than the railway trust, which is to say find no one." He rubbed a hand across his eyes, leaving a smear of brass dust at his temple like a bruise of light. "I'm sorry. You came with a summons and you're getting a funeral oration. Adeline will want to know the Guild business was attended to."

"Adeline," Mirabel said slowly, "would want to know a great deal more than that. You know what she does with lost causes. She collects them."

"She has enough."

"She doesn't believe in *enough*." Mirabel surprised herself with the sharpness of it, and sharper still with what came after, which she had not planned and could not have stopped: "Neither do I."

Elias looked at her. In the lamplight his eyes were the colour of the brass shavings, gold gone green at the rim, and for a moment neither of them moved, and the clocks ticked their rain-on-the-roof rain, and Mirabel felt the dangerous warmth of standing inside someone else's siege and finding it familiar.

Then he smiled—crooked, tired, real—and turned back to the frame, and the moment passed the way moments do, unclaimed.

"Here's a thought," he said, tapping the great blank disc of the dial. "You drive about the city all day. You know every alley and every alibi. Steal me some time, Miss Ravensworth. A week here, a fortnight there. Nobody counts it. Nobody will miss it."

It was a joke. She knew it was a joke; she could hear the joke in it. But something in her chest answered it anyway, solemnly, the way a lock answers its key.

"All right," she said.

He glanced back, amused. "All right?"

"I'll steal you time. Though I warn you, I'm out of practice. I've been spending everyone else's very carefully for years." She meant it lightly. It did not land lightly; she saw that immediately, saw him register the oddness of the phrase and file it away behind those careful eyes, and she moved quickly past it, nodding at the skeleton movement. "Show me what's left. If I'm to be a thief, I should know the size of the theft."

And he did show her—pulled the drawings off the shelf and spread them across the bench, and talked with his hands, and forgot to be guarded, and she stood in the lamplight of his catastrophe and listened as though to music. The escapement he described last, almost reverently: the little anchor that catches the wheel and lets it go, catch and release, catch and release, the whole secret of a clock being not that it holds time but that it lets it escape in measured beats.

"That's the part I haven't cut yet," he said, "when the money runs out. Everything else can be crude. The escapement has to be true, or none of the rest means anything."

"Catch and release," Mirabel murmured.

"That's the whole art," Elias said. "Everything else is just wheels."

* * *

She drove back through the thinning fog with the Guild's receipt in the document case and the smell of oil and brass still in her nose, and she found that her hands wanted to talk to each other about it, wanted to shape gears in the air, and she made them be still on the reins.

At the corner of Chancery Lane she stopped for a dray lurching across the junction, and while the mare tossed her head and the world waited, Mirabel let herself think the thought she had been not-thinking since the workshop door closed behind her.

Eight months. A compulsory purchase order, served by the Metropolitan Railway Trust—the same trust whose letters crossed her lap weekly, whose agents' names she knew from the franked envelopes, whose business was conducted in fat confident correspondence that Adeline's allies cursed and Mirabel, alone among them all, actually *read*.

She had never once opened a letter from the Trust. She told herself it was principle. She suspected, even then, that it was only that she hadn't needed to.

The dray passed. The mare stepped on. Behind her, fading, Fetter Lane kept its hundred small hearts beating out of step, and ahead of her, somewhere in the document case between her knees, the afternoon's post from Adeline's solicitor lay sealed and waiting—three letters, thick ones, and one of them bearing a crest she had seen just an hour ago on a notice of possession.

Catch and release, she thought, and did not yet know what she had caught.
