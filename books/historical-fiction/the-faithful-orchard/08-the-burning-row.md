## 8. The Burning Row

Kestrel saw the smoke before he saw the fire.

It rose from the eastern ridge in a column so straight and deliberate it might have been a tower being built rather than a world being unmade. He stood on the cart road two miles out, the satchel heavy against his ribs, and watched the smoke lean westward on the wind—leaning, he realized, toward him, toward the river, toward everything the fire had not yet been sent to eat.

They had chosen the oldest row first. He could tell by the color of the smoke, fat and resinous, the way old Halvard stock burned when the sap was up. His mother had told him once—half a life ago, in a kitchen that no longer existed—that the trees in the eastern rows had been planted by his great-grandmother's hands, that a tree could outlive three governments if the government left it alone.

No government had left it alone.

He ran. There was no strategy in it, no thought of the satchel, of Maren's testimony sewn into his coat lining, of the clerk Dorn's confession folded in oilcloth against his chest. There was only the smoke and the road and his own legs, and somewhere past them, an aunt who had bought his escape with her own arrest and an orchard that was being made into an example.

The road crested the ridge and the fire opened below him like a held breath finally released.

They had set it along the eastern rows and let the wind do the work, and the wind was doing it gladly. Flame moved down the alleys between the trees with an awful orderliness, as if it too had been given a route and a timetable. Sparks went up in sheets. The heat reached him at fifty paces and pushed against his face like a hand. And in the middle of it, small and dark and moving, figures with torches—Voss's men walking the rows, touching flame to any tree the fire had hesitated over, thorough as clerks.

Kestrel dropped into the ditch beside the road because a soldier's eye finds a standing figure before it finds anything else in a fire's light. He lay in the wet grass with his heart slamming and watched his inheritance burn.

*This is what a name costs,* he thought. *This is what a name buys.*

He had spent three years learning to be no one. No name, no kin, no history—only a satchel and a road and the talent of being forgettable. And in four days he had unlearned all of it. He had broken a seal, claimed a family, gathered witnesses, and now he lay in a ditch watching that family's land go up in a fire that had been ordered as a lesson to the province: *this is what conspiracy looks like. This is what the regime does to conspirators.* Except there had been no conspiracy. There had been only an orchard, and a general who wanted its land, and paper—forged, signed, filed—paper that Kestrel now carried against his chest while the thing the paper described was destroyed so thoroughly that no one would ever be able to check the paper's lies against anything real.

Except him. Except the testimony. Except the truth, which was, as of tonight, a fugitive with a wounded courier for a body.

He smelled blood before he understood he was bleeding. When he'd thrown himself down the ridge slope in the dark, a fence post or a stone or simply the ground itself had opened his side above the hip. It was not deep. It was also not nothing. He pressed his coat against it, felt the testimony's oilcloth crinkle there, and laughed—a short, ugly sound the fire swallowed whole.

*Everything burns,* he thought. *Everything except what we carry.*

He got up and ran west, along the fire's edge, toward the river, toward Ilse, toward the last archive in Vessany that might still say no.

* * *

Ilse had learned that a barge captain's price was never money alone.

Captain Reval of the *Maren's Luck*—she had almost smiled when she read the name painted on the stern, a coincidence so blunt it felt like an omen—stood at his gangplank with his arms crossed and his eyes doing arithmetic. Behind her, on the dock, sat forty crates of agricultural ledgers, the Halvard deeds and correspondence hidden among them in the false bottoms her father's joiner had built in a single feverish night. Behind the crates, up the hill, sat her father's archive, and inside her father's archive sat a letter from General Voss's clerks demanding the surrender of all seditious materials by week's end.

"You want me to take government freight," Reval said, "downriver, past three checkpoints, without papers."

"I want you to take a farmer's accounts downriver," Ilse said. "The papers are in order. My father stamped them himself."

"Your father stamps whatever walks through his door. That's his reputation. Neutral as river water." Reval's eyes flicked to the crates, then back. "River water, though. It finds every crack."

She had prepared for this. She had prepared three versions of this. But standing on the dock with the smoke plume visible even here, sixty miles east and still enormous against the stars, she found that all three versions had gone out of her head, and what remained was the truth, which was the one thing she had sworn never to spend.

"Captain," she said, "there are men east of here burning an orchard because a general wants its land and has written it into history as treason. The papers in those crates prove it's a lie. In a year, or ten years, someone will want to know what actually happened, and everything that says so will have been burned—except what's in those crates."

Reval said nothing. Down the dock, a lamplighter worked his way along, and the flames of the lamps stood perfectly still in the windless dark, as if the whole river town were holding its breath with her.

"My father is neutral," Ilse went on. "I am not. I never had the luxury. I was born with a stamp in my hand and I've spent my life deciding what deserves it. Those papers deserve it." She heard her own voice go quiet and steady, a voice she didn't recognize as hers. "Name your price. Money, favors, a favor owed by a man you haven't met yet—name it."

Reval looked at the crates for a long moment. "There's a checkpoint at Weir Lock," he said. "They open maybe one crate in ten, if the sergeant's bored. If he's not bored, they open all of them." He turned and spat into the water. "My price is this: you ride with them. If they open a crate and find treason, they'll want to know whose freight it is, and I'll tell them it's the archivist's daughter's, and you'll say so, and you'll hang or rot for it before they touch my boat. That's my price. Your name on the manifest, your body on the deck."

It was not what she had expected to pay. It was exactly what she had known, in the place where she kept the things she didn't say aloud, she would pay.

"Done," said Ilse.

* * *

Kestrel reached the river road at dawn, walking now, because running had become a thing other people did.

The wound had opened twice on the way. He had bound it with a strip of shirt in a ditch and again in a barn whose owner had looked at him, looked at the smoke on the horizon, and said nothing at all—one of the silences that saved lives, the kind he had once lived inside, the kind he was now asking strangers to break on his behalf. He had not asked the farmer for anything. The farmer had brought him water, bandage cloth, and a heel of bread, and had stood in the doorway watching him wrap the wound, and had said, finally, only: "The Halvards were good to their pickers. Paid through the winter, the bad years." Then he had shown Kestrel the back way to the road.

*They'll all say that, someday,* Kestrel thought, limping west. *When someone finally asks. The testimony is just the asking, made official.*

The satchel was his whole world now. Maren's letters—her real correspondence with the old republic, proving the Halvards had been the republic's loyal tenants for a century. Dorn's confession, sworn and thumbmarked, describing how he had forged the conspiracy documents on General Voss's instruction. The statements of eleven orchard workers, taken in cellars and kitchens over four desperate days, the last of them taken the morning Maren had walked into the market square in her best coat so the soldiers watching for Kestrel would follow her instead. He had seen it from the hill. He had wanted to run to her. She had turned her head once, deliberately, and looked at him—*go*, the look said, *go, go*—and he had gone, and the going was the heaviest thing he had ever carried, heavier than the satchel, heavier than the wound.

At the ferry crossing he paid the last of his coin to a fisherman who asked no questions, and stood in the bow as the river town came into view, gray and small and, for the moment, unburned. Somewhere in it was an archive. Somewhere in it was a woman who had hidden deeds inside ledgers and sent a boy runner east with a message Kestrel had intercepted at a relay station three days ago, a message he had read a dozen times until he could recite it: *The freight is ready. It needs a river. Tell the courier.*

They had never used each other's names. It was safer that way, and it was also something else, something he had no time to examine with a fire at his back and blood in his boot. She had looked at him across a cataloging table and seen a courier carrying dangerous knowledge, and he had looked at her and seen the same, and between those two recognitions something had grown that he had decided, with a courier's discipline, not to name.

*Survive first,* he told himself, as the ferry bumped the dock. *Name it later, if later exists.*

He stepped onto the dock and the world tilted, and he caught himself on a bollard, and when his vision cleared, a woman was walking toward him down the planks with a manifest roll under her arm and smoke reflected in her eyes.

"You're bleeding," Ilse said.

"The freight," Kestrel said. "Is it on the water?"

"Tonight, with the tide. I'm riding with it." She looked at the smoke, then at him, and her voice dropped. "You brought it. All of it?"

"Every page. Every name." He fumbled at the satchel strap with fingers that would not quite obey. "It needs to be in the water before they come for the archive. They'll come. They always come last for the archivists—they want the record-keepers to burn it themselves. It looks better in the histories."

"Then we'd better not be here when they do." She took his arm without asking, without ceremony, her shoulder under his as if she had been carrying him for years, and steered him up the dock toward the town. "There's a cellar behind the chandlery where you can be stitched before dark. And Kestrel—" she stopped, and he realized it was the first time she had said his name, the name he had signed to nothing for three years, the name that was now worth more than his life, "—the man who owns that boat. His boat is called the *Maren's Luck*. I thought you should know."

Kestrel looked back once, east, at the smoke that had been his mother's family, standing straight as a monument against the morning.

"Then it's already lucky," he said. "Let's get it on the river."
