## 7. What Vasco Knew

The rain had come in off the harbor an hour before dusk, and by the time the last customer left the shop, it was drumming on the shutters like a man demanding entry. Vasco let it drum. He had spent sixty years listening to weather and machinery, and he knew the difference between a sound that meant harm and a sound that merely meant the world going on. This one meant the world going on.

He lit the lamp over the workbench — the good lamp, the one with the brass reflector he had polished every Sunday for longer than Edmund had been alive — and set out two cups without asking himself why. He knew why. He had known since the moment she came back from the observatory with the master clock's heart in her hands and something changed behind her eyes, some small gear thrown out of true. She had been different ever since. Quieter, if such a thing were possible for a person who had made quiet into a trade. And careful in a new way, checking the street before locking up at night, sleeping — he suspected — with her boots within reach of the bed.

He had seen that look before. He had worn it himself, once, for thirty years.

The door opened at half past eight, exactly when he had told her to come, because punctuality was the only sermon he had ever been able to deliver without hypocrisy. Edmund shook the rain from her coat in the doorway like a cat, and he saw that she had come the long way round, through the fish market, doubling back twice. Good. That was what he had taught her, though he had taught it as a trick against pickpockets, not against colonels.

"You ate?" he asked.

"I ate."

"Liar." He pushed the bread and cheese across the bench anyway. "Sit. I want to show you something, and you'll want your hands free."

She sat. She looked at the second cup, then at him, and he watched her decide not to ask. She was good at not asking. It was one of the things he loved about her and one of the things that had kept her alive, and he understood now — had understood for weeks, really, since the tower clock stopped — that both of those things might soon stop being true.

Vasco went to the back of the shop, past the racks of pendulums and the wall of drawers labeled in his own crabbed hand, to the old iron stove that hadn't been lit in a decade because the chimney drew badly. He knelt. His knees complained; they always complained; he had stopped listening to them years ago, the way one stops hearing a clock in one's own house. He worked the loose brick free with a knife blade, the same knife, the same brick, the same motion he had rehearsed in his mind perhaps ten thousand times without once performing it, and drew out the oilcloth bundle.

It was heavier than memory. Everything was.

When he turned around, Edmund was standing. She hadn't touched the bundle — she never touched things that weren't offered — but her whole body had the tautness of a mainspring wound to its limit.

"Sit down," Vasco said. "This is a long story, and I'm an old man, and if I have to tell it standing up I'll die in the middle and you'll never know the ending."

She sat. She did not eat the bread.

* * *

"I helped build that clock," he said.

Her face did the thing it always did when the world contradicted itself: nothing at all. But her hands, folded on the bench, tightened until the knuckles went pale, and Vasco thought, as he had thought a thousand times, that whoever this woman was beneath her brother's name, she had learned stillness from someone who understood what stillness cost.

"Not the works," he said. "The works are ordinary — fine, but ordinary. A Verrell pattern, forty-year barrel, gravity escapement. Any competent hand could keep them running, which is why they hired me to do it, all those years ago, when my knees were younger liars than they are now. No. What I built was underneath the works. In the case. In the base." He laid his palm flat on the oilcloth. "I built a vault."

"Inside the clock," Edmund said. It wasn't a question. Her voice had gone very level, the level of someone walking toward the edge of something high.

"Inside the clock." He almost smiled. "Where else? Think about it, girl. Who searches a clock? The Regiment searched every warehouse, every cellar, every ship that made port, every poor devil's mattress. But the master clock of the observatory — that was sacred. That was *time itself*, and time belonged to everyone, so it belonged to no one, and no one with a warrant wanted to be the fool who pried open the town's own heartbeat and found nothing. I built it for the reformers, back then. Before the Regiment was the Regiment, when they were just men with grievances and muskets and a talent for making other people's anger sound like principle. There were men in this city who kept records. Records of debts, of bribes, of ships that sailed light and came home heavy. They needed somewhere the rot couldn't reach, and I gave them the one place in the world where nobody would ever think to dig." He tapped the oilcloth. "A clock is honest, Edmund. That's the whole joke of it. A clock can't lie. So everyone believes whatever is hidden inside one must be honest too."

"And the reformers lost," Edmund said quietly.

"The reformers lost." He said it plainly, because he had earned the right to say things plainly, having spent thirty years saying nothing at all. "Or they won, depending how you count, and then their winners became the Faithful Regiment, and the Faithful Regiment found the vault — or rather, they found *me*, because I was the only living soul who knew it existed, and I discovered that a secret is only safe while its keeper is useful and silent. They didn't take the vault apart. Why would they? A vault is a tool. They put their own papers in it. Ledgers. Manifests. And they left me to wind the clock and keep the works sweet and never, ever look below the movement plate."

"But you looked."

The rain filled the silence for him. He let it. Then he said, "I looked."

* * *

He untied the oilcloth with fingers that shook, and he hated them for shaking, and forgave them, because they had carried this weight alone for three decades and were entitled to tremble at the setting-down of it.

Inside was a key. Not a large key — a clock key, brass, worn nearly smooth, the kind that would hang unnoticed on any workbench in the city. Beside it lay a folded paper, soft as cloth with age, and a small tin of watch oil, and a wedding ring that did not fit him and never had.

Edmund looked at the ring and said nothing, and he loved her for that too.

"My wife's," he said. "Marta. You've heard me speak of her — no, don't apologize, there's no reason you'd remember, I spoke of her the way a man speaks of fair weather, briefly and without detail. She died in the fever year, twenty-two years back. She never knew what I kept under the floor. Or she knew and never asked, which would have been worse, or better. I've never decided which." He cleared his throat. "The point is, I am an old man with no children, and everything I own fits in this room except one thing, and that one thing doesn't fit anywhere, and I am tired, Edmund. I am tired in the way a spring is tired. Not broken. Just — run down past the point where winding does any good."

"The ledgers," she said. "In the tower. I found them."

"I know you found them."

Her head came up sharply. For the first time that evening, the stillness cracked, and he saw the young woman underneath — frightened, furious, magnificent. "You *knew*? You knew what was up there and you sent me into it?"

"I sent you into it because the clock stopped, and the clock never stops, and I am too old to climb the tower stairs, and if I'd gone myself and been seen prying at the movement plate, we'd both be in the cells by now." He held up a hand before she could speak. "And yes. Yes, I hoped you'd find them. God forgive me, I hoped it. I've been hoping some clever young hand would find them for thirty years, because I could not carry them out myself, and I could not destroy them, and I could not live with them, and those three impossibilities made a cage, and I have lived in it since I was younger than you are now."

"What did you see?" she asked. "That first time. When you looked."

Vasco looked at the lamp flame for a while. Some things deserved the courtesy of being faced directly.

"A list," he said. "Names. Conscripts — the Regiment took them for garrison labor, that was legal, that was the law and everyone shrugged at the law. But the list had prices next to the names. Prices. And ship dates. And a mark in the margin, a little anchor-and-chain, that I later learned belonged to a syndicate out of the southern ports. Men taken off the streets to haul stone for the bastions, sold instead before their boots were broken in. Forty-one names the first year I counted. I stopped counting after that. Counting was a thing I could have done something about, and doing something about it would have gotten me killed and gotten the vault found and gotten every name in it erased. So I didn't count. I oiled the escapement. I kept perfect time. I was," and here his voice did fail him, just slightly, just enough that he heard it and despised it, "I was *faithful*, Edmund. Isn't that the word they use? The Faithful Regiment. Well. There were two faithful men in that tower, and one of them was the clock."

Edmund reached across the bench and put her hand over his. Her hands were smaller than her brother's had been, calloused differently, and he had known what she was for four years and never once said so, because her secret was hers and his silence was the only shelter he had ever managed to build for anyone.

"Why give it to me now?" she asked. "Why tonight?"

"Because you've already decided," he said. "Don't look surprised. I've watched you wind a hundred thousand springs, girl. I know what resolution looks like in the hands before it reaches the face. You came back from that tower carrying something, and you've been walking like a woman counting exits ever since. Whatever you mean to do — and I don't want to know what, not yet, not in words, words are how men like Ashworth catch people — you mean to do it soon, and alone, and you will need that key, and you will need to know that the vault has a second door."

Her breath caught audibly. "A second door?"

"The case sits against the east wall of the clock room. Behind it, the wall is hollow — service space, for the weights. There's a panel at the bottom, behind the wainscot, locked from the inside. The key opens the vault from below. Anyone searching the clock finds a sealed box. Anyone coming through the panel finds the box's back, and a hinge, and a way to empty it without ever touching the front." He slid the key across the bench between them. "I built it that way because the men who commissioned it trusted no one, including me, and they were right to. Take it."

Edmund looked at the key and did not touch it. "You're giving me your guilt," she said. "You know that, don't you. Thirty years you've carried it, and now you're handing it to me like an heirloom."

"Yes," Vasco said simply. "That's exactly what guilt is for. It's the only inheritance worth anything. Money gets stolen, land gets seized, names get crossed off censuses — you'd know more about that than most. But guilt, properly passed down, becomes action. My silence bought thirty years of those men's freedom being sold twice over. Your courage can buy them back. That's the whole arithmetic of it. I'm not handing you a burden, child. I'm handing you the bill, and you get to be the one who finally pays it."

"And you?"

"Me?" He smiled, and felt his face do something it hadn't done in years, something unguarded. "I'm going to keep winding clocks. Someone has to make sure the town keeps honest time while you're busy making it honest in every other respect."

But even as he said it, he was looking at the wedding ring in the oilcloth, and thinking about Marta, and thinking about a thing he had not yet said aloud to anyone, a plan forming in the back of his skull with the slow inevitability of a weight descending in its channel. There was more than one way to draw a garrison's eyes. There was more than one kind of vault. An old man with nothing left to lose was the cheapest decoy in any port in the world, and he had thirty years of confession stored up in him like tension in a great wheel, waiting only for the moment of release.

Not yet. Not tonight. Tonight he would teach her the second door, and drill her until she could find the panel blind, in the dark, with soldiers' boots on the stairs above her. Tonight he would pass down the heirloom.

Edmund picked up the key. She held it in her palm as though weighing it, and then closed her fingers around it, and he watched thirty years leave his shoulders and settle onto hers, and he grieved for her even as he thanked God for her.

"There's one more thing," she said quietly. "The census woman. Miriam Halloway. She knows about me, Vasco. About — everything. And she hasn't reported me."

Vasco went very still. Outside, the rain kept up its patient drumming, and somewhere across the darkened city the observatory clock — his clock, their clock, the honest heart of a dishonest town — struck the quarter hour, clear and true and utterly indifferent to what slept inside it.

"Halloway," he repeated slowly. "The census woman with the sharp eyes." He looked at Edmund's face, at the fear and the stubbornness tangled together there, and he understood suddenly how much larger this had become than one key and one vault — and how much less time they had than he had pretended.

"Then sit down again," he said, "and bar that door, and listen carefully. Because if Miriam Halloway knows, it won't be long before someone far less gentle knows it too."
