## 9. Checkpoint One: The Harbor Gate

The pages weighed nothing. Edmund knew this because she had weighed them—three times, on the little brass balance Vasco used for mainsprings—turning them over in her mind the way she turned over any problem of mass and resistance. Eleven sheets of copied ledger, folded to the size of a prayer book, sewn flat against the underside of the clock case's lid beneath a layer of green felt that had been glued down and trimmed and aged with tea so that no seam showed, no edge lifted, no corner betrayed the secret pressed beneath it. Four ounces, perhaps less. A sparrow weighed more. A man's conscience weighed more.

And yet she could not stop feeling them, the way a tongue returns to a cracked tooth. Every time she lifted the case she imagined the whole harbor gate hearing the whisper of paper against felt, every soldier's head turning as one, Colonel Ashworth's smile widening by a single degree.

*You are a clockmaker*, she told herself, latching the case shut in the grey hour before dawn. *You are carrying a clock to be repaired at the harbor master's office, which is true. The clock is late. That is all. That is all.*

The lie was well-built. She had built it the way she built everything: carefully, with tolerances checked twice. The clock itself was real—a ship's chronometer belonging to the harbormaster, brought in a week ago with a sticky escape wheel, and genuinely due back this morning. The harbor gate checkpoint existed to inspect goods leaving the city, and a chronometer returning to its owner was the most boring object a man could carry. She had chosen it for that reason. Boredom was a kind of armor. She had worn it for six years.

What she had not planned for—the thing no amount of careful construction could plan for—was a face.

* * *

The harbor gate at first light was a throat of stone and timber between the customs yard and the quays, and every morning the whole city's commerce funneled through it: fish carts and coal drays, wool bales and mail sacks, sailors with their kit bags, women with baskets of bread for the ships. Two soldiers of the Faithful Regiment stood at the narrow arch with a customs clerk beside them at a trestle table, and the clerk's job was to look at manifests and wave things through, and the soldiers' job was to look at faces.

Edmund joined the queue with the case in both arms, its weight distributed the way Vasco had taught her—against the forearms, not the fingers, so the grip never whitened. She had dressed the part down to its smallest components: the leather apron, the ink-stained cuffs, the smudge of clock oil at her jaw that she had applied deliberately, because a workman with clean hands was a workman with something to hide. Her cap sat low. Her voice, when she used it, sat lower.

The queue moved. A fish cart passed with a wave of the clerk's hand. A wool merchant argued cheerfully about duty on forty bales and lost. A woman with bread was waved through without a glance. The sky over the harbor was the color of pewter going to silver, and the masts of the ships stood up out of the mist like the needles of a hundred clocks all stopped at different hours, and somewhere beyond them, nine days out on that grey water, a magistrate named Corvane was waiting for the truth to arrive.

*Four ounces*, Edmund thought. *A sparrow weighs more.*

Then the soldier at the arch turned, and she knew him.

Not his name—she never learned it until afterward, and by then the knowing was a wound—but his face. He had been in the shop. Eighteen months ago, perhaps two years; the dates blurred the way dates did when grief was the ink they were written in. A young Regiment soldier, still soft in the jaw, come in with a pocket watch that had been dropped down a stair, and behind him, half a step, laughing at something, came a second soldier in the same grey coat.

Her brother. Thomas. Walking, laughing, alive, in the last year of the life he had.

The young man at the arch was the one from the doorway. He was older now, harder through the shoulders, and the softness had gone out of his jaw, but the face was the face, and it was turning toward the queue with the idle attention of a man whose morning was long and dull, and Edmund had perhaps four seconds in which to become no one.

She looked down.

It was the oldest trick and the best one: the workman studying his burden, the shy man's deference, the craftsman's absorption in his craft. She shifted the case in her arms, frowned at its latches as though something about them troubled her, and let her shoulders curve inward into the posture of a man who wanted nothing from the world but to be past it. Her heart was going like a flywheel with the governor broken off, and she made herself breathe against it, slow, four counts in and six out, the rhythm Vasco used when a hairspring fought him.

*He saw Thomas once, in a doorway, laughing. He does not know Thomas is dead. He does not know Thomas was never the one who fixed his watch. He remembers a shop and a name, and the name is the one I wear, and if he speaks it, it will only prove I am who I claim to be.*

The thought arrived like a key finding its ward, and she nearly laughed at the geometry of it: her disguise was her brother's name, and the danger was the man who had known her brother, and these two facts did not cancel but *interlocked*, each one bracing the other. If he recognized the name, he would match it to the face, and the face was Edmund's, and Edmund Okonkwo was a man of twenty-four with ink-stained cuffs and oil on his jaw, and there was nothing to find.

Unless he looked too long. Unless he remembered that Thomas Okonkwo had had a sister.

The queue gave her forward. Three people now. Two.

The clerk at the trestle held out a hand for her manifest without looking up from the previous one, and she set the case on the table—gently, the felt-lined lid uppermost, the pages sleeping beneath their four ounces of secrecy—and produced the paper from her coat. The harbormaster's own signature, obtained honestly two days ago, requesting the return of his chronometer. The clerk read it the way clerks read things, with the whole of his attention on the paper and none of it on the world.

"Chronometer," he said. "Case opens?"

"For inspection, sir, of course." She unlatched the lid and folded it back, and the chronometer lay in its bed of baize, brass and glass, its balance wheel still as a held breath. The felt lining of the lid showed itself: green, unremarkable, stitched at its edges, innocent.

The clerk glanced at it for the length of a yawn. "Regiment goods?"

"Private, sir. The harbormaster's own."

"Then it's the soldiers' say." He stamped the manifest and pushed it back, already reaching for the next paper, and Edmund understood that she had passed the clerk entirely, that the whole weight of the moment now hung on the man at the arch, the man with her brother's laughter caught somewhere behind his eyes.

She closed the case. Latched it. Lifted it.

"Okonkwo."

The name came from behind her, and the world went narrow and ringing, the way sound goes when a mainspring snaps.

She turned. The soldier had left the arch and come three steps down the queue, and he was looking at her manifest, which the clerk had left face-up on the table, and his face had changed—not to suspicion, she saw with a lurch that was almost worse, but to *pleasure*, to the soft bright pleasure of a man finding his own past in an unexpected place.

"Okonkwo," he said again. "The clockmaker. Off Candle Street." He was looking at her now, really looking, and Edmund stood inside her own body the way one stands inside a stopped clock, hearing everything, daring to move nothing. "You fixed my watch. The one I dropped at the barracks. It's kept better time than the tower bell ever since."

"Then it was a good watch," Edmund said, and her voice came out low and even, a man's voice, bored and mild, and she marveled distantly at how steady it was, as if some other Edmund were speaking through her. "I remember the watch. The stair at the east barracks, I think. A bad drop."

"Two flights." He laughed, and the laugh was younger than his face, and for one unendurable moment he was the man in the doorway again, and Thomas was beside him, and Edmund's chest hurt with it, with the terrible ordinary grief of hearing a stranger carry a piece of her dead brother around in his memory like a coin in his pocket. "You had a—"

He stopped.

The pause lasted half a second. It lasted a year. Edmund felt the whole architecture of her life stand on that half second: the name, the shop, the six years of flattened voice and bound chest and lowered eyes, the ledgers sewn under green felt four inches from this man's hand, the nine days, the tide, the forty-one men sold south in the dark whose names Miriam had written out in her careful census hand until two in the morning. All of it balanced on the next words out of a soldier's mouth.

"You had an apprentice, didn't you," the soldier said. "Young fellow. Always in the back with the files."

*Thomas*, Edmund thought. *He means Thomas. He is handing me my brother and he does not know it.*

"That was me, mostly," she said. "I was the apprentice. The old man keeps me at the bench these days—his eyes aren't what they were." And then, because the moment demanded it, because a man with nothing to hide fills silence and a man with everything to hide lets it stand: "He still tells the story of your watch. How the balance staff was sheared clean through and you were certain it was ruined. He says soldiers are hard on watches and soft on everything else."

The soldier laughed again, easier now, and the danger passed through the space between them and out over the water like a bird. "He's not wrong. Well. Good luck with the harbormaster's clock. Tell old Vasco the east barracks still has two that run slow."

"Vasco will say it's the soldiers, not the clocks," Edmund said, and the soldier grinned, and waved her through the arch, and she walked out of the customs yard onto the quay with the case in her arms and the sun coming up through the mist behind her, and she did not let her knees soften until she had put a fish cart and a coal dray and forty yards of cobblestone between herself and the gate.

* * *

The harbormaster's office took the chronometer without ceremony; a clerk signed for it, another carried it into the back rooms, and Edmund walked out again with empty arms and a signed receipt and the strange lightness of a woman who has set down a weight and found the weight was not the thing she had been carrying.

The quay was waking. Gulls argued over fish heads. A stevedore cursed a crane. The tide was coming in, grey and patient, and beyond the mole the water opened out toward the nine days and the ship and the magistrate, and Edmund stood at the harbor rail with her hands flat on the cold stone and let the shaking come, now that it was safe, now that no one was watching, the deep animal shaking of a body that had understood, better than its owner, how close it had come.

He had known the shop. He had known the name. He had known *Thomas*—had stood in a doorway with him, had laughed with him, and the ledgers said Thomas Okonkwo had been conscripted in the Regiment's own rolls and sold, and the man at the gate wore the Regiment's grey coat and did not know it, would perhaps never know it, that he had shaken the hand of a trade that dealt in his friend.

*Forty-one men*, she thought. *And every one of them known to someone. Laughed with, drunk with, missed. The Regiment sold its own faces.*

She made herself count breaths until the shaking slowed. Then she pushed off from the rail and went to find Miriam, because there were eight days left, and two more gates, and the first gate had taught her the lesson she would carry through all of them: that the checkpoints were not made of soldiers and tables and stamps. They were made of memory. And memory was the one thing she could not forge.

She found Miriam at the census office door, waiting, her ledger clutched to her chest, her face doing the terrible arithmetic of a woman counting the minutes a crossing should have taken.

"Well?" Miriam said.

Edmund held up the signed receipt, and Miriam's breath went out of her all at once, and for a moment the two of them stood on the step in the morning light, laughing the shaky, disbelieving laugh of the spared.

Then Miriam's eyes went past her shoulder, down the street, and stopped, and the laugh died in her throat.

"Edmund," she said quietly. "Don't turn around. There's a Regiment man at the corner. Watching the shop."
