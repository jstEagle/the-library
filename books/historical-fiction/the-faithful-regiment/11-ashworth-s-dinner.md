## 11. Ashworth's Dinner

The invitation came on cream-colored paper, sealed in black wax, and it terrified her more than any summons to the tower had.

*Colonel R. Ashworth requests the company of Master Okonkwo at dinner, Thursday next, at the eighth hour. Dress: plain. Bring nothing but your hands.*

Edmund read it four times by lamplight in the back of the shop, while outside the rain came down in the soft grey sheets that the port city wore through most of the autumn. *Bring nothing but your hands.* It was meant as a compliment, she supposed. Craftsmen were flattered by such things. But she heard in it the tone of a man who had already decided what she was worth, and was now measuring her.

Vasco stood at the bench behind her, ostensibly cleaning a movement, actually watching her over the rims of his spectacles. He had not touched the movement in some minutes. She could tell by the silence of his files.

"You'll go," he said. It was not a question.

"I'll go."

"You could be ill. Craftsmen are permitted to be ill. It is one of our few privileges."

"I was ill when they summoned me to the tower," Edmund said. "I was ill when the sergeant came to the door. I have been ill for eleven years, Vasco. He will not believe it now."

The old man set down his file at last. His hands, she noticed, were not as steady as they had been a month ago. Age, or guilt — she had learned by then that the two could look identical on a workbench.

"Ashworth does not invite men to dinner to praise them," Vasco said. "He invites them to weigh them. There is a scale in that house, Edmund, and it is not in the pantry. He will set you upon it and see what you displace."

"Then I must be very light," she said, "or very heavy, and I have not yet decided which."

Vasco made a sound that might have been a laugh in a younger throat. "Decide before the soup. After the soup, he will already know which one you chose."

* * *

She spent two days preparing the way she would have prepared a difficult repair: methodically, part by part, with the whole laid out in her mind before she touched anything.

The uniform first. Master Okonkwo owned precisely one good coat, charcoal wool, cut in the fashion of three years past because fashion was a currency Edmund had never been able to afford. She brushed it, pressed the collar flat, and stood before the cracked mirror in the shop's back room studying the man it made — the man she had been building, seam by seam and silence by silence, since she was fourteen years old and had buried her brother's name over her own like a foundation stone. The jaw was too soft. She had learned to hold her chin high to disguise it. The hands were the great betrayal — small, quick, ink-stained — but no one looked at a clockmaker's hands and thought *woman*. They thought *craftsman*. She had counted on that for a decade, and the counting had held.

The ledgers were safe. That was the litany she repeated while she dressed, while she pinned her brother's signet ring — too large, worn on the middle finger now, a trick she'd learned — while she walked the long wet streets toward the hill where the Regiment kept its house. *The ledgers are safe. The copies are sewn into the clock case in Miriam's office. The originals are behind the third panel in the vestry wall. Nothing on your person, nothing in the shop, nothing anywhere but in your head.* She had memorized forty-one names in nine days. She could recite them in order, or backward, or sorted by the ships they'd been sold from. If Ashworth meant to search her, he would find a clockmaker with clean fingernails and an empty coat.

The Regiment's house sat at the crest of the hill like a man leaning back in a chair it did not own. It had been a merchant's palace once — she could see the old bones of it in the wrought-iron balconies and the tall arched windows — but the Regiment had grown over it the way ivy grows over a wall, uniform grey shutters, the black-and-white banner hanging motionless in the still air, and at the door, two sentries whose rifles were newer and better kept than anything in the harbor arsenal.

They did not search her. That frightened her more than a search would have. They knew her name before she gave it.

"Master Okonkwo. The Colonel is expecting you."

The hallway smelled of beeswax and gun oil in equal measure, an honest smell, at least. A servant took her coat. Another took her hat. She surrendered both the way a man surrenders a weapon at a border, and followed the servant up a staircase whose banisters had been polished by forty years of anxious hands.

* * *

The dining room was smaller than she had expected, and warmer. A fire burned low. The table was set for two, which was its own kind of message: no officers to witness, no steward within earshot, nothing that could later be called evidence of anything. Ashworth stood at the window with his back to her, hands clasped behind him, watching the harbor through the rain-streaked glass.

"Master Okonkwo." He did not turn. "Do you know what I am looking at?"

Edmund crossed the room at the pace of a man who has been invited and is not afraid. "The harbor, Colonel."

"The harbor," Ashworth agreed. "Forty-two ships at anchor, if the fog has not taken the outer moorings. Every one of them a machine, do you know. Masts and pulleys and hulls, all of it built to do one thing — to keep time. The tide keeps time. The wind keeps time. A ship is a clock that floats, Master Okonkwo, and the man who understands clocks—" he turned, then, and smiled, and the smile reached his eyes, which was the worst part, because it was not a performance; he was genuinely pleased to see her — "understands everything."

Colonel Ashworth was perhaps fifty-five, silver at the temples, broad through the chest in the way of men who had been soldiers young and never quite stopped carrying the posture of it. His uniform was immaculate without being ostentatious. His hands, when he offered one, were warm and dry and entirely still.

"Colonel. You honor the shop."

"I honor the work. Sit, sit. The soup will be cold, and I am told the cook takes that personally."

They sat. The soup was a clear consommé, and it was excellent, and Edmund ate it slowly and with the attention of a woman who has learned that every meal in an enemy's house is a negotiation conducted in cutlery.

"Tell me about the escapement," Ashworth said.

"Of the tower clock?"

"Of any clock. Of the principle. I find that a man explains his craft the way he explains himself — the parts he emphasizes tell you what he values, and the parts he skips tell you what he fears."

It was a trap built of courtesy, and Edmund admired it even as she stepped carefully around it. She talked for some minutes — the anchor escapement, the recoil, the way a pallet either gives energy back to the pendulum or steals it, and how the difference between a good clock and a great one is measured in fractions of a second per day, and how no one but another clockmaker will ever notice those fractions, and how that is precisely the point.

Ashworth listened the way she imagined a customs officer listened to a declaration: hearing everything, believing provisionally, weighing.

"And when the escapement fails," he said, when she had finished, "the clock does not stop honestly. Does it."

"Colonel?"

"A broken clock stops. A *sabotaged* clock keeps going — it ticks, it looks alive, but the time it tells is wrong. That is the more dangerous failure, I have always thought. The one that lies rather than dies." He refilled her wine glass himself, waving off the servant. "Your master clock in the observatory. It stopped, and you repaired it, and the Regiment is grateful. But my secretary tells me there were *irregularities*."

The word landed on the table between them like a coin spun on marble.

"Irregularities, sir?"

"The mechanism had been *interfered with*. Deliberately, he says. Not wear, not age — hands. Someone had been inside that movement who was not supposed to be there." Ashworth smiled again, and took a slow sip of wine. "Of course, you were the one who found it. Which makes you either the luckiest man in this city or the most interesting one."

Edmund set down her spoon. She let the silence sit for exactly as long as a careful craftsman would take to consider a question — not too long, not too short — and then she said, "May I speak plainly, Colonel?"

"I insist on it."

"The movement had been tampered with. I said so in my report. I said so because it was true. Someone had packed the going train with wax and grit — old wax, old grit, packed by a hand that knew the mechanism, because a fool would have jammed it outright and a knowledgeable hand wanted it to *seem* to die of age. That is in my report, word for word. If your secretary tells you anything else, then either he has not read my report, or he has read it and is telling you a different story for reasons of his own." She met his eyes. "I repair clocks, Colonel. I do not repair stories."

For a moment the only sound was the fire, and the rain, and somewhere below them the sentry's boots on wet stone.

Then Ashworth laughed — a real laugh, short and genuine, and he raised his glass to her.

"*I do not repair stories.* I am going to use that, Master Okonkwo. You will be credited, of course." He drank. "You understand my difficulty. A clock that stops is a nuisance. A clock that was *made* to stop is a message. And I have been receiving messages lately, from several directions, and I am trying to determine whether any of them were written by you."

"I received a message too, Colonel. It was printed on cream paper and it invited me to dinner."

He laughed again, and she understood suddenly that he was enjoying himself — that this, for him, was sport, and that her survival this evening depended on being worth the sport.

* * *

The main course came and went — a roast fowl, root vegetables, a dark gravy she tasted without registering — and Ashworth talked, and she learned that his talking was the most dangerous thing about him, because it was generous. He asked about her brother. He asked about Vasco — *old Vasco, still alive? He built half the good movements in this city, you know; I have one of his on my mantel, forty years old and losing three seconds a week, faithful as a wife* — and Edmund answered in the flat, modest tones of a craftsman discussing another craftsman, and felt each question like a probe finding the edge of a wound.

He asked about her work for the harbor offices. About her clients. About whether she took private commissions — *a man of your gifts should not be soldering alarm bells for fishmongers* — and whether she had ever considered leaving the city, and what she would do if she did.

"Where would I go, Colonel, that the clocks are worse?"

"Everywhere," he said. "The clocks are worse everywhere. It is one of the consolations of empire."

Dessert was a cheese and a dark sweet wine, and it was only when the servant had withdrawn and the door had clicked shut that Ashworth set down his glass and became, very quietly, a different man. The warmth stayed in his face, but it went still, the way a pond goes still before it freezes.

"I am going to tell you something, Master Okonkwo, because I believe you are a man who respects plain dealing." He turned his glass by its stem, once, twice. "This Regiment has kept this city for thirty years. Kept it fed, kept it ordered, kept it *faithful*. There are people — in the capital, in the shipping houses, in the census offices — who would like very much to see us fall, and they have lately begun to move. Papers. Inquiries. Little questions asked in little rooms." He looked up. "When a machine begins to run badly, a craftsman has two choices. He can repair the part that failed, or he can strip the whole movement and rebuild it, and in the rebuilding discover that the fault was never in the part at all, but in the design. I am a designer, Master Okonkwo. And I will tell you what I have designed: a city that runs. Whatever small parts must be replaced to keep it running — they are replaced. Without sentiment. Without hesitation." He smiled, and the smile was kind, and it was the cruelest thing she had seen in eleven years. "I mention this only because you are a craftsman yourself, and I believe you will understand: one does not weep over a broken spring."

"No, Colonel," Edmund said. "One replaces it."

"Precisely." He beamed. "You see? This is why I wanted to meet you. Most men, when you speak to them of machinery, think you are speaking in metaphor. You never once thought that, did you."

"No, Colonel."

"Good." He rose, and she rose with him, and the evening's performance folded itself closed around them like a case being shut. "The Regiment will have more work for the shop. Regular work. Maintenance contracts — the barracks clocks, the gate chronometer, the tower. I want *your* hands on the tower clock, Master Okonkwo. Monthly. You will report anything irregular to my secretary directly. Not to the quartermaster. Not to the observatory. To Mr. Pell." He extended his hand. "You understand, of course, that this is not an offer."

Edmund took his hand. His grip was dry and warm and perfectly calibrated, firm enough to mean something, gentle enough to leave no mark.

"I understand, Colonel."

"Wonderful. Pell will bring the papers on Friday." At the door, as the servant fetched her coat, Ashworth added, lightly, almost as an afterthought: "Oh — and Master Okonkwo. Your apprentice. The young one, the jealous one, what's his name — Tull? He came to me yesterday with the most fascinating story about late nights and locked drawers and a census woman who visits your shop rather too often. I told him that apprentices gossip the way clocks tick, constantly and without meaning anything by it." His eyes were pleasant. His eyes were terrible. "But you know how it is. One hears a small irregularity, and one begins to listen for the next. Give my regards to old Vasco."

The rain had stopped when she came out onto the hill, and the whole harbor lay below her in the lamplight, forty-two ships at anchor, every one of them a clock that floated, and the tide would turn at 4:17 in the morning, and she had eight days.

Edmund stood a moment in the wet dark, memorizing the weight of the evening — the soup, the smile, the name *Tull* set down between them like a coin on marble — and then she walked, at the pace of a man who has dined well and fears nothing, down toward the shop, where she would wake Vasco, and tell him everything, and begin to decide which parts of the movement she could still save.
