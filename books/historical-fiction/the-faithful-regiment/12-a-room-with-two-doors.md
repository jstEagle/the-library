## 12. A Room with Two Doors

The census office of Port Meridian occupied the ground floor of the old customs annex, a building of grey limestone that had been raised, according to the plaque by its door, in the grandfather of the present Colonel's time, when the Regiment still pretended to be a garrison rather than a government. Miriam Halloway had worked in that room for six years. She knew it the way a blind woman knows a house: by the drag of the third floorboard, by the way the afternoon light came through the west window in a long amber blade that crossed the counting desks at four o'clock and made the dust rise like something alive, by the smell of the ink, the paper, the cold stone breathing through the walls in winter. She had never once thought of it as a hiding place. It was a room of lists. Nothing in it was hidden; that was the whole point of it. Every soul in the district, entered in fair hand, accounted for.

Which was, she understood now, precisely why it was perfect.

She unlocked the door at half past seven in the evening, when the clerks had gone and the lamplighter was still two streets away, and held it open while Edmund slipped inside carrying a clock case the size of a hymnal. The girl — Miriam still thought of her as a girl, though she knew better, knew the years of careful living that sat in those steady hands — moved the way she always moved, as though she had practiced not being seen until the practice had eaten into her bones. She stood just inside the doorway with her hood back and her cap in her hand, and for a moment she simply looked at the room, at the ranks of shelved ledgers, the counting frames, the great map of the district pinned to the wall with its forty-one small ink crosses that Miriam had added in secret over the past week.

"You keep a census office like a chapel," Edmund said.

"It is a chapel. We just worship names instead of saints." Miriam turned the key in the lock and then, out of habit, turned it again, though the lock had caught the first time. "Every man, woman, and child from the harbor to the mill road, entered, dated, and signed. The Regiment believes in this room more than it believes in its own church. Ashworth signs the quarterly abstracts himself. He has never once questioned a figure I've submitted."

"And you've been lying to him in those figures for how long?"

"Six years." Miriam hung her coat on the peg by the stove. "Small lies. A household here with one son too many. A widow there listed as living a year past her death, because her pension fed her grandchildren. Nothing anyone would hang me for." She paused, and the truth of it settled on her like the dust in the lamplight. "Nothing anyone would hang me for until this week."

Edmund set the clock case on the counting desk, and the sound it made — a small, deliberate wooden click against stone — seemed to Miriam louder than a door slamming. Inside it, wrapped in oilcloth and packed between the brass weights of a dummy movement, were the original ledgers from the observatory. Forty-one names. Dates of conscription followed by dates of sale, prices entered in a neat clerk's hand, a syndicate's mark stamped in wax that Miriam had seen before and never understood until now: the same mark on the shipping manifests she copied every quarter, the same mark on the grain contracts, on the salt, on the rope. It had been in front of her for six years, threaded through her own fair columns, and she had copied it faithfully, page after page, into the record of the Regiment's honest commerce.

She had spent the day cross-referencing. Her eyes ached with it. She had found the wife of a rope-maker listed as widowed in the spring ledger and still drawing bread rations in the autumn one, because no one had told the census office that her husband had been sold. She had found a mother in the mill road who had petitioned three times for news of a conscripted son and been answered, all three times, with the same printed form: *your inquiry has been forwarded to the appropriate authority*. The appropriate authority had sold him in Lot Nine for eleven pounds and had entered the sale between an entry for canvas and an entry for tar.

"Put it in the back wall," she said, because if she did not give her hands an order they would begin to shake. "Third shelf, behind the 1841 abstracts. Nobody touches the old abstracts. Ashworth's own clerks call them the dead shelf."

Edmund knelt at the shelving and worked the case open with a small screwdriver she produced from her sleeve, and Miriam watched her do it — watched the economy of her hands, the way she laid the screws in a row on the floorboard in the order she would need them again, the way she never once looked at what she was doing but looked, instead, at the door. A woman who had spent years being two people at once, Miriam thought, and had gotten so good at it that she could probably unscrew her own name and pack it away in oilcloth.

The ledgers came out wrapped and heavy. Edmund passed them up to her without a word, and Miriam slid them behind the row of dead abstracts, spine inward, and set a tin of counting tokens in front of them, and a bundle of spoiled forms, and then it was done. Forty-one men, hidden behind a decade of arithmetic.

"That's it," Edmund said quietly. She was still on her knees. "That's all of it. The copies go to Corvane. The originals stay here until we know he's taken the copies and the ship has sailed. If the copies are taken from us, these are the only proof left in the world."

"And if they're found here, they hang us both and burn the ledgers before supper."

"Yes." Edmund looked up at her, and in the lamplight her face was very plain and very tired and very young. "You say that so calmly."

"I've had six years of practice saying true things calmly to dangerous men." Miriam offered her a hand up. Edmund took it. Her grip was warm and calloused and did not let go quite as quickly as it needed to.

They stood there a moment, hand in hand in the middle of a room full of names, and Miriam became aware of the silence in it — the particular silence of a building after hours, the stone ticking as it cooled, the stove ticking as it warmed, two kinds of time arguing with each other the way they argued in every room Edmund Okonkwo had ever entered, because the girl carried clocks in her blood and could not leave a room without imposing its rhythm on it.

"You should eat," Miriam said, to break it. "There's bread in the drawer. And that cheese Ashworth's steward sent at Christmas, which I've been afraid to throw out and afraid to eat."

"Afraid of cheese."

"Afraid of owing Ashworth anything, even indigestion."

Edmund laughed — a real laugh, short and startled out of her, and Miriam realized with a small internal shock that it was the first time she had heard it in full, unguarded, not the dry almost-laugh she used at checkpoints and in doorways. It changed her whole face. It made her look like someone who had never had to hide anything, which was of course the cruelest joke of it.

* * *

They ate sitting on two counting stools at the map table, bread and hard cheese and the last of a jar of pickled onions Miriam had forgotten she owned, and the amber blade of the west window had long since slid off the desks and up the wall and gone out, and the stove had won its argument with the stone, and the room had gone warm in the way rooms only go warm after dark, when the day has stopped pretending to be about work.

Miriam found herself talking. That was the danger of the hour, she thought later; the hour after the work is done, when the body relaxes its guard before the mind agrees to. She talked about her brother. She had told Edmund the outline of it in the vestry, weeks ago now — conscripted, vanished, a name on a list — but the outline was not the thing. The thing was that Daniel had been afraid of deep water and had gone to the harbor every Sunday anyway, to stand on the pier with his boots dry and watch the ships, because he said a man should know what he's afraid of. The thing was that their mother had kept setting his place at the table for a year, and Miriam had been the one who finally put the plate away, and her mother had never quite forgiven her for it, though they had both known someone had to.

"He'd have liked you," Miriam said. "He was hopeless with his hands. Broke everything he touched. He'd have thought you were a magician."

"I'm not a magician. I'm a tradeswoman." Edmund said it flatly, and then seemed to hear herself, and looked down at the bread in her hands. "A tradesman," she corrected, in the low voice she used for the correction, the voice worn smooth as a stair tread from years of use.

The silence after it was a long one. The stove ticked. Somewhere out in the dark a watchman's bell rang the half hour, muffled and far away.

"You know what I was thinking about today," Miriam said, "while I was cross-referencing? I was thinking about the form. The standard inquiry form. *Your inquiry has been forwarded to the appropriate authority.* I've issued that form four hundred times in six years. Four hundred mothers, four hundred wives, four hundred — " she stopped, and started again. "And every time, I believed it. That's the thing I can't get past. I believed the form. I thought the machine was slow, not rotten. I thought somewhere at the end of all that forwarding there was a person doing the appropriate thing, and Daniel's name had just — slipped. Paper slips. I know how paper slips. I've dropped things myself."

"It didn't slip," Edmund said. "It was sold."

"It was sold for eleven pounds, between canvas and tar." Miriam heard her own voice go strange. "And I keep the ledger of this district. I am the appropriate authority, Edmund. For six years I was the appropriate authority, and I signed my name at the bottom of every quarterly abstract, and Ashworth signed under it, and we were — " she had meant to say *colleagues* and what came out was " — we were a machine, the two of us, his name above mine, and the machine sold forty-one men and I oiled it with my fair hand."

Edmund put down the bread. She reached across the map table, over the forty-one ink crosses, and took Miriam's hand, and Miriam let her, and the touch of those calloused fingers was so precisely, unbearably kind that Miriam had to look away at the map to keep her composure, at the little crosses, at the harbor with its one ship marked in red ink that she had drawn there herself: *Corvane — dep. morning tide, nine days* — no. Eight now. Eight days.

"You weren't the machine," Edmund said. "You were the one person in this city keeping an honest count. That's why we're going to win. Ashworth's whole power is that nobody counts. You counted."

"Too late."

"Paper doesn't care when. That's the one decent thing about it." A small, crooked smile. "You taught me that, actually. The night you sat across from me in my own shop with your ledger and asked me one question too many, I thought: this woman believes paper tells the truth. And I was terrified. And then I thought: so do I. That's the whole of what I've been doing with my life. Making paper tell the truth about me. One document at a time. My brother's name, his hand, his dates — I built a paper Edmund Okonkwo so careful that even I half believe in him."

"And the other half?"

Edmund was quiet for a moment. The watchman's bell rang again, further off, the full hour, and the sound seemed to pass through the room and take something with it.

"The other half is sitting in your office eating your terrible cheese," she said, "and wishing — " and there she stopped, and Miriam watched the shutters come down, watched the girl who was not a girl assemble her careful face piece by piece, and understood suddenly, with the terrible clarity that comes once in a life and cannot be recalled, exactly what sentence she had interrupted, and what it would cost Edmund to finish it, and what it would cost her not to.

"Say it," Miriam said.

"It's not — " Edmund began. "Miriam. In eight days you're either a fugitive or a heroine, and I'm either hanged or I'm — I don't know what I am. Nothing should be said before the tide."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only safe one I have."

Miriam turned her hand over on the table, palm up, an offering, an old gesture, a census-taker's gesture really — *enter here, be counted* — and after a moment Edmund laid her hand in it, and their fingers closed around each other, and the room was very warm and very quiet and full of forty-one dead men and eight living days.

"I have kept this district's book for six years," Miriam said. "Every soul accounted for. And there is not one line in it — not one — that says what I am. What I — " her voice did the strange thing again and she let it. "I have been so careful, Edmund. Careful the way you're careful. I told myself it was the work. I told myself a woman alone keeps her name clean and her door shut and her heart in a drawer with the spoiled forms. And then a frightened clockmaker lied to me across her own counter, so badly, so beautifully, and I went home and could not sleep, and I told myself it was the case. It was never the case. It has not been the case since the second week."

Edmund's hand tightened in hers. She did not pull away. That was the answer, Miriam thought; that was the whole answer, given in the only language either of them fully trusted, the language of what the hands do when the mouth has run out of safe words.

"I keep two sets of books," Edmund said at last, very low. "I have since I was nineteen years old. The real one and the one the world sees. And I have never, not once, entered a living person in the real one, because everything I love goes in the ledger that gets seized." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "You understand? It's not that I don't — it's that the Regiment takes everything in the book, and I cannot — "

"Then don't write it down," Miriam said. "Don't write anything down. I'm the one who keeps the books. Let me do it."

And Edmund laughed again, that startled real laugh, wet this time, and pressed her forehead down until it rested against the back of their joined hands, and stayed there, breathing, while the stove ticked and the stone ticked and the two times argued and made peace.

It was Miriam who heard it first — she had lived alone with the building's noises for six years and knew its vocabulary the way Edmund knew the vocabulary of escapements — the sound of boots on the annex steps, unhurried, two men at least, and then the knock. Not a hammering. A knock. Three measured blows of a man who expects to be obeyed and has never had to raise his voice.

They came apart like guilty things, which was the worst of it, because they were not guilty of anything except the one thing the world would hang highest. Edmund was on her feet with the clock case closed and the screws back in her sleeve before the second knock. Miriam stood, straightened her collar, and became in four seconds the census taker again, dry-voiced and unremarkable, and hated herself for how fast it came.

"Who is it?" she called, and her voice did not shake, and she marveled at that from a great distance.

"Regiment business, Mistress Halloway." A voice she knew. Sergeant Bray, Ashworth's own man, the one who delivered the quarterly abstracts and always looked at the shelves a moment too long. "The Colonel sends his compliments. He'd like the district abstract for the winter quarter tonight, if it's convenient. And he asks — " a pause, the length of a man consulting a paper he knows by heart — "he asks whether you've had any recent visitors to the office. After hours."

Miriam looked at Edmund. Edmund was already moving — not toward the door but toward the second one, the narrow service door behind the shelving that opened onto the records passage, the door Miriam had never once thought of as a way out until this exact moment. Edmund had found it with her eyes in under three seconds. Of course she had. The girl read rooms the way she read movements.

"One moment, Sergeant," Miriam called, and picked up the lamp, and walked toward the front door with the calm of six years of small lies, while behind her, without a sound, the other door opened and closed on the woman she had, an hour ago, finally let into the book.
