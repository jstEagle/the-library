## 14. The Informer

The bell over the shop door had a cracked note, and Edmund had meant for three years to fix it. She knew the fault intimately — a hairline fracture in the bronze near the crown, the kind of flaw that turned a pure tone into something wavering, something that could not decide what it wanted to be. She had repaired clocks for merchants and magistrates, for the harbor master and the widow who ran the chandlery, and still her own doorbell sang out of tune every time a customer entered, because fixing it would mean taking it down, and taking it down would mean the shop sounded wrong for an hour, and an hour of wrongness in a port city was an invitation to gossip.

She thought about that bell now because it had not rung.

It was past nine in the morning, and the door had opened without its cracked announcement, and the only person who entered her shop silently was the person who did not want to be remembered entering.

Edmund kept her head bent over the movement spread before her — a ship's chronometer, Ashworth's own, its balance wheel trembling like a small captive heart — and did not look up. She listened. Boots on the floorboards. Not Vasco's shuffle; Vasco had gone to the chandlery an hour ago for shellac and would be gone until the bells rang the quarter. Not a customer's hesitant tread. These boots walked like a man rehearsing a speech.

"Apprentice Okonkwo." The voice was young. She knew it before she raised her eyes. Tam Brell, second apprentice, three years her junior in the craft and forever resentful of it, stood by the workbench with his cap in his hands, turning it as though it were a mechanism he could not make run. His face had the damp, shiny look of a boy who had not slept.

"Tam." She kept her voice level, kept the accent flat, kept the Okonkwo steadiness her brother had worn like a coat and she now wore as skin. "You're meant to be at the Harrow Street job. The vicar's turret clock won't wait on your conscience."

"I've done the Harrow Street job." He set the cap on the bench, picked it up again. "I've done everything you've asked. Three years, I've done everything."

"Then the Regiment owes you a medal." She picked up her tweezers, held a pallet stone to the light, and did not look at him. Looking at him was dangerous. Looking at him would show him something — a flicker, a calculation, a woman's patience wearing thin — and Edmund Okonkwo had spent nine years learning that the face was a clock face too, and every hand on it told the time to anyone who knew how to read it. Tam did not know how to read it. Tam only knew how to feel slighted, and he felt slighted the way a wound feels a bandage.

"You get the observatory," he said. "You get the Colonel's chronometer. You get sent to dinners I'd give my teeth for, and I get turret clocks and schoolhouse regulators and your leavings."

"You get wages and a trade," Edmund said. "In this city, that's a fortune. Ask the men who dig the harbor."

"The men who dig the harbor—" His voice cracked and rose, and he strangled it down to a whisper. "You don't even notice, do you? You walk through gates I'd be turned away from. Soldiers salute *you*. The Colonel knows your *name*."

"The Colonel knows the name on my papers," Edmund said, and the words left her before she could weigh them, and she saw at once that they had landed wrong, that they had fallen into Tam's resentment like a spark into lint. She covered it quickly, bent again to the chronometer. "Which is more than most apprentices get. Go back to Harrow Street, Tam. Check the escapement. If the vicar complains again, we both answer for it."

He stood a moment longer. She could feel him standing there, feel the shape of the thing he had come to say and had not said, and she understood — with the terrible clarity that comes too late to be useful — that he had come to say it and had lost his nerve, or had come to see her face while he decided, and she had given him nothing but a bent head and a busy hands' performance of indifference.

"Check the escapement," he repeated, flat, and put his cap on, and left, and the bell did not ring because he held the door and let it close soft behind him.

Edmund sat for a long time with the pallet stone in her tweezers, not moving.

*The name on my papers.*

She had said it as a joke of the trade, the kind of dry remark Vasco made about magistrates. But Tam had heard something else in it, or would invent something else by nightfall, because resentment was a workshop too, and it manufactured whatever its owner needed. She thought of the ledgers hidden beneath the floor of Miriam's census office, forty-one names in a dead clerk's hand. She thought of the copies sewn into the lining of the clock case upstairs, waiting for the tide in six days. She thought of Ashworth's dinner, the candlelight, the Colonel's soft voice saying *irregularities* as though the word were a delicacy he was offering her to taste.

The balance wheel of the chronometer ticked on, indifferent, faithful, counting out the six days whether she moved or not.

She went back to work. There was nothing else in the world to do but work, and work was the only prayer she had ever been certain of.

* * *

Vasco came back at the quarter bell, as promised, with shellac and a loaf and the news that the chandlery's widow had heard the Regiment was doubling the watch on the customs house.

"Doubling it why?" Edmund asked, too quickly.

Vasco looked at her over the loaf, his old eyes hooded and patient as an owl's. "Why do you think, girl. Because someone with something to hide is moving toward the water." He set the loaf down. "Or because someone with something to hide is *believed* to be moving toward the water, which in this city amounts to the same thing, and costs the same in sleep."

He unwrapped the shellac, talked of varnish and the widow's rheumatism, and let the other subject lie. That was Vasco's way: he dropped a stone in the well and let you hear how deep it was. But as he turned to his own bench, he said, quietly, without looking up, "The boy Brell was at the barracks gate this morning. I passed him on my way. He was talking to a corporal, and he was not talking about turret clocks."

Edmund's hands did not stop. She had trained them never to stop. "Tam has a cousin in the garrison. He's mentioned him."

"Has he." Vasco picked up a file. "Then let us hope his cousin is the sort of cousin who keeps his mouth shut, because I have known that boy since he was small enough to fit inside a clock case, and there is a kind of fear in a man that always finds its way to the nearest uniform." He filed, and the sound was soft and rhythmic, a heartbeat in the room. "You should move the case tonight."

"The case is ready. It's meant to sit ready."

"Meant to sit ready in a shop no one searches. Not in a shop that is about to be searched." He still did not look at her. "There is a loose board under my cot. It has held worse things than ledgers for forty years, and no one has ever lifted it, because no one has ever imagined old Vasco keeps anything under his cot but his feet."

Edmund looked at her mentor — the stooped shoulders, the liver-spotted hands that had taught hers everything they knew — and felt the full weight of what he was offering, which was himself. If they found something under Vasco's cot, it was Vasco they would take.

"I'll move it tonight," she said.

"Good." He filed on. Then, after a silence long enough for the chronometer to count a hundred seconds: "And Edmund." Her name — her brother's name — in his mouth, gentle. "If the boy has talked, it's done, and cursing him changes nothing. Fear is not treachery. It only looks the same from inside a cell."

* * *

They moved the case that night, by lantern, through the door between the shop and Vasco's rooms, the copies sewn flat into the lining where a customs officer's fingers had already once searched and found only clockwork and honest dust. Edmund's fingers shook as she laid the case down beneath the cot, and she hated them for shaking. Steady hands were the whole of her armor. Steady hands had carried her through gates and dinners and the Colonel's smiling scrutiny, and now they trembled over a loose board in an old man's floor, and she could not make them stop.

It was Miriam who stilled them, though Miriam was not there — it was the memory of Miriam's voice from the census office, three nights past, low in the lamplight: *Your hands are the steadiest thing about you, Edmund Okonkwo. Don't you dare give them to fear.* Edmund breathed the way Miriam had taught her without meaning to teach her, four counts in, four counts out, and the trembling ebbed, and she pressed the board home and scattered the dust and sat back on her heels in the dark.

The shop bell rang.

At midnight. With no footsteps she had heard approaching.

She was on her feet before thought, lantern doused, standing in the black of Vasco's room with her heart going like a flywheel. Through the door's gap she watched the shop door open and a lantern's light swing in, and a voice she knew — the young sergeant from the harbor gate, the one who had known her brother, the one whose salute had been a half-second slow — call out:

"Clockmaker! Regiment business. Open and light your lamps."

Edmund lit the lamps. There was no alternative worth the name. She opened the door between the rooms and came into the shop with her face arranged into the mild, slightly put-upon expression of a craftsman woken from sleep, and behind the mildness she counted: four soldiers, the sergeant, and — her stomach went cold and stayed cold — Tam Brell, standing at the back in his nightshirt and coat, looking at the floor as though the floor owed him money.

"Sorry for the hour," the sergeant said, not sorry at all. "We've had a report of irregularities at this address. Goods held without declaration. Correspondence with persons abroad. That sort of thing." He smiled the way Ashworth smiled, a smaller and cheaper version of it. "You'll open your cases and cupboards for us, master clockmaker, and we'll be gone before the tide."

"Of course," Edmund said. "Mind the regulator on the shelf, it's a customer's and it's running to within a second a week, and I'll thank you not to breathe on it."

The search took an hour. They were not gentle and they were not thorough; they were the kind of thorough that has already decided what it will find or not find, and Edmund understood, watching the sergeant drift past the workbench where Ashworth's chronometer lay open under glass, that the decision had been made above her, and that this search was a message and not a hunt. They pulled books from shelves. They opened the empty cases. One of them shook out Vasco's spare shirts and laughed. The sergeant stood at the bench and looked down at the chronometer for a long time.

"Fine work," he said. "The Colonel says you're the finest hand in the city. Says your work has *character*." He turned the word over like a coin he suspected of being clipped. "Character can be a strange thing in a tradesman, though, can't it. A man with too much character starts to think the rules are for other people."

"My master taught me the rules are the whole of the craft," Edmund said. "A clock that lies to you about the time is worse than no clock at all."

"Is it." The sergeant smiled again. "Funny. That's near word for word what your report said. The one that was made about *you*."

He let it hang there. At the back of the shop, Tam Brell flinched — Edmund saw it, a small whole-body flinch, like a man struck — and looked harder at the floor, and she understood that he had not known it would feel like this, whatever he had told the corporal, whatever he had told himself. Fear is not treachery. It only looks the same from inside a cell. She held his gaze for one second, no more, and gave him nothing — no hatred, no pleading, only the flat calm of a craftsman whose work has been questioned and found good — and watched him flinch again at that, harder, because calm was the one thing he had not prepared for.

They left at half past one, taking nothing, breaking nothing, leaving the shop precisely as they had found it, which was worse than if they had wrecked it. Wreckage could be reported. This could only be endured.

When the boots had faded, Vasco came out of his room and stood in the middle of the shop in his nightshirt, looking old in a way Edmund had never seen, old the way a building is old after a fire it survived.

"They'll come back," he said. "Not with a lantern next time. With a wagon." He rubbed his face with both hands. "The Colonel is not searching for your papers, girl. He's telling you he knows they exist. It's a courtesy. It's how that man hunts — he shows the fox the fence, and then he waits to see if the fox is clever enough to stay inside it."

"Then we go early," Edmund said. "Not six days. Sooner. The tide—"

"The tide is the tide. Corvane sails when Corvane sails, and a magistrate who sails early is a magistrate who can be stopped at sea by men who were warned." Vasco lowered his hands. "No. We hold the line. We hold it the way you taught me to hold a wheel — steady, and trusting the teeth." He looked at her, and there was something in his face she had never seen there, something settled and terrible, a decision already made. "And tomorrow, girl, I want you to walk with me to the square. There's something I should have done forty years ago, and I find I've run out of reasons."

"Vasco—"

"Tomorrow," he said, and went into his room, and closed the door with the softness of a man who has already said goodbye to a thing and is being careful with the rest.

Edmund stood alone in the lamplight among her clocks, all of them ticking, all of them faithful, all of them counting down the six days with a precision she could not share. Above the bench, Ashworth's chronometer turned its small bright wheels. She thought of Tam Brell's flinch, of the sergeant's coin-turning smile, of the fence the fox had been shown.

She thought of Miriam, across the dark city behind a census office door, and of the wagon Vasco had promised, and she began, very quietly, to plan for a morning that might come sooner than any of them intended.
