## 16. Checkpoint Three: The Regiment Barracks

The barracks yard smelled of horse, gun oil, and wet stone. Edmund felt it before she saw it — the particular atmosphere of a place where men were kept like tools and sharpened daily for use. She had grown up around mechanisms of every kind, and she knew instinctively that a garrison was a machine too: springs and levers made of flesh, wound each morning by the drum, released by the whistle, governed by a single hand at the top.

Colonel Ashworth's hand.

She crossed the yard at half past four in the morning, when the light was still the color of pewter and the sentries at the far gate were stamping their feet against the cold. The clock case rode heavy on her shoulder — the same case that had passed the harbor gate nine days ago with copied pages sewn into its lining, and the customs house three days after that under Miriam's forged seal. But this time it held the originals. All of them. Forty-one names, forty-one sums paid by a syndicate whose ships still anchored beyond the breakwater, and beneath those figures, in a clerk's careful hand, the initials *J.A.* repeated like a signature on a confession.

Tonight the magistrate's ship would take them out on the morning tide. Tonight was all there was.

"Name and business." The sentry at the barracks door was young, with the raw knuckles of a boy who had been conscripted himself not two years past. Edmund recognized the type. She had spent her life learning to read faces the way she read dials — the small tensions, the hairsprings of fear and duty underneath.

"Edmund Okonkwo, clockmaker. I'm expected. The Colonel's chronometer."

The boy consulted a list. His finger moved down it slowly, and Edmund watched him the way she watched an escapement about to catch or slip. Somewhere behind his eyes a decision was being made tooth by tooth.

"You're the one who fixed the observatory clock," he said.

"I am."

"My father worked the harbor cranes. He said the tower clock hadn't run true in years until you came." He stepped aside. "Second door on the left. Sergeant Bray will take you up."

*Thank you* seemed both too much and not enough. She gave him a nod instead, craftsman to craftsman's son, and went through.

* * *

The Colonel's quarters occupied the top floor of the barracks' eastern wing, and they were nothing like what Edmund had expected. She had imagined a room of trophies and maps, of captured colors and the mounted heads of beasts killed for pleasure. Instead she found austerity so severe it approached devotion: a narrow bed made with parade-ground corners, a single chair, a writing desk bare except for ink, paper, and a small brass crucifix polished bright from handling. And on the mantel above a cold fireplace, resting on a square of green baize as though it were a relic on an altar —

— the chronometer.

It was a marine timekeeper of the finest make, a London instrument with a chain-driven remontoire and a silvered dial, the kind of piece that cost more than most families earned in a decade. It had stopped. That much she could see from across the room, its seconds hand frozen at eleven minutes past some dead hour, and she understood immediately why Ashworth had summoned a clockmaker rather than send the thing back to London. A man like this did not tolerate imperfection in others that he could not first conquer in himself. The stopped watch shamed him. It sat on his mantel like an accusation, and he wanted it silenced by craft rather than admitted as fault.

Sergeant Bray, a gray-whiskered man with the flattened voice of someone who had shouted orders until shouting became his only register, stood in the doorway while she set down her case.

"You'll work here. In the room. Alone with the door open." He said it as regulation, but Edmund heard the other thing underneath: *we are watching you.*

"Naturally," she said. "I'd want no one touching my tools either."

Bray grunted and left. His boots receded down the stair, and then — she counted — stopped two floors below. He would stand there. Perhaps he had orders. Perhaps he was simply bored. It changed nothing. She opened her case and began to lay out her instruments on the desk with the ritual care of a surgeon: the eyeglass, the tweezers, the oilers fine as needles, the movement holder padded with wool.

Her hands were steady.

That was the miracle of it, the thing she had learned across fifteen years of living inside a dead man's name: the hands did not tremble because she did not permit them to tremble. Fear lived in her chest, in her throat, in the animal part of her that wanted to bolt for the window and drop into the yard below. But the hands belonged to the work, and the work was sovereign. When she picked up the tweezers, the fear went quiet, the way a room goes quiet when a master begins to play.

She lifted the chronometer from its baize altar and felt its weight settle into her palms — four pounds of brass and steel and intention. Under the eyeglass, the movement opened like a city seen from above: wheels in their districts, jewels at their intersections, the mainspring coiled in its barrel like something sleeping that wished very much not to be.

She found the fault within a minute. The remontoire's detent spring had fatigued — a hairline fracture invisible to any eye but one trained to look for exactly such betrayals. The spring still held, mostly, which meant the watch ran, mostly, which meant whoever owned it believed it was sound. It was the kind of flaw she might have missed herself, ten years ago. It was the kind of flaw that brought down bridges.

*Like the Regiment,* she thought, fitting the new spring she'd ground herself over three sleepless nights. *Sound, mostly. Until the day it isn't.*

* * *

She heard Ashworth's boots on the stair an hour into the repair — she knew them by their rhythm, unhurried, metronomic, the tread of a man who had never once needed to hurry because the world arranged itself around his pace. The door opened without a knock. Of course it did. Doors were for other people.

"Master Okonkwo."

"Colonel." She did not rise. Craftsmen at their benches did not rise; it was one of the few courtesies her trade permitted her, and she took it.

He came to stand beside her, close enough that she could smell the bay rum on him and, beneath it, the cold iron scent of the yard. He watched her hands for a long moment. She let him watch. There was nothing in her fingers now but competence, and competence was the best disguise she owned — better than her brother's coat, better than the flat way she'd taught her voice to sit.

"The remontoire?" he asked.

"A fatigued detent spring. It will be replaced within the hour. Your watch will keep time better than it ever has."

"Better than London intended."

"London intends its instruments to be maintained. Most owners fail them." She said it mildly, eyes on the movement, and heard the silence stretch — the particular silence of a man deciding whether he had been insulted.

When he spoke again, his voice had the pleasantness of a blade laid flat on a table. "You've become quite the fixture in this city, Master Okonkwo. The observatory. My own chronometer. Old Vasco's shop taking apprentices again after all these years." A pause, measured like a pendulum swing. "You move through doors easily. Gates. Checkpoints. It's remarkable how little friction your work encounters."

*He knows,* said the animal part of her. *He knows and he is enjoying this.* But another part — the part that had spent fifteen years reading dials — said: *No. He suspects. Suspicion is not knowledge, and knowledge is not proof, and a man like this does not act on anything less than proof because a man like this cannot afford to be wrong.*

She set the new spring into its seat with a click no louder than a heartbeat.

"Friction wears out mechanisms, Colonel. I remove it where I find it. It's the whole of my trade."

"And what do you find when you look inside things, Master Okonkwo? You must see a great deal, in your line of work. The insides of clocks. The insides of buildings." He turned the brass crucifix on the mantel a quarter-turn, absently, the way a man touches a talisman. "The insides of institutions."

"I find that everything runs on the same principle." She reached for the oiler. "A stored force, released in measured amounts. Nothing more. Nothing less."

"Mm." He watched her a moment longer. Then, at the door, without turning: "My sergeant tells me you arrived before dawn. Diligent. I admire diligence." The pause stretched, delicate as a balance staff. "I also keep records, Master Okonkwo. Of comings and goings. One learns, in my position, that records have a way of becoming... inconvenient. For some people."

The door closed behind him. His boots descended, unhurried, metronomic.

Edmund sat very still, the oiler suspended above the jewel bearing, and let herself shake — just once, head to heel, like a mechanism releasing a jammed tooth. Then she breathed, and finished the work.

* * *

By lamplight, with the shutters half-closed against the yard below, she completed the adjustment. The chronometer ticked in her hands, alive again, its beat even and deep as a sleeping heart. She regulated it against her own pocket watch, corrected by two seconds, corrected again, until the two instruments agreed like old friends.

And then she did the thing she had come here to do — the thing Miriam had argued against in the census office two nights ago, her face lit by candle-glow and fury:

*"You can't. If he catches you near his papers—"*

*"He invited me into his rooms. Men like Ashworth believe their walls are proof against everyone. That belief is the flaw in the mechanism. I'm going to inspect it."*

The chronometer sat on the mantel again, running true, a gift returned. Ashworth's writing desk stood against the wall, bare except for ink and paper — but the second drawer, the shallow one beneath the desktop, did not match the others. She had noticed it within her first five minutes in the room, the way she noticed everything: the wood grain ran wrong, the shadow line sat a hair too deep. A locksmith's drawer. A hiding place built by someone who understood hiding places.

Her picks were in her sleeve before she had decided to reach for them. Fifteen years of passing as a man had taught her that decisions were luxuries; action came first and conscience caught up after, if it caught up at all.

The lock surrendered in ninety seconds. Inside lay a ledger — slim, bound in black leather, unmarked — and a bundle of letters tied with cord.

She did not read the letters. There was no time, and she already knew their shape; she had spent nine days living inside the shape of them. But the ledger she opened, and the first page struck her like a blow: not the syndicate accounts, not the sold men, but something worse in its smallness. A private record. Dates. Names. *Irregularities*, each one noted in Ashworth's own precise hand.

*Okonkwo — observatory — 3 visits.*
*Halloway, M. — census — queries re: muster rolls — 2 warnings issued.*
*Vasco — clockmaker — associate of O. — surveillance ordered.*

And at the bottom of the page, dated yesterday, the entry that stopped her breath:

*Census office — tonight. Halloway to be detained for questioning. Okonkwo to be left undisturbed until the ledgers surface. Then both.*

*Then both.* He had known. Not suspected — known, perhaps for days, and he had let her come anyway, had put his own chronometer in her hands and watched her work, because a trap is most certain when the prey believes itself clever. The raid on Miriam's office was not coming. It was happening tonight. Perhaps it was happening now, at this very moment, while Edmund stood in the Colonel's quarters holding the proof of it in her shaking hands.

She photographed the page in her memory — every date, every name, every word — then closed the ledger, locked the drawer, wiped the brass with her sleeve, and packed her tools with hands that had gone steady again because she forced them to be steady, because the work was not done, because the work was never done.

The chronometer ticked on the mantel, keeping perfect time. Eleven minutes past the hour. She thought of it counting down toward the tide, toward the census office, toward Miriam.

Edmund shouldered her empty case and walked down through the barracks, past the sentry who had smiled at her, past Sergeant Bray standing his silent watch on the stairs, out into the pewter dawn of the yard. Behind her, in the Colonel's quarters, the repaired watch marked each second faithfully — a machine doing honestly what its master did not.

She broke into a run at the gate, and did not stop until the towers of the census office rose against the reddening sky — and she saw, in the street below them, the lanterns of a file of soldiers already climbing the steps.
