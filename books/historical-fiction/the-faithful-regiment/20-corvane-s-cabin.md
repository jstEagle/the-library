## 20. Corvane's Cabin

The ship was called the *Meridian*, and she did not so much sail as lean, shouldering through the harbor swell with the impatient grace of a thing that had somewhere better to be. Edmund felt every degree of that lean in her knees as she climbed the rope ladder they had thrown down for her — a ladder, not gangplanks; the *Meridian* had already begun to cast off when she and Miriam reached the quay, running, and some sailor with more mercy than sense had thrown them a line.

Her hands were burned. She noticed it only now, halfway up, salt water stinging the raw places where Vasco's workshop fire had kissed her knuckles two nights ago. She had not bandaged them. There had been no time. There had been no time since the moment she first opened the false back of the master clock's case, and now there was no time left at all, because behind her the harbor bells of the city were ringing out of pattern — three strikes, pause, three strikes — the Regiment's alarm, carrying across the water like a hand reaching for her collar.

She climbed. Miriam climbed above her, census ledger strapped to her back like a shield, her braid coming loose, her breath tearing out of her in white rags. Below them both the quay receded, and on it, small with distance, figures in Regiment grey spilled from the gatehouse like ink from a broken bottle.

Then the deck took her weight, and hands took her arms, and the line was hauled up after Miriam, and someone shouted *cast off, cast off, you madwoman, we're past the mooring* — and the *Meridian* leaned away from the world Edmund had spent eleven years learning to be invisible inside.

She stood at the rail. She could not make herself do anything else. The city shrank against the dawn: the tower first, then the customs house roof, then the barracks wall where — she made herself look, made herself count the seconds — no, she could see nothing of the cells, nothing of Vasco, nothing but smoke rising thin and ordinary from the garrison kitchens, as though it were any morning at all.

"Edmund." Miriam's hand found hers over the rail. Her grip was cold and shaking. "Edmund, we have to go below. The magistrate—"

"I know."

"You're bleeding."

"I know."

But still she stood until the tower itself went under the horizon haze, and something in her chest went with it, and what remained was lighter and worse.

* * *

The cabin was aft, below the quarterdeck, and it smelled of lamp oil and wet wool and, faintly, of oranges. A steward knocked twice and opened the door without waiting, which told Edmund everything about how urgently word had traveled up from the deck: the magistrate had been expecting them since before the tide.

Magistrate Corvane was smaller than his reputation. She had imagined him tall — reformers always seemed to be imagined tall, looming, carved in marble by their enemies' pamphlets — but the man who rose from behind a desk buried in papers was narrow-shouldered and grey at the temples, with ink stains on three fingers and eyes that moved the way a good movement-train moves: quick, precise, missing nothing, resting nowhere long enough to be caught.

He looked at Edmund's burned hands first. Then at the clock case she carried, its brass fittings dull with salt spray, its weight wrong for a clock. Then at Miriam, and at the census ledger strapped to her back, and last at the door, as if confirming that no one else had come.

"You are the clockmaker," he said. Not a question.

"I am."

"And the clockmaker is bleeding, and the census taker is armed with paperwork, and between you you have made the Faithful Regiment ring its own alarm bells before breakfast." He gestured to the chairs across the desk. "Sit. Both of you. I have been reading about this city for four months, gentlemen, and I confess I have grown very tired of reading."

The word *gentlemen* landed in the cabin like a dropped tool.

Edmund heard it and did not flinch — years of practice held her face still, held her voice level, held her shoulders square beneath the padded coat — but she felt Miriam go rigid beside her, felt the small sharp intake of breath, and understood that Miriam was watching her, waiting to see what she would do, what she would permit, what this room would be allowed to become.

Corvane saw it too. His eyes came back to Edmund and stayed.

"Ah," he said quietly. "I see that I have said something true by accident. That is often how the truth gets into a room." He sat down himself, folding his ink-stained hands. "Forgive me. Or don't. But understand that whatever you are, whoever you are, I have crossed this water to hear evidence against men who sell human beings by the ledger-line, and I will take that evidence from any mouth willing to speak it. The rest is your affair, not mine."

Edmund set the clock case on the desk. Her hands, she noted distantly, were steady. They had been steady through checkpoints and raids and a burning shop and a rope ladder, and they were steady now, and she thought: *Vasco would be proud of the hands, at least.*

"The case opens from the bottom," she said. "The hinge pin is a replacement. Turn it counterclockwise."

Corvane turned it. The false base swung open, and he lifted out the ledgers one at a time — forty-one names in the first, conscription dates and transfer dates and sums received, written in the neat administrative hand of the Regiment's paymaster; the second book worse, the second book the syndicate's own copy, with prices; the third a bundle of loose pages in Miriam's careful script, cross-referenced against nine years of census records, each missing man accounted for, each silence documented.

For a long time the magistrate read. The ship rolled. Somewhere above them boots crossed the deck in a rhythm Edmund's ear automatically measured — seven seconds apart, a man pacing, worried — and somewhere below them the hull thrummed with the engine of the tide.

When Corvane finally looked up, his face had changed. It was not rage; it was something colder and more useful than rage. It was the face of a man doing arithmetic.

"Forty-one," he said.

"Forty-one confirmed," Miriam said. Her voice was hoarse from the run, from the salt air, from nine days of fear, but it did not waver. "There may be more. The census records only cover the last nine years. The ledgers go back further."

"They go back further," Edmund said. "The earliest entry is dated nineteen years ago. The same year the observatory clock was built."

Corvane looked at her sharply. "You know this how?"

"Because my master helped build it." The words came out of her flat and even, each one placed like a gear tooth into its socket. "Vasco Reyes. He built the vault into the case. He built the mechanism that hid these books for nineteen years while the Regiment sold men out of your harbor, Magistrate, and he has spent those nineteen years knowing it and saying nothing, and two nights ago he stood in a public square and confessed it to the whole city so that I could carry these books to you." Her voice cracked on the last words, finally, the gear slipping. "They have him in the cells. I watched them take him. He was smiling."

The cabin was silent except for the sea.

Corvane closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a coffin.

"Then we will not waste his gift," he said. "Miss—" he paused, delicately, an offer rather than a demand, "—Okonkwo. I will need your testimony. All of it. Every name you can give me, every date, every detail of what you found in that clock, and how, and when. And I will need it under your true name, sworn and witnessed, because evidence given by a ghost can be dismissed by a clever advocate, and Ashworth will retain the cleverest advocate money can buy."

The room tilted slightly — or the ship did; it was impossible to say.

*Your true name.* Four words, and Edmund felt eleven years of craft begin to tremble around her: the binding of her chest, the low register she had trained her throat into, the way she signed *E. Okonkwo* with a flourish copied from a dead boy's letters, the way she had taught herself to sit, to walk, to laugh too rarely and never too high. All of it built to survive exactly this question. And here it was, asked not with cruelty but with procedure, by a tired man with ink on his fingers who only wanted the record to hold.

Miriam's hand found hers under the desk. Did not squeeze. Just rested there, warm despite the cold of the crossing, an anchor offered, not imposed.

"He means it kindly," Miriam said softly. "But he also means it truly. Testimony needs a name, Edmund. It needs *you*."

And Edmund found that she was looking at the lamp flame, the way she looked at a difficult escapement, and that her mind was doing what her mind always did — breaking the problem into parts, finding the pivot, locating the point of least resistance.

The problem was not courage. She had discovered that somewhere on the quay, watching the tower sink. Courage had stopped being the obstacle days ago; she had run through gunfire for a stranger's brother and a dead man's redemption, and courage had turned out to be the cheapest thing she owned.

The problem was grief. Because the name she had worn for eleven years was her brother's, and to set it down felt like burying him a second time, and she had not finished burying him the first.

The problem was also — and this surprised her, turning it over in the lamplight — hope. Because underneath the fear, underneath the grief, there was a small bright terrible wanting, the wanting she had kept locked in the deepest drawer of herself, behind the false panel, weighted with brass: *to be seen. To walk into a room and have the room contain all of her. To sign her work with her own hand and have the work answer for itself.*

Vasco had known. Of course he had known; he had known since she was fourteen and re-cutting her first escape wheel, and he had never once said it aloud, and his silence had been the largest kindness of her life. And his last lesson, shouted across a square while soldiers dragged him down, had been: *the truth keeps better time than any lie.*

"The name," Edmund said, and her voice came out strange to her, unpracticed, higher than the register she had lived in for a decade, "is not Okonkwo."

Corvane waited. Even the ship seemed to wait, hanging at the top of a swell.

"My brother was Okonkwo. Chidi Okonkwo. He died of fever when I was sixteen, and I took his name because a girl alone cannot inherit a trade, cannot hold a lease, cannot stand in a guild hall, cannot—" she stopped, breathed, started again. "Cannot exist, in that city, without being someone's property or someone's problem. So I became him. I became him so well that I forgot, some mornings, which of us was alive."

She stood up. She did not decide to; her body simply rose, the way a mainspring releases, all that stored tension finding its purpose at last.

"My name is Adaeze Okonkwo. My mother named me for the first daughter of her line, and I have not said it aloud in eleven years, and I am saying it now in front of a magistrate of the Crown because—" and here her voice did break, fully, and she let it, "—because a dead man's ghost gave his life for these books, and a living woman is going to swear to them."

The lamp flame steadied. The ship came off the swell and settled.

Miriam was crying, silently, furiously, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand as though the tears were an inefficiency she refused to permit. Corvane had risen too, and he inclined his head — not a bow, magistrates did not bow, but something adjacent to one, something older.

"Adaeze Okonkwo," he repeated, testing the weight of it, and then, formally: "Witness. Your testimony will be taken at eight bells. I warn you it will be thorough, and I warn you that once it is given, there is no unnamed woman to hide behind. Ashworth's advocates will pull your life apart seam by seam to discredit you. They will call you a liar, an impostor, a criminal, worse. Are you prepared for that?"

"No," Adaeze said honestly. And then: "Yes. Both. Ask your questions."

* * *

It took four hours. Corvane was as good as his warning: he was thorough the way a watchmaker is thorough, taking the movement apart piece by piece, asking her to account for every wheel. When had she first entered the observatory. What had the jammed mechanism looked like. Which page, which entry, whose signature. Where had she hidden the copies. Who else knew. What did Vasco Reyes say, exactly, on the night he gave her the key — *word for word, if you please, witness; the exact words matter in law.*

She answered everything. Her voice grew hoarse. A steward brought tea and Miriam made her drink it. Twice Corvane sent for his clerk, a young man with a beautiful copperplate hand, and dictated passages for verification, and twice the clerk glanced up at Adaeze with naked curiosity and twice Corvane silenced him with a look.

Near the end, Corvane laid the paymaster's ledger open before her and tapped one entry near the middle.

"This name. Lieutenant Pell. He appears in the Regiment rolls as transferred to coastal duty, and he appears here as sold, with a price. Do you understand what that means?"

Adaeze leaned close. The entry was in the same neat hand as all the others, but the ink was different — newer.

"It means someone in the Regiment is still selling," she said. "This entry is recent. Within the year."

"It means," Corvane said quietly, "that Ashworth may not be the head of the beast. It means that when we take him, whatever remains of the syndicate will already know we are coming, and will already be moving its books." He closed the ledger and looked at her for a long moment. "You have a craftsman's eye, Witness. You notice what does not fit. I will want that eye again. There is a trial coming, and trials are machines, and machines jam."

Above them, eight bells rang out across the water, and the sound came down through the deck and into Adaeze's chest, and she thought of another bell, in another tower, that had struck true at last because an old man had given everything to wind it.

She thought: *I will testify for you, Vasco. I will testify with my own name, and my own voice, and I will not stop until every one of those forty-one entries is answered for.*

Outside the cabin's small stern window, the open sea ran silver toward morning, and behind them — far behind now, a smudge, a memory, a wound — the port city lay under the flag of a Regiment that did not yet know it was already falling.

Corvane gathered his papers, then paused at the door.

"One more thing, Witness. The *Meridian* makes for the assize court at Kingshaven — six days' sail. But a fast cutter left the harbor an hour behind us, flying syndicate colors under a merchant's flag. If it carries word ahead of us..." He did not finish. He did not need to.

Adaeze looked at Miriam, and Miriam looked back, and neither of them needed to speak, because they had learned each other's silences the way one learns a clock: by listening to what ticked underneath.

"Then we'd best arrive first," Adaeze said, "and in working order."

Corvane almost smiled. "I begin to see why the Regiment feared you, Miss Okonkwo."

"Not Miss," she said, and found that the correction cost her nothing at all. "Just Okonkwo. It's mine now. Both names. I'm keeping them."

The door closed behind him. The ship leaned into the long swells of the open water, and Adaeze Okonkwo — daughter, sister, clockmaker, witness — sat down beside the woman she loved, in front of a window full of morning, and began, calmly, to plan.
