## 22. The Faithful

The sea had a sound Edmund had never learned in the shop: not ticking, but breathing. A long inhale against the hull, a long exhale, and between them the creak of timber working under strain, patient as an old man rising from a chair. She stood at the rail of the *Corvane's* chartered brig — she still did not know its name; Miriam had told her twice and twice the name had slid off her like water off oiled brass — and watched the port fall away behind them.

The Faithful Regiment's tower was still visible, a gray finger against the morning haze, and at its summit the observatory dome caught the first true sunlight. Somewhere inside it, the master clock was running. Vasco's chimes had rung across the square three days ago, eleven strokes where there should have been none, and the whole city had lifted its head like a deer scenting smoke. By now, she supposed, the clock was keeping perfect time again. It deserved to. It had done more honest work in one morning than the Regiment had done in forty years.

"You'll wear a groove in that deck rail," Miriam said behind her.

Edmund did not turn immediately. There was a strange luxury in being slow, in letting a moment stretch without fear that someone would notice her lingering, question why a clockmaker's apprentice stood idle, ask for papers she could not produce. For years every second of her life had been accounted for — to customers, to soldiers, to the fiction of Edmund Okonkwo, son of a dead man, brother to a dead boy. Now the only thing accounting for her time was the tide.

"I'm not wearing anything," she said finally. "I'm listening."

"To what?"

"The ship. It keeps time too. Just looser about it." She turned. Miriam stood wrapped in a borrowed coat two sizes too large, her census ledger — the real one, the one with forty-one names struck through in her own careful hand — clutched against her chest as though the wind might take it. Her hair had come loose from its knot. There was a bruise fading along her jaw from the cell, yellow-green now, the color of old verdigris. Edmund had catalogued every mark on her during those first hours after the cells, when they'd sat in Corvane's cabin shaking so hard they could barely hold the tea, and she found she could not stop cataloguing even now. Not out of horror. Out of something closer to devotion. Every mark was proof they had both survived.

"Corvane wants us on deck by noon," Miriam said. "He wants to go over the ledgers again before we make the crossing. He says Ashworth's advocates will attack the chain of custody first and the contents second."

"Ashworth's advocates." Edmund tasted the words. "It still doesn't seem real that he'll have advocates. That he'll have a trial at all."

"That's rather the point of everything we did." Miriam came to stand beside her at the rail, close enough that their shoulders touched through the coat. "If we'd simply put a bullet in him, he'd be a martyr by autumn. This way he has to sit in a room while men read his own handwriting aloud."

Edmund looked at her. "You've been rehearsing that speech since the docks."

"Since the customs house," Miriam admitted. "I find that if I don't rehearse my outrages I lose track of them, and then I start feeling charitable, and charity is how the Regiment survived forty years. Everyone felt charitable. Everyone felt sure that someone else would look into the ledgers eventually." She paused. "I looked into the ledgers eventually. It took me a census cycle and a forged seal and my entire career, but I got there."

Below them, a sailor coiled rope with quick economical movements, each loop identical to the last. Edmund watched his hands and felt the familiar pull of craft — the desire to understand any motion repeated until it became truth. That was what clocks had given her, before everything: the belief that if you studied a mechanism long enough, it would confess. The Regiment had been a mechanism too. It had simply been built by people who understood that most souls never look inside the case.

* * *

They went below at noon, into the cabin where Magistrate Corvane had spread the ledgers across his table like a general's maps. He was not what Edmund had expected. In the shop, over the years, she had built up a picture of reformist magistrates from the talk of merchants: firebrands, thunderers, men who enjoyed the sound of their own indignation. Corvane was a small, dry, precise man with ink-stained fingers and the habit of reading with one eye closed, as though each document were a sight he was taking.

"Sit," he said, without looking up. "Both of you. Master Okonkwo — or should I say—"

"Edmund is fine," she said. "For now."

His closed eye opened. He regarded her for a moment with the same attention he gave his documents, and she braced herself for the question everyone aboard had been too polite to ask directly: *which name is true?*

What he said instead was: "Your repair work on the chronometer in Ashworth's quarters. Describe it for me."

She blinked. "The — you want me to describe the repair?"

"In detail. As though I were a hostile examiner who knows nothing of horology and less of mercy."

So she described it. The worn pallet stones, replaced and re-oiled; the mainspring that had been let down improperly and nearly ruined; the telltale scratch inside the case where someone had pried at the movement with a knife — Ashworth himself, she suspected, checking for hidden compartments, finding nothing because there was nothing to find, because the evidence had never been in the colonel's own room but in the tower above his head all along. She described the steadiness of her hands, which had not been steady at all, and how she had made them steady by treating terror as a specification: *the hands must not tremble; therefore the breath must be counted; therefore count the breath.* Corvane listened with both eyes open now, and when she finished he nodded once, slowly.

"You understand mechanisms," he said. "That will matter. At trial, the defense will call the ledgers forgeries. They will bring their own experts. I need witnesses who can speak to paper and ink and binding the way you speak to springs and escapements. Can you do that?"

"I can learn," Edmund said. "Paper has a grain like wood. Ink ages the way oil does. Everything leaves a record of its own making. That's—" She stopped, hearing her mentor's voice beneath her own. "That's what Vasco taught me. Nothing can be made without leaving the marks of the maker. You just have to know where to look."

"Then we will teach you where to look." Corvane gathered a ledger and squared its edges against the table. "And you, Mistress Halloway, will testify to the census discrepancies. Forty-one names."

"Forty-one confirmed," Miriam said. "There may be more. The earlier cycles were kept sloppily — deliberately, I think. A sloppy census hides a great deal."

"It hid your brother."

The cabin went very quiet. Even the ship seemed to hold its creaking. Edmund reached under the table and found Miriam's hand, and Miriam gripped back hard enough to hurt, and neither of them looked away from the magistrate, because looking away would have been a kind of lie, and they had spent enough of their lives lying.

"Yes," Miriam said. "It hid my brother. And I will say so on the stand, in those words, if they let me. And if they don't let me, I'll find other words that mean the same thing."

Corvane inclined his head — a small motion, formal, almost courtly — and Edmund understood that this dry little man had just given them something no one in authority had ever given either of them: acknowledgment without pity. Then he returned to his papers, and the audience was over, and they climbed back into the gray-gold light of the afternoon deck.

* * *

The days at sea took on a shape, and Edmund, who had spent her life imposing shapes on time, was surprised to find how willingly she surrendered to this one. Mornings: lessons with Corvane's clerks, learning the anatomy of documents, the watermarks of the syndicate's paper stock, the way the Regiment's record-ink had been thinned in certain years — thinned, Miriam noted grimly, precisely in the years of the largest sales, as though even the ink had been made complicit. Afternoons: Miriam cross-referencing, her pen moving in swift sure strokes, rebuilding the vanished men out of arithmetic. Forty-one names, read aloud each evening at supper like a litany, so that none of them would become mere exhibits.

Evenings belonged to the two of them. They walked the deck. They stood at the rail. They talked about nothing — about whether gulls slept on the water, about whether the cook's stew contained mutton or treason, about the exact shade of the sky at the moment the sun touched the horizon — and the talking-about-nothing was itself a discovery, because neither of them had ever been safe enough to waste words before.

On the fifth night, Miriam said, "Tell me about him. Your brother."

Edmund leaned on the rail. Below, the bow wave unspooled white and endless, a ribbon being cut forever and never severed.

"Tomas was louder than me," she said. "Everyone always assumes the one who passes must be the bold one, but it wasn't boldness, it was just — quietness. Tomas filled rooms. I fill doorways. When the fever took him, Father couldn't bear to say his name, and I — I think I thought if I said it often enough, wore it often enough, it would keep him alive somehow. And then it stopped being grief and started being survival, and then it stopped being survival and started being just... my name. And I hated it. And I couldn't set it down." She laughed softly, without much humor. "You spend nine years carrying a name and it grows roots."

"Do you want to set it down?"

"No." The answer came faster than she expected, and surer. She turned it over in her mind, testing it, the way she'd test a repaired wheel — does it run true, does it bind, does it sing? It ran true. "No. I don't. Everyone thinks the ending of a story like mine is the unveiling — the wig comes off, the crowd gasps, the truth triumphs. But that's a scene, not a life. Tomas was real. He was eight years of a real boy, and I loved him, and the name is the only part of him left walking around. I'm not going to bury him twice." She looked at Miriam. "I'm going to keep it. And I'm going to add mine underneath it. Both. On the testimony roll, on the guild register, on whatever document they put in front of me. *Edmund Okonkwo* — and beneath it, in smaller letters, the name my mother gave me. Let the clerks figure out how to file it. Let them invent a new column. Someone has to be first in the new column."

Miriam was silent a long moment. The wind moved her loose hair, and the lantern at the masthead swung its slow pendulum arc above them, and Edmund thought: *there it is again, everywhere, everything swinging, everything keeping some kind of time.*

"My brother's name was Daniel," Miriam said at last. "I haven't said it aloud since the cells. On the census rolls he's a dash — *departed, destination unknown.* I wrote that dash myself, four years ago, before I knew what the dashes meant. I signed my brother's erasure with my own hand." Her voice did not break, but it went thin, like wire drawn too fine. "At the trial, when they read the forty-one names, I want to be the one who reads Daniel's. Not the magistrate. Me. I erased him; I'll restore him. Same hand. That seems right."

"It is right," Edmund said. "It's exactly right. It's — " she searched for the word, the craftsman's word — "it's truing. You bend a thing wrong, you bend it back yourself. No one else's hands know the shape of the error."

Miriam looked at her then, fully, in the lantern light, and whatever was in her face made Edmund's chest ache in a way that had nothing to do with fear or grief, a new sensation entirely, one for which her vocabulary — built of tolerances and escapements and the cold mathematics of gears — had no ready term. So she did what she always did with an unfamiliar mechanism: she reached out carefully and touched it. She took Miriam's hand, and Miriam let her, and they stood like that at the rail while the ship breathed and the stars wheeled overhead in their ancient, indifferent, perfectly regulated courses.

"We could have met differently," Miriam said. "In another life. You repairing a clock in my parlor. Me taking the census at your door. One question, politely asked, politely answered. No ledgers. No cells."

"In another life I'd have answered honestly and been hanged for it."

"In another life there'd be nothing to hang you for." Miriam squeezed her hand. "I keep trying to imagine that life. A city where the Regiment never rotted. Where the tower clock is just a clock. Do you know what I feel when I imagine it?" She shook her head. "Nothing. It won't hold an image. It's like trying to picture a room with no walls. Everything I am was built against them — my suspicion, my stubbornness, my — " she glanced sideways at Edmund, and the glance was warm and rueful at once — "my taste in company. Take the Regiment out of the world and I don't know who's standing here. But take them out of the *future*, and — that I can picture. That I can build."

"Then we build it," Edmund said. Simply. The way she'd say *then we replace the spring.*

* * *

Land came back on the ninth day, a low dark line that grew teeth and towers as the morning advanced, and by afternoon they were standing in the harbor of the capital, a harbor ten times the size of the one they'd fled, crammed with ships whose flags Edmund did not recognize and whose bells rang in harmonies she resolved, idly, professionally, to go and study someday.

Corvane came on deck in full official dress, transformed from the ink-stained reader into something formidable, and paused beside them at the gangway.

"The trial opens in six days," he said. "There will be crowds. There will be the Regiment's friends in the gallery, and the syndicate's money behind them, and reporters from three cities. Whatever you are to each other, decide it before you walk in, because they will use it. They will use everything. That is what trials are: machines for extracting truth, and like all machines they do not care what they crush in the process." He adjusted his cuffs. "I say this not to frighten you. I say it because you are the two bravest people I have transported in twenty years on the bench, and brave people deserve honest warnings, not comfort."

He descended the gangway. His clerk followed with the ledgers locked in an iron box, and Edmund watched the box until it vanished into the harbor crowd, feeling the strangest sensation: lightness. For nine days the weight of the evidence had ridden in her chest as surely as in the iron box. Now it belonged to the law, such as it was, such as it might become.

Miriam stood beside her with her small traveling bag and her borrowed coat, looking up at the capital's skyline with an expression Edmund had seen once before — in the observatory, at the stopped clock, in the moment before she began to work.

"Six days," Miriam said.

"Six days," Edmund agreed. "And then the rest of it. However long the rest of it takes."

They went down the gangway together onto foreign solid ground, and the crowd took them, and somewhere behind them the ship's bell rang the hour — slightly fast, Edmund noted, half a minute fast at least, someone ought to see to it — and ahead of them the city opened like a case whose works, for the first time in her life, she intended not merely to repair but to rebuild.

She carried her brother's name. Beneath it, waiting, her own. Between them, holding both, her hand in Miriam's, steady as a well-hung pendulum, keeping faith.
