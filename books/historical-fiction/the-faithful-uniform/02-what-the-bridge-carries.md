## 2. What the Bridge Carries

The bridge closed on a Tuesday, and the whole town came out to watch it happen, the way people will gather for anything that marks time — a hanging, a wedding, a tide turning.

Vesper Dunmore stood at the edge of the crowd with her hands in her coat pockets and her breath making small ghosts in front of her face, and she thought: *this is what a trap looks like when it closes politely.* No snap of iron. No shout. Just two men in oilskins walking out along the span with wrenches in their fists, unbolting the planked roadway section by section while the town watched and approved and even applauded a little, because the bridge had been groaning all autumn and everyone agreed it was better this way. Better to close it now than let winter take it down with someone on it.

Better. Safer. The words the war had taught them all to say without hearing.

Marrow's Reach sat at the end of its own long patience — a fishing town folded into a harbor that faced north, where the water went grey-glass by December and held its ice until March. The bridge was the only road out across the estuary. When it closed, the town closed with it. What came in after today would come by sea or not at all.

"Last crossing!" somebody called from the far end, and there was a scatter of laughter, and a boy on his father's shoulders waved both arms as though he were seeing off a ship.

Vesper did not laugh. She counted.

It was an old habit, seven years old, and it had followed her home from France like a dog that wouldn't be put down. She counted the gulls on the pilings — nine. She counted the boats riding at mooring — fourteen, three of them due to be hauled out before the ice. She counted the crowd — perhaps two hundred, most of Marrow's Reach, which meant most of her patients' families, which meant most of the people who would ask her questions over the coming months about sons and brothers and husbands whose names appeared in no official list she was permitted to acknowledge.

And she counted the last vehicles on the bridge.

There were four. A mail cart, already through. A farmer's lorry stacked with turnips, already through. Then a black motorcar, mud-splashed to the windows, moving slowly because the bridge men had asked it to wait while they lifted a section of rail.

And behind it, on a grey army horse that looked as though it had been ridden hard from the rail junction forty miles inland, a single officer in a greatcoat with the collar turned up against the wind.

Vesper knew the way he sat a horse before she knew his face. There was a particular stillness some men had learned in France — not the stillness of calm but the stillness of men who had stopped trusting their own bodies to flinch, who had trained the flinch out of themselves at cost, and rode and walked and stood afterward with a kind of deliberate economy, as if every motion had to be justified to a board of inquiry. She had nursed beside that stillness. She had watched it come off trains wrapped in blankets.

The motorcar cleared the far span. The horseman didn't follow immediately. He reined in at the midpoint of the bridge, where the estuary showed through the gaps between the planks, dark water moving under the road like something alive beneath a floorboard, and he looked down at it for a long moment.

Then he looked up, along the crowd, along the quay, along the row of grey stone buildings with their red crosses painted on the roofs so that German gunners — if German gunners ever came, which they never had, which was the joke everyone made — would know what not to bomb.

He looked at the hospital. And then, because the world is not kind enough to leave things at almost, he looked at Vesper.

At first she thought it was nothing. Two hundred faces; one pair of eyes passing over them; probability alone said some fraction of a second would land on her. But the look *stopped*. It stayed. And even at four hundred yards, even through wind and the haze of coal smoke off the fish-curing sheds, Vesper felt the recognition move between them like current through wire.

*Major Aldous Renwick,* she thought. *Of course.*

Of course, because the war did not release you. It only changed your posting.

* * *

She had met him twice, and both times had been in France, and both times had been about paper.

The first time was 1915, at a casualty clearing station outside a town whose name she had since allowed herself to forget, because forgetting the name was easier than forgetting everything that happened near it. Renwick had been a captain then, attached to the records section — a thin, precise man with ink-stained cuffs and a voice so quiet that orderlies leaned in to hear him, which Vesper later understood was the entire design of the voice. He had come to reconcile the admission books against the regimental returns, and he had found eleven men admitted whom no regiment claimed.

"You have eleven men here," he told the matron, "whom the Army says do not exist."

"They exist," Matron Fenn had said. "I buried six of them Tuesday."

"Yes," Renwick had said, and written something down. He hadn't argued. That was the thing about him that Vesper remembered — he didn't argue, didn't bluster, didn't do any of the things that gave you somewhere to push back against. He wrote things down. He treated every discrepancy not as an insult to be shouted down but as a debt to be collected, patiently, line by line, until the ledger balanced or the man holding it confessed.

The second time was worse. The second time was 1916, and the discrepancy was a single name, and the name was Elias Thorne.

She had been carrying the admission slip to the records tent herself — she'd taken to doing that, in those days, inserting herself into the paperwork chain wherever she could, adjusting, omitting, misfiling with a nurse's gentle hands — and Renwick had been standing at the tent flap going through the day's returns, and Elias's slip had been in her apron pocket, and Renwick had looked at her and said, "Sister, you're bleeding."

She wasn't. One of her patients was, had been, all over her sleeve; she'd forgotten it was there. But for one vertiginous moment she had thought: *he knows, he can see it, the slip is showing through the fabric like a wound.*

"No, Captain," she'd said. "Not mine."

"No," he'd agreed mildly. "It rarely is." And he'd gone back to his papers, and she had walked past him into the tent, and filed Elias Thorne's admission under a regiment that had been destroyed at Loos and could not contradict her, and walked out again, and Captain Aldous Renwick had nodded to her as she passed, courteous as a churchwarden.

She had told herself for a year afterward that he hadn't known. She had mostly believed it.

Now he was dismounting at the foot of the bridge ramp in her own town, and an army corporal was taking his horse, and the two bridge men were swinging the final section of roadway up on its chains like a drawbridge in a storybook, and the crowd was making a sound — a soft communal *ah*, half grief and half satisfaction, the sound of a door closing on a house you're glad to be shut inside of.

The season was sealed. Whatever happened in Marrow's Reach between now and spring thaw would happen without witnesses, without reinforcements, without appeal.

Renwick handed his riding crop to the corporal, straightened his greatcoat, and walked toward the crowd.

Toward the hospital.

Toward her.

* * *

"Marrow's Reach," he said, when he reached her, and it was not quite a question. "I thought it was you. Sister Dunmore."

"Captain Renwick." She kept her voice level and found, to her surprise, that it cooperated. Seven years of practice. "Though I believe you'd be Major now."

"Major." He tested the word as if checking whether it fit. "Yes. Time does that." He was older — they both were — grey coming in at his temples, lines around his eyes that hadn't been carved yet in 1916. But the eyes themselves were unchanged: pale, patient, the eyes of a man reading a document he intended to finish. "You're settled here, then. Permanently?"

"The hospital needed staff. I needed—" She paused, and chose honesty, because Renwick could smell the other thing at forty yards. "Somewhere the wounded stop arriving."

"And do they? Stop?"

"Slower than I'd like." She gestured up the hill toward the convalescent hospital, its grey walls, its red-crossed roofs. "We've forty-two beds. Most of them full. Men sent to us to finish healing, mostly. Frostbite, fevers, nerves. The war has gotten tired of them and hopes the sea air will finish the job."

"It usually does," Renwick said. "One way or another." He looked past her at the harbor, at the boats, at the low white sky pressing down on all of it like a lid. "A closed town. I confess I've never worked in one before. Everything arrives by sea, everything leaves by sea, and the sea keeps its own schedule." He smiled — a small, correct smile, the smile of a man being pleasant at a function. "It should make my work very simple."

"And what is your work, Major? If I may ask."

"You may." He turned back to her, and for a moment the pleasantness dropped away entirely, the way a coat drops, revealing the same thing underneath that had always been there. "Discrepancies, Sister Dunmore. I investigate discrepancies. Somewhere between what the regiments report and what the hospitals admit, men go missing. Not missing the way you mean at the Front — missing the way paper means. Names that appear in one book and vanish from all the others. Forty-one of them, routed through or near this coast in the past three years." He said the number lightly, the way a man mentions the weather. "Someone, somewhere, has been keeping a different set of books. Or tearing pages out of the right ones. Either way, the numbers must balance. It's the only thing I'm good for, I find. Balancing."

Behind him, the bridge men locked the last chain. Someone in the crowd began to sing — an old hauling song, tuneless and cheerful, and others joined in, and the sound rolled out over the freezing water.

"Forty-one," Vesper repeated. Her heart was doing something complicated and silent, the way a machine does its real work inside its casing. "That seems a large number for clerical error."

"It is," Renwick agreed. "That's rather the point." He took a card from his breast pocket and offered it to her — cream stock, printed name, the crest of the Records Section. "I'll be interviewing the hospital staff beginning tomorrow. Routine. Everyone, in order of seniority. Nothing to be alarmed about." He said *nothing to be alarmed about* the way doctors say *you'll feel a small pinch.*

"I see."

"Sister Dunmore." He inclined his head, and the pale eyes rested on her one beat longer than courtesy required — one beat, no more, a metronome's extra tick. "It's good to see you survived. I remember thinking, in France, that you were the sort of person who takes care of things. I'm glad to find the war hasn't cured you of it."

He turned and walked up the hill toward the hospital, his boots ringing on the frozen cobbles, and the crowd parted for him without knowing why, the way water parts for a keel.

Vesper stood in the cold with his card in her glove and listened to the singing and did not join it.

*Forty-one.* He had the number. He had the number, and the number was exact, and exactness meant he had already done the arithmetic she had spent seven years trying to blur — every forged transfer, every sympathetic clerk, every name she had whispered to a chaplain or slipped into a mass grave's margin or carried, once, in a pocket over her heart across the Channel. All of it reduced, by a patient man with ink-stained cuffs, to a clean figure and a closed bridge.

Above the town, the hospital's chimneys were already smoking against the dusk. Two streets beyond it, past the customs house with its barred windows, the harbor lay flat and grey and already thinking about ice.

Vesper put the card in her pocket, next to nothing, and went up the hill to count her patients before dark — all of them, every bed, every name, the ones on the books and the one who wasn't.

Winter had arrived. It had arrived politely, tipped its hat, and shown her its arithmetic.

Tomorrow, she thought, climbing, tomorrow I will think what to do.

But some part of her, the part that had started the ledger in the first place, already knew that the thinking was over — that the moment a man with a number in his mouth had crossed onto her side of the closing bridge, the only question left was how much she was willing to lose, and in what order.
