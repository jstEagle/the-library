## 14. Fitting a Dead Man's Coat

The trunk smelled of camphor and someone else's life.

Vesper had carried it up from the linen store after lights-out, hugging it against her chest like a child, and now it sat open on the floor of her room at the top of the nurses' quarters, its lid propped against the wall. Lieutenant Harrow's effects, catalogued in Matron Calloway's neat hand: a pocket watch that no longer ran, a photograph of a woman squinting into sun outside a bakery, a tin of tobacco gone dry as autumn leaves, two shirts, a razor strop, and folded in tissue paper at the bottom — because Harrow had been the sort of man who kept things in tissue paper — a spare officer's uniform.

It was the uniform of a man who had intended to survive. That was what struck Vesper first, kneeling over the trunk with the lamp turned low. Men who expected to die sent their spare kit home or traded it for cigarettes. Men who expected to live kept a second coat pressed and mothballed against the day they'd need to look respectable again. Harrow had died of fever on a Tuesday, delirious, calling for the woman from the photograph, and his spare uniform had waited three weeks in the dark for someone to claim it. No one had. His regiment was somewhere east; his mother, if he had one, was a name on a form.

Vesper lifted the coat out by its shoulders and held it up to the lamplight.

Wool, good quality, the brass buttons dulled but sound. The tailoring was generous through the chest — Harrow had been a bigger man than Elias, broader, taller by an inch or more — but Elias had been a hospital orderly for seven years and knew how to stand so that clothes hung right. It could be done. It would have to be done. She laid the coat across her bed and stood looking at it the way you might look at a door you'd decided to walk through without knowing what was on the other side.

*You're wearing a dead man,* she thought. *You're about to dress a living one in him.*

She went to work.

* * *

The sewing took until well past midnight. Vesper had learned needlework from her grandmother — the same grandmother whose fortune, she now knew, had been built on ships that sailed under other flags — and her hands remembered everything even when her mind wanted to forget. She let out the seams at the shoulders first, working by feel as much as sight, the lamp turned so its light fell away from the window. A lit window at one in the morning was a confession. She had checked the street twice before striking the match.

The tunic came apart under her fingers like something being persuaded rather than forced. She thought, absurdly, of the ledger beneath the floorboard downstairs — seven years of names stitched into a hidden place, each one a small act of surgery performed on the official record. Now she was performing the same operation in reverse: taking a true thing, a dead lieutenant's coat, and altering it just enough to hold a different truth inside it. The wool didn't care whose shoulders it warmed. Wool had no politics. It was only people who insisted on names.

By half past one the shoulders were done. By two, the trousers were hemmed and the belt punched with a new hole. She tried none of it on. There wasn't time for vanity and no mirror large enough anyway, and besides — the uniform wasn't meant for her. It was meant for a man two streets away behind bars, who would put it on at dawn the morning after next and become, for eleven minutes between the customs house door and the Meridian's gangway, a lieutenant in His Majesty's Army named Harrow.

Eleven minutes. She had counted them out already, walking the route in her head: the customs house steps, the cobbled lane, the corner by the chandler's, the quay. Eleven minutes if he walked slowly enough not to draw notice and quickly enough not to be late for a tide that waited for no one.

Vesper set down the needle and flexed her aching fingers. Then she took the transit papers from where they lay flat between the pages of her Bible — blank forms, requisitioned from Harrow's kit, waiting for a hand to bring them to life — and dipped her pen.

* * *

The signature was the hard part.

Anyone could forge a name. What couldn't be forged, what clerks and majors and suspicious men with careful eyes looked for, was habit. A signature written once is a performance. A signature written a hundred times is a scar. Renwick — and she thought of Renwick now constantly, the way you think of weather coming — Renwick would have seen ten thousand signatures. He would know the difference between ink laid down by its owner and ink laid down by a stranger imitating him.

So Vesper did not try to write *Harrow* the way Harrow wrote it. She had never seen how Harrow wrote it. Instead she turned the problem over the way her grandfather — the smuggler, the ghost in the family tree — might have turned it. You don't counterfeit a coin, he would have said, in whatever smoke-filled room such men conducted their lives in. You find a coin already worn smooth and you help it along.

Lieutenant Harrow had signed the ward admission book eleven times during his final week, fever climbing, handwriting deteriorating from crisp officer's script to something loose and slanting, the letters leaning like tired soldiers. The last three signatures were barely legible. And here was the fact Vesper had built everything on: the transit papers would be dated after those signatures. A dying man's hand, days from collapse. Whatever appeared on those papers would be read against that standard.

She practiced on scrap paper first, forty times, fifty, matching the slant, the way the H opened too wide, the way the r's collapsed into the w like men linking arms. Then she ruined the practice sheets — tore them small, wetted them, pushed the pulp down the drain — and began on the real forms.

*Received for transport, one Lt. J. Harrow, convalescent detachment, Marrow's Reach.* Her pen moved in Harrow's failing hand. The ink dried brownish in the cold. She blew on it gently, the way a real clerk might, and found she had stopped feeling like a forger. That frightened her more than anything else that night. Not the risk — she had lived with risk so long it had become furniture — but the ease. How naturally the lie fit her hand. How little resistance there was in her.

*Who am I, when I do this?* she asked the lamp. The lamp said nothing. Lamps are honest that way.

* * *

She broke at three o'clock and made tea on the spirit stove, holding the cup in both hands and watching steam climb toward the ceiling beams. Below her, the hospital slept. Somewhere down the corridor Odile Ferrant was awake on nights, moving through the wards on soft feet, keeping watch without having been asked to keep it. Two streets away, in a cell with a barred window, Elias Thorne was either sleeping or staring at the ceiling, and Vesper hoped — she found she genuinely hoped — that he was sleeping. One of them should.

Seven years ago she had made him a promise on an afternoon so mild it felt borrowed from another century. They had been shelling peas on the step behind the kitchen, back when the war was still a word people said carefully, and Elias had told her, laughing, that if England ever decided he was the enemy, he supposed he'd simply have to trust someone kind to look the other way. And Vesper — twenty-four years old, full of a certainty she hadn't yet earned — had said, *Then it will be me. If they ever call you enemy, I'll get you home.*

She had said it the way you promise to write. The way you promise to visit. She had not known she was signing something.

Now the bill had come due, and she sat in a cold room at three in the morning altering a dead man's coat to pay it, and the strange thing — the thing she kept circling like a tongue finding a broken tooth — was that she did not regret the promise. She regretted only that she'd made it lightly. Some promises deserve to be made in fear. Hers had been made in sunshine, and sunshine, it turned out, was not collateral. Winter had come to collect.

* * *

At four she tried the finished uniform on herself.

Not to wear it — to test it. She stood before the narrow washstand mirror with the tunic buttoned wrong-side-out and watched her own face above a dead man's collar, and the effect was so strange she laughed once, a short ugly sound with no humor in it. The coat made her straighter. That was the trick of uniforms, she realized; she had watched it happen to boys in France, watched cloth turn shop assistants into officers overnight. The costume didn't hide the person. It *replaced* them, temporarily, publicly, and everyone agreed to believe it because believing was easier than checking.

Which raised the question she had been avoiding all night: if a coat could make a German-born orderly into Lieutenant Harrow for eleven minutes, what had her own name ever made of her? *Vesper Dunmore* — respectable daughter of a respectable house, built on contraband and salt water and other people's blindness. She had spent seven years being invisible on purpose, keeping a ledger no one could see, loving no one openly, promising things in peacetime and paying for them in war. The uniform in the mirror was only doing openly what she had done in secret her whole adult life: wearing a name that wasn't quite the truth and making it fit.

Maybe that was all identity ever was. A coat you kept brushed. A signature practiced until it looked lived-in. And underneath — underneath, the same arms, the same stubborn heart, the same hands that had sewn the seams and signed the papers and would carry the consequences if it all came apart.

She took off the coat and folded it, Harrow's ghost dismissed, and felt oddly grateful to him. Whoever he had been — the man with the bakery photograph and the dry tobacco — his spare uniform was going to buy a life tonight. Of all the uses a soldier's kit could be put to, she thought he might have approved this one. He had kept it in tissue paper, after all. He had meant it for saving.

* * *

Dawn was still an hour off when she packed everything away: the uniform wrapped in her spare blanket, the papers in the false bottom of her sewing basket, the scraps burned in the stove and the ash stirred into the cinders. The trunk went back to the linen store on her shoulder, heavy as a coffin, and she walked it down the dark stairs past the sleeping wards, past the floorboard under which forty-one names lay flat and patient, and set it exactly where it had stood three weeks running. No one would ever know it had moved. That was the whole art of her life, she thought bitterly. Moving things so no one would ever know.

Outside, snow had begun again — fine, dry, indifferent snow, sifting down through the gaslight onto the empty street. Two days. The Meridian sailed the morning after next, and tomorrow night the plan would move or die. She stood on the steps a moment, letting the cold find her face, and rehearsed the last piece: the escort order, which she had yet to write, and the timing, which depended on Odile, and the tide, which depended on nothing and no one.

Then she saw the figure at the gate.

A man in a major's greatcoat, standing very still in the falling snow, watching the hospital the way accountants watch numbers — patiently, without malice, certain they will eventually balance. Major Aldous Renwick tipped his cap to her, courteous as a knife laid on clean linen.

"Nurse Dunmore," he called through the dark. "You're up early."

"So are you, Major."

"I don't sleep well when sums won't add." He smiled, snow gathering on his shoulders. "I wonder — might I ask you to accompany me to the customs house at dawn, day after tomorrow? There's a matter there I'd value your professional eye on."

Day after tomorrow. Dawn. The hour of the tide.

"Of course, Major," Vesper heard herself say, in a voice as calm and lived-in as any forged signature. "I'd be glad to."
