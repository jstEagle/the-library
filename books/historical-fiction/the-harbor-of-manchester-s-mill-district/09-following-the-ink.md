## 9. Following the Ink

**Theo**

There is a particular kind of silence a clock makes when it is lying to you. Most people think clocks are honest things—gears and springs, no capacity for deceit—but I have spent my life among them, and I can tell you they lie constantly. They lose seconds. They gain minutes. They tick along with perfect confidence while being entirely wrong about the hour.

I had been thinking about this on the walk to the docks because I was trying not to think about anything else. Specifically, I was trying not to think about the letter folded against my chest, inside my waistcoat, where it had lived for three days now like a coal that wouldn't cool.

Ada Quill walked beside me with the brisk economy of a woman who considered walking itself a mild inefficiency. She had suggested we meet at the corner of Chapel Street at eight o'clock, and she had arrived at seven minutes to, which told me something about her. People who arrive early are either anxious or ambitious. With Ada, I suspected both, in roughly equal measure.

"The stationer's name is Hobbs," she said, without preamble. "Gideon Hobbs, paper and ink, Wharf Lane. Your father's letters were written on laid paper, cream stock, and the ink is iron gall with a violet cast—which means an additive, which means not the cheap stuff every man buys from the corner shop. There are perhaps four stationers in Manchester who stock it."

"Four?"

"I asked three of them already. You're welcome."

"When did you ask them?"

"Yesterday. While you were being followed through the canal district and nearly murdered, some of us were doing useful work." She said it without heat, but the words landed anyway. She had heard about the fog, the man, the page I'd torn from his notebook. Everyone in Ancoats heard everything eventually; it was only the important things that stayed secret.

"Hobbs is the fourth," I said.

"He's the interesting one. The other three sell to anyone with a penny. Hobbs keeps a ledger of his regular customers. Old-fashioned. Careful." She glanced at me sideways. "Careful men make careful mistakes."

The morning was grey in the way Manchester does grey—not weather so much as a permanent condition, the sky a ceiling of unwashed wool. Gulls wheeled over the warehouse roofs, screaming their grievances at nothing. The smell of the docks came up to meet us: tar, rope, river mud, and beneath it all the sweetish rot of spilled grain that no amount of rain ever quite washed away.

"You've done this before," I said. "Following paper."

"I'm a shipping clerk. Paper is the whole world. Every bale of cotton that comes up the Mersey and Irwell exists twice—once as cargo and once as a document, and the document is the one that matters. Men have hanged over documents." She said this cheerfully. "Men have also escaped hanging over them. It depends who holds the pen."

"And you want to hold the pen."

"I want to know who held it," she corrected. "For eleven names that should be on a memorial wall and aren't."

I had no answer for that. Her brother was one of those names—the eleventh, or near enough—and every time she spoke of him it was with the flat precision of someone handling glass. I understood. It was how I'd spoken of my father for ten years: *drowned, 1839, body never recovered*, delivered like a line from a ledger, because anything warmer would have drowned me too.

Hobbs's shop sat where Wharf Lane bent toward the water, a narrow frontage wedged between a chandler's and a public house that hadn't yet opened its doors but was already breathing beer fumes into the street. The window displayed reams of paper stacked like masonry, ink bottles glinting green and blue-black, quills bundled upright like a small arrested flock.

Inside, the air smelled of gum arabic and dust. A bell above the door announced us, and a man emerged from the back room wiping his hands on an apron—Hobbs himself, sixty-odd, spectacles thick as portholes, with the squint of a lifetime spent reading fine print by bad light.

"Mr. Hobbs," Ada began, and produced a story so smooth I nearly applauded. She was acting for a firm of solicitors, she said, tracing correspondence in a matter of disputed inheritance—a tedious business, she apologized, involving a deceased gentleman's quarterly payments to persons unknown. She needed to know whether he stocked a particular ink, and if so, who bought it.

Hobbs listened with the wariness of a man who had been visited by solicitors before and found them only marginally more trustworthy than bailiffs.

"Violet-cast iron gall," he repeated. "That'll be Renshaw's Violet Black. I'm the only stockist south of Deansgate." He said it with a craftsman's pride, then seemed to hear what he'd admitted and closed his face like a drawer.

Ada set a coin on the counter. Not a bribe—she was too clever for that—but payment, ostentatiously, for two bottles of the ink itself, which neither of us wanted. The transaction gave the conversation a legitimate spine. Solicitors buy things. That's most of what they do.

"A quarterly account," she pressed, lightly, while Hobbs wrapped the bottles. "Paid in advance, likely. Such accounts must be rare."

"They're not common," Hobbs allowed.

"This one would run back years. Ten, perhaps."

Something moved behind the spectacles. Not recognition exactly. Arithmetic. I watched him decide, and I watched him decide wrong—or rather, I watched him decide that whatever trouble might come of talking was smaller than the trouble that might come of refusing a solicitor's clerk who knew too much already.

"There was such an account," he said slowly. "Quarterly. Standing order, paid at the start of each quarter, ten years running. The buyer collects the letters ready-made—paper, ink, wax, the lot. Never writes here. Collects and goes."

"Ten years," Ada said. "Since when?"

"Spring of '39." Hobbs frowned at his own memory. "Odd thing, that. First quarter after the mill fire, it started. I remember because everyone in the district was talking of nothing else, and here was this quiet fellow setting up a standing order like the world hadn't turned upside down."

My hands, I am pleased to report, did not shake. They wanted to. Inside my waistcoat, the letter seemed to press its weight against my ribs, and I thought: *ten years. Quarterly. While I mourned you, Father, someone has been buying your paper four times a year, within a mile of our shop.*

"Who collects it?" I asked. My voice came out steadier than I deserved.

Hobbs looked at me properly for the first time. "Now that, young man, is where even a solicitor's clerk finds her road ends. I don't give out names. A stationer who gives out names is a stationer whose customers go elsewhere, and some of my customers would take it unkindly."

"Would they take it unkindly," Ada said, "or would they take it to Mr. Crane?"

The name dropped into the shop like a stone into a well. Hobbs went very still. Then he took off his spectacles, polished them on his apron with great thoroughness, and put them back on, and in that small ceremony I watched a man weigh ten years of discretion against something heavier.

"I don't know anything about Mr. Crane," he said. "But I'll tell you what I know for free, since you've bought your ink. The collector isn't the payer. The money comes one way, the collection another. The payer—" he hesitated, and his eyes flicked toward the window, toward the east, toward Ancoats, though he didn't seem to know he'd done it—"the payer sends a boy with the coin each quarter. Boy from a respectable house. And the collector—" 

He stopped.

"Yes?" Ada said.

"The collector is a woman," Hobbs said. "Comes at dusk, always dusk, always alone. Grey coat. Youngish. Keeps her face down. And last quarter—" he leaned across the counter, and his voice dropped to almost nothing— "last quarter she wasn't alone. There was a man waiting across the lane. Big fellow, broken nose, stood there an hour smoking pipe after pipe. When she came out he fell in behind her. I don't know who either of them serve. But I know watching when I see it, and that was watching."

* * *

**Ada**

I had been in Hobbs's shop perhaps twenty minutes, and in that time I had learned three things, arranged in ascending order of importance: the ink was Renshaw's Violet Black; the account ran ten years; and someone local had been collecting Nathaniel Sterling's letters since before his son could legally sign his own indentures.

The fourth thing I learned on the doorstep, when Theo grabbed my sleeve hard enough to leave a mark.

"Not here," he said.

We walked. Or rather, he walked and I permitted myself to be walked, which is a distinction I maintain even now. He headed away from the main streets, into the tangle of lanes behind the warehouses where the cotton waste drifted like dirty snow and every second doorway smelled of dye. When we were sufficiently lost, he stopped and turned to me, and his face had gone the colour of tallow.

"A woman," he said. "Grey coat. Dusk."

"You know her."

"I don't know anyone who wears a grey coat."

"That's a very specific denial for a man who knows no one in a grey coat."

He passed a hand over his face. "Mrs. Calloway. She keeps house for us. For my uncle. She's been with Edmund ten years—came the spring after—" He stopped. I watched him arrive at the arithmetic, the same arithmetic old Hobbs had done behind his spectacles. Spring of '39. The quarter after the fire. The quarter after a man named Sterling drowned, or didn't.

"Your housekeeper has been collecting your father's letters for a decade," I said, because someone had to say it plainly and Theo was clearly in no state.

"She brings the post in. She's always brought the post in." His voice had gone strange, thin, like wire pulled too tight. "Ten years of letters coming to my uncle's house, and I never—I never saw one. Not one. Do you understand what that means? Either she destroyed them, or she gave them to Edmund, or—"

"Or she kept them herself," I said. "A woman doesn't stand in a stationer's at dusk every quarter for ten years out of loyalty to an employer. Loyalty pays monthly. This smells like wages of another kind."

"You think she's bought."

"I think everyone's bought, Mr. Sterling. The only question is the price and the seller." I said it more harshly than I meant to, and saw him flinch, and did not take it back. Some part of me—the part that had spent two years reading manifests and finding my brother's name absent from every one of them—had stopped apologizing for harshness somewhere around the second year. "Your father wrote these letters to someone. Letters are written *to* people. If Mrs. Calloway collected them, then either she was the intended reader, or she was standing in for whoever was. And given that your uncle apparently booked a berth under a false name matching instructions in a letter he claims never to have seen—"

"Don't." He said it quietly, but with an edge that surprised me. "You don't know what Edmund— you don't know him. He raised me. He buried my mother. He—" He stopped again, jaw working. "There has to be another explanation."

"There usually is," I said. "It's usually worse."

* * *

**Theo**

We parted at the corner of Great Ancoats Street, Ada to the shipping office, I to the shop, and I walked the long way because I could not bear the short way. The long way went past the observatory, and I told myself I was going that direction for reasons of prudence—to look, from across the road, at the place where the first letter had been wedged into the escapement like a seed in a crack of pavement.

The observatory clock had run true for six days now. I checked it from the far side of the street, the way a man checks a wound. True. Which meant whoever had sabotaged it had done so once, deliberately, as a means of summoning me specifically—and had not returned to do it again. Which meant the summons was finished with. Which meant the next move belonged to someone else.

I stood there perhaps five minutes, feeling the city flow around me—carters, mill girls, a pie-man crying his wares—and let myself think the thought I had been refusing since Hobbs's shop.

*Someone local has been receiving them.*

Not receiving and ignoring. Receiving and keeping. Ten years, forty letters, give or take. My father, alive somewhere, writing home four times a year like a tide, and every letter landing in a house I lived in, passing through the hands of a woman who poured my tea, and vanishing.

Unless they hadn't vanished.

I went home the short way after all.

The shop was empty when I let myself in—Edmund was at a customer's in Cheetham Hill, a fitting for a regulator clock, gone since noon. The house above was quiet in the late-afternoon way, lamplight not yet lit, the stairs familiar under my boots. Mrs. Calloway's room was at the back, small, neat, a widow's room with a widow's objects: a photograph of a dead husband in uniform, a Bible, a geranium that survived on neglect.

I did not enter it. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger heaven keeps: I stood at her threshold with my hand on the latch, and I did not enter, because searching a loyal woman's room felt like a sin I couldn't yet afford. Instead I did something worse, or at least more cowardly. I waited until she came up with the evening tea, and I watched her face.

She carried the tray the way she always did, steady, unhurried. She set it down. She looked at me—at my face, which I knew wore the question like a bruise—and for one moment, one held breath, something crossed her features that I had never seen there in ten years.

Fear. Not surprise. Fear, and beneath it, terribly, pity.

"Your tea, Mr. Theo," she said. "You're looking peaky. Eat something."

"Mrs. Calloway." My voice sounded like someone else's. "How long have you worked for my uncle?"

"Ten years this spring." She smoothed her apron. Her hands were perfectly steady. Only her eyes weren't. "Why do you ask, love?"

Ten years this spring. Spring of '39. The quarter after the fire. The quarter after the drowning that wasn't.

"No reason," I said, and drank my tea, and tasted nothing at all.

She left the tray and went down the stairs, and I sat in the gathering dark listening to her footsteps cross the kitchen below, and I understood that the conspiracy Hobbs had described—the payer, the boy, the collector, the watcher—was not a conspiracy located somewhere out in the smoke of the docks.

It was located downstairs. It was located at my own table. It had fed me supper for ten years.

And the worst of it—the thought that kept me sitting in the dark long after the tea went cold—was not that Mrs. Calloway had lied to me.

It was that when she looked at me with pity, I had known, instantly and certainly, that the pity was real. Whatever she was, whatever she'd been paid to carry or conceal, she was sorry for me. And a woman is only sorry for a boy whose father writes him letters she burns—if the letters say something worth burning.

Down in the kitchen, a match flared. Through the floorboards I heard the scratch of a pen.

Then, quieter still: the sound of the back door opening, and closing, and the click of a key turning in the lock—from the outside.
