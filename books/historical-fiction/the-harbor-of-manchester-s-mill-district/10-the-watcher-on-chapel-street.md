## 10. The Watcher on Chapel Street

There is a particular way a man walks when he is not following you, and I had learned it the way most apprentices learn anything—by watching my betters do it badly.

The trick of it is in the shoulders. A man going about his honest business walks with his shoulders aimed where he's going. A man following someone walks with his shoulders aimed at *you*, no matter which way his feet point. His feet will dawdle past a window of cheap tinware. His feet will develop a sudden scholarly interest in a handbill for Dr. Marlowe's Celebrated Worm Lozenges. But his shoulders never lie. Shoulders are the one part of a man that hasn't been taught manners.

I noticed him first on Chapel Street, three shops down from Edmund's, pretending to read the notice board outside the corn factor's while wearing the expression of a man who could not read. He'd been there when I left at half eight, and he was there still when I came back from delivering a repaired carriage clock to the solicitor on Princess Street, and in between—or so Ada would later insist, with the grim satisfaction of a woman being proven right—he had also been outside the shipping office, because she'd seen him there too and had assumed, charitably, that he was waiting for her.

"He has," she told me that evening, "the look of a man being paid by the hour."

"Most men in this city have that look."

"Yes, but most of them are paid to *work*. This one is paid to stand. There's a difference, and the difference is called Crane."

I should explain how I knew the name meant danger now, when ten days earlier it had been nothing but a name on a letter written in a dead man's hand. It meant danger because I had spent those ten days doing what I do best, which is taking things apart to see what makes them move. The letter had come apart beautifully. Its paper traced to a stationer near the docks—a stationer who, when Ada smiled her clerk's smile at him, remembered selling a dozen such sheets quarterly for years, always collected by a different hand, always paid in coin. Someone local received them. Someone local kept them quiet. And the only person with both the means and the motive to arrange a quarterly silence was the same man whose name appeared in my father's letter beside the words *eleven dead and buried twice over*.

Josiah Crane did not get rich burying eleven men without learning to watch the survivors.

So: the man on Chapel Street. Big through the chest, small through the patience, with a bowler hat worn at the angle of a man who owns exactly one hat. He had a notebook—I saw it when he turned to spit—and I confess I thought about it the way a clockmaker thinks about an escapement: as a mechanism containing something that wanted releasing.

I did not go home. That was the first sensible thing I did all week. Instead I walked toward Ancoats, because if a man means to follow you, you may as well learn what he does when you turn down streets he doesn't expect.

* * *

The fog came off the canal that evening the way guilt comes off a man in church—slowly, thoroughly, and settling into everything. By six o'clock the gas lamps were burning holes in it rather than lighting through it, and the district had become a series of sounds: the slap of water against lock gates, the groan of some mill still working its night shift, a woman calling a child home in a voice that expected to be obeyed by the third repetition.

I used the fog. Any boy raised in Ancoats knows the canal paths the way he knows his own pulse—the cut behind the dye works where the towpath narrows, the steps down to the coal wharf, the footbridge that sways if you trust it too much. I moved from lamp to lamp like a man crossing a river on stones, and each time I looked back, the bowler hat was there, forty yards back, patient as interest accruing.

He was good. I'll grant him that. He didn't hurry when I hurried or slow when I slowed; he simply persisted, the way damp persists. But persistence is a quality suited to standing outside shops, and the canal paths after dark require a different education. When I dropped down the wharf steps and cut along the waterline beneath the warehouses, he lost me for four full minutes, and I heard him above me on the towpath, moving fast now, his patience finally cracking into hurry.

Which is when I made my mistake. Or rather, when I made the mistake I'd been making all along, which was believing I was the hunter because I had decided to be.

I came up the far steps intending to circle behind him—to see his face, to learn who sent him, perhaps even to relieve him of that notebook, though I hadn't the faintest plan for how. And I stepped out onto the towpath directly into the reach of his arm.

He was quicker than he looked. They always are. His hand closed on my collar before I'd finished turning, and we went around together, two fools waltzing on wet flagstones, and then his fist arrived at the side of my head and the canal, the lamps, and the whole foggy city performed a single revolution and settled somewhere slightly to the left of where they'd been.

"You're the Sterling lad," he said. Not a question. He said it the way a man reads a label.

"And you're Crane's," I managed, tasting copper.

"I'm nobody's." He hit me again, more out of professional thoroughness than anger, and I went down onto one knee on the stones. "I'm asking questions and you're answering them. What did you find in the observatory clock?"

The world steadied enough for me to appreciate the situation. He knew about the clock. Which meant Crane knew about the clock, which meant the sabotage had been discovered and reported, which meant every hour since I pocketed that letter had been an hour borrowed against interest. Somewhere behind my ringing ears, the clockmaker in me observed that a mechanism once discovered runs down very quickly indeed.

"Springs," I said. "Gudgeons. The usual rot."

"The usual rot doesn't bring the master's own son up from a shop in—" he paused, consulting memory, "—Chapel Street."

"It brings anyone the observatory summons. I'm the cheapest hands they know."

He considered this. In the lamplight leaking through the fog his face was a study in the economics of violence: a man calculating whether beating me further would pay. Then footsteps sounded on the towpath behind him—two men, maybe three, walking with the unhurried confidence of people whose business keeps regular hours—and something changed in his arithmetic.

"Another time, then," he said, and reached inside his coat.

For the notebook. I want to be clear that I understood that in the moment, because afterward Ada refused to believe I wasn't a hero, and I'd like the record to show I was simply a frightened man grabbing at the one object within reach that mattered more than my ribs. He meant to put the notebook away. I caught his wrist. We went down again, both of us, onto the wet stones, and the notebook skidded free across the flags, and I did the single most athletic thing I have done in my adult life, which was to lunge after it on hands and knees like a beggar after a dropped coin.

My fingers closed on it. His boot closed on my shoulder. And then the footsteps arrived—actual footsteps, belonging to actual men, two stokers off the night shift by the look of their boots and their language—and the follower made the calculation that professionals make, which is that witnesses are expensive, and melted backward into the fog with a last look at me that promised, quite courteously, to reschedule.

* * *

I sat on the towpath with my back against a bollard and opened the notebook with shaking hands.

It was not, as I had imagined, a book of his own keeping. It was a ledger of another kind entirely—a list, page after page, in a clerk's careful hand, of names. Names with dates. Names with sums beside them. And beside certain names, a word, written small and final as a stamp:

*Settled.*

I turned the pages by lamplight with the fog pressing close, and I read names I didn't know, and then names I did—names from my father's letter, the witnesses, the men who had asked questions in 1839 and stopped asking them permanently. Each with a date. Each with a sum. Each marked *settled*, in the sense that a debt is settled, or an account closed, or—in the case of at least six entries—a life.

And near the bottom of the second-to-last page, in the same clerk's hand, dated the autumn of 1839:

*Sterling, T. — drowned, Bridgewater Canal — settled.*

My father. Reduced to one line. Drowned, the ledger agreed, just as everyone had always agreed, just as the parish record agreed and the coroner's report agreed and Uncle Edmund's grief had agreed for ten years. Settled. Except that my father had written me a letter last month in ink bought quarterly from a stationer by the docks, which made him either a ghost with excellent penmanship or the most elaborate fraud in Lancashire, and either way the word *settled* sat on that page like a lie wearing mourning clothes.

But it was the entry below it that stopped my breath.

Because the list continued past 1839. There were newer names. Recent ones. And the last entry, dated not ten years ago but nine days ago, in fresh ink, read:

*Quill, A. — shipping clerk — under observation.*

Ada. They were watching Ada. Which meant they knew about Ada, which meant they knew about me, which meant the two of us had been circling each other for a fortnight inside a ring that had already closed.

I tore the page free—my father's line and hers, evidence and warning in one—and folded it into my waistcoat, over my heart, where a man keeps the things he cannot afford to lose. The rest of the notebook I weighed in my hand for a long moment. Names. Dates. Sums. Enough, perhaps, to hang a man, if a magistrate could be found brave enough to read it aloud.

Then I heard the fog give back the sound of boots, unhurried, coming along the towpath, and I understood that the stokers' departure had not ended the evening's business but merely adjourned it.

I ran. I am not proud of it. I ran along the canal with my ribs screaming and the torn page crackling against my chest, and I did not stop until the lights of Chapel Street appeared, and even then I did not go to the shop. I stood in the doorway of the shuttered corn factor's across the road, bleeding quietly into my collar, and watched the windows of my uncle's house.

One candle burned in the front room. My uncle's silhouette crossed it once, twice, pacing. Waiting up. For me? For news?

Or—for a visitor?

I touched the folded page over my heart and made myself a promise there in the cold, with blood drying stiff on my ear and the fog swallowing Chapel Street whole: whatever else happened, whoever else the ledger named, I would not let Ada Quill's name end up in it marked *settled*.

Behind me, out of the dark, a voice said: "You look terrible, Mr. Sterling. We need to talk."

Ada. Of course it was Ada—stepping out of the shadow of the corn factor's wall with her arms folded and her eyes on the candlelit window, as if she had known exactly where I would run, and why, and what I would find when I got there.

"How long," I said, "have you been standing there?"

"Long enough," she said, "to know that whatever's in your waistcoat, we're both of us in it now."
