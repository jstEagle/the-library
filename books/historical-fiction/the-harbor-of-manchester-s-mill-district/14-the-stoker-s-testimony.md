## 14. The Stoker's Testimony

There is a particular kind of courage required to walk back into a place where you have already been threatened, and I will tell you honestly that I did not have it. What I had instead was a pocket watch that needed collecting, a lie about the watch, and the observation—arrived at somewhere around three in the morning, staring at my ceiling—that if I let fear choose my errands for me, I might as well hand Edmund the keys to the shop and take up residence in a cupboard.

So on Thursday morning I told my uncle I had a repair to make in Collyhurst, which was even true, in the sense that a man can be true in the way a stopped clock is true: twice a day, and useless otherwise.

The stoker's name was Amos Brierley, though everyone at the ruined mill had called him Old Ash, on account of the colour he'd turned after thirty years of feeding furnaces. He lived above a chandler's off Store Street, in two rooms that smelled of tallow and coal smoke and, faintly, of the sea—which struck me as a strange thing to find so far inland, until I understood it was probably the tar from downstairs, and felt briefly foolish for having romanticised it.

He opened the door before I knocked the second time.

"You're the clockmaker's lad," he said. "The one who came asking after the Ancoats boiler."

"I'm the one who came asking."

"And now you've come back." He looked me over with the unhurried appraisal of a man who had spent his working life deciding whether machinery was about to kill him. "That means either you didn't believe me, or you believed me too well."

"Can I come in?"

"You can come in. Whether you'll want to stay is another matter. Sit down, mind the chair by the window, its leg's gone philosophical."

I sat on the philosophical chair anyway. The room was spare in the way of rooms belonging to men who expected to leave them: a bed, a stove, a table scarred with rings from forty years of mugs, and on the wall a single framed print of a ship, which explained the smell better than the tar did. He'd been to sea once, or wanted to. Men who live beside water their whole lives either love it or hate it, and usually manage both.

"I've brought something," I said, and took the paper from inside my coat.

Ada had written it out fair, in her clerk's hand—straight lines, no flourishes, every word load-bearing. *Statement of Amos Brierley, stoker, respecting the explosion at Crane & Sons Mill, Ancoats, on the fourteenth day of March, 1839.* Below it, space for his mark or signature, and below that, a line for a witness. My name would go there. I had not asked Ada whether she intended that when she drafted it, but I knew her, and I knew it was deliberate. She wanted my name on the page as much as his. A witness makes a document harder to dismiss, and harder to burn.

Brierley took the paper and read it the way he'd have read a pressure gauge: slowly, twice, looking for the flaw that would blow the whole thing apart.

"It says what I said," he announced finally, surprised, as though he'd expected Ada to improve upon the truth.

"That was the intention."

"Huh." He set it down on the table, flat, and put his mug on top of it, which I chose to read as a form of endorsement. "You know what happened to the last man who put Josiah Crane's name on a piece of paper?"

"He drowned," I said. "Officially."

Brierley looked at me for a long moment. Up close, his eyes were a pale washed-out blue, like sky seen through smoke, and there was something in them that hadn't been there when we'd met among the mill ruins—a kind of tired decision.

"Aye," he said. "Officially."

* * *

I want to record, because it matters, exactly how it happened—not the way I'd imagined it on the walk over, all grim resolve and ringing declarations, but the shabby human way it actually went.

He made tea. We drank it. He talked about his knees. Then, apropos of nothing—or rather, apropos of everything—he said, "Eleven men. You know that? Eleven. And they buried six of them without inquest papers, just a line in the parish book saying *accidental death by misadventure*, as if the boiler had tripped over its own feet."

"I know."

"My brother-in-law were one of them. Daniel Hargreaves. Worked the firing floor with me eight years." He turned the mug in his hands. "After it happened, Crane's man comes round—not Crane himself, he'd never dirty his cuffs—comes round to every family with an envelope. Compensation, he calls it. Ten pounds for a husband, five for a son. And a paper to sign saying you'd received it in full settlement and held no grievance. And folk signed, because ten pounds is ten pounds and grief don't feed children." His jaw worked. "My sister signed. I never blamed her. But I kept mine. Never signed it. Kept the envelope unopened on my mantelpiece for two years till the guilt of taking it got worse than the guilt of refusing, and I gave the money to the widow next door and burned the paper."

"Why are you telling me this now?" I asked. "Last week you wouldn't give me your name."

"Last week you were a lad poking about in ruins. Curiosity's cheap." He nodded at the statement under the mug. "This is a different animal. This has your name on it too, and a shipping clerk's, and ink, and a date. This is a thing that could hang a man." He paused. "Or get three more people killed. I've been doing sums since you left, lad, and I don't like either answer, but I'm seventy-one years old and my knees are shot and I'm damned if I'll die owing eleven men a debt I could've paid with one signature."

He picked up the pen I'd brought—Ada's instruction: bring your own pen, never borrow theirs—and held it over the paper for a long moment.

Then he wrote his name. Not a mark. A full, careful, schoolroom name, *Amos Brierley*, each letter formed like he was setting type, and beneath it the date, and beneath that he said, "Read it out loud, will you? All of it. I want to hear how it sounds in the air."

So I did. I stood in his tallow-smelling room and read aloud the account of the fourteenth of March, 1839: the gauge that had been reading wrong for a fortnight, the report Brierley himself had carried to the mill manager and been laughed at for, the second report that vanished, the explosion at twenty past four in the afternoon, and afterward—the part that mattered—the men who came with carts before the coroner was summoned, and what was loaded onto them besides rubble, and the name of the man who directed it all on behalf of the owner: Josiah Crane.

When I finished, the room was very quiet. Somewhere below us the chandler was wrapping rope, the sound rhythmic and ordinary, the world carrying on as it does.

"There," Brierley said. "Thirty years I've been carrying that. Lighter than I thought, telling it." He almost smiled. "Now get it out of my house before I think better of it."

I folded the statement along Ada's creases and put it inside my shirt, against my chest, where I could feel it like a second heartbeat.

"Where will you keep yours?" he asked, meaning the copy—I'd written one out in my own hand the night before, because Ada trusted no single document to survive, and by now neither did I.

"Somewhere safe," I said. "A place nobody would think to look."

"Observatory?"

I must have started, because he chuckled, dry as furnace ash. "Lad, you've the face of a church congregation. Everyone can read you." He saw me to the door, and there, in the doorway, with the grey light of Store Street behind him, he gripped my arm with a strength that belied the knees. "One thing. If anything happens to me—if I fall down some stairs, or drink myself unlucky, or catch a sudden case of being dead—you go to the harbormaster directly. Not the constables. The harbormaster. There's one or two of them still honest, and it's the only office Crane hasn't bought entire."

"Nothing will happen to you," I said, and heard how young and stupid it sounded.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "Probably not. Good day to you, Mr. Sterling."

* * *

I spent the afternoon in a state of unbearable lightness. That is the truth of it, and I record it to my shame: I walked back through Ancoats feeling *good*. The sun came through the mill chimneys in slanted columns and turned the canal water to hammered pewter, and I thought, *it's done, it's begun, the wheel is turning and nothing can stop it now.* I had a signed statement naming Josiah Crane pressed against my ribs. I had a copy hidden in the observatory, wedged into the great clock's case behind the weight channel, in the cache my father had described in his letter—a hiding place so obvious once you knew it, and invisible while you didn't. I had allies. I had, God help me, a *plan*.

I even whistled, briefly, outside the shop, before remembering that whistling in our household was reserved for funerals and Edmund's rare good moods, and neither applied.

Edmund was in the back room doing the books when I came in. He looked up, and for a moment—one moment, no longer—his eyes went to my chest, to the place where the paper lay, as though the statement were visible through wool and linen like a brand.

"Good repair?" he asked.

"Long job," I said. "Weeks yet."

"Mm." He returned to his ledger. The nib scratched. Outside, a cart went by, and a woman called a child home, and Manchester did what Manchester does, which is continue.

I should say something here about my uncle, since this chapter of my life seems determined to be about him whether I will it or not. Edmund raised me after the drowning. He taught me escapements and patience and the value of saying less than you know. He is not a monster; I want that understood, whatever else gets written down about him in the end. He is a man who made one bargain in the dark and then spent ten years building a house around it, room by room, until the bargain was the house and the house was our family, and pulling out one brick meant the whole structure coming down on both our heads. When he glanced at my chest that afternoon, what I saw in his face was not menace. It was arithmetic. He was calculating, as he always calculated, how much silence would cost, and whether he could still afford it.

I did not know then that the price had already been paid elsewhere, and in a currency I couldn't replace.

* * *

Ada found me at the shop near closing, which she never did—she had been careful, always, to meet me away from Edmund's territory, at the observatory, at the stationer's, at the corner by the canal bridge where the gaslight was bad and conversations could pretend to be chance. Her breaking that rule told me everything before she opened her mouth.

Her face did that thing faces do when someone is holding themselves together by craft alone: very pale, very controlled, the eyes doing all the weather.

"Theo." She got my name out and then had to start again. "Theo, it's Brierley."

The bottom dropped out of my stomach with a lurch I felt physically, like a lift cable snapping.

"What."

"His landlady sent a boy round to the shipping office an hour ago. He's at the infirmary. They found him at the foot of his stairs—" She stopped. Corrected herself, in the voice of a clerk amending an entry. "They *say* he fell."

I was already reaching for my coat.

The infirmary on Piccadilly was candlelit chaos, the usual population of the city's misfortunes packed into wards built for half of them, and we were directed up and along to a small side room where they put men the surgeons had given up organising. Amos Brierley lay in a bed too short for him, one eye swollen shut and the other open, tracking me across the room with terrible steadiness. His hands, those furnace-fed hands, lay on the blanket looking oddly formal, like tools laid out for inspection.

"Lad," he said. His voice was a ruin. "You'll want to hear it from me and not the gossip."

"Don't talk. I'll—"

"Sit down and listen." It was the mill-floor voice, the one that had shouted over furnaces for thirty years, and I sat.

He told it in fragments, sparing himself nothing because there was nothing left to spare. Two men had come at dusk. He'd known what they were before they reached the landing—knew it from the way they climbed, heavy and unhurried, men being paid by the hour rather than the job. They'd asked him, politely, whether he'd been visited by a young gentleman with papers. He'd told them, politely, where to put their question. After that the details lost their order: boots, the banister, the wall, the floor. While he was insensible they went through his rooms. Every drawer, the mattress, the loose brick behind the stove, the lining of his coat.

"They took the envelope," he said. "The old one. From '39. Crane's compensation money—I'd kept it, all these years, couldn't bring myself to spend blood-money nor throw it away neither. Foolish sentiment. They'll have found the date on it. Now they know I was there before, and they know what I am." He worked his mouth. "Did they ask you—did anyone follow you today?"

"No," I said. "No one."

"Your copy's safe?"

"Safe," I said, and thought of the great clock ticking over the city, patient as a heart, holding its secret in its ribs.

"Then it weren't a total loss." He tried to smile and it cost him visibly. "They wanted the statement. Didn't have it. Wanted to know who I'd signed for. I told them I'd signed for King George, and he's been dead long enough not to mind." A breath that was nearly a laugh. "But listen, lad. Listen. The tall one, the one did the talking—he says, says he, *the old man's lucky it's only a warning. Next time it's the river.* River, Theo. Not stairs. They're done being gentle."

Ada had gone very white. "We should move him," she said. "Tonight. There are ships' lodgings, places where—"

"Where?" Brierley said mildly. "They know my face now, girl. And I'm not leaving this bed on my own legs for a month, if ever. No." He turned the good eye on me, and in it was none of the self-pity I'd have forgiven him for. Only that tired decision again, arrived at fully now. "You wanted testimony. You've got it. Question is what you do with it, and how fast, and whether you've got the stomach for what it costs. Because this—" he lifted one hand an inch off the blanket and let it fall— "this is the cheap part. This is what they do to an old man nobody counts. Wait till they work out what *you're* worth."

* * *

I walked Ada home as far as the bridge, because neither of us wanted to say goodbye and neither of us wanted to talk, and walking is how you do both at once. The fog was coming up off the canal in slow grey ropes. A barge went past with a single lantern, its wake slapping the stone, going somewhere.

"This is my fault," I said.

"Don't be stupid."

"I brought the statement to his house. I put my name on it. I—"

"Theo." She stopped and faced me, and the fog beaded in her hair and on her lashes, and she looked, for a moment, unbearably young and unbearably old at once. "I drafted that statement. I chose the witness line. I have been managing this like a manifest, entries and berths and cargo, and I forgot—" her voice caught, and she mastered it, furious at it— "I forgot that names aren't cargo. My brother was a name on a list once, and then he wasn't anywhere at all, erased so clean that I've spent nine years trying to prove he existed. I will not do that to Amos Brierley. Whatever happens next, he goes in the record. First name, last name, full entry. Even if the record is only us."

The fog closed around us. Somewhere across the water a dog barked twice and gave up.

"I used to think silence was protection," I said. "Our whole house runs on it. Say nothing, mend the clocks, keep the peace. Edmund taught me that. And I thought I was keeping people safe." I looked at the black water. "Ten years of quiet, and it bought us nothing. It just let the debt sit there gathering interest, and now the interest is being collected in other people's blood."

"So what do we do?"

What we did, in the end, was what we'd been circling for days: we went to the observatory. It was past midnight when we let ourselves in with the caretaker's grudging key, and the great clock filled the dark stairwell with its vast patient tick, and I climbed to the case with the fog still on my coat and knelt where my father had knelt, and drew out the copy of Brierley's statement from behind the weight channel.

It was safe. Of course it was safe. But I held it a long time anyway, feeling the clock's pulse through the wood, and Ada stood beside me with the lamp turned low, and neither of us spoke, because there was a new sound underneath the ticking—faint, far below, at the bottom of the tower.

Footsteps.
