## 22. A New Hand on the Escapement

The sign above the shop still read *E. STERLING & SON — CLOCKS AND WATCHES REPAIRED*, and for the first three weeks after the trial began, I couldn't bring myself to change it. Not out of sentiment, exactly. Sentiment implies a clean feeling, and what I felt about that sign was more like a mainspring wound too tight — you could hear it ticking in the back of your skull if you stood under it long enough. *Sterling & Son.* My father had painted those letters himself, before I was born, when he was still a man who fixed things instead of fleeing them. Edmund had never once corrected the grammar of it, though he had no son. He'd simply let the lie hang there over the door for thirty years, gilded and patient, waiting for me to grow into it.

I suppose that's the thing about lies. The good ones are built to be inherited.

In the end I took down the sign on a Tuesday morning in November, with a screwdriver and a ladder borrowed from Mrs. Hargreaves next door, who watched me from her window with the frank enjoyment of a woman who has finally been given something to talk about besides the weather. I carried the board inside and set it against the wall behind the counter, next to the frame I'd hung there a week earlier.

The letter.

My father's letter — the first one, the one I'd found wedged into the escapement of the observatory's master clock like a splinter in a heart — now sat behind glass in a plain black frame, hung at eye level where any customer waiting for their watch to be mended would have no choice but to read it. Ada had argued for keeping it private. "It's evidence," she'd said, in her shipping-clerk voice, the one that treated everything in the world as cargo to be properly documented. "It should go to the solicitors."

"It already went to the solicitors," I said. "They copied it. This is the original."

"The original belongs in an archive."

"The original belongs where I can see it." And then, because she was giving me that look — the one that said she found my reasoning sentimental but was prepared to allow it the way one allows a cat on the furniture — I gave her the true reason. "Ten years, Ada. Ten years this family kept the truth locked in a case and called it protection. I'm not doing it again. If the truth lives in my shop, it lives in the open, where I can wind it every morning."

She'd looked at the letter a long moment. Then she'd said, "You'll want better glass than that. The damp will get at the ink." Which was Ada Quill's way of agreeing, and also of taking over the project, and by Friday the letter sat behind glass so clear you could read the watermark.

Customers read it. That was the strange part. They came in with stopped watches and slow clocks and they waited, and while they waited their eyes went to the frame, and I watched them go pale, or go still, or go quietly furious, because half of Manchester had lost someone in those mills and nobody had ever said the names aloud. A woman came in last Thursday whose husband had worked the Ancoats boilers. She didn't say anything. She just touched the glass with two fingers, the way you'd touch a headstone, and left a farthing on the counter, and I understood it was not payment. It was an offering.

* * *

The trial of Josiah Crane opened on the fourth of November and ran through the whole of that grey month, and Manchester talked of nothing else. The depositions we'd pulled from the clock's case — sworn statements from 1839, eleven men's voices preserved in iron-gall ink — were read into the record one by one. Eleven men. The same number as the dead. I don't believe in symmetry as a rule; a clockmaker learns early that the universe is not elegant, only persistent. But even I felt the weight of that arithmetic when the clerk read the eleventh name.

Ada attended every session. She filled three notebooks with testimony in her small, ferocious hand, cross-referenced against the manifests, the wage ledgers, the burial records she'd spent two years assembling in secret — the record of her brother Daniel, who died at nineteen in a boiler room and appeared in no ledger anywhere except hers. When Crane's barrister suggested that Miss Quill was "an interested party of no official standing," she stood up in the gallery and said, clearly enough to be entered into the record anyway: "My brother is dead, sir. That is my standing." The gallery applauded until the judge threatened them all with contempt, and Ada sat back down looking neither triumphant nor moved, only satisfied, the way she looks when a manifest finally balances.

Edmund's reckoning came separately. He pleaded guilty to conspiracy and perverting the course of justice — his solicitor advised against it, and Edmund overruled him with the same quiet stubbornness he'd once used to shield me, which is to say badly and at great cost. He would not say the word *blackmail* in his own defense. He would not put Theo Sterling on the stand and make a child testify that Josiah Crane had threatened a boy witness into silence, and that a frightened uncle had paid for that silence with ten years of complicity. "The boy saw what he saw," Edmund told the court, in the flat voice of a man reading his own sentence. "What was done afterward was done by me."

He got four years. He looked smaller walking down to the cells, but he looked up once and found me in the gallery, and he nodded — one nod, brief as a tick — and I nodded back, and that was the whole of our farewell for now. Four years is not forever. I've learned to measure time differently than most men. I intend to be there when he comes out, and I intend to have the shop running true when he does, and I intend — this is the part I'm still learning how to carry — to visit him, and to sit across the table from the man who both broke and saved my life, and to let both things be true without letting either one swallow the other.

My father, meanwhile, remained dead. Officially. Permanently, he'd decided, and I had agreed, and the agreement had cost us both more than either of us said on the quay that morning. He stood in the crowd in a dockworker's coat with a weathered face and twenty years of salt in him, and he watched his own son hand the truth to a stranger on a departing ship, and when our eyes met across forty feet of Manchester humanity, he did not wave. He touched his hat brim. One gesture, deliberate, unmistakable: *well done, lad.* Then he turned and walked into the crowd, and I let him go, because the living needed him dead and I had finally learned to count the cost of things honestly.

Some nights I regretted it. Most nights I didn't. On the nights I did, I went to the observatory and climbed to the clock room and listened to the master run — true now, repaired twice over, once by my hands and once by the truth itself — and I reminded myself that a clock does not need its maker standing beside it. It needs only to have been built well, and to be kept.

* * *

The shop changed hands on paper in December. There was nothing to inherit — Edmund's assets were forfeit — so I bought it outright with the money my father had left in the observatory cache, which felt right in a way I can't fully explain: blood money laundered through ten years of silence, converted at last into a place where the truth was displayed behind glass. Ada handled the conveyance. She handled it with such ruthless efficiency that the solicitor asked her, twice, whether she was perhaps in trade herself, and she said, "I keep records," in a tone that made him stop asking.

We made changes. Small ones at first. I rehung the shelves so the light from the window fell across the workbench instead of behind it — a craftsman should be visible at his work, I'd decided; secrecy had done enough damage in this family. I cleared out the back room where Edmund had kept his private papers and found, tucked in a drawer beneath a false bottom I might have known about if I'd been the sort of nephew who snooped, a bundle of letters. Quarterly letters, dockside postmarks, going back nine years. The ones the stationer had told us about — the ones someone local had been receiving all along. Edmund had kept every one. He'd never opened most of them. He'd just... kept them, the way you keep a wound dressed, changing the bandage and never looking underneath.

I read them all in one night. They were short. Reports, mostly — my father writing in the clipped style of a man who has taught himself to leave nothing readable in a intercepted letter. *The men remember. Crane grows careless. The boy must stay hidden.* Never signed. Always the same closing line: *Tell him the clocks are right.*

He'd meant me. All those years, some part of my father had been writing to a boy he couldn't reach, hiding messages inside reports to a brother he didn't trust, trusting that somewhere in the house a clockmaker's apprentice would one day take apart the right clock and find the right letter and understand. Tell him the clocks are right. It was the closest thing to *I love you* that a fugitive could safely send, and I sat on the floor of the back room at three in the morning with nine years of letters spread around me like fallen leaves, and I cried the way I hadn't cried at the funeral, or at the quay, or at any point in ten careful years — loudly, uselessly, alone in a shop full of ticking.

Ada found me there at dawn. She didn't ask questions. She sat down on the floor among the letters, picked one up, read it, set it down, and said, "You're going to want to catalog these by date, not postmark. Postmarks lie." And that was how we started.

* * *

The record began, formally, on a cold bright morning in January, at the workbench by the window, with two pots of ink and a ream of good paper Ada had sourced from the stationer near the docks — the same stationer, though neither of us said so, whose paper and ink had once traced a conspiracy home. She'd chosen him deliberately. "He knows what his goods were used for," she said. "He'll sell us his best stock at cost for the rest of his life, and he'll never breathe a word about our business, because he'd rather die than be mentioned in connection with it again." Practical forgiveness. Very Ada.

The title went at the top of the first page, in her hand, because her hand was the one that had earned the right:

*A True Record of the Men Lost in the Mills of Ancoats, Being an Account of the Boiler Disaster of 1839, the Eleven Dead, and the Long Silence That Followed; Compiled from Sworn Depositions, Shipping Manifests, Wage Ledgers, and the Testimony of Living Witnesses.*

"Living witnesses," I said. "Plural?"

"Three, so far. The stoker's cousin. A widow on Chapel Street who's been waiting ten years for someone to ask. And a girl who was a bobbin-tenter in '39 and remembers everything." Ada uncapped her ink. "The stoker himself can't write anymore — his hands were too badly hurt in the beating — but he dictates, and I take it down, and he signs with a mark. His mark is as legal as a signature. I checked."

Of course she'd checked. Ada Quill had probably checked the legality of breathing.

We worked out a system over those first weeks, the way you work out any mechanism: trial, adjustment, refinement. She gathered testimony and managed the documents; I transcribed the deposition pages from 1839 into fair copy, because my hands were trained to fine work and because — though I never said this aloud — copying those dead men's words stroke by stroke felt like repairing them. Every name I wrote out whole was a name that had been broken into fragments and scattered through falsified ledgers. *Settled,* Crane's notebook had called them. Well. I was unsettled them, one page at a time.

The woman from Chapel Street came on a Thursday. Her name was Martha Ellery, and her husband had been one of the eleven, and she brought with her a tin box containing everything the mills had left her: a wedding ring, a pawn ticket, and a scrap of boiler plate she'd pried from the wreckage herself, which she set on my counter as though setting down an infant.

"They told me it was his fault," she said. "That he'd been drinking. That the gauge was read wrong." Her voice was steady in the way of things that have been steady so long the steadiness has become a kind of scar tissue. "Forty years I've carried that. He never drank. He couldn't — his stomach wouldn't bear it. But there was no one to say so, and after a while I stopped saying it myself, because what's the use of saying a thing into a room where everyone's agreed to be deaf?"

"There's use in it now," Ada said. "Say it to me. Slowly. We've got all day and better ink than the coroner ever had."

And Martha Ellery said it. All of it. For six hours, while the shop's clocks ticked around her like a congregation, and I wrote it down, and when she finished she looked at the pages — at her own words come back to her in permanent ink — and she pressed her hand flat against them and wept without sound, the way people weep when something long dislocated finally goes back into joint.

Before she left, she paused at the framed letter behind the counter. Read it through, top to bottom.

"That's your father's hand?" she asked.

"It is."

"He wrote true, then." She touched the glass, the way the other woman had, the way you touch a headstone. "You keep it where folk can see. That's the whole job, isn't it? Keeping it where folk can see."

* * *

February came in hard, with a wind off the Irwell that rattled the windows, and on the first clear Saturday of the month Ada arrived at the shop carrying a parcel wrapped in brown paper and tied with string, and wearing an expression I had learned to distrust slightly less than most expressions but still not entirely.

"What's that?" I asked.

"The proof sheets." She set it on the counter. "From the printer. First hundred copies of the Record, done proper — sewn bindings, title pages, the lot. The rest of the run finishes next week. Two thousand copies." She untied the string with the efficiency of a woman opening cargo. "One goes to the assize library. One goes to Parliament — I know a man who knows a man. One goes to the *Manchester Guardian*, who have already written asking whether we'd consider serialization, and I've told them yes, at a rate I negotiated upward twice."

"You negotiated with the *Guardian*."

"Somebody had to. You'd have accepted their first offer and then spent a week feeling noble about it."

She was right, which was irritating, and I said so, and she smiled — the real smile, the rare one, the one that reached all the way up and made her look briefly like the girl she must have been before grief made her a clerk of the dead. Then she went quiet, turning the proof sheets, and I recognized the particular quality of her silence. Ada's silences, unlike my family's, always preceded a decision.

"Out with it," I said.

"I've had a letter." She drew it from her coat. Dockside postmark. "From the shipping agent in Bristol. The ship that sailed in spring — the one that carried my copies of the manifests, the ones I sent ahead as insurance. It put in at Charleston, then Baltimore. The documents were delivered to a lawyer there. A man who specializes in..." she consulted the letter, though I suspect she knew it by heart, "...in cases of industrial negligence, American courts being somewhat more hospitable to such claims. Crane's cotton interests extend to three mills in Massachusetts. The lawyer writes to ask whether we would swear affidavits supporting a suit filed on behalf of the victims' families. Including—" she paused, and here her clerk's composure flickered, just once, like a lamp in wind, "—including the estate of Daniel Quill. Which requires, legally, a surviving family member to swear to his identity, his age, his death. To make him exist on paper. Ten years after they erased him."

The shop ticked around us. Forty clocks, all running true, all kept.

"You'd have to sail," I said.

"In April. Six weeks' crossing, maybe eight. The affidavits, the depositions, my ledgers — they'll want the originals examined." She laid the letter on the counter between us, next to the proof sheets, next to the farthing some widow had left for a dead man, and she looked at me with the level gray gaze that had once circled me across this very counter like a duelist sizing up an opponent. "I'm telling you because you're named in this too. Witness to the depositions' discovery. Your father's letter is exhibit material. If I go, I go carrying both our names."

"And if Crane's people—"

"Crane is awaiting trial. Crane's people are scattered. And I've survived worse than an ocean, Theo. I survived being the only person in Manchester who knew the truth and couldn't prove it." She almost smiled. "Besides. Someone has to make sure the Americans spell Quill correctly."

I looked past her, at the frame on the wall. My father's handwriting, behind clear glass, in the good light. *Tell him the clocks are right.* Nine years of quarterly letters in the drawer upstairs, cataloged by date. Two thousand copies of the Record coming off a press within the week. A stoker who dictated his testimony with ruined hands. A widow's words gone back into joint. A brother in Strangeways, serving his four years with what his chaplain reportedly called "unexpected dignity," whatever dignity means to a man counting days.

And an ocean, and a ship, and a question standing on my side of the counter wearing a shipping clerk's coat.

"When do you sail?" I asked.

"April the ninth. Spring tide." She said it evenly, but her hand had gone to the counter's edge, gripping it lightly, the way you grip a rail.

I thought about tides. I thought about how much water can carry away, and how much it brings back, given time, given patience, given someone willing to wait at the shore and keep the lamps lit. I thought about a man touching his hat brim in a crowd, choosing to stay dead so the living could live, and about how I had learned — finally, at cost — to let the water take what it takes and trust what it returns.

"Then I'll walk you to the quay," I said. "And I'll mind the shop. And when the Record's proofs come back from the Americans with their corrections—" I tapped the parcel, and heard the future rustle inside it, "—you can tell them their spelling is English or it's nothing."

Ada laughed — actually laughed, out loud, in my shop, startling a customer who'd come in for a fusee spring — and then she sobered, and reached across the counter, and closed her hand over mine, ink-stained and file-scarred, a clockmaker's hand and a clerk's hand, both of them instruments for keeping record.

"Write to me," she said. "Weekly. I want dates, times, and the state of every clock in that shop. I want the master at the observatory logged daily."

"You want a manifest."

"I want," she said, "to know that everything I love is running true while I'm gone."

The word sat there between us on the counter, small and enormous, alongside the farthing and the proof sheets and the letter behind the glass. Neither of us picked it up. Neither of us put it away. Some mechanisms, I've learned, you don't force. You oil them, and you wait, and you trust the escapement to let the time through one measured second at a time.

"I'll log it daily," I said. "Everything. Dates, times, deviations. Signed."

"Good." She straightened, businesslike again, gathering her coat, her letter, her parcel of futures. At the door she paused, and looked back at the frame one last time — at the dead man's living hand, kept where folk could see — and then at me.

"April the ninth," she said. "Spring tide. Don't be late."

And the door bell rang her out into the February cold, and I stood a moment in the ticking quiet of the shop that was mine now — every clock in it running true, every name in the Record accounted for, every secret I owned hanging openly on the wall — and then I sat down at the workbench by the window, where the light falls on the work, and I dipped my pen, and I began to write.
