## 1. The Weight of Other People's Words

There were two hundred and six steps from the kitchen door of the Ashworth house to the motorcar, and Rafael Sandoval had counted them so many times that the number had stopped meaning anything. It was simply the distance between one life and the other: the kitchen where she drank her morning tea standing up, and the motorcar where she became, for the hours between eight and dusk, the voice of Marguerite Ashworth.

She counted them anyway. It was a Tuesday habit. On Tuesdays they drove to the harbor district, and the harbor district meant letters, and letters meant the part of the day Rafael loved and dreaded in equal measure, though she would have cut out her tongue before admitting the dread to anyone.

"Rafael." Marguerite's voice came down through the floorboards, the way it did every morning, pitched precisely so that it would carry and precisely so that it would not wake the neighbors. "The gray dress. And tell Mrs. Ota I will not be lectured about the damp."

Rafael told Mrs. Ota, who lectured anyway, in the particular silence of a Japanese housekeeper who had opinions and had learned, over four years of employment, that they were best expressed through the placement of a second blanket on a chair. The blanket appeared on Marguerite's chair by the window. Marguerite pretended not to see it. This, too, was ritual.

By half past eight the motorcar was coughing itself awake in the lane — a 1919 Wolseley that Rafael had coaxed through two winters and one flood — and Marguerite came down the steps with her cane in one hand and her gloves in the other, already talking.

"The Dejima committee first. Then the printer on the Embankment, if the Embankment is passable. Then the post office, and Rafael — we will take the long way back along the harbor road. I want to see the water before they close it to us."

"Before who closes it?"

"The weather." Marguerite said it the way other people said *the government* or *the war*, a vast impersonal thing that made its arrangements without consulting anyone. "They'll shut the bridge by the first of November if the storms come the way the fishermen say. You mark it. By spring this whole district will be an island, and everything we want to send out of it will have to go by steamer, and the steamers will be fewer than the things."

Rafael marked it. She marked most things. That was, when it came down to it, her profession.

She drove them down out of the foreign quarter, past the church with its soot-blackened bell, past the camphor trees that the typhoon of '17 had bent but not broken, and into the tangle of streets that climbed and dropped toward the harbor like a sentence that kept revising itself. Nagasaki in October smelled of salt and coal smoke and the last stubborn chrysanthemums in the pots outside the teahouses. Marguerite rode with the window down even in cold weather. She said a woman who shut her windows shut her mind, and she said it often enough that Rafael had stopped hearing it, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat.

"Read me back the committee letter," Marguerite said, as they took the corner by the customs house.

Rafael did not need to look down. She had written it out two nights ago at the kitchen table, in her careful round hand, and she had read it aloud twice then, and Marguerite had made her read it a third time, and somewhere in the third reading Rafael had heard the sentence that would cause trouble and had done what she always did with such sentences.

"'Dear Mrs. Hara,'" she recited now, hands steady on the wheel, eyes on the tram wires. "'The committee thanks you for your letter of the eleventh. The question of the schoolhouse is not, as your correspondent suggests, a question of charity. It is a question of justice, and justice does not require our patience, only our persistence.'"

"And the rest?"

"The rest is the part you gave me at midnight and I cut."

Marguerite smiled at the window. "Which part?"

"The part where you called Mrs. Hara's correspondent a coward with a fountain pen."

"I said no such thing."

"You said a man who writes anonymous letters about schoolhouses should have the courage to sign his fear. I made it a question of justice and persistence."

Marguerite was quiet for a quarter of a mile. Rafael watched her in the small oval of the driving mirror: the strong jaw, the white hair pinned with the severity of a woman who had decided long ago what she would not spend time on. Sixty-one years old and still, when she turned her head, there was something in the movement that made strangers on the street stop and look, though most of them could not have said why.

"You made it better," Marguerite said at last.

"I made it safer."

"Those are not always different things."

"No," Rafael agreed. "But they're not the same thing either."

This was the old dance. They had been dancing it for twelve years, and like all dances danced that long, it had worn grooves into the floor. Rafael Sandoval was the daughter of a dockworker who had run away from a dockworker, and she had come to Marguerite Ashworth's door at nineteen with a forged reference, a stolen overcoat, and the ability to drive an automobile she had learned on by bribing a livery stable boy. Marguerite had looked at the reference, looked at her, and said, "The reference is a lie and your hands are shaking. Can you drive?"

"Yes."

"Then the reference doesn't matter. Get in."

She had gotten in. She had not, in twelve years, gotten out.

The letters were the part nobody else saw. Marguerite Ashworth wrote — dictated — forty, fifty letters in a good week. Petitions and polemics, letters to newspapers in three languages, letters to suffrage societies in Kobe and Tokyo and one furious annual letter to her brother in London that Rafael transcribed and Marguerite always, at the last moment, softened by exactly one degree. And every letter, before it was sealed, Rafael read aloud. That was the ritual. That was the whole architecture of their days: the dictation in the morning, the reading-back in the motorcar or by the stove, Marguerite's eyes closed, listening to her own voice come back to her through another woman's mouth, hearing the shape of it before the world did.

And Rafael — Rafael listened for the other thing. The thing underneath. The sentence that would get a printer arrested. The name mentioned too warmly, or not warmly enough. The silence where a person should have been.

Because here was what twelve years had taught her, and what she had never said aloud to anyone, not even Marguerite, not even in the third reading of anything: truth was not a thing you told. Truth was a thing you *rationed*. You gave it out in portions, at the right hours, to the right people, and the art — the whole art of it — was knowing which silences kept which people safe. A silence could protect a printer. A silence could protect a friend. A silence could protect a woman from her own midnight anger, and Rafael had built a career, a life, an entire self out of knowing where to put the silence.

She was good at it. She was, she suspected, better at it than Marguerite knew.

* * *

The Dejima committee met in a room above a shipping office, and it went the way those meetings went: men talking over each other for an hour, Marguerite letting them, and then Marguerite saying four sentences that rearranged the room. Rafael sat by the door with the notebook on her knees and watched the four sentences land, one after another, like stones dropped into still water. She had drafted two of them. The other two Marguerite had found on her own, in the moment, and Rafael had written them down fast enough to catch them, and would read them back to her tonight so she could hear how good she'd been.

On the Embankment the printer was passable and so was the printer, a stooped man named Ueda who set the committee's pamphlets and charged them half what he charged anyone else and pretended this was because their copy was clean. Their copy was clean because Rafael made it clean. Ueda knew this. He never said so. He would hand her the proofs with a small nod, one professional to another, and that nod was worth more to her than most of the praise she'd ever heard spoken aloud.

It was at the post office that the day broke its pattern.

The post office was the last stop and the best one, because the post office was where the world wrote back. Marguerite received sacks of it — well, not sacks, but enough — and she had a system, and the system was Rafael's. Letters from organizations went into the canvas folder. Letters from journalists went into the leather case. Letters from cranks went into the drawer at home that Rafael privately called the museum. And letters from the small number of people Marguerite actually loved — a list so short Rafael could recite it — went into Marguerite's own hands, unopened, on the drive home, so that she could open them herself with the particular slowness she allowed herself for nothing else.

The clerk brought the tray. Rafael sorted standing at the counter, fast, the way a dealer sorts cards. Organization. Organization. Journalist. A bill from the coal merchant that she slipped into her own pocket, because Marguerite's arithmetic with bills was a form of optimism Rafael had learned to intercept. Crank — she recognized the handwriting of the man in Osaka who believed women's votes would summon earthquakes, and she almost smiled.

Then she stopped.

The envelope at the bottom of the tray was thin, cheap paper, the kind sold in any stationer's for a sen and a half. The handwriting on it was careful and small and slightly formal, the hand of someone who had learned to write in school and still wrote the way school taught, each character placed rather than flowed. The address was correct down to the postal district. The name in the upper left corner of the envelope, where senders were meant to sign, was a single letter.

*E.*

No family name. No return address beyond the district. Just *E.*, written with a kind of deliberation, as if the writer had considered writing more and had decided, at the last moment, against it.

"Is there a problem?" Marguerite asked. She was at the window, watching a steamer come in through the harbor mouth, but she missed nothing. She had missed nothing for twelve years. That was the trap of working for a woman like that — you were watched by someone whose whole profession was watching.

"No," Rafael said. "A letter."

"From whom?"

Rafael turned the envelope over in her hands. There was nothing on the back. She thought: *organization, journalist, crank, loved.* Four categories, twelve years, and this envelope fit none of them, and the fact of that — the small wrongness of it — sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone.

"It's not signed," she said. "Initial only. E."

Something happened to Marguerite's face.

Rafael had spent twelve years learning to read that face the way she read letters, and she could catalogue its weathers: the thunder of the committee room, the dry wind of her humor, the long gray overcast of the days when her knees ached and the movement seemed to her a thing for younger women. She had never once seen this. It lasted perhaps a second and a half — the face going still, not pale exactly but *emptied*, like a room from which the furniture has been carried out — and then it was gone, and Marguerite Ashworth was looking out the window at the steamer again as if nothing in the world had occurred.

"Put it in the folder," she said.

"It's not from an organization."

"Put it in the folder, Rafael."

The use of her full name. Marguerite used it perhaps four times a year, and never in kindness, and never by accident. Rafael put the envelope in the canvas folder. Her hands were steady. She was professionally proud of how steady her hands were, and how little that steadiness had ever cost her, until this moment, when she noticed that it was costing her something now — that the ritual had a crack in it, hairline, running from the corner of the envelope to the center of the morning.

They took the long way back along the harbor road, as ordered. The water was the color of hammered tin, and the fishing boats were coming in early, and Rafael drove slowly and said nothing, because Marguerite had not spoken since the post office, and there was a silence in the motorcar that was not one of their silences. It was not the comfortable silence of two people who have finished each other's clauses for twelve years. It was a silence with a letter in it.

Marguerite broke it herself, three streets from home.

"Rafael. When we return — the letters can wait until tomorrow. All of them."

"The committee expects—"

"The committee can expect. It's what committees are for." She was quiet a moment longer. Then, in a voice so level it had to be leveled, deliberately, like a table with a folded paper under one leg: "You will bring me the one from E. Alone. And you will not read it aloud."

Rafael kept her eyes on the road. Twelve years, and Marguerite had never once asked to open a letter without the reading-back. It was the sacrament of their church, the thing they did, the way they did it. And now, in one sentence, on a Tuesday in October, with the harbor closing for winter somewhere out there in the weather — now it was to be done differently, and done differently meant done in secret, and done in secret meant that whatever the letter from E. contained, it was not for her.

Which meant, though she did not let herself think the thought all the way through, that after twelve years of rationing everyone else's truth, there was a portion of Marguerite Ashworth's truth that had never been rationed out to her at all. There was a silence in this house that was older than her service in it.

"Yes, ma'am," Rafael said.

They came up the hill through the last of the light. The camphor trees went by. The kitchen door waited at the top of its two hundred and six steps, and beyond it the evening's small machinery: the lamp lit, the coal scuttle filled, the letters sorted into their cases and folders and drawers, the world filed away where it belonged.

Rafael carried the canvas folder up the steps herself. At the door of Marguerite's study she took out the thin envelope with its single letter and its schoolroom hand, and she held it a moment — just a moment, the length of a breath — feeling through the cheap paper the small weight of whatever was written inside, the words of some E. who knew this house, who knew this woman, who had written with deliberation and then stopped.

She handed it over unopened. She watched the study door close.

And she stood a long time in the corridor, in the smell of lamp oil and camphor, listening to the silence on the other side of the door — a silence she did not know, after twelve years of knowing every silence in that house — and understood that the routine she had built her whole life inside of had just, quietly, without her permission, begun to crack.
