## 9. Nine Days

Nine days. I counted them the way a miser counts coins — alone, at night, by the light of the stove — and every morning the pile was smaller.

I had learned arithmetic from Marguerite Ashworth. She taught me that time is not a river but a ledger, that every hour spent is an entry you cannot strike. For twelve years I had kept her books: the letters dictated, the silences edited, the words rationed out like sugar during a blockade. Now I kept a different ledger. Nine days until the *Kaisei Maru* cast off. Eight days until the transfer request had to reach the harbormaster's clerk. One berth. Three names, if you counted Tomas, which I did, though no ledger in Nagasaki knew he existed.

I stood in the kitchen at six in the morning, watching steam climb off the rice pot, and made myself say it plainly, the way I never said anything plainly: *Rafael, you are going to put someone on that ship and leave two people behind.*

The rice boiled over. I let it.

* * *

**Day eight.**

Errands were my religion. I had built twelve years of competence out of small motions — the right queue at the post office, the right coin to the right clerk, the right nod to the right gatekeeper. That morning I ran four of them before the fog lifted.

First, the chemist on Tōjin-machi street, for Emiko's cough syrup. The chemist was a narrow man named Ueda who wrapped everything twice and asked nothing once. "The same as last week?" he said, and when I said yes he looked at me over his spectacles with the particular pity of a man who has filled too many prescriptions for too many dying seasons. He doubled the bottle without charging for it. I pretended not to notice. Kindness in this city moved like water under ice — everywhere, invisible, and cold if you fell through.

Second, the harbormaster's office, where I did not go inside. I stood across the street beneath the awning of a tea stall and watched the clerk — a young woman with ink-stained cuffs whose name I had learned from Marguerite's files was Miss Oshiro — carry a stack of manifests from the door to a waiting cart. One berth. One written request. One seal. I drank tea I didn't want and memorized the rhythm of her day, because a thing you might have to do quickly is a thing you should know how to do slowly.

Third, the fish market, where I bought mackerel because Emiko had mentioned — not asked, mentioned, in that flat voice she used for wanting things — that her mother used to salt-grill it on Sundays. Whether that was true or a test, I couldn't tell. Emiko dealt in tests the way her mother dealt in letters.

Fourth, the crates.

I went down the back stairs to the storage room off the courtyard where the Ashworth shipping trunks waited for spring, carrying a covered bowl of rice and egg, and knocked twice on the third crate from the wall. A pause. Then the panel slid, and Tomas's face appeared in the gap — all elbows and watchfulness, ten years old with the eyes of a customs inspector.

"You're late," he whispered.

"The fish took long." I passed the bowl through. His hand shot out and took it the way gulls take bread — fast, certain, already gone. "There's grilled mackerel too. Don't eat it all at once."

"I eat how I want." But he was already eating it all at once, crouched in the straw between the trunks, and I sat on an upturned crate and watched him with a feeling I hadn't budgeted for. It had no line item. It simply accrued.

"Day eight," he said, mouth full. "You said nine days yesterday."

"I did."

"So it's eight now." He nodded, satisfied with his own mathematics, and then, quieter, without looking up: "Is the ship big?"

"Big enough."

"Big enough to hide in?"

My stomach dropped through the floorboards. "Tomas—"

"I'm not stupid," he said. "You bring food to a crate. Nobody brings food to a crate unless the person in it is going somewhere or hiding forever. And nobody hides forever." He licked his thumb. "So. Is it big enough?"

I thought about lying. I had made a profession of lying — the gentle kind, the structural kind, the kind that holds a house up. But there in the straw-smelling dark with a boy who had lost everything to the storm except his appetite, the lie wouldn't come.

"It's big enough," I said. "But it's not yours yet. And it's not safe to hope out loud. Not here."

He considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Hope's quiet," he said finally. "I can do quiet."

God help me, so could I. It was the only language I'd ever been fluent in.

* * *

**Day seven.**

Emiko's cough woke me at three in the morning. I heard it through two walls — a dry, tearing sound, like paper being ripped slowly — and then the silence after it, which was worse, because silence meant she was holding still so no one would come.

I came anyway. I told myself it was practical: the medicine schedule, the water glass, the errands of care. But standing in her doorway with the lamp turned low, watching her lie rigid under the quilt pretending to be asleep, I understood that I had crossed some border I couldn't name. You don't check on cargo. You check on family, or on something wearing family's clothes.

"Your mother used to do that," I said. "Hold still so no one would worry. Twelve years, she did it. I always came anyway. She never once thanked me for it."

Emiko opened her eyes. In the lamplight they were Marguerite's eyes, thirty years younger and forty years harder. "Did you want thanking?"

"No," I said, and was surprised to find it true. "I wanted to be let in."

She looked at me a long moment. Then she shifted, minutely, making a space on the edge of the bed — not an invitation, exactly. An acreage survey. A measuring of ground.

I sat. Neither of us spoke. Outside, the harbor groaned against the seawall, and somewhere a dog gave up on barking. After a while her breathing evened out into something like sleep, and I stayed until the lamp burned low, keeping a vigil I would deny under oath.

That was the night I understood the trap I'd built for myself. I had spent twelve years learning which silences keep people safe. Nobody had taught me what to do when the people themselves became the thing you couldn't lose.

* * *

**Day six.**

Tomas nearly died of curiosity.

It happened at midday, while the household was at market. He had grown bold in his crate — nine days of rice and grilled fish will do that — and he climbed the back stairs to see the house he was living in the way prisoners study escape routes. I found him in the upstairs corridor, barefoot, frozen mid-step, staring through the banister at the front door where Marguerite Ashworth stood shaking rain from her umbrella.

Marguerite looked up. Her gaze went past me, past the boy, snagged on him like a hook in cloth.

For one full second, nobody breathed.

Then I stepped sideways — one step, the width of my body — and broke the sightline, and said, loudly, in the voice I use for deliveries: "Madam, the chemist sent the wrong strength again. Third time this month. Shall I take it back?"

It was nonsense. There was no parcel. There was no wrong strength. But it was a script, and scripts are doors, and Marguerite Ashworth had spent sixty years walking through doors other people opened for her. Her eyes came back to mine. Something moved behind them — calculation, or mercy, or both; with her they wore the same coat.

"Yes," she said. "Do that."

Tomas was gone before the word finished, down the back stairs, silent as smoke. I took the rebuke of the imaginary parcel and carried it out of the room like a real thing, and Marguerite let me, and we never spoke of it, and that — I want to be honest, since honesty is supposed to be this story's business — that felt more intimate than anything we had ever said aloud.

At dinner she looked at me once across the soup, a long look, and I understood that she knew. That she had known since the corridor. That she was choosing, for reasons of her own, to keep my secret the way I had kept hers for twelve years.

The debt between us changed shape that day. Debts do that, quietly, the way tides do.

* * *

**Day five.**

Emiko stopped pretending the cough was a cold.

She sat at the table in the afternoon sorting her mother's correspondence — a task Marguerite had assigned her, or perhaps surrendered to her; with those two, delegation was the only dialect of love either spoke — and midway through the stack she bent double with a fit that lasted a full minute. When it passed she straightened, smoothed her sleeves, and picked up the next letter as though nothing had occurred.

I took the letter out of her hands.

"Give it back," she said.

"In a minute." I poured the syrup — Ueda's doubled bottle, the good strength — and set it in front of her, and stood there until she drank it, glaring at me over the rim like a cat being dosed. "There," I said. "Now you can have it back."

"You're not my nurse."

"No," I agreed. "I'm worse. I'm the person who reads everything and forgets nothing, and I have decided your health is my business. You can fight me about it, but you'll lose, because I've been fighting Marguerite Ashworth for twelve years and I'm undefeated."

Something cracked in her face — not a smile, exactly. The place where a smile would go, cleared and graded, ready for construction. "Undefeated," she repeated. "Does she know that?"

"She suspects. It keeps her humble."

And then Emiko laughed — one short, rusty, astonished syllable, as if the machinery hadn't been oiled in years — and pressed her hand briefly over mine on the tabletop, and took the letter back, and we went on working side by side in the lamplight like two clerks in the office of some small country that existed nowhere but that room.

* * *

**Day four.**

Marguerite dictated letters all morning — the faction correspondence, the port authority, a blistering note to a newspaper editor — and I took them down in shorthand and read them back, editing as I went. *Strike "coward." Strike "coward" and put "a man of limited imagination." Same wound, cleaner bandage.* She approved each change with a grunt. This was our liturgy. We had performed it a thousand times.

But at noon she paused, pen hovering, and said, without looking up: "The boy eats well?"

The pen scratched. My heart did not. "When he's fed," I said.

"He'll need a coat for the crossing. April seas are filthy." She signed the last letter. "The gray one in the cedar trunk. It was going to the rag man anyway."

I wrote *the end* on the page and discovered my hand wasn't steady. Twelve years of composure, undone by a coat. "Thank you," I said.

"Don't thank me. Thank the rag man." She rose, gathered her shawl, and at the door she stopped — half-turned, the light behind her — and said, in the voice she used for things that mattered: "Rafael. Whatever you're carrying this week. Carry it upright."

Then she was gone to her study, and I sat with the letters, and outside the gulls wheeled over a harbor that was counting down with us.

* * *

**Day three.**

We ate dinner together.

I want to record it precisely, because it happened without anyone deciding it, the way weather happens. Marguerite came down at seven instead of taking her tray in the study. Emiko was already seated — she had begun eating at the table that week, citing the poor light in her room, fooling no one. I had cooked, because I had been cooking all week, because feeding people is the one prayer I trust. And Tomas —

Tomas was the reason it almost broke.

He had grown restless in the crate, and that evening, drawn up the back stairs by the smell of the mackerel like a small ghost following its own funeral feast, he got as far as the kitchen doorway before Emiko saw him.

The silence lasted three seconds. In it I watched Emiko take in the boy — the hollow cheeks, the too-big jacket, the stance of a creature perpetually ready to run — and I watched her arrive, visibly, at the same arithmetic I lived inside. One berth. Three names.

"There's a plate missing," she said at last, to no one, to everyone. "Someone fetch a plate."

I fetched it. Marguerite, at the head of the table, unfolded her napkin with the unhurried gravity of a woman launching a fleet, and said, "Sit down, boy, before it goes cold," and that was the whole of the ceremony. No questions. No explanations. Tomas slid into the chair between Emiko and me as if it had been measured for him, and ate with the terrible efficiency of the formerly hungry, and nobody commented, and the candles burned, and the rain came down on the roof like applause from a very great distance.

Somewhere in the middle of the meal, Marguerite told a story — an old one, about a suffrage march in Manchester and a horse that ate her banner — and Emiko said, "You've never told me that," and Marguerite said, "You've never asked," and neither of them softened, exactly, but something between them unclenched by a single degree, and I sat there in the warm noise of it feeling like a man watching a fire in a house he knows is scheduled for demolition.

Emiko laughed twice. Tomas stole food off my plate and I let him. Marguerite caught my eye across the table and raised her glass a half-inch — to me, or to the evening, or to the fiction we were all maintaining — and I raised mine back.

A family at dinner. None of us admitted it. All of us knew.

Afterward, washing dishes while Emiko dried and Tomas dried his sleeves on the towel when he thought I wasn't looking, I did the arithmetic one more time. Three days. One berth. Two names too many, and every one of them now sitting at my table eating my mackerel.

* * *

**Day two.**

Emiko's fever came at dusk.

I found her in her room with the window open to the cold — she claimed the air helped, and maybe it did, and maybe she just preferred suffering on her own terms — flushed and shivering and furious about both. The cough had gone wet overnight. I had heard enough sickrooms to know what wet meant.

I sent Tomas for Ueda with instructions I made him repeat twice, and I sat with Emiko and held the basin and did not flinch at any of it, and near midnight, in the trough between fevers, she gripped my wrist with a strength that surprised us both.

"If it comes to it," she said, "put the boy on the ship."

"Don't talk like that."

"Listen to me." Her eyes were too bright. "I've done the sums. I'm the worst investment in this house. She owes me thirty years and an apology, and neither of those crosses water. But he's ten, and he's got nobody, and—" the cough took her, and she rode it out, jaw clenched, and finished, "—and I know what it is to be nobody's. Put him on."

I said nothing. I held her wrist until her grip loosened into sleep, and then I sat in the dark doing the thing I do best, which is keeping a silence — except this one wasn't keeping anyone safe. This one was just me, alone with a decision that had quietly stopped being Marguerite's and started being mine.

Because here is what I hadn't told anyone, what I had barely told myself: the transfer request form was in my coat pocket. I had collected it from Miss Oshiro that morning — one berth, one written request, one seal, transferable once. It was blank. It had been blank for two days. Every night I took it out and looked at the empty line where a name goes, and every night I put it back.

Tomorrow the *Kaisei Maru* would begin loading. Tomorrow the request had to be filed.

I took out the form one more time and laid it on the table under the lamp, and sharpened a pen, and sat before that single blank line like a sinner before an altar.

Down the hall, Emiko coughed in her sleep. Below the window, faintly, from the crate in the courtyard, came the small restless sound of a boy turning over in the straw. And at the far end of the house, a lamp still burned in Marguerite's study — burning, I would learn later, over a form of her own, blank in the same place, weighted with the same impossible arithmetic.

Three names. One line. And neither of us knew what the other was writing.
