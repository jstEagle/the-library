## 7. What Can Be Carried

There is a particular arithmetic to leaving a place you never expected to leave. You do not count what you love. You count what fits.

I learned this before dawn on the third day of the occupation, kneeling in the back room of Master Perrin's shop with a canvas sack open between my knees like a mouth waiting to be fed. The British had been in Port Mahon four days now. Long enough for the requisition notices to yellow slightly at their nails. Long enough for the lower town to learn the new rhythm of boots on cobbles — not the French rhythm, which had been brisk and bored, but something heavier, more deliberate, as though each soldier wished to be remembered by the stones.

The sack would hold perhaps what a strong man could carry at a walk. I am not a strong man. I am — was — an apprentice clockmaker with ink-stained fingers and a left shoulder that ached in damp weather from a childhood fall down the observatory stair. So the arithmetic was crueler for me than most.

Father's tools first. Always the tools.

* * *

My father taught me that a craftsman's kit is a biography written in steel. Open his roll and you could read his whole life: the burnished pliers worn bright where his thumb had worried them through thirty years of worry; the screwdrivers ground down and reground until they were half their christened length; the loupe cracked across one lens because he'd dropped it running from a bailiff in Genoa and had never replaced it, saying a flaw in the glass teaches humility better than any sermon.

He'd been dead six years by then, my father, carried off by the fever the winter the harbor froze. But his tools had come to me the way poor men's inheritances do — folded into a bundle, handed over with an apology, worth less at pawn than at the bench. I had kept them anyway. Not sentiment, I told myself. Economy. A new set of files cost three livres. Sentiment is free, and free things are the only luxuries the poor can afford without shame.

I unrolled the leather now on the floorboards and went through it piece by piece, the way a priest goes through a soul. Pliers, two pair. Files, five, one nearly spent. Screwdrivers, four. The pin-vice with its collection of drills no thicker than a hair. The staking set, wrapped in oilcloth against rust. And at the bottom, wrapped separately, the thing that was not a tool at all: the template.

* * *

You should understand about the forged receipt, because everything that followed — the cellar, the ship, the stopped clock, all of it — grew out of that single sheet of paper the way a mainspring's power grows out of its coiling.

In the summer of 1753, when Élise Vidal saved my father from debtor's prison, she did not do it with money or charm or any of the instruments the rich use to bend the world. She did it with paper. Her father's shop in the old town kept a press for printing warranty cards and repair tickets, and from it she pulled a receipt — dated correctly, signed convincingly, stamped with a seal her own hand had cut into soft lead — showing that my father's debt of forty-one livres had been discharged in full to a merchant who, conveniently, had died the previous spring and could raise no objection.

It was, she told me afterward, laughing, the finest work either of us would ever do. Better than any escapement. *A clock only measures time,* she said. *Paper can change it.*

She had made me a copy of the template that same summer — a blank form, the seal already impressed, the signature line empty — pressed flat between two boards of a clock case we built together and never sold. A joke, then. A keepsake. Two young people drunk on their own cleverness, hiding a forgery inside a timepiece because neither of them could imagine a world where it might matter.

Three years later I sat on the floor of a dead man's shop and understood that she had given me the most dangerous object in Port Mahon, and had done it as a gift, the way you give someone a knife for their birthday without imagining throats.

I wrapped it in oilcloth and put it in the sack beside the tools.

* * *

Master Perrin died sometime in the night between Tuesday and Wednesday, and I want to be honest about how I learned this, because honesty is the one luxury I have never been able to pawn.

I did not find him. I did not sit vigil. I was not even in the shop when it happened.

I had spent the night at the observatory — or near it — because the ledger was there. You will remember, if you have followed me this far, that the occupation's requisition ledger lay hidden in the case of the master clock, every name and debt and denunciation recorded by the French administration in a hand so neat it looked like print. My own master's name among them, marked with a black cross. And since the British had taken the town and begun asking after exactly such papers, I had made a decision that seemed wise at midnight and monstrous by morning: I had gone back for it.

Getting in had been absurdly easy. The sentry at the observatory door was nineteen, cold, and grateful to anyone who claimed to be the clockmaker come to check the mechanism against damp. *The garrison wants the guns' timing kept true,* he said, with the earnestness of a boy reciting scripture he didn't believe. *Officer's orders.* I nodded gravely and let him believe I was obeying those orders rather than robbing the empire blind, and he held his lantern high while I knelt at the case, and I lifted the ledger out from beneath the going-train where I had wedged it days before, and thanked him, and walked out into the dark with a hundred lives under my coat.

Including, though I did not yet know it, my own.

* * *

I came back to the shop at half past four in the morning, ledger against my ribs, hands still smelling of clock oil and fear. The lamp in the workroom was burning. That was the first wrong thing. Master Perrin had economized on light the way other men economized on food; he had not burned a lamp past dark in the eleven years I had served him, except the night his wife died, and once when the King of Sweden was reported passing through the strait and he wanted to see.

He was at his bench. He had died at his bench, which I suppose is the death every craftsman prays for and few get. His head rested on his folded arms, his spectacles pushed up onto his forehead, and before him lay the movement of the chapel regulator, disassembled into its neat rows of parts, each piece aligned with the precision of a man who intended to return to it. He had been working. He had simply stopped, the way a clock stops — not broken, merely run down, the mainspring of him finally spent.

He was seventy-one years old. He had taken me in at fourteen when my father died, fed me from his own pot, taught me everything I knew that was worth knowing, paid me barely enough to live and never enough to leave, complained daily about my slowness, my carelessness, my handwriting, and my politics. In eleven years he had praised my work aloud exactly twice.

I stood in the doorway holding a stolen ledger and could not make myself cross the room.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about grief when it arrives in wartime: it does not get its own hour. It does not spread itself across the seven proper stages like a fine cloth. It arrives already compressed, packed tight as goods in a smuggler's boat, because there is no room for it. Somewhere below the window, boots. Somewhere beyond the mole, warships. In my coat, a book full of names that could hang people. And on the bench, my master, cold, with his spectacles on his forehead like a man startled by something above him.

I crossed the room eventually. I took the spectacles off his forehead and folded them and put them in his breast pocket, where they belonged. I closed his eyes, which resisted, being dry, and required a certain firmness I did not enjoy. Then I did the practical things, because the practical things were all I had, and doing them was the only prayer I know how to say.

* * *

Survival, I have come to believe, is not a virtue. It is a discipline, like scales for a musician, and it must be practiced daily whether or not you feel like practicing, especially when you don't.

So: I closed his eyes. I straightened his arms. I covered him with the good blanket from his cot, the wool one he saved for winter and pretended not to value. I opened the window a hand's width, because the room was warm and would grow warmer, and because some part of me — the wretched, practical part that was already making lists — understood that a body discovered too soon would bring questions, and questions would bring soldiers, and soldiers would bring a search, and in the shop's back room, behind the false drawer where Master Perrin kept his private papers, there were letters from his son in Toulon that read very much like correspondence with the enemy, because they were.

Then I sat down on the floor with my back against his stool — his stool, which still held the shape of him the way a glove holds a hand — and I allowed myself exactly ten minutes.

I know it was ten minutes because I counted them by my pulse, the way Élise had taught me to keep time when there was no clock. Sixty beats to the minute, near enough, if you are calm, and I was not calm, so perhaps it was eight minutes or twelve, but I gave grief its ration and no more. I thought about the twice he had praised me. Once for a remontoire I'd fitted to the observatory's secondary dial, when he'd said, grudgingly, *Hm. That will do.* Once, near the end, when I'd shown him the escapement I'd cut from scratch, and he'd turned it over in his fingers for a long time and said nothing at all, which from Master Perrin was a eulogy.

Then the ten minutes ended, and I got up, and I began to pack.

* * *

What can be carried. Let me give you the inventory, because the inventory is the truest autobiography I will ever write.

Father's tool roll. The forged-receipt template. The ledger, which went not in the sack but wrapped flat against my belly under my shirt, where it rode like a second heartbeat. Three days of bread, hard, and a twist of salt. The small hammer with the brass face, because brass forgives mistakes that steel punishes. My spare shirt, which was also my only spare shirt. The letter Élise had written me in the last summer of peace, which I told myself I kept for the paper, good rag paper being scarce, and which I did not reread, because I am a coward in precisely one direction and it points at her.

And nothing else. That was the discipline of it. The bed I slept in stayed. The pots stayed. The half-finished movements on the shelves stayed, each one tagged in Master Perrin's crabbed hand with a customer's name and a balance owed — little debts, honest ones, the kind that keep a town stitched together. The portrait of his wife stayed on its nail. The cat — there was a cat, a gray thief named Voltaire whom the master had tolerated and secretly fed — stayed, and looked at me with the contempt of his species as I tied the sack, and I said, *Someone will feed you, you cynical beast,* and did not entirely believe it.

I left money in the flour jar. Not much. Enough, I judged, for whoever came to deal with the body to buy a coffin of the plain sort Master Perrin would have specified, and a mass of the short kind, and a stone with just his name and years, no epitaph, because he would have called an epitaph vanity and charged himself for it.

That was the hardest arithmetic of the morning, harder than the packing. Deciding what my master's life weighed. It fit in a flour jar.

* * *

I worked until the window went gray, and here is what I did with the gray hours, because a mind cannot be left idle any more than a movement can be left dirty.

I hid the ledger again. Not in the clock this time — the observatory was finished as a hiding place, watched now by boys with lanterns and officers with lists. I hid it in the chapel loft, inside the case of the grandfather clock that stood in the corner there, the one bound for storage above the nave, its case tall enough and hollow enough at the base to swallow a ream of paper and never confess. I had moved it there two nights before, and now I went back in the gray light, up through the sacristy, past the altar where a French crucifix hung above a British flag nailed to the wall with what struck me as theological optimism, and I pressed the ledger down into the clock's skirts among the dust and dead flies, and I weighted it with the pendulum bob, and I stood back and looked at the great dumb case and thought: *Time keeps score. Let it keep mine.*

Then home again. Then the sack. Then the last item, which I had been avoiding.

Master Perrin's own tools — the good ones, the Swiss files and the English jeweling tools he'd bought in better years and touched rarely, like a bishop handling relics. By rights they belonged to his estate, to his son in Toulon, to whatever clerk the army would appoint to inventory the shops of the dead. Taking them was theft. Leaving them meant they would vanish into some sergeant's knapsack within the week, which was also theft, merely by a man with better paperwork.

I took them. I tell you this without apology and without pride. I wrapped them in his own apron and put them in the sack, and if his ghost objected, it objected in silence, which from Master Perrin would have been praise.

* * *

The sun came up over the mole the color of a healing bruise, and with it came the bells — wrong, still wrong, the cathedral's Angelus dragging a quarter-minute behind the citadel's, the whole town ringing slightly out of true, and every soldier who noticed blamed saboteurs, and every townsman who noticed said nothing, because we had all learned in four days what the occupation had not yet learned: that whoever controls the time controls the town, and that the man who repairs the clocks is the most invisible man in any garrison.

I shouldered the sack. I looked once around the workroom — at the benches, the shavings, the rows of patient unfinished clocks with their hands stopped at whatever hour their owners had last wound them, a whole shelf of small deaths — and I blew out the lamp, though the sun was up, because Master Perrin would have risen from the grave to scold me for wasting oil.

At the door I paused. Behind me, under the good blanket, the shape of him. Ahead of me, down the street, the sound of boots, and among the boots a voice giving orders in English, and attached to the voice, I would learn within the hour, an offer.

Because that was the morning's final arithmetic, the sum I had not yet seen coming. Sergeant Hollis wanted a clockmaker. The observatory's timing must be kept true for the guns. And the only apprentice left in Port Mahon who knew that mechanism gear by gear was standing in a doorway with a stolen ledger under his shirt and a dead man's tools on his back, trying to look like exactly the kind of invisible, useful, harmless young man that an occupying army likes to protect.

Protection, I would discover, has a price list. And I was about to be quoted.
