## 8. The Officer's Offer

They came for me on the third morning after my master died, which I have always thought showed a certain tact, or else a certain efficiency, and with soldiers those are often the same thing.

I was in the shop, doing what I had done every day since: winding his clocks. There were eleven of them in the front room, and they had gone out of agreement with one another within hours of his death, each drifting to its own private time like mourners who cannot agree on the date of the funeral. I wound them anyway. It was not sentiment, I told myself. A stopped clock is a reproach to everyone who walks past it, and I could not afford reproaches. But my hands knew better than my head did. Hands always do.

The knock came at seven, three blows of a rifle butt against the doorframe, because British soldiers knock the way weather arrives — without apology and without waiting to be asked in.

"Tamsin Crane." The sergeant in the doorway read my name off a paper as though it were an accusation. Behind him stood a tall officer in a coat that had been brushed that morning but not recently enough, with tired eyes set above a mouth that looked like it had once known how to smile and had misplaced the habit somewhere between Gibraltar and here. "You'll come with us. The Captain wants a word."

"About what?" I asked, though I already knew. In an occupied town there are only about four subjects, and none of them end well.

"The observatory clock," he said. And then, because sergeants are honest creatures even when their masters are not: "And other matters."

* * *

Captain Hollis received me in the Governor's residence, which now belonged to the King of England by right of conquest and smelled, incongruously, of French lavender still burning in the braziers, because no army on earth has yet worked out what to do with a defeated people's soap. He sat behind a desk that was too large for him, or he was too small for the desk; either way he looked like a man who had been assigned furniture rather than chosen it.

"You're the apprentice," he said. "Crane."

"I'm the clockmaker," I said. "The apprentice part died with Master Ferrand yesterday. Or the day before. He wasn't precise about it at the end, which for a horologist is a kind of failure."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment that a smile had been attempted and found insufficient to the occasion.

"You repaired the master clock in March. Before the siege."

"I did."

"How long have you been at the trade?"

"Since I was nine. My father was a watchmaker in Falmouth before the debts took him. I was raised inside movements. While other children learned their letters, I learned why a balance spring sulks in damp weather."

He studied me for a long moment, the way a man studies a mechanism he suspects of having more wheels than are visible from the outside. Then he opened a drawer and set something on the desk, and my heart performed a maneuver no escapement should be asked to perform.

It was a page. One page only, water-stained at one corner, written in the neat administrative hand I knew better than my own prayers. Names. Sums. Notations in the margin. The requisition ledger of the French occupation, or rather the surviving fragment of it, fished out of some fire or drawer by some soldier who understood paper was worth more than the wood it was written on.

"We recovered this," Hollis said, "and eleven others like it. Partially burned. The garrison destroyed most of their records when your fleet appeared off the mole — very thorough, very French — but paper is stubborn. Some of it survived." He turned the page toward me. "You've seen this document before."

There was no point lying. Lying to an officer about a thing he can prove is like winding a clock that isn't broken: it makes noise and accomplishes nothing.

"I repaired the clock it was hidden in," I said. "I saw it. I didn't take it. When the shelling started, I left it where it lay."

"Why?"

"Because taking it seemed like the sort of decision a person doesn't come back from."

He looked at me again, longer this time, and I had the uncomfortable sensation of being weighed — not as a suspect weighs, but as a merchant weighs goods he intends to buy.

"And yet you know what's in it."

"Names," I said. "Debts. Who sold flour to the garrison and who sold information. Who denounced whom. Three years of Port Mahon keeping score on itself." I paused. "Including, I believe, a black cross beside my late master's name. Though he's past caring now."

Hollis did not flinch, which told me he had already seen the cross, and had already drawn whatever conclusions he intended to draw from it.

"Here is my situation, Crane, and I'll give it to you plainly because I find plainness saves time and I am short of both." He laced his fingers together on the desk. "The fleet wants the harbor batteries timed to the minute. Gunnery without accurate time is just noise with ambition. The observatory clock governs every chronometer in this garrison, and it is currently — I'm told — unreliable. The man who kept it is dead. You are the only soul on this island who can make it speak English again, so to speak."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange—" He drew a second paper from the drawer, and this one made my chest go tight in a way I resented deeply, because I pride myself on being legible to no one. It was a protection order. A printed form, half filled in, awaiting only a name. *Protected person. Employed in His Majesty's service. To be molested at peril of military discipline.* Beneath it, a pass. Freedom of movement through the town and gates. Papers. The two things an invisible person cannot buy and cannot live without.

"Protection," he said. "Rations. Wages. And these." He tapped the papers. "No soldier touches you. No neighbor denounces you into a cell. No magistrate asks where you slept during the occupation or whose bread you ate. As far as the army is concerned, Tamsin Crane was never French-anything. Crane was a prisoner of circumstance who kept their head down and their hands clean, and now serves the Crown."

It was, I will admit it freely, beautifully done. He had taken every fear I carried and arranged them in a row, and then walked down the row extinguishing them one by one, like lamps at dawn. Safety. Bread. Legitimacy. A name that meant nothing and therefore cost nothing.

"And the price," I said, because there is always a price, and officers who pretend otherwise are merely officers who haven't gotten to that paragraph yet.

His eyes came up to mine, and for the first time I saw that the tiredness in them was not laziness. It was the specific fatigue of a decent man doing indecent arithmetic.

"The pages we recovered are damaged. Burned at the edges, water-blurred in places. Some names are whole. Some are fragments — a surname, half a street address, initials. The commandant wants the list reconstructed. Every collaborator identified. Every denunciation confirmed." He slid the recovered page an inch closer to me. "You lived in this town through the occupation. You know these people. You know who traded with the French garrison, who informed, who profited. I need someone who can look at a blurred line and tell me whether it reads *Basset* or *Bassat*, whether the house on the Calle was number nine or nineteen."

"You want me to name names."

"I want you to confirm names," he said. "There's a difference. The names are already on the paper. The French wrote them. I'm asking you to read your neighbors' handwriting, nothing more."

"That's a very fine distinction, Captain."

"Yes," he said quietly. "It is. I've been making fine distinctions my whole career. They're how men like me sleep." He leaned back. "Refuse, and you walk out of here with nothing — no protection, no papers, and the full attention of a garrison that has been told a collaborator list exists and is incomplete. Someone in this town will be looking for whoever knows about that ledger, Crane. Someone always is. Accept, and you are untouchable."

Untouchable. I turned the word over the way I'd turn a suspicious coin, checking its weight, biting its edge. Untouchable meant safe. Safe meant alive. Alive meant — and here the thought arrived unbidden, unwelcome, wearing Élise Vidal's face — alive meant able to keep a promise.

Because that was the trap inside the bait, wasn't it. Papers. Movement. Access. A protected clockmaker could walk where the desperate couldn't. Could stand at the water stair. Could stand anywhere.

I said none of this. I said, "I'll need to see all the pages. Damaged documents mislead. If I'm to confirm names, I need the whole fragment, examined properly, under good light."

Something flickered across Hollis's face — satisfaction, I thought, or perhaps relief, though relief at what I couldn't yet say.

"You'll work here," he said. "In this room. Under guard. Hours at my discretion."

"Under guard," I repeated. "Of course. Trust is such an expensive commodity these days. Cheaper to post a sentry."

"It's cheaper to post a sentry," he agreed, with the ghost of that almost-smile again, "because trust, unlike a sentry, can't be shot if it fails." He stood, and the interview was plainly over, but at the door I paused, because there was one question I could not leave unasked, and asking it was itself a risk I judged worth taking.

"Captain. The pages that were burned — the ones the French destroyed. Do you expect to recover any others?"

He regarded me for a moment with those tired eyes, and I watched him decide how much truth a clockmaker could be trusted with.

"There's intelligence," he said, "that at least one substantial portion survived the garrison's fires. Hidden, not destroyed. The commandant believes it will surface." He picked up his pen. "When it does, I want the man who knows this town reading it. That's all, Crane. Go home. Report here tomorrow at eight. Bring your tools."

I went home. The sentry followed me as far as the corner of the Calle de la Mar, then stopped, planted, and became a fixture, and I understood that I had acquired a shadow with a bayonet, and that from this hour forward I would never again be entirely alone — which, for a person whose entire survival had been built on being overlooked, was either the safest thing that had ever happened to me or the most dangerous, and I genuinely could not tell which.

* * *

From the journal of Captain Nathaniel Hollis, 14th June:

The clockmaker interests me. I have interviewed perhaps two hundred inhabitants of this town since we took the harbor — fishermen, priests, widows, smugglers, one memorable woman who tried to sell me her sister as a spy and her sister's silence as a separate transaction — and they divide cleanly into two sorts: those who are afraid, and those who are pretending not to be. Crane is neither. Crane is *calculating*. I watched it happen across my own desk: the offer made, the eyes moving through it like a hand through a case of works, testing each wheel for play, finding the flaw, saying nothing about the flaw.

The flaw, I think, is this: Crane accepted too easily and too precisely. An innocent person, offered protection, grabs at it with both hands and thanks God. A guilty one bargains. Crane did neither — Crane *negotiated terms of employment*, as though safety were a wage and I an employer, which I suppose, in fairness, I am. But there was something underneath the negotiation. A second calculation running behind the first, the way a striking train runs behind the going train in a good clock — silent, geared to the same barrel, moving to a purpose the dial never shows.

I am not a fool. I know what the commandant wants from that ledger, and it is not justice; it is leverage, catalogued and bound. Half the merchants of this town bought their lives from the French administration, and half the other half sold their neighbors to buy theirs, and the paper proving it is worth more than the harbor. Whoever holds the complete ledger holds Port Mahon by the throat. The commandant knows it. I suspect the clockmaker knows it better.

And I suspect — I cannot prove it, but suspicion is my trade as much as soldiering — that Crane knows where the missing pages are. The request to see *all* the fragments was not craftsmanship. It was reconnaissance.

Why does this trouble me? Because I liked the answer Crane gave to my first question. *Taking it seemed like the sort of decision a person doesn't come back from.* That is not the answer of a traitor. It is the answer of someone who understands exactly what the ledger is — a rope with three hundred ends — and wanted no part of holding it. Which means Crane took no pages.

Which means the pages Crane wants to examine are pages Crane expects to *need*.

For what purpose does a clockmaker need a list of collaborators?

I have until Friday, by my own promise to myself, to learn the answer. I have posted a discreet watch on the clockmaker's lodgings — Moreau's boy, who can lean on a wall better than any man in the regiment. If Crane moves at night, moves strangely, moves toward the water, I will know it.

I find I am hoping I am wrong. This is unprofessional of me. I record it anyway, because a journal that flatters its author is worthless, and because — I do not know why — I looked at that thin, sharp-faced apprentice across my desk and thought: *here is a person who has spent a lifetime being unseen, and would very much like to remain so, and I have just offered them the one thing they cannot refuse and the one thing that will destroy them.*

Safety is a strange gift. Given to the wrong person, it burns the house down.

Tomorrow, eight o'clock. We shall see what the clock says.

* * *

That night I sat in the dark shop among my dead master's eleven disagreeing clocks and thought about what I had done.

I had put my name on a protection order. I had promised to read the handwriting of my neighbors for an occupying army — for *another* occupying army, the town having changed hands the way a bad coin changes hands, passing from pocket to pocket while remaining equally spendable. I had acquired papers, wages, a sentry, and a shadow, and I had surrendered the only currency I had ever truly owned, which was my invisibility.

And I had done it all with one thought beating beneath the going train of my mind like a striking train geared to the same barrel:

Papers mean movement. Protection means access. Eight o'clock tomorrow means I stand, every day, in a guarded room, over the reconstructed list of everyone in Port Mahon the British intend to hang —

— including, if anyone ever spoke of it, a Frenchwoman sheltering in a flooded cellar below the old clock shop, keeping time by counting her own pulse.

I had told Hollis I needed to see all the pages. That much was true. What I had not told him — what I would carry out of that guarded room in my head, page by page, name by name, if it came to it — was the rest.

Somewhere in those burned fragments, or in the missing portion the commandant was hunting, there might be a name. *Vidal.* Wine merchants, Calle de la Mar. And if it was there, I needed to reach it before the ink reached a gallows.

One of my master's clocks struck ten, alone, out of true, faithful to nobody.

I got up, lit no lamp, and began to plan.
