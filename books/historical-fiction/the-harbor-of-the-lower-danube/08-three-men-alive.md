## 8. Three Men Alive

The man's name was Petru Salko, and according to the harbor manifests he had been dead for eleven days.

Ines had found him the way he found everything now — by subtraction. The manifest of the *Corvina*, Anton Vess's last commission, listed six crew lost when she broke up on the bar at Sulina: six names in a clerk's careful hand, each one initialed by a harbormaster who had never once come down to look at the water. But Ines had seen three of those names walking. He had seen them at the timber yards, at the chandlers', at the fish market where the gulls screamed over the offal. Dead men did not buy herring. Dead men did not spit into the river and laugh.

Two of them had left Galati within days of his first sighting — gone upriver or overland, no one would say. The third, Petru Salko, boatswain of the *Corvina*, had stayed. And staying, in Ines's experience of the lower Danube that autumn, was either courage or a debt too large to run from.

He watched the tavern from across the lane for most of an afternoon, wrapped in a borrowed coat that smelled of tar and other men's sweat, doing what he did best: keeping busy hands, keeping a low profile. He mended a strap on a porter's harness for a coin. He sat against a wall and cleaned his boots with a knife blade until the knife was dirtier than the boots. A man who looked like he belonged nowhere drew eyes; a man absorbed in small labor drew none. It was the rule he had lived by since the Crimea, and it had kept him alive through things he did not let himself remember in daylight.

The tavern was called the Drowned Bell, which Ines thought a name only a harbor could produce without irony. It squatted between a rope-walk and a cooperage, its lower half stone and its upper half timber, leaning toward the water as if listening. By dusk the lamps inside made amber squares of its windows, and through the largest of them Ines could see the boatswain at a corner table, drinking steadily, alone, with his back to the wall.

A dead man with a survivor's instincts. That was worth noting too.

* * *

Ines waited until the third hour after dark, when the tavern's noise had thickened into the comfortable roar of men paid on Saturday and spending it badly. Then he went in.

He had dressed for it. The coat was a stevedore's; his hands, always cracked and stained from the sickroom, needed no disguise. He carried two glasses of the house wine — vinegar with ambitions — and crossed the room with the unhurried gait of a man delivering a drink to a friend, so that by the time Salko looked up, the glass was already being set down before him and Ines was already seated opposite, as though this had been arranged years ago.

Salko's hand went under the table. Ines did not pretend not to notice.

"Whatever you're holding," Ines said quietly, in Romanian, "I'd rather you didn't use it in here. The wine's bad enough already."

"Who are you?" The boatswain was perhaps forty, built like a bollard, with a beard going gray in patches and a face that had been handsome before drink and fear got their shares. His eyes moved past Ines to the door, to the windows, to the dark mouths of the back passage. Counting exits. "I don't know you."

"No," Ines agreed. "You know my work, though. Or you will." He turned his own glass slowly on the table, watching the lamplight swim in it. "You were boatswain on the *Corvina*. You went down with her off Sulina. It's in the manifest — signed, sealed, entered in the harbor book. Six souls lost." He looked up. "And yet here you are, drinking wine that could strip paint, very much among the living."

Something happened in Salko's face then that Ines had seen before, on the faces of soldiers the morning after an action they'd survived and others hadn't. Not guilt exactly. Something older and more animal than guilt — the look of a man who has been counted among the dead and has discovered that the counting was done by someone else, for someone else's purposes, and that his own account of himself counts for nothing.

"You've made a mistake," Salko said. "Wrong man."

"I never make mistakes about faces." Ines said it simply, without heat, and saw the words land harder than any threat could have. "It's my trade. I keep them. I have kept four hundred and eleven since I came to this harbor, and every one of them is written down somewhere safe, with a name attached, because names are the only thing the river can't take unless men help it." He leaned forward a fraction. "Petru Salko. Boatswain. Wife in Tulcea, a sister in Brăila. Lost at sea, eleventh of October. Shall I tell you what else is written next to your name?"

The tavern roared around them. Somewhere a fiddle started up and was shouted down. Between the two men at the corner table there was a silence with a current in it, like the water under the ice on the harbor in January.

"What else," Salko said. It was not quite a question.

"That's what I came to find out." Ines pushed the wine glass an inch closer to him. "Drink that. You'll want it. And then you'll tell me about the night the *Corvina* didn't sink."

* * *

It took another glass, and then half of Ines's, and it took Ines saying — truthfully, because lies came hard to him even now, even necessary ones — "I'm not from the Meridian House, and I'm not from the magistrate, and if I were either, you'd already be in the river." Only then did the boatswain begin to talk, low and halting, his eyes fixed on the table as if the story were written there in spilled wine and he was reading it back.

"She didn't break up on the bar," he said. "That's the first lie. We came up from Sulina light — just Vess and five of us, running a small cargo, nothing worth the paper of a manifest. Weather was fair. Fair as a church calendar. And then off the bar we take orders to stand in to shore, close in, closer than any sane master would bring her, and Vess — Anton, Mr. Vess — he's white as sailcloth, but he does it, because there's a boat waiting in the lee of the point with a lantern hung wrong, green over red, and that's a signal, see, that's a signal someone paid for."

Ines kept his hands still on the table. Busy hands, quiet hands. Inside, something had begun to move, slow and cold, the way floodwater moves under a floor.

"The cargo goes over the side to the boat. Crates. Sealed crates, iron-banded, and heavy enough that we strained the davits. And then—" Salko stopped. He turned his glass around twice, three times. "Then a gentleman comes out of the stern cabin. Fine coat. Gloves. Never gave a name. He has papers, and he makes Vess sign, and Vess's hand is shaking so bad the signature looks like a spider drowned in ink. And the gentleman says, 'Now the ship.' Just that. Now the ship."

"And you scuttled her."

"We opened her seams with the augers we carry for repairs. Offshore, where the water's deep. We took the ship's boat back to the point, and the gentleman's men took the crates, and the gentleman paid us." Salko laughed, and it was the worst sound Ines had heard in a season full of bad sounds. "Paid us to be dead. Fifty leu apiece and our names in the manifest as lost, and Vess telling us, 'Better drowned than witnesses, boys, better drowned,' and we took the money, because a man takes money, doesn't he, a man always—"

He drank. His hand was not steady.

"Vess wouldn't stay bought," Ines said. It was not a guess. He had spent two weeks inside the drowned shipwright's workshop; he had inventoried the tools of a careful man and read, in the arrangement of those tools, the mind behind them. "He was going to talk."

"He was going to do worse than talk." For the first time Salko looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious. "He had papers of his own. He told us — the night before, at the workshop, he'd had us all in, drinking his plum brandy, and he tells us he's made a will. Proper will, witnessed, sealed with his ring. Says whatever happens to him, the truth goes where it hurts. Says there's a boy, too, and the boy's provided for, and any man who touches that boy answers to the will and to God and to Petre Vess's memory—" He caught himself. "To old Vess's family. The boy's kin, understand? Kin of his wife's line. He showed us the seal. Black stone in gold. I held it in my own hand, warm from his finger, and he says, 'Remember this seal, lads. If ever you see it, you'll know the truth is moving.'"

The tavern noise seemed to recede, as though the room had been dropped underwater. Ines sat very still. A will. A seal. A boy provided for — and ten days later the shipwright floating in his own harbor basin with his lungs full of river, and a mute child asleep in sawdust with a note pinned to his coat instructing the world to forget his face.

"And the payment," Ines said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "Your fifty leu. Who paid it? Whose money?"

Salko's mouth worked. When he answered, he had dropped his voice so low that Ines had to lean across the table into the smell of wine and terror to catch it.

"It came in an envelope. No name. But the paper — I know paper, I kept books for Vess two seasons — the paper was Meridian stock paper. Cream wove, with the little anchor-and-sun watermark in the corner. Same as the shipping house uses for everything out of their Galati office." He swallowed. "And the gentleman in the fine coat, the night on the *Corvina* — when he took off his glove to count the second payment, I saw the ring on his hand. Not Vess's ring. Another one. Gold, with a crest cut deep, and I only saw it a moment, but I've been turning it over in my head every night since, because I've seen that crest before, on the crates, branded into the crate-ends. Anchor and sun."

Meridian. The name settled into Ines like a stone into mud. He had heard it in the harbor offices, seen it stenciled on bales and barrels, the respectable face of the river trade, a firm old enough to have its fingers in every manifest between Vienna and the sea. Respectable firms did not scuttle ships. Respectable firms did not pay men to be dead.

Respectable firms, said a voice in him that had learned its lessons in another war, in another army, were precisely the ones that could afford to make the world agree with their paperwork.

"Why are you still here?" Ines asked. "Your companions ran. You should have run with them."

"Because I'm the one who held the auger." Salko said it flatly, the confession of a man long past the country where shame could still surprise him. "I opened her seams. My hands did it. And every night I lie down and I'm not properly alive and not properly dead, and I think — Vess is in the basin, and I'm drinking his murderers' money, and there's a boy—" He stopped again. His jaw worked. "There's a boy, they said. Vess's people. They asked me, the ones who paid, they asked casual-like, had Vess spoken of a boy, where the boy might be. And I told them nothing, because I knew nothing, and thank God for ignorance, it's the only honest thing I owned." He gripped the table edge. "But they're asking everywhere. There's a man comes round the taverns, quiet fellow, well-spoken, asks after children, offers money for word of a stray. And the men who took the money with me — Toma and Ilie — they stopped coming round three days ago. Their rooms stand empty. Their women say they went off in the night without a word."

"Gone where?"

"Gone." Salko's voice cracked on it. "Like men who were never born. And I sit here every night because I don't know how to be anywhere else, and every night I think, tonight they'll come for the last drowned man, tonight they'll finish the manifest." He looked at Ines with terrible directness. "So. You keep faces. You keep names. What do you want with mine?"

Ines took out his notebook — the small one, the day-book, not the ledger itself, which slept wrapped in oilcloth beneath a loose plank under his cot where no searcher had yet thought to kneel — and he wrote, in his small exact hand, while Salko watched: *Petru Salko, boatswain, Corvina. Alive. Will of Anton Vess. Seal, black stone in gold. Payment, Meridian stock paper. Companions Toma and Ilie, missing.* He dated it. He signed it. Then he tore the page free, folded it twice, and held it out.

"Eat it, burn it, or keep it," he said. "If anything happens to me, that page finds its way to the inquiry that must eventually come, because inquiries always come, sooner or later, when enough paper disagrees with enough graves. If anything happens to you —" he met the boatswain's eyes — "then you were alive on this date, in this place, and you told the truth, and it is written down, and I will spend whatever's left of my life making sure someone reads it aloud."

Salko stared at the folded page. His big scarred hands came up around it the way a drowning man's hands come up around a spar.

"Nobody's ever," he began, and could not finish. He put the page inside his shirt, against his skin. "Who are you?" he asked again, but softly now, almost reverent, the way men in hospitals sometimes asked it of the nurses who washed them at the end.

"My name is Ines Amberdale," he said. "I'm nobody. That's rather the point of me."

* * *

They parted outside the Drowned Bell a little before midnight, Salko turning toward the lodging-house over the chandlery where he slept above the waterline — Ines noted that, noted everything, the habit of a lifetime — and Ines walking the long way back along the quay, past the dark warehouses, past the hospital stairs where a lamp still burned for the fever cases, his mind moving fast and cold over what he had heard, fitting pieces together the way he had once fitted together the inventory of a dead man's tools.

A will, witnessed and sealed. A boy named heir. A ship sunk to bury a transaction, a shipwright drowned to bury a witness, a fortune waiting for proof of a child's death — and a shipping house paying for all of it in its own stationery, because power that has grown old enough stops bothering to hide its handwriting.

And subtracting, always subtracting. Two crewmen fled, two more vanished from their beds. Witnesses were being removed from the world the way clerks struck errors from a ledger — neatly, quietly, one line at a time. Until the only copy of the truth left standing would be the one in the murderer's books.

Unless someone kept an original text.

Ines stood a moment at the rail above the black water, listening to the river breathe against the pilings, and allowed himself, briefly, to feel the full weight of what he had taken up. Then he made himself a promise in the old grammar of his grief: *I will write it all down. Every name. Every hour. Let them subtract; I will add.*

He reached the workshop near one o'clock, checked the false bulkhead — Miri asleep, breathing slow and even, one hand curled around a stub of charcoal even in sleep — and lay down on the cot with his boots on.

He woke before dawn to a silence with a shape to it, the particular silence of a thing missing that had been present the night before. He was in the street in under a minute, coat over his nightshirt, breath smoking in the gray air.

The lodging-house over the chandlery stood shuttered and quiet. But the chandler, opening his doors below, jerked his thumb at the river and said the strangest thing he'd seen in thirty years: a man's boots, set neatly side by side on the lowest step of the water-stair, laces tied, pointing out at the current — and no man anywhere to be seen.

No body would ever come ashore. Ines understood that before the sun was fully up. The river had learned, this season, to swallow on request.

He walked back to the workshop slowly, and at the door he took out his day-book, and with hands that did not shake — he was proud of that, later, that they did not shake — he wrote beneath Salko's entry the only sentence the record would ever bear:

*Alive on the twelfth of November. Spoke the truth. Vanished before dawn.*

Three men alive, and now one. And somewhere in the cream-wove paper offices of the Meridian Shipping House, a clerk was drawing a line through a name that had never officially existed — which meant, Ines thought with a clarity like ice forming, that the firm believed its work was finished. That the last witness was a dead man's notebook, hidden under a dead man's cot, kept by a nurse nobody had thought to count.

Let them go on not counting him. For a little longer.

He closed the book, went inside, and began, methodically, to plan how a nobody might get into the Meridian Shipping House and read its files.
