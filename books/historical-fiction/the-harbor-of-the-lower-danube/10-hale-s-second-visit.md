## 10. Hale's Second Visit

The knock came at dawn, and it was not a knock so much as a verdict.

Ines heard it from the cot where he had not slept, and he knew before his hand touched the latch what stood on the other side. There was only one kind of visitor who announced himself at that hour with such perfect confidence — the kind who had already decided how the morning would end.

He opened the door to gray light and gray faces. Magistrate Corvin Hale stood at the center of six soldiers in imperial blue, their bayonets catching the first weak sun off the water, and behind them, at a respectful distance, a clerk with a leather folio under one arm and a warrant held out flat on both palms, as though presenting bread.

"Master Amberdale." Hale's voice was warm as bathwater. "I hope we find you well."

"You find me tired," Ines said. "There is cholera in the quarter."

"There is always something in the quarter." Hale stepped past him without waiting to be asked — a small man, neat as a ledger line, his coat brushed, his boots dry despite the mud everywhere else in Galati. He carried a walking stick he did not need and tapped it once against the doorframe, as if taking possession. "You will forgive the hour. The law keeps its own schedule."

*The law,* Ines thought, *has learned to keep yours.*

He had lied to this man once before, eleven days ago, when Hale had come alone with his soft questions about strays and his offer of coin. Then the visit had been a fisherman's cast — patient, almost idle. This was the haul.

The clerk read the warrant aloud in a voice like a man reciting shipping rates: search of the premises formerly occupied by Anton Vess, shipwright, deceased; authority granted by the office of the magistrate of Galati; all persons found upon the premises to be enumerated. Ines listened with his hands folded, keeping them still, because busy hands were for work and these were not working hours. His rule held even now. Especially now.

"Enumerated," Hale repeated pleasantly, catching the word as it fell. "That means you, Master Amberdale. And anyone else."

"I live alone," Ines said. "As I told you."

"As you told me." Hale smiled. It was a good smile, well-made, the kind a craftsman might put years into. "And yet here we are again. The harbor is a small place, and small places talk. Someone says they saw lamplight in this workshop past midnight. Someone says a boy's coat was hung to dry on your rail. Someone says—" he paused, savoring it, "—that you have been seen carrying food enough for two."

Ines felt the fear arrive, cold and complete, the way floodwater arrives under a door — not suddenly but inevitably, filling the room from the floor up. He let none of it reach his face. He had learned that much in the hospital wards: the dying watch your face more closely than your hands, and if your face breaks, theirs does too.

"People say many things about a man who works alone," he said. "They said worse about Vess, and look where it got him. At the bottom of the harbor."

Something flickered in Hale's eyes at that — quick, unreadable, gone. "Indeed. Look where it got him." He turned to the soldiers. "Begin."

* * *

They searched the way water searches — without malice, without mercy, finding every low place.

Ines stood by the cold stove while they went through the workshop, and he made himself catalog them as he cataloged everything, because cataloging kept the terror in its box. Two soldiers took the tool wall, lifting each plane and auger from its pegs, weighing them, setting them down wrong. One went through Vess's sea chest, shaking out shirts that still smelled of tar and pipe smoke. Another knelt at the sawdust bins, driving his bayonet down into them twice, three times, a butcher testing a carcass. The clerk followed with his folio, writing nothing, because there was nothing yet to write, and the emptiness of the page seemed to offend him.

Miri was thirty feet away.

Thirty feet, one partition wall, and four inches of oak. Ines did not look toward the back of the workshop. Looking was a confession; he had understood that since his first week nursing, when a doctor's glance toward a soldier's bandaged leg told the man he was losing it before any word was spoken. So he looked at the stove instead, at the kettle, at Hale's dry boots, and he fed the fire as though this were an ordinary morning and armed men turned over a dead friend's belongings every day of the week.

But his mind went where his eyes could not.

Vess had built the bulkhead in autumn, Ines was nearly certain — he had found the seam only by accident, running his thumb along the wainscoting while inventorying tools, feeling the joinery that was too fine for a wall that hid nothing. Behind it lay a space perhaps four feet deep between the false panel and the true outer wall: a coffin of good oak, airless, dark, big enough for a boy to crouch in with his knees drawn up, not big enough for him to stand. Ines had measured it with his eyes and hated himself for measuring. He had laid a blanket inside, and a jar of water, and had shown Miri the trick of the hidden catch — a carved rosette that turned a quarter-inch and gave — and the boy had practiced it until his fingers knew it blind, the way children learn anything, quickly and without complaint, as though being hunted were simply another skill the world required of them.

*If they find the seam,* Ines thought, *it is over.* A bayonet driven along the wainscoting would find the hollow the way a finger finds a bruise. Four inches of oak against six soldiers. He had never believed arithmetic could be so cruel.

Hale drifted through the wreckage of the search with his stick tapping, touching things lightly — a lathe, a coil of rope, the dead man's half-carved figurehead leaning in its corner, a woman's face with the features still rough, waiting to be born. He paused before it longer than anywhere else.

"Fine work," he said. "Vess was better with wood than with accounts, I'm told. Debts everywhere. A man drowning slowly long before the river finished the job." He glanced at Ines. "You were fond of him?"

"He paid me in honesty," Ines said. "Which is rarer than coin."

"Mm." Hale set two fingers against the figurehead's cheek, almost tenderly, then withdrew them. "The estate will be settled soon. Meridian House has claims. Creditors have claims. Everyone has claims, and no one has proof of anything except that the man is dead and there is no heir." He said the last words lightly, tasting them. *No heir.* As though he were reading from a document already drawn. Ines thought of the papers he had not yet seen but already knew existed somewhere in a clean office in Bucharest or Galati, awaiting only a signature and a silence.

"The mother?" Ines asked, carefully idle. "Elena Vess. Drowned with the crew, I heard."

"Drowned," Hale agreed. "The river takes whole families in this country. Sad. Convenient." He caught himself, or pretended to. "Efficient, I meant. Grief is efficient here. It spares the courts a great deal of work."

A soldier called out from the loft — a rat, or a shadow, nothing — and Ines's heart slammed once against his ribs and settled. He poured tea he did not want into a tin cup and offered it to Hale, who declined with a small bow, and the refusal hung between them like a blade laid on a table.

Then the searching soldier reached the back wall.

* * *

Later, Ines would remember those minutes in fragments, the way he remembered the worst nights in the ward: not as continuous time but as isolated instants, lit too brightly.

The soldier running his palm along the wainscoting. The sound of his fingernails — Ines could hear them, a faint scratching, a man reading a wall by touch. The clerk looking up. Hale turning his head with the unhurried interest of a man watching weather.

Behind the panel, silence. Miri knew the drill; they had rehearsed it, God help them both, they had *rehearsed* it, the boy folding himself into the dark with the blanket over his head to muffle even breathing, because Ines had told him — had made himself say the words — *if they come, you do not make a sound, not if they strike the wall, not if you cannot breathe, not even then.* And the boy had nodded with those grave dark eyes and never once asked why, because he already knew why. Children of danger are fluent in its grammar.

The soldier knocked on the panel. Once. Twice. Solid oak answered him, honest and dense.

"Hollow places," the soldier said to the sergeant. "Old workshops, they're full of them. Caches for smuggled tobacco, sometimes."

"Then open it," said the sergeant.

Time went strange. Ines watched the soldier step back and lift his boot, and in the interval between the boot rising and the boot falling, his mind performed a series of insane calculations: the distance to the stove iron; whether the window over the timber yard was latched; how many seconds it would take six men to kill a nurse with a poker, and whether those seconds would be enough for an eight-year-old to run. He picked up the poker. He did not know he had done it. His hand had simply decided, some older and stupider part of him voting with the body while the rest stood by helpless.

The boot struck the panel.

Oak boomed. Dust sifted. And the panel held — held because Vess had built it as he built ships, to take punishment, the false face backed by true frame, the hollow behind braced so that knocking returned the dull honest note of solid wood. Ines understood it a heartbeat later and nearly wept with the understanding: the shipwright had known. Had built the hiding place the way he'd built hulls, expecting it to be struck, tested, trusted with a life. *Four inches of oak,* Ines thought, *and forty years of craft.*

Again the boot. Again the boom. The soldier frowned, pressed his ear to the wood, knocked with his knuckles this time, listening like a physician auscultating a chest.

"Solid," he admitted finally, straightening. "Good oak. Old work."

"Move on," said the sergeant.

Hale had not moved at all. He stood by the figurehead, watching Ines — watching, Ines realized with a chill that outdid anything the soldiers had raised in him, not the wall but *him*, reading his face the way Ines read the faces of the dying. And Ines understood, in that moment, the true architecture of his danger: it was not the bayonets, not the warrant, not the four inches of oak. It was this man's patience. Hale did not need to find the boy today. He needed only to keep searching, day after day, legally, politely, until luck or hunger or a neighbor's loose tongue delivered what force could not. The law was not a shield against men like Hale. It was his instrument — a slow, courteous, immaculate machine for dismantling lives, and every gear in it was polished and lawful and turning exactly as designed.

Ines set down the poker. He smiled — he felt his own smile assemble itself, plank by plank, the finest piece of carpentry he had ever done — and said:

"You'll want to see the cellar, Magistrate. It floods. Mind the third step; it's rotten. Vess never fixed it, and I've been meaning to, but my hands are nurses' hands, not shipwrights'."

It was a gamble dressed as courtesy: give them somewhere else to look, somewhere genuinely disappointing, and let disappointment do the work of concealment. Hale considered him for a long moment — long enough that Ines counted his own heartbeats and lost count — and then inclined his head.

"Yes," Hale said softly. "Let's see the cellar."

* * *

The cellar gave them mud, standing water to the ankles, and a drowned crate of caulking pitch. The soldiers searched it anyway, thoroughly, miserably, their boots sucking at the muck, and came up with nothing but curses and one drowned rat that the clerk recorded, with visible satisfaction, as *evidence of disrepair.*

By nine o'clock it was over. The warrant was folded. The folio closed. Hale stood in the doorway with the gray morning behind him and the wrecked workshop at his back, and he looked at Ines with that warm, terrible patience.

"You're a careful man, Master Amberdale," he said. "Careful men are useful. Or careful men are obstacles. It often comes down to very small choices." He tapped his stick once on the threshold — the same tap, Ines noted, as at the doorframe, the surveyor marking his boundary. "The estate settles within the month. After that, none of this will matter to anyone. I'd think about that, if I were you. About how little time remains for a boy who doesn't exist."

And he was gone, down the muddy lane, the soldiers falling in behind him, the clerk last, closing the gate with exaggerated care as though the property were already forfeit.

Ines stood in the doorway until the footsteps faded into the harbor sounds — gulls, hammers, the groan of barges — and then he shut the door, shot the bolt, and leaned his forehead against the wet cold wood.

* * *

The rosette turned. The panel swung. Miri came out of the dark stiff-legged, blinking, sawdust in his hair, and for a moment he simply stood there, a small ghost returned from a coffin he had entered willingly. Then his composure broke — not into tears, Ines noticed, but into motion: he crossed the room and began, silently, furiously, righting the fallen tools, hanging the planes back on their pegs, squaring the sea chest, restoring order with the desperate competence of a child who had learned that disorder invites attention.

Ines knelt and stilled his hands.

"It's over," he said quietly. "They're gone."

The boy looked at him. He did not speak — he had never spoken, not once in all these weeks — but his eyes asked the question that speech would have: *again? will they come again?*

"Yes," Ines answered honestly, because the boy deserved honesty more than comfort. "They'll come again. Maybe not him, maybe not soon. But yes."

Miri absorbed this the way stone absorbs rain, showing nothing, and went back to the tools. Ines watched him and felt something harden in his own chest, layer over layer, like lacquer
