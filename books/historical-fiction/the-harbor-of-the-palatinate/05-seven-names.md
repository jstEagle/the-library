## 5. Seven Names

Fetter Lane smelled of ink and cold iron, and by the third visit I had stopped noticing the smell and started to need it.

Elias Vane kept his shop like a man who expected it to be taken from him at any hour — type sorted into cases that could be carried, paper stacked in bundles light enough to run with, the heavy press bolted to the floor as though he meant to defend it. Perhaps he did. There was a bar across the back door and a second one beneath the counter, and when I came in off the street on the last Tuesday of October, shaking rain from my hat, he looked up from his composing stick with the expression of a man who had been waiting for me without admitting it.

"You're late," he said.

"The roads are flooded past Aldgate. Lady Harrow's carriage took water to the axle."

"Lady Harrow's carriage," he repeated, and there was something in the way he said it — not mockery exactly, but a weighing — that made me set my jaw. "And does her ladyship know where her driver spends her afternoons?"

"Her ladyship knows I take the carriage for maintenance on Tuesdays. The farrier's boy will swear to it if anyone asks, because I pay him to."

He almost smiled. It was the nearest he ever came in those first weeks: a twitch at the corner of his mouth, quickly mastered, as though smiling were a luxury he had given up along with other things. He wiped his hands on his apron and said, "Then sit down, Miss Pemberton, because I've finished what I promised you, and you're not going to like it."

I sat on the stool by the stove. He went to the back of the shop, to a drawer he had never opened in my presence, and returned with a sheet of paper folded twice. He did not hand it over at once. He held it flat between us, the way a doctor holds a chart.

"I told you I'd written to everyone my brother Tom ever mentioned. Everyone who was on the dock that morning in June of 1910, or who lived close enough to have seen it, or who buried someone who did." His voice was level, but his thumb pressed white against the paper's edge. "Thirty-one letters. Fourteen came back undeliverable — men moved on, lodgings gone, names wrong. Nine never answered. Five answered and asked me never to write again." He paused. "Three answered properly."

"Three," I said. "Out of thirty-one."

"Three still living, so far as anyone can find. Three willing to say what they saw." He turned the paper around and laid it on the counter between us, and I read the list he had compiled in his small, hard printer's hand:

*Doyle, Margaret (widow), lodging house of Mrs. Cahill, Cable Street, Stepney.*
*Croft, William (formerly sergeant, 2nd Battalion), last known Portsmouth.*
*Okafor, Joseph (stoker), Limehouse.*

"Tom wrote about Maggie Doyle," Elias said quietly. "She pulled him behind a bollard when the firing started. She'd have been the closest witness of all of them — close enough to see the officers' faces."

I looked at the three names and felt the weight of them settle somewhere under my ribs. Three names. Seven men dead and officially drowned, an inquest thinned to a pamphlet, a government historian assembling a tidy account of a regrettable incident — and against all of it, three living voices, scattered between Stepney, Portsmouth, and Limehouse, held together by nothing but a dead man's correspondence and a printer's stubbornness.

"And the others?" I asked. "The nine who didn't answer, the five who refused?"

"The five who refused all used the same phrase." He recited it flatly, without inflection, and I understood that he had made himself memorize it precisely because it hurt. "'I've said all I'm going to say.' Word for word, Miss Pemberton. Dockers and widows and a retired pilot, men and women who never met each other, all arriving at the same sentence like it was printed for them."

The stove ticked. Somewhere above us a cart rattled over the cobbles and the whole shop shivered faintly, type ringing against type in its case.

"They've been visited," I said.

"They've been *interviewed*," Elias corrected. "That's the word the man used, according to the pilot's daughter, who answered his letter for him. A gentleman from a government office, very courteous, taking down their accounts for the historical record. Assuring them their words would be treated with respect." He picked up the folded sheet and put it away again, back in its drawer, back under lock. "You don't frighten witnesses if you want their testimony to serve you. You flatter it. You write it down yourself, in your own words, and then their words are yours forever."

I thought of the letter I had steamed open in Lady Constance's parlor — the government crest, the name Harrow beside the word Wapping, the mention of a Mr. Ambrose Fenn of the Historical Section who was preparing the official account. I had not yet told Elias about Fenn. I had told him almost nothing about how I came by what I knew, and every day I delayed, the omission grew heavier, until carrying it felt like carrying the carriage itself.

"There is a man," I said slowly, "named Fenn. Ambrose Fenn. He corresponds with people I — with people connected to the family I drive for. He is preparing the official history of the strike. Of the incident."

Elias went very still. "Connected how?"

"That isn't mine to tell. Not yet." I met his eyes and did not look away, though everything in me wanted to. "You'll have to trust me a little longer, Mr. Vane. I know how that sounds. I know what I'm asking."

"What you're asking," he said, "is that I trust the driver of a woman whose family name appears beside a massacre, because she says she'll explain later." But he didn't turn me out. He stood there with his ink-stained hands hanging at his sides, and after a long moment he said, "My brother trusted people too easily. I've spent three years learning to be careful. And here I am, handing lists to a stranger because she reads like someone who means it." He exhaled through his nose. "Fenn. Write down everything you can remember about him. Every letter, every date, every phrase. If he's steering the survivors, I want to see the wheel."

* * *

I wrote it that night in my room above the mews, by candlelight, in the shorthand I use for things that must never be found in plain hand. Fenn's letters to Lady Constance were models of their kind — courteous, scholarly, faintly self-deprecating, the prose of a man who wished only to get the record right. *The truth requires no embellishment,* he had written. *A tragedy honestly told honors the dead more than any polemic.* And beneath the courtesy, always, the same gentle machinery: *Lady Harrow will recall that the crowd was warned. That the strikers had broken windows at the Custom House. That the soldiers were young and frightened.* Each sentence true, perhaps, in isolation. Arranged together, they built a different June than the one Maggie Doyle had lived through.

That was the thing I could not stop turning over as I lay awake listening to the horses shift below: Fenn was not lying. Not once, not anywhere, in anything I had read. He was *arranging*. He interviewed the survivors and wrote down what they said and then he placed what they said inside a frame that made the shooting inevitable, regrettable, nobody's decision — weather, essentially. History as a well-made argument. And the witnesses signed their names to it because he was kind to them and because the alternative was to believe that their own words had been taken and turned against the dead.

Three names, Elias had said. And Fenn had already reached at least six of the others.

I got up and dressed in the dark. There is a particular restlessness that comes from knowing something is being lost while you sleep — pages razored, memories steered, old women persuaded — and I could not lie still under the weight of it. I lit the candle again and wrote to Elias in ordinary hand, one line only: *Which of the three is closest?*

His answer came by the morning post, which meant he had risen earlier than I had, which meant he had not slept either. One line in return: *Doyle. Cable Street. And my correspondent says she is dying.*

* * *

We went together on Thursday, which required a fiction elaborate enough that I blushed composing it: the carriage to be inspected at a Stepney farrier's of Lady Constance's acquaintance — a fiction with just enough truth in it to survive scrutiny, since the farrier did exist and did owe me a favor. I left the Daimler in his yard and walked the last half mile with Elias through streets where the fog sat low and yellow and the costers' barrows were lit by hurricane lamps before noon.

Cable Street was narrower than its name suggested, the houses leaning together overhead like conspirators. Mrs. Cahill's lodging house was the one with a Virgin in the window and washing strung across the upper floor like rigging. Elias knocked; a girl of perhaps ten opened the door on a chain, assessed us both with a single sweep of dark eyes, and shouted into the interior, "Ma! It's the printer and a toff in a good coat!"

"That'll be me," I said.

"It's the coat," the girl agreed, and let us in.

Mrs. Doyle was propped up in a bed on the third floor, in a room that smelled of camphor and coal smoke and the sweetish undertone of illness that I had learned to recognize during my mother's last year. She was smaller than I had imagined — a sparrow of a woman with white hair cut short for convenience and hands folded on the blanket like folded gloves. Her eyes, though. Her eyes went straight past Elias and fixed on me, and stayed.

"You're not from the office," she said.

"No, ma'am."

"The other one was from the office. Polite gentleman. Came twice." She coughed, a deep wet sound that seemed to come up from her boots. "Wrote down every word I said and thanked me for my service to posterity. Posterity." She said the word the way you'd spit out a pip. "Me daughter Nellie gave him tea and he complimented her scones. Then he reads it back to me, what I'd said, only it wasn't quite what I'd said. Little changes. The warning bell I heard — now I'd only *thought* I heard it. The officer giving the order — now I couldn't be *sure*, at my age, who gave what order. Your memory's a funny thing at my time of life, he says, very kind, and writes his version in his book, and asks me to sign."

"Did you sign?" Elias asked. His voice had gone rough.

"I signed the first page," she said. "Before I understood. I didn't sign the rest. He wasn't pleased, but there's nothing a polite man can do about an old woman's tiredness." She looked at me again, that unnerving direct gaze. "Now. You're not from the office, and you're not press, because press carry notebooks and this one—" a nod at Elias "—carries grief on him like cheap soap. So who are you?"

I knelt by the bed, because standing over a dying woman felt like another kind of interview, and I said, "My name is Sebastian Pemberton. I keep papers for people. And I've come to ask whether you'll let me write down what you saw — exactly what you saw, in your words, read back to you line by line until you agree every word is yours — and then let me keep those pages somewhere no polite gentleman will ever reach them."

She was quiet a long moment. Down in the street a barrel organ ground out something cheerful and died away.

"You'll read it back," she said. "Every line."

"Every line. As many times as you require."

"And you won't soften it. Even the parts that make the soldiers out human, because they were human, poor lads, most of them younger than my Tom. They were frightened and they fired anyway, and both of those things are true, and your gentleman's version keeps only the frightened part."

"I won't soften anything," I said. "Not in either direction."

Something in her face eased, some tension I hadn't known was there. She looked at Elias. "Your brother was Thomas Vane. Bollard, near the gate. I've prayed for him every night for three years, son, because I got him down behind that iron and I couldn't hold on when they came through clearing the street, and they took him up standing and he was on his feet when—" She stopped. Her hands turned over on the blanket, palms up, empty. "I never knew if he'd have wanted me to say it out loud."

"He spent three years trying to say it out loud," Elias said. "In letters. To everyone. You'd have been doing it for him."

Mrs. Doyle nodded slowly, as if settling an account. Then she said, "Nellie! Paper, girl, and my spectacles, and mind you don't bring me the pencil, I'll have ink if I'm signing my name to history." And to me, with a ghost of the woman she must have been at a picket line: "Well, miss. Fetch your chair up close. We'll do it proper."

* * *

It took four hours. I wrote in my clearest hand while she talked in a voice that gained strength as it went, as though the words themselves were a meal she had been too long without. She gave me the morning of the ninth of June, 1910, hour by hour: the strike meeting on the quay, the numbers, the mood; the arrival of the militia in two wagons; the reading of the Riot Act and the way the crowd's noise swallowed half of it; the single order — she named the officer, she described his voice, she imitated the cadence of it — and then the volley, and the seven men going down, and the hours afterward when the wounded were carried into the customs shed and the sheds were closed, and what the carts took away at dawn wrapped in sailcloth, bound for a boat whose name she had learned only later, when the newspapers printed the word *drowned* and she had stood in her kitchen and understood.

I read every line back. She corrected me twice — once for a word she hadn't used, once for an emphasis I had misplaced — and initialed each page at the bottom margin, and signed the last page with a signature that shook and did not hesitate.

When it was done she lay back exhausted, and Nellie brought bread and cheese, and we ate sitting on the end of the bed like family, which neither of us was and both of us, in that room, briefly were.

At the door, Mrs. Doyle caught my wrist. Her grip was stronger than the rest of her.

"The polite gentleman," she said. "He said he'd come again next week, for the pages I hadn't signed. He said he'd bring a solicitor, to make it all regular." Her eyes searched mine. "Whatever you're doing, miss — do it faster than he does."

I walked back through the fog with the pages sewn into the lining of my coat, feeling them against my ribs like a second heartbeat, and Elias silent beside me, and the whole city dripping and indifferent around us. At the corner of Cable Street he stopped.

"She's right," he said. "Fenn has a schedule and we don't. He's got Croft in Portsmouth and Okafor in Limehouse and however many of the silent nine he cares to revisit, and every week he works, our account gets thinner and his gets thicker." He looked at me, and in the gaslight his face was stripped of all its guardedness; what was underneath was simply a brother, three years late. "How fast can you move?"

I thought of the mews, of Lady Constance's warm voice calling me *child*, of Sir Edmund's nod across the breakfast table, of the locked drawer in a parlor where the Harrow name sat beside the word Wapping in a government hand. I thought of the farrier expecting his carriage back by four.

"Faster than is safe," I said. "Starting tomorrow. Which of the two — Croft or Okafor?"

"Croft," Elias said. "Portsmouth. An ex-sergeant who watched his own men fire and never forgave it — or never forgave himself, which is worse. My correspondent says he drinks." He hesitated. "Sebastian. If Fenn reaches him first—"

"Then we go tonight," I said, "and to hell with the farrier."

But even as I said it, even with the pages warm against my body and the clock finally running, some colder part of me was already counting what tomorrow would cost: the questions, the lies, the widening gap between the woman the Harrows believed they employed and the one walking through Stepney fog with a dead man's testimony sewn into her coat. The race had acquired its clock. What I did not yet know was how many of the people I loved would be running on the other side of it.
