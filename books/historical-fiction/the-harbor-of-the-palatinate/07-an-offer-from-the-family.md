## 7. An Offer from the Family

Sir Edmund Harrow had learned, over sixty years of public life, that the most effective threats were never spoken. A man who said *I know what you did* had already lost; a man who arranged circumstances so the knowledge was understood had won before the conversation began. He had practiced this art in boardrooms and committee rooms, in the smoking rooms of clubs where the empire was administered between courses, and he intended to practice it now, in his own study, upon a chauffeuse.

He stood at the window while he waited, hands clasped behind him, watching the September light fail over the park. The elms were turning early this year. He found himself cataloguing the fact with the same part of his mind that catalogued everything — tonnages, majorities, liabilities — because there was comfort in inventory, and he needed comfort. What he was about to do sat poorly even on a conscience as well-armored as his.

Not because it was cruel. He had done crueler things and slept soundly after them; Wapping itself had not cost him a night's sleep, though the historians were proving more expensive than the militia had been. No — what troubled him was that the girl deserved better than what he was about to do, and he knew it, and he was going to do it anyway. That was the whole of statesmanship, in the end: knowing precisely whom you were spending, and spending them regardless.

*Sebastian Pemberton.* He turned the name over. Three years in his household. Punctual, silent, observant — too observant, though he had only confirmed it eleven days ago, when the man from the Home Office — Caudle, a bloodless little instrument with ink-stained cuffs — laid three photographs on Edmund's desk and said, in the tone of a man reporting weather, that Miss Pemberton had developed a habit with the post.

The photographs were excellent work. Sebastian at the hall table, a letter flat against the kettle's steam. Sebastian's thumb under the flap. Sebastian reading, her lips moving slightly — she read the way some people prayed, with her whole body bent toward the page. In the third photograph she was copying something into a small notebook, and her face wore an expression Edmund recognized because he had seen it in mirrors across five decades: the expression of a person who has just become dangerous without yet knowing it.

"She files things," Caudle had said. "Dates, names, sums. We don't know for whom. Possibly for no one. Possibly for herself."

"And Lady Constance?"

"Her ladyship's correspondence is, of course, her own affair." Caudle's delicacy was its own kind of accusation. Everyone in this business understood that Constance's letters were a warehouse of the movement's secrets, and everyone had decided, for reasons of policy, to pretend otherwise. Edmund sometimes wondered how many quiet arrangements like that held up the country — how much of England was load-bearing pretense.

"Does my wife know?" he had asked.

"I couldn't say, sir."

Which meant yes, or meant Caudle didn't know, and either answer was intolerable, so Edmund had filed the question away with the others.

Now he heard the motor come round the side of the house, the particular low complaint of the Daimler settling onto gravel, and a minute later footsteps in the corridor — unhurried, even. She didn't scurry. He'd noticed that about her from the first week. Servants scurried; Sebastian arrived.

"Come in," he called, before she could knock. "Sit down, if you please."

She entered the way she did everything — composed, watchful, her cap straight, her gloves tucked under one arm. She took the chair opposite the desk but perched on its edge rather than sinking into it, a woman keeping her weight ready. Good, Edmund thought. Instinct. You'll need it.

"You wanted to see me, sir."

"I did." He let the pause stretch just long enough to be felt. "First, I want to thank you. My wife speaks of you constantly — you know that, I imagine. These last years have been difficult ones for her health and her spirits, and you've been steadier company than any companion we might have hired. I don't say that lightly. This family is not easy to serve."

Something moved behind her eyes — not suspicion exactly, but a recalculation. She had expected a rebuke about the motor, perhaps, or the coal accounts. He watched her decide to accept the thanks at face value, provisionally, the way one accepts change from a stranger.

"That's kind of you, sir. Her ladyship is—" She stopped, chose. "It's no hardship, serving her."

"No," Edmund agreed. "I believe it isn't." He came around the desk and settled against its front edge rather than behind it — a calculated informality; men trusted a man who leaned. "That's rather what I want to speak to you about. Service. Its rewards, and its — duration."

"Sir?"

"My wife will not always need a driver." He said it gently, looking out the window rather than at her, letting her watch his profile do the work. "And you are — forgive me — thirty-one years old, with no pension, no property, and a trade that ruins knees and lungs by fifty. I've made inquiries." He hadn't, actually; Caudle had, and the file was thorough. "You have a widowed mother in York who writes to you monthly. You send her two pounds a month, which is generous on your wages, which is to say it is unsustainable. You own one good coat and you keep your savings in a Post Office account under a name that is nearly yours."

He saw the stillness take her then — the absolute, disciplined stillness of a woman understanding that the ground had shifted beneath her feet and refusing to give it the satisfaction of a stumble. He pressed on, softly, because softness was the blade here.

"The cottage at Marlow. You know it — the gamekeeper's old place, empty since spring. It wants a roof and a garden and nothing else. I mean to put it in order. And I mean to offer it, with a permanent post — not driver, that would waste you — but steward of the household accounts, and of my wife's correspondence. A salary of ninety pounds a year. For life, if you want it. There are women in this country who would call that a fortune, and they would be right."

The silence went on so long he counted the clock in the hall striking nothing at all — the thing was stopped, had been stopped since June, another item for inventory.

"Why?" she said at last. Simply. No tremor in it. "Why me, sir? Why now?"

*Because you open our mail,* he thought. *Because you have a notebook full of dates and names and sums, and somewhere in it, God knows how deep, sits Wapping. Because Caudle wants me to dismiss you tonight and hand you to a magistrate on some invented charge, and I have spent three days persuading him that a girl who vanishes becomes a martyr, while a girl who prospers becomes a hostage.*

What he said was: "Because loyalty should be rewarded before it needs to be tested. That's a principle I've held all my life, and it has served this family through three generations."

He watched her weigh it. She was cleverer than Caudle gave her credit for — Caudle thought her a clerk's daughter playing at espionage; Edmund suspected she was something rarer, a person who kept faith with facts. Which was precisely the problem.

"It's very generous," she said carefully.

"It is," he agreed pleasantly. "Generosity usually is."

He crossed to the sideboard and poured two glasses of the Madeira — the good Bual, not the table stuff — and brought her one. Their fingers did not touch; he saw her arrange it so, a half-inch of calculation in the transfer, and admired it.

"Miss Pemberton," he said, and now he allowed himself to look at her directly, and let her see him look. "There is a matter I should mention, because I dislike debts going unacknowledged in either direction. This house receives a great deal of post. Some of it is confidential — political, personal, matters touching on people whose reputations are not theirs alone to spend. My wife's correspondents trust this address. I trust this address." He sipped. "It has come to my attention — through channels I won't bore you with — that the seals on certain letters have been, shall we say, less than robust of late. Steam is a wonderful servant. It asks no questions and remembers nothing."

The color left her face and returned, managed, banked down to something like composure. She set the untouched glass on the desk with a small precise click.

"Sir, I—"

"Please." He raised one hand, and the gentleness of it was the cruelty of it. "I'm not angry. I want you to understand that I am not angry. Curiosity is not a sin; in a woman of your intelligence it may even be a duty. But letters are like doors, Miss Pemberton. One opens them, and after that one is responsible for what comes through. Whatever you have read in this house — whatever you have copied into that notebook of yours" — he nodded toward her jacket, and saw her hand twitch toward the pocket and master itself — "has stayed within these walls, so far as I am concerned. It stays there, and none of it has ever happened, and you are the daughter of this house in every way that matters. That is what the cottage means. That is what the ninety pounds means. It means a life in which the past is past, and kept."

He let it sit. Outside, a rook crossed the grey sky toward the park, and somewhere below a gardener's shears ticked through a hedge, patient as a metronome.

"And if I decline, sir?"

Ah. There it was. He had known she would ask, because she was honest, and honesty always asked the question that makes everything worse.

"Decline?" He smiled — warmly, he hoped; he had a good smile, he'd been told, the smile of a man who chaired charities. "My dear girl, there is nothing to decline. An old man offers a young woman security, and she thinks about it, and either way the world goes on. If you'd rather stay on as driver, stay on as driver — the wages would improve, naturally. If you'd rather seek a position elsewhere, I'll write you the finest reference any servant ever carried out of an English door." He paused, and let the warmth drain from his voice by a single degree, no more. "Only consider that references travel, and so do inquiries, and a young woman with a notebook full of other people's secrets might find that the world beyond these walls is considerably less forgiving of curiosity than I am. I am protecting you, Miss Pemberton. Do try to see it that way. It happens to be true."

"Is it, sir?" Quiet. Steady. Her chin had come up a fraction — the same fractional defiance he had seen in photographs of suffragettes being dragged from Whitehall, and he felt, absurdly, a flicker of respect, and crushed it, because respect was a luxury he could not extend to her this morning.

"It is the truth as I am able to offer it," he said. "Which, at my age, is the only kind there is."

She stood. She had not touched the Madeira. She smoothed her skirt once, and when she spoke her voice was level as a spirit gauge.

"I'll consider the position, Sir Edmund. Thank you for your confidence in me."

"Take all the time you need," he said graciously. "But not more than a day or two. Decisions ferment, and sour." He opened the study door for her himself — a gesture no baronet owed a chauffeuse, and both of them knew what it cost and what it purchased. At the threshold she paused, half-turned, and for a moment he thought she would say something unforgivable, something true, and he braced himself for it almost hopefully.

What she said was: "Her ladyship will be wanting the car at four. The library, then Mrs. Fenton's in Marylebone."

"Yes," he said. "Of course."

And she was gone, her footsteps fading down the corridor, even and unhurried, a woman who had arrived and departed as servants do, carrying everything and showing nothing.

Edmund stood a long moment in the doorway, listening to the house breathe. Then he went back to his desk, unlocked the bottom drawer, and drew out the thin folder Caudle had left — the Wapping summary, the historian's tidy pages, the list of names with their statuses annotated in a clerk's hand: *deceased, deceased, deceased, emigrated, deceased, unreliable, located.*

Seven names. Four struck through. Two dead soon, if the doctors were right. One remaining, and Croft was drinking himself toward the grave in Portsmouth at a rate Caudle described as "convenient."

He read the list twice, the way he read all documents, hunting the flaw. Then he took up his pen and wrote a note to Caudle, four lines only:

*The girl has been offered terms. She will accept or she will be manageable regardless. Proceed with the Portsmouth matter this week — quietly. And find out what she copies, and for whom. If it is the printer on Fetter Lane, tell me at once.*

He blotted it, sealed it with wax — his own seal, unbroken, unsteamed — and rang for the footman.

Outside the window the rook had gone, and the first drops of rain were stippling the glass, and somewhere in the house a girl who read other people's letters was telling his wife that the car would be ready at four, and smiling, and keeping her weight ready, and he found — to his surprise, and briefly, and never again — that he wished her luck.

* * *

In the corridor, Sebastian walked evenly until she reached the back stairs, and there she stopped, one hand flat against the cold plaster, and breathed.

*A day or two,* he had said. She had perhaps forty hours in which to decide whether to sell her silence for a cottage or keep it and lose everything — and he had told her, in his gentle terrible way, that losing everything might mean more than dismissal.

She thought of the notebook against her ribs. Of Maggie Doyle's name on Elias's list. Of the letter with the government crest, and the word *Wapping*, and the Harrow name beside it.

Then she heard, below her, the front door opening, and a voice she did not know asking for Sir Edmund — a dry, courteous voice, the voice of a man from a ministry — and she pressed herself into the stairwell shadow and listened, and memorized, and began, right there on the servants' stairs, to count her hours differently.
