## 9. The Archive at Kew

The forgery took me three evenings and cost me more sleep than the work itself deserved.

I had learned to copy Lady Constance's hand years ago — not from any dark design, but because she was forever dashing off notes in the carriage and asking me to finish them while she dictated the rest to her secretary. A woman who drives for a suffragist learns her employer's letters the way a priest learns Latin: by repetition, by necessity, until the shapes of them live in your wrist. The *H* that leaned like a tired woman against its neighbor. The way she crossed her *t*s with an impatient slash, as though time itself were an imposition. I could write Constance Harrow in my sleep.

What I could not do, at first, was write Lord Ashcombe.

He was a retired under-secretary of some department whose name changed every few years like a man avoiding creditors, and he had written to Lady Constance twice that autumn on matters of mutual reformist interest. His hand was small, dry, and precise — the handwriting of a man who had spent thirty years initialing memoranda he did not read. I practiced it on the backs of laundry lists, on the flyleaf of a railway timetable, on the fogged window of the carriage house while rain ran down the glass outside. Elias supplied me with the letterhead — a single sheet, cream-colored, bearing the crest of a learned society that existed mostly as a dining club for men who believed history was a finished thing requiring only occasional dusting.

*To the Keeper of the Public Records,* my forged letter began. *I have the honour to introduce Miss S. Pemberton, who is engaged in research touching upon the administration of the Thames district, anno 1910–11, and who requires access to certain coroner's files for purposes of scholarly comparison.*

*Scholarly comparison.* Even now the phrase makes me smile with something between admiration and disgust. It is astonishing what a man will permit when you speak to him in his own liturgy. I did not ask for the Wapping file. You do not ask for the thing you want; you ask for the drawer it lives in, and you let him open the drawer himself.

Elias read the finished letter twice, holding it to the gaslight in the back room at Fetter Lane, ink still sharp-smelling on the page.

"You've done this before," he said. Not accusing. Curious, the way a man is curious about a scar.

"I've driven before," I said. "It's the same skill. You learn where people are looking and you put yourself somewhere else."

He laughed — a short, surprised sound, as if the laugh had gotten out without permission — and set the letter down flat, smoothing it with both hands as though it were something alive that might startle.

"Thursday," he said. "The reading rooms close at four. And Sebastian—" He hesitated, and I watched him decide not to say whatever he had been about to say. "Wear gloves. Old paper takes offense at fingers."

---

Kew in November is a study in respectable desolation. The river runs brown and patient past the green lawns, and the great building stands with its Portland stone face turned modestly away from the water, as though archives, too, had reputations to keep. I went down by train in a gray coat and a hat chosen specifically because no one remembers a hat like that. In my bag: the forged reference, a pencil (pens were discouraged, which told me everything about how much they feared amendment), and beneath a folded newspaper, the camera.

The camera was Elias's — a half-plate folding thing, older than either of us, with a bellows that wheezed like a consumptive when extended. We had rehearsed in the back room at Fetter Lane until two in the morning: how long an exposure needed by lamplight, how to shield the plate under my coat, how to look bored. Boredom, Elias said, was the great camouflage of the age. No one searches a bored woman.

The attendant at the desk was a thin man with a cold and the resigned manner of someone who had spent his working life fetching boxes for people who would never understand what they were given. He read my reference with the attention of a man reading a bus schedule.

"Ashcombe," he said. "Society. Yes." He made a mark in a ledger. "Coroner's files, Thames district. You'll want the index first."

"I should think so," I said, and was rewarded with the ghost of a nod — one professional acknowledging another's competence in the liturgy.

The index was a fat volume bound in marbled boards gone soft at the corners, and I carried it to a table by the window where the light was good and the nearest reader was a clergyman so deep in ecclesiastical correspondence that the Second Coming could have arrived without disturbing him. I ran my finger down the columns. *Inquests held, River and Dockside, 1910.* There it was, in a clerk's upright hand:

*Wapping — St. Saviour's Dock — fire and fatalities — 14 October 1910 — File Cor/1910/W-41.*

Seven names were listed in the margin beside the word *deceased*. I had seen those names before, in Elias's brother's letters, in Maggie Doyle's testimony, in the list Elias kept folded in his breast pocket like a prayer. But seeing them here — entered, indexed, filed, *official* — did something to me that I had not prepared for. My hand shook, just slightly, turning the page.

They had been counted. That was the terrible thing. Someone had stood over their bodies with a ledger and written them down, and for one bright moment in October 1910, seven dead dockworkers had been facts. The state had looked at them and recorded what it saw.

Which meant the state had also, at some point since, looked again — and decided otherwise.

* * *

The box came up on a trolley wheeled by a boy who sneezed through the entire delivery. Inside, tied with the flat government tape that always reminds me of tourniquets, were the folders. I waited until the boy had gone, until the clergyman had turned a page, until the attendant had settled back into his chair and his handkerchief, and then I cut the tape with my thumbnail and opened the file.

I knew within thirty seconds. Any reader knows. A thinned file has a feel to it, the way a picked pocket has a feel — a lightness where weight should be, a looseness where stitching should hold. The folder for Cor/1910/W-41 was perhaps a third as thick as its neighbors. When I fanned the contents, they slid apart too easily, like cards from a deck someone has dealt from all evening.

And there were the wounds themselves. Not torn — razored. Cut out with a blade, close and clean along the spine, so that only a pale stripe remained where each page had been, a scar of unyellowed paper in the middle of the tanned leaf. I counted eleven such scars. Eleven pages, excised. Witness depositions, most likely. The coroner's own notes, possibly. Whatever had been written down in the days after the shooting, whatever had been *counted*, had been removed with a straight-edge and a steady hand.

In place of the missing pages, near the front of the file, lay a typescript of four leaves, crisp and white among the foxed documents like a new coin dropped in a beggar's cup.

*Memorandum: Summary of Proceedings, Inquest touchinge the Occurrence at St. Saviour's Dock, Wapping, 14th October 1910.*

I read it standing up, because sitting felt suddenly unsafe, as if the chair might be implicated too.

The summary was beautiful. That is the only honest word for it. It was composed in the smooth administrative English that can make anything sound like weather — regrettable, isolated, arising from misunderstanding, resolved without further incident. The militia had been summoned, it said, *in aid of the civil power*, following disturbances. Shots had been discharged, *the number and circumstances whereof could not subsequently be established with precision*. Seven men had died; the verdict returned had been *death by misadventure*, the jury having heard *such evidence as was available*.

*Such evidence as was available.*

I put my gloved fingertip against that phrase and pressed until the glove dimpled. Such evidence as was available. As though evidence were tide-borne, washed in or washed out by chance. As though eleven pages had simply failed to arrive, the way a letter fails, the way a witness fails, the way Maggie Doyle's husband had failed to come home.

At the foot of the last leaf, a signature: *R. G. Calloway, Historical Section.* And beneath it, in smaller type, a line that chilled me more than anything else in the file, more than the razored scars, more than the whole tidy architecture of omission:

*Prepared for reference purposes. Supersedes original documentation where inconsistent.*

Supersedes. I stood in that reading room with the river running gray outside the window and understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, like cold water taken into the lungs, that I had been thinking about this all wrong. I had imagined — when I had imagined anything beyond the next errand, the next sealed letter — that a cover-up was a deed done once, in a night, by frightened men with lanterns and shovels. A crime with a scene, a moment, a smoking gun that could be found if only one searched hard enough.

But this was not a shovel. This was a *method*. Somewhere there was a room — Calloway's Historical Section, with its brass nameplate and its reference purposes — where men sat at desks under good light and rewrote October 1910 the way other men drafted tariffs. They did not burn the record. Burning leaves ash, and ash invites questions. They *edited* it. They left the file fat enough to look honest and hollow enough to say nothing. They wrote a summary so reasonable, so gently regretful, that a researcher fifty years hence would read it and feel he had been told the truth — because it sounded exactly like truth sounds when the telling is done by power.

You cannot fight a man like that. A man can be exposed, ruined, hanged even. But a method — a method just goes on working, quiet as a clerk, correct as a clock, producing its tidy supersessions year after year, and the dead stay dead and unrecorded, and the living are told that no record exists of what they saw with their own eyes.

I am not a violent woman. I want that entered somewhere, since I have learned that things must be entered somewhere to be true. But standing over that memorandum I experienced something I can only call fury of the cold kind — not the hot flood that makes a person shout, but a slow freezing from the feet upward, a fury with excellent posture and perfect patience. Very well, I thought. You have your method. I will have mine.

* * *

Photographing the file was the loneliest work I have ever done, and I have sat all night in a Stepney lodging house listening to a dying woman describe muzzle flash.

The reading room emptied gradually as the afternoon wore on. The clergyman left at three. Two students, arguing in whispers about some medieval bishop, left at half past. By twenty to four only the attendant remained, dozing upright behind his desk with his handkerchief spread over his knee like a fallen flag of surrender.

I worked fast and low. The camera came out of the newspaper in three movements we had drilled until my hands did it without consulting me. Bellows out. Plate in. I laid the surviving documents flat under the window light — the coroner's covering report, the thin remnant of a juror's list, a police sergeant's statement that had somehow escaped the razor, perhaps because it said nothing, perhaps because someone had missed it — and bent over the ground glass in the attitude of a woman taking notes.

The exposures took eight seconds each by that light. Eight seconds is nothing; eight seconds is also a very long time to stand motionless over stolen documents while a coughing attendant sleeps ten feet behind you. On the fourth plate my hands were steady but my heart was behaving like a trapped bird, and I found myself thinking absurdly of Constance — of the way she holds her chin when she is afraid, lifted, offering her throat to whatever comes, daring it. I lifted my chin. I made the exposure.

Then, because I could not help myself, because some part of me insisted that the record must be complete even if the record was only mine, I photographed the scars themselves. The pale stripes where the pages had been. Eleven absences, documented. Let Calloway's method meet its mirror: I would preserve not only what remained but the shape of what was taken. An inventory of the wound.

I packed the camera away, retied the government tape in a bow slightly neater than the one I'd cut, and carried the box back to the desk. The attendant woke, stamped my slip, and told me the society's members usually came on Tuesdays. I thanked him and said I would bear it in mind.

Outside, the light was going amber over the river, and the tide was low, exposing the black ribs of old barges rotting in the mud — Wapping's dead, I thought, everywhere you looked, if you knew how to see. I walked to the station with the plates wrapped against my body, inside my coat, over my heart, feeling them there like a second skeleton.

On the platform, waiting for the train, I took out Elias's list and read the seven names yet again. Then I turned the paper over, and on the blank side, in pencil, in my own hand — my hand, not Constance's, not Ashcombe's, not anyone's forged liturgy — I wrote:

*Calloway, R. G., Historical Section. Supersedes where inconsistent. Find the originals. Find who ordered the razor.*

Because here is what I had understood on that platform, watching the brown river carry the day away: the summary was not the end of the trail. It was a confession wearing a clerk's collar. Men do not razor eleven pages out of an inquest file for reference purposes. They do it on instruction. Which meant that somewhere above R. G. Calloway and his tidy typescript there was a name — a minister, a commissioner, a gentleman with a country seat — someone who had decided that seven dead men at St. Saviour's Dock were *inconsistent*, and must be superseded.

I had spent my life believing history happened elsewhere, to important people, in rooms I would never enter. Now I knew better. History happened in filing rooms. It happened wherever a page met a blade. And it could be unmade the same way — page by page, plate by plate, by a woman with a borrowed camera and a forged letter, standing in the ordinary gray light of an ordinary afternoon.

The train came sighing into Kew station. I boarded it with the plates warm against my ribs and one thought riding with me all the way back into London, growing heavier at every stop:

Calloway had signed his work. And a man who signs his work believes no one will ever come asking about it.

I intended to prove him wrong — and I already knew, with a dread I could not shake, that the road to Calloway's Historical Section ran, sooner or later, straight through the doors of Harrow House.
