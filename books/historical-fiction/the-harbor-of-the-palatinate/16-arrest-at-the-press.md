## 16. Arrest at the Press

I had always believed the press would die the way printers die — slowly, of ink in the lungs and debts unpaid — not the way it died that Thursday, which was quickly, and by the hand of the law.

It began, as most of the worst days of my life have begun, with good news.

Sebastian had come at half past seven, before the shops on Fetter Lane had thrown back their shutters, before the lamplighter made his rounds in reverse. She arrived in the grey coat she wore when she did not wish to be remembered, and she carried the Croft statement inside a hymnal, and she was — I can say this now, with the clarity that only a cell affords — she was happy. Not radiant; Sebastian does not radiate. But she had the particular stillness of a woman who has set down something heavy and not yet picked up anything else. She set the hymnal on the composing stone between us and said, "That's all of it. That's every living voice we're going to get."

"Then we print."

"Then we print," she agreed, and for a moment we simply looked at the thing — the folio, as we had taken to calling it, though it was only a bundle of typescript and carbons and my own careful proofs: Maggie Doyle's testimony in her own cadence, Croft's sworn pages, the photographs of the razored inquest file, the inventory of what had been cut and what had been left to stand in its place. Seven names. Three voices, two of them now silenced, one of them recanted and then un-recanted by the man's own hand in the dark of a Portsmouth pew. It weighed perhaps four pounds. I have seen four pounds of paper end a government. I believed it that morning. I believe it still, though belief has cost me differently than I expected.

"We'll need a week," I said. "The type alone — I want it set by hand, no linotype, no record of it in the shop's books. I want it to look like it came from nowhere."

"Nothing comes from nowhere," Sebastian said. "That's rather the point of it."

She was right, of course. She was right about nearly everything, which is one of the many reasons the days that followed were so unbearable: because I had been wrong in only one particular, and that particular was everything.

* * *

The warrant, I learned later, named three works. *The Burning Question*, a pamphlet on the suffrage trials. *Songs of the Unemployed*, a chapbook of which I had printed forty copies and sold perhaps six. And — this was the flourish, the signature of a man with a sense of humour I did not appreciate until much later — an edition of Swinburne. Obscenity. That was the charge. That was the legal instrument they brought through my door at nine minutes past ten on a Thursday morning in November: that I had printed Swinburne.

There were six of them. Two in uniform, four not, and the four not-in-uniform were the ones who knew what they had come for. I understood this within the first thirty seconds, because the two constables went to the pamphlet racks and the other four went to the back of the shop, where the composing stone stood, where the folio lay under a proofing blanket where I had left it not an hour before, intending to begin setting the first page that afternoon.

"Where's the rest of it?" said the one who did the talking. He had a soft voice, a clerk's voice, and he never once raised it, which frightened me more than shouting would have. "The Wapping material."

"I don't know what you mean," I said, and heard my own voice do the thing voices do in such moments — go up half a tone, go thin, go young. "This is a print shop. We print pamphlets. You're welcome to read all of them."

He smiled. It was not a cruel smile. That was the worst of it. It was the smile of a man who had done this eleven times before and would do it eleven times again and had long ago stopped needing to hate anyone in order to do it well. "Mr. Vane. Your brother was at Wapping."

The sentence landed in the room like a dropped type case — everything scattering, everything finding the wrong order. They knew. Not suspected; knew. Knew the connection, knew the motive, knew the shape of what I was building. Which meant they had not learned it this morning. Which meant they had learned it days ago, or weeks. Which meant —

I did not finish the thought then. There was no time. One of the others had lifted the proofing blanket, and the soft-voiced man looked down at the empty stone, and for the first time something moved behind his face — not disappointment exactly. Recalculation.

"Search the premises," he said. "Thoroughly."

* * *

I want to set down what it looks like when they break a press, because the newspapers will never describe it and the official record — I have learned something of official records — will record only "certain machinery damaged in the course of the execution of a lawful warrant."

A hand press does not break easily. That is its whole character; that is why we love them. Ours was an Albion, forty years old, older than me, older than my brother, iron and beechwood and a screw that my father's hands had worn to a polish. It took them eleven minutes. They did not smash it in a fury. They worked at it with method, with the pry bars they had brought for the purpose, and the sound it made — I can only tell you it was not metal. Metal breaking has a ring to it. This had none. It was the sound of something being persuaded, patiently and with the full authority of the Crown, to stop.

The type they swept into a sack. Twenty-six cases, some of them fonts I had bought secondhand when I was nineteen, letters that had set my brother's name on his apprenticeship papers. The paper stock they trampled, not to destroy it — trampled paper can be dried and sold — but because trampling is a message and messages must be delivered. One of the uniformed men, younger than the rest, hesitated at the case of twelve-point Baskerville, and the soft-voiced man said, without looking up, "All of it, constable," and the boy's face did something I recognised, because I had seen it on Sebastian's face in a Stepney doorway: the moment a man understands whose work he is doing and does it anyway.

They took the folio's absence personally. I could see it in the way they went through the place — the floorboards sounded with a mallet's butt, the plaster probed, the water closet tank lifted, my mattress opened along its seams. They had been told it was here. They had been told precisely, I think, where. And it was not here, because Sebastian — God keep her, God keep her — had taken it with her when she left that morning, folded into the false bottom of a hatbox, because she had a habit of never leaving words where she could not watch them, and because she had looked at my composing stone at half past eight and said, "It shouldn't sleep here," and I had laughed at her.

I laughed at her. I remember the exact sound of my own laughter, and I expect I shall hear it for the rest of my life.

* * *

The charge sheet, when they read it to me at Leman Street, was a document of exquisite comedy. Obscenity, three counts. Resisting a lawful search, one count — I had put my hand on the sack of type, that was all, one hand on a sack, and a constable had removed it, and that was resistance. Publishing matter tending to incite disaffection — this one was added later, I'm told, at the insistence of someone senior, and it was the one that mattered, because obscenity is a fine and disaffection is a door.

They put me in a cell and took my bootlaces and my belt and the pencil I kept behind my ear, and I sat on a plank bed in a room the colour of porridge and I did the arithmetic that prisoners do.

They had come for the folio. They had known its name — *the Wapping material*, in a clerk's soft voice, before any search had justified the phrase. They had known my brother. They had come with pry bars for a press on an obscenity warrant that named Swinburne. None of this is improvisation. All of it is preparation. And preparation requires information, and information requires a source, and the source had to be someone who knew three things: that the folio existed, that it was at Fetter Lane, and when it would be there.

Sebastian knew all three. I knew all three. And one other person in the world knew all three, or nearly — because there had been a letter, a fortnight past, a note in Constance Harrow's own hand thanking me for "the care you are taking with the papers entrusted to you," which I had read as kindness, as benediction, as the blessing of the great house on the work of the small one.

I sat in the porridge-coloured dark and I turned that note over and over like a coin I could not identify by touch alone.

Because there was another possibility, and I forced myself to hold it in the light, because a man who prints evidence must be able to weigh his own. The solicitor. Harrow's solicitor — Sebastian had traced the historian's tidy summary to the family's man of business, and a solicitor's clerk reads correspondence, that is what clerks do. The note might have passed through other hands before it reached mine. The trap might have been baited without the trapper's knowledge.

Or not. Or the great house had decided that the small press was a wound that must be cauterised, that prison would stop me more surely than any argument, that a cell in Leman Street was a kinder instrument than some of the instruments available, and had chosen it the way one chooses a medicine — distasteful, effective, for my own good.

I lay down on the plank bed and discovered that I could not make these two possibilities separate. They had the same weight. They had, in fact, the same face: the face of a woman I had — I will write it plainly, in a cell, with no pencil, where no one will read it until it no longer matters — a woman I loved.

* * *

They let me see no one for two days. On the third day they let me see my solicitor, a tired man named Prewitt who charged by the minute and earned it. On the fourth day they let me see no one again, and it was on the fourth day, in the hour before the warder's rounds, that I understood the shape of the thing they were building.

They were not building a case against me. A case against me was a by-product, a shell, the obscenity warrant around the true charge the way the hymnal was folded around Croft's statement. What they were building was a demonstration. Every printer on Fleet Street would hear that Vane of Fetter Lane had been raided, pressed broken, type confiscated, jailed on Swinburne. And every printer would do the arithmetic I had done on the plank bed, and every printer would conclude that the Wapping material was not worth four pounds of paper. That was the method. I had seen it in the razored inquest file and I had been too slow to see it turned on me: you do not refute the record. You make the record impossible to keep.

The soft-voiced man had said it to me, almost kindly, as they walked me out. He paused at the door of the shop — my shop, the door I had unlocked ten thousand mornings — and looked back at the ruin of the Albion, and said, "You understand, Mr. Vane, this is nothing. This is a formality. Whatever it was you were assembling — it's already been handled at a higher level. The history's been written. It's quite elegant, actually. You'd have admired the prose."

I said nothing. But I have thought of that sentence every hour since, and I will tell you what I have found in it, turning it in the dark like the note from Constance Harrow: *it's already been handled.* Not *we have it.* Not *you will tell us where it is.* Handled. Past tense. Dismissed. Which means either that the man was lying to comfort himself, or —

Or that they believe the folio was destroyed in the raid. That they searched for it, did not find it, and concluded it had never truly been there — that Vane was a blustering man with a grievance and a bundle of secondhand paper, and the thing itself had gone into the fire days ago, and the raid was only the period at the end of a sentence already written.

They do not know it is still in the world. They do not know that a woman in a grey coat carried it out under a hatbox at half past eight on a Thursday morning, while I laughed at her.

* * *

On the sixth day, they brought me a newspaper, because I asked for one and obscenity is not sedition and a man on remand has small rights and I spent one of them on the *Evening Standard*. I read it standing at the barred window, because sitting felt like agreement.

There it was, on page five, four paragraphs, between a shipping notice and an advertisement for cough syrup: PRINTERS RAIDED IN FETTER LANE — OBSCENE PUBLICATIONS SEIZED — ONE ARRESTED. The prose was elegant. I did not admire it. It named no pamphlet I had printed except the Swinburne, and it described me as "a man of previously good character whose premises had, regrettably, been used for the circulation of indecent matter," and it closed with a sentence I read four times because I could not at first believe the audacity of it: *The raid is understood to be unconnected with recent public discussions of the Wapping regrettable incident.*

Unconnected. They had written the connection's denial in advance, which is the surest proof of connection there is. And I stood at the window with the newspaper shaking slightly in my hands, and I understood that I was not looking at a report. I was looking at the first page of the counter-folio. The official version, already typeset, already circulating, already elegant. They had not come to seize my book. They had come to publish theirs, using my ruin as the printing press.

And beneath that understanding, cold as the cell floor, the other one: *someone told them where it was.*

Three people knew. I knew, and I was in the cell. Sebastian knew, and Sebastian had carried it out in a hatbox — and if she had wanted to be rid of it, rid of me, rid of the whole terrible arithmetic of loyalty, there was no cleaner way than to let it be found. Except it had not been found. Which argued for her, or argued for a trap within the trap, or argued —

Or argued for the third. For the note in the fine hand. *The care you are taking with the papers entrusted to you.* Entrusted. By whom? To whom? I had read it as poetry. A solicitor's clerk would read it as inventory.

I thought of Sebastian's face the morning of the raid — the stillness, the happiness, the hymnal — and I thought: she would not. And then I thought, with a coldness I did not know I possessed: I said the same of the great house, once. I said the same of everyone, once. That is what being trusted does; it makes you illiterate.

* * *

They moved me on the ninth day. Remand at Holloway, they said, though for a man the arrangements were irregular and I did not ask questions. In the corridor of the van, handcuffed to a warder who smelled of tobacco and pity, I passed a window and saw, on the pavement opposite, a woman in a grey coat who did not look at me, did not stop, did not do anything but stand with a hatbox under her arm while the traffic went by — and then turn, deliberately, the way one turns to leave a grave, and walk north.

And I could not tell, as the van pulled away, whether what I felt was relief or the last stage of the trap closing — whether I had just seen the woman who saved the folio, or the woman who had spent it, walking away from the exchange.

They put me in a cell with a window facing east. In the mornings the light comes in grey and then white, and I lie on the plank and set type in my head, because it is the only thing they cannot confiscate — I set the first page of the folio in my mind, Maggie Doyle's words, *I saw them fire and I counted*, in twelve-point Baskerville, and I check my spacing, and I wait.

Because here is what I know that the soft-voiced man does not: the folio exists. The words exist. And words, I have lately learned, are like the tide at Wapping — you can build all the walls you like, elegant as you please, but the water is patient, and it remembers its level, and one morning someone walks down to the harbour and finds the wall cracked from the other side.

Someone is walking. I only pray to a God I have not spoken to since my brother died that when she arrives, she is carrying it — and not the name of the person who told them where it slept.
