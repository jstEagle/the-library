## 1. The Weight of Blank Paper

The lamp had burned low before Petra Thornbury noticed how much of the night had gone. Outside the window of the workshop on Cerro Concepción, the harbor of Valparaíso lay scattered with lights — the riding lamps of merchantmen, the red and green eyes of coasting steamers, the long gaslit spine of the mole — and beyond them, invisible now, the dark line of the sea where seven years ago a mapmaker had drowned with the truth folded in his coat.

Petra dipped the pen, touched it to the blotter, and drew the coastline of a bay that did not officially exist.

His hand was steady. Seven years of practice had made it steady, the way a liar's face grows smooth. The line ran south from the Punta Ángeles light in a long shallow arc, and where the official charts showed nothing but open water and the notation *no anchorage — surf heavy*, Petra's pen laid down a cove, a shelf of deep water close under the cliffs, a roadstead sheltered from the southwesterlies by a shoulder of rock that the Admiralty's hydrographers had somehow never thought to draw. He inked the soundings in Sandoval's hand — he had learned to counterfeit it so well that sometimes, late at night, he could almost believe the dead man was guiding his fingers.

*Fourteen fathoms, sand and shell. Good holding.*

A boy could drown in fourteen fathoms. A fleet could anchor in them.

He set the pen down and flexed his fingers, and for a moment he let himself look at the plate the way a stranger might: just a chart, beautifully made, the work of a careful man. That was the whole art of it. Aurelio Sandoval had taught him that a map was never innocent — that every chart was an argument about what mattered, and the most powerful lie was not a false line but an omitted one. *The sea does not lie,* the old man used to say, tapping his pen against the copper. *But the men who print the sea — they lie the way other men breathe.*

Petra had been twenty-two when Sandoval took him on, an English clerk's orphan with quick hands and no prospects, and he had thought he was learning a trade. It had taken him three years to understand he was being entrusted with a weapon, and five to understand why.

He slid the finished plate from the easel and held it up to the lamp. The paper was thick, the engraving fine, the title cartouche empty and waiting: *PLATE NINE.* Nine of forty. Seven years, and the atlas was not half done — because it could not be done quickly, and could not be done safely, and because every plate had to be two things at once.

He turned to the press in the corner, the old Albion Sandoval had bought secondhand from a bankrupt job printer, and lifted the false bed. Beneath it, in a shallow tray lined with oiled silk, lay the true plates — nine of them now, each one engraved twice, the dangerous version hidden under a harmless twin. The plate he had just finished would go into the tray tonight, and on top of it would go its twin: the same bay, the same coastline, but with the cove erased, the soundings altered, the anchorage struck out entirely — a chart so dull that any man who seized Sandoval's papers would find nothing in them but the competent, obedient work of a dead mapmaker. The lie the victors wanted, drawn in the master's own hand.

*They will come for the papers eventually,* Sandoval had said, in the last autumn, when the first rumors of an armistice had begun to move through the port like weather. *They came for Merlo's press, they will come for my plates. A man who hides a thing makes it precious. A man who leaves it lying in the open, wearing a dull coat — that man keeps it.* He had smiled then, his dry, creased smile. *You will learn to engrave two truths, Petra. One for the drawer and one for the wall. The trick is to never forget which is which.*

Sandoval had drowned in April of '64, eleven days after the massacre at Punta Ángeles, and the coroner's verdict — a mapmaker of unsound habits, taken by the swell while sketching from the rocks — had been written before his body was found. Petra had gone to identify him. He had stood in the deadhouse looking at the swollen ruin of the face and understood, with a clarity that had never left him, that the sea had not done that. The sea does not leave a man's wrists bound with twine.

He had said nothing. He had taken the plates, the tools, the cipher notebooks, and the lease on the workshop, and he had become the dull, respectable engraver of Cerro Concepción — Señor Thornbury, the Englishman, quiet, punctual, unremarkable — and for seven years he had worked by lamplight on a book that, if it were ever finished and ever read, would hang admirals.

He looked at the clock. Past eleven. Doña Inés would have left his supper covered on the stove two floors down, and would have left, with it, one of her silences — the particular disapproving silence she deployed against his late hours, which he had long ago learned to read as affection.

He was cleaning the pen when he heard the footsteps on the stair.

Not Doña Inés's step — she climbed slowly, with a hand on the rail, and she would not climb at all at this hour. These steps were quick and light and uncertain, the steps of someone who had paused twice on the way up, and Petra's body knew before his mind did. He set down the pen. He moved the Plate Nine tray back under the false bed. He blew out the second lamp so that only one light burned, and he took from the drawer the long engraver's knife — not to threaten anyone with, only because his hand wanted something in it — and he stood to the side of the door.

The knock came. Three raps, soft, uneven. A child's knock, or a frightened man's.

"Who is it?"

Nothing. The boards creaked, as if someone had shifted their weight.

He opened the door.

The boy stood in the corridor with the lamp from the landing throwing his shadow long across the floorboards. He was small — eight, perhaps, or small for nine — in a sailor's jacket much too big for him, the cuffs turned back twice, and his feet were bare and gray with the dust of the hills, as if he had walked a long way. His hair had been cut badly and recently. He did not look at Petra's face. He looked at Petra's chest, the way a dog looks at a man it has decided not to challenge, and he did not move, and he did not speak.

Petra looked past him down the stair. Empty. He looked along the corridor, with its row of closed doors and its smell of tallow and damp plaster. Empty.

"Are you lost?"

The boy's mouth opened. His throat worked. Nothing came out — not a word, not a sound, not even the small animal noises a frightened child makes. He tried twice, and then his face did something terrible and brief: it collapsed into an expression of pure frustration, of a door slammed from the inside, and then it smoothed over again into that careful blankness, and Petra understood that the boy was not refusing to speak. He could not. Whatever had been done to him, or whatever he had seen, had taken the words and locked them away.

"Come in," Petra said, before he had decided to say it.

The boy came in the way a cat comes into a strange room, close to the walls, and stood just inside the door while Petra closed it and shot the bolt, and then stood there while Petra lit the second lamp and looked at him properly. There was a bruise going yellow-green along his jaw. There was a healing cut above his eyebrow. His hands, when he finally took them out of the jacket pockets, were clean — someone had washed them recently, scrubbed them, the nails trimmed — the hands of a child who had been cared for until very recently by someone with a bar of soap and a conscience.

In his right hand, clenched so tightly that the paper had gone soft and warm, was a folded scrap.

"Is that for me?"

The boy looked at the paper, then at Petra, then at the door, and held it out at arm's length, the way one holds something that might be hot.

Petra took it. It was a piece of chart paper — he knew the weight of it in his fingers before he had unfolded it, the heavy wove stock Sandoval had imported from Hamburg, and his heart began, quietly, without permission, to accelerate. He turned it to the lamp.

It was a fragment of a chart, torn roughly, perhaps a sixth of a plate. A section of coastline, a few soundings in a fine italic hand, and — his breath stopped — a portion of an anchorage, marked with the small anchor symbol and the notation *fondeadero*, in Sandoval's cipher: the numbers displaced, the letters substituted, the whole thing legible only to the two people alive who had ever learned the key. It was Plate Nine. It was the lost section of Plate Nine — the section that had been on the press the night Sandoval died, the section that had vanished with the master's working papers, that Petra had spent two years re-engraving from memory and half-remembered proofs, and that he had never been certain he had gotten right.

The boy was watching his face. Whatever he saw there made him step back half a pace.

"Where did you get this?" Petra asked, and made his voice gentle, and crouched down to the boy's level. "Who gave it to you?"

The boy's jaw worked. His eyes filled, and he blinked the tears back with a ferocity that was almost adult, and he pointed — at the paper, at his own mouth, and then he shook his head, hard, three times, and Petra understood: *I cannot tell you. I cannot tell anyone. That is why I am here.*

"There's more," Petra said. "Isn't there. Someone wrote something."

The boy dug in the jacket pocket again and produced a second paper, folded small, and this one Petra opened with hands that were not quite steady.

It was a note, six words long, in a hand that Petra knew better than he knew his own — a hand he had imitated ten thousand times, a hand he had mourned, a hand that had been at the bottom of the harbor for seven years.

*Forget the face. Keep the chart.*

The lamp hissed. Somewhere down the hill a dog began to bark, and a ship's bell rang the half-hour off the mole, and Petra Thornbury stood in his workshop holding six words written in a dead man's hand and felt the floor tilt very slightly under him, the way the floor of a boat tilts.

It was Sandoval's writing. There was no mistaking it — the particular forward slant, the way the *f* descended in a long hook, the *p* with its open bowl. Petra had the man's correspondence in a locked drawer; he had studied that hand the way priests study scripture. Either Sandoval had written this note before he died and someone had kept it seven years, waiting — or Sandoval had not drowned at all — or someone alive knew that hand well enough to forge it, and there were perhaps three people in the world who could have, and Petra was one of them, and he had not written it.

The boy was shivering. Whatever else the night held, the boy was shivering, and barefoot, and had a bruise on his jaw the shape of a grown man's thumb.

"All right," Petra said. His voice came out rough. He cleared his throat. "All right. You're cold. Come and sit by the stove."

The boy did not move. He was looking past Petra now, at the workbench, at the easel with the harmless twin of Plate Nine still clipped to it — at the coastline of the cove that officially did not exist — and his face, which had been blank with discipline all this while, changed. The blankness broke. Not into tears. Into recognition. He looked at that chart the way a man looks at the face of someone he has seen die.

Then he pointed at it, once, with a finger that shook, and looked up at Petra, and his mouth opened again, and again no sound came — but this time Petra saw the shape the mouth made, and it was not a word.

It was *there*, or it was *that one*, or it was a scream with the sound torn out of it.

Petra closed the distance between them and put a hand on the boy's shoulder, and the boy did not flinch, and did not weep, and did not speak. Outside, the harbor lights burned over water that kept its secrets, and down in the city the presses of the official historians were, at this very hour, setting type.

"Tomorrow," Petra said, to the boy, to the dead man, to himself. "Tomorrow we'll see."

But he did not sleep, and long after the boy was curled on the cot under Sandoval's old coat, Petra sat with the scrap of Plate Nine under the lamp, matching it against his own re-engraved lines, and found that they agreed — soundings, bearings, the hidden anchorage, all of it — and understood that the boy had not been sent to him at random.

Someone knew. Someone had always known. And now they had given the truth a face, and left it at his door, and told him to forget it.
