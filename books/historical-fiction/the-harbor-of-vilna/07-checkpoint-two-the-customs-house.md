## 7. Checkpoint Two: The Customs House

Aurel Voss had read four thousand confiscated pamphlets in his career, and he had learned this: the truth never travels alone. It travels with a child, or a bribe, or a woman who has spent her whole life practicing the art of not being looked at.

So when the manifest from the harbor checkpoint crossed his desk that morning — a crate of printer's sorts, consigned to a chandler in the western quarter, flagged because a soldier with an excellent memory had recognized a watermark — Voss did not send soldiers. Soldiers were loud. Loud men broke things before they understood what they were breaking, and inside that crate, if he was right, was the only thing in Vilna worth understanding.

He put on his good coat instead. The grey one. The one that made him look like a tired clerk rather than the second most feared man in the governorate. Fear was a tool he kept for special occasions; today called for patience.

* * *

The customs house smelled of tar, wet wool, and the particular despair of paperwork. Voss arrived early enough to watch the morning queue form: fishermen with nothing to declare declaring it anyway, a widow arguing about a bolt of cloth as though the empire's fate hung on her haggling, and — there, third from the end, hood up despite the mild weather — Isandro Okonkwo, carrying a bundle that cried once, softly, and was immediately shushed with the practiced tenderness of someone who had been doing it for days.

Voss watched her from the doorway of the inspector's office, where he had installed himself with a cup of the customs house's terrible coffee and the inspector's terrified silence.

She was thinner than she'd been at the weekly inspections. There was ash under one fingernail. Her eyes did the thing they always did — catalogued him, dismissed him, moved on — but slower now, and with something underneath that hadn't been there before. Grief, perhaps. Or its cousin, purpose.

The courier's coat had been burned; he'd confirmed that much through two informants and one very talkative chimney sweep. But coats could be replaced. Documents, once printed, multiplied like mice. And somewhere between the burning shop and this queue, the girl had decided not to run west like any sensible person but to walk toward the governor's own gate, which meant she had a plan, and plans required checkpoints, and checkpoints —

— belonged to him.

"Next," said the clerk, bored.

Isandro stepped forward. Voss let her get exactly as far as the declaration table before he spoke.

"Master Okonkwo."

He had the pleasure of watching her stillness — the full-body discipline of it, the way she turned without hurry, as if she'd known all along. She probably had. That was the trouble with clever people; they ruined your surprises by having anticipated them.

"Censor Voss." Her voice was level. "I'm shipping type."

"I can see that." He nodded at the crate on her handcart, then at the bundle against her chest. "And cargo of a more delicate sort. The customs house charges by weight, you know. I'd hate for you to be surprised by the tariff."

Something flickered across her face — fear, quickly filed away behind that flat composure. "If you're going to arrest me, do it here. The queue's long enough as it is."

"Arrest you?" He smiled, and made it a kind smile, because kindness disarmed where menace only stiffened. "Child, if I wanted you arrested, you'd have been arrested in your sleep three nights ago, along with whatever remains of your father's shop worth arresting. Walk with me. There's a room through here with chairs that don't wobble. You look like a woman who hasn't sat in a chair that doesn't wobble in some time."

* * *

The side room had once been the harbormaster's office and still bore a map of the coastline from thirty years ago, when the empire drew bolder lines. Voss poured her tea from his own flask — the customs house coffee was punishment, not refreshment — and set the cup before her without ceremony.

"You went through the harbor checkpoint," he said. "Crate marked with your father's watermark. A smuggler named Marek Dziedzic carried it, who owes your father money and loyalty in roughly equal, insufficient measure. A soldier named Brasov memorized your face so thoroughly he drew it twice." He sipped his coffee and grimaced. "You are either very brave or very new to crime. In my experience the two feel identical from the inside."

Isandro didn't touch the tea. "How long have you known?"

"Since the harbor. Possibly since the night the courier died on your step — we found blood on the cobbles, and dead couriers are the empire's way of sending telegrams. Definitely since your shop burned." He watched her flinch and did not pretend not to see it. "I'm sorry about your father. Truly. Whatever else I am, I am not a man who burns printers. Printers are civilization. Burning them is what barbarians and governors do."

"And which are you?"

"A patient man," Voss said. "Which is rarer than either."

He set down his cup and folded his hands, and let the silence do a little work. She held up well — better than most, better than he'd expected — but her eyes went once, involuntarily, to the door, calculating distances, exits, odds. He let her finish the arithmetic before he spoke again.

"You have a lead case," he said. "Locked. Heavy. Full of shipping manifests, bills of sale, and correspondence proving that His Excellency the Governor-General has been selling condemned prisoners to buyers abroad — men our courts sentenced to die on our soil, sold instead like livestock, their deaths forged in our own ledgers while they row galleys in foreign waters." He recited it evenly, the way one recites scripture. "You've had this knowledge for eleven days. You haven't gone to the authorities, because I am the nearest authority and you don't trust me — correct instinct, incidentally. You haven't fled, because fleeing means the documents die with you. Which leaves one destination. The amnesty at year's end. The governor's gate. You mean to trade."

Her jaw tightened. "You've read my mail without the courtesy of intercepting it."

"I've read your intentions. It's a habit of the trade." He leaned forward. "Now let me tell you what happens at that gate, since no one else will. You present your evidence to a cornered man surrounded by his own guards, on the one night of the decade when the crowd outside is large enough to make violence inconvenient. He will smile. He will agree to everything. And the moment the amnesty bells stop ringing, you and your father and that child will die in ways the ledgers will record as fever, fire, and misadventure. Because that is what governors do, Master Okonkwo. They do not honor bargains. They honor power."

"And you're offering me yours instead."

"I'm offering you mine," Voss corrected gently, "which is smaller but honest about its size."

* * *

She laughed then — short, bitter, genuine. It startled them both.

"You want the case," she said. "That's what this is. Not rescue. Acquisition."

"I want the case," Voss agreed, "the way a surgeon wants the knife. Yes. I have spent nineteen years reading what this empire forbids, cataloguing its lies in triplicate, filing the confessions of its victims in alphabetical order, and handing the useful ones upward to men who use them to decorate their careers. I know where every body in this city is buried, because I indexed them." His voice stayed mild; he noticed, distantly, that his hands had not. "The governor-general is old, and the capital is hungry, and there is a vacancy coming. With those manifests, I don't apply for the seat. I arrive holding the previous occupant's crimes like a lantern, and the appointment writes itself."

"And the child?"

The question came quiet and sharp as a bodkin. Voss met her eyes and gave her the truth, because with this one, lies would cost more than they bought.

"The child is leverage I didn't know existed until this moment, and I suspect you've already guessed more about her than I have. The locket?" He saw the answer land. "Yes. I do my homework too. Here is what I propose, and I'll only say it once."

He laid it out plainly, the way he drafted confiscation orders — numbered, unadorned.

"She passes the gate tonight. The child sails west within the week, papers I provide, wet nurse of my choosing, a name that opens doors in three capitals. Your father walks out of wherever they're keeping him — I'll find him; I find everyone — before the new year. You and he go where you like, with money I'll call severance. In exchange, the case comes to me. All of it."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you keep your documents and your principles and your appointment at the governor's gate, and I attend your funeral out of respect." He spread his hands. "I'm not threatening you, Master Okonkwo. I'm describing the weather."

For a long moment she said nothing. She looked at the sleeping bundle against her chest, then at the map on the wall with its bold old lines, then — last, longest — at him.

"You'll let us pass customs," she said finally. "Today. No flags, no follow."

"No flags. No follow."

"And the case is a loan," she said. "Say it."

"It's a loan," Voss said, and found, to his mild surprise, that he almost meant it as one. "With generous terms."

She stood, shifted the child to her shoulder, and extended her hand. Her grip was ink-stained and stronger than it looked.

"One condition of my own," she said. "When you take the seat — if you take it — the first thing you burn is the list of names. The informants' index. Every person this city ever denounced."

Voss considered telling her that the index was the foundation of his entire career. He considered explaining that power, once acquired, has its own appetite.

Instead he heard himself say: "Agreed."

She paused at the door, the crate on her cart, the future in her arms.

"For what it's worth, Censor Voss — my father says the most dangerous thing in a print shop isn't the press. It's a compositor who's patient." She glanced back. "I think he'd say the same about censors."

Then she was gone, into the queue and the noise, and Voss sat alone with his cold coffee and the shape of what he'd just done.

It was only later — much later, when the bells rang and the harbor burned and the ledger of that winter was being totaled — that he would understand what she had handed him along with the case. Not just a weapon. A leash. And that somewhere between the handshake and the gate, the girl who had survived by being invisible had quietly looped the other end of it around his throat.

But that afternoon, Aurel Voss felt only the clean, cold pleasure of a man who has finally been dealt the right cards — and he began, humming, to plan his inauguration.
