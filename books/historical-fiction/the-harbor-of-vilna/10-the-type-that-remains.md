## 10. The Type That Remains

My father came out of the governor's prison on the fourth day of the new year, and he came out smaller.

Not shorter — he had always been a compact man, built like a piece of furniture meant to last a century — but smaller in the way a book is smaller after water. The same shape. Fewer words inside. He walked through the gate on his own feet because he refused every offered arm, and when he saw me waiting he did not embrace me. He looked at me for a long moment, the way he looked at a page that had come off the press crooked, deciding whether it could be corrected or had to be thrown away.

"You're thinner," he said.

"You're grayer."

"That's the natural order of things." He touched my shoulder then, briefly, with ink-stained fingers that would never be ink-stained again. "Your mother would have had opinions about all of this."

"She had opinions about everything. It's where I got mine."

He almost smiled. Almost. It was the almost that told me how much of him the prison had kept.

They walked us home through streets that no longer belonged to either of us. The harbor had changed its skin. Where the customs house had stood — where Voss had bargained with us like a man buying a horse he intended to ride to a coronation — there was now a barricade of overturned fish carts and a hand-painted sign reading THE PEOPLE'S CUSTOMS HOUSE, the last word painted larger than the rest, as though whoever painted it had run out of conviction around the same time he ran out of wall. Boys who had never held anything heavier than a gull's egg now carried rifles with the solemnity of altar servers. A woman was selling roasted chestnuts to the revolution. The revolution was buying.

"Look at that," my father said quietly. "A week ago that boy would have been beaten for standing there. Now they salute him." He paused. "I set type for forty years and never moved a city. You moved one in a month, and you didn't even print anything."

"I printed one thing."

"One folio. Hidden in your coat." He shook his head. "Your mother would say you take after her. I would say you take after the press. You put the words in, you pull the lever, and you stand well back from what comes out."

* * *

The child sailed on a Thursday, on the *Meridian Star*, west for Halvard, with a wet nurse named Serafina Ott who had lost her own daughter to the fever that autumn and who held the governor's granddaughter the way you hold something you have decided, privately and against all evidence, is yours.

I had chosen Serafina the way my father chose paper: by weight, by grain, by how it behaved under pressure. She had asked no questions. She had looked at the child, and the child had looked at her, and something had passed between them that required no documents, no lockets, no bargains struck at gates in the small hours. I envied that. Everything in my life that winter had required documents.

The governor had kept his word. He was a monster of the ordinary kind — the kind who sells prisoners and signs receipts and attends mass — but he was a monster who honored contracts, which is the only kind of monster you can do business with. My father walked free. The child sailed with papers in a name that had nothing to do with any of us. And I stood on the pier in the gray morning light and watched the *Meridian Star* shrink until it was a smudge, and then a rumor, and then nothing at all.

She had been named, in the locket's secret engraving, *Liesl*. The L had been the first letter I set after reading it. I don't know why. It seemed important that somewhere in the world, in lead, in a case, there should be a record of her that no one could burn.

"You could come with them," Serafina had said, the night before. "There's room on the ship."

"I know."

"You could start over. Nobody in Halvard knows your face."

"Nobody in Vilna knows it either," I said. "That used to be my whole talent."

She had laughed, and the baby had laughed with her, a small astonished sound, as though laughing were a thing she was discovering the world could do. I had held her then — held Liesl, held the governor's granddaughter, held the reason I had walked through three checkpoints and a burning shop and a bargain I still hadn't finished paying — and she had grabbed my collar with one fist, the way babies do, as though signing something.

"Go well," I told her. "Forget the face."

* * *

Voss rose the way water rises in a locked cellar: steadily, quietly, and with the certain intention of finding every crack.

By the end of January he had a seat on the Provisional Council. By February he had the Council. He gave speeches about the harbor's shame — the manifests, the sold prisoners, the governor's "unspeakable commerce" — and he never once mentioned where the documents had come from. I read the transcripts in a coffee house near the fish market, in a newspaper printed by a shop that had been, three months earlier, a rival of my father's. The type was sloppy. The margins wandered. The revolution, I thought, needed a good compositor badly.

"You could go to him," my father said. "Tell him what you have. The folio. He'd owe you."

"He'd owe me the way a leash owes a dog."

"You could sell it."

"To whom? The governor's finished. Voss has the rest. What I have is a folio that says what everyone now knows." I turned the page of the newspaper. "It's not evidence anymore, Papa. It's just paper."

"Paper is never just paper. I taught you that."

"You taught me that. And then they burned the shop to teach me the rest."

He was quiet a while. The coffee house was warm and smelled of burnt grounds and tobacco, and outside the window a patrol of the new citizens' militia marched past with a drum that was slightly out of rhythm, which bothered my father visibly, the way crooked type bothered him.

"I'm sorry," he said at last. "For telling you to give the child back. In the beginning. When we found the locket."

"I know."

"You were right and I was afraid, and those are not the same thing, though I have spent seventy years pretending they are." He wrapped his hands around his cup. "Your mother was right about you. She said, *that girl will carry what she decides to carry, and no one will be able to take it from her, and no one will be able to give it back.* I thought she was describing a mule."

I laughed — really laughed, for the first time since the shop burned — and an old man at the next table looked over at us as though laughter were something he'd read about in a pamphlet and half believed.

* * *

We did not rebuild the shop.

My father said he was too old, and it was true, but it was also not the reason. The reason, which he finally admitted in March, standing in the blackened doorway where the press had been, was that he could not bear to set type in a city where the type might be confiscated again. "A press is a promise," he said. "You promise the words will stay set. I can't promise that here anymore. Not yet. Maybe not for years."

So he taught instead — compositing, in a room above a baker's shop, to apprentices who paid in bread and in the fierce, unguarded attention of people who had recently discovered that reading was a weapon. He was good at it. He was, I realized watching him correct a boy's spacing with two fingers and a stream of quiet profanity, happier than he had ever been at the press. Teaching, it turned out, was printing that talked back.

And I — I did what I had always done. I noticed things and said nothing, and it turned out that a city rebuilding itself from ash needs people who notice things. I found Serafina's letters a route around the censors. I found the baker's son a place in the militia that didn't require him to shoot anyone. I found, in the wreckage of our shop, the typecase.

* * *

It was in the corner of the cellar, under a fallen beam, scorched at one edge but sound. Empty. Every sort had been scattered or melted or carried off by looters who knew, even in their greed, that lead is lead. I sat on the cellar floor with it in my lap for a long time, in the cold, in the smell of ash and old ink, and I thought about everything it had held. Pamphlets. Manifests. The governor's name, set a thousand times in headlines I had never read closely enough. Liesl's name, set once, in secret, at midnight.

And there, wedged in the far corner where it had slipped behind the spring during the fire, one sort remained.

The L.

I held it up to the light from the broken hatch. It was warm — no, it wasn't, it was freezing, lead holds the cold like a grudge — but my hands were shaking and I decided it was warm. It was a beautiful letter. My father had cast the face himself, years ago, from a matrix he'd traded a sailor three bottles of rum for, and it had the slight irregularity of all his best work: the foot a hair too wide, the serif on the upper arm flared like a small defiant wing.

I put it in the case, in the corner, alone. An entire typecase for one letter. My father would have called it waste. My father, I suspected, would have understood it perfectly.

* * *

In April, Voss was elected to the governorship — the old title, the old office, a new man in the old chair — and the harbor celebrated with fireworks that set fire to two warehouses and a fishing boat, which everyone agreed was at least honest. I watched from the pier with my father, who had brought chestnuts and a newspaper he refused to read.

"He'll sell prisoners too, eventually," my father said. "Different buyers. Better receipts."

"I know."

"Will you do something about it?"

I thought about it honestly, the way he had taught me to think about a page: what can be corrected, what must be thrown away, what is worth the ink.

"Not today," I said.

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow I'm going to teach your Tuesday class. The boy with the wandering margins. He has talent, and if nobody fixes his spacing he'll grow up to print newspapers like that one." I nodded at the paper under his arm. "The revolution can wait. It's very patient. It's the patientest thing I've ever met."

He laughed then — a real laugh, from whatever was left of him, which was more than I'd thought — and the fireworks went off over the water, red and gold and slightly out of rhythm, like a drum, like a heartbeat, like a press starting up in a city that had not yet learned what it wanted to say but had, at last, and at terrible cost, decided that it would say it itself.

That night I set the typecase on my windowsill, open, the single L catching the harbor light.

One letter. The first of her name. Enough to begin a word, if I ever found the rest.

And somewhere west, past the smudge and the rumor, a ship was carrying a child who would grow up never knowing that a print shop had burned for her, that a courier had died at its door, that a woman whose whole talent was being unseen had once stood in a governor's gate and been seen by everyone. She would never know. That was the point. That was the entire point, and I had paid everything I had for it, and I would pay it again.

I blew out the lamp. The L held the last of the light a moment longer, the way lead holds the cold — stubbornly, and against all evidence, and as though it had decided, privately and forever, that it was warm.
