## 3. What the Clock Kept

The master clock of the Vellamare Observatory had been lying about the time for eleven days, and the Director wanted it corrected before anyone important noticed that the empire's most precise instrument was, in fact, a liar.

"Eleven days," he said, as though I might not have heard him the first time. "It loses four minutes a day. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means it's losing four minutes a day," I said. "That's usually how clocks fail. They don't gain character."

He did not laugh. Directors of observatories under occupation rarely do; humor requires a certain confidence in tomorrow that their position does not afford them. He signed my work permit with the weary flourish of a man signing away something he'd rather keep, and a soldier walked me up the tower stairs as if I were cargo that might bolt.

I want to be clear about what this summons was and wasn't. It wasn't trust. Nobody in Vellamare trusts an Oyelaran face with anything heavier than a broom, and my face is unmistakably Oyelaran — my mother's cheekbones, my father's deep brown skin, a combination the new regime's paperwork has no tidy box for, which is precisely why I've spent fifteen years making sure no one looks closely enough to try. The summons was necessity. The clock was a state instrument, state instruments required licensed hands, and there were perhaps three licensed clockmakers left in the city who hadn't fled or been fled *for*. I was the cheapest of the three, because cheap is another way of staying invisible. Nobody resents a bargain.

So: the tower, the stairs, the soldier whose boots announced every one of my movements like a town crier, and at the top, behind a door older than the occupation, older than the last two empires, the great clock itself.

She was beautiful, even sick. Eleven feet of walnut case and brass skeleton, the pendulum hanging still as a hanged man, the dial faces clouded with fifteen years of harbor salt. I put my ear against the case the way you'd press your ear to a chest, and I listened.

*Tick.* A long pause, too long. *Tock.* Another pause, longer still.

"There you are," I murmured.

"What?" said the soldier.

"The escapement," I said. "The anchor's worn. Every swing, it lets the wheel slip a hair further than it should. Four minutes a day, give or take." I unpacked my tools on a cloth I'd brought for the purpose — clockmakers learn early that observatory floors are marble and marble eats dropped screwdrivers — and began the slow, respectful business of undressing a machine that had been running since before my grandmother crossed the sea.

Here is what nobody tells you about repairing clocks: most of the work is listening, and most of the listening is to things the clock would rather not say. A gear train is honest in a way people are not. It cannot help telling you where it hurts.

* * *

By midday the soldier had been replaced by boredom, which is the occupational disease of guards everywhere, and by late afternoon he'd decamped to the stairwell to smoke where the draft wouldn't carry the smell back down to his sergeant. Good. What I needed next required quiet and both hands.

The escapement came out in three pieces. The anchor was worn exactly as I'd guessed — a groove cut into the left pallet, deep as a fingernail paring, fifteen years of tiny thefts adding up to four minutes a day. I could re-cut the pallet faces, true them, lap them smooth. Two hours' work, maybe three. But the pallet arbor sat in a bracket, and the bracket was mounted to a crossbeam, and when I leaned in to check the beam for flex — because a worn escapement is sometimes a symptom, not a disease, and I have been lied to by symptoms before —

— my knuckles knocked against wood where there should have been air.

I stopped. Knocked again. Solid. Then I moved my hand six inches left and knocked, and got the hollow ring of a cavity.

Now, a sensible person — a person committed to invisibility, to silence, to the long patient art of not noticing things — would have noted the discrepancy, repaired the escapement, collected her fee, and gone home to sleep soundly above her little shop on Rue Cendre. That is what fifteen years of training said to do. That is what my mother's ghost, if ghosts counsel anything, would have counseled: *Sable. Look at your hands. Look at your work. Look at nothing else.*

But I was alone in a tower full of gears, and I was tired of being careful in the one place where careful had never once kept me safe, and — I'll admit it — I was curious. Curiosity is the flaw they never bred out of me. My father had it too. It's why he's gone.

The panel was walnut, matched to the beam so well that only a knock would find it. There was no lock, just a wooden catch worn smooth by a thumb — by *a* thumb, once, years ago, someone had opened this often enough to wear the catch soft. I pressed it. The panel swung inward on oiled hinges, silent as a held breath, and inside was a space about the size of a bread loaf, lined in felt gone grey with age.

A book lay in it. Hand-bound, oilcloth-wrapped, tied with string.

My hands were steady. I remember being surprised by that. Fifteen years of practicing steadiness, and here at last was the moment it was actually for.

* * *

I untied the string with a jeweler's patience I did not feel.

The cover was plain board. Inside, in ink faded from black to the brown of old blood — though it was only ink, I told myself, only ink — a hand had written, in a small, disciplined script:

*Record of the Harbor District. Kept against forgetting.*

Below that, a date. The date. The ninth of Marrow, fifteen years ago, though the ledger didn't call it that. It called it *the night the ships burned*, and beneath that heading, page after page after page, names.

Names, ages, streets. *Alenna Sufi, fishwife, Quay Lane. The Doss brothers, pilots. Yannick Ferro and his daughter, aged six.* Each entry with a date of death — all the same night, all within the same four hours — and beside many of them, in a different, later hand, a line of testimony. *Seen taken from the customs house. Heard the order given by name. Saw the boats return empty.*

I knew these streets. I had grown up on them, before. Quay Lane was where Madame Sufi sold mackerel and pinched my cheek and called me *petit horloger* before I'd ever touched a gear. The Doss brothers had taught me to whistle through my teeth. These weren't entries in a ledger. These were the whole street, catalogued, the entire world I'd been seven years old in, written down by someone who had stood in the smoke and refused to let the smoke be the only witness.

And then I turned a page near the middle, and the handwriting changed.

Not the testimony hand. An earlier hand — cramped, hurried, the letters leaning like people running. And at the foot of a page of shipping contracts, cargo manifests, orders of seizure — paper trails showing that the burning hadn't been reprisal, hadn't been chaos, hadn't been any of the comfortable lies the city had swallowed — showing it had been *business*, a massacre arranged over freight rates — at the bottom of that page, a signature:

*Clerk of the Harbor Registry: E. Oyelaran.*

My father's initials. My father's hand. I know his writing better than I know his face now; I have his copybook from school, saved from a drawer when everything else burned, and the E leans exactly that way, the y descends exactly that far.

He didn't run, then. Whatever my mother told me, whatever the neighbors whispered, whatever story I built at seven and maintained at twenty-two like a load-bearing wall — he didn't simply vanish. He copied. He hid. He wrote down the names of the dead in a hand that leaned like a man running, and he hid the record inside the one machine in the city guaranteed to be tended forever, because a clock must always be repaired, mustn't it, and who repairs clocks?

Oyelarans repair clocks.

I sat down on the marble floor of the observatory tower with a dead city in my lap and laughed, once, a horrible small sound, because of course. Of course he chose the clock. Of course he trusted the trade, the guild, the patient machinery of time itself to keep his secret until someone with our name and our hands came to open the case. He'd addressed it, in his way. He'd mailed it fifteen years into the future, postage paid in walnut and brass, and I had signed for it without knowing.

The soldier coughed in the stairwell. Smoke drifted up through the door seam.

I wrapped the ledger back in its oilcloth, and then I did not put it back.

Understand — every instinct I own said *put it back*. Put it back, close the panel, fix the escapement, walk out clean. The regime had spent fifteen years teaching this city that the past was a country you could be deported to. The historians were already at work; everyone knew it, the way you know weather coming. Whoever kept this book had died for it or fled for it, and the book had waited safely in the dark, and the dark was where it belonged.

But my thumb found the signature again through the cloth. E. Oyelaran. The man who taught me to true a wheel by feel. The man whose name I had spent half my life declining to say aloud, as if a name could be set down like a tool at the end of a shift.

He had hidden the truth in a clock. I was the clockmaker. Some appointments you keep whether you want to or not.

I fixed the escapement first. I am a professional, and besides, a clock that lies about time offends me more than fear does. I re-cut the pallets, lapped them bright, reset the beat until the tick and the tock fell into perfect alternation, and the great pendulum began to swing with the deep contented rhythm of a heart remembering its proper rate. By the time I closed the case, the clock was keeping time better than it had in fifteen years.

Fifteen years. The same span. I chose not to find that meaningful. I failed.

I strapped the ledger flat against my chest, under my shirt, where a slim young apprentice carrying tools might plausibly have a slight thickness of canvas apron. I packed my tools. I thanked the Director through his clerk, accepted my fee, and walked down out of the tower past the bored soldier, past the checkpoint, past the census notice freshly pasted on the customs house wall — *ALL RESIDENTS MUST REGISTER FULL FAMILY ORIGINS BY ORDER OF THE COMMISSIONER OF RECORDS* — and the words snagged on me like a nail on cloth, because I had registered last month, and the census taker, a pale meticulous man named Holt, had asked me questions about my father's line that no routine form had ever needed answered.

At the time I'd given him the smooth non-answers I give everyone, and thought nothing more of it.

Walking home along the quay with a massacre beating against my ribs like a second heart, I thought quite a lot more of it.

Because if someone had gone looking for Oyelarans — if the name was moving through the regime's paperwork like a coin through a pickpocket's fingers — then my father's hiding place had not been a hiding place at all. It had been a fuse. And somewhere in the city, a man with a clipboard was walking toward Rue Cendre, asking his careful questions, and I had just lit the other end.
