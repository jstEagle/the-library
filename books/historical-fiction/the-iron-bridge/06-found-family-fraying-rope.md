## 6. Found Family, Fraying Rope

**Tomas**

Tomas Okafor had carried iron for eleven years and never once thought of it as a thing that could lie. Iron was honest. It rang true or it didn't; it held or it sheared; it told you its temper in the sound the hammer drew from it. It was men who lied, and he had learned to live among their lies the way a man learns to sleep beside a railway — by ceasing to hear them.

But lying in the dark of the lighthouse's lower room, with the sea grinding against the rocks below like a millstone that would never finish its work, Tomas found he could not stop hearing them now.

Three days since Wren had come running down the ice road with Harrow's foremen behind, breath tearing, small boots skidding on the frozen shingle. Three days since Tomas had stepped out of the work line, caught the child by the collar, and shoved them into the lee of the coal shed as if adjusting a crooked lamp — casual as breathing, while Denny Rill walked past whistling. Three days since he'd smuggled Wren up the cliff path in the blue dark, through wind that came off the water like something with intent, and knocked at the door of the woman the whole river town spoke of only in lowered voices.

The widow Pemberton had opened the door with a lamp in one hand and something older than fear in her face. She had looked at Wren, then at Tomas, then at the two men huddled behind him — Ferro Callas with his split knuckles, young Ivo Marsh still bleeding through the rag tied over his brow where a foreman's lantern-hook had caught him — and she had stood aside without a word, the way one stands aside for weather.

Now they were five in a tower built for one, and Tomas lay listening to the sounds of it: Paloma Pemberton's slow tread overhead as she trimmed the wicks for the night watch; the tick and settle of the great lens turning above like a patient heart; Wren's breathing, quick even in sleep, a small animal that had not yet learned the world was safe enough to breathe slowly in.

Ferro slept like a dropped anchor. Ivo whimpered now and then, dreaming, his bandage dark in the lamplight that leaked up the stairwell.

And Denny Rill —

Denny had not come with them. Denny had stayed on the crew, laughing at foreman Brack's jokes, eating Harrow bread, and when Tomas had caught his eye across the yard that last morning there had been nothing in it at all. That was the thing about Denny. There was never anything in it. A man could be your friend for nine years and be nothing at all underneath, the way a bridge can look sound and be eaten through.

The light swept its long arm across the ceiling, around, around. Tomas counted the sweeps because counting kept him from thinking about what they'd found tonight, spread out on the kitchen table under Paloma's steady hand: the ledger's columns laid against Edmund Pemberton's drawings, and the arithmetic of theft sitting there plain as a scar.

They had stolen from a dead man's name. And they meant to hang their crime on it.

Around, around, went the light. Tomas closed his eyes and let it carry him under.

* * *

**Wren**

Wren woke to the smell of bread and did not know, for three full breaths, where they were.

Then: the lighthouse. The narrow bed in the store room that smelled of oil and salt. The blanket that smelled of someone else — a man, maybe, though everything here smelled faintly of him, the dead keeper, Edmund, whose name was said in this house the way you'd say the name of someone sleeping in the next room.

Bread. Someone was baking bread, in February, at the end of the world.

Wren crept up the spiral stair, past the lamp room's glass legs, down into the kitchen where the whole strange household had gathered itself around the table like hands around a fire. Mrs. Pemberton stood at the stove with her sleeves rolled, flour to her elbows, her grey hair escaping its coil in a way Wren suspected no one else alive had ever seen. Ferro was mending a net that belonged to no one. Ivo sat with his head bandaged, peeling potatoes badly. And Mr. Okafor — Tomas, he'd said, call me Tomas, lad — was bent over the table with the ledger open before him and Edmund Pemberton's drawings weighted flat with teacups.

"Sit," said Mrs. Pemberton, without turning. "There's bread."

Wren sat. The bread came warm, split and buttered, and Wren ate it too fast and was given more without comment, and this — this small transaction, this being fed without question — made their throat close so suddenly they had to stare hard at the table grain until it passed.

At the other end of the table, Tomas ran a blunt finger down a column of figures.

"Here," he said. "Month of the first frost. Payment drawn on the bridge account — four hundred pounds — routed through—" he tapped the entry, "—Pemberton & Sons, Ironworks." His voice did something careful around the name. "But the workshop's been shut since spring. Dead men don't draw payments."

"Someone kept the account open," Paloma said. She came to the table wiping her hands, and Wren watched her look at the ledger the way she looked at the sea in bad weather: measuring it, refusing to flinch from its size. "Someone with access to Edmund's seals and stationery. Someone who needed his name on paper."

"And his designs on the bridge." Ivo set down his potato. He was the youngest of them except Wren, and he spoke the way young men do when they've just understood something that will cost them. "That's the trick of it, isn't it. They took his truss pattern — the good one, the one he was working on when he died — and they built the cheap version. Scaled-down iron, thinner webbing, whatever scrap Brack could buy at half price. And if anyone ever asks why the bridge fails—"

"They point at the drawings," Ferro said quietly. He'd stopped pretending to mend the net. "Signed Pemberton. Dead man's fault. Dead man can't argue."

The kitchen went very still. The stove ticked. Somewhere above them the lens turned, sweeping its patient circle, and Wren thought: he knew. Edmund Pemberton had known, somehow, near the end — that was why the unfinished design in the workshop was different, heavier, corrected, the iron doubled where the built bridge was thin. He'd seen what they were doing with his name and he'd been drawing the truth when the river took him.

Or the river hadn't taken him at all. Wren did not say this. But they saw Mrs. Pemberton go to the window and stand looking out at the grey water for a long time, and Wren thought she was saying it to herself.

"The hearing's set for the thaw," Tomas said at last. "Harrow means to have the bridge opened, blessed, praised, and paid for before the ice goes out. After that, any failure is an act of God on a Pemberton bridge."

"Then we speak before the thaw." Paloma turned from the window. Her face had changed; Wren had been watching it change all week, grief cracking off it like ice off a hull, something harder and brighter showing beneath. "We speak at the hearing, with the ledger and the true design both, in front of every soul in this town."

"With respect, ma'am," Ferro said, "men like Harrow don't lose hearings. They own the men who run them."

"Then we find the ones they don't own." She looked around the table — at Ferro, at Ivo, at Tomas, and last at Wren, and her eyes on Wren were warm and terrible. "We are five. That is not nothing."

*Five*, Wren thought, and the word sat in their chest like a swallowed coal. Five. A week ago they had been no one, carried through a storm and left at a door with a note that said *forget the face*. Now they were one of five, sitting in a circle of bread and lamplight, and the terrifying thing — the thing that made their hands shake so they had to hide them under the table — was how much they wanted it to be true.

* * *

**Paloma**

Paloma sent the men down to the boat shed at dusk to cache the ledger copies where no search would think to look, and kept Wren back to help trim the lamps, because the child had begun asking to learn the work and Paloma had discovered she could not refuse them anything. This was new. This was dangerous. Grief had taught her to hold the world at arm's length, and now here was a child of perhaps ten with soot on their chin and Edmund's own frown of concentration, and her arms had opinions about it that she refused to consult.

She was showing Wren the clockwork rotation — *gently, it's older than both of us* — when she saw the light on the water.

Not her light. A light below, out past the point, where no boat should be in this cold and no fisherman would show a lamp at all. One flash. Two. A pause. One flash again.

Her blood went slow and heavy, like the tide going out.

"Wren," she said, keeping her voice level, "fetch my spyglass from the window seat. Quickly, now."

The child brought it. Paloma braced the glass on the sill and found the point, the black teeth of the rocks, the small boat riding at anchor where the channel deepened — and on the bluff above it, a figure with a shuttered lantern, signaling down toward the town. Toward the Harrow works. Toward the telegraph office that Aldous Harrow kept, by remarkable coincidence, staffed all night.

One flash. Two. Pause. One.

She knew the code. Every keeper's wife knew the code. It spelled a single word, simple as a knife:

*FOUND.*

Behind her, Wren asked something — she heard the shape of the question but not the words. Below, the boat put about and ran for town, its lamp doused. On the bluff, the shuttered lantern clicked once more and went dark.

Paloma lowered the spyglass. Her hands were steady; twenty years of storms had taught them that much. She thought of the household downstairs, the bread, the ledger cached in the boat shed, the five of them arranged like candles in a draft. She thought of Denny Rill, whom Tomas trusted, who had stayed behind on the crew, who had been ashore on the point this evening — she'd seen him walking there at low tide and thought nothing of it, thought *the man wants air after the works*, thought nothing, nothing, nothing.

"Mrs. Pemberton?" Wren's voice, small in the lamp room. "What is it? What did you see?"

She turned and knelt so their eyes were level, and she smiled the smile she used on the worst nights, the one that meant the opposite of what it showed.

"I need you to wake the others," she said. "All of them. We're leaving within the hour — and Wren? Bring the ledger. Not the copies. The original never leaves my sight again."

Above them the great lens turned, faithful and blind, throwing its light out over water that had already given away everything it knew.
