## 16. The Ice Wharf

The river froze in the night, and by morning it had begun to lie about itself.

Cyrus saw it from the embankment as she came down through the fish-market lanes: a skin of grey ice along the pilings, thin as a lie, already cracked and re-frozen in a hundred places where the current beneath refused to surrender. The wharfmen were out on it in cork boots, testing, cursing, driving iron bars into the surface to break channels for the barges that would not come. Somewhere under all that treachery the true current still ran, black and patient, and Cyrus thought: *that's me. That is exactly me.* A surface that looks solid. A current underneath that has somewhere to be.

She had not slept. The Customs House was behind her — Elias Brandt's stamp drying in her memory like a brand of her own — and ahead lay the last door between her and the river: the Ice Wharf, where Vellmark's winter trade crawled out onto the frozen channel and where, if Halloway had done his arithmetic, a girl carrying a dead master's instruments would eventually present herself.

She stopped in the shadow of a salt shed and took inventory, because Master Verren had taught her that a map is only as good as its survey, and a lie only as good as its details.

The coat: Verren's old surveyor's coat, too broad in the shoulders, smelling of camphor and the ghost of his pipe. The crate at her feet: pine, rope-handled, stenciled in her own careful imitation of the workshop's shipping hand — *V. & OKAFOR, SURVEYING INSTRUMENTS, VELLMARK — FOR THE PROVINCIAL ASSIZES, KETTERING* — and inside it, beneath a false floor of planed cedar, the sealed letter wrapped in oilcloth and wedged between Verren's theodolite and his brass parallel rule, where even if they opened the crate they would find instruments, real ones, lovingly packed, and nothing else unless they knew to measure the floor.

The manifest: Elias Brandt's stamp, blue-black, genuine, on a manifest Cyrus had written in a hand she had practiced for eleven years without knowing why — Verren's hand, the small cramped *v*, the way he crossed his *t*s like a man in a hurry to be elsewhere.

The papers in her pocket: a journeyman's transit pass forged by Tomas Rehl, whose ink-stained fingers she had trusted, whose green-ink copies were probably at this moment in Halloway's coat pocket, and which she now carried anyway because a lie you know is a lie can still be walked across a bridge if your face doesn't know it's lying.

And herself: half-frozen, three days without proper sleep, her hands steady only when she watched them and not when she thought about them.

*Every skill I have,* she thought, *spent at once, on one morning, at one wharf.* Verren had made her measure everything in what could be carried. She had never imagined the final cargo would be herself.

* * *

The Ice Wharf was not a wharf in any honest sense. It was a tongue of timber and frozen mud thrust out into the channel, lined with ice-houses on one side and grain sheds on the other, and at its root, where the cobbled lane came down from the Customs Row, a plank office on stilts with a stovepipe smoking out of its roof and a bell-pull frozen into a knot of icicles. The wharfmaster's flag hung from the office eaves, stiff as a shingle.

Cyrus had mapped this place once, two years ago, in the ordinary course of apprenticeship — a survey of the winter trade for a commercial atlas that Verren had never lived to finish. She had stood where she stood now with a plane table and a stadia rod, and the wharfmaster of that season, a fat man named Groll, had brought her hot cider and complained about his gout while she measured his kingdom. Groll was dead. The new wharfmaster she knew only by reputation and by one glimpse through a window: a lean, grey-whiskered man named Ossler, formerly of the river police, who had taken the post when the imperial commission reorganized the wharves after Verren's death.

*After Verren's death.* Everything in Vellmark now came in two ages: before and after.

She had spent the walk rehearsing, the way Verren had made her rehearse fieldwork. *Say the true things first,* he would say, tapping the table. *A lie stands longest on a foundation of truth. You are a courier. True. You carry instruments for the assizes. True. The manifest is stamped. True. Let the truth do the weight-bearing and your falsehood will never feel the strain.*

But there was one true thing she could not say, and it was the heaviest thing she carried: her name. At the Ice Wharf, if Ossler had been briefed — and Halloway was not a man to leave a door unbriefed — there might be a question about the workshop, about the Okafor household, about a girl with her brother's name. She had chosen, in the small hours, with the stove ticking and Adaeze's burned papers still a wound in the family's memory, to walk through as a stranger. *Mira Voss, courier, engaged by the workshop of the late Aldous Verren.* A name with no history. A name that weighed nothing.

She had thought, all her life, that weighing nothing was what she wanted.

* * *

The lane down to the wharf was watched. She saw it the way Verren had taught her to see — not with her eyes first but with her habits. A man leaning on the salt-shed wall with nothing to lean *for*. A boy selling roasted chestnuts who had not sold a single bag in the hour she'd watched him. The chestnut boy's eyes tracked every person who came down the lane, and his hands never moved toward the coals.

They weren't looking for her, she judged. Not yet, not specifically. They were looking for *anyone* — the doubled patrols had made the whole river nervous — and a girl with a crate was either beneath suspicion or exactly the shape of it, and she would not know which until she was through.

So she did the thing Verren had drilled into her on the survey of the Kessel marshes, when she was fourteen and terrified of the toll-farmers: she gave herself a task that was not the task. She stopped at the chestnut boy's brazier, bought a bag she did not want with a coin she could not spare, and said, loudly, in the flat accent of the upper town that she had practiced against Verren's teasing until it was seamless: "Cold enough to crack the pilings. Is the wharfmaster taking freight today, or is the ice still winning?"

The boy shrugged with the magnificent indifference of the employed. "Ossler's taking anything with a stamp. Ice don't matter to him. Ice matters to the bargemen." And then, because she had paid and he was fifteen: "You want the side gate if you're freight. Front's for passengers and trouble."

*The side gate.* She had mapped the side gate herself, two years ago, a plank walk from the ice-houses to the office's rear door, used by carters so the passenger lane stayed clear. Her own map had just handed her the route. She almost laughed — a small, dangerous laugh, kept behind her teeth — and went on down the lane past the salt shed, past the man with nothing to lean for, feeling his gaze slide off her the way water slides off waxed canvas.

*Invisible,* she thought, and for the first time in her life the word tasted like a tool instead of a cage. *One more use. One more, and then I'm done with you.*

* * *

The side gate was a plank walk slick with frozen spray, and she crossed it with the crate on her shoulder, taking small deliberate steps, a carter's gait, hips leading, weight low. The ice-houses breathed their cold breath around her. Through a gap in the planks she could see the channel: the grey ice stretching out toward the middle of the river, broken into plates by the current, the plates grinding against each other with a sound like slow teeth. Somewhere out there, when the ice went, a grain barge would go too — she had her eye on the *Maren Lise*, moored at the far wharf, loading for Kettering, her master's old friend Captain Ilsa Brand at the rail. But that was the next problem. There was only ever the next problem. Verren had said that too: *A mapmaker who looks at the whole journey freezes at the first ford. Look at the ford.*

The rear door of the office stood open against the cold, and the stove's warmth came out of it like a held hand.

Ossler sat behind a desk built from a hatch cover, and he was exactly what the river police made of men: lean, weathered, with eyes that had spent thirty years measuring boats for contraband and had developed a surveyor's instinct for the false floor, the false bottom, the false name. He had a ledger open before him and a pot of tea going cold at his elbow, and he looked up at Cyrus with the unhurried attention of a man who had never once in his life been made to hurry.

"Freight," he said. Not a question. A category.

"Courier for the workshop of Aldous Verren, surveyor." She set the crate on the hatch cover with a thump that was honest in its weight. "Instruments, going to the provincial assizes at Kettering. Manifest's stamped at the Customs House." She laid the manifest down, and her hand — she noted it with a strange, floating calm — did not shake. "Elias Brandt's office. Yesterday afternoon."

Ossler took the manifest. He read it the way she read maps: twice, and the second time for what wasn't on it.

"Verren," he said. "The one who drowned."

"The one who drowned," Cyrus agreed, and let the truth bear the weight. Grief was the best disguise she owned; it required no forging.

Ossler grunted. He turned the manifest over. The Customs House stamp sat in the lower corner, blue-black, genuine, and beside it the clerk's mark — *E.B.* — small and tired. He looked at it for a long moment. Long enough that Cyrus heard the ice grinding in the channel, heard the stove tick, heard her own heart doing its work like a pump in a flooded mine.

"Verren's workshop," Ossler said. "That's the one on Tanner's Row. The one under seizure."

The cold walked up Cyrus's spine, and she was grateful for it, because it explained what her face did next — the flinch, the narrowing, the wariness of a small tradesperson hearing a dangerous name.

"I wouldn't know about that," she said. "I know what I was paid to carry and where it goes. The instruments are the master's work. The assizes want them for evidence — that's what the manifest says, and I'll thank you to read it again if you like. I'm a courier, wharfmaster. I'm not the seizure. I'm not the assizes. I'm a girl with a crate and cold feet, and the sooner both of us are warm, the better I'll like it."

She heard herself, and marveled. Not the words — the *voice*. Flat, practical, faintly aggrieved, the voice of a woman who had carried other people's freight for ten years and had opinions about it. Verren had taught her hands to draw coastlines she had never seen. Apparently he had taught her the rest of herself too.

Ossler set the manifest down. He looked at her — a long, level, river-police look, the look that had weighed ten thousand faces — and then, to her quiet horror, he stood up and came around the desk.

"Open the crate."

* * *

There is a kind of stillness that is not calm. Cyrus stood in it while her hands went to the rope handles and lifted the lid, and inside her skull a survey was running at desperate speed: *He'll lift the theodolite. He'll see the cedar floor. Will he think to knock on it? Will the oilcloth show at the edge? The letter is under the parallel rule's tray — if he lifts the tray —*

"Careful," she said, and made it sound like a courier's possessiveness. "Those instruments are evidence. You scratch the theodolite and it's my name on the loss, not yours."

Ossler's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. He reached into the crate with a wharfman's hands — careful, despite her, because river men respected instruments the way they respected anchors — and lifted out the theodolite. Turned it. Set it down. Took up the parallel rule in its felt-lined case, weighed it, put it back. His fingers walked the edges of the crate the way hers had walked a thousand coastlines, and Cyrus understood with terrible clarity that this man could find a false floor in his sleep.

He stopped at the cedar panel. He rapped it once, with one knuckle.

The sound was solid. She had planed that panel herself, glued it over a frame of true pine, weighted it while it dried, and she had made one mistake or no mistakes and she would not know which until —

"Good cedar," Ossler said. "Better than the crate." He looked at her. "Verren's work?"

"Mine," Cyrus said. "I pack for the workshop." And then, because the truth was the only thing left that would bear weight: "I apprenticed under him. Before."

Something moved behind Ossler's eyes — not suspicion; recognition, the look of a man finding a familiar current where he expected none. He put the theodolite back. He closed the lid. He lashed it with a length of cord from a hook on the wall, with quick, seamanlike turns, and wrote in his ledger: *One crate, instruments, V. workshop, to Kettering, assizes. Manifest stamped C.H. Freight passage: grain barge Maren Lise, when ice permits.*

"When ice permits," he said aloud. "That's the honest part. Ice is lying to us this morning — you saw it coming down the lane, I'll wager. It'll hold a day, maybe two. Maybe an hour. The *Maren Lise* sails the moment the channel opens, and not before, and no papers on this river change that. Not the Customs House's, not the commission's, not mine." He slid the manifest back to her across the hatch cover. "You'll wait with the freight. There's a shed — the second grain shed, the one with the green door. There's a stove in it and a wharfman named Piet who talks too much and sees nothing. You'll be warm, and you'll be out of the wind, and you'll be *here*, courier, where I can find you if anyone comes asking after Verren's crates."

The last sentence lay on the hatch cover between them like a drawn line on a map.

"And will someone come asking?" Cyrus asked, and held his eyes, because Verren had taught her that the eyes are the last coastline to be forged and the first to be believed.

"Someone always comes asking," Ossler said. "That's the river. Green door. Go on."

* * *

The green door shed smelled of grain dust and stove iron, and Piet, true to reputation, talked — about the ice, his knee, his brother-in-law, the price of tar — while Cyrus sat on a grain sack with the crate between her feet and the stove's heat finding the frozen places in her one by one, each thaw an ache, each ache a small surrender. She let Piet's voice wash over her and inside herself she did what she had done at every checkpoint: surveyed.

*The Tollgate Bridge: passed. The Customs House: passed. The Ice Wharf: passed — and yet not passed, because Ossler had said "someone always comes asking" the way a barometer says "falling," and she had read the pressure right.* Halloway would come. Not because Tomas had told him where she was — Tomas's betrayal had been older and wider than that, a seep, not a flood — but because there were only so many doors out of Vellmark in a frozen river, and Halloway was a man who could count doors.

She thought of Adaeze, somewhere in this city, under Halloway's eye since the burning of the false papers. She thought of the letter under the cedar floor, an inch from her boots, patient as the current. She thought of the signature she had not yet written anywhere — *Cyrus Okafor* — and of how it waited in her like the spring waited in the ice.

Piet paused for breath. Out through the shed's cracked window, the channel ice gave a long groan, shifted, held. The *Maren Lise* lay at her mooring, and at her rail, wrapped in a boat cloak, stood a broad-shouldered figure who raised one arm — not a wave, exactly; a signal, the kind Verren used to make from the workshop window when the coast was clear for Cyrus to cross the street.

Captain Ilsa Brand had seen her come aboard soon. The river was lying about itself this morning.

But under the ice, the current was already moving.

Down the wharf lane, unhurried, in a coat too fine for the weather, came a man with a ledger under his arm and a deputy surveyor's chain of office glinting at his throat, and behind him, at a distance that fooled no one, walked the chestnut boy, his brazier abandoned, his hands finally empty.

Cyrus put her palm flat against the crate's cedar floor, over the letter, over her name not yet signed, and began, quietly, to plan.
