## 16. Ilsa's Choice

The report arrived at dawn, folded twice and sealed with the garrison's wax, and Ilsa Brandt read it standing at her window while the fog still held the harbor in its gray fist.

*Re: the Corso matter. A source within the infirmary confirms the existence of a written record. Documents copied by hand. The nurse who reported it could not say where they are kept, only that they are moved nightly. The commander requests your immediate action.*

She read it three times. The words did not change. The harbor did not change. Somewhere below, a winch groaned as the *Vrijheid*'s crew began loading crates in the half-dark, and she thought: four days. Perhaps fewer.

A written record. Copied by hand. Moved nightly.

She had known, of course. She had known since the death lists—since the careful corrections in a clerk's hand that were not corrections at all but resurrections, names given back their spellings, their ranks, their ages, the small dignities the bureaucracy stripped away like bark from a tree. She had known when she let Rafael Oyelaran walk out of the lamplit room with his lie sitting between them like a third person neither would acknowledge. She had told herself then that she was being prudent. That a good officer verified before she acted. That there was no proof yet, and an officer without proof is only suspicion wearing a uniform.

Now there was proof. Now the proof had a name on it, or nearly did, and her orders were to find it before it reached foreign hands, and every instinct she had spent thirty-one years training into herself said: *do your duty.*

And beneath that instinct, quieter, older, something her mother had said once over mending: *Ilsa, you can obey a rule and betray everything the rule was for. The trick is knowing which day it is.*

She folded the report and put it in her tunic, against her heart, where she could feel its small weight like a stone swallowed whole.

* * *

Colonel Erich Voss received her in his office at the top of the customs house, a room designed to remind visitors of their altitude—or lack of it. The windows behind his desk looked down on the quay, and beyond the quay, through the thinning fog, the masts of the neutral ships stood up out of the water like the ribs of something beached and waiting.

"Captain Brandt." He did not rise. He never rose; it was part of the architecture of him. "You have my report."

"I have it, Colonel."

"And?"

"And I have questions." She kept her voice level, the voice of staff work, of agendas and minutes. "The source. A nurse at the infirmary. What is her name?"

"That is not your concern."

"With respect, it is entirely my concern. If I am to act on a denunciation, I need to know whether the informant saw these documents or heard about them. Whether she has a grudge. Whether she was pressured." She paused. "Whether she was paid."

Something moved behind Voss's eyes—not anger, not yet. Calculation. He was a heavy man gone soft in the way of men who had stopped carrying anything themselves and now only directed the carrying. His uniform fit him the way upholstery fits a chair. But his hands, she noticed, were steady, and his mind, she knew from three years of watching him, was a ledger of its own: debts, favors, sums owed and sums collected.

"You are thorough, Captain," he said. "That is why I chose you for this."

"Chose me for what, exactly? Find the documents? Or find them and bury them?"

The silence lasted four seconds. She counted them the way her father had taught her to count seconds between lightning and thunder—to know how far away the danger was.

Voss smiled. It was a good smile, practiced, warm at the edges. "Sit down, Captain."

"I prefer to stand."

"Sit *down*."

She sat. The chair was lower than his, another piece of architecture, and she refused to let it matter.

He opened a drawer—not the one with the brandy, the other one—and took out a folder, and laid it on the desk without opening it, the way a card player lays down a hand he already knows wins.

"Do you know what happened at the fish market?" he asked.

"I know what the official report says."

"The official report says collateral clearing. Unfortunate, regrettable, necessary." He said the words the way a man reads a shopping list. "Do you know what actually happened?"

"I have heard things."

"You have heard things." He nodded slowly. "Everyone has heard things. That is the problem with this city, Captain. Everyone has heard things, and no one can prove them, and so the things remain rumors, and rumors are weather. They pass." He tapped the folder once. "Lieutenant Corso thought he could do more than hear. He thought he could *prove*. He collected requisition orders, cargo manifests, payment authorizations. Do you know what those documents show?"

She said nothing. He wanted her to ask. She would not give him the satisfaction.

"They show," he continued, "that certain goods intended for the garrison—grain, medical supplies, coal—were diverted and sold through intermediaries in the fish-market quarter. They show that the clearing operation was not military necessity. It was the destruction of witnesses. Merchants who had seen the manifests. Clerks who had handled the payments." He leaned back. "They also show, if one reads carelessly, that the diversion was authorized under signatures connected to a certain family name. A family of some local standing. Oyelaran."

The name landed in her chest like a dropped instrument.

"You see the elegance of it," Voss went on, mild as a man discussing drainage. "Corso believed the family was complicit. Perhaps he even believed it right up until the end. But Corso was cleverer than people gave him credit for, and before he died he understood the truth—that the signatures were forged, that the family was chosen precisely because they were prominent enough to blame and foreign enough to hang without inquiry. Scapegoats, Captain. The oldest supply-chain technology in the world." He spread his hands. "So. Somewhere in this city is a packet of documents that proves both halves of that sentence. The theft, and the frame. And I want it found."

"And destroyed."

"And *secured*," he corrected gently. "Words matter. You taught me that, in your last report on the death lists. 'Names matter,' you wrote. Very moving. I agreed with you, which is why you are sitting here and not on a transport north." He opened the folder now, and turned it around, and slid it across the desk.

She looked. Photographs. Her own signature, countersigning death lists—the same lists where a clerk's careful hand had restored the erased names. Below the photographs, transcriptions. Her marginal notes, lifted from files she had assumed were confidential: *discrepancy noted. cause of death inconsistent with combat. recommend review.* Page after page of her own doubt, assembled into a case.

"Treason has a broad definition in wartime," Voss said. "Concealing evidence. Failing to report irregularities. Aiding, knowingly or unknowingly, the transmission of classified material to foreign parties." He let the last word sit. "You have been diligent, Captain. Diligence leaves fingerprints."

She made herself breathe. In through the nose, the way she'd been taught in the field hospital at Kessel, where panic killed faster than shrapnel. Out slow.

"You're threatening me."

"I am *informing* you," Voss said, "of the situation. There is a difference. A threat implies I want something from you that I cannot get otherwise. What I want, you will give me anyway, because you are an officer of this garrison and because the alternative is a court-martial conducted by men who owe me their careers, in a city where the fish market is a smoking hole and nobody asks why." He closed the folder. "Find the documents. Bring them to me personally. No clerks, no adjutants, no reports in writing. You will have my protection, your career, and the satisfaction of having served. Or—" he shrugged, a small, terrible shrug— "you will have a cell, and the documents will be found anyway, by someone less gentle than yourself, and the Oyelaran family will hang for a theft they did not commit, and nothing you ever wrote about names will have mattered at all."

The fog outside had burned off. Through the window she could see the quay clearly now, the crates going up the gangway of the *Vrijheid*, the stevedores small as ants, and she thought of Rafael Oyelaran walking among them somewhere with his careful hands and his careful lies, and she understood suddenly, completely, the shape of the trap: it was not built to catch him. It was built to catch *her*, and he was the bait, and the door was already closed behind her.

"May I speak freely, Colonel?"

"You may."

"What you just described—the theft, the forged signatures, the massacre to cover it—is treason. Actual treason. The kind we shoot people for. And your solution is to shoot the innocent instead."

"My solution," Voss said, "is stability. This port holds the line for the entire coast. If the theft becomes public, the supply contracts collapse, the command structure collapses, and the enemy walks in through a door we left open while we were busy confessing to each other. You may call that treason. I call it arithmetic." He stood, finally, and came around the desk, and for a moment she thought he might touch her shoulder, and she steeled herself against it the way one steels against a blow. He didn't. He simply looked down at her, and his voice lost its warmth entirely, and what was underneath was worse: tiredness. The genuine tiredness of a man who had done this many times and expected to do it many more.

"You are a good officer, Brandt. That is not flattery; I have ruined better officers than you for less. Good officers believe the institution is worth more than any single truth. Most of them die comfortable." He returned to his chair. "Four days. The Dutch ship sails, or it doesn't. You have until then. Dismissed."

She rose. Saluted. Her arm did not shake, and she was proud of that, in the small cold way one is proud of anything at all in a falling house.

At the door he spoke again, without looking up from his papers.

"Captain. The nurse who made the report. If anything happens to her before this is concluded, I will assume you silenced her, and I will add it to your file. Protect her. That is an order."

"Yes, Colonel."

"One more thing." Now he looked up. "Oyelaran. The nurse's brother. If he comes to you—if he has come to you already—you will tell me. Not because I suspect you. Because I suspect everyone, and it is kinder for us both if you confirm my suspicions early rather than late."

She met his eyes and gave him the truth, or the shape of it, which in that room was the only currency accepted.

"He has never come to me, Colonel."

It was not a lie. He had never come to her. She had gone to him—in the lamplight, in the infirmary, in the long silent accounting of two people who kept books the state would never audit.

She walked out past the sentry, down the stairs of the customs house, and out onto the quay, where the wind off the water hit her face like a hand.

* * *

She did not go back to the garrison. She walked. Past the bonded warehouses, past the coaling yard, past the chapel with its shell-shattered bell that rang wrong notes when the wind caught it, all the way to the seawall at the harbor's mouth, where the fishermen used to mend nets before the quarter was cleared, and where now there was only broken pilings and the smell of ash that rain had never quite washed out.

Duty inverted. She turned the phrase over as she walked, because naming a thing was the first step of every operation she had ever run, and this thing needed naming before it killed her.

Her oath was to the Crown, through the Army, through the chain of command. Every regulation, every promotion, every medal traced upward along that line like ivy along a wall. And now the man at the top of the wall had shown her the root system underneath: theft, murder, forgery, and himself at the center of it, watering it all with other people's blood. To obey him was to serve the rot. To defy him was treason by every statute she had memorized. There was no branch of the choice that bore clean fruit. There was only the question her mother had planted in her years ago, blooming at last in the worst possible season:

*Which day is it?*

She stood at the seawall and watched the *Vrijheid* riding at anchor, Dutch colors limp in the windless morning, and she did the arithmetic Voss had taught her, because his arithmetic was honest even when nothing else about him was.

If she reported Rafael Oyelaran, the documents would be seized and burned, the family would hang, the theft would be buried under a scapegoat's grave, and she would keep her rank and her pension and her comfortable death. If she warned him, she committed court-martial, prison, possibly the same wall the deserters had faced—but the papers might reach the outside world, and the outside world had a habit, slow and unreliable but real, of making inquiries. Of reading names aloud.

Somewhere in the city, a nurse had already sold her courage for whatever Voss's agents paid. Somewhere Tomás of Rope Street—she had read the arrest report an hour before Voss's summons, and the timing had told her everything—sat in a stockade cell, arrested on charges thin as paper, and she understood now that his arrest was not a coincidence but a move, pressure applied to a board she hadn't known she was playing on.

They were closing the net, and the net was made of her own regulations.

She took out the report and read it once more, standing in the wind. *Documents copied by hand. Moved nightly.* Then she took out her pencil, and on the back of it, in the shorthand she had learned as a signals officer and never reported knowing, she wrote four words:

*Ledger real. Ship soon.*

She did not yet know whom she would give it to. She did not yet know if she was warning him or condemning him, saving the evidence or delivering it to the man who would burn it. She only knew that the woman who had walked up the stairs of the customs house an hour ago no longer existed, and the officer walking back along the seawall was someone new, someone whose loyalty had finally, irrevocably, changed addresses.

Behind her, on the quay, a sergeant was hurrying toward her with a message clutched in his fist, and even at fifty paces Ilsa could see by his face that it was bad news, and even at fifty paces she found she was already deciding whose side she would take when she heard it.
