## 17. The Cell Below

They let Tomás keep his boots. He had not expected that. In the stories—the ones told in the doorways of Rope Street, the ones he'd half-believed and half-laughed at—they took your boots first, then your belt, then your dignity, in that order, like a ritual as old as hanging. But the sergeant who booked him had looked at Tomás's shoes, at the fine stitching his brother had once called *clerk's shoes, church shoes, shoes for a man who never runs*, and had waved him through with something close to pity.

So now he sat on a plank bed in the cell below the stockade, wearing his boots, and found that this small mercy was worse than cruelty. Cruelty would have given him something to curse. The boots just gave him time.

Three days. Three days since they'd come for him at the rope loft with the smell of pitch still on his hands, three days since the nurse—Serafina, her name was Serafina, he'd known her laugh before he knew her face—had stood in the doorway of the infirmary and pointed at him with her chin, unable to look at him directly, the way you point at a dog you're giving away. Three days of questions conducted in a tone so reasonable it was almost gentle. *Who taught you the seals? Who supplied the stock paper? Where is the ledger?*

He had given them nothing, which surprised him more than anyone. Not out of courage—he wanted to be clear about that, wanted it written down somewhere, in one of those ledgers people kept about other people: *Tomás Ferro, coward, Rope Street, acted bravely by accident.* He had given them nothing because every lie he constructed collapsed under its own weight within a sentence, and because silence, he discovered, was the only forgery he could still execute flawlessly. Silence was just a blank document. He had spent twenty years learning what blanks could do.

The cell smelled of wet stone and old blood and, beneath it, the harbor—that low mineral breath that got into everything in Vela Cruz, into bread and hair and the pages of books. Through the slit window, high and barred, he could see a wedge of sky going from grey to the color of pewter, and once, briefly, a gull, hanging in the wind like a scrap of paper someone had lost.

His brother had liked gulls. That thought arrived without permission, the way all thoughts of Emilio arrived now, and Tomás pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until the dark behind them went red.

*Emilio Ferro. Listed among the executed deserters.*

He had seen the name in Rafael Oyelaran's ledger, in that careful, upright hand, each letter standing at attention like men who refused to break formation even for death. *Ferro, E., private, 3rd Coastal. Executed 14 March. Cause recorded: desertion. Witnessed by: none.* None. As if the word itself were a wound the clerk had been too honest to dress.

Tomás had laughed when Rafael showed him the page. That was the shameful part, the part he kept turning over like a coin with two bad faces: he had laughed, one short ugly bark, because some animal part of him had believed the ledger was a trick, a sales pitch, the nurse showing him the merchandise. And then he had read the date again, and the entry beneath Emilio's name—a boy named Quist, seventeen years old, executed the same morning—and the laughter had died in his throat so completely that he could still feel the place where it had been, a cold hollow, like a tooth pulled without anesthesia.

Emilio had not deserted. Emilio had come home on leave in February with a broken wrist and a girl's ribbon tied around it, laughing, saying the war couldn't hold a man who could forge a signature that well, saying he'd be out by summer. Then the orders came down, and Emilio had gone back because their mother raised them to believe that orders were like weather—you complained but you obeyed—and in March the weather had killed him and called it desertion, and some clerk with handwriting very much like Tomás's own had written *none* where witnesses should have been.

*They shot him for running,* Tomás thought, *and I'm the one who never ran from anything except myself.*

The bolt in the door turned. Tomás did not stand; standing felt like volunteering. He watched the door swing open and saw, framed in the lamplight of the corridor, a figure carrying a medical satchel, and for one absurd heartbeat he thought: *they've sent a doctor, they're going to shoot me after all, they want me healthy for it.*

Then the light moved and it was Rafael.

"Prisoner Ferro," Rafael said, in a voice pitched to carry to whatever guard was listening, "complaints of fever, per the duty roster." He stepped inside, and the door closed behind him with a sound like a book shutting.

"I don't have a fever," Tomás said.

"You have a fever now." Rafael set the satchel on the floor and knelt beside it, opening it with the unhurried economy of a man who had done ten thousand small medical tasks and could do them in his sleep. Inside, among the bandages and the brown bottles, Tomás saw the glint of a thermometer case, a folded blanket, and—wedged flat against the leather lining—an envelope wrapped in oilcloth.

Tomás's mouth went dry. "You shouldn't have come."

"No," Rafael agreed. "Probably not." He took Tomás's wrist with two fingers, professional, counting a pulse that Tomás knew was hammering. "Slow it down. You look like a man being examined, not a man being visited. There's a difference. Guards can tell the difference if you teach them to look for it."

"Is that in a medical text?"

"It's in six days of practice." Rafael released his wrist and began, absurdly, to unpack the satchel properly—bandage roll, iodine, a tin of carbolic salve—arranging them on the plank bed as though preparing a treatment. His hands were steady. His eyes were not. They had the look Tomás had learned to recognize in mirrors during the worst weeks: a man reading something terrible by lamplight and refusing to blink until he'd finished the line.

"They arrested me instead of you," Tomás said. "I've been doing the arithmetic. It doesn't balance. You were the one copying documents at midnight. I was just—" he gestured vaguely at his own hands, "—decorating."

"They took you because taking you costs them nothing. A disgraced clerk with forged papers in his possession? Open and shut. They'll try you in an afternoon." Rafael said it flatly, without softening, and Tomás loved him a little for that, and hated him, and understood that both feelings were the same feeling wearing different coats. "I've been working on getting you out."

"Have you." Tomás leaned back against the stone. "And how does a nurse get a man out of a military stockade? Bribery? I know bribery. Bribery is my native language. And I'm telling you, there isn't enough money left in Vela Cruz—"

"There's no money involved." Rafael reached into the satchel and drew out the oilcloth packet. He held it between them, low, below the sightline of the window slit. "There's this."

Tomás looked at it and understood, in the way you understand a wave is coming before it breaks, exactly what it was. "No," he said.

"It's the last pass. Your duplicate. The one you hid in the—"

"I know where I hid it, I hid it, that's the whole point of hiding things—" His voice cracked upward and he strangled it, dropped it to a whisper. "That pass is for *you*. Garrison gate, customs house, quay. All three countersigned. It took me four hours to cut the customs seal alone, do you understand? Four hours, and my hands shook for two of them, and I drank half a bottle of aguardiente to stop them and then the other half because they started again. That document is the single best thing I have ever made. It is better than anything I made before the war and it will certainly be better than anything I make after it, because there is not going to be an after it, not for me, so you will take that pass and you will get on that Dutch tub and you will—"

"Tomás."

"—and you will send it to a newspaper, and they will print the names, all of them, including—" and here the sentence he had been building for three days, the sentence he had carried up from the cell's floor every morning like a bucket from a well, finally broke over him. He stopped. He put his hand over his mouth. Above his fingers his eyes had gone bright and furious, and he blinked hard, twice, refusing, absolutely refusing, because if he started he did not think there was a bottom to it, and a man in a cell cannot afford an ocean.

"Including Emilio," Rafael finished quietly.

"Don't say it like that."

"Like what?"

"Like a name in your ledger. Like it helps. Like ink is a—" Tomás pulled a breath through his nose, held it, let it go. When he spoke again his voice was level, almost bored, and he was proud of that, prouder than he had ever been of any forgery. "Ink isn't a resurrection, nurse. I've read your pages. They're beautiful. They're also just paper. My brother is just as dead whether some Dutch printer sets his name in type or not."

"Yes," Rafael said. "He is."

Tomás stared at him. He had expected argument. He had built the whole rampart of his cynicism expecting argument, expecting the nurse's stubborn, maddening faith to come marching at him so he could fire down on it. Instead Rafael simply agreed, and the agreement disarmed the wall entirely.

"But," Rafael went on, "his name is dead too, right now. Shot twice. Once in March and once in the report. What I write doesn't bring him back. It brings back the *name*. And a name, once it's out, does things. It makes questions. Questions make inquiries. Inquiries make the next Emilio harder to bury." He tapped the oilcloth packet against his knee. "That's all it is. I don't promise more. I'm a nurse, not a priest."

Tomás laughed—really laughed, short and wrecked. "A nurse, not a priest. You should put that on a sign. Hang it over the infirmary. Half your colleagues would need it tattooed on the inside of their eyelids."

"Take the pass."

"No."

"Tomás—"

"*No.*" He said it firmly, and then, because firmness had never once worked in his life, he said it again, louder, and heard how thin it rang against the stone. He scrubbed a hand down his face. "Listen to me. Listen. I have thought about nothing else for three days, and I am, whatever else I am, the best thinker in this city when it comes to documents and exits—it's the only subject on which I flatter myself. If you give me that pass, I will use it. Of course I will. I'm a coward; I told you that the first night, on the stairs, with the lamp. I will walk out of here on your pass and I will feel wonderful and I will hate myself in Rotterdam or wherever until I die of it. And they will know. Don't you see? A prisoner escapes the day before the ship sails, using papers only one forger in Vela Cruz could have cut? They won't search the ship harder—they'll burn it at anchor. They'll turn the harbor inside out. Your evidence dies with my freedom, and I'll have traded the only thing I've ever made that mattered for the only thing I've ever wanted that didn't." He shook his head slowly. "It's bad design, nurse. Sloppy work. I'm ashamed of you."

Rafael was silent for a long moment. In the corridor beyond the door, boots passed and faded. Somewhere above, a bell marked the hour—five, Tomás thought, or six; the days in the cell had begun to run together like wet ink.

"The trial," Rafael said at last. "For the forging. It's a flogging and two years, if the commander wants it quiet. Hard labor, maybe. You could survive that."

"And if he doesn't want it quiet?" Tomás smiled, and found, to his surprise, that the smile was real. "If he needs a scapegoat more than a conviction? You told me yourself how these people work. They erased a massacre with the phrase *collateral clearing*. Do you think a man like that hesitates over a clerk?" He shrugged. "Maybe I hang. Maybe I rot. Either way, I die knowing the names got out. That's a better ledger entry than most men get, and I never even kept one."

"That's not grief talking?"

"Grief is talking constantly. Grief hasn't stopped talking since Tuesday." Tomás looked at his hands, at the ink stains in the creases that no amount of lye would ever fully lift. "But underneath the grief there's a thing that's very calm, and the calm thing says: *you spent your life making false versions of true documents. Here, at the end, is one true version of a false world. Carry it out or die holding the door.* The calm thing wins. The calm thing always wins; that's why I drink."

Rafael looked at him for a long moment. Then he did something Tomás did not expect: he took the oilcloth packet and placed it, gently, on the plank bed between them—not offered, not withdrawn. Just set down, like a card played face-up.

"Then we do it properly," Rafael said. "Because if I'm taking your pass, I'm not taking it as a gift. I'm taking it as a debt. And I pay debts."

"You're a nurse with no money and no country that will claim him. What could you possibly—"

"Serafina."

The name landed in the cell like a dropped glass. Tomás went still.

"She reported you under pressure. Her brother is in the commander's custody—she told the agents what they already suspected, and she has hated herself for three days, and yesterday she came to me and confessed it and asked what she could do." Rafael's voice stayed level, clinical, but his eyes had that lamplight look again. "She can testify that the agents coerced her. She can name the man who pressured her—Lieutenant Voss, whom I have in my ledger at the fish market on the fourteenth of March, contrary to his sworn statement. Her testimony plus my records could reduce your sentence to the flogging and the two years. Quietly. After the ship sails, when the commander needs the whole business buried, not litigated."

Tomás stared at him. "You'd trade that? Trade Serafina's safety, trade Voss, trade a piece of your evidence—your *evidence*, the holy ledger—for me?"

"For a witness who can keep you alive long enough to serve two years and walk out." Rafael picked up the packet and pressed it into Tomás's hands, closing Tomás's fingers around it with his own, and his hands were warm and steady and did not shake at all. "You told me once that everything is leverage. Fine. This is leverage. Take it."

For a moment Tomás could not speak. The ocean he had been refusing all week rose in him again, and this time he let one wave of it through—one, no more—his eyes stinging, his jaw tight, blinking at the ceiling slit where the pewter sky was going gold at its lowest edge. Dawn tomorrow. The *Vrijheid* loaded at dawn.

"Run without me," he said, when he could trust his voice. "That's the whole speech. That's the eulogy. Run without me, nurse, and don't you dare look back at the gate, because I'll be watching from the little window and I want to remember you walking, not turning around." He swallowed. "And when they print the names—when they print them aloud, in whatever language the Dutch use for justice—I want you to find Emilio's. Third column. *Ferro, E.* Read it twice. Once for me."

Rafael stood, repacking the satchel with those same unhurried hands, and at the door he paused. "Two years," he said. "Survive two years. I'll be waiting at the gate when they open it, and I'll bring a pen, and you can start a new ledger—true entries only."

"A forger keeping honest books," Tomás said. "The scandal would kill our mother."

The bolt turned. The door opened. Lamplight spilled across the floor, and Rafael stepped through it, and was gone, and the cell closed around Tomás like a fist around a coal.

He sat for a while with the packet in his hands. Then, because there was nothing else to do, because the calm thing had won and the boots were still on his feet, he unwrapped the oilcloth and looked at his own work by the grey-gold light of the last dawn he might ever see in Vela Cruz.

It was good. God help him, it was good—the seal crisp, the countersignatures confident, the watermarked stock catching the light like the real thing, because it *was* the real thing, bought with the last of his savings from a quartermaster who owed him. Four hours for the customs seal. Shaking hands. Half a bottle of aguardiente.

The best thing he had ever made, and it was a door, and the door was for someone else.

Above, the bell rang the hour again, and this time he counted it: six. Eight bells to sailing. Somewhere up there, a Dutch freighter was taking on cargo in the fog, and a nurse was folding a ledger into his coat, and a captain named Brandt was—doing whatever careful women did in the hour before they decided everything.

Tomás slid the pass into his boot, against his ankle, where a man keeps the things he cannot afford to lose.

Then he lay back on the plank bed, folded his hands on his chest like a man posing for his own monument, and began, silently, patiently, to plan how a prisoner in a locked cell gets himself moved to the infirmary by morning—because if Rafael Oyelaran was going to run past three checkpoints on Tomás Ferro's paper, then Tomás Ferro intended, by fraud, bribery, or a beautifully timed fake seizure, to be conscious enough at the little window to watch him go.
