## 5. The Village That Watches

The fever broke on the third day, which was fortunate, because on the fourth day Freya Crane ran out of patience for nursing me and told me so in the gentlest possible way, which was by putting a bucket, a bar of soap, and a set of borrowed clothes on the floor beside my cot and saying nothing at all.

The clothes were Callum's. I knew it before she said it, knew it from the way she handled them — not tenderly, exactly, but with the careful flatness of a woman carrying something that might still go off. They fit me well enough. The dead man had been broader through the shoulders than I was and narrower everywhere else, so I went down the tower stairs looking like a coat that had been left out in weather.

"You'll want to walk," Freya said. It was not a suggestion. "You'll want to eat, too, and I've nothing here but what the sea gives and what I'm willing to gut. Go to Port Ellar. The Gull's Rest does a broth that's mostly salt and rumor, but it's hot."

"And you'll be here."

"I'm always here." She said it like a sentence she'd served out already, three years of it, and had the key to the cell hanging on a nail by the door.

I want to record, in my own defense, that I did not want to go. My legs were still disagreeing with each other about the fundamental business of walking, and my head had a bell in it that rang whenever I turned too fast. But there was a larger consideration, and it was this: I had been carrying a dead man's letters for eleven days across half of Scotland, and I had not yet had the chance to find out whether anyone else was carrying them too. Or carrying me. A courier who's been fished out of the surf by the very woman he was delivering to is either the luckiest man alive or the most carefully managed one, and I have never in my life been lucky.

So I walked to Port Ellar.

* * *

The village announced itself the way a cat announces itself — you heard it before you saw it, and when you did see it, it was already watching you.

Port Ellar sat in the fold of the coast like something the tide had left and the land had grudgingly agreed to keep: one street, crooked as a fishhook, running down to a harbor that was more stones than water at low tide. There was a kirk with a roof like a dropped hat, a smokehouse breathing its slow grey breath, a chandlery, a bakehouse, and the Gull's Rest, which was identifiable as an inn chiefly by the sign, the gull on it having been painted by someone who had clearly never met a gull and was working from a frightened description.

I had been in a hundred villages like it, or near enough. Orphans learn to read a place quickly. You learn which doorways are kind and which are arithmetic — which faces calculate what you might cost them. I had thought myself good at it.

I had not accounted for being a stranger in a village whose whole trade was strangers, and whose whole survival, I came to understand within ten minutes, depended on knowing exactly which strangers to fear.

The first thing that happened was that conversation stopped. Not all at once — that would have been crude, and these were not crude people. It stopped the way a tide goes out, so gradually that you only noticed when you were standing in wet sand. A woman mending nets by the chandlery kept her hands moving and her eyes elsewhere. Two old men on a bench achieved a silence so complete it had texture. A boy of perhaps nine looked at me, looked back down the street toward the point where the lighthouse stood, looked at me again, and then ran off with the urgent purpose of a child who has news worth more than his supper.

I was, I realized, wearing the dead keeper's coat. In a village of perhaps two hundred souls, where everyone had known Callum Crane's shoulders as well as their own doorframes, I was walking down the high street dressed in a ghost, and every one of them knew it, and every one of them was deciding, silently and in the manner of people long practiced at it, what to do about me.

I did what I'd been taught. I became boring. I hunched slightly, let my limp show more than it needed to, and developed an expression of a man thinking about nothing harder than his boots. Invisibility, I have said before and will say again, is a currency, and I had been spending it my whole life. But I felt, walking that street, the strange sensation of a man counting coins and finding fewer than he remembered.

The Gull's Rest was low and dark and smelled of peat smoke, old ale, and the particular despair of floorboards that have been mopped for a century and never once been dry. The innkeeper was a broad woman with forearms like hawser rope and a face that had decided, sometime around the Crimean War, to stop giving anything away for free.

"Broth," I said. "And bread, if the bread's alive."

"It's alive," she said, in a tone suggesting the bread had better be grateful. "Sixpence."

I paid. I took a table in the corner — the corner being the courier's natural habitat, the place where you can put your back to a wall and watch the door and pretend you're doing neither — and the broth arrived, and it was, as promised, mostly salt and rumor, and it was hot, and I was so hungry I nearly wept into it.

I was halfway through the bread when the two men came in.

I knew they were trouble before I knew why, which is the way trouble usually arrives. It was the coats. Everyone in the Gull's Rest was dressed the way people dress in fishing villages, which is to say in wool that has been patched so many times the original garment is now a minority shareholder. These two wore city coats — good ones, too, not the rough tweed of Edinburgh clerks but the dark, well-cut, expensive kind that says *I am not from here and I do not need to be*. One was tall and thin, with a narrow face and small quick hands that kept moving even when the rest of him was still, like a man playing an invisible instrument. The other was shorter, wider, grey at the temples, with the settled heaviness of a man who has spent his life being obeyed and sees no reason to alter the arrangement.

They did not look at me. That was the first wrong thing. A stranger walks into a village inn wearing a dead man's coat and a week of fever on his face, and every local eye has already been on him for an hour — but these two walked past me as if I were furniture, and furniture, I knew from long experience, was exactly what they took everyone for.

They took a table near the window. The tall one ordered tea, in English so precise it sounded translated, and then he did something that made the bell in my head ring hard enough to hurt.

He opened a leather portfolio, took out paper and a pencil, and began to draw.

I turned my shoulder to them and looked out the window at the harbor, and let my eyes do the work while my face did nothing. The window gave a fair view down the crook of the street to the water, and beyond it, when the cloud lifted, to the headland north — Skerrow Point, the tower standing against the sky like a finger raised in permanent admonition. The tall man was drawing the coastline. Not idly — I could see the economy of his hand, the way he measured with his pencil against his thumb, marking distances, noting the reefs, the soundings, the little islets. And beside the drawing, in a small neat hand, he was writing. Numbers. Bearings. And once — I caught it for only a second when he turned a page — a word I would have given a great deal to read entire, but of which I caught the first four letters plainly enough.

*Sker.*

The older one drank nothing. That was the second wrong thing. A man who orders nothing in an inn is not a man resting; he is a man working, and his work was watching. He watched the innkeeper. He watched the smokehouse boy who came in for a jug of ale. He watched, with a long unhurried look, the woman who had been mending nets, when she passed the window, and she saw him watching and did not look back, and the way she did not look back was itself a kind of answer, and he noted it, I am certain, in whatever ledger he kept behind his eyes.

I finished my broth. I finished my bread. I sat very still and did the arithmetic that couriers do, the arithmetic I had been doing since a man I knew only as the Engineer put an oilskin packet in my hands in a Glasgow close and told me, with a smile I had trusted because I had wanted to, *to Skerrow Point, lad, and if anyone asks, you're carrying nothing but your own bad luck.*

Eleven days. Eleven days of back roads and borrowed carts and one bad night in a bothy where I woke at every sound. I had told myself the fever was my own, the fall from the rocks my own clumsiness in the squall. But the squall had come up fast, and I had taken the cliff path in the dark because I was sure — *sure* — that I had seen a lantern behind me on the moor, and Freya had said, in her flat way, that no one walked that coast in a storm, no one ever had in three years, and where had I said I'd come from, exactly?

I had said Glasgow. I had said I'd come by the coast road. I had been feverish and honest, which is the most dangerous combination there is.

The tall man closed his portfolio. The older one rose, dropped a coin on the table — too much, deliberately, the way city men do when they want a village to remember them as generous rather than remember their faces — and the two of them went out. Through the window I watched them stand a moment in the street, and I watched the tall one look north, at the point, for exactly as long as a man looks at a thing he has already measured.

Then they walked down toward the harbor, and out of my sight, and I sat in the salt-smelling dark of the Gull's Rest with my heart going like a drum in a parade and made myself think it through, because panic is just arithmetic done badly.

They had not followed me from Glasgow — or if they had, they'd have taken me on the road, in the night, where a body is easily lost. No. They had come the other way, the way I should have thought of and hadn't: by sea, or by the southern road, ahead of me or behind me, to the destination instead of the courier. Why chase the letter when you can be waiting at the address? And if they were at the address, then they knew it. They knew Skerrow Point. They might already know that their packet had arrived — might be waiting only to see who had carried it, and what the keeper's widow would do with it, and whether there was anything at the point worth the trouble of a warrant.

And I had walked into the one inn in the village, in the dead man's coat, and ordered broth, and paid with a Glasgow mint, and limped like a man with a story, and the boy had run off down the street with news worth more than his supper.

The innkeeper came to clear my bowl. Up close her face was less a wall than I'd thought — there was weather in it, and behind the weather, something that might have been curiosity, or might have been the local equivalent of mercy, rationed.

"You're stopping at the light, then," she said. Not a question.

"A few days," I said. "Till my legs agree with each other."

"Aye." She stacked the bowl on the tray and looked at me a moment longer, and what was in her face then I couldn't read, and it occurred to me that not being able to read it was the whole point of it. "Those two gents," she said, mildly, to the bowl rather than to me, "asked after the light too. Asked was the keeper a widow still, and did she take visitors, and had anyone new come to the village of late." She straightened. "I told them what I tell all folk who ask after that family. That the Cranes keep to themselves, and that what comes ashore at Skerrow Point is the sea's business, and the sea doesn't gossip."

She turned to go, and then, without looking at me, she added, in the same mild voice, the voice of a woman remarking on the price of herring:

"Back road to the point runs behind the kirk. Shorter, and out of the wind."

She went. I sat there and understood that I had just been given something I had no right to expect and less ability to repay: a warning, a route, and a piece of advice about which faces in this village were kind, delivered in the language of a woman who would deny all three to her dying day.

I paid for the broth a second time — she took that coin without comment, which was the closest thing to a blessing I was going to get — and I went out into the street.

The village was still watching. It would always be watching; that was its nature and its armor. But as I turned up toward the kirk and the back road, I passed the net-mending woman, and she did not look at me, and her hands did not pause, and yet as I went by she said, softly, to her nets, in the voice of a woman remarking on the weather:

"Tell her the tide's turning early tonight."

I walked fast after that. The back road was mud and gorse and the wind coming off the moor with the smell of rain in it, and I walked it with the dead man's coat collar up and my mind doing its arithmetic faster and faster, because the tide was turning early, and the tide was not the only thing.

They had asked had anyone new come to the village of late.

The answer, as of this morning, was yes. And by nightfall, if the boy with the valuable news had done his work, they would have a description of me, and the coat I wore, and the direction I had gone — north, toward the light, toward the widow, toward the oilskin packet that I no longer had because it was in her hands, and God only knew what she would do with it, burn it or read it or worse, and I had left her alone with it for a whole afternoon while I sat in an inn eating bread.

The first spits of rain came across the moor as I walked, and behind me, far back down the coast road, so far I might have imagined it, there was a glint of something that was not gull-wing and not wave-flash, moving north along the shore, keeping pace.

I put my head down and walked faster. Invisibility, I had always believed, was a currency. Standing on that road with the rain coming in and the glint keeping pace behind me, I understood, with the clarity that only fear provides, that my purse was nearly empty — and that the only person left to spend what remained on was a woman who had every reason in the world to put me back out with the tide.
