## 14. Betrayal at the Inn

There is a particular kind of fool who trusts a boy because the boy reminds him of himself. I know this now. I knew it then, too, if I'm honest — that's the worst of it. I looked at Ewan Tait, fourteen years old, all elbows and hunger and a grin he deployed like currency, and I thought: *there but for the grace of one dead engineer go I.* And because I saw myself in him, I gave him things. Small things. A shilling for running messages. A story about London. The location of a bothy in the cliffs where a widow's dead husband had built a machine that could burn through fog.

I didn't give him the last one all at once. That's what I told myself, anyway, on the walk back from Port Ellar with the wind picking at my collar like a customs officer. I'd only said *the cliffs north of the point*, I'd only let him carry one crate of brass fittings up the path, I'd only trusted him with the edges of the secret and not its heart. But secrets are like water. Give them an edge and they'll find their own way to the center.

The inn was called the Gull and Anchor, which was optimistic on both counts, as there were few gulls that day and no anchor in living memory had held off Skerrow Point. I'd gone in for bread and lamp oil and the small human comfort of hearing voices that weren't Freya Crane's or my own. What I got instead was the sound of my own name spoken by a stranger.

"—the courier," someone was saying, low, through the door of the private parlor. "Youngish. Dark hair. Came ashore near three weeks back."

I stopped in the passage with my parcel under my arm and my heart doing something unscheduled against my ribs.

"Description's useless," said a second voice, dry as ledger paper. "Half the coast is youngish and dark-haired. It's the boy I want. The Tait boy. He runs errands for everyone. Errands leave tracks."

I knew that voice. Mr. Quill. I'd heard it once before, through a workshop door in another life, delivering a packet I hadn't been allowed to open. It was the voice of a man who read contracts aloud for pleasure.

I should have left. Any sensible courier would have set down his bread, walked out the front door, and kept walking until Scotland ran out. Instead I stood in the dim passage smelling of tallow and old rope and listened, because Callum Crane had taught me that information was the only cargo worth dying over, and apparently some lessons take.

"He'll talk for coin?" This was a third voice, younger, eager. Hargrove, I'd learn later. Quill's shadow with better boots.

"He'll talk for coin," Quill said, "because everyone talks for coin. The ones who say they won't simply have a higher price." A pause, the creak of a chair. "Start with a crown. Go to five if he weeps. Boys always weep."

I stood there in the dark of the passage and felt something crack in me that I would spend a long time learning the shape of. Not surprise — I can't claim surprise. Quill had found me once already; of course he'd cast lines into every pool on the coast. No, what cracked was smaller and stupider than that. It was the memory of Ewan Tait's face when I'd taught him to skip stones off the harbor wall, the fierce concentration, the shout of triumph when the stone went four, five, six times. I had looked at that face and seen a rescue in progress. Mine, not his. I'd been so busy saving myself in miniature that I never asked what a hungry boy does when a man in a city coat offers him a month of meals for a sentence of words.

He'd do what I would have done at fourteen. That was the crack. He'd do exactly what I would have done.

* * *

I did not go home by the road.

The road from Port Ellar to Skerrow Point ran along the top of the low cliffs, exposed as a lie, and if Quill had bought the boy then Quill had bought directions, and if he had directions he might have men on the road. So I went the fisherman's way, down through the black rocks where the tide chewed at the foundations of everything, with my bread clutched to my chest like a talisman and the weather coming down out of the northwest in long gray smears.

It had been threatening all day — the barometer in the inn passage had been reading lower than a sinner's opinion of itself — and by the time I reached the first headland it had made good on the threat. Rain came sideways. The sea, which I had spent three weeks learning to respect and still hadn't learned to fear properly, stood up on its hind legs against the rocks and roared.

I climbed. God, how I climbed. The fisherman's path was less a path than a suggestion the rock occasionally honored, and twice I went on hands and knees with the wind pressing me flat like a hand on the back of my neck. Somewhere below me the surf detonated against the skerries, and above me the gulls hung motionless in the rain, watching, taking notes for nobody.

And behind me — I want to be precise about this, because later accounts would differ — behind me, faint under the wind, a whistle. Two notes, repeated. Not a shepherd's call. Shepherds around Ellar used a rising trill; Maren Boyd had demonstrated it once, mocking the men who couldn't master it. This was two flat notes, even-spaced, deliberate. A signal. The kind of thing you teach hired men so they don't shoot each other on a moor.

I stopped behind an outcrop of gneiss older than sin and looked back the way I'd come.

Two figures on the skyline. City coats, wrong for the weather, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who believed the world was a corridor built for them. One raised an arm and pointed — not at me, I think, or not knowingly, but at the lighthouse. At the tower standing up out of the murk with its light burning steady through the rain, the way it had burned for three years, the way Freya Crane kept it burning the way other people keep their hearts beating: without discussion.

They were between me and the road. They were not, thank whatever gods look after couriers, between me and the moor.

The moor was the problem. The moor was two miles of peat hag and heather and standing water with no paths and no shelter and a sky that had decided to come down and join the land. Crossing it in fair weather was a knee-deep argument. Crossing it in this, with the light going, was the sort of thing that ended with your name in the kirk records and your boots in a bog until Judgment Day.

Behind me, the two notes whistled again, closer, or the wind had turned to carry them closer, which amounted to the same thing.

I went onto the moor.

* * *

Here is what I knew about the moor, gathered from fishermen in the way you gather things from fishermen, which is to say in fragments wrapped in insults: stay off the black pools, they're deeper than they look; follow the deer tracks if you find them, the deer know the dry ground; and whatever happens, keep the sea on your left, because the land slopes down to it eventually and the slope will bring you to the point whether you deserve it or not.

The sea stayed on my left. The sea made sure I knew about it, too — a constant gray roar out of the corner of my eye, sometimes visible, sometimes just a sound, sometimes a presence so total I could have believed the whole world was water and I was crossing the bottom of it.

The rain found the gap between my collar and my neck within the first hundred yards and settled in for the duration. My boots filled. The heather grabbed at my ankles like the hands of the recently drowned, which is a comparison I did not make at the time because I was saving my imagination for more practical purposes, such as imagining what Quill's men would do if they caught me, and what Quill would do to Freya if they caught her without warning, and how fast a man could cross two miles of bog if the alternative was unbearable.

Faster than sensible, it turned out. I fell four times. Once I went in to the hip in a black pool that had advertised itself as heather, and came up gasping with cold that closed on my chest like a fist, and lay there for one full second — one — considering the elegant simplicity of just staying. The water was quiet down there. The water didn't ask questions.

Then I thought of Freya Crane standing in the lamp room with her sleeves rolled, checking mercury seals by candlelight, saying *hold it steady, Ash, no — steady means steady* in a voice that had forgotten how to be gentle and was relearning it one instruction at a time. And I thought of her opening her door to two men in city coats with a forged warrant and no warning at all.

I got up. I got up the way you get up when staying down has stopped being an option and become an insult.

Somewhere in the middle third of the moor, the whistle came again, and this time it wasn't behind me. It was ahead and to the right, thin through the rain, and I understood with a lurch that they'd split, that one of them had cut across to flank the point, that the corridor was closing. I also understood something else, something that arrived not as a thought but as a fact, the way cold arrives: they weren't hunting me. I was a courier. Couriers were replaceable. They were converging on the tower, and I was incidental, and the only value I had left in the world was speed.

So I spent it. All of it. I ran the last mile of the moor like a man being paid by the yard, which in a sense I was, though the coin was a woman's life and the exchange rate was terrible. I ran through peat and rain and the last gray rags of daylight, and I fell twice more and stopped counting, and when the ground finally tipped downward toward the point and the lighthouse swam up out of the murk — a black finger with a burning nail — I was making sounds I'd be embarrassed to describe, and I did not care.

* * *

The door of the tower opened before I knocked. Later she claimed she'd heard me coming, that I ran like a cart with a loose wheel, but I've never believed it. I think she just knew. Three years alone teaches a person the exact weight of footsteps that mean something.

"Tobias." She took in the state of me — the bog to the hips, the blood on one palm where a rock had introduced itself, the breathing like a broken bellows — and her face did the thing it did instead of expression. "You're bleeding."

"The moor objected to me." I got the words out between breaths. "Freya. The boy. Ewan Tait."

Something moved behind her eyes. She stepped aside to let me in, and I stood dripping on her clean floor, and I said it plainly because there was no other way left to say anything.

"I heard them at the inn. Quill and another man. They were buying him — a crown to start, five if he wept. And I don't know what he told them, but I know what he *could* tell them. The cliffs. The path. Maybe the bothy itself, maybe—" My voice cracked on the boy's name and I hated it. "Maybe everything."

She was quiet for a moment. Rain hammered the glass of the lantern room overhead, and the light turned and turned above us, patient as ever, sweeping its beam across a sea full of nothing.

"You trusted him," she said at last. Not an accusation. An inventory.

"I trusted him because he was me," I said. "Hungry and grinning and ready to be useful to anyone who noticed he existed. I handed him the edge of the secret and told myself the center was safe." I wiped my face with a hand that left it no cleaner. "Quill was right, you know. Everyone talks for coin. I just thought the boy's price would be higher."

Freya crossed to the window, where the beam swept past and lit her face in intervals, on-off, on-off, like a decision being made over and over. When she spoke, her voice had the flat calm I'd learned to dread, the keeper's calm, the calm that meant the storm inside had been logged and scheduled.

"Then we have until they finish their supper," she said. "The bothy can't be saved — if they know where it is, it's already lost. But the keystone isn't there anymore, and neither are the letters, and the core mechanism is in pieces under this floor." She turned. "They'll come here expecting to find a workshop. They'll find a lighthouse. There's a difference, Mr. Ash, and tonight we're going to make it a fortress."

"And the boy?"

She paused. I watched her weigh it, watched grief and fury and something stubborn sit down together at the table of her face.

"Ewan Tait is fourteen," she said finally, "and Quill will use him until he's used up, and then he'll be a loose end, and Quill does not keep loose ends." Her jaw tightened. "When this is done — if we're still here to do it — someone owes that boy a conversation. It may as well be the two fools who trusted him."

Outside, far off across the darkening moor, I thought I heard it again: two flat notes, even-spaced, patient. Or perhaps it was only the wind. On the Skerrow coast, in the autumn of 1893, those had become the same thing.

Freya was already pulling the floorboards up.
