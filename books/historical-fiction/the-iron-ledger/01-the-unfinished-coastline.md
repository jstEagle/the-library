## 1. The Unfinished Coastline

The candle had burned down to a stub by the time Silas Amberdale reached the place where the land met the sea.

She did not notice. She had learned, over eleven years of apprenticeship and three more of inheritance, that the coastline was the part of a map where a cartographer either told the truth or did not, and there was no middle ground. Rivers could be drawn generously. Mountains could be flattered. But a coast was a coast, and every league of it was a promise made to every sailor who would ever trust the page beneath his thumb.

Master Aldous Vane had promised the truth. He had died before he could keep it.

Silas dipped the pen, touched it to the lip of the inkwell to shed the excess, and drew the line north. The nib whispered against the vellum — a sound like snow falling on snow, like a secret told in an empty room. The coastline of the northern provinces unspooled beneath her hand: the shingle bar at Colder's Reach, the drowned forest visible at low tide, the estuary where the Greywash split into three fingers and lost itself in the marsh.

Three fingers. She checked the survey notes again, though she had checked them a dozen times tonight. Three fingers. The Crown's published charts showed two.

She had found that discrepancy on her first night alone in the workshop, and she had spent the better part of a week convincing herself it was an error — that Aldous, in his last months, his eyes failing, had simply miscounted channels on some waterlogged field chart. It was the comfortable explanation. She had wanted it badly.

Then she had started reading the margins.

* * *

The workshop occupied the attic of the house on Tanner's Stair, and the house had belonged to Aldous Vane for forty years, and Aldous Vane had belonged to no one, which was why the arrangement worked. A mapmaker needed a household. A household needed a name at the head of it, a man to sign the leases and answer the tax assessors and stand in the doorway when the census came. Aldous had never married, never kept a live-in apprentice, and had been, by the end, a solitary and celebrated old man whom the neighborhood saw perhaps twice a week on his slow walks to the river.

When he died in the autumn — his heart, the physician said, though Silas sometimes thought it was simply that a man can only carry so much weight before something gives — the neighborhood had expected the workshop to close.

Instead, a nephew had arrived from upcountry. Silas Amberdale, son of Aldous's estranged sister, come to settle the estate and, finding the commissions profitable, to stay. He was quiet. He kept odd hours. He had his uncle's hands, people said, and his uncle's stoop, and he wore his collar high and his coat loose, and if anyone had ever asked him to remove the binder that flattened his chest or to speak above the register she had practiced in the dark of her childhood bedroom until it stopped feeling like a lie, he would have been finished in Vellamark before the week was out.

But no one asked. That was the strange, merciful arithmetic of it. A man alone at his trade drew no curiosity. A man was presumed to be what he appeared, and Silas had spent fifteen years learning to appear as nothing at all — as a surface, as a margin, as the blank space around the map that no one looked at twice.

She set down the pen and flexed her fingers, and in the silence she heard the house settle around her: the tick of the roof beams contracting in the cold, the mutter of the river two streets away, the far-off clang of the night watch. Vellamark in midwinter was a city holding its breath. The river ran slow and dark under its first skin of ice, and the granaries stood locked, and the criers had been calling the same notice for a month now — *By order of the Crown, a general census of persons, lands, and stores shall be taken in the new year, that His Majesty's officers may know the true state of the realm.*

*The true state of the realm.* Silas looked at the atlas open on her worktable and thought: you have no idea what the true state of it is, and neither, until three weeks ago, did I.

* * *

She had found the first marginal note by accident, the way one finds anything in Aldous's papers — buried, disguised, half-erased. He had been a careful man in his craft and a paranoid one in everything else, and his marginalia looked, at a glance, like the idle scratchings of an old cartographer: little calculations, corrections, complaints about the weather. She had nearly thrown the first folio into the scrap bin.

Then she had noticed that the calculations were wrong. Not carelessly wrong — *deliberately* wrong, wrong in the way of a cipher, each figure off by a consistent factor, so that a column of numbers that appeared to record grain measures actually recorded something else entirely when you divided through by the factor and read the remainders.

She had spent four nights with the folio and a slate, and at the end of the fourth night she had sat back on her stool with her hands shaking and the candle guttering, and she had understood.

The numbers were people. Counts of people. Household by household, parish by parish, the figures Aldous had gathered himself over twenty years of survey work — and every one of them disagreed with the Crown's census. Not by a little. By thousands. Whole parishes in the northern districts recorded in the royal ledgers at a third of their true size. Some recorded at nothing at all. Some recorded as *uninhabited* — and Silas had grown up two days' ride from the northern districts, and she knew for a fact that the parish of Harrow Fen had a church, a market, a school, and four hundred souls.

She had gone to the shelf where Aldous kept the commercial drafts — the pretty maps, the ones that paid for the candles and the vellum — and pulled the northern sheet, the one printed from the Crown survey, the one every guildhall and magistrate's office in the realm hung on its wall. Harrow Fen was on it. A neat little mark, a name, a parish boundary.

And beside it, in Aldous's hand, so faint she had to hold the page to the candle and tilt it to catch the impression left by a stylus pressed through from the sheet above: *razed the register '09. count stands at 0. the count is a lie.*

* * *

Now she worked, and the work was a kind of grief, and the grief was a kind of fuel, and she had stopped trying to separate them.

There were forty-one marginal annotations in the atlas that she had catalogued so far, and she had made a list of them in her own cipher, in a small notebook she kept inside her shirt, against her skin, where no searcher would look. She reviewed them each night before she began, the way a soldier checks his kit.

Item the first: the population figures, ciphered as grain measures, contradicting the royal census in thirty parishes.

Item the second: a sequence of dates and place-marks along the northern coast that did not correspond to any survey Aldous had ever been commissioned to make — private expeditions, she now understood, made in the years after the famine, when the Crown's ledgers had begun to diverge from the land beneath them.

Item the third: a name, *E.M.*, appearing beside three of the vanished parishes, in a hand smaller than Aldous's, and she did not know who E.M. was or what had become of them, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Item the fourth — and this was the one she returned to last, always last, the way one saves the worst medicine: a page near the end of the atlas where the marginalia stopped being figures and became, for the space of a single line, plain words. Aldous's plain words, pressed hard enough to score the sheet beneath:

*If I am stopped, the truth must go to the press. Quillon will know what to do with it. Trust the guild, not the Crown, not the magistrates, not the Church. Trust the press.*

Quillon. She had searched the workshop's correspondence for the name and found one letter, years old, businesslike, from a Master Quillon of the Printing Guild in the capital, discussing paper stock and delivery schedules. Nothing else. No address beyond the guildhall. No instruction. Aldous had trusted her — had left her his workshop, his name, his unfinished life's work — and had trusted her to find the rest herself, or had died before he could tell her, and she did not know which was worse.

She blew out the spent candle stub and lit a fresh one from the coals of the brazier. The light steadied. She bent back over the vellum.

The coastline ran on. North of the Greywash estuary the land rose in low clay cliffs, and she drew them with the short, broken strokes Aldous had taught her — *the coast is honest, girl, it shows you its wounds* — and past the cliffs the shore flattened toward the fen country, and here her pen slowed.

Harrow Fen.

She drew the coastline the surveys showed. She drew the parish mark. And then, in the margin, in the smallest hand she could manage, she copied the notation she was beginning to think of as the atlas's heartbeat: the true count. Four hundred and six souls, as of Aldous's last visit. She did not know how many remained now. The census criers had been through the northern districts in the autumn, and after the criers, in every district where the figures had come in wrong, the Crown's men had come through too — quiet men, in plain coats, asking questions about registers and priests and who remembered what.

The atlas would tell them. That was the point of the atlas. That was why Aldous had died with the proofs of it locked in a chest under his bed and the key on a cord around his neck, and why the key had passed to her along with everything else, and why she sat in a dead man's attic at three in the morning wearing a dead man's name, inking a coastline that could hang every man who had signed the falsified ledgers.

*Whatever it costs.* She said it to herself the way other people said prayers. It did not feel like bravery. It felt like arithmetic. Aldous had spent his truth on her — the workshop, the name, the key, the years of trust he had never once withdrawn even when she had given him cause, the night she had told him what she was at seventeen, shaking, certain she was about to be turned out, and he had looked at her for a long moment and said only, *well, you'll want to learn to bind your own chests then, the tailor's prices are robbery.* He had spent his truth on her, and she meant to spend it back, all of it, at whatever price the ledger demanded.

She finished the last league of coast as the candle burned low again — the final promontory, the northernmost point of the realm, where the sea went grey and the charts went blank and the Crown's interest, apparently, went blank with them. She signed the sheet in the lower corner. *S. Amberdale, del.* The ink dried in the cold. Her own name, in a manner of speaking. The only name she had left.

* * *

She was cleaning the pen when she heard the knock.

Not the knocker — the knock. Three raps, measured, official, the kind of knock that came with a warrant behind it or a ledger in front of it, and at this hour, before dawn, with the census season opening and the criers still calling their notice through the frozen streets.

Silas went very still. Below her, the shop was dark. The front door of the house on Tanner's Stair had a bell, and the bell had not rung, which meant the knock had fallen on the street door, the outer one, the one that did not expect an answer before sunup — and yet had been knocked on anyway, by someone who wanted the house to know they were there.

She looked down at the atlas, open on the table, the fresh ink of the northern coast still faintly shining. Forty-one marginal notes. A dead man's cipher. A name in the margin that could buy a rope.

She closed the atlas, slid it beneath the floorboard, and went down the stairs to see who had come asking questions in the dark.

The knock came again. Patient. Unhurried. The knock of someone who had all the time in the world and a list of questions to fill it with.
