## 6. An Uneasy Truce

Edwina Corliss had made forty-one arrests in her six years with the Census Office, and she had never once needed to rehearse the words. *You are detained under the Records Act. Come quietly and it will go easier for you.* The words were a key that turned in every lock, and she had carried them the way other women carried prayer books.

Tonight, standing across the street from Aldous Amberdale's old workshop, she found she could not say them at all.

The lamp in the attic window burned past midnight. That was the first irregularity. The second was that she had come on foot, alone, without a witness clerk, without a warrant countersigned — without anything but the fragment of margin note copied into her field book in her own careful hand, the numbers that could not be numbers, the census of three parishes contradicting itself by eleven thousand souls.

*Report it.* Her instructor's voice, dry as ledger paper. *An officer who finds an anomaly reports the anomaly. She does not sit up all night doing arithmetic against figures she is not cleared to see.*

She crossed the street.

* * *

The door was unlocked. That was the third irregularity, and by then Edwina understood it was not carelessness. It was invitation.

She climbed the stairs with her hand on her baton and did not draw it. The attic was warm, candlelit, smelling of ink and tallow and something older — size and vellum, the smell of a trade. And there, bent over the great worktable with a pen in her hand and her collar open at the throat, was the person she had come to arrest.

Not a nephew. Not Silas Amberdale, journeyman mapmaker, dead man's heir. A woman. Younger than Edwina had guessed from the way she held her shoulders in public — shoulders deliberately squared too wide, a posture Edwina now recognized as costume. In the attic, alone, the costume had come off. What remained was someone tired, and precise, and entirely unafraid.

"You took the long way from your lodgings," the woman said, without looking up. "Past the chandlery. I've watched you do it twice."

"Silas Amberdale."

"That's one of my names." She set down the pen and turned. "Would you like to see it? You climbed two flights of stairs to arrest me over it. It seems rude to make you leave without looking."

Edwina had planned this part. She had planned it in the bath, on the walk, in the interval between lighting and snuffing her candle: enter, name the charge, secure the documents before they could be destroyed. Documents first. Always documents first.

But the worktable was already bare of concealment. The atlas lay open under four weighted stones, its pages spread like a dissected animal, and beside it — arranged with a curator's neatness — were everything else: Aldous's letter, a bundle of parish tallies, a sheaf of marginalia transcribed into fair copy, and a single sheet headed in a firm hand: *What the Crown Counts, and What It Does Not.*

"You knew I was coming," Edwina said.

"I knew you'd either come or report me. I couldn't control which." Silas — Edwina would learn to think of her as Silas; the name fit her like a coat worn so long it had grown into the skin — gestured at the table. "So I prepared for both. If you came to arrest me, everything you need for the prosecution is laid out in order of evidentiary weight. I did that to save us both time. If you came for any other reason—" she paused, and for the first time something moved behind her eyes, something that was not calm at all— "then read the last page first."

"I'm an officer of the Crown."

"Yes."

"My duty is defined by statute."

"Yes."

"Then why am I still holding my baton instead of using it?"

Silas smiled, and it was not a pleasant smile; it was the smile of someone watching a coin spin and knowing it must land on one face or the other. "Because you're the only census taker in Vellamark who noticed the ink on my cuff and didn't write it down immediately. Because you walked here alone. Because you've spent — what, six days? — doing sums you weren't authorized to do, and if you'd wanted to bury them you'd have buried them." She nodded at the table. "Read the last page, Miss Corliss. Then arrest me if you can."

* * *

Edwina read.

She had expected forgery, sedition, the usual architecture of treason — and yes, all of it was there, plain as a road on a map. But what stopped her breath was not the crime. It was the arithmetic.

Aldous Amberdale had spent twenty years as the Crown's most trusted cartographer, and in the margins of his masterwork he had done what no honest man was permitted to do: he had counted. Harrow Fen, recorded at four hundred souls in the royal census, showed smoke-houses, byres, a church with a register — a settlement of better than nine hundred, erased to a rounding error. Two more villages, names Edwina half-knew from childhood songs. Grain levies assessed against populations that no longer officially existed, so that the surplus vanished into requisitions signed by men whose signatures Edwina recognized because she processed their correspondence.

"They starved," Silas said quietly, when Edwina finally looked up. "Not in a famine. In a bookkeeping decision. My master watched them strike the villages from the rolls and then watched the grain wagons roll out anyway, and he couldn't publish, so he hid the truth where only someone reading closely would ever find it. He died before he could finish. I'm finishing it. That's the whole of my treason."

"The whole of it." Edwina set the page down with a hand that was not quite steady. "You impersonate a man. You forge commissions. You intend to move seditious material through three checkpoints to the capital. And you think the *whole* of it is arithmetic."

"What would you call it?"

"Duty," Edwina said. "Twisted inside out. Duty is the census being true. Do you imagine I don't know that? I have walked through villages counting people for six years, and every count I've ever taken has gone into a machine that grinds the truth off it like a millstone. I know exactly what the ledgers are worth. I also know what happens to officers who decide which laws deserve obeying." She heard her own voice rise. "I was in Harrow Fen district when I was a girl. Before. There was a village near ours — Mallow Cross. One autumn the assessors came and the next spring it wasn't there. Not burned. Not flooded. *Struck.* People still lived there. They just stopped existing on paper, and once they didn't exist, nothing protected them. Nothing at all. I was eight years old and I asked my father why, and he told me never to ask questions with answers like that in them."

Silas had gone very still. "And you joined the Census Office anyway."

"Because I thought I could be the one honest count in a false column." Edwina laughed, and it came out wrong. "Because I was young. Because the uniform was better than my mother's coat. Pick a reason; they're all stupid."

They looked at each other across the table, across the spread corpse of the atlas, and Edwina felt the strange vertigo of recognizing herself in an enemy.

"If you arrest me," Silas said, "the atlas goes into evidence, the evidence goes into a sealed archive, and the archive goes into the Citadel vault where Aldous's original notes already went to die. Eleven thousand people stay unpersoned. Halloway keeps signing requisitions. And in thirty years some other clerk finds another margin note and we do all this again."

"And if I don't arrest you, and it comes out that I knew — I don't lose my post, Amberdale. Officers who aid sedition hang. Or worse; the Crown is inventive about women."

"So you have a choice between your neck and mine."

"I have a choice between two versions of myself," Edwina said, "and I liked one of them. I'd served her for six years. I don't know yet whether I get to keep her."

* * *

They argued until the candles burned down to pools.

It was not, Edwina thought later, like any argument she had ever had. With her colleagues, argument was position and counter-position, a duel with rules. This was different. Silas attacked her certainties and Edwina counterattacked with procedure, and somewhere past midnight the shapes of the fight changed, because they stopped defending positions and started defending the dead.

Silas spoke of Master Aldous — his cough, his terrible handwriting, the way he'd tested her for years before showing her the margins, making her earn each village name. "He told me once that a map is a weapon that looks like a gift. Whoever draws the lines decides where the roads go, where the grain goes, where the soldiers go. The Crown understands that. Why do you think they audit cartographers and never auditors?"

Edwina spoke of the forms. Of the beautiful, brutal machinery of records — how a number entered becomes a number believed, how a village struck from a census can be starved with perfect legality because legally there is no one there to starve. "You want to smuggle a book. I understand the book. But the book isn't the disease, it's a symptom. The disease is that truth in this kingdom requires a permit, and the permits are issued by the men the truth indicts."

"Then help me forge the permits."

"I should arrest you for saying that."

"You keep saying that." Silas leaned forward, elbows on the table, and Edwina saw that beneath the calm she was exhausted in a way that went deeper than sleeplessness — the exhaustion of a person who has been someone else for years. "Say it one more time and mean it, and I'll open the door for you myself. But mean it, Corliss. Don't say it because it's the sentence you're supposed to reach."

The fire ticked. Somewhere below, the river ice boomed and settled, the sound of the city turning in its sleep.

Edwina looked at the last page again. *What the Crown Counts, and What It Does Not.* Eleven thousand. She thought of Mallow Cross, of her father's warning, of the forty-one arrests and whether any of them had ever weighed as much as a feather against this table.

"I can't decide tonight," she said finally.

"It's nearly morning. Tonight's over."

"Then I can't decide today either. Give me until—" she stopped, recalculated, hated herself for the calculation— "the winter audit is in nineteen days. If I haven't reported you by then, the audit will expose me anyway. So: nineteen days. Either I bring you in, or I'm in."

Silas studied her for a long moment. Whatever she saw seemed to satisfy her, because she began gathering the pages, squaring the corners, restoring order to the dissection. "Then you'll need to know the protocols," she said. "Tollgate, River Guard, Citadel inspection. Three locks between here and the printing guild."

"I know the protocols. I enforce them."

"Even better." Silas looked up, and the smile this time was small and real and more frightening than the other one. "You'll know exactly where they're weakest."

Edwina should have left then. Should have walked out into the grey beginning of the day, gone to her office, written the report, saved the version of herself she'd mourned all night. Instead she heard herself say:

"The tripod. When you move it — whatever you're planning — the Tollgate guards weigh freight over forty pounds. Anything lighter gets waved through on the mail coach. Build your hiding place heavy enough to pass and light enough to carry, and don't let anyone help you lift it."

Silas blinked. "That's advice."

"That's insomnia," Edwina said. "Don't thank me. I haven't decided whose side insomnia is on."

She went down the stairs and out into the blue hour before dawn, and behind her the lamp in the attic window stayed lit a moment longer — then went dark, the way a lamp goes dark when two people have finally agreed on the same dangerous fact:

Neither of them was going to report the other.

Which meant, though neither said it, that the Crown now had two problems where yesterday it had one — and that the woman walking home through the empty streets with her baton unused at her belt was no longer merely a census taker.

She was a count that had learned to lie.
