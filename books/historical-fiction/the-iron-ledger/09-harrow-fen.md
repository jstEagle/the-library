## 9. Harrow Fen

The coach let me off at a crossroads that wasn't on any map I carried.

That was the first thing I noticed, standing in the rutted snow with my satchel heavy on my hip and the wind coming flat off the fens like a blade laid sideways. The driver had taken my coin without question, but when I'd said *Harrow Fen* he'd looked at me the way men look at a woman who has named a grave. "Road ends at the dike," he'd said. "Walk the rest." He hadn't wished me a good journey. Neither had I.

I had told myself, on the ride out from Vellamark, that this was procedure. That a census officer who suspected a document of falsification had a duty to verify claims in the field before she did anything irreversible — before she filed a report, or failed to file one, or burned her career in a grate the way I had burned those letters three nights ago and still smelled the smoke in my cuffs when the wind was right. Verification. That was the word I kept using, the way a man keeps using a rope to hold up a beam he knows is rotten.

The truth was simpler and uglier. I had read the margin note. *Harrow Fen, 1,140 souls, year of the great requisition — 412 remain.* And I had remembered a village of that name from my childhood, from my father's stories, from the harvest fair we'd ridden to when I was seven and the world was a cart and a pony and my mother's laugh. A village that should have been full. And I needed to see with my own eyes whether Aldous's numbers were ink or iron.

I walked the dike road for the better part of two hours. The fens in midwinter are not empty; that's the mistake outsiders make. They are *withheld*. Reeds standing in ranks, black water in the drains moving slow as syrup, a heron lifting off somewhere I never saw. And the whole time, the sense of being watched from the houses that weren't there — from the gaps in the dike where chimneys had stood and now didn't, their brick scavenged down to foundation lines that filled with snow like open wounds dressed in white.

I counted them. It's an occupational habit; you can take the tally-book out of the officer but not the officer out of the walk. Fourteen foundation lines between the crossroads and the church. Fourteen houses that the Crown's census, the one I had helped administer in three districts myself, recorded as *never extant*.

The church came up out of the reeds like a ship's hull run aground. Squat, grey-stoned, its bell tower leaning a degree or two east as if craning to look at the road. Smoke rose from a cottage beside it — the only smoke in sight — and a dog began to bark, a thin, rusty sound, as though it had forgotten how.

I made myself walk up to the door and knock.

* * *

The woman who opened it was perhaps sixty, wrapped in a man's coat three sizes too large, with a face like weathered oak and eyes that did the arithmetic of me in a single pass. Satchel. Good boots. The particular way I stood, weight even, hands visible. She had seen officers before. You could read it in her the way you read a tide table.

"Lost?" she said.

"Passing through," I said. "I wondered if I might warm myself and ask a few questions. I'm conducting a survey of—"

"I know what you're conducting." She didn't move from the doorway. Behind her, in the dim interior, I saw a second figure withdraw — a man, or the shape of one — and a door close softly, the way doors are closed when the closing itself must make no sound. "Survey's been done. You did it yourselves. Seven years back."

"Madam, I'm not—"

"You're the Crown or you're nothing, and nothing doesn't wear boots like that." She began to shut the door. "Good day."

"Please." And I confess I used it the way I'd been taught to use it — a small word, a lever word, a word that makes the person hearing it feel the weight of refusing. "The register. I need to see the parish register. Births, deaths, marriages. It's a matter of record correction. There may be an error in the Crown's rolls, and I am authorized to correct it."

That stopped her. Not because it persuaded her — because it *amused* her, a terrible dry amusement with no warmth in it at all.

"An error," she repeated. "In the Crown's rolls."

"Records can be misfiled. Villages can be—"

"Villages can be *what*?" She opened the door a hand's width wider, and the cold came out of her voice colder than the wind off the water. "Say the word, girl. You've been circling it since you knocked. Say what you think happened here, and I'll tell you if you're close."

I stood in the snow and found, to my considerable professional embarrassment, that I had no word. Every term I reached for — *undercount, discrepancy, administrative oversight* — was a word I had written myself, in my own neat hand, in reports that had moved up through the bureau and been stamped and shelved and used to set requisition quotas. I had been the pen. I was standing in the ink.

"The census," I said finally. "I believe the census for Harrow Fen was falsified. I believe people who were counted were later struck from the rolls, and that the striking was done to conceal deaths. Deaths from the requisition years."

The old woman looked at me for a long moment. Then she stepped back from the door — not an invitation, exactly. More like a woman stepping back from a horse she'd decided not to spook.

"Come in, then," she said. "And mind you wipe your boots. We've little enough clean in this house and none of it for the likes of you."

* * *

Her name was Maren Ostler, and she had been the parish sexton's wife before the sexton was the parish's problem anymore, and she told me all of this in the flat reciting voice of a woman who had told it before, to no one, to the walls, to the reeds.

The cottage was one room and a loft, scrubbed to bareness, a fire small enough to be deniable. The man in the loft — her son, I gathered, or her son-in-law; the distinctions had worn away out here — did not come down. I heard him shift his weight on the boards above us the entire time we talked, a soft regular creak, like a conscience.

"Requisition year," she said, "they came with wagons. Not soldiers at first — assessors. Men with books. They weighed our stores and wrote it all down very fair and proper, and then the wagons came back with soldiers, and they took what the books said and then some, because the books had been written generous." She fed a stick to the fire without looking at it. "You understand? The books said we had more than we had. So they took more than there was. The error went one way only. It always goes one way only."

"And the winter after."

"And the winter after." The stick caught. "We buried forty-one that season in the churchyard, and my husband kept the register honest because he was that kind of fool. Every name. Every date. Cause of death, age, the whole column, written fair. He said — he said *the book is the only witness we'll ever get, Maren, so the book must tell the truth.*" Her mouth did something that was not a smile. "The Crown came back in the spring. Not for grain that time."

"For the register."

"For the register. Two men and a magistrate's clerk, very civil, very sorry, said there'd been a clerical consolidation, that outlying parishes were being merged into the district rolls at Vellamark, that the old book would be *properly archived*. My husband asked for a receipt." She looked at me then, straight on, and I had been looked at by smugglers and forgers and one magistrate who thought a commendation bought him my spine, and none of it was like this. "He asked for a receipt for the souls of his parish, girl. And they gave him one. That's the part I can't make my peace with. They gave him a *receipt*."

"What happened to him?"

"He wrote to the district office. Three letters. Then he went to Vellamark himself, in the autumn, to ask in person." The fire ticked. "He never arrived. Or he arrived, depending on which story you keep. There's a grave in the churchyard with no name on it because we couldn't prove which story to put on the stone."

I sat in that scrubbed little room with my hands around a cup of nettle tea she'd given me the way you feed a stray — not kindness, habit — and I understood that I was not here verifying a document. I was here being *shown* a document. Every cracked beam and empty foundation line and unnamed grave was a line in Aldous's margin, rendered in materials that could not be razored out, and I was reading it at last, seven years late, with the Crown's seal still warm on my satchel.

"The register," I said. "You said they took it. But the church—"

"Go and look," Maren Ostler said. "You've a survey to conduct. Go and conduct it."

* * *

The church was unlocked. Nothing in Harrow Fen was worth locking anything for, and besides, I think she wanted me to see it alone.

Inside it was cold in the way stone buildings get cold — a cold that has settled in and made arrangements, that considers itself permanent. The pews were scarred and mended and mended again. The windows had been patched with oilcloth where the glass had gone. And on the lectern at the front, open, weighted down with a stone, lay the parish register.

Or what was left of it.

I had imagined theft — the whole book gone, the shelf empty, a clean erasure. This was worse. This was *surgery*. The binding remained, the covers, the flyleaves, the early pages of baptisms in a fine old copperplate. But the middle of the book had been gutted. Someone had gone through with a razor, page by page, and cut out the years that mattered — the requisition years, the burial years — so precisely, so *lovingly*, that at a glance the book looked whole. You had to touch it to understand. You had to feel the stubble of the cut edges under your thumb, hundreds of them, like the stubble on a shaved head, like something that had been made to look normal from a distance and was a mutilation up close.

I stood there with my thumb on the razored edge and I thought: *I have seen this before.* Not this book. This *act*. I had seen it in the district archives in Vellamark, in the ledgers I'd audited, in the neat columns where district totals never quite reconciled with the sum of their parishes and the discrepancy had been noted, initialled, and *resolved* — resolved by adjusting the parishes, never the total. I had initialled some of those resolutions myself. My initials were in books like this. My hand had held the razor.

The wind found a gap in the oilcloth and the patch lifted and slapped, lifted and slapped, a small dead heartbeat.

I heard the footsteps behind me and did not turn. I knew who it would be. She had followed me; of course she had. You don't send the Crown into your church alone with the last honest book your husband kept.

"It was my husband's hand, the early pages," Maren said from the doorway. "His father's before him. You can see where the teaching changes — the letters go worse after the fever year, his eyes were going." She came and stood beside me, looking down at the mutilated book with the expression of a woman looking at a scar she has stopped being able to see as anything but hers. "They left the baptisms. Did you notice? They left the births."

I had noticed. I hadn't understood.

"Why?" I asked, and my voice came out wrong, scraped.

"Because the dead don't pay tithes," she said, "and the dead don't pay taxes, and the dead can't be conscripted. But the *born* — the born are future revenue. They couldn't have us striking our own children from the rolls, could they? A village might claim its children were never born and so never owed. So the births stay and the deaths go, and the arithmetic comes out however they need it to come out." She turned and looked at me, and the firelight from the little lamp I'd lit — no, I hadn't lit it; it was already burning, someone had left it burning for me, for *this*, for the showing — the lamplight made her face all planes and shadow. "That's what your books are, girl. Not a record. A harvest schedule."

I wanted to say something. I had a career's worth of something to say — about procedure, about oversight, about how an officer of the census could not have known, could not have — and every word of it died in my throat, because I *had* known. Not the particulars. The shape. The shape of it had been in every ledger I'd ever reconciled, and I had smoothed it flat and moved on to the next district, because the alternative was to stop, and to stop was to fall, and I had a career and a reputation and a set of commendation letters that I had burned three nights ago in a grate and still, God help me, still caught myself missing them.

* * *

It happened outside, at the churchyard gate, and it happened fast.

I had asked her — I don't know why, some reflex of the trade, some last twitch of the officer — how many remained. How many souls, as the rolls would say, as *my* rolls would say, were resident at Harrow Fen as of this winter.

"Count the smoke," Maren Ostler said.

I looked down the valley of the dike, at the grey afternoon coming on, and I counted the smoke. Seven threads of it, thin and low, the fires kept small. Seven chimneys where the census of my bureau, the census I had helped administer, recorded a thriving fen parish of eleven hundred.

And that was when the old woman saw the satchel.

I had shifted it on my shoulder as I turned, and the flap had fallen open, and there in the lamplight — no, the daylight, the grey honest daylight — the Crown seal was visible on the document wallet inside. The embossed blind-stamp. The thing I had carried for eleven years without ever once thinking of it as anything but a credential.

Maren Ostler looked at it. Then she looked at me. And she spat.

Not at my feet — at the seal, at the embossed blind-stamp itself, a short precise spit of an old country woman with a dead husband and a razored book and seven chimneys, and it landed on the snow an inch from the leather, and she did not wipe her mouth afterward, and she did not apologize, and she stood there in the falling dark of the fen with her chin up like a woman who had been waiting seven years for the chance and was only sorry it was over so soon.

"You'll write it down," she said. It was not a question, and it was not a plea. "Whatever you came to write down. You'll write it, and it'll go in a book, and the book will go somewhere, and in seven years I'll be dead and the smoke will be four chimneys and then two and then none, and the book will say whatever the book is for. That's how it goes. That's how it's gone." She pulled the man's coat tighter around herself. "But I'll tell you one thing for nothing, since you walked two hours on the dike road to hear it. My husband said the book was the only witness we'd get. He was wrong. The book can be cut." She nodded past me, at the church, at the stubbled register lying open on its lectern like a body on a bier. "The only witness they can't razor is the one standing in the yard looking at the smoke. So look. *Look*, girl. And then decide which side of the razor you're standing on."

She walked back to her cottage and shut the door, and the latch did not click, because even her door had learned to close quietly.

* * *

I walked back along the dike in the dark, and I did not feel the cold, which frightened me more than the cold would have.

At the crossroads there was no coach — of course there was no coach; there had never been a coach; the road ended at the dike — and there was a drover's cart loading at the far gate, and I paid a boy two bits to walk me to the turnpike, and he chattered the whole way about his sister in Vellamark and asked me twice if I was a tax-woman and I said no, and it was the truest thing I'd said all day.

At the turnpike inn, under a lamp that smoked, I opened my field book to a fresh page. The officer's discipline is the last thing to leave you; it leaves last the way the ring leaves last. I wrote the date. I wrote the location. And then, instead of the survey columns, instead of the tidy reconciliations, I wrote what Maren Ostler had said, as near to her words as memory would hold, and I wrote the number of chimneys, and I wrote *forty-one buried, register razored, husband vanished en route to Vellamark*, and my hand was steady until the last line and then it was not.

Because the last line I wrote was this: *The atlas is accurate. Every figure. It is the Crown that lies.*

And under it, without deciding to, my pen added: *Aldous knew. Aldous counted. Who else knows — and who else is hunting the man who finished his book?*

I sat back. The inn was loud around me, drovers and carriers and a card game going badly in the corner, and none of them knew that the woman in the corner booth had just written her own treason in a field book with the Crown's seal three feet away.

I thought of Silas Amberdale — the soft-spoken nephew with the ink-stained cuffs and the eyes that never once blinked at the wrong moment, which was itself the tell, because everyone blinks — sitting in that attic with a dead man's atlas, and I thought: *she has known all of this for months. She has been inking coastlines over graves.* And I thought of the ferry log, and the sergeant who had written down a name, and the report I had already falsified once, and the distance between that small treason and this one, which was no distance at all, only a road walked in the dark.

I closed the field book. In nineteen days the winter audit would open in the capital, and the atlas was somewhere on the road ahead of me, and so, I now understood with perfect clarity, were men whose trade was razors.

I had come to Harrow Fen to verify a document.

I was leaving as one.
