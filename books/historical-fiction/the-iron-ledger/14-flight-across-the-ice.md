## 14. Flight Across the Ice

The knock came at dusk, and Silas knew before she opened it that it was over.

Not Halloway's knock — his would come with soldiers and paperwork. This was a boy from the chandler's, breathless, holding a coin she'd paid him that morning for exactly this errand. "Man's been asking after you at the Tollgate inn," he said. "Riding coat, magistrate's colors under his cloak. Asked did a mapmaker pass north today."

She tipped him and closed the door and stood in the dark of her own workshop, listening to her heart count.

The decoy had lasted four hours. Less. Aldous's commercial draft — coastlines and trade routes, innocent as bread — had bought her an afternoon, no more. Halloway would have unrolled it in his chambers, run his thumb along the margins, found them clean, and understood in one cold instant that he'd been handed a lie by a woman wearing a dead man's name.

Which meant the riders were already on the road. Which meant she had perhaps an hour, maybe two, before Vellamark filled with men who knew her face — or thought they did.

She worked fast. The real atlas went into its oilskin wrapping, then into the false bottom of her satchel, beneath a layer of surveyor's instruments that made the whole thing look like nothing more than a clerk's kit. She took coin, not much. She took Edwina's letter — the one Edwina had left three nights ago, unsigned, telling her where to wait if the worst happened. *The old drover's track along the river. Where the willows bend. I'll find you or die of trying.*

*Die of trying.* The woman wrote love letters like arrest warrants.

Silas banked the fire, left the lamps cold, and let herself out the back into the alley where the frost had grown teeth overnight. She did not look at the attic window. Looking back was how people got caught.

* * *

Edwina reached the willows first, which surprised neither of them when Silas came stumbling out of the dark an hour later, satchel clutched to her chest like a child.

"You're late," Edwina said.

"I said goodbye to my house."

"With your eyes?"

"With my eyes."

Edwina had a hand-sled, stolen or borrowed — with Edwina one never learned which — loaded with a canvas bundle, a coil of rope, and a sack of hard bread and smoked fish that smelled like survival. She looked at Silas a moment longer than necessary, the way she always did, cataloguing: frost in the loose hair at Silas's temples, the tremor she couldn't quite suppress in her hands.

"They'll have found the decoy by now," Silas said.

"Then they know you lied to a magistrate." Edwina shouldered the sled rope. "That's a crime with teeth. Good. Let them chase us angry. Angry men make mistakes."

"And what are we?"

"Tired," Edwina said, and started walking. "Come on."

The river froze early this far upcountry, and the drover's track ran parallel to it through country that flattened out into white nothing. They walked until the town lights died behind them and the only illumination was the moon off the ice, turning the world into a page of blank vellum. Silas thought, absurdly, of all the hours she'd spent inking coastlines by candlelight, and how none of it had prepared her for actual geography — the kind that got into your boots and stayed there.

They spoke little. There was a discipline to flight, they were both learning. You saved your breath. You saved your fear. You spent neither until you had to.

It was past midnight when Edwina stopped so abruptly that Silas walked into her back.

"What—"

"Listen."

Silas listened. Wind. Ice ticking as it settled. And underneath, faint but unmistakable, the rhythm of hooves on frozen ground — not on the river, but on the road above the bank, moving fast, moving north.

"Halloway's riders," Edwina breathed. "He didn't wait for warrants. He sent them straight out."

"How many?"

"Three sets of hooves. Maybe four." She turned, scanning the riverbank, and Silas watched her do it — the census taker's mind working, sorting terrain the way she sorted ledgers. "We can't outrun horses. We need to be somewhere they can't see."

"The river itself," Silas said. "Out on the ice. No tracks hold, and if the ice holds us—"

"If the ice doesn't hold us, we won't need to worry about magistrates." But Edwina was already stepping down onto the frozen surface, testing each stride, weight rolling heel to toe the way she'd apparently learned somewhere in a childhood Silas knew nothing about. "Stay where I walk. The current runs fastest near the banks. Middle is thickest."

They went out onto the river like sinners crossing into a country with no laws. The ice groaned beneath them once, a long whale-song moan that stopped Silas's heart entirely, and Edwina reached back without looking and gripped her wrist — just gripped it, hard, and held on until the sound passed.

"Still with me?" Edwina asked.

"I'm deciding," Silas said, and felt rather than saw her smile.

* * *

The blizzard caught them an hour before dawn.

It came off the northern flats like something with intent, erasing the moon, the stars, the shoreline, the difference between ground and sky. One moment Edwina was navigating by a crooked alder on the bank; the next there was no bank, no alder, no anything. Snow hit sideways. The wind found every seam in their clothing and signed its name there.

"We can't—" Silas shouted, and the wind ate the rest.

Edwina grabbed her sleeve and hauled her toward a shape that might have been imagination — a low dark hump against the whiteness, growing more solid with every lurching step. A bothy. A shepherd's shelter, turf-walled, turf-roofed, built by someone who understood that the moor forgave nothing.

The door wasn't locked because it wasn't a door, properly — just a hurdle of woven hazel propped against the gap. Edwina shouldered it aside and pulled them both into darkness that smelled of peat smoke and sheep and years of weather.

Inside was a stone hearth, a stack of dried peat someone had left with a shepherd's courtesy, a wooden trough, and — miracle upon miracles — a single grey blanket, folded on a bench, dry.

Edwina got a fire going with hands that shook from cold, and for a long time neither of them spoke. They just sat close to the small flame, steaming, while the storm screamed against the turf walls like something trying to get in and eat them.

Then Edwina began to laugh. Quietly at first, then helplessly, bent double, snowmelt dripping off her nose.

"What," Silas said, "is funny."

"We're fugitives." Edwina wiped her eyes. "I spent eleven years building a career on being incorruptible. Commendations. Two offers of promotion. I turned down a post in the capital because the census work here mattered." She fed another peat block to the fire. "And now I'm sitting in a sheep shed committing treason with a woman who isn't named Silas Amberdale, and do you know what I feel?"

"Tell me."

"Lighter," Edwina said. "I feel lighter than I've felt since I was nine years old."

Silas looked at her across the fire. The storm howled. The blanket sat on the bench between them, grey and ordinary and suddenly the most significant object in the realm.

"There's one blanket," Silas said.

"I counted."

"It's going to be a cold night."

"It's going to be a freezing night." Edwina met her eyes. "I'm telling you now so you can compose some objection and we can skip the part where we're both awkward about it."

"I don't get awkward."

"You inked your own cuff this morning and then lied about it to my face for a month."

"That was strategy."

"That was *awkwardness with a quill.*"

But they were both laughing again, and then they weren't laughing, because the fire was low and the night was long and there was really no reason left to sit apart. Edwina spread the blanket against the wall nearest the hearth. They lay down side by side, backs to the stone, shoulder to shoulder, wrapped in one grey expanse of wool that smelled of sheep and smoke and, increasingly, of each other.

For a while, silence. The storm. The fire settling.

"My village," Edwina said, into the dark, "was called Mereholt. It's not on any map anymore. That's why I became a census taker — I thought if I worked inside the machine, I could keep places like Mereholt from disappearing quietly. Count the souls so they couldn't be unwritten." A pause. "I was naive. The machine doesn't want counting. It wants arithmetic that flatters it."

"Aldous used to say maps are arguments," Silas said. "Every line you draw is a claim about what's true."

"He sounds like he was right."

"He was murdered for being right."

Edwina turned her head. In the firelight Silas could see her face fully for the first time in hours — the windburn, the exhaustion, the thing underneath the exhaustion that had been growing between them since a workshop full of evidence and a night-long argument about duty.

"Why did you stay?" Edwina asked. "When I confronted you. You could have denied everything, burned the atlas, vanished. Instead you laid it all out on the table like a merchant displaying wares. You gambled everything on me. Why?"

Silas was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice.

"Because you noticed the ink on my cuff," she said finally. "Everyone else looked at me and saw a man. A nephew. A name. You looked at me and saw *me* — the first person in eleven years who actually looked. And I thought: if she's sharp enough to see that much, she might be honest enough to see the rest." Her voice dropped. "I gambled because I wanted to be seen. By you specifically. I've known that for weeks. I've been a coward about it for weeks too."

The storm pressed against the walls. Somewhere out in the white dark, pursuit was presumably circling, lost, furious.

Edwina reached over and took Silas's hand — the ink-stained one, the one that had started everything — and held it in both of hers, warming it, or perhaps warming herself.

"When they catch us," Edwina said quietly, "and they may catch us — I want you to know that I don't regret a single step of this. Not the erased report. Not the burned letters. Not this sheep shed." Her thumb moved across Silas's knuckles. "I spent my whole life believing duty meant obedience. You taught me it means witness. Whatever happens on this river tomorrow, or at the Citadel, or after — I've already chosen. I chose the night I read Aldous's margins and couldn't sleep."

"Edwina—"

"Don't say it," Edwina said softly. "If you say it, it becomes a thing that can be taken away. Say it in the capital. Say it when the atlas is printed and the villages have their names back. Say it somewhere the Crown can't reach it." She smiled, tired and fierce. "Until then, let this be enough."

Silas turned her hand over and laced their fingers together.

"This is enough," she said. "This is more than enough."

They slept like that, or half-slept, in shifts, sharing warmth and watchfulness, while outside the blizzard buried the world and every track they'd ever made. Once, deep in the small hours, Silas woke to find Edwina watching her instead of the fire, and neither of them pretended otherwise.

At grey dawn the storm had spent itself. Edwina climbed to the gap in the wall and looked out at a landscape scoured clean, unmarked, featureless — and then stiffened.

"Silas."

Silas joined her. Far to the south, small as ink flecks on fresh vellum, riders moved along the river road. Four of them. Not searching anymore.

*Tracking.*

"They found something," Edwina murmured. "A track. A witness. Something." She turned, and the tenderness of the night hardened instantly into the census taker's arithmetic. "The ice road runs north from here. If we make the ferry ford by midday, we can be at the capital's outskirts by tomorrow night."

"And if we don't?"

"Then we find out how much this blanket is worth," Edwina said, already rolling it, "because we'll be needing it a great deal longer."

They stepped out into the blue morning cold, two small figures on an endless white page, and behind them, patient as ink drying, the riders came on.
