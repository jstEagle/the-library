## 7. Mercy Makes Her Claim

I have buried a husband, two stillborn sons, and my father, and I will tell you what none of the mourning songs will: grief is an accounting problem. The dead leave behind debts and papers, and someone must tally both. In Greyharrow, that someone has always been me.

So when word came that Aldous Vane had died — fallen from the cliff path above Smuggler's Cove, they said, as though the man had tripped over forty years of careful living — I did not weep. Not at first. I put on my black wool dress with the good buttons, counted out coin for the wreath, and instructed our girl Tansy to air the front room. Mourning, like everything else in this house, must be done properly or not at all.

My brother Hal called it coldness when he was alive, my habit of ordering the world before permitting myself to feel it. But Hal is eleven years drowned off the northern coast, and I have learned that feeling comes whether you invite it or no. It simply behaves better when it arrives to find the house already in order.

The dinner was my idea. That will surprise no one who knew me, and it should have surprised Gideon Dunmore most of all, though the boy has never once managed to look surprised by anything, which I have always found either admirable or sinister depending on the hour.

* * *

I arrived at Vane's workshop on the fourth of November, an hour before dark, with a covered dish of braised mutton and a ledger under my arm. The dish was courtesy. The ledger was necessity.

Gideon opened the door before I knocked. He does that. A thin young man, ink-stained to the elbows, with his father's grey eyes and none of his father's warmth. He looked at the ledger first and the mutton second, which told me everything about how the evening would go.

"Aunt Mercy," he said.

"Nephew." I stepped past him into the workshop, because waiting on thresholds is for people without business inside. "You'll set another place. I've brought enough for three."

"Three?"

"Your half-brother's ship docked this morning. Corin. Surely he's told you."

Something moved behind those grey eyes — quick, quickly mastered. "He hasn't."

"Well. He's here." I set the mutton on the workbench, among the dividers and the dried squid-ink pots, and pretended not to notice the way Gideon's hand drifted, casual as weather, toward the great atlas lying open on the drafting table. The final atlas. Vane's last commission, the coastal survey of the northern reaches, half-finished now and orphaned. "We are family," I said, untying my bonnet strings. "Family eats together. Especially now."

"Especially now," Gideon repeated, in the tone of a man agreeing to a tooth extraction.

* * *

Corin arrived smelling of the ferry and of something sweeter underneath — brandy, or the memory of it. He was always the handsome one, my brother's byblow raised legitimate after the scandal, all easy smiles and clever hands. Customs officer, once, until the inquiry. He filled the doorway the way charming men do, arms wide.

"Aunt Mercy." He kissed my cheek. His lips were warm; his eyes went past me to the workshop shelves and stayed there a half-second too long. I marked it. I mark everything. It is the one talent God saw fit to grant me, and I have never once wasted it.

"You look well," I lied.

"You look terrible," he said cheerfully, which was truer, so I forgave him.

Gideon laid three places at the long table beneath the hanging charts. We ate the mutton while the wind worried the shutters and the tide muttered somewhere below the cliffs. Corin talked — about the mainland, about the price of wool, about a customs post he'd nearly taken in Kingsport before it fell through. Gideon ate in silence, precise as a clerk. And I watched them both over the rim of my cup and did my arithmetic.

Two claimants. One letter.

Ah — you didn't know about the letter yet. Patience. Even I learned of it only three days prior, from a source I shall keep to myself a while longer, the way one keeps a knife in a sleeve rather than on the table. Suffice it to say that Aldous Vane, that dry stick of a mapmaker, had spent his last months doing more than drawing coastlines. He had written something. Sealed it in iron, my source said, and hidden it where only patience would find it. Addressed, so the whisper ran, to *the rightful Dunmore*.

Rightful. There is a word to curdle a dinner.

* * *

Here is what I will confess, since confession costs me nothing now: I loved Aldous Vane. Not the way ballads mean it. I loved him the way one loves a lighthouse — distant, reliable, useful. For thirty years he charted these coasts, and for thirty years he kept his mouth shut about what he saw on them. Every cove, every cave, every moonless-night landing where Dunmore goods came ashore untaxed and unrecorded. My father's fortune. My brother's fortune. Mine, if I am honest, though honesty has never been the family trade.

Vane knew. Of course he knew. You cannot map a coast that closely and not see the comings and goings. And he never said a word — not to the revenue men, not to the magistrates, not even to me, though I brought him pies at Christmas for two decades hoping to buy what money couldn't. His silence was the one thing in Greyharrow I trusted absolutely.

And then he died on the very cliffs above the very cove, with an unfinished atlas and, apparently, a sealed letter naming heirs.

You may imagine what that did to my trust.

* * *

It was Corin who broke the silence first, as I knew he would. Charm is a tool, but greed is an engine, and Corin's engine runs hot.

"Poor old Vane," he said, pushing cabbage around his plate. "Terrible thing. Slipped on the wet rocks, they say."

"So they say," I agreed.

"Odd, though." He glanced up, all innocence, which in Corin is its own kind of confession. "A man sixty-eight years old, walking that path at night, alone, in October. Carrying nothing worth stealing, they say. Though I wonder—" and here the smile widened by exactly one tooth, "—what he *was* carrying. Or keeping. A man in his trade keeps things, doesn't he? Charts. Papers. Correspondence."

Gideon's fork stopped halfway to his mouth. Only for a moment. Then it continued its journey, and the boy chewed, and swallowed, and said, "His papers are being catalogued. For the guild."

"Catalogued," Corin repeated, tasting the word. "By whom?"

"By me. I'm his apprentice."

"Were," I corrected gently. "A master dead, the apprenticeship dies with him. That's the law, Gideon. I looked it up before I came." This was not entirely true. I had asked the toll-keeper's cousin, who once served a magistrate's clerk, and the answer had been vague enough to serve either purpose. But vagueness, deployed confidently, is nine-tenths of law.

"The atlas isn't finished," Gideon said. "Someone has to finish it. The guild expects delivery before the bridge closes."

"And you intend to be that someone." I set down my cup. The porcelain clicked against the wood, a small clean sound, and I let it sit in the silence like a period at the end of a sentence. "Alone. In this shop. With all his papers."

"I'm the only one who can read his shorthand."

"Can you." I smiled at him, and I confess I enjoyed it, the little flinch he tried not to show. "Then you'll understand why what I propose next is not an accusation but a kindness."

Corin leaned back, folding his arms, settling in like a man at a play he'd paid good money for. Let him watch. Let him think himself audience rather than subject. I had plans for Corin too, but one opens accounts in order.

"Gideon," I said. "You are nineteen years old, fatherless, motherless since spring, and apprenticed to a dead man. You have no standing in this town, no name of your own weight, and no protection worth the word. Whatever is in this shop — charts, letters, the master's effects — it is dangerous. You know that better than any of us, I think. Men have already died within sight of these windows." I let that breathe. "So. As the eldest of the Dunmore line, as the one who kept this family's books through famine and fever and my brother's drowning — I am declaring myself head of this household. What remains of it. Which means the responsibility for its safety falls to me, as it always has, whether anyone thought to ask or no."

Gideon said nothing. His hands were flat on the table, and I noticed — because I notice — that they were trembling very slightly, and that he pressed them flat to stop it. Good. Fear is manageable. Fear respects arithmetic.

"Therefore," I continued, "the papers stay here, yes, but under seal. Under *my* seal. Tomorrow morning you will gather every sheet in Aldous Vane's hand — drafts, journals, correspondence, everything — and surrender them to my keeping until the magistrate arrives in spring. For safekeeping. Nothing more. No one is accusing you of anything, nephew. I am simply ensuring that whatever killed your master does not come looking for a second Dunmore."

The wind chose that moment to slam a shutter somewhere above us, and all three of us started like guilty things, and I confess the coincidence pleased me even as it unnerved me.

"And if I refuse?" Gideon asked quietly.

"Refuse?" I laughed — genuinely, I'm sorry to say; the boy's nerve surprised real laughter out of me. "Nephew, you misunderstand your position entirely. Refusal is not among your options. It is merely among your *impulses*, and impulses are what safekeeping exists to prevent."

Corin chuckled. "She's got you there, little brother."

"Do not," I said, without turning my head, "*little brother* him, Corin. You are no more legitimate than he is, and considerably less employed." Corin's chuckle died with commendable speed. "You will both surrender claims and cooperate, or you will discover that I have spent fifty years learning where every body in this family is buried, and I am not too old to add to the collection. Metaphorically," I added, because Tansy was clearing plates in the corner and one cannot be too careful with servants.

* * *

Afterward — after Corin had gone out into the dark with a lantern and a promise to call again, after Gideon had cleared the table with the mechanical obedience of a man conserving his strength for a better battle — I stood alone in the workshop while the boy banked the fire.

The atlas lay open on the drafting table. Half-finished. The northern coast rendered in Vane's immaculate hand up to a certain point — and there, at the edge of the last completed sheet, the coastline simply stopped. Ran out into blank paper, as though the land itself had lost its nerve.

I looked at that blank space for a long time.

And I felt it, then — the grief I'd been holding at arm's length all evening, all week, all thirty years. Not grief for Vane exactly, though that too, God help me, that too. Grief for the shape of things. My father dead, my brother drowned, the old mapmaker cold in the ground, and the fortune they'd all built on midnight landings and unstamped barrels now reduced to a rumor in a sealed letter that some fool had let me hear about. Everything I had counted, all my life, and the count coming due at last.

Grief is an accounting problem. But hunger — hunger is simpler. Hunger just wants.

"Tomorrow morning, then," I said to Gideon's bent back. "The papers. First light."

He didn't turn around. "First light," he said.

And I want it recorded — here, in whatever account this testimony becomes — that I believed him. That is the humiliation of it. I read ledgers the way priests read souls, and I looked at that boy's bowed head and obedient voice and believed every word.

It was only walking home through the fog, past the toll-house with its lamp burning low, that I remembered the detail my source had given me and I had filed away and failed, for one whole evening, to weigh properly.

The letter was hidden, my source said, *in the binding of the atlas.*

And the atlas was not on my list of papers to be surrendered.

I stopped in the middle of the road, fog beading on my veil, and cursed Aldous Vane with genuine feeling — the first honest prayer I'd offered in years.

Then I turned toward the toll-house, where a certain toll-keeper owed me a favor and could be bought for the price of a second one, because if the boy meant to run, I would know it before his boots touched the bridge planks.

Winter was coming. The bridge would close soon, and Greyharrow would become a box with all of us rattling inside it.

Boxes, in my experience, keep their secrets poorly — and their bodies not at all.
