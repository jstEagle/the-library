## 18. Theft in the Night

I have been accused of many things in my sixty-one years — stinginess, ambition, a certain surgical cruelty with the truth — but never of being a fool, and I do not intend to earn the accusation at this late hour. So let me be precise about what I did and why, because history will be written by whoever survives this winter, and I intend to be the one holding the pen.

Or rather, holding the atlas.

It was the storm that decided me, though I would like it noted that the storm merely confirmed what I had already decided. Three days of wind like the fist of God hammering on every shutter of Dunmore House, the whole of us shut up together like pickled things in a jar — my nephew Gideon locked in the chart room by my own instruction, my other nephew Corin sulking through the corridors like a ghost with debts, the servants whispering, the fire eating wood faster than I could count it, and me, Mercy Dunmore, sitting in the dark with my accounts book and the growing, sickening certainty that I was about to lose everything to a boy with ink on his fingers.

I had counted everything my whole life. That is what they never forgave me for. When Father died I counted the barrels in the cellar and the coins in the strongbox and the lies in everyone's mouths, and I wrote the numbers down, because numbers do not flatter and do not flee. When my husband died — a mild man, a forgettable man, may he rest in whatever mild forgettable corner of heaven he was allotted — I counted again. And when Aldous Vane fell from the cliffs in October, I came to Greyharrow within the week, and I counted the maps in his workshop, and I found that the most important number was missing.

One letter. Iron-sealed. Addressed to the rightful Dunmore.

I had known about the letter for eleven years. I will not tell you how — a dying man talks too much when a woman brings him brandy and listens without blinking — but I knew, and I had waited eleven years for it to surface, the way a fisherman waits on a hole in the ice. And then it surfaced, and it surfaced into the hands of an apprentice. An *apprentice*. A boy with no blood in him worth the name, no claim, no standing — nothing but possession, which in this world is nine-tenths of the law and rather more than that of inheritance.

*

On the third night of the storm, the house went quiet in the way houses do when everyone is exhausted of hating each other. Corin had drunk himself into a belligerent sleep. Gideon was locked where I had put him, though I will confess the lock gave me less comfort than it should have; the boy had a way of looking at me through the door's little window that suggested he was not the one imprisoned. The servants slept in the attic like roosting birds. The wind had dropped to a moan. The fire in the study had burned down to a red, patient eye.

I went down the back stairs in my dressing gown and wool stockings, carrying a candle I did not light. I did not need light. I had spent three days in that study and knew its geography the way I knew the lines on my own hand.

The atlas was in the chart room, not the study — that was the difficulty. Gideon had kept it with him, sensibly, the little wretch. But the magistrate was expected in spring, and Gideon's confinement had an end date, and I had watched through that little window as the boy worked by lamplight, and on the second night of the storm I had seen him do the thing that decided everything: he had opened the back binding of the atlas, checked the letter was there, and closed it again, and then he had looked up — directly at me, through the glass, across the dark — and I swear to you the boy *smiled*.

He wanted me to know where it was. He wanted me to come for it. Which meant he had a plan for what happened when I did.

Well. I have been outmaneuvered by cleverer men than Gideon Dunmore, and I have buried every one of them, figuratively, in paperwork.

I did not go to the chart room. I went to the kitchen, and I woke Tam, the youngest of the servants, a boy of sixteen with a weak chin and a weaker conscience, and I put a silver crown in his palm and told him that if he did exactly as I said, and said nothing to anyone, there would be another crown in spring and a reference that would carry him into any house on the coast. And if he did not, I told him — pleasantly, I am told I can be very pleasant — I would tell the magistrate that it was Tam who had been seen carrying a lantern toward the cliffs the night Aldous Vane died.

I had not seen any such thing. But Tam did not know that, and fear is a currency that never debases.

What I needed Tam for was simple: Gideon's door was locked from the outside, but the chart room had a second entrance. There is always a second entrance in an old house built by smugglers; the Dunmores never built a room they couldn't get out of, or into. The passage behind the linen press on the landing ran behind the chart room's north wall and came out through a panel disguised as shelving. My father showed it to me when I was nine years old. He said, *Mercy, remember this, because the day will come when you need to take something from this house without asking.* He was teaching me the family trade, though he didn't know it. The Dunmore fortune was built on moving goods past the eyes of the law, and I am my father's daughter to the marrow.

The panel opened with the sound of a held breath released. The chart room smelled of lamp oil and cold ink. Gideon slept at the desk, head on his arms, the lamp guttering, and in front of him, open, lay the atlas — and beside the atlas, the letter, out of its binding, its iron seal catching the last of the lamplight.

He had left it out. That was the part I did not understand until later, and I confess it troubled me even as I stood there with my heart going like a mill wheel. A boy that careful does not leave the prize in the open. Unless the prize he was guarding was not the one I wanted.

I looked at the letter for a long moment. I could have opened it. The seal was iron, but iron yields to a patient woman and a kitchen knife. And I will tell you honestly, standing there in the dark in my wool stockings, I wanted to. I wanted it the way I have wanted very few things — to break that seal and *know*, finally, after eleven years of counting, whether the letter said my name.

I did not open it. Not because of honor — let us not be vulgar — but because an opened letter is a spent argument. A sealed letter, intact, unbroken, presented to witnesses with the seal unviolated — that is a weapon. The moment I broke it, it became only paper and my word against everyone's. I had learned that watching Gideon's forgery burn through his reputation: the boy had counterfeited Vane's hand and been caught in a day, and the catching had taught the whole household that the letter mattered. I would not make the same mistake in reverse. The seal stays whole until it is broken in front of the right people, at the right time, by me.

So I took it. The letter, and the atlas with it, because the atlas was the letter's context and a letter without context is a rumor. Gideon did not stir. I stood over him for a moment — I will admit this, since I am confessing everything — and I felt something that was almost tenderness, looking at him asleep among the dead man's maps. He had Vane's stoop already, the way he bent over the work. He had Vane's patience. He would have made a fine mapmaker, in a world where none of this had ever been hidden in any binding.

But the world is exactly the world, and I had not spent eleven years waiting to be generous.

*

The private boat was arranged through a man named Hesk, who runs oysters and other things out of the cove below the cliffs, and who owes me for a kindness done in 1849 that I will not describe in a document that might be read. Hesk asked no questions, which is why I use Hesk. The plan was simple: the storm was breaking — the wind had turned, and any sailor on this coast knows what a turned wind means — and the bridge would reopen within two days, and the last ship would sail on the morning tide after that. I intended to be gone before either. A boat from the cove at first light, out past the point before the household woke, down the coast to Millhaven, where there is a magistrate who is a cousin of my late husband and a notary who is a cousin of the magistrate, and where a sealed letter from Aldous Vane, opened before proper witnesses, naming Mercy Dunmore head of the Dunmore estate — why, such a thing, properly witnessed in Millhaven, becomes *fact* before Greyharrow is even awake to dispute it.

Let them wake. Let them shout. Facts travel faster than shouts when you own the notary.

Was it theft? Of course it was theft. I am not so foolish as to dress it in anything else. But consider — and I did consider, all night, sitting dressed in the dark with the atlas on my knees like a child with a scripture — consider what Gideon intended. I had watched him for weeks. The boy had read Vane's journals. He had found the story of the apprentice who vanished. He had deciphered the seal's inscription — Corin told me that much, Corin who tells everyone everything when you let him talk long enough — and the inscription said the letter named whoever *keeps the coast honest*. Ambiguity, deliberate, Vane's little trap. And a boy who believes a letter is a trap does not preserve it. A boy who believes that burns it. I had seen the resolution come into his face like weather. He was going to feed eleven years of my waiting to the study fire and call it mercy.

So yes: I stole it. I stole it *back*. The letter concerned my family, my name, my father's house, my father's sins and my father's gold. If there was a claim in that iron, the claim was mine — or if not mine, then it was no one's, and no one is not a thing you can inherit, and I refuse to be disinherited by an abstraction.

You will say I would have destroyed the family to own its name. I say the family was already destroyed; I merely intended to salvage the one piece worth keeping. A house on fire — you do not stand in the hall weeping about the family portraits. You carry out the silver.

*

There was one more thing to be done, and it is the thing I am least proud of, which among my sins is a competitive field.

Corin.

I went to his room in the grey hour before dawn, when the storm had died to a wet whisper and the light through the shutters was the color of dishwater. He was awake — he was always awake at that hour, the drink having released him back into his guilt — and he looked at me sitting on the edge of his chair with the atlas in a sacking wrap across my knees, and I watched him understand it, watched the arithmetic move behind his handsome ruined face.

"You're going to the cove," he said.

"I am going to Millhaven."

"And Gideon?"

"Will wake to an empty chart room and a full winter to consider his position. He is confined pending the magistrate. Nothing about his situation changes except that the evidence has been moved somewhere safer."

"You mean somewhere *yours*."

"I mean somewhere that exists," I said. "Corin. Listen to me, because I am about to offer you the only honest hour of your life. Come with me. In Millhaven, when the letter is opened, I will need a witness with the Dunmore name and the customs office's old authority. You. Your disgrace ends in the spring; your name is restored; the estate finds a place for you that is not this freezing rock. Or stay, and when the magistrate comes, explain to him why your brother's forgery was in your handwriting's vicinity, why you concealed knowledge of the letter for a month, and why the last man to speak with Aldous Vane on the cliffs —"

"I told you that in confidence."

"You told me that because you needed to give it to someone, and I am the someone things are given to. That is not confidence, Corin, that is storage." I stood, and I put my hand — my real hand, not the counting hand — on his shoulder, and I will admit his shoulder was warm and he looked at me with his mother's eyes, and for one moment I remembered him at seven years old, showing me a bird's nest he'd found, so proud, so easily proud, and I felt the old thing, the thing I do not have a name for and have never once permitted to affect an entry in the ledger.

"Come with me," I said again, quietly. "It is the last boat out of your old life. Either direction."

And do you know what he said? He said nothing. He looked at the atlas, and he looked at me, and he turned his face to the wall like a boy sent to bed without supper. Which I chose to record as consent, because a woman in my position must keep her books balanced, and I wrote it down in my head: *Corin — abstained.* In my experience, abstention is how the guilty vote.

*

The cove at first light was black glass. The storm had wrung the sky out and left it the pale grey of old linen, and the water moved under the cliffs with that long slow breathing the sea does when it has finished shouting and is thinking. Hesk's boat sat low in the water. Hesk sat lower in the boat, smoking, patient as driftwood.

I went down the cliff path with the sacking bundle under my coat, and I will not pretend my hands were steady, because the path was wet and the drop was the drop — the same drop, I could not help noticing, that had taken Aldous Vane. The rocks below were the ones from the charts, the cove Vane had drawn a dozen times in his careful secret hand, the cove where the Dunmore goods had come ashore for sixty years, the cove where the whole fortune of us had been smuggled in the dark and called inheritance in the light. It was fitting, I suppose. Everything in this family begins and ends in that cove. I stepped carefully. I have never been a woman who falls.

I was ten feet from the boat when the light changed.

Not the sky — the path. Behind me. Above me, at the head of the cliff path, a lantern came around the rocks, and under it a figure, and I did not need the lantern to tell me who it was. I knew the walk. I had watched that walk through a little window for a month, bent and patient, Vane's stoop, Vane's patience, coming down the cliff path at a pace that was not running and could not be anything but.

Gideon had the journal in one hand. I saw that much even at that distance. And he was not shouting my name, which frightened me more than shouting would have, because a boy who does not shout is a boy who does not need to.

Hesk pushed off two feet from the shingle, the boat rocking, my hand out, the atlas heavy under my coat, the letter flat and iron-sealed against my heart where it had spent the whole walk down, warm as a second pulse.

"Mrs. Dunmore," Gideon called, and his voice carried across the water the way voices do at dawn, flat and clear, "before you take another step — you should know what Vane wrote on the last page. I think you've earned the right to hear it. I think that's why he wrote it."

And I stood there on the wet rocks with the boat waiting and the letter against my heart, and I told myself I could simply step aboard, that facts travel faster than shouts, that the silver must be carried from the fire.

But I have counted everything my whole life.

And I found I needed to know the final number.
