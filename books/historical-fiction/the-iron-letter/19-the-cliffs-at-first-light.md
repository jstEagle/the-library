## 19. The Cliffs at First Light

I woke to the sound of a door that should not have been open.

In Greyharrow you learn the house's voice the way a sailor learns a ship's — every creak has a name, every groan an owner. The workshop door had a particular complaint it made only when lifted rather than pushed, and at some hour that was neither night nor morning, someone had lifted it. I lay in my cot in the chart room and listened to the silence afterward, which was worse than any sound.

The atlas was gone from its shelf. So was the letter.

I did not light a lamp. I dressed in the dark with the clumsy speed of a man who has rehearsed disaster without ever believing he'd perform it, and I went out into the street where the fog stood so thick it seemed to have opinions about my presence. The tide was out. The gulls were not yet awake. And there, at the far end of Harbour Row, a lantern bobbed toward the cliff path like a will-o'-the-wisp with poor judgment and excellent boots.

Mercy walked fast for a woman of fifty-three who claimed her knees kept her from chapel stairs.

I did not stop to think. Thinking, by then, was a luxury we had all spent. I ran.

* * *

You would think a town built on smuggling would have better lighting on the one path everyone used to avoid being seen. But Greyharrow keeps its secrets the way Mercy keeps her ledgers — jealously, and in the dark. The cliff path above Vane's Cove was a ribbon of wet shale and old sheep-track, falling away on my left to black water that breathed against the rocks below. The fog made the world a corridor ten feet long. Somewhere ahead, the lantern swung.

"Mercy!" My voice came out cracked and too loud. "Aunt Mercy!"

She didn't answer. Of course she didn't. Mercy Dunmore had spent three weeks teaching us all that questions were a form of weakness, and she wasn't going to invalidate her own curriculum now.

I gained on her where the path steepened. She was carrying the atlas under one arm and the letter — I could see it now, pale as a fish belly in her fist — and she climbed with the grim economy of a woman who had counted every step of her life and found them all owed to her. When she heard me behind her she stopped, turned, and held the letter out over the drop.

And there we were. The whole Dunmore inheritance balanced on the edge of a cliff, held by a woman whose hands, I noticed with a strange detached clarity, were shaking.

"Gideon." She said my name the way she said *debt* and *interest* — precisely, without warmth. "Go back to bed."

"You're going to the cove."

"I'm going to the boat." She corrected me the way she corrected everything, as though the error were mine for having expected anything else. "Tam Beale is waiting below with his lugger. By the time the magistrate arrives in spring, I'll be in Kingsport with witnesses enough to swear the letter names me. By the time anyone can sail after me, the estate will be settled. You'll have your atlas back when it no longer matters."

"And Corin?"

"What about him?" A flicker of something crossed her face — contempt, or hurt wearing contempt's coat. "He sold himself to me for passage money two years ago and then developed a conscience at the worst possible moment. Conscience is a luxury, Gideon. It's what people buy when they can afford to be good."

"And what does it cost to be what you are?"

For a moment I thought she'd push the letter off the cliff just to spite the question. Her knuckles whitened around it. Below us the sea worked at the rocks with the patience of something that always wins.

"It costs everything," she said quietly, "and it pays better than honesty ever did. Your master Vane learned that too late."

The words landed harder than she knew. Because I had read the journals. I had sat up three nights with Vane's cramped hand telling me what he'd found on the northern coast — the coves, the signal fires, the Dunmore marks on the smugglers' manifests going back four generations — and I had understood that Aldous Vane hadn't died for a letter. He'd died because he'd tried to give the truth away, and there are men paid to prevent exactly that.

"Vane wasn't killed by strangers," I said. "Corin told me. Your man Brack confronted him on these cliffs."

Something moved behind her eyes — not guilt, exactly. Guilt requires a standard you've failed. This was more like arithmetic arriving at a sum she'd already known.

"He was warned," she said. "Brack was supposed to frighten him. Take the charts. The old man fought — why do they always fight? — and the ground was wet." She looked down at the path beneath her feet as if seeing it for the first time. "I've done the sums a thousand times since. It doesn't come out different. It never comes out different."

"No," I said. "It doesn't. That's the thing about murder, Aunt. It's the one entry you can't amend."

Behind me, boots on shale. Corin came up the path at a run, coatless, breathing hard, and stopped when he saw the geometry of us — Mercy at the edge, the letter in her fist, me between her and the way home.

"Aunt Mercy." His voice was gentle in a way I'd never heard from him, all the charm burned off it. "Give it to him."

"To him?" She laughed, short and bitter. "To the apprentice? To the boy who forged a letter in Vane's hand and thought none of us would notice? Oh yes, I know about that too. This family runs on secrets the way the cove runs on tide, and I have counted every one of them." She raised the letter higher. "This piece of paper is the only thing in forty years that has ever been entirely mine. Do you understand? Not managed. Not shared. Mine."

"That's not ownership," Corin said. "That's drowning."

* * *

What happened next happened quickly, the way bad things do, and slowly, the way you remember them.

Mercy stepped back along the path toward the cove descent, and the shale betrayed her — it betrays everyone; it had betrayed Vane — and her heel skidded and she went down onto one knee with a cry, the atlas tumbling from under her arm, and the letter flew free of her hand.

It did not go over the cliff. That would have been clean, and nothing in this story had been clean. It caught on the wind off the water and drifted back along the path, white against the fog, and both Corin and I moved for it at the same instant, and for one absurd moment we were boys again chasing a kite string across the harbour ice.

Then it wasn't absurd. Because Corin got there first, and caught it, and stood up holding it, and I saw the calculation cross his face like weather — the same calculation I'd seen in him since he came back: *what could this buy, whom could this ruin, what was the price of being the one who holds it.*

And I saw him choose.

It took perhaps two seconds. I watched him weigh the letter in his hand the way you'd weigh a purse, and I watched Mercy struggle up from the shale with blood on her lip and her eyes fixed on the paper with a hunger that had eaten everything else in her, and I watched Corin understand — really understand, in the place where understanding actually changes things — that whatever he decided in the next breath, he would be deciding for the rest of his life.

He handed it to me.

"There," he said, and his voice broke on it, just slightly. "There. Now it's yours. God help you."

I took the letter. It weighed almost nothing. That was the whole horror of it — three weeks of our lives, Vane's life, a family bent near to breaking, and the thing itself weighed less than a gull's feather. Iron-stamped, wax-sealed, addressed in Vane's careful hand: *To the rightful Dunmore.* We had all assumed the word *rightful* pointed at one of us. Standing on the wet rock with the fog lifting grey off the water, I finally understood that Vane had never said which way it pointed. Maybe it pointed at whoever deserved it. Maybe it pointed at whoever was standing here.

Maybe it pointed at whoever was willing to let go.

Below us, in the cove, Tam Beale's lantern appeared at the waterline, and a voice called up — "Mistress Dunmore? Tide's turning!" — patient, indifferent, the voice of a man who ferried other people's greed twice weekly and charged by the passenger.

Mercy got to her feet. She wiped her lip with the back of her hand and looked at the blood, then at me, then at the letter, and I saw her take inventory — she couldn't help it; she was counting even now, even bleeding, even beaten — and the count came up short, and something in her simply stopped. Like a clock running down. Like a ledger with no more pages.

"The boat," she said, mostly to herself. "I could still—"

"You could still run," Corin said. "Yes. And in spring the magistrate comes, and Brack talks — Brack always talks, he talked to me for the price of two pints — and instead of a family scandal you'll have a warrant. Or—" he glanced at me, and I nodded, because we had agreed on this much on the walk up through the dark, agreeing the way enemies agree, which is to say barely and completely— "or you come home, and we face it together, and maybe it's only ruin instead of hanging."

"Generous," Mercy whispered.

"No," I said. "Honest. There's a difference. Vane could tell you." I looked down at the letter in my hand, and then at the two of them — my aunt with blood on her mouth and forty years of arithmetic in her eyes, my half-brother shivering in his shirtsleeves with the dawn coming up behind him like a debt finally called — and I said the truest thing I had said in weeks:

"We're taking it home. All of it. And tomorrow morning, before the household, I'm reading it aloud. Every word. Whatever it says, whoever it names — they hear it from my mouth, in front of witnesses, and then we decide together what happens to it. No more secrets. Vane died trying to end them. The least we owe him is to try."

Mercy stared at me for a long moment. The fog was thinning now, tearing into rags, and the first real light came off the water and touched her face, and for once I couldn't read her at all. Then she turned and started down toward the cove — not to the boat, I realized, but past it, to the path home, walking with her back straight and her chin up as though she were leading a procession instead of surrendering.

"Well?" she said over her shoulder, without turning. "Are you coming? The fire will want lighting, and I am *not* doing it."

Corin exhaled a laugh that was half sob. He put a hand on my shoulder — briefly, awkwardly, the first time he'd touched me since childhood — and we followed her down.

The letter rode in my breast pocket, light as a sin, heavy as a name.

Tomorrow, before the whole household, I would break Vane's iron seal and read aloud whatever truth he had stamped inside it. I told myself I was ready for anything it might say.

I was lying. But lying, I was learning, is just the truth practiced in front of a mirror — and by morning, one way or another, the mirror was going to break.
