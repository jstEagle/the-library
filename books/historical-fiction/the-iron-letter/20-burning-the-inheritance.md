## 20. Burning the Inheritance

The storm had spent itself by morning, and Greyharrow woke to a sky scrubbed pale and a sea lying flat as poured pewter. The house smelled of wet wool and woodsmoke and the particular staleness of three days of people who had not been able to leave one another's company. I stood at the workshop window and watched gulls argue over something in the tide wrack, and I thought: *today*.

I had slept, insofar as sleep was possible, with Vane's journal open on my chest like a breastplate. The last pages were simple. That was the thing that undid me — after all the cipher and concealment, all the iron and the hiding places, his final instruction was four lines long and written in the plain hand he used for tide tables:

*The letter names no one. It cannot. Let them fight over it if they must — the fighting will show you what they are. Then burn it. Burn it before witnesses, so that no one can say it said what they wanted it to say. An inheritance is only a chain you agree to wear. Refuse.*

He had known. Of course he had known. He had sat in this room with his bad lamp and his steady hand and he had understood exactly what would happen when he died — that Mercy would come counting, that Corin would come charming, that the letter would become a weapon passed from grip to grip until someone's fingers bled. And rather than prevent it, he had built a trap out of honesty itself. The letter's power was never in its words. It was in what people believed it could give them.

Which meant the only way to disarm it was to show them there was nothing inside but their own wanting.

I dressed carefully, as one dresses for a funeral or an execution, and I went down to assemble my family.

* * *

The great room of the Dunmore house had not hosted all of us at once since before Vane died, and it wore the occasion badly. The fire had been fed into a proper blaze — Mrs. Tallow, our housekeeper of forty years and the only person in Greyharrow whose loyalty had never once been for sale, had taken one look at my face and understood her instructions without my speaking them. *A good fire, Tallow. Big enough to be seen from the doorway.* She'd raised an eyebrow at me, the eyebrow she reserved for my childhood lies about stolen jam, and then she'd built a fire like a small bonfire anyway.

Mercy sat in the high-backed chair nearest the hearth, which she had claimed sometime during the storm and defended with the quiet ferocity of a general holding a hill. She had dressed for the morning as though it were a tribunal — black wool, jet brooch, hair pinned with mathematical severity. Her hands were folded in her lap. They did not fold themselves there peacefully; they were folded the way a trap is set.

Corin stood at the window with his back to the room, watching the harbor. He had barely spoken since the cliffs. Whatever confession had cracked him open on that wet path, he was still carrying the pieces of it around, and every few minutes his eyes found mine with an expression I couldn't name — part apology, part plea, part the look of a man checking whether the rope he's tied himself to is still holding.

Mrs. Tallow hovered. Two of the fishing families who had worked the Dunmore boats for generations — old Bram Hedley and his daughter Sennet — stood awkwardly near the door because I had asked them to come, and witnesses require witnesses. Sennet kept glancing at the fire as though it might explain itself.

And on the table between us all, where I had placed it an hour earlier under a linen cloth like a dish at a wake, lay the atlas. Beside it, the letter. Iron seal unbroken now — I had opened it at dawn, alone, with shaking hands, because I refused to stand before these people and break a seal whose contents I hadn't read. A man should know his own executioner's face.

"Whatever theater this is," Mercy said, without preamble, "I trust it will be brief. There are accounts to see to."

"There always are," I said. "That's rather the point."

She looked at me then, fully, and I saw what the storm and the cliffs and the theft had left in her: not remorse — I don't believe remorse lived anywhere in that house of hers — but a kind of exhaustion, the deep structural fatigue of a woman who had been holding up a building with her shoulders for thirty years and had begun to suspect the building wasn't worth it. It was the closest thing to humanity she'd shown since her arrival, and it frightened me more than her hunger had. Hungry people can be fed. Hollowed people simply collapse, and take whatever stands near them down into the hole.

"Gideon." My name in her mouth sounded like a summons. "You were confined to the workshop pending the magistrate. You stole from this household—"

"I forged," I corrected. "Let's have our crimes precise, Aunt. Precision is the family trade."

Corin turned from the window. "Gideon."

"No." I held up a hand. "No, let's be precise, because precision is the only inheritance we've actually got. Mercy counts everything. Corin charms everyone. I draw coastlines. And Aldous Vane—" my voice caught, and I let it catch; there was no dignity left in pretending otherwise, "—Aldous Vane mapped the truth of this coast for forty years, including the parts of it that made this family rich, and he died on the cliffs above Cove Hallow with his charts finished and his conscience clean, and I am going to tell you what he left behind."

I lifted the linen cloth.

The atlas sat there, bound in grey leather, its spine stitched with the double-line pattern Vane used on all his masterworks. Next to it, the letter — heavy paper, iron seal cracked down the middle like a struck bell.

Sennet shifted. Old Bram took off his cap, an instinct from church.

"The last ship of the season sails tomorrow morning," I said. "The bridge opens at first light — the toll-keeper sent word an hour ago. Which means that whatever happens in this room today happens in front of the five people standing here, and then the sea closes behind it until spring. So." I picked up the letter. The iron seal was cold even now, cold the way metal stays cold, as though it remembered being stamped. "Before witnesses, as the law requires and as decency suggests, I am going to read Aldous Vane's final letter aloud. All of it. Every word. And then I am going to burn it."

"You'll do no such thing," Mercy said, rising.

"Aunt Mercy—"

"That letter names the rightful heir to this estate. To the houses, the boats, the ledgers, the *routes*—" she stopped herself, one beat too late, and I watched the word *routes* hang in the air like smoke. Even now. Even hollowed, she reached for it.

"It names the rightful heir," I agreed. "Those are nearly its first words. Would you like to hear the rest?"

Her jaw worked. I watched her calculate — I could almost see the abacus behind her eyes, beads sliding, columns summing. If I read it and it named her, she won everything in front of witnesses. If it named Corin, she'd contest it. If it named me—

If it named me, she'd have learned nothing new, because she already believed I was hiding it precisely because it named me.

"Read it," she said finally, sitting back down, and her voice was arctic. "Read it. And may God forgive you for what you've done with an honest man's dying words."

"He'd have liked that," I said. "He didn't much care for priests but he loved forgiveness. Said it was the only arithmetic that never balanced."

I broke the remaining wax and unfolded the paper. Vane's hand marched across it, steady to the last line. I read aloud.

*To the rightful Dunmore.*

The room went still. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

*I write this knowing you will find it only after I am past explaining myself, and knowing too that whoever holds it will want it to say something it does not say. So let me say plainly what it says.*

*It names no one.*

Mercy's breath came out slow and controlled, the exhale of a woman refusing to gasp.

*There is no rightful heir to the Dunmore estate. There is no estate. There is a fortune built on coves I have charted and men I have watched drown in them, on routes your grandfather ran and your father denied and your aunt has counted to the penny, and there is no honest way to inherit it. I looked for one for forty years. I charted every inch of this coast looking for the honest branch of that tree. It does not exist.*

*So the letter names whoever keeps the coast honest. Not the one who owns it. The one who keeps it honest. And since I have met this family, I expect that is no one either — unless the burning of this letter is itself the keeping-honest, in which case the burner is the heir, and the inheritance is ashes, and ashes weigh nothing, and a man can carry ashes anywhere.*

*Burn it. Before witnesses. Do not sell it, do not trade it, do not use it, do not let it be used. It is a test with no passing grade except refusal.*

*An atlas is honest. A letter is not. Be the atlas.*

*— A.V.*

Silence. The particular silence of a room full of people re-arranging their entire understanding of the last two months.

Old Bram put his cap back on, which I took to mean the church portion was concluded. Sennet was staring at the fire. Corin had both hands flat on the windowsill, head bowed, and I heard him laugh once — a short, broken sound, half grief and half admiration. "The old fox," he said softly. "The absolute old fox. He made us do it to ourselves."

Mercy had not moved. She sat with her spine straight and her hands still folded, and her face was doing something I had never seen it do: nothing. No calculation. No count. The abacus behind her eyes had simply stopped, beads scattered, frame cracked, because there was nothing left to count. Forty years of ledgers, and the final entry read *nothing to inherit, nothing owed, nothing held.*

"You knew," she said at last. Her voice was very quiet. "You opened it before us. You knew what it said and you let us — you let me—"

"I let you show me who you were," I said. "Yes. He asked me to. It's in the journal — the whole design of it. I'm sorry, Aunt. I'm sorry for the forgery, and I'm sorry for the game, and I'm sorriest of all that the game was rigged from the start by a dead man who loved this family enough to hate it honestly."

"Hate it—" She rose again, and for one moment I thought she would strike me, and some distant part of me noted that it would be the most honest act she'd committed in years. But her arm didn't fall. It just hung there, and then slowly, joint by joint, she lowered it, and the lowering took something with it. I watched it go. Whatever had driven Mercy Dunmore across two months and one storm and one cliff path — ambition, grief, hunger, love twisted into ledger-form — it drained out through her lowered hand and pooled on the floorboards and was gone.

She looked old. Not frail — she would never be frail — but old, the way a lighthouse is old, still standing, still painted, guiding no ships because the channel has moved.

"Burn it, then," she said.

I had expected more fight. I think we all had. But she walked to the window — Corin stepped aside to let her pass, and something passed between them too, some settlement of accounts that had nothing to do with money — and she stood where he had stood, looking at the harbor, and she did not watch the burning. I understood. She could survive losing. What she could not survive was witnessing it.

I carried the letter to the fire.

For a moment I held it over the flames, and I will confess here, in the privacy of my own telling, that I hesitated. Not because I doubted. Because the paper was Vane's, and his hand was on it, and burning a dead man's writing feels like a second death, a smaller one, the kind you commit yourself. Then I remembered the last line. *Be the atlas.* The atlas was safe in its grey binding, every coastline true, every cove named, every smuggler's route recorded not to be used but to be *known* — memory carried forward honestly, which is the only inheritance worth the word.

I let go.

The corner caught, flared orange, and the fire took the letter the way the sea takes a body — thoroughly, without malice, leaving nothing. The iron seal fragment, pried loose, sat glowing in the coals for a moment, then dulled to black. Ashes weighed nothing. He'd been right about that too.

"Well," said old Bram Hedley into the silence, with the impeccable timing of fishermen at funerals, "that's that, then. Kettle's on, I shouldn't wonder."

And that, absurdly, was that. Sennet smiled at me — the first unguarded expression anyone had aimed my way in weeks. Corin crossed the room and gripped my shoulder once, hard, saying nothing, which from Corin was a eulogy and an apology and a promise all pressed flat into five pounds of pressure. Mrs. Tallow poured tea as though the fate of a dynasty had not just been dissolved in her fireplace, because to her it hadn't; to her it was Tuesday.

Only Mercy stayed at the window. When I brought her a cup — because she was still my aunt, and cruelty and kinship are not mutually exclusive — she took it without looking at me.

"Your grandfather ran rum through Cove Hallow," she said, to the glass, to the harbor, to nobody. "My father denied it. I counted it. And a mapmaker with ink-stained fingers just burned the whole account book in front of the help." A pause. "He was the best of us, Gideon. That's the worst thing I've learned all winter."

"Yes," I said. "It is."

She drank her tea. Outside, far across the flat pewter water, the first light caught the mast of a ship riding at anchor in the roads — the *Kestrel*, last vessel of the season, waiting on the morning tide.

I had an atlas to finish packing, and a captain to find, and one blank page I intended to keep.

The bridge opened at first light.
