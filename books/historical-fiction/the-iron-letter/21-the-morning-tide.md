## 21. The Morning Tide

The bridge opened three days before it had any right to.

Old Hollis the toll-keeper came banging at the workshop door before dawn, still wearing his nightshirt under his coat, shouting the news up the stairwell like a man who had been promised a funeral and been handed a christening instead. The engineers from the mainland had crossed at first light with their chains and their instruments and declared the span sound. The winter had broken its own siege. The storms had spent themselves somewhere over the open water, and Greyharrow — which had spent two months as a sealed box, a locked room with gulls — was suddenly, absurdly, a port again.

I heard the news from my bed and did not move for a long while. There is a particular kind of silence that follows a siege lifting. You have arranged your whole soul around confinement, and then the walls politely step aside, and you are left standing in a room with no shape, wondering what you were defending and from whom.

The last ship of the season would sail on the morning tide after next. The *Kestrel's Mercy* — a name that had always struck me as the sort of thing a shipowner chooses when he wants his creditors to think well of him — would take on cargo at the quay and carry whatever and whoever remained to the mainland before the ice closed the northern passage for true.

Which meant I had two days to decide what I was carrying, and what I was leaving, and which of those was the heavier.

* * *

I found Corin at the quay that first morning, standing at the rail of the *Kestrel's Mercy* like a man pricing his own escape. He had shaved. That was the detail that undid me, more than anything he said. In all the months of his return — the charm, the probing, the bargain he'd offered me in the smoke room of the Anchor, the confession he'd finally dragged out of himself on the cliff path like a splinter from the palm — he had worn the same gray stubble, the same disreputable half-dress of a man who had stopped performing for anyone. Now the stubble was gone and his coat was brushed and he looked, for the first time since he'd come back to Greyharrow, like the customs officer he had once been rather than the ruin he had become.

"You're leaving," he said. Not a question. He'd read it in my walk, I suppose. Brothers — half-brothers, but the blood doesn't consult the fractions — read each other the way Vane read coastlines. By the shape of the approach.

"On the morning tide," I said. "The atlas goes to the guild. I go with it."

"And me?" He said it lightly, but his hand was doing something at the rail, worrying the wood the way he used to worry his sleeve when we were boys and he'd broken something of Father's.

"You're staying."

He looked at me then, and I watched him decide not to argue. That was new, too. The old Corin would have argued with the tide itself, on principle, and then bribed it.

"Hollis tells me the magistrate's sloop can make the crossing inside the week, now the bridge is open," he said. "I sent the letter yesterday. Full confession. Vane, the cliffs, Mercy's man, all of it. My hand, my seal, my name at the bottom." He almost smiled. "First honest document I've produced since I was dismissed from the customs house. It felt strange. Like wearing someone else's coat."

"What did you tell them about yourself?"

"Everything." He said it flatly, and I understood what it cost, because Corin had spent his whole life telling people only the version of himself that opened the next door. "The smuggling I ignored for the right payments. The ledgers I cooked. The years I knew what the Dunmore money was and drank rather than look at it. If I'm going to stand up in front of a magistrate and say my aunt had a man murdered, I can't do it from behind a wall of my own clean hands. There are no clean hands on that coast, Gideon. Vane knew that better than anyone. That's rather the point of the letter, isn't it? Was."

It was. I didn't say so. We had agreed, he and I and the household, that the letter's contents would go to the grave with the letter itself — that what Vane had built as a test would die unadministered, and we would all of us have to go on living without a verdict. Some mornings I thought that was mercy. Some mornings I thought it was just a slower way of never knowing. Most mornings I thought Vane would have found the distinction very funny.

"Mercy knows you're staying?" I asked.

"She knows." He turned back to the water. The *Kestrel's Mercy* rode low with the first of its cargo — wool, salt fish, the ordinary commerce of an ordinary port, none of it hidden in false bottoms, none of it bought with blood money. It looked almost boring. I found I liked it boring. "We had it out last night. Properly. She didn't weep and she didn't threaten, which from Mercy is practically an embrace. She said—" and here he did something I had never heard him do, which was hesitate, "—she said that of the two of us, she'd always thought I was the one who'd come to a bad end, and she was sorry to learn she'd been the bad end herself."

I had no answer for that. There wasn't one. Mercy Dunmore had spent a lifetime counting everything — spoons, ledgers, grievances, people — and the final audit had come back against her, and she had signed it, and now she would go on living in the house her grandfather had built on smugglers' coves, managing a diminished estate with the same ferocious competence she'd once aimed at owning all of us. Not redeemed. Mercy was not a woman who could be redeemed; redemption would have required her to believe she'd been wrong, and she believed, to the marrow, that she had merely been outmaneuvered — by Vane, by the letter, by me. But freed, perhaps. Freed the way a man is freed from a debt he can never pay: the collector stops coming, and the debt remains, and he carries it, and he learns to walk with the weight.

"She asked me to tell you something," Corin said. "She wouldn't say it herself. She said—" he closed his eyes briefly, as if reading from a document he wished didn't exist, "—she said: tell the boy the atlas was the only thing in that workshop worth anything, and she always knew it."

"That's not an apology."

"No. It's Mercy. It's the closest thing she has." He pushed off the rail. "She's having the east wing closed for the winter. Says she'll take in mending and sell the Vane commissions the guild doesn't want. She asked — and Gideon, I want you to appreciate what this cost her — she asked whether you'd leave her the draft sheets. The northern coast. She said the house should know what it was built on, even if the town never does."

I had thought about those sheets for a long time. They were the truest thing Vane ever drew — every cove, every landing stair, every dark window on the cliffs where the Dunmore goods had come ashore for three generations, rendered in his small exact hand as if honesty were simply a matter of sufficient detail. I had wanted to burn them, once. Then I had wanted to publish them, which was the same impulse wearing better clothes. In the end I had done neither, because Vane had taught me — too late, in the only language he'd ever used, which was ink — that a map is not an accusation. It is a record. What people do with a record is their own affair, and their own sin.

"She can have the drafts," I said. "The finished atlas goes to the guild. That was the arrangement, and I'm keeping it."

"He'd have liked that," Corin said. "The arrangement being kept, I mean. He was a great one for arrangements." He paused. "He was a great one for you, too. I don't know if he ever said it. Men like that don't. But he talked about his apprentice the way other men talk about sons, and I was in the room often enough to hear it."

"He never said it."

"No. I didn't think so." Corin looked at me for a moment, and for once there was no calculation behind his eyes, only the plain gray weather of a man who had run out of moves. "I'll be here when you come back, if you come back. Assuming the magistrate doesn't hang me, in which case I'll be here in a more permanent capacity."

"They don't hang for ledgers."

"They don't hang for ledgers," he agreed. "But I watched my aunt's agent put a mapmaker off a cliff, and I said nothing for two months, and there are magistrates on the mainland who take a dim view of silence." He stuck out his hand — an absurd, formal gesture, entirely unlike him, and entirely like the boy who'd taught me to gut fish on this same quay twenty years ago — and I took it. "Fair tides, Gideon."

"Fair tides, Corin."

He walked off up the quay toward the toll-house, where he'd taken to helping Hollis with the winter accounts, unpaid, because he said a man should learn one honest trade before the magistrate made him learn a dishonest one. I watched him go and felt the strange arithmetic of it: I had come to Greyharrow with no family worth the name, and I was leaving it with less than that and somehow more — a brother staying to face what he'd done, an aunt retreating to count what remained, and between us nothing settled, nothing saved, nothing destroyed. Just freed. All of us turned loose from a letter that had never said what any of us wanted it to say, which was, I suppose, the only true thing it could have said.

* * *

The last full day, I spent in the workshop.

I had thought it would be harder. I had rehearsed it — the closing of the shutters, the banking of the stove, the small ceremonies of leaving a dead man's room for the last time. In practice it was mostly sweeping. Grief, it turns out, is largely housekeeping when you give it long enough. I swept the ink grit from under the drafting table. I oiled the hinges on the flat files. I wrapped the good pens in waxed cloth the way Vane had taught me, nibs up, always nibs up, because a pen laid on its side forgets its purpose, and he had delivered that instruction with such gravity that I had never once, in six years, disobeyed it.

The draft sheets for Mercy I left in the second flat file, labeled in my own hand: *Northern Coast — final surveys — for the Dunmore house.* Under it, after some deliberation, I added: *Read them all the way through.* She would. She was incapable of not reading a document all the way through. It was the cruelest and kindest thing I could think of, and I have never been entirely sure which it was.

The journal I took with me. That was not in the arrangement, and I expect the guild would have wanted it, and I expect Mercy would have called it theft if she'd known, and I found I did not care. Some things belong to the person who was meant to read them, and Vane had written the last pages in a hand that loosened as it went, the letters of a man setting down the truth at last because he had run out of time to be careful. *The letter was never meant to name anyone,* he had written. *It was meant to make them name themselves. If the boy is reading this, then it worked, and I am sorry, and I am proud, and the atlas is the only heirloom I have to leave him. Let him keep one page blank. A map of the whole coast is worth nothing to a man who has no room left to chart what he finds.*

So I kept one page blank. The last one. I don't know yet what goes on it. That is rather the point.

* * *

On the morning of the tide, the whole household came down to the quay, which surprised me, and then didn't. Greyharrow is a small town; there is nowhere else for a household to be.

Mercy stood at the head of the slip in her good gray wool, upright as a mast, and she did not embrace me and I did not expect her to. She looked at me for a long moment with those counting eyes — I could nearly hear the abacus of her, tallying what I had cost her and what she had cost herself and arriving, for once, at no total she was willing to speak aloud.

"The atlas," she said.

"Goes to the guild. As arranged."

"And you'll write."

It was not a question, but it was the nearest thing to one she had produced in two months, so I treated it as one. "When there's something worth writing."

"There's always something worth writing," said Mercy Dunmore, who had never in her life written a letter she did not later wish to burn. "That's the trouble with the world."

Then she did the thing I will keep as long as I keep anything. She reached into her reticule and pressed a coin into my hand — a single silver crown, old mint, worn nearly smooth — and closed my fingers over it with her dry strong grip. "Passage money," she said. "From the estate. It's owed. I keep accounts, Gideon. Even the ones nobody audits."

And she turned and walked back up the slip before I could thank her, because Mercy Dunmore would rather be struck by lightning than be thanked, and I stood there holding a coin that was not passage money at all, and we both knew it, and neither of us said it, and that was the whole of our reconciliation, and it was enough.

The crew took my sea chest. The captain — a leathery, unimpressed man named Brack who had heard, as everyone in Greyharrow had heard, at least four versions of the winter's events and believed none of them — looked me up and down on the gangplank.

"You the mapmaker, then?"

"The apprentice."

"Same thing, on a boat this size." He jerked his chin at the parcel under my arm, wrapped in oilcloth against the spray. "That the famous atlas?"

"It's an atlas."

"Everything in this town is famous and nothing in it is what it's said to be," Brack observed, without malice, and turned to shout at his mate about the tide, and I went up the gangplank into the ordinary, boring, honest business of leaving.

* * *

The *Kestrel's Mercy* warped off the quay with the tide under her and the morning sun burning the last of the fog off the water, and Greyharrow fell away behind us the way places do — not all at once but in pieces, the toll-house first, then the church spire, then the long gray shoulder of the cliffs where Vane had died and where, somewhere under the grass and the gorse, the truth about him was finally allowed to rest. Corin stood at the toll-house window. I couldn't see his face at that distance. I raised my hand anyway, and the figure in the window raised its hand, and that was the last of my family that I saw, and it was not nothing, and for a long time it would have to be enough.

I went below and unwrapped the atlas and sat with it on my knees in the heave and clatter of the deck, checking the binding the way you check a sleeping child. Every chart was finished. Every coast was true. Somewhere in the guild hall on the mainland, men with clean hands and clean ledgers would open it and find exactly what a coastal atlas ought to contain and nothing more, and they would never know what it had cost, and that was right. A record is not an accusation. It is only a record, and the keeping of it is the whole of the honor there is.

I turned to the last page. Blank.

The gulls followed us out past the harbor light, and the wind came around into the west, fair and cold and smelling of nothing but open water, and I sat there with Vane's atlas in my lap and the silver crown in my pocket and one empty page waiting, and I found I was not mourning and not rejoicing but simply — ready. Which is, I think, the finest thing a dead master can leave an apprentice. Not a fortune. Not a name. Just the room to chart what he finds.

The coast fell below the horizon. I took out a pen.

Nibs up, always nibs up. I set it to the page, and the first line of whatever came next went down clean.
