## 39. The Diver Who Testifies

The ferry that runs to Verrenhollow now is a proper passenger boat, painted white, with benches and a bell and a boy who collects fares and calls everyone "sir" or "madam" without checking which they'd prefer. I paid my fare like anyone else. The boy looked at me, looked at the scar along my forearm where the pen-bars caught me in the pens, looked at the way I stood at the rail with my weight already braced for weather, and said, "You're her, aren't you. The one from the trial."

"I'm a diver," I said.

"That's what I meant," he said, and gave me back my coin.

Twelve years since I left this harbor in a stolen sloop with smoke behind me and a stranger's child in Wren's berth. Nine years since I stood in the naval court at Meridian and read names aloud until the admirals' faces went the color of drowned things. Four years since the charters were revoked and the Meridian Trading Company became, on paper, three smaller companies with kinder letterheads and, if Tam were alive to say it — and he is not, so I will say it for him — the same arithmetic underneath.

The harbor comes up out of the haze the way it always did: the long curve of the mole, the drying sheds rebuilt twice and whitewashed badly, the fort on the headland with its guns removed and its windows glazed, because even forts get old eventually and become something people live in. The iron orchards lie beneath us still, the pearl beds dark and patient as ever. Water doesn't hold grudges. That's the first thing I teach the children, and the truest.

I had told myself I was coming home for practical reasons. There's a school now in the old net-loft — Odile's doing, Odile's war continued by gentler means — and she wrote to me every season until I stopped pretending the letters went unread. *They need someone who knew the water before the ledgers did,* she wrote last spring. *They need someone who can show them the hand-signals aren't a curiosity. They're a birthright.*

I am fifty-one years old. My lungs are Sefa's lungs, which is to say they are a debt I am still paying down, and I have decided to pay it forward instead of down, because forward is a direction and down is just where she is.

* * *

Odile Ferran met me at the dock, and we did not embrace, because some friendships are built entirely out of restraint and would collapse if you added warmth to them. She is gray now, and rounder, and wears spectacles she pretends are for reading only. She held out her hand. I shook it. It was exactly as dry and exact as it had been when she pressed a hidden page into my palm in the shadow of the salt-house all those years ago and said nothing, because saying nothing was the whole language we shared.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You look like a schoolmistress."

"I am a schoolmistress." She took my bag before I could argue about it. "Forty-one pupils. Half of them can't swim properly, which in Verrenhollow is a scandal nobody has the decency to be embarrassed by. Their parents work the beds for the new companies, who pay better than Meridian did and keep books almost as crooked, but crooked in smaller handwriting, so it's harder to prove."

"And you're proving it."

"I'm teaching children their letters," she said mildly. "What they do with them afterward is between them and their consciences." She glanced sideways at me. "And yours, apparently, since you came."

We walked up through the town. It was market day, and the stalls had spilled past their chalk lines the way they always had, and an old woman selling smoked eel called my name before Odile could steer me past — "Lior Thackeray, as I breathe!" — and pressed an eel into my hands and refused payment, and by the time we'd escaped the third such ambush I was carrying enough food to provision a small ship and Odile was wearing the expression of a woman whose careful plans have been eaten by a town.

"They'll want the story," she said.

"They've heard the story."

"They've heard the *trial*. Nobody tells it right. They have you diving the vault alone in moonlight, fighting off soldiers with a boat hook." She sniffed. "There were no soldiers. There was nearly no moonlight. You fought off a strongbox chain and your own good sense, and then you carried a drowning man up two hundred feet of flooded stairwell, which was the stupidest brave thing I ever saw done in that harbor, and I watched a dockworker get shot for a page he never touched."

"Kolvey says the same thing. About the stupid part."

"Kolvey," Odile said, "is waiting at the school with oysters, because she believes every occasion should be marked with shellfish, and because she farmed them herself and will tell you so at length whether you ask or not."

* * *

Maren Kolvey had changed less than any of us, which I put down to guilt keeping a person young the way worry keeps a person thin. Her hair was cropped short and going silver at the temples, and her hands were ruined in the particular way of oyster farmers' hands — cut, salted, healed crooked — and when she saw me she stood very straight, the old officer's posture surfacing through the farmer like a wreck coming up on a spring tide, and then she let it go and hugged me, hard, which surprised us both.

"Lieutenant," I said into her shoulder.

"Not for nine years." She pulled back and studied my face the way she used to study manifests, looking for the line that didn't add up. "You got old."

"You got honest."

"One of us had to." She handed me an oyster, pried open, sitting in its own liquor on the half shell. "Eat. You'll want your strength. Forty-one children, Lior. Odile undersold it. It's forty-one children and their mothers and their grandmothers and at least one dog, and they all want to know if the hand-signals are true."

"Are what true?"

"The ones from the story." Kolvey's mouth twisted. "That the divers talked underwater. That the messages moved faster than the soldiers could march. That a whole town conspired in silence and the silence won." She sat down heavily on a bench in the old net-loft, which still smelled faintly of hemp and tar under the new smell of chalk and ink. "Because here's what keeps me up at night, Thackeray. If it becomes a story, it stops being a method. Stories get told. Methods get taught. And there are new companies out there with new books, and somewhere right now some clerk is quietly falsifying a line to shield a family she pities, and somewhere right now some captain is doing arithmetic on lives, and those children—" she nodded toward the door, beyond which the sounds of the morning tide and the town came soft and constant, "—those children need to know that what happened here wasn't a miracle. It was work. Done by tired people. Mostly frightened. Often too late."

I ate the oyster. It was excellent, and I told her so, and she waved it off as though excellence were merely adequate.

"When do I start?" I asked.

"Tomorrow at dawn," Odile said from the doorway. "Low tide. We start where everything starts here." She smiled, and it was the old dry smile, the one that had falsified a ledger line and hidden a page and burned a copy rather than surrender names. "In the water."

* * *

Teaching children to dive is teaching children to be afraid correctly.

That's the whole curriculum, if you boil it down. Anyone can learn to hold breath; the body wants to do it, the body is a miser with its air. What takes seasons is teaching a child that fear is information, not instruction. That the tightening in the chest at forty feet is a message, not a command. That panic is the only thing down there that can actually kill you, and it kills you by making you forget everything you know.

I had eleven of them in the shallows off the mole the first week, and I taught them the way Sefa taught me, which is to say I taught them silences first. How to enter the water without a sound. How to move along the bottom without raising silt. How to read another diver's body at twenty feet — the set of the shoulders, the angle of the chin — well enough to hold a conversation without a single bubble wasted.

Then the hand-signals.

Fist closed: stop. Flat hand sweeping: come. Two fingers walking across the palm: *count.* Thumb and forefinger making a small circle over the heart: *remember.*

That last one isn't in any company manual. It never was. It was ours, born in the orchards during the requisition, passed down the ropes of the salvage pens, carried by children through cordon lines where soldiers' eyes couldn't follow. *Remember.* A name given. A promise made. A list kept.

Wren had added signals since — she writes to me from whatever coast her salvage crew is working, little diagrams inked on scrap paper, new signs for new needs: *storm coming*, *man overboard*, *money owed, pay it*, and my favorite, a flat hand chopping the other palm twice, which means *enough talk, do the thing.* She invented that one, she admits, specifically for me.

On the fourth morning, a girl named Brissa — ten years old, sharp-elbowed, the daughter of a bed-diver and a rope-maker — surfaced next to me after her first real dive among the young oysters and made the circle over her heart without being taught it. Just did it. Pointed down at the water, at the orchards below, at everything living and dead down there in the cold green dark.

"Who taught you that?" I asked, treading water.

"My gran," she said. "She says it's how you talk to the ones who stayed down."

I looked at this child, this fierce unindentured child with her gran's superstition and her own stubbornness, and I thought: Sefa, look. Look what survived you. Not the lungs. The language.

"Yes," I told Brissa. "It is. And today I'll teach you the rest of what your gran only half-remembers. And then you'll teach it to someone younger, and they'll teach it to someone younger, and that's how the company loses forever — not in a courtroom. In a conversation it isn't invited to."

* * *

Some evenings I walk out to the point where the old fort stands with its glazed windows and its geraniums — someone's grandmother grows geraniums in the gun embrasures now, red as warning flags, defiantly cheerful — and I sit on the seawall and do the tallying that Kolvey says I shouldn't do and that I will do until I die, because somebody has to keep the count even if the courts won't.

Sefa Thackeray. Died seven years ago, in a house above the harbor with the window open to the water, holding my hand and making me promise to keep diving so the orchards would remember. I keep the promise. The orchards remember. Some mornings, working the beds, I pass over ground she worked, and I make the circle over my heart, and I don't care if the young divers think it's superstition. Superstition is just memory wearing work clothes.

Tam. Died four years ago, in his sleep, in Meridian, with his book of the drowned finally entered into evidence and finally, officially, believed. He'd wanted to see the verdict. He got within a year of it. The book sits now in the naval archive under glass, and I have been to see it, and I stood there like a fool while tourists shuffled past, looking at my uncle's careful handwriting cataloguing the company's "equipment losses" — forty-three names, each one a person the ledger had reduced to gear — and I thought: they put it under glass to protect it. Tam would have laughed himself sick. He spent twelve years hiding that book inside a false bottom of his sea chest, wrapped in oilcloth, and now it sleeps behind museum glass with a brass plaque, and the plaque gets the dates wrong.

Odile corrected the plaque. In pencil. Small enough that the curators haven't noticed yet. She told me this with the satisfaction of a woman who has committed a crime so minor it circles back around to virtue.

And the stranger's child — the girl who took Wren's berth while I stayed on the burning dock — her name is Ismey Corvane, and she is twenty-four years old, and she captains a pilot boat out of Meridian harbor, and she found me at the trial, afterward, in the corridor full of newspaper men, and she took both my hands and said, "You don't know me. You gave me your life and I intend to spend it loudly." And she has. Loudly is the word. She sails rescue runs in weather that keeps the navy in port, and she named her first boat *Thackeray*, and I have never once managed to thank her without my voice doing something embarrassing, so mostly I don't try.

This is the price of survival, tallied: a mother, an uncle, twelve years of servitude, a harbor burned, a childhood spent learning which silences keep people safe. This is what it bought: forty-one children who can swim. A school where the books are kept honestly, or dishonestly in the direction of mercy, which Odile maintains is the only honest kind. An officer turned oyster farmer who cries at weddings and lies to no one anymore, not even herself. A cousin who owns her own ship. A girl who spends her life loudly.

Worth paying. I write that sometimes, in the margin of the old hidden list, when the counting gets heavy. *Worth paying.* Ink doesn't believe it. But ink is patient, and so am I, and some debts you settle by living.

* * *

It was near the end of my second month back that the letter came, and it came the way trouble always comes to Verrenhollow: on the water, carried by someone wind-burned and out of breath.

Brissa brought it up from the dock at a run, sand spraying, and thrust it at me where I sat mending nets with the older children — a skill I teach alongside the signals, because a diver who can't mend a net is a diver who owes someone money for mending, and we are done with that economy if I have anything to say about it, which, the trial proved, occasionally I do.

"It's the pilot boat," Brissa said, panting. "The *Thackeray*. She's standing in past the mole and flying the yellow flag, and the harbormaster's boys are running around like gulls in a fish fight, and—"

Yellow flag. Quarantine, or distress, or — in the new code Wren's crews use, the code that grew out of the old divers' signals the way a reef grows out of drowned wood — *come now, bring everyone who matters.*

I opened the letter. Wren's handwriting, fast and slanting, the handwriting of a woman writing on a deck that was not sitting still:

*Lior — picked up survivors off the Serrat shoals. Not storm victims. They say they were put off a company transport — one of the NEW companies, the reorganized ones, our friends with the kinder letterheads — put off in open boats for "manifest irregularities." Nineteen of them. Three died before we reached them. One of the survivors recognized me. She says there are more boats. She says they're calling it a correction of accounts.*

*Correction of accounts. Twelve years, and they're still using the same words. I'm bringing her to you. I'm bringing all of them. Get Odile. Get the school. And Lior — get your testimony voice ready. Because either we broke the company, or we taught it better manners, and I'm starting to think we only taught it to forge smaller handwriting.*

*I'm two hours out. Light the lamp on the point.*

*— W*

I read it twice. Then I folded it into my shirt, against my skin, the way you carry a thing you may need to produce as evidence.

"Brissa," I said. "Run to the school. Tell Odile Ferran that the lesson tonight is history, and history is arriving by sea, and she'll know what it means. Then run to the point and light the lamp. Can you do both?"

The girl was already moving. Fierce, unindentured, quick as a signal passed down a line of ropes. At the edge of the dock she stopped, turned, and made the circle over her heart — *remember* — not at me, I realized. Past me. Out at the water, at the orchards, at everyone who stayed down and everyone still afloat.

Then she ran, and I stood up, and my knees complained, and my lungs ached in their old borrowed way, and I looked out at the darkening harbor where a pilot boat was coming in under a yellow flag carrying nineteen reasons why the work is not finished.

"Well," I said to the water, to the town, to the dead who keep the count with me. "Back to it, then."

I went to find my testimony voice.
