## 8. Marta's Watch

Marta Kess had kept the observatory through three governments, two bombardments, and one winter so cold the mercury in the barometer climbed down out of its tube and lay sulking in the bulb like a small silver eel refusing to be read. She had learned, in those years, to measure the character of a person by the way they touched things.

The Commission officials who came through with their clipboards touched nothing. They pointed. They gestured at the dome, at the instruments, at the scorch marks along the east wall, as though the building were a diagram of itself. The soldiers who had garrisoned here during the occupation touched everything, but carelessly, the way a man pats a horse he intends to sell. And the few survivors who drifted in during the worst years—asking, always asking, if the clock still ran—had touched the doorframe on their way out, gently, the way you touch a shoulder at a funeral.

The new apprentice was a fourth thing entirely.

Marta watched him from the doorway of the instrument gallery on his third morning, ostensibly on her way to the coal scuttle, actually going nowhere at all. Edmund Thornbury—no. *Edmund Thorn, that was what he'd written on the work ledger. Edmund Thorn, apprentice clockmaker, no references, no history, paid in advance.* She watched him work.

He had good hands. That was not remarkable; the city was full of good hands since the war, hands that had been soldiers' hands and dockworkers' hands and, in one memorable case, a puppeteer's. But it was not the quality of the hands she was watching. It was the grammar of them.

He cleaned as he went. That was the first thing. Most repairmen cleaned after, sweeping the week's filings into a corner on Friday like sins deferred. This one brushed each brass shaving from the plate the moment it was cut, and he did it with the flat of his palm, in a single stroke, outward and away, the way a baker clears a board. Aldous Thornbury had cleared a board exactly like that. Marta knew because she had watched Aldous Thornbury do it for eleven years, standing in this same gallery, before the world decided his family's name was a crime and his clock was a monument to treason and his daughter—his child—was carried out through the tunnels and never spoken of again.

She was not a sentimental woman. Sentiment, in the occupation years, had been a luxury taxed at a rate no one could afford. But she felt something move in her chest, old and rusty, like a gear that had not turned since the winter the Regiment fell.

*Coincidence,* she told herself. *The city is full of men who sweep outward. It means nothing.*

Then, that afternoon, he wound the great clock.

* * *

The master clock stood in its stone chamber beneath the dome, a wall of wheels and weights and polished steel that had told this city the time for forty years and had, for the past seven, told it a small and stubborn lie: that it was five minutes slow, because Marta had never corrected the drift after the escapement was damaged, because correcting it felt like agreeing that something had broken that could not be mended. The Commission had ordered it stopped at the hour of surrender—*frozen at the official moment of capitulation, for historical purposes*—but Marta had pleaded mechanical necessity and been permitted to keep the train running, the pendulum swinging, the hour hand crawling its honest circle, as long as the chimes stayed silent. A stopped clock is a symbol. A running clock is only a clock. Symbols they would take from her. The clock itself, she had refused to give.

Edmund descended the spiral stair with the winding crank over his shoulder, and Marta followed at a distance, carrying a lantern she did not need, because the chamber's windows threw enough winter light to work by, and she wanted a reason to be there that would survive scrutiny.

She watched him fit the crank to the winding square.

There are a hundred ways to wind a clock. The clumsy way, with the shoulder and the back, all heave. The careful way, with the wrist, counting turns. The frightened way, fast, as though the clock might object. Marta had seen them all, from a hundred hired hands across forty years.

Aldous Thornbury had wound the master clock the way a man greets an old friend: two fingers and a thumb on the crank, elbow tucked, a rhythm of three-quarter turns with a pause at the top of each, not for rest but for listening—head tilted, ear toward the great wheel, checking by sound alone whether the ratchet was seating true. *You can hear a lie in a clock before you can see one,* he used to say. *The teeth tell you everything.*

Edmund wound with two fingers and a thumb, elbow tucked, three-quarter turns with a pause at the top of each, head tilted, ear toward the great wheel.

At the fourth turn he stopped. Frowned. Listened. Then he set down the crank, took a fine file from his apron, and dressed a burr off the click-spring that Marta had been meaning to dress for six years and had not, because dressing it meant opening the back plate, and opening the back plate meant the Commission's quarterly inspector would ask questions about why the plate was open, and Marta had grown tired, in her old age, of composing answers for men who had already decided what the answers should be.

He did it in four minutes. He did not announce it. He did not look for praise. He simply did the thing that needed doing, the way Aldous had done the thing that needed doing, and then he wound the rest of the train, and the great wheel turned with a sound like a long-held breath released, and Marta stood in the shadows with her unnecessary lantern and felt her eyes sting in a manner she attributed, firmly, to coal dust.

*Coincidence,* she told herself again, and the word had grown thin as paper.

* * *

That evening she took down the work ledger and read his entry. *E. Thorn. Escapement: replaced two pallets, dressed click-spring, oiled train. Chime mechanism: seized, requires parts. Est. two weeks.* The hand was small, upright, economical. Not Aldous's hand—Aldous had written like a man arguing with the page, all slant and pressure. This hand had been taught to take up no room. She had seen hands like that before, in the occupation, on documents written by people who did not wish to be remembered by what they wrote.

She turned back through the ledger, to the entries from before the war. *A. Thornbury. Cleaned dome track. Regulated sidereal clock. Note: the city bells run seven minutes fast and I have written to the mayor about it every year for nine years and I shall write again.* She had laughed, the first time she'd read that, a young assistant keeper then, and Aldous had glared at her over his spectacles and said, *It is not a joke, Marta. A city that cannot agree on the time cannot agree on anything.* She had not known then how prophetic it was. Nobody had.

She closed the ledger and sat a long while in the keeper's office with the lamp turned low.

If the boy was who she thought he was—and she was not yet willing to say the name even inside her own head, because names, in this city, had a way of becoming evidence—then he was here under a false one, doing honest work, asking nothing, wanting nothing but to be left alone. And the wisest thing, the safest thing, the thing any sensible keeper of anything would do, was to notice nothing, say nothing, and let the past stay buried where the war had put it.

But.

But the chime mechanism was seized, and the chime mechanism had been built by Aldous Thornbury, and the parts it required were not the sort of parts a man could buy in the market. They were the sort of parts a man might find in the observatory archive, in the maker's own drawers, in the cabinet at the back of the lower vault where Aldous had kept his spares and his patterns and his papers, and which Marta had kept locked for seven years, dusted monthly, and defended from three separate attempts at confiscation with a combination of bureaucratic obstinacy and a fire axe she kept visibly behind the door.

The boy would need that cabinet. And he would never ask for the key. That was the whole architecture of him—she could see it now, plain as a diagram. He would rather let the chimes stay silent forever than ask one question that might invite another. He had built himself into a man who did not ask, because asking was how you got noticed, and being noticed was how you got erased.

Marta had spent seven years keeping a clock running so that the city would have one honest thing left when everything else was rewritten. She had told herself that was the whole of her rebellion, and a small and sufficient one. But a clock that runs and never strikes is only half a clock. And a truth that is kept and never given is only half a truth.

She was seventy-one years old. She had buried a brother at Marsten Ford and a marriage in the first year of the occupation and her patience somewhere around the third requisition notice. She did not have time to be prudent anymore. Prudence was for people with more future.

* * *

She went down to the vault that night, after the boy had gone—after *Edmund* had gone, out into the blue snow-light with his collar up and his step quick, the step of a man who has somewhere to be that is nowhere in particular. She took the ring of keys from her belt and stood before the archive door a long moment, breath fogging in the cold stone.

The archive was the observatory's memory: charts, logbooks, the maker's drawings, the correspondence of forty years, and in the back, behind the fire axe and a stack of requisition forms she had never filed, the cabinet. She did not open it. She had not opened it since the fall. Opening it was a thing she would do once, and only once, and she knew in her bones that when she did, the world would have shifted around her like ice before the break.

Instead she took the key—the archive key, the heavy one, brass, with a worn shank that fit her hand the way a tool fits the hand that made it—and she carried it up the stairs, and she set it on the workbench in the gallery, in the exact center of Edmund's cleared space, where no hand could pass the morning without seeing it.

She set it down and looked at it.

It was, she reflected, a dangerous thing she was doing. Not for him—for her. If the Commission learned she had given archive access to an unregistered apprentice with no verifiable history, she would lose the observatory, and with it the last honest clock in the city, and with it the only thing she had ever been good at, which was keeping time for people who had forgotten what time was for. If the boy was a spy—some Commission plant testing her loyalty, some clever trap in an apron—then she had just handed the victors the keys to the one room in this city where their version of history could be contradicted.

She looked at the key on the bench, small and brass and absurd, like a coin left on a grave.

*Well,* she thought. *Either he's Aldous's blood, in which case he'll know what to do with it, or he isn't, in which case he'll bring it back to me and apologize, and I'll know that too, and it will have cost me nothing but a night's sleep.*

She was not a betting woman, but she had watched hands for forty years, and hands did not lie. Hands could not be coached. A man could forge a name, a ledger entry, a whole life; but no one had ever yet forged the way a Thornbury listened to a wheel.

She banked the stove, trimmed the lamp, and climbed to her rooms above the gallery. Through the frost-ferned window she could see the dome against the stars, and below it the dark bulk of the master clock, keeping its stubborn, slightly slow, entirely honest time.

"Five minutes," she said aloud to the empty room, which was a habit of hers and the closest she came to prayer. "Seven minutes fast, the bells. You'd have written to the mayor about it, Aldous. Every year. You'd have written again."

Below, in the gallery, the key sat on the workbench, catching the last of the lamplight.

And in the morning, when Edmund came down the stairs and stopped dead at the sight of it—when she heard, from the kitchen where she was deliberately making noise with a kettle, the long silence of a man looking at a thing he had not been offered and could not refuse—Marta kept her back turned, and stirred her tea, and waited to hear whether the floorboards would creak toward the archive stair, or toward the door, or toward her, with the key held out and an apology forming.

The floorboards creaked.

They creaked toward the archive.

Marta closed her eyes over her tea, and allowed herself, for the first time in seven years, the dangerous luxury of hope—and heard, beneath it, faint and clear as a struck bell, the sound of the archive door opening, and a voice—his voice, low, wondering, almost broken—say words that stopped her heart where she stood:

"Marta. What is my father's coat doing on this wall?"
