## 14. The Saboteur's Trace

There is a particular kind of silence that lives inside a stopped clock, and Edmund Thornbury had spent three weeks learning to hate it.

A running clock is never truly quiet. It breathes—a soft tick and a softer tock, the pendulum's whisper against the air, the faint creak of the great weight cables taking their slow turn through the night. The observatory's master clock, when it ran, filled the tower like a heartbeat fills a chest: unnoticed until it stops, and then unbearable in its absence.

Edmund sat cross-legged on the scaffolding platform beside the movement, sleeves rolled, a jeweler's loupe screwed into one eye, holding the escapement up to the light of a work lamp. Around them, the tower rose in its familiar geometry of brass and iron and dust. Below, through the floor grating, they could hear Marta sweeping the gallery with the methodical violence of a woman who believed dust was a personal insult.

"Thornbury!" her voice floated up. "You've been up there since dawn. Have you eaten?"

"I ate."

"You ate half a roll at seven o'clock. I heard it. A whole person makes more noise than that when they eat."

"That's an unusual metric for nutrition, Keeper."

"It's a reliable one." Her broom paused. "There's soup on the stove. If it goes cold, I'm blaming you personally, and I keep records."

Edmund smiled despite themselves—one of those small, involuntary smiles that had become, over these strange weeks, less rare than they used to be. "I'll come down when I've finished this pass."

They hadn't finished this pass. They weren't sure they wanted to finish this pass, because finishing it meant admitting what they were finding.

The sabotage had been obvious from the first day—obvious to anyone who knew what wear looked like versus what intent looked like. Wear was patient. Wear rounded corners, thinned pivots, left a fine gray sediment of sacrificed metal in the oil channels. What Edmund had found instead was a tooth filed off the escape wheel—not snapped, not worn, *filed*, with a smooth, deliberate stroke—and a locking face on the pallet stone ground just slightly out of true, so that the mechanism would run beautifully for hours and then bind, precisely, catastrophically, at a single predetermined moment.

Someone had engineered a failure. Someone had wanted the master clock of the city observatory to stop at one specific instant and never start again.

And now, on the third week, turning the escape wheel under the loupe, Edmund found the signature.

* * *

It was stamped on the inner face of the wheel's hub, where no one would ever look unless they disassembled the entire train—which, in fairness, almost no one ever did, because the entire train weighed as much as a horse and required two people and a block-and-tackle to lift. The stamp was small, a maker's mark pressed into the brass before machining: a circle, a set of scales, and beneath them a serial number beginning with the letters **CR**.

Edmund knew that mark. Every tradesperson in the city knew that mark. It hung, freshly painted and slightly too large, over the Commission workshops by the east gate—the Reconciliation Commission's own manufactory, where victors' carpenters built victors' furniture and victors' surveyors' instruments and, apparently, victors' sabotage.

CR. Commission of Reconciliation. Stamped into the heart of the clock, right beside the older mark that Edmund's thumb had been avoiding for three weeks—the one their father had cut by hand into every piece he ever made, a small rising sun over a gear tooth, worn nearly smooth now but still legible if you knew where to look. If you were a Thornbury.

Edmund lowered the loupe. Their hands, those famously steady hands, were not entirely steady.

"Well," they said aloud, to nobody, in the tone of a man discovering a rat in the flour bin. "Well, well, well."

The arithmetic assembled itself with the same terrible neatness as the escapement. The clock had been built to stop at the hour the city fell—the hour the Iron Regiment's last stand ended, the hour the surrender documents were signed in the customs house below. Everyone knew the official hour. It was in the histories already being printed downstream: *the guns fell silent at four minutes past six in the evening.* Children recited it. The Commission read it aloud at ceremonies.

But clocks don't stop themselves at convenient moments. And here, stamped in Commission brass, was proof that the moment had been manufactured—that someone had reached into the city's own heartbeat and pinned it at the number the historians preferred.

If the clock had run on—if it had struck seven, eight, nine while the fighting sputtered on in the riverside districts, while wounded men waited hours for surrender terms to reach them—then "four minutes past six" would have been not a fact but a choice. An ugly, inconvenient, human choice. So they had made the clock agree with the story. They had filed time itself into shape.

Edmund sat very still on the scaffolding, holding a piece of evidence that could hang somebody, and felt something hot and unfamiliar move through their chest.

It took them a moment to identify it, having spent seven years carefully cultivating nothing stronger than mild professional satisfaction.

It was rage.

* * *

They came down for the soup eventually, because Marta's threats about record-keeping were not idle, and because a body runs better on broth than on outrage. But they carried the escape wheel down with them wrapped in a rag, tucked inside their coat like contraband, which—in a sense—it was.

Marta watched them eat from across the kitchen table with the frank, unblinking scrutiny she applied to everything, including weather, politics, and other people's eating habits.

"You've got your thinking face on," she said.

"This is my eating face."

"No, your eating face is more grateful. This is the face you make when you've found something wrong in the works and you're deciding whether to say so." She refilled their bowl without asking. "I've watched apprentices for thirty years, Thornbury. You're transparent as a watch crystal."

"A watch crystal," Edmund said, "is actually quite difficult to see through at certain angles."

"Don't deflect with horology at me. I invented that trick." She sat back, arms folded. "Is it bad? The thing you found?"

Edmund considered the wrapped wheel in their coat, heavy against their ribs. Considered Marta—her swept floors, her kept keys, her seven years of keeping a dead clock dusted and oiled for no reason she'd ever explained. Considered the word *traitor*, which the Commission applied generously, like whitewash.

"The clock didn't fail," they said finally. "It was made to fail. Deliberately. By someone who wanted it to stop at exactly the hour the histories say the city surrendered."

Marta did not gasp. Marta was constitutionally incapable of gasping; surprise, in her, manifested as a slight narrowing of the eyes and a longer pause before speaking, as though she were filing the information somewhere deep and alphabetical.

"Hm," she said.

"*Hm?*"

"What did you expect me to say? I'm seventy-one years old, boy. I watched them raise new flags over the gate while the smoke was still coming off the river. Nothing about this city surprises me anymore except how good the bread's gotten since the war ended. Even sieges have silver linings." She leaned forward. "Tell me the rest."

So Edmund unwrapped the wheel and laid it on the scrubbed pine table between the bread board and the salt cellar, and turned it so the lamplight caught the stamp. Marta put on her spectacles, bent close, and read it without touching.

"Commission workshop," she said flatly. "They stamped their own name into it."

"They stamp everything. Pride or bureaucracy—I've never decided which."

"Both, usually. Bureaucratic pride." She straightened up and removed her spectacles, and for a long moment she looked at the little circle of scales as though it were a spider she was deciding whether to kill or simply evict. "Do you know what they'll say if you show that around? They'll say the clock was old. They'll say parts were replaced during repairs, standard practice, nothing sinister. They'll say whoever claims otherwise is a malcontent stirring up grievance. That's how it works now. The truth isn't false, it's merely *unhelpful*, and unhelpful things get filed where the sun doesn't reach."

"I know how it works now," Edmund said quietly. "I've been living in the sunless part of it for seven years."

Something flickered across Marta's face at that—something quick and knowing—but she let it pass, which was its own kind of message. Instead she said: "What will you do with it?"

That was the question, wasn't it. Edmund turned the wheel slowly on the tabletop, watching the scales catch the light and vanish and catch it again.

"If I give it to the Commission, it disappears. If I hide it, it protects no one but me. If I use it—" They stopped.

"If you use it, you have to be someone," Marta said. Not unkindly. Just accurately, the way she was accurate about most things, including soup consumption.

"Yes," Edmund said. "That's rather the problem."

* * *

They should have gone home after that. It was past dark, the river wind was coming up raw off the ice, and there was nothing more to learn from a lamp and a loupe that night. Instead Edmund climbed back up the tower, because there was one more thing nagging at them, one loose thread in the mechanism's history that wouldn't stop ticking in the back of their mind.

The maintenance log.

Every serious installation kept one—the observatory's went back forty years, a shelf of fat ledgers recording every cleaning, every adjustment, every replaced part, in the handwriting of every keeper who had ever served. Edmund had consulted it in week one, casually, professionally. Now they pulled the volume covering the war years down from the shelf and carried it to the work lamp, and read it properly this time, the way one reads a confession.

Most of it was routine. *Cleaned train. Oiled pivots. Replaced weight cable.* Marta's hand, steady and economical, marching through the occupation years like a woman walking a picket line. Then, dated eleven days before the fall:

*Escapement serviced. New escape wheel fitted, source: Commission supply requisition 4471. Old wheel returned per directive.*

Edmund stared at the entry for a long time.

Eleven days. The Commission's own part, installed by the Commission's own requisition, eleven days before the city fell—before anyone could have known the exact hour anything would happen, unless someone already knew how the ending was meant to go. Unless the ending had been drafted in advance, like a playbill, and the clock was simply being rehearsed for its role.

And *old wheel returned per directive*. Returned where? To a warehouse? To a furnace? Or into a drawer somewhere, preserved with bureaucratic thoroughness by some clerk who followed instructions without ever asking why the Commission wanted defective clock parts catalogued?

Edmund closed the ledger with a snap that echoed in the tower.

They understood now—or thought they did, or feared they did—what they were standing inside of. Not a broken clock. A forged document. The largest one in the city, tall as a steeple, made of brass and iron instead of ink, and signed, in its way, with a stamp and a serial number. The histories being barged downstream in spring weren't just incomplete. They were *manufactured*, start to finish, and the machine that had manufactured them was sitting here in the dark above the frozen river, stopped at the exact minute that made the lie hold together.

Their father had built this clock to tell the truth. That was the whole creed of the man's life, hammered into his children along with his trade: *a clock that lies is worse than no clock at all, because people believe it.* And now his clock stood in the dark, gagged, wearing a Commission stamp on its tongue.

Edmund pressed their palm flat against the cold case of the great movement, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal, or a grave.

"I'm going to fix this," they told it. "Properly. All of it."

The clock, being a clock, said nothing. But somewhere below, the pendulum hung plumb and patient in its long arc of stillness, waiting—everything in this tower, Edmund realized, had been waiting for years—for someone to come along who knew the difference between what was broken and what was broken *on purpose*.

* * *

They walked home late through streets glazed with refrozen slush, the escape wheel riding in their coat like a second heartbeat. At the corner of Chandler's Lane they passed a Commission notice board, freshly pasted, announcing the schedule for the spring ceremonies: the sealing of the register, the reading of the official history, the barges that would carry the approved memory of the war downriver to the capital. Four minutes past six, the notice said, would be observed with a moment of silence.

*A moment of silence,* Edmund thought bitterly. *We've had three years of silence. It hasn't improved anything.*

They were still composing better versions of that joke when they saw the light burning in the census office window—late hours, a lone figure bent over a desk, and even at this distance, even through frosted glass, there was no mistaking the posture. Straight-backed, unhurried, precise. Adeline Voss, working long after honest people had gone home, doing whatever it was census takers did with their evenings.

Filling in gaps, probably. Chasing discrepancies. Writing down names, and noticing which names didn't fit.

Edmond drew back into the shadow of the chandler's awning, heart tapping out a quick irregular beat that no escapement would have certified. Because here was the trouble with what they'd found upstairs—here was the flaw in the beautiful, terrible plan forming in their mind. Evidence needed witnesses. Testimony needed credibility. And the one person in this city whose whole profession was noticing things, whose ledger was already full of Edmund's small inconsistencies, whose faith in accurate memory seemed genuine enough to be dangerous—

That person worked for the Commission. The same Commission whose scales were stamped into the sabotaged wheel in Edmund's coat.

Through the glass, Adeline Voss turned a page, dipped her pen, and wrote something down.

Edmund stood in the cold a moment longer, watching, and understood that the next move in this game was no longer theirs alone to choose. Somewhere in that lit window, in that tidy ledger, their name was either going to be erased—or written down at last, in ink that might outlast them all.

They just had no way yet of knowing which.
