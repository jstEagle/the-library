## 15. Adeline's Own File

The Commission on Reconciliation kept its offices in the old customs house, which was fitting, Adeline thought, because the Commission's whole business was weighing what came into the city and what went out of it—and lately the cargo was memory.

Her desk stood by a window that looked down on the frozen river. She had chosen it herself, in the first week, when desks were still being assigned by seniority and nobody had seniority but the clerks who'd survived the occupation indoors. From that window she could watch the ice barges and the smoke from the chandler's chimneys and, on clear mornings, the dome of the observatory catching light like a coin at the bottom of a well. It steadied her, that view. Numbers were easier to love when you could see the people they stood for.

She had three hundred and eleven files on her desk. She knew because she had counted them twice, once for accuracy and once because counting things calmed her the way prayer calmed other people.

The file she opened first, every morning, was not one of the three hundred and eleven. It was a thin folder she kept in the bottom drawer, beneath a spare inkwell and a tin of peppermints she never ate. Inside were two pages: the service records of Corporal Tomas Voss and Private Ilya Voss, killed within four months of each other on opposite sides of the same river, which was the sort of arithmetic the war had specialized in. Tomas had been twenty-two. Ilya had been nineteen and had lied about his age to get in, so officially he was twenty-one forever, which Adeline had always thought was the cruelest clerical error she'd ever seen—except that she now made errors like it for a living, or rather, she prevented them, which was the same work done from the other side of the desk.

Accurate memory honors the dead. Her mother had said something like that once, though her mother had said most things through a closed door in those years, and Adeline might have improved the phrasing in the retelling. Still. The sentiment held. If you wrote down what actually happened—who lived, who died, who signed what and when—the dead got to keep their names. That was the whole job. That was the sacred part of it, buried under the forms and the carbon paper and the smell of the Commission's terrible coffee.

So when Supervisor Grell appeared at her desk that morning with his coat already buttoned for outdoors and his expression already arranged for delivering bad news gently, Adeline felt the first cold thread of dread go through her, because Grell only delivered bad news gently when the bad news was also an instruction.

"Voss." He set a folder on top of her three hundred and eleven. "A moment."

"I have several hundred moments," Adeline said. "They're all spoken for, but I can lend you one."

Grell did not smile. Grell had been a customs man before the war too, and the war had taken from him whatever small muscle it is that operates smiles. He lowered his voice anyway, which was worse than smiling would have been.

"The register seals at the ice-break. You know that."

"Six weeks. Everyone knows that. There's a poster."

"There are posters," Grell agreed. "Which means there will be questions. Which means the entries we close now need to be closed *cleanly*. No open threads. No discrepancies left dangling for some historian to trip over in ten years." He tapped the new folder. "These are flagged. Inconvenient entries. Incomplete documentation, contradictory testimony, matters better resolved administratively than publicly. You'll mark them *closed—insufficient verification* and move them to the sealed annex."

Adeline looked at the folder without touching it. "Closed how? These people exist. They're in my notes. Some of them I've interviewed myself."

"Some of them," Grell said, "are in your notes. Whether they exist is precisely the question, isn't it?" And he said it kindly, which was the trick of him; he made erasure sound like epistemology. "We're building a history, Voss. A history that holds together. A loose stone brings down the arch."

"A loose stone," Adeline said, "is how you know where the arch is weak."

Grell regarded her for a long moment. Somewhere behind them a clerk dropped a stack of forms and swore with real feeling, and the ordinary noise of the office rushed back in around them like water filling a hole.

"You lost brothers," Grell said quietly. It wasn't a question; it was in her file, of course it was in her file, everything was in someone's file. "I'm not asking you to forget them. I'm asking you to give the city a record their grandchildren can read without a lawyer." He straightened his coat. "The flagged ones first. Before Friday."

He left. Adeline sat with the folder under her hand and did not open it for a full minute, which for Adeline was roughly equivalent to a sit-down strike.

Then she opened it.

* * *

There were eleven entries inside. She read them all before lunch, because she was not the sort of person who could leave a drawer half-open, and by the time the bells across the river rang noon—wrongly, as they always did now, since the master clock had stopped and every smaller clock in the city had drifted off into its own private opinion of time—she had sorted them into three piles.

Pile one: genuinely nothing. Two families whose paperwork had burned with the parish house, whose existence could be verified by any neighbor in ten minutes. Closing those was merely lazy, and laziness she could live with, or at least outlive.

Pile two: uncomfortable but survivable. A merchant who had sold grain to both armies and wished only one of those transactions remembered. A midwife who had delivered babies on both sides of the wall and whose ledger, if published, would embarrass exactly the sort of respectable people who funded the Commission. Adeline marked these *closed* with a sour taste and told herself the midwife probably preferred obscurity anyway. She did not quite believe it. She wrote it down anyway, in pencil, in the margin, where pencil could be erased: *prefers? verify.*

Pile three contained one entry.

It wasn't Edmund Thornbury's—not yet. It was the apprentice clockmaker, E. Thornbury, trade listed, address listed, arrived-in-city date listed as 1864. The discrepancy was hers; she'd found it herself, weeks ago, in the gallery of the observatory over Marta's excellent tea, and she had gone home that night feeling the pleasant itch of a puzzle well-spotted. An apprentice who knew the pre-war bell schedule. An apprentice whose name appeared on no roll she could find, no parish book, no debt ledger, no regimental list—which itself was odd, because the Commission's standing instruction was to note military service of any kind, and nearly every man in this city had some, even if it was only carrying stretchers.

She had written *follow up* in the margin. She had meant it the way she always meant it: accurately. Find the truth, write the truth, let the truth be whatever it turned out to be. If the man was a deserter, write deserter. If he was a liar, write liar. If he was something stranger—a ghost, a runaway, a person who had survived by becoming no one—then write that, in the space provided, in triplicate.

But now the folder sat on her desk with Grell's flag clipped to it, and the flag said *inconvenient*, and Adeline understood with a clarity that felt like falling that inconvenient did not mean difficult. Inconvenient meant *someone upstairs has decided what this entry should say, and the truth is not it.*

She took out the file again and laid it flat. E. Thornbury. She had met him twice now. Once professionally, in the observatory gallery, where he had answered her questions with the careful, rehearsed smoothness of a schoolboy reciting a lesson he didn't believe. And once less professionally, when she'd come back with follow-ups and caught him with his sleeves rolled and a gear train spread across a cloth like surgery, talking about escapements with such unguarded delight that for a quarter of an hour she'd forgotten to take notes. He had hands that moved like they'd been doing this work since before he could walk. He had flinched—actually flinched—when she'd asked about his family.

People flinched for many reasons. She had catalogued most of them by now. Grief. Shame. Boredom. But there was a particular flinch, rare and unmistakable, that belonged to people carrying a name too heavy to say aloud. She'd seen it once before, years ago, on a woman in the refugee lines who had given her name as Anna Smith, Anna Smith, Anna Smith, three times, until Adeline stopped writing and just looked at her, and the woman had put one finger to her lips.

Adeline had written Anna Smith. Some entries you close to protect the living. She had believed, then, that this too was accurate memory—accurate to what the moment required.

She was no longer sure the Commission agreed with her about what moments required.

* * *

In the afternoon, Grell's clerk brought around the sealing schedule and a fresh stack of *closed—insufficient verification* stamps, red rubber, brand new, smelling of ink and finality. Adeline stamped pile one. She stamped pile two, after a fight with herself that lasted the length of one cup of coffee and ended in a draw. Then she reached pile three, and the stamp hovered, and she set it down.

"Not today," she said aloud, to no one, and was embarrassed, and then decided she didn't care. The office was full of people talking to themselves these days. Half the city talked to itself. It was practically civic participation.

Instead she did what she always did when she couldn't decide: she gathered more information, on the theory that facts were ballast and would steady her. She pulled the workshop records for the Commission's own engineering division—routine diligence, she told herself, cross-referencing trades—and spent two hours reading requisition forms, and it was there, between a request for brass stock and an invoice for lamp oil, that she saw the mark. A small stamp, a compass rose inside a gear, on a work order dated eleven days before the city fell. *One escapement gear, modified to specification. Return original to stores.*

Modified to specification. Whose specification? She read the order four times. There was no signature above a certain rank. There was a reference number, and when she looked up the reference number in the index, the index card was missing. Not misfiled—missing, the gap in the sequence neat as a pulled tooth.

Adeline sat back in her chair and looked out the window at the river, white and silent and holding its breath until spring.

She had taken this job because she believed a count was a kind of candle. You lit it for the dead; you said, *you were here, you were this many, you mattered in units.* Her brothers were numbers in her bottom drawer, and she visited them every morning, and the numbers were true, and the truth was the only grave marker anyone had given her that couldn't be kicked over.

And now her own employer was pulling teeth out of indexes and handing her a red stamp and calling it reconciliation.

She thought of the clockmaker's hands. She thought of the flinch. She thought of the missing card, and of Grell saying *a loose stone brings down the arch*, and she thought—suddenly, unwelcome, with total conviction—that perhaps the arch was supposed to come down. Perhaps it had been built on the wrong foundation entirely, on the hour somebody chose instead of the hour that happened, and every clean entry she stamped was a bucket of mortar.

The bells rang three o'clock, wrong, as usual. Nobody in the office looked up. Everyone had gotten used to time being broken. That was the thing about people, Adeline thought, gathering her coat and the Thornbury file and slipping it into her satchel rather than the drawer, where it would be safe, where it would be hidden, where it would be hers. That was the thing about people: they got used to anything, if you broke it slowly enough and called the breaking peace.

She walked home along the river road. The ice groaned somewhere out in the dark, a long low sound like a door being tested, and she stopped to listen, hands in her pockets, breath steaming.

Tomorrow, she told herself, she would decide. Tomorrow she would look at the file with morning eyes and do the correct administrative thing, whatever that was. She was a census taker. She counted. She did not take sides; sides were not countable.

But her hand, she noticed, stayed resting on the satchel flap the whole way home, the way you rest a hand on something you intend to keep.

And in the morning, when she reached her desk, the first thing she did—before the coffee, before the three hundred and eleven, before the red stamp—was open the bottom drawer and check that the two pages with her brothers' names were still there. They were. She read them, as always. *Corporal Tomas Voss. Private Ilya Voss, age recorded as twenty-one.*

Then she took out a fresh sheet of paper, and at the top of it, in her neatest hand, she wrote: *E. Thornbury — outstanding questions.*

Underneath, she wrote the first question. It was not the question Grell would have asked, or the Commission, or the tidy historians waiting downstream for their sealed register.

It was simply: *Who are you, really?*

She underlined it twice, put the paper in her satchel next to the file, and went to find out—starting, she decided, with a visit to the observatory, and a very honest conversation with the keeper, Marta, who poured tea like a woman guarding something and watched strangers like a woman who had been waiting for one in particular.
