## 23. The Commission Closes In

The Commission on Reconciliation occupied the old customs house, which was fitting, Adeline thought, because nothing that passed through its doors ever left without being taxed in some way—usually of the truth.

Her office was a converted counting room on the third floor, with a window overlooking the river and a stove that heated exactly one corner of the room and no more. Adeline had learned to do her paperwork standing at the window in winter, one shoulder toward the warmth like a woman leaning against a friend who talked too much.

On this particular gray morning, she was copying figures from her ledger into the master register when Supervisor Hennig appeared in her doorway.

Hennig was a large man built along the lines of a wardrobe, and he moved through the Commission's corridors the way wardrobes move—slowly, inevitably, and with the certainty that rooms existed to contain him. He carried Brann's rumor file in one hand as though it were something he'd found on his boot.

"Voss," he said. "You flagged an entry. Census subject, clockmaker's apprentice, observatory district."

Adeline did not let her pen stop moving. This was a skill she had perfected over two years: writing numbers while every other part of her went very still. "I flag everything inconsistent, sir. It's the mandate."

"Mm." He came into the room without being asked, which was also how wardrobes worked. He dropped the file on her desk. "The Thornbury business."

The pen did stop then. Adeline made herself look up with the mild, slightly bored expression of a functionary who has heard a hundred names and cared about none of them. "The Regiment family? That's ancient history, sir. The register treats them as closed entries."

"The register treats them as *convenient* entries." Hennig lowered himself into her visitor's chair, which groaned. "There's a witness now. A sergeant—Brann, formerly of the garrison—claims a Thornbury child survived the fall of the tower. Claims he saw it himself. Smuggled out through the tunnels."

"And you believe a drunk in the Salt Cellar tavern?"

"I believe what I can file." Hennig tapped the folder. "Here's what troubles me. Your own notes—very thorough, Voss, I'll grant you that, sometimes I think you'd count the stones in your breakfast porridge—your notes say this apprentice arrived in the city in 1864. No regimental roll. No parish record before that year. No debt ledger, no guild registration, no militia exemption. Nothing. A man with no paper trail is either dead or hiding, and this one is clearly neither."

"He repairs clocks," Adeline said. "Perhaps paper trails are beneath him."

Hennig smiled. It was not a warm smile; it was the smile of a man who has read many files and learned that everyone lies about something, usually something small, and the small things are where you pull. "Your flagged entry crossed my desk because of the Brann statement. The dates align. The child would be—" he consulted the folder, though Adeline suspected he knew by heart— "twenty-one now. The apprentice claims to be twenty-three. Close enough for a man who shaves badly and keeps to himself."

Adeline felt the floor tilt very slightly, the way it did when the river ice shifted, though the river was three streets away and frozen solid.

"What is it you want from me, sir?"

"An order of detention." He produced a printed form and laid it beside her ledger with the care of a man setting down a chess piece. "Standard questioning. After the register seals, of course—we can't have open files wandering around during the ceremony. The ice breaks, the barges go downstream, the history goes with them, tidy and finished. Then we collect this apprentice and ask our questions at leisure."

"After the seals," Adeline repeated.

"After the seals." He stood, wardrobe-like, and paused at the door. "You've done good work on this one, Voss. Thorough. The Commissioner likes thorough people. They make the record clean." He said the word *clean* the way some men said *God-fearing*—with an appetite that had nothing to do with cleanliness.

He left. Adeline sat looking at the form.

It was, she noted distantly, a well-printed document. Good heavy stock. The Commission spared no expense on the forms by which it took people's freedom; only the truth got the cheap paper.

* * *

She should have filed it immediately. That was the procedure: supervisor issues order, census taker enters it in the day book, constables execute after the sealing. Three lines of ink and done.

Instead Adeline walked to the window and watched the river.

The ice was beginning to change color—that subtle shift from white to a bruised blue-gray that meant the water underneath had started talking again. Six weeks ago it had been silent, a road of death. Now, if you listened at the right hour, you could hear it muttering to itself, cracks propagating somewhere below like rumors through a crowded room.

Days, the ferrymen said. Maybe a week. The old-timers could taste the thaw in the wind off the water.

She looked down at the detention order. Edmund Thornbury—or whatever the name truly was—had been detained once already, in a sense, for seven years, by a father's dying instruction and their own good sense. And now the Commission proposed to detain them properly, after the history hardened, when any testimony they gave would be the testimony of a liar contradicting an official record. That was how it worked. First you sealed the book, then you arrested anyone who tried to add a page.

Adeline had lost two brothers to the war. Tomas at Kessel Ford, and little Pieter—not so little by the end, seventeen and taller than her—in the retreat nobody official admitted had happened. She had taken this job because she believed, with a ferocity that surprised even her, that accurate memory was the only monument worth building. Names spelled right. Dates honest. The dead deserved at least that much arithmetic.

And here was the Commission, preparing to seal a register with a living person written out of it, and to arrest that person for the crime of having been written out.

"It's not my decision," she said aloud, to the river.

The river, being a river, declined to reassure her.

* * *

At midday she went down to the records annex, ostensibly to cross-reference the 1864 immigration rolls—a task so dull it was practically camouflage—and pulled the Brann file while she was there.

Sergeant Odo Brann. Garrison infantry, mustered out 1865, no pension, no trade registered, three citations for public drunkenness and one for brawling. His statement, taken by a junior clerk named Pell, ran half a page and read like a man dictating through a hangover:

*Saw the Thornbury boy come out of the tower tunnel the night it fell. Aldous himself put him over the wall. Paid me to look elsewhere and I looked. Boy was fourteen, maybe. Skinny. Had his mother's chin.*

Adeline read it twice. *Had his mother's chin.*

She thought of the apprentice at the observatory, bent over the escapement with those careful hands, answering her questions with the smoothness of rehearsed testimony and the eyes of someone listening for footsteps behind every answer. She thought of the tea Marta had poured, and how the keeper had watched Edmund wind the regulator key—with what Adeline had taken, at the time, for mere professional scrutiny.

*Had his mother's chin.* Adeline had seen a portrait of Aldous Thornbury once, in the pre-war almanac, back when the Regiment's founder had been a hero instead of a traitor. Strong jaw. Dark hair going gray at the temples. And the apprentice, when she looked past the shabby coat and the deliberately forgettable face—

"Oh," she said quietly, to the annex, full of sleeping paper.

Pell, the junior clerk, looked up from his desk. "Something, ma'am?"

"Just remembering a date." She smiled her functionary smile. "They slip away from you."

But they didn't, she thought, climbing back upstairs. That was the whole point of her. Dates didn't slip away from Adeline Voss. Dates stayed where she put them, and she had just put several together, and the shape they made was a person she'd been interviewing for weeks under false pretenses—hers, not his.

*His,* she corrected herself, and then, honestly: *theirs. Possibly. I don't actually know, and that is precisely the problem.*

* * *

The trouble with conscience, Adeline reflected that evening over a supper of bread and hard cheese in her cold rooms, was that it never arrived all at once, like an army. It arrived piecemeal, like laundry. One sock at a time until suddenly you couldn't deny the basket was full.

Sock one: the discrepancies in Edmund's story, which she had noticed in week one and chosen, professionally, to keep chasing rather than close.

Sock two: the sabotage evidence—the filed gear, the Commission workshop stamp—which she had copied and told herself it was archival diligence, and which sat now in her mattress ticking next to the copies of Edmund's file, whispering to her at night like a guilty roommate.

Sock three: the supervisor's order, sitting unfiled in her satchel, due in the day book by morning.

And now the whole basket was about to tip, because the register sealed when the ice broke, and the ice was breaking soon, and once it broke, Edmund Thornbury would pass from *inconsistency* to *criminal*, and Adeline Voss would have helped do it.

She thought about what her brothers would have said. Tomas would have said follow the rules, rules kept men alive. Pieter—who had died because somebody followed orders about a retreat that officially hadn't happened—would have said something sharper, and probably funnier, and then gone and done the reckless thing anyway.

"You're talking to dead boys," she told herself. "That's either grief or madness, and either way the cheese isn't helping."

She got out her pen and the day book.

* * *

What she wrote, in the end, was this: *Order received. Entered pending verification of subject's current residence.* Which was true—it was pending, she had not verified anything, and she did not intend to verify anything until she had decided what verification was *for*.

It bought her days. Not weeks. Days.

She blew out the lamp and lay in the dark listening to the city. Somewhere far off, faint as a pulse under a bandage, she could hear the observatory bell—not striking, the master clock still stood stopped at the fall-of-the-city hour, but the wind moving through the tower made a low note sometimes, like an instrument remembering a song.

Days, not weeks.

She got up, relit the lamp, and did the thing she had been not-doing for three nights running: she took out the copies, spread them across the bed, and began, methodically, with a census taker's love of completeness, to build a second file—one the Commission would never see. Discrepancies. Evidence. The Brann statement. And at the top of the first page, in her small exact hand, a heading she had not permitted herself until now:

*Thornbury, Edmund. Status: alive. Verification: mine to determine.*

Underneath, after a moment, she added: *Note to self — accuracy is not the same as obedience. Decide which one you serve before the ice goes out.*

Outside, very faintly, the river muttered. Something vast was shifting its weight, getting ready to move.

So was she.

* * *

Across the city, in a rented room above a chandler's shop that smelled of tallow and secrets, Edmund Thornbury sat polishing the last of their father's tools and did not know yet that the net had been drawn tight enough to see the knots.

They knew only that the ice was groaning, that the deposition waited with Okafor, that Marta's sworn pages were done, and that time—which they had spent seven years learning to repair for other people—was finally running out for themselves.

The chandler's dog lifted its head at the window, ears pricked toward the river, toward the sound of the world cracking open.

Spring was coming for everybody.
