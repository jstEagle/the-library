## 31. Adeline Crosses

Edmund did not go home after the raid.

They told themselves it was caution—Okafor's lodging would be watched, the chandler's shop staked out like a heron over a fishpond—but the truth was simpler and worse. They could not bear to sit alone in their rented room with the silence where a certified deposition used to be. So they walked. Up one embankment and down another, past the customs house where victors' banners hung stiff in the cold, past the bakery that had reopened its ovens because bread was one of the few things the war had not managed to make political. The river moved beneath the ice, dark and patient, and Edmund walked until their feet ached and the lamps were lit and there was nowhere left to walk to.

Which is how they came to be standing at their own door when Adeline Voss knocked on it.

She stood on the landing with her ledger under one arm and a satchel under the other, hat slightly askew, breath fogging in the stairwell light. She looked, for the first time since Edmund had known her, less like an instrument of the Commission and more like a woman who had run the last three streets.

"Mr. Thornbury," she said. "Or should I say—" She stopped herself. Glanced at the empty stairwell. "May I come in? It's rather important, and I'd rather not conduct it on your stairs."

Every instinct Edmund had spent seven years sharpening said *no*. Say you're busy. Say the room is cold. Say anything that closes the door gently and keeps the world on the far side of it.

But Okafor was in custody. Marta was in custody. And here was the Commission's own census taker, standing in the cold with a satchel held the way one holds something breakable.

"You'd better," Edmund said, and opened the door.

* * *

The room was small and honest about it: a bed, a stove, a table scarred by previous tenants, a window that looked out on a wall and, if one leaned, a sliver of the frozen river. Adeline took off her gloves finger by finger and warmed her hands at the stove without being asked, which Edmund found either rude or reassuring and couldn't decide which.

"I was there tonight," she said. "At the chandler's. Not making the arrest—I'm never asked to make arrests, I only count things—but present. Taking inventory." Her voice had the flat carefulness of someone reading from a document she hated. "They seized everything. The deposition pages, Okafor's certification book, his private correspondence, a letter he was writing to his sister in Marlow that had nothing to do with anything but they took it anyway because taking things is easier than sorting them."

Edmund sat down slowly on the edge of the bed. "So it's gone."

"The original pages are gone." Adeline turned from the stove. "What isn't gone is this." She set the satchel on the table and opened it, and began, with the methodical care Edmund had watched her bring to every census entry, to lay papers out in rows. "Page one, certified in Okafor's hand, dated the fourteenth. Page two. Page three. Pages four through nine. My own notes on the filed gear—the Commission workshop stamp, the measurements, the date the part was ordered, which predates the surrender by eleven days, in case anyone asks why a clock needed sabotaging before there was a battle to lose."

Edmund stared at the papers as though they might dissolve. "Those are copies."

"Exact copies. I have excellent handwriting; it's the one vanity they never managed to train out of me." She smoothed the top page flat. "I copied them the night you made your deposition. All of them. And again two days later, because I wanted to be certain I hadn't made errors, and then I told myself I was being diligent, and then I stopped telling myself anything at all and simply kept them somewhere my supervisor doesn't know exists."

"Why?" It came out rougher than Edmund intended. "Why would you risk—"

"Because they raided a notary's rooms at midnight for the crime of writing down what a man said." Adeline's composure cracked—not dramatically, but visibly, like ice showing a seam. "Because I have spent three years believing that accurate memory honors the dead, and tonight I watched men carry accuracy out of a building in an evidence box so that it could be buried in a basement where no one would ever read it. And because—" She stopped. Drew a breath. Started again from a different direction, the way she did when a witness gave an answer that didn't match her notes.

"My brothers died in the third year of the war. Both of them. Tomas at Harrow Ford, and Pieter six months later at a place so small it doesn't appear on most maps, which I have always thought was its own kind of obscenity." She said it evenly, the way one recites figures. "When the Commission offered me work counting survivors—for the official history, so that no one would be forgotten—I wept with gratitude. I actually wept. I thought: *this is how I keep them.* Every name entered correctly, every record true, and somewhere in the great ledger of the country my brothers will live because I helped build it."

"And now?"

"Now I've learned that the ledger has a back room." She touched the papers on the table, lightly, as if they were sleeping animals. "Entries get closed. Inconvenient people become unverifiable. A clock gets broken so the hour of surrender can be whatever the Commission says it was. And I am expected to initial all of it, because I am thorough, and thoroughness makes a fine rubber stamp."

The stove ticked. Somewhere below, the chandler's dog barked twice at nothing and gave up.

"They'll come for me next," Edmund said quietly. "You know that. Brann talked, or someone did, and the supervisor's order will come down after the register seals. If you're seen delivering—"

"I wasn't seen. I counted myself out of the building past two guards who were more interested in the confiscated brandy than in a census taker leaving late." A ghost of her usual briskness returned. "But yes. I know what comes next for you. Which is why I'm here tonight instead of tomorrow, when it might be too late to matter."

She drew out one last paper—not a copy, but a fresh sheet in her own hand—and laid it beside the deposition pages. Edmund read it upside down before she turned it around. It was a sworn statement. *I, Adeline Voss, census taker, First District, do attest...*

"You can't," Edmund said. "Your position, your pension, your—"

"My mandate," she interrupted, and here her voice changed entirely—all the clerkly flatness burned away, leaving something older and steadier underneath. "Do you know what my actual mandate is, Mr. Thornbury? Not the Commission's version. The real one, the one I wrote for myself the day I signed on. Three words' worth of job description: *I count the living.* That's the whole of it. That's what the census is, underneath the forms and the seals and the supervisors—someone going door to door saying *you survived, you are real, you will be written down.*"

She put her hand flat on the deposition copies.

"You're alive. That's my whole mandate. Everything else—the register, the ice-break ceremony, the tidy narrative—they built that on top. And when the thing built on top starts crushing the thing underneath, a person has to choose which one she serves." She met Edmund's eyes. "I've chosen. I'm testifying. At the reading, if we get that far; in front of a tribunal, if it comes to that; in front of God and the Commission both, if they insist."

Edmund found they couldn't speak for a moment. Seven years of practicing smallness, of being a man to whom nothing happened and nothing attached, and here was a woman laying down her livelihood on a scarred table between a bed and a stove, as casually as if she were setting out tea things.

"There's a word for what you're doing," Edmund managed at last.

"Treason," Adeline agreed pleasantly. "I looked it up this afternoon, while I was waiting for the guards to finish their brandy inventory. The penalty section is quite detailed. I read it twice, which I suppose tells you something about how long I've been deciding." She tucked a loose strand of hair back under her hat. "It also occurs to me that if the Commission's history is lawful and mine is treason, then the law and the truth have parted company, and I was raised to follow the second one. My mother was very firm about it. She'd have boxed my ears otherwise."

Despite everything—a raid, a seizure, a friend in custody, six days until the ice broke—Edmund laughed. It startled them both. It sounded rusty, like a mechanism left unwound too long, and it kept going longer than laughter strictly required, until Edmund had to press a hand over their mouth and Adeline was smiling in a tight, surprised way, as though she hadn't intended to and was annoyed to find she had.

"I'm sorry," Edmund said, recovering. "It's only—you sound like my father. He had exactly that tone. *The law and the truth have parted company,* he'd say, and then he'd go and do something brave and inconvenient and we'd all pay for it later." The laugh faded into something softer. "He'd have liked you."

"He sounds like a man worth liking." Adeline gathered the papers back into their careful stack, then paused. "There's one more thing. Two, actually, and then I'll let you sleep, if sleep is a thing you still do."

"Go on."

"First: Marta. I sat outside her questioning room for an hour today—officially I was cross-checking district rolls, unofficially I was eavesdropping, and I've stopped pretending I don't do that. They have nothing on her. Nothing provable. The archive shows no forced entry, the charter is accounted for, and Marta has spent two days giving them answers so technically complete and so profoundly unhelpful that I genuinely believe one examiner asked to be reassigned." A flicker of admiration crossed her face. "She's protecting you. She hasn't given them a single thread to pull. But they're patient, and patience is the one weapon the Commission manufactures domestically, so we shouldn't count on it lasting."

"And the second?"

Adeline hesitated. For the first time all evening she looked uncertain—not of her choice, Edmund guessed, but of how it would land.

"The supervisor's order came through this evening. Detainment, post-sealing. Your name was in it—or rather, the name they think is yours, the apprentice name, which means they're close enough to suspect but not close enough to prove. Six days. Maybe five, if the weather turns warm." She met Edmund's eyes. "You need more than testimony and copies, Thornbury. You need the originals. The charter. The brass plate. Things that predate the Commission, that no one can call forgery or rumor. Physical proof with age in its bones."

"I know where those are," Edmund said slowly.

"Do you?"

"The archive. And—" Edmund's hand went, without thinking, to their breast pocket, where a small rectangular weight had lived for weeks now, worn smooth as a river stone. "And closer than that."

Adeline nodded, unsurprised. Of course she'd deduced the plate; she'd probably deduced it in the first week, along with half a dozen other things she'd had the grace never to write down. "Then we have a problem, because the observatory is being watched now too. Two men on the night rotation, changed at dawn. I checked."

"Then we go under it."

Adeline blinked. "Under it?"

Edmund stood, and something in them straightened that had been bent for seven years—not the back, but something behind the back. "There's a tunnel beneath the tower. My father showed it to me when I was small enough to think it was a game. It runs from the clock room down through the old foundation to the river gate. It's how—" Their voice caught, just once. "It's how I left, the night everything fell. No one else knows it's there. Not even Marta, I think. Or if she does, she's been polite enough never to mention it."

"A secret passage," Adeline said, in the tone of a woman updating a ledger entry she'd already begun drafting in her head. "Of course there's a secret passage. Why would there not be a secret passage. This city runs on them."

"It runs on clocks," Edmund said. "The passages are just how we keep them honest."

Outside, the wind shifted, and from the direction of the river came a sound—faint, distant, unmistakable. A groan of ice, deep and structural, like a giant turning over in bed. Both of them went still and listened to it fade.

"Sooner than six days," Adeline said quietly.

Edmund looked at the stack of copied pages on the table—at the self restored in ink, carried out of a raiding party's hands by a woman who had chosen treason over tidiness—and felt, for the first time since Okafor's lamp had gone dark, something that was almost hope.

"Then we'd best not waste the thaw," they said. "How are you at crawling through tunnels in the dark?"

"I count steps, Mr. Thornbury. I count everything." Adeline picked up her hat and set it properly straight at last. "Tell me when we start."
