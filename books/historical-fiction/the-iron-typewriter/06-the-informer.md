## 6. The Informer

The trouble began with a sack of flour.

Not even a large one. Half a sack, bought from the miller's boy on the corner, along with a twist of salt pork and a jar of rendered fat — enough to feed a growing girl who had appeared in the Reyes household three weeks ago and, in the eyes of the street, had always been there. Coral had done everything right. She had walked Lucia to the chandler's and let her carry the parcel, had taught her the shop's rhythms until her hands were properly black at the nails, had given her a name to answer to and a story to tell: Lucia Reyes, cousin's daughter from upriver, apprenticed against the winter. The garrison ledger had been amended with a coin slipped to the right clerk. The girl ate at their table, slept in the loft, set type with a beginner's crooked enthusiasm that Coral corrected each morning with a compositor's stick and a patience she did not entirely feel.

But flour leaves a mark on a ledger too, and Señor Brill read ledgers the way Tomás read proofs — closely, hungrily, line by line.

She learned of it from the miller's boy himself, a gap-toothed child of twelve named Ferro who delivered their paper stock and considered himself part of the shop's economy. He came in on a gray Tuesday, stamping slush off his boots, and said, too casually, the way children repeat things they don't understand the weight of: "Señor Brill was asking after you. At the mill."

"Asking what?"

"How many mouths you're feeding now." Ferro shrugged. "I said four. He said, *four*, like it was a sum that didn't come out right." He frowned, working at it. "He asked when the cousin's girl arrived. I said before the bridge closed. He said, *before*, like that was interesting too."

Coral kept her hands moving on the stone, leveling a fresh ream. "He's a curious man."

"He's a *leech*," Ferro said, with the moral clarity of the young. "My mother says he'd sell his own shadow if the light was good." Then he was gone, and Coral stood in the cold of the shop with her heart going like a loose platen.

Four mouths. Herself, Tomás, Marta the pressman's widow who came in for wages three days a week, and Lucia. The number was defensible. The number was *true*. But Brill would not be counting mouths. He would be counting what the mouths meant — a girl of perhaps twelve, dark-haired, unsmiling, who had materialized in the last week of open road with no luggage worth the name and no kin anyone in Valdorra had ever heard of. A girl who flinched at boots on cobblestones.

Coral went to the front window and looked across the street at Brill's dry-goods shop, at the man himself standing in his doorway under the awning, arms folded, watching the street with the mild, patient attention of a man watching a slow tide come in. He caught her looking. He raised two fingers off his forearm — not a wave, an *acknowledgment*, the gesture of a creditor — and went back inside.

* * *

That night the city answered Brill's patience with fire.

It started, they said later, at the customs house — a petition refused, a clerk beaten, and then the crowd that had been standing in the cold all week simply stopped being a crowd and became something else. Coral heard it from the loft window as a sound like surf, far off, then nearer, then everywhere: the roar that is not one voice but a city exhaling. Orange light climbed the face of the customs house. Bells began — not the church bells, which rang the hours, but the garrison bells, which rang alarm, and the two sets of bells argued with each other across the rooftops like hostile birds.

"Inside," Tomás said, though they were already inside. He stood in the middle of the workshop with the lamp unlit, a black shape against the blacker window, listening the way a printer listens to a press — for the note that means something has gone wrong with the mechanism. "It will burn itself out by morning."

"It's not a fire, Papá. It's a *beginning*."

"Beginnings burn people too." He said it flatly, without philosophy, a man stating the weather. But he stayed at the window a long time, and Coral, watching him from the stairs, saw his hand rise and rest flat against the glass — against the city, against the noise — the way you'd steady something that was falling.

Lucia had not come down from the loft. Coral climbed up and found her sitting against the wall with her knees drawn up, watching the orange reflections slide across the ceiling. The girl's eyes were enormous. But she was not crying, and when Coral sat down beside her she said, in her small, scraped voice, "They came to our street too. In Marisol. The first night. My mother said, *when they come the first time, it is a visit. When they come the second time, it is a plan.*" She turned her head. "Is this the first time or the second time?"

Coral thought of Elena's letter, of the courier's ruined back, of the word *enemy* stenciled on a hundred walls. "I don't know," she said, because lying to this girl had become a thing her throat refused. "But if it's the second, we'll have had a plan first. That's what this shop is. That's what my father's type is for."

"The forbidden kind?"

"Especially the forbidden kind."

Lucia considered this. Then she did something she had not done in three weeks: she leaned, very slightly, until her shoulder touched Coral's arm, and stayed there while the bells argued and the city burned its first orange hours.

* * *

The curfew came down the next evening like a lid.

Drums at dusk. White placards pasted to every corner — Coral read one by torchlight on her way back from the well: ALL PERSONS OFF THE STREETS AFTER SIXTH BELL. GARRISON PATROLS AUTHORIZED TO FIRE. She noticed, with a small cold shock, that the placards had been printed. Set in a heavy government gothic, badly kerned, the ink applied by a press that some other printer in this city was running long into the night for wages. *Someone set that type,* she thought. *Someone inked that roller. Someone pulled that lever and hung his conscience on the hook by the door.* It was the first time in her life she had looked at an enemy broadside and thought of the hands that made it, and the thought made her feel unclean in a way she couldn't name.

The streets emptied. Valdorra became a city of shutters and lamplight seen through shutters, of boots in rhythm on the quay stones, of doors that did not open. And into that silence, Señor Brill began to knock.

Not on their door. Brill was too careful for that. He knocked on every other door — the chandler's, the ropewalk office, the widow Quiroga who sold chestnuts — and his questions arrived at the Reyes shop secondhand, wrapped in neighborly concern, like packages left on a step. *He wonders if you're warm enough this winter, with four to feed.* *He says the garrison is asking all the riverfront merchants to report their households, for the ration rolls, purely administrative.* *He says a cousin's daughter, that's fortunate, so soon after the bridge closed, the roads being what they were.*

Each message Coral received with a smile and a returned pleasantry, and each one she carried back inside like a splinter working deeper. Marta, the pressman's widow, said what Coral was thinking: "He's not gathering answers. He's building a *case*. There's a difference, and the difference is what he intends to do with it."

"And what does he intend?"

Marta spat into the stove. "The lease. Your father's lease on this frontage has eleven years left, but leases are paper, and paper burns, and who reissues a lease to a traitor's family?" She said *traitor* the way you test a blade — lightly, to feel the edge. "The garrison will need loyal merchants on the riverfront. Men who reported things. Men who can be *grateful*."

* * *

Señor Brill, in the privacy of his back room, with his ledgers open and his tea going cold, was not a wicked man, and this was the most useful thing about him.

He thought of himself as a careful man, a patient man, a man who understood that the great turns of history were not seized but *received*, by those positioned to receive them. He had watched the Reyes shop for nineteen years from across the street — the comings and goings, the paper deliveries, the odd hours, the old man's stubborn refusal to sell or expand or die on any convenient schedule. He had watched the daughter grow from a child in the doorway to a woman who ran the front of the house with her father's exact economy of motion, and he had thought, more than once: *there is a business being wasted on people who do not love business.*

He had no hatred for them. That was what made his mind such a clean instrument. Hatred blurred figures. He simply observed that the Reyes shop occupied sixty feet of the best riverfront in Valdorra, that the times were about to make riverfront either very dangerous or very valuable depending on which side of the ledger you stood, and that he intended to stand on the correct side. He had survived three governments by the same method: he had never once been on the wrong side of a door when it was kicked in.

The girl was the flaw in their arrangement. He was nearly certain of it. Four mouths, and one of them new, and the new one a child of the precise age to be someone's hidden daughter, arriving in the last days of the open bridge — Brill had checked the ferry manifests, such as remained; the garrison had impounded them, but impoundment was a word for *moved to a building Brill's brother-in-law cleaned*. He had not found the name Vasquez. He had found three crossings on the final day with incomplete entries, and he had found that sufficient. Certainty was for fanatics. Probability was for professionals.

He dipped his pen. He did not write a denunciation — not yet, not with the city still smoking and the garrison drowning in denunciations from every coward with a grudge. A denunciation now was a drop in a bucket. A denunciation *timed* was a key.

He wrote instead a short, courteous letter to the garrison quartermaster, offering his services as a loyal merchant of the riverfront district, and noting — in the spirit of administrative thoroughness — that the Reyes print shop's household had grown by one in recent weeks, and that he was sure the family would welcome the garrison's assistance in ensuring all their papers were in order. He signed it. He sanded it. He set it aside to dry, in the drawer where he kept things that ripen.

Across the street, the print shop's lamp burned late. Brill watched it a moment from his window — the small warm rectangle of it, the shadows of the type cases moving behind the glass — and felt nothing so dramatic as malice. He felt the way a man feels watching a tide: that the water was simply doing what water did, and that he had, as always, read the moon correctly.

Then he blew out his lamp, and the street was dark, and only the Reyes window burned — a lit target in a darkened city, printing loyalist broadsides by day and, Brill would have wagered his lease, something else entirely by night.

* * *

Coral found the letter's existence before she found the letter — the way you feel weather before it breaks.

It was Ferro again, three days after the curfew fell, breathless, snow in his hair, refusing payment for the paper stock. "My mother heard it from the chestnut woman, who heard it from Brill's own clerk. He wrote to the garrison. About *us*." The boy's eyes were wide and frightened and thrilled. "Not a report. Something *filed*. The clerk said Brill has a drawer — he calls it his ripening drawer — and our name is in it now."

Coral paid him anyway, pressed a coin into his mittened hand, and stood in the doorway watching him run. Behind her the shop was warm and smelled of ink and oil and the bread Lucia had learned to bake. On the press stood a half-finished loyalist broadside, and beneath the floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth, fifteen years of names waited in steel.

She closed the door. She turned the lock, which had never before been turned in daylight.

"Lucia," she called up to the loft, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Bring me the good paper. We're done printing what the garrison wants."

Above her, the floorboards creaked — and then, beneath them, faint as a heartbeat, she could almost hear the type waiting.
