## 8. The Iron Typewriter

The soldiers' boots had barely faded from the alley before Tomás Reyes began pulling up the floor.

Coral stood in the doorway of the composing room, Lucia's ink-stained sleeve still drying behind her, and watched her father kneel at the corner beneath the proofing press — the same corner she had knelt at a hundred nights, lifting the same three boards, hiding the same small tin of type. But Tomás did not stop at three boards. He worked his fingernail along a seam she had never noticed, a hairline crack in the joist that ran the length of the room, and the floor opened like a book.

Not a cache. An archive.

Boards lifted in sequence down the entire wall, each one hinged on leather straps gone stiff with age, and beneath them, set into the earth in wax-sealed cedar trays, lay type. Rows upon rows of it. Not the respectable Caslon and Garamond matrices that lined the shop's cases — those were her father's public face, bought and paid for. These were cut by hand. She could see it even from the doorway: the slight asymmetry of the serifs, the deep confident bite of a punch driven by a man who had something to say and no legal way to say it.

"How long," she said. Her voice came out smaller than she intended.

"Fifteen years." Tomás did not look up. He lifted a tray, blew the dust from it with the tenderness other men reserved for sleeping children, and set it on the composing stone. "Since the night they took Mateo."

* * *

Tomás had not spoken the apprentice's name aloud in fifteen years, and now it came out of him the way blood comes from a reopened wound — all at once, and then in a slow seeping he could not stop.

"You were nine," he said. "You remember him as the young man who taught you to fold pamphlets without creasing them. That's what I let you remember. That's what I let *myself* remember, for a long time." He turned a matrix in the lamplight, and the letter it bore caught the flame — a lowercase *r* with a spur like a thorn. "He was nineteen. He cut this. His first full alphabet, cut against my orders, in the nights, because he said a typeface should belong to the man who cuts it, the way a voice belongs to the man who speaks."

Coral came closer. She could not help it. The trays kept going — she counted eight, ten, twelve — and each one held a different face, and each face was different the way faces are different: some severe, some almost playful, one so fine and spidery it could only have been cut by someone with failing eyesight working by feel.

"Whose are these?" she asked, though she already knew, the way you know the shape of a thing before you admit its name.

"The disappeared." Tomás laid the trays out in a line down the composing stone, and the lamplight made a small city of them. "Every family the Ministry erased. Every printer shuttered, every pamphleteer fled or shot. When they confiscate a shop, they melt the type — did you know that? Officially it's 'reclamation of state metal.' Unofficially it's so that no one can ever set their words again. So I went to the melt-yards. I bribed the foundrymen. I took impressions from whatever proofs survived and I cut the faces again, by hand, from memory and from grief." He touched the spidery one. "This was Doña Ferreira's. She printed obituaries for the disappeared disguised as shipping notices. They took her in the fourth year. Her son crossed the river with nothing but her eyeglasses."

Coral looked at the archive and understood, with a lurch, that she had slept above a cemetery for fifteen years. And that her father had tended it alone.

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because telling you would have made you guilty, and I had already buried one child of this shop." His jaw worked. "Because I told myself silence was protection. I told myself that so long I believed it." He looked up at her then, and his eyes were the eyes of a man who has been carrying furniture up stairs for fifteen years and has just been told the building is condemned. "Then a captain stood in my shop yesterday and put his hand on my press, and a girl hid in the frame with ink running down her arm, and my daughter lied to his face with a calm I have never had — and I understood that the silence hadn't protected anyone. It had only made me a curator. A man who keeps a museum of dead voices and calls it prudence."

* * *

Lucia found them there at dawn, or what passed for dawn under the curfew clouds. Coral had sent her to bed and the girl had not stayed there; she stood now at the edge of the composing room in her borrowed apron, staring at the trays with an expression Coral had not seen on her before. Not fear. Recognition.

"That one," Lucia said, pointing at the spidery face. "My mother has a pamphlet printed in that. She keeps it inside the lining of her coat. I used to think the letters looked like frost on a window."

Tomás went very still. "Doña Ferreira's face."

"Elena reads it to me," Lucia said. "She says the woman who cut the original died so the words wouldn't."

Coral watched her father absorb this — watched it move through him like current through a press frame — and saw the last of the old Tomás, the survivalist, the curator, go out like a lamp.

* * *

They worked through the morning with the shutters closed and the press muffled with blankets, and it was Tomás who proposed it, which was the thing Coral could not have predicted in fifteen years of predictions.

"The ferry runs until the ice," he said, setting type with hands that shook only slightly, and Coral knew the shaking was not age. "After the ice, nothing crosses until spring. The girl needs papers that say she is what you've already made her — a printer's daughter, traveling to family. But the garrison seal on a forged pass is the hard part. Ibarra's men check the embossing."

"Then we don't forge the seal," Coral said. The idea arrived whole, the way the best ones always did, fully formed, smelling of ink. "We print it. A seal is just type and a press. You taught me that when I was six — *everything is type, mija, everything is ink laid down by somebody's hand.*"

Tomás looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a press engaging after years idle — and went to the back of the archive and lifted a tray Coral had taken for scrap. Inside, bedded in wax, lay a steel die: the garrison seal of Valdorra, cut mirror-perfect, fifteen years old and waiting.

"I cut it the year they took Mateo," he said. "In case there was ever a night like this one. I told myself I'd never use it. A man can tell himself anything for fifteen years."

* * *

They set the documents by lamplight, three generations of craft in a room built for one. Lucia held the copy text — Elena's letter, its instructions memorized and burned. Coral composed the body: *Lucia Vasquez-Reyes, apprentice and daughter of the house, licensed to travel to her aunt across the river for the winter term.* Tomás locked the form into the chase with the tenderness of a man setting scripture, and when he stepped back from the stone, he did not reach for the lever.

"You pull it," he said.

Coral looked at the press. Her whole life she had worked it under her father's eye, proofing respectable broadsides, loyalist almanacs, wedding announcements for families who would have reported them for the contents of the floorboards. She had thought the inheritance was the shop. The lease. The name over the door.

She put her hand on the lever. The iron was cold and then it wasn't. The press closed with a sound like a held breath released, and when she lifted the frisket, the page lay there — true, sharp, every letter bearing the thorn-spurred *r* of Mateo's alphabet, the seal crisp as an official's conscience — and Coral understood that she had just set the first honest line of her life.

Tomás touched the wet ink and then touched her shoulder, the same gesture, the same pressure, and said nothing, because there was nothing left that type could not say better.

* * *

It was Lucia who heard the knock — not the heavy knock of the garrison but the quick, apologetic rap of a neighbor — and it was Coral who opened the door to Señor Brill's oiled smile, his eyes going past her shoulder to the lamplight still burning over the composing stone, to the smell of fresh ink thick enough to taste.

"Forgive the hour," Brill said. "I saw light. I worried." His gaze lingered on the blankets muffled over the press. "Printing late, Señor Reyes? The garrison does so admire industry."

Behind him, down the street, the river wind carried the first smell of true cold, and somewhere on the black water, ice was already forming.
