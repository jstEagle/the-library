## 7. Type Stolen in the Night

The moon was a coin bitten by a careless mouth, and Ulyana hated it for shining.

She had spent her whole life grateful for light—the amber glow of the shop lamps at dusk, the winter sun that came slanting through the frosted panes and lit the dust above the composing stone like a congregation of small angels. Light meant work; work meant the shop went on printing announcements of cattle auctions and lost dogs and the names of men promoted to minor offices, and as long as it printed those things, no one looked twice at the Zhou family. Invisibility had been their trade as much as ink.

But tonight she stood in the yard behind the shop with a sack over her shoulder full of stolen lead, and she wanted the sky black as a closed drawer.

"Move," Kolya whispered behind her. "You're standing in the doorway like a signpost."

"I'm listening."

"You're panicking. There's a difference, but it's small." He pressed past her, bread-sack over his own shoulder, and his breath came out white in the cold. Seventeen years old and he moved through the dark like a cat that had grown up in this town's alleys—which, in fairness, he had. "Marina said the back gate sticks. Lift, don't push."

"Everything in this town sticks," Ulyana muttered, and lifted.

The gate gave with a groan that seemed loud enough to wake the garrison, though it was probably no louder than a hinge complaining since before either of them was born. They slipped through into Marina's yard, where the smell of yesterday's baking still hung around the ovens like a ghost that refused to leave its house. The bakery windows were dark. Somewhere inside, a floorboard settled.

Then the cellar door opened a hand's width, and Marina's voice came out of the gap, low and flat: "In. Now."

* * *

The cellar smelled of earth and salt pork and the faint sweetness of flour sifting down through the floorboards overhead. Marina had cleared a corner near the far wall—moved the pickle barrels, swept the flagstones, laid an old blanket down—and on the blanket sat the spare press stone they'd smuggled in two nights before, wrapped in sacking, looking absurd and holy, like a relic awaiting consecration.

Ulyana set her sack down beside it and untied the drawstring with fingers that would not quite obey her.

They had taken the type from the shop in three nights of small thefts. That was the part that still made her stomach turn when she let herself think about it—not the danger of the streets, but the moment in the type cabinet when her hand hovered over the compartments and she understood what she was doing. Her father's type. Forty years of it, some of the matrices older than she was, bought secondhand from a printer in Tver who had gone under in the bad year, sorted and re-sorted by Anton Zhou's careful fingers until every letter knew its place the way a monk knows his cell. She had been stealing not from the army, not from Reznik, but from her father.

Except that her father knew. That was the strangest part, the part she turned over at night like a stone with something alive under it. Three nights ago she had come down to the shop after supper to make the first withdrawal, and the cabinet had already been open, and a paper lay on the composing stone in her father's cramped, upright hand: *Take the 12-point body. Not the display faces—they'll be missed. And take the quoins. You'll never get a page to lock up without quoins.*

He had not said a word about it at breakfast. He had not said a word ever. He had simply left the drawer open and the note waiting, which was Anton Zhou's way of saying everything he could not bring himself to say aloud: *I will not help you, and I cannot stop you, and God forgive me, here are the quoins.*

"Is that all of it?" Marina asked now. She crouched by the press stone, sleeves rolled, forearms floured to the elbow even at midnight. She was a broad woman built like her own ovens, made for heat and endurance, and grief had carved her face into something harder than age alone could manage. Her brother Stepan had been a miller's man, and then last week he had been nothing at all—no arrest anyone witnessed, no burial anyone attended, no name in any list. Just a hole in the town where a laughing man used to be.

"Twelve-point body, mostly," Ulyana said. "Enough e's and n's and t's to set a page, if we're clever about it. I took the numbers too. Names mean nothing without dates." She heard her own voice steadying as it listed inventory, the way it always did. Numbers were safe. Numbers were the shop's language. "And the quoins, and a chase, and my father's smaller roller."

"And ink?"

"Ink I can make. Lampblack and linseed oil, the old way. Papa taught me when I was nine because he said a printer who can't grind her own ink is only half a printer and no kind of survivor." She almost smiled. Almost. "I don't think he imagined the lesson would be used like this."

"No one imagines anything until it happens," Marina said. "That's how they get away with it."

Kolya had dropped his sack by the stairs and was standing with his arms crossed, watching the cellar door as if the whole Russian army might come through it sideways. "Can we do the speech-making after?" he said. "Every minute we're down here is a minute the light shows under the door. Marina, you put the blanket over the gap or not?"

"It's covered." But Marina moved to check anyway, tucking the sacking tighter along the door's bottom edge, and Ulyana felt the fear in the room change shape—become something shared, distributed, lighter per person than it would have been alone. That was the arithmetic nobody taught you: terror divided among three is not a third each. It is something else entirely. It might even be courage.

"All right," Ulyana said, and knelt at the press stone. "Let's see if we're printers."

* * *

Setting type by candlelight in a damp cellar was a different craft from setting it in the shop. In the shop, the case sat at the correct height, the lamp hung exactly where forty years of habit had hung it, and her father's voice drifted over her shoulder—*no, from the left, girl, you read the type upside down and backwards, so your hands must learn to read forwards*—until the letters arranged themselves almost without her thinking. Here, the case was a bread crate balanced on two barrels. The candle guttered in air that moved with the breathing of the house above. And there was no voice over her shoulder, only the memory of one, which was worse and better in equal measure.

She composed slowly. The first test page was not ambitious: a single column, a heading she had agonized over for two days before choosing the plainest words possible.

*CIVIL LEDGER OF VELSK — TRUE COPY.*

Underneath, the first eleven names. She had copied them out fair that afternoon, sitting in the shop with the requisitioned ledger draft on her lap and the old civil ledger open beneath a loose board under the type cabinet, transcribing in a schoolgirl's hand that she hoped looked like nothing at all. Eleven names out of forty-one. The miller Osip Vasilievich and his wife and daughter. The schoolteacher who had once written a letter to a newspaper in Petersburg. Two debtors whose debts had died with them and should not have been allowed to erase them. A drunk whom everyone had forgotten and whom Reznik's clerks had remembered just enough to delete.

Her hands shook on the composing stick. She cursed them silently. In the shop she could set a column of auction notices faster than Kolya could count, and here she fumbled the third line twice and dropped an *n* that rang against the flagstones with a sound like a small bell tolling for her specifically.

"Pick it up," Kolya whispered. "If a soldier finds one letter in the yard tomorrow, we're dead over one letter."

"I know."

"So pick it up quietly instead of staring at it."

She picked it up quietly. She locked the line into the stick, tightened the quoin key until the type stood firm as masonry, and slid the finished galley onto the press stone. Marina watched all of it with the expression of a woman judging dough—neither impressed nor disappointed, merely assessing whether the thing would rise.

"The stone's level," Marina said. "I checked it with water. My husband was a carpenter before the bakery; he'd have found the tilt in it if there was one." A pause, and something crossed her face—the place where the dead husband lived, visited briefly and left again. "There isn't one."

Ulyana inked the roller—her father's small hand roller, its leather worn smooth—and drew it across the type. The ink caught unevenly in the cold; she had warmed it near the candle, but the cellar drank heat like a debt collector drinks credit. She laid a sheet of cheap paper over the form, laid the frisket board over that, and pressed down with both palms, rocking her weight, the way she had done ten thousand times on proofs that never mattered.

Then she lifted the paper, and the room went quiet.

It was crooked. The lines wandered like a drunk walking home; the impression was heavy on the left and ghostly on the right; the heading sat a hair off true so that *TRUE COPY* looked slightly ashamed of itself. Ink had smudged where her palm had slipped.

But it was legible. Every name. Every date. Eleven people who officially did not exist, existing now in ink on paper, in a cellar, under a widow's bakery, in defiance of the commandant of the entire district.

"Oh," said Marina softly.

"Yeah," said Kolya.

Ulyana said nothing. She was looking at the fourth line, at the name *Osip Vasilyev*, and she found that her eyes were burning and she did not entirely know why. It was ugly work. It was the worst printing of her life. And it was the first page she had ever set that mattered, and the two facts arrived together in her chest and had nowhere to go except outward, as heat, as tears she wiped furiously with the heel of her hand before either of the others could see.

"Second pull will be better," she managed. "I need to pack the stone under the low side. And warm the ink properly. And—" She stopped. Breathed. "It works. That's the point. It *works*."

Marina took the crooked sheet and held it at arm's length, reading the names the way she read a recipe, lips moving slightly. When she reached her brother's name she went very still, and Ulyana saw her thumb move once across the printed letters, the way you'd touch a face.

"He couldn't write," Marina said. "Stepan. Signed his mark, an X, all his life. Said letters were for priests and liars." Her voice was steady in the way of a beam bearing more weight than it was rated for. "Now he's in print. He'd laugh himself sick."

* * *

They pulled six copies before the candle burned down to a stub. Each one came out better than the last as Ulyana shimmed the stone with folded paper, warmed the ink ball between her palms, learned the particular stubbornness of this borrowed press. By the sixth, the lines still wandered but the names stood clear and black, and Kolya had stopped hovering by the door and come to stand over her shoulder, watching each sheet emerge with the wide-eyed greed of a boy watching coins being minted.

"We should do the rest tonight," he said. "All forty-one. Get it done."

"No." Ulyana surprised herself with how quickly the word came. "We do eleven, and we stop, and we clean everything, and we go home and sleep and act like idiots tomorrow. Idiots who know nothing." She began distributing the type back into the crate-case, each letter home in its compartment. "Papa always says the amateurs print everything at once and the professionals print one page and live to print the next."

"Your papa also says never to do what we're doing."

"My papa," Ulyana said, "left the quoins out for me."

Kolya shut up. Even Marina looked over. In the candlelight the sentence hung in the damp air, and Ulyana realized she had never said it aloud before, and that saying it made it real in a new and frightening way—that her father's silence had been a co-conspirator's silence, that the family creed of invisibility had cracked somewhere in the last week and neither of them had spoken of the crack.

"He knows?" Marina asked carefully.

"He knows something. He knows enough to arm me for it." Ulyana snapped the case lid shut. "Whether he knows what I'm printing or only that I'm printing *something*—I honestly don't know. And I'm afraid to find out, because if he asks me directly, I'll have to lie to him, and I've never lied to him, and I think he knows that too, and that's why he doesn't ask."

"That's not lying," Kolya said. "That's mercy. There's a difference, but it's small."

She threw a rag at him. He caught it and grinned, and for a moment the cellar was just a cellar and they were just young people hiding from the dark, and the terror receded enough to let the other feeling through—the one she had been chasing all night underneath the fear, the one that had no decent name. Exhilaration was close. So was pride, which frightened her more, because pride was how people got caught. But it was there, humming in her blood like fresh ink: *I did this. We did this. The commandant owns the shop and the ledgers and the gallows-notice boards, and he does not own this page.*

Marina banked the candle stub, wrapped the six sheets in oilcloth, and pushed them deep into the false bottom of a flour bin that had clearly been prepared days ago—prepared, Ulyana understood suddenly, since the night Marina had first come to her with the proposition, since before there was anything to hide in it. Hope had built that false bottom. Hope and grief, which in Marina seemed to be the same material, differently milled.

"Same time in three nights," Marina said. "Not sooner. The neighbor counts the lights on this street; old habits. And Ulyana—" She paused at the foot of the stairs, a broad silhouette against the thin gray line of the door. "The eleventh name on your page. Fyodor the drunk. Everyone forgot him alive. Nobody will forget him now. That's worth more than you think it is."

Then they were out through the sticking gate, into the bitten-moon night, Kolya ranging ahead through the alleys while Ulyana walked the long way home with empty sacks and a heart going like a press at full run.

* * *

The shop was dark when she let herself in the back, and for one lurching instant she thought—*they've come, they've taken him*—before she saw the shape of her father in the chair by the cold stove, awake, fully dressed, hands folded, waiting. He did not light a lamp. Neither did she.

"You were careful?" he asked. His voice was rough with the hour.

"Yes."

"The type is all accounted? Nothing left where a searching eye would find it?"

"Nothing. Papa—"

"Don't tell me." He raised one hand, and even in the dark she could see how it trembled, and how firmly he pressed it still against his knee. "Whatever it is, I don't want it said in this house. What I don't hear, I can't be made to repeat." A long silence. The stove ticked as it cooled. Then, quieter, in the voice he used for corrections on her proofs—gentle, precise, final: "But Ulyana. If you must do this thing that we are not speaking of—check your spacing. Your father has eyes like a hawk for spacing, even failing eyes, and whatever comes off that stone of yours will be read by strangers, and strangers judge us by our kerning as much as by our sins."

She stood in the dark with her hand over her mouth, laughing or crying, unable to determine which, doing both.

She was still smiling when she climbed to her room, still smiling as she blew out her candle—and then she heard it, faint through the frost-furred window: boots. Many boots, unhurried, coming up the street toward the shop, stopping outside it. A fist on wood. Once. Twice.

"Papa," she breathed. "Papa, someone's at the door."
