## 10. Forty-One Names

The cellar smelled of damp stone and lamp oil and, faintly, of Marina's bread — the yeast had worked its way into the walls the way grief works its way into a house, so that everything down there was fed by it. Ulyana knelt on the cold floor with her sleeves pushed to the elbow, chasing a crooked line of type across the press stone with a composing stick that Kolya had stolen from his own uncle's shop three nights before.

"Hold it," she said. "Hold it — don't breathe."

Kolya held it. He was crouched at the corner of the frame with both hands flat against the wood, keeping the whole improvised chase from shivering apart, and he did not breathe, or made a great theatrical show of not breathing, cheeks puffed, eyes crossed, until she laughed despite herself and the laugh cost them the line anyway. The type clattered loose like teeth.

"You breathed," she accused him.

"I exhaled *once*."

"Once is enough. Type hears everything." She gathered the letters back into the stick, sorting by touch in the lamplight, the way her father had taught her when she was seven years old and could barely lift the case. *Ink remembers, Ulyana. Lead remembers. Only men forget.* He'd said it as a joke then, a printer's proverb to make a child careful. Now it read like scripture.

Above them, through the floorboards, came the ordinary sounds of the bakery — the scrape of peels on oven brick, Marina's low voice serving the morning queue, soldiers' boots among civilian ones because the garrison ate where it pleased now. That was the arrangement: noise above, silence below, and between them six inches of pine flooring and all their lives.

It was the third night of printing, and the first night the pages were coming out straight.

* * *

They worked from two documents. The first was the old civil ledger itself, which Ulyana had copied out in her own hand over four evenings, sitting in the shop after dark with the shutters drawn, transcribing every name, trade, street, and household number while her father pretended not to see. The second was Reznik's draft — the corrected census, the clean town — which Sasha had carried out of the commandant's office folded inside a requisition for lamp oil, back when carrying it was still something a man could tell himself was an accident.

Forty-one names. She had counted them eleven times. Debtors struck from the rolls so their property could pass quietly to cooperative men. Two schoolteachers who had signed a petition the year before. The widow Gorskaya, whose son had deserted, though the son himself had never been seen again either. And the miller Ostrovsky with his wife and three children, five names gone in a single stroke, erased last week along with the family, erased so thoroughly that the draft ledger showed the mill as standing empty since spring.

Empty since spring. As if the town had never known them. As if flour had milled itself.

Ulyana set the names one by one, and each one went into the stick heavier than lead had any right to be.

*Ostrovsky, Matvei. Miller.*

*Ostrovskaya, Anna.*

*Ostrovsky, Petya, aged nine.*

She stopped with Petya's name in the stick. Nine years old. She had sold him a twist of cheap candy once, from the tray her father kept by the door for children who hung around the shop watching the press run. He'd asked how the letters got backwards and she'd told him they weren't backwards, they were only waiting to be turned around, and he'd looked at her with the enormous seriousness of a nine-year-old being let in on a secret.

Now he was waiting to be turned around too. That was the whole work of this press, she thought. Turning things around. Making the mirror show what actually stood in front of it.

"Ulyana." Kolya's voice, quiet, from the foot of the stairs. "Someone's coming."

* * *

They had a signal for Sasha and no signal for anyone else, which meant that any knock was Sasha and any other knock was the end of everything, and they had agreed not to discuss what they'd do about the second kind. The knock came: two, pause, one. Kolya lifted the bar. Sasha came down the steps sideways, the way he always did now, as if the cellar ceiling had been built for a smaller man, and he was drunk — not tavern-drunk, not falling-down drunk, but that particular half-measure he'd learned, enough to steady the hands without loosening the tongue, though Ulyana suspected the tongue had been lost some time ago.

He carried a canvas satchel and he set it on the flour bin with exaggerated care, the care of a man handling something that might go off.

"The rest," he said.

Ulyana wiped her inky fingers on her apron and opened the satchel. Papers. Not copies — originals, she saw at once, army stock, the garrison stamp in faded violet at the head of each page. Purge orders. Transport manifests with columns of names and destinations left blank where destinations should have been. And at the bottom, wrapped separately in oilcloth, a single sheet in Reznik's own small precise hand: a list of fourteen names, annotated in the margins with dates.

Dates. She understood what the dates meant a moment before she let herself understand it, and her stomach dropped through the floor of her body.

"These are—" She couldn't say it.

"Execution warrants," Sasha said. Flatly. A clerk's word, a filing word. He sat down heavily on an upturned crate and put his face in his hands. "Pending. Three of them are already — the notices are being composed this week. Your father will set them, I expect. He'll be ordered to."

The cellar was very quiet. Above them someone laughed in the bakery, a customer, an ordinary laugh, and the ordinariness of it was obscene.

"You stole these," Kolya said. "Out of the office. Out of *Reznik's* office."

"I copied the first ones. These I took." Sasha lifted his head. His eyes were red-rimmed and perfectly sober-looking, which was worse than drunk. "There's a difference. Copying, I could tell myself — a clerk makes memoranda, a clerk keeps notes, it's a bad habit, it means nothing. Taking is different. Taking has a shape. It has a direction." He looked at Ulyana. "I've been walking in one direction for two weeks pretending I was wandering."

"Why?" It came out harder than she intended. "Why now? Why risk—"

"Because of the dates." He said it simply. "Because I transcribed the Ostrovsky order myself, in fair hand, for the file, and my pen didn't shake, and I noticed that my pen didn't shake, and I thought: this is what I am now. A pen that doesn't shake." He rubbed his jaw. "My father was a deacon. In Tver. He believed a record kept honestly was a kind of prayer. I used to think that was just the sort of thing fathers said." A short, terrible laugh. "Then I got assigned to rewrite a town out of existence and discovered I'd inherited the belief without the courage, and the courage is what you people have been manufacturing down here in the dark while I drank myself brave upstairs."

"You're not brave," Kolya said. "You're terrified. I've watched you come down those stairs. You look like a man walking to his own funeral."

"Yes," Sasha agreed. "Both things can be true. Ask any soldier."

* * *

Marina came down at midnight, after the ovens were banked, bringing bread still warm and a jug of milk tea and the particular stern tenderness she wore like an apron. She spread a cloth on the flour bin, cut the loaf, and would not let anyone print another line until they had eaten.

"You can't set type on an empty stomach," she said. "The letters come out thin."

"That's not how it works," Ulyana began.

"It's how *my* kitchen works, and this is my kitchen." Marina sat, folding her hands, and looked at the papers laid out under the lamp — the true ledger pages, crooked early attempts pinned to a string like laundry, the purge orders fanned open. Her brother's name was among the forty-one. *Volkov, Dmitri. Cooper.* Ulyana had set it on the first night, and Marina had stood behind her and watched the letters go into the stick one at a time and had not said a word, and had not needed to.

"Now," Marina said. "Show me where we are."

Ulyana showed her. Twenty-six names set and proofed. Fifteen to go, plus the annotations — trades, households, the little histories that made a name more than ink. Then the preamble, which she had drafted and redrafted a dozen times and which still didn't satisfy her:

*Be it known that the following persons, residents of the town of Velsk, living or lately living, do exist, and that the annexed official register, which omits them, is false.*

"Do exist," Marina repeated. She turned the phrase over. "Living or lately living."

"The dead count too," Ulyana said. "That's the point. They've buried people without records. Unrecorded dead are the easiest kind — no grave marker, no paperwork, nothing to contradict whatever story comes next. So we put them back. All of them. The living get their names restored and the dead get their deaths witnessed. That's all a ledger is, in the end. Witnessing."

Sasha was looking at the preamble with an expression she couldn't read. "Reznik will know it was set professionally," he said. "The justification, the spacing. It reads like a government document. That's what will frighten him — not that someone opposed him, but that someone opposed him *in his own format*, with his own conventions. It'll look official. People will believe it's official."

"Good," said Kolya.

"It also means he'll know it came from a press. There are two presses in Velsk. One is his."

"And one," said Marina, "is a rumor in a cellar." She poured the tea. "Drink. All of you. Then back to work."

* * *

They finished the twenty-seventh name at one in the morning and the twenty-eighth at half past, and somewhere past two Ulyana locked the final fifteen entries into the forme, tightened the quoins with the little iron key that had been her father's father's, and stepped back.

"Well," she said. Her voice sounded strange in the cellar, too loud and too young. "First full sheet."

Kolya fetched the paper — real printing paper, bought a ream at a time from three different stationers so no single purchase would be remembered. Marina trimmed the lamp wick. Sasha stood at the edge of the light with his arms folded, wanting to help and knowing he had no skill to offer, a man whose entire competence belonged to the other side.

Ulyana inked the ball, rolled it across the type, heard the small wet kiss of it, laid the sheet, pulled the bar.

The press spoke. In the shop above, in her childhood, the press had spoken all day long and she had never listened; down here, in the dark, with everyone holding their breath, it sounded like a voice.

She peeled the sheet and held it up.

Straight. Every line straight, justified clean to both margins, the preamble black and certain, and below it the names in their columns — twenty-eight of the forty-one — each one set by her own hands, each one refusing, with the dumb stubborn permanence of lead, to be erased. *Ostrovsky, Matvei. Miller.* *Volkov, Dmitri. Cooper.* *Gorskaya, Serafima.* *Petya, aged nine.* All of them existing. All of them witnessed.

Nobody spoke. Marina put her hand over her mouth. Kolya made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled into something else. Sasha stepped forward into the light at last and read the sheet top to bottom, slowly, the way he must once have read orders he was ashamed of, and when he reached the bottom he touched the margin with two fingers, lightly, the way a man touches a rail in a church.

"It's real," he said. "It looks — it looks like the truth. It *is* the truth, but it looks like it, too. Do you understand? That's the weapon. Anyone who sees this will believe it, because it believes itself."

"My father says ink remembers," Ulyana said. Her eyes were stinging and she didn't fight it. "Lead remembers. Only men forget. Well — we've made something that won't forget even if we all forget, even if we all die. Forty-one names. Thirteen more tomorrow, and the annotations, and then five hundred copies if the paper holds out."

"Three hundred," Marina corrected. "Five hundred is greed. Greed gets caught."

"Four hundred," said Kolya, "and I carry them myself, every one, on my own back, and no one argues with me about it."

They argued about it. It was, absurdly, the happiest moment of Ulyana's life — the four of them bent over a single printed sheet in a bread cellar at three in the morning, quarreling lovingly about distribution logistics while the town above slept inside its lie. She looked around the lamplight at these people who were not her family and were: a disgraced clerk with a deacon's ghost on his shoulders, a widow feeding the men who'd killed her brother's name, a baker's boy with flour in his eyebrows keeping watch at the stairs. Her father would call it treason. Her father, she thought, and the thought snagged like a nail in cloth — her father, who had burned one page of the draft rather than surrender the plates, who committed his small treasons alone and called them prudence. He didn't know about the cellar. He knew something was wrong with his daughter; he read it in her hands, the way he read type, by touch. But he didn't know.

And Sasha's words came back to her: *the execution notices are being composed this week. Your father will set them. He'll be ordered to.*

Three of the erased names. Three of her forty-one, cast in her father's shop, in her father's type, by her father's hands.

She looked down at the fresh sheet, at the beautiful stubborn black columns, and the joy in her chest curdled into something colder and more urgent. Because the sheet was right and the world was wrong and the gap between them was closing fast, and somewhere across town, in a guarded office, a man with a precise hand was already planning his next correction.

"We need to move faster," she said. "All of it. Copies, distribution, everything. Before—"

Before the trap she couldn't yet see. Before the neighbor's boy and the cellar light. Before the mill, and the fire, and the price none of them had begun to imagine.

But that night, in the candlelight, with the first full sheet drying on the string among its crooked sisters, none of them knew. They only knew that the truth, at last, was in print.

Marina snuffed the lamp. In the dark, the smell of ink and bread mingled, and Ulyana lay on the pallet beside the press and did not sleep, and did not want to.
