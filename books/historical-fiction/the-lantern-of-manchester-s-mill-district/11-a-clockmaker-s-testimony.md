## 11. A Clockmaker's Testimony

The Oakeses lived in a back-court off Angel Meadow, where the houses leaned together like tired men holding each other upright. Rafael had passed through such streets all her life without seeing them — the apprentice with his collar up and his eyes down, minding his own small business of wheels and springs. Now she walked them as something else, though she could not yet have said what. A witness to witnesses. A keeper of other people's hours.

Hannah Oakes opened the door before Rafael finished knocking.

She was not what Rafael had expected, which shamed her, because she had not known she'd been expecting anything. She had imagined — she supposed — a broken woman, wrung soft by grief. Instead Hannah Oakes stood square in her doorway in a clean apron, red-eyed but level, with the look of someone who had been crying on a schedule and intended to stop by noon.

"You're the clockmaker." Not a question. "Brand said you'd come."

"May I come in?"

"You may." She stepped aside. "But I'll say now what I said to the constables: my Sam never set a fire in his life. He couldn't even light the stove without me standing over him."

* * *

The room inside was small and fiercely tidy, the way rooms are when tidiness is the only order left to command. A loom stood against one wall, silent, its warp still threaded — Samuel Oakes had been arrested mid-work, then, or near enough. On the mantel, between two chipped figurines, sat a tin watch on a chain, its case gone brown with handling.

Rafael's eye went to it the way water goes downhill.

"That was his father's," Hannah said, following her gaze. "It stopped the night they took him. Eleven minutes past nine, and it's not moved since." Her mouth tightened. "I know what that sounds like. Sentiment. But I keep thinking — if it started again, it would mean something."

"It would mean it needed cleaning," Rafael said gently. "Nothing more. Grief doesn't live in mechanisms, Mrs. Oakes. That's rather the point of them."

Something flickered across the woman's face — surprise, then a hard, grateful steadiness, as if Rafael had handed her a tool instead of a condolence. "Sit down, then. You're the first person this fortnight who's talked to me like I've a head on my shoulders."

Rafael sat, and laid her satchel on her knees, and did not open it yet.

"I want to take down what you tell me," she said. "In writing. Properly. With your mark at the bottom and mine beneath it, saying I took it true."

Hannah went very still. "Writing for what? The magistrate's men already wrote plenty. Wrote Sam into a fire he never struck a match for."

"Not for Corvan." Rafael kept her voice low and even, the way she spoke to a movement she didn't yet trust. "For the record. There's an inquest coming. What's sworn before it can't be unwritten after. I've seen how these things go, Mrs. Oakes. The fire cools, the smoke clears, and then the men who made the smoke sit down with comfortable pens and write the story they wanted all along. I mean to put something in their way first."

"And who are you, that you can do that?" Hannah asked. Not hostile. Simply a woman counting costs, as she must have counted every cost since the knock came on her own door.

Rafael considered several answers. *An apprentice. A clockmaker. A friend of Sergeant Brand.* All true and none sufficient, and one of them a lie she had worn so long it had grown into her skin.

"A repairer," she said at last. "I mend things that have been made to lie. Lately the things are larger than clocks."

* * *

She had brought good paper — bought dear, from the stationer off Shudehill, who had looked at her twice and asked no questions, which was itself a kind of currency in Manchester just now. She had brought her best pen and a bottle of ink wrapped in flannel against the jolts of the streets. And she had brought, folded in her breast pocket next to the sealed letter whose very weight she felt like a coal against her skin, a page of instructions she had copied out the night before from a law handbill Brand had procured: the forms of a deposition, the phrases that gave words the force of evidence.

*I, Hannah Oakes, of Angel Meadow, in the township of Manchester, do make oath and say—*

No. Not oath. An oath required a magistrate, and every magistrate within reach belonged to Corvan. A declaration, then. Sworn before witnesses when witnesses could be found. It was less than she wanted and more than nothing, and Rafael had spent twenty-two years learning the difference between the mechanism you wished for and the mechanism that would actually run.

"Tell me about the night of the fire," she said. "Not the fire itself. Before it. Everything ordinary."

Hannah blinked. "Ordinary?"

"Everything ordinary is what they'll erase first. Anyone can swear a man wasn't at a fire. I need to show where he was, minute by minute, and who saw him, and why those people remember. Memory is a mechanism too, Mrs. Oakes. It holds better if you oil it with particulars."

And so Hannah began, hesitantly at first, then with gathering force, and Rafael wrote.

Samuel had come home at half six, as always, hands black to the wrist, complaining of a slub in the new cotton batch. He had eaten — potato pie, and he'd said the crust was better than last week's, which from Sam was a sonnet. At seven the neighbor's boy, Enoch Whitlow, had come for him because the strike committee met at the Whitlows' that evening, and Hannah had gone with him as far as the corner because the gas lamp there was out and she wouldn't have him walk dark streets alone. They had stood under the dead lamp and argued — mildly, the way of long-married folk — about whether he should wear his coat, and he had gone back for it, and she had watched him come out again wearing it, and that was the last time she saw him free.

"He came home past midnight," she said, and here her voice finally cracked and she let it, without apology. "He smelled of smoke and I thought — I thought, *they've been burning rubbish again*, the mill yards are full of it. He washed at the sink and went to bed. Two days later they came for him and said he'd fired Hartley's. My Sam, who fell asleep during sermons because he hadn't the heart to walk out on the preacher."

Rafael wrote it all. Her hand, trained on ledger lines and engraver's flourishes, moved steadily, and she found herself doing something she had never done with words: listening *for* them. As a girl she had learned to hear a train in a wheel's song, to know by sound alone which tooth of a ratchet was worn. Now she heard the load-bearing parts of Hannah's testimony — the coat, the dead lamp, the pie, the washing at the sink — each one a pivot on which the whole account turned. And she heard the places where memory ran rough, and instead of smoothing them she asked.

"The coat. Where is it now?"

Hannah stared. Then she rose, crossed to the press bed, and pulled out a wool coat, smelling of camphor, with a burn hole scorched through the left cuff.

"He came home with that. Said a spark from a yard brazier caught him passing. I mended it." Her fingers found the darned place. "You think — the cuff. If he'd been at the fire—"

"If he'd been setting fires, the burns would be elsewhere, and likely worse. But I'm not the one who decides. The ones who decide will have their own experts, or men dressed as experts." Rafael looked up. "So we give them a coat they can't argue with, and a lamp that was out, and a neighbor boy, and a pie. Small parts, Mrs. Oakes. A case is built of small parts same as a clock. Theirs is built of smoke. Smoke doesn't hold a thread."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon. Rafael learned, as the pages filled, that taking a deposition was its own craft, with its own tolerances and its own ways of failing.

She learned to write faster than elegant, and to forgive her hand its haste. She learned to read back each answer aloud, exactly as written, because Hannah had the right to hear what would be done with her voice — and because hearing it, Hannah remembered more. The dead lamp had been reported to the lamplighter, who'd shrugged; there was a name. The strike committee meeting had ended at ten by the chapel bell, which rang the quarter-hours audibly in the Whitlows' front room; there was a bell, and bells were Rafael's native country. She began, without quite deciding to, to build the deposition the way she'd build a repair: identify the worn part, find what sound it ought to make, fit the testimony until the account ran true.

At one point Hannah said, "You write like you're angry."

Rafael looked down. Her strokes had gone deep, nearly cutting the paper.

"I'm sorry. I'll copy it fair."

"Don't be sorry." Hannah's chin came up. "Leave it. Let them see somebody was angry when they wrote us down. Everyone else who writes about us is calm as Sunday."

Rafael left it.

Near dusk, footsteps on the stair made them both freeze — but it was only Enoch Whitlow, fourteen and solemn, come as Hannah had asked, to add what he'd seen. He stood by the door with his cap in his hands and answered Rafael's questions in a flat, careful voice, and when she read his statement back he listened with the gravity of a man twice his age, and signed — marked, rather, a careful X — and then asked, "Will it do any good?"

Rafael thought of the letter in her pocket. Of Marta's face at the dinner table. Of everything the truth had already cost and might yet cost.

"I don't know," she said, because he deserved honesty more than comfort. "But it will exist. That's more than they planned for."

Enoch nodded slowly, as if weighing existence itself and finding it worth the risk, and went down the stairs into the dark.

* * *

By lamplight Rafael made the fair copies while Hannah cooked — insisted on cooking, would not be argued with, set a bowl of stew before her guest with the fierce hospitality of a woman who refused to be only a victim in her own kitchen. Rafael ate and copied, and copied and ate, and somewhere between the second page and the third she understood that her hands had changed trades.

All her life she had worked inside records she didn't control. Ledgers kept by masters. Pay books kept by overlookers. The register where her apprenticeship papers lived under a name that was half true and wholly a disguise. She had been, always, a presence hidden in other people's accounting — invisible precisely because the record belonged to someone else. Even the letter in her pocket, Corvan's poisoned instrument, was a record meant to bury people.

This was different. This was hers. Every line on these pages existed because she had asked for it, shaped it, preserved it. She was not hiding in the record anymore. She was making it. And a record-maker, she realized, ink drying under her pen, was a far more dangerous thing than a record-hider — because hiders could be ignored, but makers had to be stopped.

The thought should have frightened her. It settled instead into her chest with the quiet click of a part finding its seat.

"There," she said at last, blotting the final page. "Two copies. One goes where no one will think to look. One stays with me." She slid both toward Hannah. "Your mark on each. Mine beneath, with the date and the words *taken down truly from her own mouth*."

Hannah signed, then held the top sheet a moment before letting it go, as if releasing a bird she wasn't sure could fly.

"What happens to it?"

"It goes into keeping. Good keeping — I'll say no more than that, for your sake." Rafael packed her satchel. "Mrs. Oakes — Hannah. When the inquest comes, they will send clever men to say your husband is a liar and you are a fool and I am whatever suits them. When that day comes, will you stand by these words?"

Hannah looked at the silent loom, at the stopped watch on the mantel, at the mended coat hanging by the door.

"My Sam used to say a machine's only honest when it's running," she said. "These words are him running. Aye. I'll stand by them."

* * *

Sergeant Brand was waiting in the court below, leaning against the wall with the studied idleness of a man who had learned surveillance from the wrong side. He fell into step beside her without greeting, matching her pace, eyes moving over the street the way hers moved over a movement.

"Well?"

"She'll hold. The boy will hold. And I have the coat, the lamp, the bell, and a burn hole in exactly the right place to ruin their story." Rafael shifted the satchel higher on her shoulder. "How many more can I reach before the inquest?"

"Three days. Maybe four." His jaw worked. "There's a spinner's widow off Great Ancoats whose man was taken the same night — he was seen at the Whitlows' too. And there's the overlooker at Hartley's who locked the fire doors early, which either makes him Corvan's creature or Corvan's problem, depending which way he breaks."

"Then I'll see them tomorrow. Both."

Brand glanced at her sideways. In the lamplight spilling from a gin shop doorway, his scarred face wore something that on another man might have been called wonder.

"You know what you've become, don't you?" he said. "A week ago you were a lad who fixed watches. Now Corvan's clerk risks his neck to bring you papers, and widows sign their names to your hand, and I'd follow you into a magistrate's court on your word alone." He shook his head slowly. "That's power, miss. Real power. The kind he can't buy, because he never learned it exists."

Rafael walked on without answering, but the words went with her, heavy and warm as a carried engine.

At the corner where their ways divided, Brand caught her sleeve. His voice dropped.

"One thing more. Wren sent word — says you're to come to the observatory. Tomorrow, before you make your rounds. Says—" Brand's mouth twisted, trying on the old man's phrasing and finding it strange. "Says there's something you should know about what the observatory has been *for*. All these years. Says you'll understand, being a keeper of mechanisms yourself."

Above the rooftops, faint through the smoke-haze, Rafael could just make out the observatory dome, black against the lesser black of the sky, and somewhere inside it the master clock she had repaired, keeping its honest seconds over a district full of lies.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "I'll go and learn what I've been winding all along."
