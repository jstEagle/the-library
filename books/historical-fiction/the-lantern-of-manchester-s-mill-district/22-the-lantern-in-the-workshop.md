## 22. The Lantern in the Workshop

The sign went up on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays were for beginnings in Rafael's private calendar—the day her father had always wound the shop's regulators after the Sunday rest, the day small parts were counted and set right.

Nystrom & Apprentices, Clockmakers, it read, painted by a signwriter who had asked twice whether she was certain of the wording. Not Nystrom & Son. Not even Master Nystrom, which would have been defensible, respectable, a man's name with a man's title bolted to it like a bracket.

*And Apprentices.* She had chosen it deliberately. A shop named for its master was a monument; a shop named for its apprentices was a promise.

Marta held the ladder while Elias Brand—still favoring his left side, still moving like a man who had learned that stairs could be ambushes—screwed the brackets home. Below them on the cobbles, a knot of neighbors gathered the way knots of neighbors do, half curiosity and half appetite for scandal, and Rafael stood in the doorway of the workshop her father had kept under another man's name for thirty years and let herself be looked at.

She had been looked at before, of course. At the inquest she had been looked at by two hundred people and a coroner and Corvan's counsel, who had spent three hours trying to make her name into a weapon—*Rafael Nystrom, is it? And is that a man's name or a woman's? Which are we to write down, madam, when you swear your oath?*—and she had answered him in the voice she used for escapements, level and precise: *You may write down that I am a clockmaker, sir, and that I keep time honestly, which is more than can be said for some who have held office in this city.*

The coroner's clerk had written it down. It was in the record now, bound in grey board in the registry office, unremarkable and indestructible, one line among ten thousand lines. That was the whole secret of records, she had learned. They did not triumph. They simply persisted, the way a good mainspring persisted, quietly coiled at the heart of things, waiting to be needed.

"Rafael." Marta touched her elbow. "They're waiting."

The neighbors wanted a speech. Rafael gave them a demonstration instead. She opened the workshop doors wide—doors that had been broken by Corvan's men in October and rehung by Brand with soldier's thoroughness—and showed them the benches, cleaned and oiled, the tools laid out in rows, the great wheel of the old turret clock her grandfather had salvaged from a demolished chapel mounted above the hearth where her father's portrait had once hung. Her father was not gone. He was simply everywhere else in the room, in the placement of every file and the angle of every lamp, and she had decided that this too was a kind of portrait.

Then she brought out the thing she had made all winter, working nights while the inquest ground through its weeks and the newspapers discovered and discarded the story of Magistrate Corvan as quickly as they discovered everything: a regulator clock, wall-mounted, its case of plain oak, its dial enamel-white, its hands blued steel. Nothing ornamental about it. She had built it the way she built everything now—as an argument.

"This keeps Manchester time," she told the crowd, "to within a second a week. Any apprentice in this shop will learn to build such a clock inside two years. Any child in this district may apply. I will teach girls as readily as boys, and I will say so plainly, so that no parent need wonder and no magistrate need infer."

A murmur ran through the street. Somewhere in it a woman laughed—not mockery; relief, Rafael thought, the sound of a person hearing a rule spoken aloud that they had only ever felt as a silence.

"You'll have the mills down on you," someone called from the back. "Factory owners won't send their repairs to a shop that trains their daughters out of their spinning."

"The factory owners," Rafael said, "may take their business wherever they like. There are four thousand households in this district, and every one of them owns something that ticks."

Brand snorted behind her, low enough that only Marta heard, and Marta elbowed him, and the two of them wore the particular expressions of people who had watched Rafael rehearse exactly this speech against the pantry door for a fortnight.

* * *

The first apprentice arrived before the paint on the sign had fully cured.

Her name was Susanna Hartley, and she was fourteen, and she was the daughter of the weaver whose deposition Rafael had taken in her best hand eleven months ago, the weaver who had walked out of the assizes a free man with four months of his life stolen and a limp he would keep. He came with her to the door, cap in his fists, and stood in the workshop looking at the benches as if they were foreign territory.

"I can't pay a premium," he said. Premiums—the fees families paid to place a child in a trade—were how apprenticeships worked, and everyone in the room knew what four months without wages did to a family's savings, which was to say there were none.

"There's no premium," Rafael said. "There's a contract. Seven years. She learns the trade properly—files first, then turning, then wheels, then escapements. She lives at home. She's paid a small wage from the second year, as the law allows, and I'll pay a little more than the law requires, because the law is stingy. In return she works honest hours and keeps honest books, and when she's done she'll be able to walk into any shop in England and be taken seriously, or open her own and put my name to shame."

Hartley looked at his daughter. Susanna looked at the great wheel above the hearth with the expression Rafael remembered wearing at that age, the hunger that has found an object.

"She's got steady hands," Hartley said, as if offering evidence.

"They're the only kind I take."

He shook Rafael's hand then, hard, and said nothing else, because men of his generation and class did not say thank-you to the gentry and did not yet know what to call Rafael, and Rafael understood both difficulties and let the handshake carry the weight.

By spring there were five apprentices. Two girls, three boys. The boys' parents had hesitated—one father had come to the door and asked, bluntly, whether his son would be taught by *the woman*, and Rafael had answered, equally bluntly, that his son would be taught by the clockmaker who had beaten the magistrate of Manchester, and that if that was insufficient he might try the shop on Deansgate where the master drank. The boy stayed. He turned out to have a gift for pinions and no gift whatsoever for sweeping, which Rafael considered the correct distribution.

Marta kept the accounts. This had not been planned; it had simply happened, the way water finds its channel. Marta had always been the one who could look at a column of figures and see the lie in it, a skill she had learned, though neither sister said so aloud, from watching their father's ledgers during the years he carried Corvan's messages. Now she aimed the skill at honest targets. She negotiated with brass suppliers and refused short weights; she tracked every shilling of the settlement the city had eventually, grudgingly, paid to the Hartley widows; she kept a separate book, locked in the drawer with the depositions' fair copies, recording what the archive cost to maintain—paper, ink, postage, the occasional bribe disguised as a filing fee—and Rafael never asked where the money came from, because she knew: it came from the shop, willingly given, the family's complicity converted at last into the family's investment.

Their father came to the shop most days. He did not work at the bench—he claimed his eyes, but everyone knew it was his hands, which had begun to tremble in the way of men who had carried secrets too long and set them down too late—but he sat by the window with the light on him and inspected finished work, holding each piece up to the glass, and the apprentices learned to bring him their failures as well as their successes, because he was kinder about failures than Rafael was, and wiser about them too.

One evening in March he called Rafael over and held up a balance spring Susanna had made, her third attempt, still a hair off true.

"Do you remember," he said, "when you were nine, and you filed a pivot crooked, and you hid the wheel in the coal scuttle rather than show me?"

"I remember you finding it."

"I remember deciding not to have found it." He turned the spring in the light. "I thought, this child will hide whatever frightens her, and hiding will keep her alive, and I was glad of it. God forgive me, I was glad. I spent her whole childhood teaching her to be invisible and calling it love." He set the spring down. "Look at her now. Standing in a window with her name on the glass."

"Papa—"

"He was a courier," he said, to the spring, to the window, to no one. "Thirty years a courier. And she—" his hand, the steady one, gestured at the shop, at the sign, at the girl at the bench with the file, "—she is the message."

Rafael kissed the top of his head, which smelled as it always had of oil and tea, and went back to her bench, and neither of them said anything further, because everything had been said, and the shop around them went on ticking.

* * *

The observatory clock struck six, and Rafael, three streets away, heard it and checked her pocket watch against it out of habit, and smiled at the discrepancy of nothing.

Wren wrote to her monthly now. His letters were models of compression—a lifetime's habit, she supposed, of a man who had spent decades making sure his correspondence could survive being read by the wrong eyes. *The clock is well. The case is well. The visitor's book shows interest from certain parties which I receive with the hospitality due to interested parties. Your oil mixture has improved the third wheel's performance measurably. Come and wind it yourself sometime; my hands are seventy-eight years old.*

She went, the first Thursday of every month. It had become liturgy.

The double case was hers alone to open—Wren had insisted, formally, before witnesses, that the mechanism of concealment be known to one living person besides himself, and that person should be its maker. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth and laid in the space between the outer case and the movement, where no search would find them and no damp would reach them, lay the archive: Ibbetson's copied orders in their careful clerk's hand; the depositions of eleven mill workers taken in Rafael's best script; Sergeant Brand's sworn statement about the fire at Greenfield's warehouse in '39, the one that had been called an accident until it wasn't; Thomas Ibbetson's own testimony, dictated before his family's removal to Canada, where he now kept books for a grain merchant and slept, he wrote, better than he deserved.

And one more document, added last December. Wren's confession—not of crime but of silence. Forty years of knowing what the magistracy did and keeping the observatory's peace, keeping the instruments true and the record empty, telling himself that honest time was enough. *I kept the clocks and lost the hours,* he had written, and Rafael had read it aloud to him in the transit room, and the old man had listened with his eyes shut like a man hearing a verdict he had waited a long time to earn.

Corvan himself was suspended pending inquiry, and the inquiry moved with the speed of all official inquiries, which is to say glacially and with every appearance of motion. He had left Manchester in February for a house in Cheltenham, taking his velvet voice and his knowledge of Rafael's secret with him. She thought about that sometimes at night—that somewhere a comfortable man sat by a comfortable fire holding the one fact that could still hurt her, and that he did not use it because using it now would accomplish nothing except to confirm, publicly, the character already entered against him in the record. Her safety, she understood, was not a gift. It was a ruin's shadow. But ruins cast long shadows, and she had stopped flinching from them.

Besides. There were other levers now, and they were hers. Every deposition copied and posted to three cities. Every newspaper clipping Marta pasted into the archive book. Every apprentice taught, as part of the trade itself, to keep a logbook—to date the page, to state what was done, to sign it. *Because,* Rafael told them, standing at the bench with the lamplight on her scarred knuckles, *a thing written down and signed is a thing that happened. Everything else is weather.*

Susanna, who missed nothing, had asked her once: "Is that why you testified? So it would be weather-proof?"

Rafael had considered the question with the seriousness it deserved. "I testified," she said, "because four men were going to hang for a fire they didn't set, and I knew, and knowing is a debt. But yes. Also that. Write it down, Susanna. Both reasons. A record that only holds the noble parts isn't a record, it's a portrait."

* * *

Summer came to the mill district the way summer always came—smoke and sunlight fighting to a draw, the canal shining greasily green, children swimming where they shouldn't, the mills roaring their six-day roar. But something underneath had shifted, the way a foundation shifts: invisibly, permanently, load-bearing.

The strike leaders, freed, had gone back to organizing, and this time they organized with clerks. Ibbetson's example had spread like a rumor: men inside offices copying what passed through them, passing copies outward. The masters raged about it. The masters had discovered that a workforce which could read and write and keep its own minutes was a different animal entirely from one that could only shout, and several of the louder masters had begun, in the privacy of their clubs, to use the word *concession* without spitting.

Rafael took no credit. She had learned, from the newspapers softening week by week—from the arson becoming "the Hartley affair," the frame becoming "irregularities," Corvan becoming "a controversial figure"—exactly how fast the victors' ink dried and how pale it dried. The record was the only thing that didn't soften. That was why the archive grew, crate by crate, copy by copy, in the observatory's double case and in Brand's lodgings and in a bank box in Leeds under Marta's false maiden name. Redundancy, she called it, teaching Susanna to cut a spare gear for every critical wheel. *No single point of failure. Not in clocks. Not in truth.*

Brand stayed. There had never been a discussion about it; he had simply been present one morning, mending the broken shutter, and had continued to be present through the seasons, taking the room above the shop, walking the district at night on rounds nobody paid him for, teaching the older apprentices drill and discipline and, without ever naming it, the difference between fear and caution. On Sundays he and Rafael walked out along the canal, and he told her pieces of Greenfield's, the fire in '39, and she wrote them down afterward, and the archive grew by one page a week, and neither of them called it anything but what it was: two people keeping a watch.

In September, the master clock at the observatory marked its first full year of honest time since before the tampering. Wren held a small ceremony consisting of tea, one slice of seed cake, and a reading of the year's error: four seconds total. "Better than Greenwich manages, in this smoke," he said, and Rafael, who had rebuilt the escapement herself, allowed herself the luxury of agreeing.

They stood together at the west window afterward, looking down over the district as the lamps came on—thousands of them, workshop windows and cellar windows and the gaslights along the new streets, each one a small defiance of the dark.

"When I was young," Wren said, "I believed an observatory's purpose was to know the sky. Forty years I've revised it downward. Now I think it is to know the hour, and to say it truly, and to let whoever needs the truth come and check it against us." He tapped the case of the master clock, gently, the way one taps a trusted horse. "You've given it back its voice, Miss Nystrom. I intend to be buried close enough to hear it."

"You'll hear it for years yet."

"One hopes. One winds the days and hopes." He smiled his dry smile. "Go home. Your lantern's lit—I can see it from here. Third window from the corner. You leave it burning later than anyone."

"It's not for me," Rafael said. "It's so the ones coming late can find the door."

* * *

On the first anniversary of the inquest, the workshop held its first examination day. Five apprentices stood before five clocks of their own construction, and three neighbors and Hartley and a reporter from the *Manchester Guardian*—who had come expecting a curiosity and left, visibly recalibrating, with notes for a serious article—watched as each clock was opened, inspected, judged. Susanna's regulator ran to within a second and a half a week. She was sixteen now, and her hands had the stillness of someone who had found her life's work early, and when Rafael handed her the certificate she read it twice and folded it into her bodice over her heart, and Rafael recognized the gesture and said nothing and added a line to her own mental ledger: *one more keeper of the watch, entered into service.*

That night the shop emptied slowly. Father went home on Marta's arm, arguing amiably about the price of brass. Brand locked up the back and climbed to his room, his tread on the stairs still favoring the left side, still counting the steps out of habit. The apprentices scattered homeward into the warm dark, calling goodnights.

Rafael stayed last, as she always did. She made her round of the benches, checking the covers over the movements in progress, winding the shop regulators, setting each pendulum swinging with a fingertip's touch. Then she took the lantern down from its hook by the door—the same lantern, dented, that her father had carried through thirty years of other men's errands—and trimmed its wick and lit it and set it in the third window from the corner, where Wren could see it from the observatory hill, where the late-walkers could find the door, where anyone at all could look up from the smoky street and know that here, at least, one light was kept on purpose.

Outside, the district settled into its night noises: the canal lapping, a drunk singing two streets over, the far-off sigh of the mills banking their fires. Above it all, faint but exact, the observatory clock began to strike the hour, and Rafael stood in her doorway with her hand on the latch and counted the strokes—ten of them, unhurried, incorruptible, falling on the town like small sure footsteps.

Somewhere in the dark, she knew, a historian was already writing the version of these years that would suit the people still in power. Let him write. The paper would yellow and the editions would crumble, and the depositions would sit in their oilcloth in the double case, patient as springs, waiting for the next hand brave enough to open the case and read.

She blew out the lamp over the bench but left the lantern burning.

It was, after all, the whole of her philosophy now, distilled to a single flame behind glass: that the dark is large, and the dark is old, and the dark does not need to be defeated—only outlasted, night by night, by whoever is willing to keep the light lit and the record true, and to put their own name at the bottom of the page.
