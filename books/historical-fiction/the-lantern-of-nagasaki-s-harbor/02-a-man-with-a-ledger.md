## 2. A Man with a Ledger

The census season came to Nagasaki the way the fog came off the harbor—without announcement, without malice, and without any intention of leaving quickly.

Mirabel Marchetti saw him first as a smudge on the cliff path, a dark figure climbing through the morning haze with the unhurried gait of a man who had never once been late for anything. She was on the gallery deck with a chamois cloth in her hand, working the salt haze off the lantern room's outer panes, and she watched him come for the better part of twenty minutes. He walked as though the cliff belonged to him. That was the first thing she noticed, and the first thing she disliked.

The second thing was the ledger. He carried it under his arm, bound in oxblood leather, thick as a Bible and handled with the same reverence. Census men had been coming up the headland since spring—she had signed three documents already this season, each one more elaborate than the last, each one asking a few questions more. But none of them had carried a ledger like that. The others had carried forms. This man carried a *record*.

She finished the pane she was working and did not hurry. That was a discipline she had learned from Tomas, who used to say that a lighthouse keeper's greatest virtue was not courage but patience—that the sea punished haste more reliably than it punished cowardice, and men were no different. She set the chamois over the rail, went down the iron stairs, and opened the door before his knock could land.

He stood on the step with his hat in one hand and the ledger balanced on his forearm, and he smiled the way a customs officer smiles: warmly, and with arithmetic behind it.

"Mrs. Marchetti." He said her name as though confirming it rather than greeting it. "Elias Thorn. Bureau of Household Registration, Nagasaki Prefecture. I wonder if I might trouble you for a few minutes."

"You've climbed a hundred and sixty steps to trouble me," Mirabel said. "You may as well come in and sit down while you do it."

The smile deepened a degree. "Thank you. I will."

* * *

She gave him tea because refusing would have been a statement, and statements were the one currency she could not afford. He sat at the kitchen table with his ledger squared to the table's edge, his pen laid parallel to it, and he looked around the room the way she had seen buyers look through a house after a death—gently, thoroughly, missing nothing.

The kitchen was small and immaculate. A copper kettle. Two plates in the rack, one chipped. A man's coat on a peg by the door, twenty years out of fashion, kept there because taking it down would have been another kind of statement. On the shelf above the stove, a photograph in a silver frame: Tomas, thirty years old, squinting into Genoese sunlight, his arm around a young woman whose face had not yet learned what it would cost to be happy.

"You've been here a long time," Thorn said.

"Twenty-two years. Since 1876."

"Your husband was the keeper before you."

"He was the keeper. I am the keeper now. He died in '93." She poured the tea. "You'll have all of this in your book already. The prefecture sent a man in April."

"Yamaguchi-san." Thorn nodded. "A thorough man. But the prefecture's records and the harbor office's records are two different animals, Mrs. Marchetti, and lately they've been eating each other." He opened the ledger. "I'm here to reconcile them."

He turned pages with a dry, papery whisper. Mirabel drank her tea and watched his hands. They were clean hands, careful hands, the hands of a man who had never hauled rope or bled on ironwork. There was ink in the creases of his fingers, worked in deep, the stain of a man who wrote all day and dreamed in it too.

"Here we are." He turned the ledger toward her. "The Marchetti household, Nagasaki headland station. Registered 1876. Head of household, Tomas Marchetti, subject of the Kingdom of Italy, occupation: keeper of lights. Spouse: Mirabel Marchetti, born—"

"Born in Genoa," she said. "You can copy it from the parish record if the prefecture's lost it. They seem to lose a great deal lately."

The barb landed somewhere behind his eyes, though his face didn't move. "Born in Genoa," he repeated, writing it anyway. "And the household as it stands today?"

"One person. Myself."

"One person." He looked at the coat on the peg. He looked at the two plates in the rack. He looked at the second chair, which she kept pushed slightly beneath the table as if it had simply never been moved. "No servants?"

"I keep a lighthouse, Mr. Thorn. Servants would be redundant."

"No boarders. No relations visiting. No—assistants?"

"The lamp doesn't require an assistant. It requires a keeper who doesn't sleep much." She smiled at him, and it cost her something. "You've climbed a hundred and sixty steps to count me. You've counted me. There's one."

Thorn closed the ledger gently, the way a man closes a door on a sleeping child, and folded his hands on top of it. "May I be frank with you, Mrs. Marchetti?"

"I'd be disappointed in you if you weren't."

"The census this year is not only a census." He said it quietly, almost kindly, as though offering her a warning rather than a threat. "There are irregularities in the harbor records. A ledger has gone missing from the harbor office—pages of contacts, shipping arrangements, names of foreign nationals and their local associates. The prefecture takes a dim view of its disappearance. There are men in this city who take an even dimmer view of what might be written in it."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, a gull cried once and was answered by another, farther off, and then the wind took both sounds away.

"That sounds like a matter for the harbor police," Mirabel said.

"It is. And the harbor police are very busy." He picked up his pen, turned it once between his fingers, and set it down again. "I'm told your husband was a well-known man on the waterfront. An Italian who spoke Japanese like a Nagasaki man, who drank with captains, who kept accounts for half the coastal trade out of this kitchen. Is that true?"

"It's true that he drank with captains. It's true that he talked too much. It's true that he'd have traded you his coat for a good story about a storm." She looked at the coat on the peg. "He's been dead five years, Mr. Thorn. Whatever he kept in this kitchen, he kept in his head, and his head went into the ground with him."

"That's what I told them," Thorn said. "That's what I keep telling them." He rose, and she rose with him, and for a moment they stood across the table from each other like two fencers who had agreed, without saying so, that the bout was over. "Thank you for the tea. You'll see me again, I'm afraid. The reconciliation takes time, and the headland stations are the last on my list."

"Then you'll save the best climb for last."

"I'm told the view is worth it." He put on his hat at the door, and paused there, one hand on the frame, not looking at her but at the coat. "Your husband's?"

"Yes."

"He was a large man."

"Larger than life. Everyone said so."

Thorn nodded slowly, and Mirabel understood, with the cold, precise clarity of a woman who had spent twenty years reading weather, that he had not been asking about the coat at all. "Good day, Mrs. Marchetti. Keep the light burning."

"It's what I'm for," she said, and closed the door behind him, and stood with her hand on the wood until his footsteps faded down the iron stairs, and then for a long time after that.

* * *

Elias Thorn counted the steps on the way down. It was a habit. He counted everything—stairs, names, discrepancies, the days since his brother's funeral, which stood at four thousand one hundred and six, not that anyone asked. A man who counted could not be lied to, because a lie was only ever a number that didn't come out right.

The Marchetti household did not come out right.

He had read the file twice on the boat across the harbor, and once more at the foot of the cliff, and he had walked up those hundred and sixty steps knowing three things. First: Tomas Marchetti had been the harbor's most connected foreigner, a man whose signature appeared in the missing ledger no fewer than eleven times, always in the column marked *contacts, personal*. Second: Mirabel Marchetti had signed the prefecture's household register every year since her husband's death, and every year the entry read the same—*one person, no dependents, no visitors of record*. Third: the harbor office's own visitor log, the one that had *not* gone missing, recorded a launch to the headland station on the fourteenth of August, 1895, carrying two passengers, names entered in the old clerk's careless hand as *Capt. H. and family*.

And Captain Hara Tetsuo's family, as of this spring, had no fixed address at all.

Thorn reached the bottom of the stairs and stood a moment on the black rocks, looking back up at the white tower. The light above it was dark in the daylight, of course, but the glass caught the sun and threw it back in a long white blade, and he had to look away.

They thought he was a clerk. That was useful, and he encouraged it. A thin, precise, unremarkable man with ink in his fingers and a ledger under his arm—men talked in front of him the way they talked in front of furniture. But Elias Thorn was not hunting names in general. The prefecture wanted the missing ledger; the prefecture could want what it liked. Thorn wanted one page. One list. Eleven signatures, and the names written beside them, and above all the name that the old harbor clerk had recorded on the fourteenth of August, 1895, in a hand so careless it might have been deliberate.

Because his brother had died with a list too. A shorter one. A list of men who had signed their loyalty to foreign interests in a year when loyalty was being weighed in rice, and the men on that list had been ruined one by one, and his brother—foolish, loyal, half-Italian himself by blood if not by name—had defended them until the defending destroyed him. Thorn had been nineteen. He had learned what a list could do. He had learned it so thoroughly that he had made lists his life's work, and told himself every morning for twenty years that this time the list was on the right side.

The woman up there was lying. He was certain of it, and certainty was the one thing he trusted more than arithmetic. The coat on the peg, kept twenty years out of fashion. The second plate. The chair tucked in like a guest was expected. Grief explained a coat. Grief did not explain a plate set for a man who had been dead five years.

But she was lying well. That was the complication. She had lied the way a keeper keeps a light—steadily, economically, without a flicker—and she had given him nothing to carry down the cliff except a dead man's coat and a cup of very good tea.

Thorn took out his notebook and wrote, in his small, exact hand: *M. Marchetti — composed. Practiced. Hiding something — not necessarily the ledger.*

He looked at the sentence for a long moment. Then he added: *Not necessarily.*

The tide was turning in the harbor below, the water in the roads going slack and then beginning to move, and somewhere out past the breakwater a coal barge was coming in on the flood, low in the water, riding heavy. Thorn watched it for a while without knowing why, and then he put his notebook away, settled the ledger under his arm, and started along the cliff path toward the city, already composing in his head the report he would not quite write, and the questions he would save for his next visit.

Behind him, high on the headland, the great lens turned in its bath of mercury, patient as a heart, keeping its own count of the hours.

And in a kitchen that smelled of tea and brass polish, Mirabel Marchetti took Tomas's coat down from the peg, held it against her chest for a moment with her eyes closed, and hung it in the sea chest instead—the first thing she had moved in five years, and not the last.

The census season had come. The fog had come with it. And neither of them, she understood now, had any intention of leaving quickly.
