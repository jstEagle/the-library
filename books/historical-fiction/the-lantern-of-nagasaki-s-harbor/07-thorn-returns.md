## 7. Thorn Returns

The boy had been with her nine days when she learned that fear has a sound.

It was not the sound she expected. She had imagined it would be loud—a shout from the water, boots on the gravel path, a knock like a fist on God's own door. Instead it came as a silence: the gulls lifting off the rocks all at once, without wind to move them, and the channel below gone suddenly empty of fishing boats.

Mirabel stood at the parapet above Nagasaki's harbor and watched the birds scatter and understood.

"Kenji," she said quietly, not turning. "The trapdoor."

He was in the gallery behind her, sleeves rolled, learning the names of the gears—she had been teaching him the clockwork rotation that turned the lens, the slow patient machinery of Tomas's pride—and he went still the way animals go still, all his young weight settling into the floorboards.

"The trapdoor," he repeated. Not a question. He had grown up in a navy household; he knew what an empty harbor meant.

"Beneath the lantern room. Under the floor panels by the oil store. There's a space for the counterweights—it's shallow, but you'll fit if you fold." She was already moving, pulling the canvas apron off its hook, wiping lamp soot from his hands herself because he would forget, because he was nineteen and nineteen forgot things. "If I whistle twice, stay down no matter what you hear. If I scream—" she met his eyes, and made herself finish it, "—if I scream, you run for the seaward stairs and you swim if you must. Do you understand me?"

"You shouldn't lie for me," he said. It was the first time he had said anything of the kind, and it landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. "Okafor-san is coming. The barge. You said after the next storm—"

"After the next storm," she agreed. "Which means today, we are only a widow and her lamp. Go."

He went. She heard the panel lift and settle, soft as a book closing, and then the lantern room was hers alone, humming faintly with the clockwork, smelling of brass polish and rapeseed oil and the salt that got into everything on this headland and never left.

She counted to sixty. Then she took up her polishing cloth, because there was nothing in the world so innocent as a woman cleaning glass, and began to work the great lens in long, even strokes, and waited for Elias Thorn to climb her hill.

* * *

He came on foot, which surprised her. She had expected the census office to send a boat; instead he appeared on the cliff path in a grey Western suit gone dark at the knees with sweat, hatless, carrying nothing but a leather folio and a paper bag whose shape suggested rice crackers bought at the last village shop. A peace offering, or the appearance of one. With Thorn she was learning, the two were often the same thing.

"You keep a hard house to reach, Mrs. Marchetti," he called up, when he was still twenty steps below the station door. His Japanese was good—better than hers in some registers, worse in others, the vowels too round where a native tongue would flatten them. "I nearly turned back twice."

"The light doesn't come down the mountain," Mirabel said. "People do or they don't."

"And yet here I am." He smiled. It was a careful smile, built like the man himself: correct, unremarkable, designed to be forgotten. "May I trouble you for water? The office sent me out again about the coastal returns. There are discrepancies." He said the word lightly, the way one says *weather*. "Nothing alarming. Paper dislikes gaps, that's all. Paper wants everything accounted for."

She brought him water in the good cup—the one with the blue rim, Tomas's mother's—and set out the crackers he had carried up himself, which struck her as either very humble or very shrewd, and possibly both. He sat at her kitchen table as though he had sat at a hundred such tables, and perhaps he had. Census men, customs men, health inspectors: the empire moved through kitchens like this, politely, taking inventory of lives.

"The coastal stations," he began, unfolding a printed form, "house foreign nationals. Italians, Russians, the occasional Englishman with more money than sense. The ministry wants a current accounting. Who resides, who visits, who—" a small pause, delicate as a scalpel finding its line, "—who *stays*."

"My husband is dead four years," Mirabel said. "I reside alone. You have my return from the spring. Nothing has changed."

"Nothing?"

"Nothing."

Thorn wrote something. She could not see what. That was the trick of the man, she thought—he made the act of writing itself a threat, so that every silence of hers seemed to be entering the record against her.

"The spring return lists one resident," he said. "Female, forty-one, widow of Tomaso Marchetti, keeper. But Mrs. Marchetti—" he looked up, and his eyes were mild and bottomless, like the water over the shoals, "—the supply manifests show double rations drawn since August. Oil, rice, salt fish. The chandlery at Dejima notes it. The harbor office notes it. A woman alone eats very little, and burns only what the lamp requires. Yet your draws have increased by nearly half."

The clockwork ticked overhead. Somewhere beneath it, folded into a space barely wider than a coffin, a nineteen-year-old boy was listening to every word.

Mirabel discovered, in that moment, something about lying that she had never known before: that it was not hard. That all her life she had believed honesty was a discipline and falsehood a failure of nerve, and she had been wrong. The truth was the thing that trembled. The lie, done properly, was stillness.

"I've had a cat," she said.

Thorn blinked. For the first time since she had known him, the careful face lost its footing—not much, a half-second, a man stepping on a stair that wasn't where he'd built it.

"A cat," he repeated.

"A large one. A ship's cat, off a Norwegian brig that put in during the typhoon scare. The captain couldn't feed her and asked if I'd take her on. Cats eat fish, Mr. Thorn, and cats eat rice if the fish runs short, and I draw rations the way the chandlery sells them—in lots, not portions." She shrugged, and let a little dry humor into it, the way a widow might. "You may inspect the larder if you like. You will find fish bones. Whether they belonged to the cat or to me, I decline to say."

"And the cat?"

"Gone home with the brig. They came back for her in September." She met his eyes and did not look away, and understood that this, too, was part of the craft: the lie is not in the words, it is in the refusal to flinch while saying them. "I'm sorry, Mr. Thorn. Was there something else? Only the tide turns at four, and the lens wants polishing before the evening watch."

For a long moment he studied her, and she felt the study like weather, like pressure before rain. Then he did the last thing she expected. He laughed—a short, genuine bark of amusement, quickly mastered, but real.

"A cat," he said again, shaking his head, and wrote something else in the margin of his form, and she would give a year of her life to know whether it was *cat, verified* or *cat, noted with contempt*. "Mrs. Marchetti, do you know why I keep coming up this hill? It isn't the rations. Rations are nothing; half the coast draws double and calls it mice." He closed the folio. "It's the ledger."

The word went through her like cold water through cloth.

"There was a harbor contact ledger kept at the customs annex," Thorn went on, conversational now, packing away his papers with unhurried neatness. "Names of foreign residents, their correspondents, their debts, their associations. Naval officers, merchants, missionaries—who broke bread with whom. It disappeared in July. Before it disappeared, someone copied pages out of it. And those copies are circulating, Mrs. Marchetti, among people who have no business having them." He stood, settled his jacket, and smiled the careful smile again. "Your late husband signed the annex register eleven times between 1893 and 1896. Eleven visits. More than any other keeper on this coast. I simply wonder what a lighthouse man wanted so often with the customs house."

"Lamp oil contracts," Mirabel said. "Tomas handled procurement for three stations. Ask anyone at the annex."

"I have." A pause, precisely measured. "They don't remember him."

The clockwork turned. The sea breathed against the rocks below, patient, enormous, keeping its own counsel.

"Then the annex keeps poor records," she said, "which would explain how they lost their ledger."

Something flickered across his face—annoyance, or admiration, or the place where the two live together in people who are dangerous. He put on his hat.

"Storm season's opening," he said at the door. "There's a low pressure system out past the Goto Islands. The office may requisition shelter at the coastal stations for travelers caught out. I hope you won't object to company, Mrs. Marchetti."

"This house has sheltered strangers before," she said. "It's what lighthouses are for."

"Yes," said Elias Thorn. "So I've begun to think."

* * *

She waited until his footsteps faded down the cliff path, until the gulls resettled on their rocks, until the fishing boats crept back into the channel one by one like sheep returning to a field. Then she went up to the lantern room, knelt, and lifted the panel.

Kenji lay folded in the counterweight space, knees to chest, eyes open, and he had been crying without noise, which was somehow worse than sobbing.

"He's gone," Mirabel said. "Come out slowly. Your legs will be asleep."

He unfolded himself joint by joint, wincing, and sat on the floor of the lantern room with the great lens towering over them both, throwing its slow wheel of light even in daylight, useless and faithful.

"He talked about my father," Kenji said hoarsely. "I heard it through the boards. The ledger. Papa's name is in a ledger somewhere, and men like that read it the way priests read sins." His hands were shaking. Nineteen years old, and his hands shaking. "Mrs. Marchetti—if they find me here, they won't just take me. They'll take you. You know that. Whatever he decides upstairs, whatever mercy—"

"Stop." She said it gently, but she said it, and he stopped. She sat down beside him on the warm boards, in the smell of oil and brass, and looked out at the harbor where the afternoon light was turning gold and the patrol boat lay at anchor exactly where it had lain all week, a black seed in the bright water.

"When I was seventeen," she said, "in Genoa, my father hid a man in our cellar for six weeks. A printer who'd written things the government disliked. My mother complained about the smell of the ink. That was her only complaint—the ink. I used to bring the man bread and ask him questions, and once I asked him if he was afraid, and he said—" she smiled at the memory, and the memory hurt, and she let it hurt, "—he said, *child, fear is rent. Everyone pays it. The only question is what you get for your money.*"

Kenji looked at her.

"I paid rent for four years, hiding in this house, being nobody's widow but my own. Quiet, correct, invisible. And then you knocked on my door soaked through, and I found out what the money buys." She stood, and brushed off her skirt, and became again the keeper, brisk and practical, because grief disciplined into habit was the only way she knew to carry it. "Up. You'll learn the mercury bath tonight, and the emergency shutter rig, and if you're going to hide under my floor you'll at least be able to work the lamp above it. Okafor comes after the storm. Three days, maybe four."

"And if Thorn comes back before then?"

She looked at the boy—at Tomas's promise wearing a young man's face—and told the truth, because he deserved one clean truth to balance her clean lie.

"Then you'll go down that hole again," she said, "and I'll tell him about the cat."

* * *

That night, after Kenji had learned the mercury bath and burned his fingers twice and laughed once despite himself, Mirabel climbed to the gallery alone and stood in the wind looking down at the black water.

The patrol boat rode at anchor, riding lights burning. Somewhere in the city below, Hara's wife was hidden in a stranger's back room. Somewhere in a customs drawer, a page bore Tomas's signature beside names that were now contraband. Somewhere out past the Goto Islands, clouds were stacking themselves into the shape of what was coming.

And in the morning, she knew—knew the way one knows weather in one's bones—she would have to walk down into Nagasaki, into the fish market where tongues wagged loose over scales and ice, and find out what the harbor already knew about her.

Because Elias Thorn had written something in the margin of his form. And rent, as the printer said, came due.
