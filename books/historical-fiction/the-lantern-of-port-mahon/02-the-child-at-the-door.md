## 2. The Child at the Door

The storm came in on a Tuesday, which I mention only because the logbook demands a day and because Tuesdays had never done me any harm before. By Wednesday morning it had rearranged its opinion of me entirely.

I had been keeping the light through three nights without proper sleep — the lamp needed trimming every ninety minutes in that wind, and the wind did not believe in schedules. The sea off Port Mahon had gone from gray to a color I can only describe as furious, all white teeth and black gums, and the tower hummed like a struck bell from top to bottom. Twice I checked the moorings of the supply shed. Once I checked my own sanity by talking aloud to the Fresnel lens, which is a habit Tomas would have mocked and which I therefore permitted myself freely.

*Hold*, I told the light. *You hold, and I'll hold.*

It was an arrangement we had. The lamp and I.

By dawn on Thursday the storm had not broken so much as paused to consider how best to break me instead. I went down the hundred and nine steps with oil-stained hands and the particular exhaustion that sits behind the eyes like sand. There was bread, there was tea made of something that was only nominally tea, and there was the logbook, open to a fresh page, waiting for my tidy hand: *Wind NW by W, force 11. Sea mountainous. Lamp burned steady.*

That was when I heard the knocking.

Now, understand — nobody knocks at a lighthouse. People arrive at lighthouses the way weather arrives, or they don't arrive at all. In two years of widowhood I had trained myself out of flinching at sounds, because the tower produces sounds the way a drunk produces excuses: constantly, and none of them meaningful. But this was a knock. Small. Fists, not wind. And it came from below, from the door at the base of the stairs, the one that opened onto rock and spray and nothing else for half a mile of drowned coast.

I took the lantern hook down from its peg — old habits being the skeleton you hang your courage on — and went down the last flight with my heart doing arithmetic it hadn't been asked to do.

* * *

She was perhaps eight years old, though it was hard to say, because she was also approximately sixty percent seawater. She stood on the bottom step with her back against the doorframe as if the door itself had been holding her up, and when I opened it she did not scream, did not cry, did not do any of the things a child is supposed to do upon being found. She simply looked at me with an expression I recognized, because I wore it myself some mornings in front of the shaving mirror: the look of someone who has used up all their astonishment and has nothing left but logistics.

Her lips were blue. Her hair was plastered to her skull in ropes. Her coat — a man's coat cut down badly, the sleeves rolled four times — dripped onto the stone in a small patient puddle.

"Where in God's name did you come from?" I asked, which was, I grant you, a stupid question, since the answer was visibly *the sea*, and the sea does not give things back. That's the first rule of this coast. The sea takes; the harbor keeps what washes up; and no one asks the water to explain itself.

She didn't answer. Of course she didn't. Her jaw was chattering too hard for words, and besides, I had the distinct impression — even then, even soaked and shaking — that silence was not something happening *to* her. It was something she was *doing*. A discipline. Wherever she'd learned it, she'd learned it well enough to make me, a grown woman with a dead husband and a functioning suspicion of everything, feel briefly outmatched.

"Inside," I said. "Before the sea changes its mind."

She came in the way a cat comes into a room it intends to own.

* * *

Here is what I know about children, which is nearly nothing: they are heavier than they look, wet ones especially, and they weigh more still when they are carrying something they refuse to put down. I got her out of the coat by the fire in the keeper's room — she fought me for exactly as long as it took to establish that fighting was expected of her, then went limp and let it happen, which frightened me more than the struggle would have — and that was when I felt it.

The left side of the coat was wrong. Stiff. There was a shape sewn into the lining, flat and rectangular, about the size of a prayer book, and it had been sewn by someone in a hurry with strong thread and stronger intentions. My fingers found it and stopped, the way your hand stops on a stove.

I looked at the girl. The girl looked at the fire.

There is a moment — I have read about it in novels, and I can now report firsthand — when your life quietly divides into the part before a thing and the part after it, and the hinge of the division is so small you could miss it if you blinked. Mine was a rectangle of oilcloth inside a child's coat, and the sound of the fire popping, and the rain hammering the tower above us like a fist on a table.

I did not open it. Let the record show that much. I wrapped her in Tomas's old blanket — the gray one, the one I still could not bring myself to wash because it smelled, faintly, stubbornly, of him — and I set the coat over a chair to dry with the seam facing the wall, and I told myself I would decide what kind of person I was going to be after she'd had soup.

The soup was fish stock and barley and it was terrible and she ate all of it, slowly, watching me between spoonfuls the way you watch a large animal whose intentions are unclear. I ate mine across the table. Neither of us spoke. The storm filled in the conversation admirably.

When she finished, she pushed the bowl away with both hands — a precise, deliberate gesture, almost formal — and folded those hands in her lap, and looked at me. Waiting. As though I were the one who had washed up on someone's steps and owed an accounting.

"You'll sleep," I said. "Then we'll see about who you are."

Something moved across her face at the word *who* — quick as a fish under a boat — and was gone.

* * *

She slept in my bed and I sat in Tomas's chair with the mended netting in my lap that I never actually mended, just held, and listened to the storm work the tower like a lockpick. Every quarter hour I told myself I would check the coat. Every quarter hour I did not. This is called discipline, or cowardice, depending on the hour, and I have become fluent in both.

Toward midnight she cried out once in her sleep — a single syllable, clipped short, the first half of a word amputated — and then went silent again so completely that I crossed the room to make sure she was breathing. She was. Children breathe the way tides move: without anyone's permission.

It was while standing over her, feeling like an intruder in my own bedroom, that I noticed her hand was closed around something. I did not pry it loose. I want that noted too, since later no one will believe how much I didn't do that night. But when she turned in her sleep the object slipped free onto the blanket, and the lamplight found it, and I confess I looked.

A note. Folded twice, water-spotted, written in a man's hand — a hurried, educated hand, the letters leaning like men running:

*Forget this face.*

No name. No direction. Nothing but the imperative, sitting there on my blanket like a stone dropped in my lap by a stranger.

I stood there a long time. Rain on glass. Fire settling. A child breathing below me with a face somebody had already ordered forgotten, and a sealed packet sewn into her coat like a tumor, and the whole apparatus of my quiet, orderly, grief-shaped life balanced on the head of a pin I could feel turning underfoot.

Here is the thing about Port Mahon. The war had stayed, for two years, a rumor with a radio voice. It took our men — it had taken Tomas, in its indirect, deniable way, the sea being merely the war's local agent — but it had never come up our road. We traded with it, mourned it, cursed it at the fish market, and believed, the way coastal people believe, that geography was a favor the land did for us.

Standing over that sleeping girl with a stranger's handwriting in my hand, I understood that the favor had been recalled.

* * *

Morning came gray and lower-voiced, the storm down to a sulk. I had decided, somewhere around three o'clock, on a policy: I would treat the packet as I treated everything else in my keeping — as cargo awaiting instructions. I would not open it. I would ask Brannoc. Brannoc knew everyone, owed me favors, and possessed the great coastal virtue of knowing which questions not to repeat. It was a good plan. It had the two qualities I prize most in plans: simplicity and delay.

I was still holding to it when I took the coat down from the chair to shake the damp out, and the stitching caught the light.

Whoever had sewn that pocket had used sail thread and a sailor's stitch — I knew the hand of it, I'd watched Tomas close a torn mainsail with exactly that flat, urgent pattern — and whoever had sewn it had also, in the last inch, run out of time. The final stitches were a scramble. And beneath them, where the needle had punched through the oilcloth itself, a corner of what lay inside had printed itself faintly into the waxed fabric. Not words. A stamp. An official seal pressed into paper, the ghost of it showing through like a bruise through a sleeve.

Naval wax. Naval paper. On a child.

I set the coat down. I walked to the window. Below the tower the harbor was recovering itself, boats righting, gulls resuming their extortion rackets, the world performing normalcy with the desperate energy of a liar changing the subject. Somewhere out past the point, under that same recovering water, lay whatever the sea had given her up from. Boats sink. Children do not swim ashore alone at night in a force-eleven gale and stand on a stranger's step with their fists already raised to knock. Someone had put her there. Someone had chosen my door — the farthest door, the last door, the one at the end of the road where a woman lives alone with a light and a dead man's blankets — and had trusted, or gambled, or calculated, that Xanthe Delacroix would not turn a child back into the storm.

They had guessed correctly, damn them. That was the humiliating part. Whoever they were, they had read me like a chart.

Behind me, floorboards creaked. She was awake, standing in the doorway wrapped in the gray blanket, hair drying into a pale wild halo, watching me watch the sea. Her eyes went from me to the coat in my hands and back again, and I saw her do the same arithmetic I had done all night — measure the distance, weigh the exits, calculate whether the woman by the window was a harbor or a hazard.

I held up the coat, seam outward, and said nothing. Then I folded it, laid it on the chair, and pointed to the table, where breakfast waited: bread, the last of the honey, tea that continued its impersonation.

"Eat," I said. "We're going to talk about what you're carrying. And this time—" I tapped the table, once, the way Brannoc taps a barrel to hear if it's honest, "—you're going to tell me your name."

She sat. She picked up the bread. And she looked at me with those old, unchildlike eyes, and shook her head — slow, deliberate, final — the way you refuse a king.

Which is, of course, precisely when I should have known that whatever was sewn into that coat was going to cost me everything I had.
