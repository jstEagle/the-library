## 3. Instructions to Forget

The storm spent itself sometime before dawn, the way storms do — not with a surrender but a slow, sulking withdrawal, like a guest who has broken your furniture and leaves by the window. By morning the sea had settled into that bruised, exhausted swell it keeps after violence, and I climbed the tower to trim the wick and found my hands steady, which surprised me. They are not always steady. There is no shame in this; there is only the work, and the work does not care what your hands remember.

The girl slept in my bed.

I had put her there at some hour I cannot precisely name, after the fire had been built up and the brandy — Tomas's brandy, hoarded against a celebration that never came — had been forced past her blue lips in increments small enough not to choke her. She had taken it the way she took everything: silently, obediently, with those enormous grey eyes fixed on my face as though I were a page she was trying to memorize before someone tore it out. Then she slept, and did not stir even when the wind found the shutters again and worried them like a dog with a bone.

I sat at the table with her coat across my knees.

I want to be honest about what I felt, because honesty is the one discipline I have never managed to abandon, though God knows I've tried. What I felt was not curiosity. Curiosity is a warm thing, a domestic thing; curiosity is wondering whether the herring boats will run on Thursday. What I felt was colder and more precise. The packet sewn into the lining was rectangular, hard-edged, heavier than paper alone would account for — oilcloth over something stiffer beneath, board or leather — and it had been stitched in with a sailor's palm-and-needle haste, the thread doubled, the knots meant never to be undone by gentle hands. Whoever had put it there had expected the coat to be searched, torn, possibly burned, and had wanted the contents to survive all three.

And they had written *forget this face*.

Four words on a scrap of paper gone soft with seawater, the ink feathered at the edges but legible, the hand cramped and hurried. Not a plea. A plea would have said *protect her*, or *forgive me*, or any of the things people say when they are drowning in the ordinary sense. This was an instruction. It had the flat authority of a signal lamp: do this, and do not ask why.

I am, by trade and temperament, a keeper of instructions. The Service issues them in books; the dead issue them in silence; the sea issues them constantly and expects them obeyed on pain of everything. So I did what any sensible woman does with an instruction she intends to disobey.

I made tea first.

There is a version of myself — the one who existed two winters ago, before the sea took Tomas and left me his grief like an inheritance nobody wants — who might have sat with that packet for days, turning it over, telling herself stories about ship manifests and a father's letters. That woman is gone. The woman who replaced her knows that waiting is how the dangerous things win. You do not let a leak wait for fair weather. You do not let a question sit in the house overnight with a child sleeping ten feet away.

But first, tea. And bread. Because whatever was in that packet, I would read it fed and warm, with my back to the wall and the door in my sightline, and I would read it as a keeper reads a logbook: looking for what changed.

* * *

She woke while the kettle was coming up.

Not gradually, the way children usually surface from sleep, but all at once, upright, silent, her eyes going around the room in a fast professional sweep — door, window, stairs, me — before anything so soft as recognition arrived in them. I have seen men come out of torpedoes' dreams like that. I have done it myself. It is not a child's way of waking, and I noted it the way I note everything now, which is to say: with dread dressed up as competence.

"Tea," I said, because I have discovered that in the absence of any other common language, the offering of food will carry you further than words. "And bread. There's butter if the mice have left any."

She looked at the tea. She looked at me. Her mouth opened, and I thought — foolishly, hopefully — *now she'll speak*, and what came out instead was nothing. Her mouth closed. She shook her head once, a small economical motion, and got out of bed, and stood beside it with her hands at her sides like a soldier awaiting inspection.

"You have a name," I said. "Everyone does. Even lighthouses have names, and we're only lamps in towers."

Nothing. But something moved behind her eyes — not refusal exactly. Refusal has a texture, a set of the jaw. This was more like a person standing at the edge of a bridge and finding the planks missing. She wasn't refusing to tell me her name. She was unable to find the road to it.

"All right," I said. "We'll call you Tuesday, then. You arrived on a Tuesday."

That earned me the ghost of a reaction — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile lives before anyone has taught it to come out. I counted it a victory and did not press. Pressing, I have learned, is for dough and for enemies.

She ate standing up. I noticed that too. Children sit when they feel safe; she ate the way gulls eat, quick and watchful, one eye always on the door. When she finished she carried her plate to the basin without being asked and washed it, thoroughly, with a competence that sat wrong on eight-year-old bones. Somewhere she had been trained. Or somewhere she had learned that usefulness is the price of being allowed to stay, which is a training of its own, and a crueler one.

While she dried the plate I put the kettle back on the burner, and this time I set the packet's corner near the spout, where the steam gathered thickest.

* * *

Wax, I should say, is an honest material. It yields to heat the way a witness yields to patience — slowly, then all at once, and afterward it can never pretend it held its shape. The seal on the packet was navy wax, dark, stamped with a device I didn't recognize: an anchor within a wreath, and letters around the rim worn nearly smooth. Not an official dispatch stamp, or not only. Something older, or more private.

I steamed it until the wax went dull and soft, and I worked a thin knife under the edge — Tomas's knife, another inheritance — and the seal lifted whole, and inside were papers. Dispatches. Naval dispatches, in official hands, several of them, dated across the autumn just past, and between them a covering letter in the same cramped hand that had written *forget this face*.

I read the letter first. It was short. It began mid-thought, as though earlier pages had been burned:

*—cannot send this through channels. Every channel I trusted is either complicit or watched. If you are reading this, then the girl reached you, which means the boat reached the shore, which means one thing tonight went as intended. Do not look for me. Do not let her be seen. What follows is the record of the convoy Meridian — eleven ships, grain and coal, sailing the outer channel on the night of the ninth — and of the orders that stripped her escort forty miles from port and held every patrol vessel at anchor until the U-boats had finished. The orders bear signatures. I copied the signatures. I was adjutant to the man whose signature appears most often, and I tell you plainly: he knew. He was told the escort was needed. He signed the order releasing it anyway, and the reason is in the third enclosure, and the reason has a price attached.*

*I was not brave. Remember that about me. I did this because I could not sleep, and then I did it properly because I could not live otherwise. My daughter must forget my face. That is not sentiment. Men have already come asking for her. If they find her, they will not ask questions; they will tidy.*

*Burn nothing. Show no one. Trust no one in uniform.*

*— R.*

I sat with that for a long moment while the kettle ticked itself cool and the girl — Tuesday, I had decided, and the name was already sticking — stood drying the same clean plate over and over, watching me with those grey eyes.

Then I read the dispatches.

I will not reproduce them here; the logbook is not the place, and besides, the words have a way of repeating themselves in the head without any help from ink. But I will say what they said, plainly, because plainness is the only armor I own.

On the night of the ninth of October, a convoy of eleven merchant vessels sailed the outer channel with a full escort — four patrol craft and a converted yacht, adequate, unremarkable, the ordinary arithmetic of keeping grain ships alive. At midday on the eighth, an order released that escort to "urgent duties elsewhere." The urgent duties, per the second enclosure, consisted of sitting at anchor thirty miles away with steam up and no orders to move. On the night of the ninth, the convoy — slow, fat, lit against regulations by a signal error that was no error — proceeded down a channel whose minefields had been mapped and reported, and the report had been marked *received* by the same office that released the escort.

Eleven ships. The dispatches listed them. Grain, coal, timber, and men — the numbers were there, in columns, in the beautiful indifferent handwriting of clerks. Two hundred and six souls, reduced to a sum, and the sum had been paid to someone for something, and the third enclosure named the something.

I stopped reading there. Not because I lacked the courage — I'd like to believe that — but because the girl had come to stand beside my chair, and she was looking at the pages upside down, and her face had gone the color of the wax.

* * *

Here is what I know about vertigo, having lived in a tower through two winters of it: it does not come from heights. Heights are innocent. Vertigo comes when the ground you trusted turns out to have been moving the whole time, and your inner ear — that faithful clerk — insists on reporting the old ground, the solid ground, the ground that was there yesterday. The body grieves for a floor that no longer exists.

Reading those dispatches, I felt it in the mind instead of the ear. The floor was Port Mahon. The floor was the harbor that had kept me — Brannoc who found me the post, the fishwives who left salt cod at my door the first winter without ever once mentioning that I was a foreigner married to a local boy who drowned, the whole web of small debts and smaller kindnesses that had held me above the waterline since Tomas died. That floor was real. I was standing on it.

And underneath it, apparently, was a hole the size of two hundred and six lives, and the harbor had been built over it, and everyone had gone on walking.

No. That is vertigo talking, and vertigo lies. I did not know yet who knew and who didn't. I did not know why the convoy mattered, or to whom, or whether the reason in the third enclosure was greed or fear or something with a flag on it. I knew only what the papers said, and what the papers said was lethal — lethal to me for holding them, lethal to the girl for carrying them, lethal to whoever wrote that letter, who had signed himself R. and instructed his daughter to forget his face, which is the kind of instruction only a man gives when he has already measured the distance between himself and the grave and found it short.

The girl touched my sleeve. One finger, very lightly, the way you test whether a stove is hot.

"They're papers," she said.

It was the first thing she had said since *nothing*. Her voice was rusty, low for her age, careful. I turned in the chair to face her properly.

"Yes," I said. "Papers."

"Papa burns papers." Her brow furrowed, working at something. "He burned them in the little stove. But these he sewed. Sewing means keep." She looked up at me, and the professional sweep of her eyes was gone, and what was underneath was eight years old and terrified. "Sewing means *keep*, doesn't it?"

"It does," I said.

"He pushed me." The words came out of her in a flood now, the way water comes when a seam finally gives — not weeping, not yet, just memory with the pressure behind it. "There was a boat. It was night and the engine was quiet, wrapped in — in cloth, Papa wrapped the engine. And there was a man on the shore with a lamp and Papa gave him money and then Papa pushed me. Onto the rocks. And he said—" Her breath hitched. "He said forget this face. He said it twice. He said if I remember his face I'll be sad forever, and if I don't remember it I'll be safe forever, and I only get to pick one."

The kettle had gone cold. The fire needed wood. Outside, the sea was doing its ordinary business against the tower's base, patient as arithmetic, and I understood suddenly that I was holding the most dangerous object on this coast and that the second most dangerous object was standing in my kitchen learning to trust me.

"What did you pick?" I asked quietly.

"I'm still picking," she said.

I put the papers back in their oilcloth. I did not resew them — that skill is beyond me, and besides, I had not yet decided whether they were going to be kept or delivered or used, and a thing's stitching should match its purpose. I set them on the high shelf behind the flour tin, which is where Tomas hid the brandy from himself, and I made fresh tea, and I cut the last of the bread, and Tuesday and I ate together at the table like two survivors of a wreck comparing notes on the sinking.

"The convoy," she said, as I was clearing the plates. "Was it the Meridian?"

The name went through me like cold water through a seam. "Why do you ask that?"

"Because Papa said it in his sleep." She turned her plate in a slow circle, studying it. "Every night for a week he said *the Meridian, the Meridian, I counted them out and I couldn't count them home.* And then one night he stopped saying it, and the next night we got in the boat."

I looked at her — this scrap of a girl with a drowned man's vocabulary and a soldier's way of waking — and I felt the vertigo tilt again, because the Meridian was not an abstraction to Port Mahon. Eleven ships. The harbor had mourned for a month; the fishwives had flown black rags from their masts; Brannoc had drunk himself quiet for a week and come back saying nothing, which for Brannoc was a eulogy. Half the town had lost someone on the ninth of October. And here in my kitchen was proof that the loss had not been the sea's doing at all.

The sea, at least, kills honestly.

"Tuesday," I said. "Tomorrow I'm going to need to go down to the harbor. There are people there who—" I stopped, recalibrated. There were people there who would help me, and people there who might be the reason the letter said *trust no one*, and I had no way yet to sort one from the other. "There are people I need to see. While I'm gone, you'll keep the light log. Just the times and the weather. Can you write?"

"I can write in three languages," she said, with the first flicker of pride I'd seen in her, and something in my chest that had been clenched since the storm loosened half a degree.

"Good. Times and weather. Nothing else." I hesitated, then asked the question I had been circling all morning. "The man on the shore with the lamp. Did you see his face?"

She went very still. Stillness, in her, was not calm; it was a fortress going up brick by brick.

"No," she said, too quickly.

Which is how I learned that she had seen it, and that somewhere behind those grey eyes she was keeping a face the way I was keeping a packet — sealed, weighted, waiting to see who could be trusted with the opening.

I banked the fire. I checked the lamp, though the sun was still hours from setting, because checking the lamp is what my hands do when my mind is elsewhere. And all through the afternoon, while Tuesday practiced writing *times and weather* in the logbook in her careful three-language hand, I kept hearing the sentence from the letter, the one I had skimmed past in the first shock of the rest:

*Men have already come asking for her.*

Asking. Past tense. Which meant they had started before the storm, before the boat, before the rocks — and men who ask do not stop asking because the weather turns bad.

They stop asking when they find what they're looking for.

At dusk I lit the lamp, and Tuesday watched from the gallery doorway as the beam swung out over the recovering sea, and I caught her lips moving — counting the flashes, maybe, or counting something else entirely. Tomorrow I would go down to the harbor. Tomorrow I would look at Brannoc's face and the fishwives' faces and try to see, for the first time in two years, what was actually underneath the kindness.

Tonight I taught her the wick-trimmer, and she held it like it might bite, and outside the wind began to rise again.
