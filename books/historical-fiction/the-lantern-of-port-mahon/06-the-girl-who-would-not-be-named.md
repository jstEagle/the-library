## 6. The Girl Who Would Not Be Named

The woman asked me my name again this morning. Third time now.

I looked at her the way Papa taught me to look at the customs men on the quay — not angry, not frightened, just a face like weather, nothing to read. She wrote something in her book anyway. Probably *still won't say it*. Fine. Let the book have that. The book can't be drowned or shot or put on a night boat, so it may as well have everything.

She said, "We'll call you something else until you remember."

I didn't tell her I remember perfectly well. That's the trick of it. Everyone thinks forgetting is like losing a glove. It isn't. Forgetting is a thing you do on purpose, with your hands, like folding paper small enough to swallow.

* * *

Before the boat there was the burning.

Papa burned papers in the winter too, but a different winter, one where the stove was lit for warmth and not for hiding things. He fed the pages in one at a time, and he made a game of it, which is how I knew it wasn't a game. Grown-ups only make games of the things they need you to survive.

"Read me the first line," he'd say, "then let it go."

I read. *Convoy proceeding as scheduled.* I let it go. The fire took the words and turned them into light, which seemed to me then like a fair trade. Words go in, light comes out. I was seven and I thought the world worked that way.

It doesn't. Sometimes the words come back out again, sewn into a coat, riding against your ribs like a second heartbeat. Sometimes the light they make is the kind that gets men hanged.

* * *

The night boat smelled of tar and fish and fear, though the first two might have been the same smell. There were three of us aboard: Papa, a man who never gave a name, and me. The man held the tiller and kept looking over his shoulder at the shore lights, and every time he did, the boat wandered, and every time the boat wandered, Papa's jaw got tighter.

Papa crouched in front of me and buttoned my coat to the throat. Inside the lining there was a hard flat weight I had been carrying since the burning night, except this part hadn't burned. He'd cut the seam himself with his razor, badly, the way he did everything with his hands that wasn't writing.

"You're going ashore," he said. "There's a light. You walk toward the light. A woman keeps it. Her name—" and then he stopped, and I watched him decide not to give me the name, because names are what soldiers ask for, and he pressed his forehead against mine instead, which was warm, which is the last warm thing I keep.

The man from the tiller lifted me like cargo. I want to write that I fought him. I didn't. Cargo doesn't fight; cargo is carried because someone wants it delivered. I understood even then that I was being delivered somewhere, and that the wanting wasn't mine.

The water took us to the rocks. Hands pushed me ashore — Papa's hands, I know them anywhere, even through wet wool, even now — and then the boat went backward into the dark the way boats do when they are leaving, which is all boats ever do.

I walked toward the light.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about lighthouses: they are mostly stairs.

I counted them the first day, out of spite, because I refused to speak and counting is a way of speaking without giving anything away. One hundred and twelve steps to the lamp room. The woman climbs them four times a night. Her name is Xanthe, which is not a name, it's a letter wearing a coat, and when I thought that I almost laughed, and laughing felt like a door opening onto a room full of cold air, so I shut it again.

But she climbs the stairs without complaining, and she carries oil up them in cans that must weigh as much as I do, and she does this in storms, and she does it tired, and she does it sad — I know sad when I see it; I'm fluent — and none of it is a game she's making for my benefit. That's how I decided she might be real.

Real people are rare. Most grown-ups are uniforms with opinions.

* * *

The rituals of the light, as I have learned them:

First, the wick trimmers. You trim the wick so the flame burns clean, and if you do it wrong the glass goes black with soot and the light goes stupid. Xanthe let me hold the trimmers on the fourth day. She didn't ask permission and she didn't make a ceremony of it. She just handed them over the way you hand someone bread, and watched me with her eyes half closed like a cat deciding about a lap.

Second, the clockwork. The lens turns by weights, like a grandfather clock the size of a room, and you wind it and it turns and the light sweeps the sea and comes around again. Four seconds dark, one second bright. I timed it against my pulse. My pulse is faster. Everything alive is faster than the light. Maybe that's why sailors trust it.

Third, the logbook. Every hour, weather and wind and the state of the lamp, in her handwriting, which slants forward like it's in a hurry to get somewhere. On the fifth day she put the pen in my hand and said, "You don't have to talk. But the book needs to know someone besides me is here." So I wrote: *Wind NW, moderate. Lamp good.* And she read it and nodded, and that was a conversation, and it was the first conversation I'd had since the boat that didn't require me to lie.

* * *

At night I sleep in the room below the lamp room, in Tomas's old bed. That's what she calls him — *Tomas's bed*, *Tomas's charts*, *Tomas would have* — always in the present tense, which is either love or madness and is probably both. He was her husband. The sea has him now. She says it plainly, the way you'd say the neighbor has your ladder.

I like that she doesn't hide him from the house. In our house we hid everything. Papers in fires, daughters in coats, fathers' faces in the dark. Here the dead man is allowed to keep his hooks on the wall and his name in the air, and no one burns anything.

Except I am carrying the thing that could burn down everything she has, and she knows it, and she lets me sleep anyway.

That is either very brave or very foolish, and I have started to suspect those are the same thing seen from different sides of a door.

* * *

Yesterday the storm came back and she took me up to watch it, or maybe she just needed company, or maybe — this is the one I believe — she wanted me to see that the sea does this to everyone, that it isn't personal, that the thing that took my father and her husband is not a monster with a grudge but a great dumb animal that loves nothing. There is a horrible comfort in that. If the sea loves nothing, then it didn't choose. Then Papa wasn't taken; Papa was *spent*, the way oil is spent, the way wicks are spent, and spending is what lights are for.

I stood at the rail of the gallery with the wind trying to peel me off the world, and the beam went out over the water — dark, dark, dark, BRIGHT — and far below, waves the size of churches stood up and fell down and stood up again.

Xanthe shouted over the wind: "This is why! Whatever you're carrying, whatever they made you carry — this is what it's *for*. Lights exist because of nights like this."

I didn't answer. But I thought: she assumes I'm carrying grief. She hasn't guessed yet that I'm carrying paper.

Or she has, and she's waiting for me to hand it over the way she handed me the wick trimmers — no ceremony, just bread.

* * *

Tonight I dreamed of the burning again, but it changed. In the dream Papa didn't feed the pages to the fire. He fed them to me. Read me the first line, he said, then let it go, and I swallowed each page whole and my belly filled with light and the light leaked out through my seams, the bad seam, the one he cut with the razor, and the whole room glowed, and the customs men came with their lanterns saying *where is the convoy, little girl, where is the escort,* and I opened my mouth and it was only light coming out, no words at all, and they couldn't hang a child made of lamplight.

I woke up and the real lamp was turning overhead — dark, dark, dark, BRIGHT — and I lay there doing sums, because sums are safe.

One hundred and twelve steps. Four seconds dark, one second bright. One packet sewn into one coat. One woman who climbs the stairs four times a night for strangers she will never meet.

If the light can spend itself on ships that never say thank you, maybe the paper can spend itself too. Maybe that's what Papa meant. Not *hide it.* Not *keep it.* *Let it go* — the way you let a page go into a fire, the way a lamp lets its light go out over water, uselessly, faithfully, every four seconds, whether anyone is saved or not.

Tomorrow I will show her the seam.

I've decided. I'll take her hand and put it on the stitching myself, because some things shouldn't be said out loud, and because I want to watch her face when she understands that the child she's been feeding is also the dispatches, that she's been keeping two lights burning in one small body.

And I think — I think this is what safety feels like. Not the absence of danger. It's choosing, on purpose, with your own hands, who gets to know where you're folded.

Downstairs, boots on the stairs. Xanthe, climbing early, carrying something heavier than oil.

Someone is knocking at the harbor door below, and it isn't the wind.
