## 8. The Coroner's Convenient Pen

The Salt Barrel smelled of tar, herring brine, and the particular despair of men who drank before noon to make the afternoon arrive faster. Vesper had passed its door a hundred times in his nineteen years, and a hundred times he had kept walking, because the Salt Barrel was where the town's discarded men went to ferment—pilots who'd run ships aground, clerks who'd dipped into tills, and once, so the story went, a customs officer who'd looked too long at a Halloway manifest.

He pushed open the door now with the deliberate slowness of a man who has rehearsed this entrance all morning and is still not sure of it.

Pell was exactly where Sera Marr's cousin had said he'd be: third stool from the end, nearest the window, where the fog-light came through salt-hazed glass and made everything look drowned already. He was a big man gone soft, with a drinker's redness mapped across his nose and cheeks like a nautical chart of his own decline, and hands that lay on the bar as if they'd forgotten what they were for. Once those hands had held pens over the dead. Vesper tried to imagine it and could not.

"Whiskey," Vesper told the barman, and then, because Bram Tull's voice was in his head saying *never buy a man's first drink, buy his second, the first he'll remember, the second he'll owe you*, "and whatever he's having. On me."

Pell turned his head with the slow gravity of a ship coming about. His eyes were the pale, washed-out blue of things that live in deep water, and they took Vesper's measure without hurry—the company coat, the clerk's ink-stained cuffs, the face trained into blankness.

"You're young," Pell said. His voice was gravel rolled in honey. "Company?"

"I'm nobody," Vesper said. It was true enough to be disarming. "A clerk."

"A clerk buying whiskey for a coroner." Pell smiled, and it was a terrible thing, that smile—all the warmth wrung out of it years ago, leaving only the shape. "I'm not a coroner anymore, boy. Ask anyone. I'm a cautionary tale."

"I don't want anything from you."

"Everyone wants something." Pell lifted the fresh glass the barman set down, examined it against the window light as though checking it for flaws, and drank half of it in one motion. "That's the first lesson of Cape Route. The second lesson is that wanting gets you drowned."

Vesper sat down one stool away—not close enough to crowd, not far enough to shout. Outside, the fog pressed against the glass like a palm laid flat over a mouth. Somewhere down the harbor a bell buoy clanked its slow idiot rhythm.

* * *

He came back the next day. And the day after that.

He learned the architecture of it the way he'd learned the counting floor: by patience, by pattern, by never appearing to want what he wanted. He learned that Pell drank steadily from ten in the morning until the fog closed in, that he ate pickled eggs and nothing else, that he talked to the barman about tide tables with the precision of a man clinging to the last professional thing he owned. He learned that Pell had been coroner for eleven years and had been removed—or had removed himself; the stories disagreed—in the spring of '89, after the winter that took nine divers off the Cape Route shoals in six weeks.

He did not ask about the divers. Not at first. He asked about weather instead, because weather was safe, weather was what old men loved to hold forth on, and because he had spent three nights in the records room cross-referencing casualty rolls against the harbor master's logs and he knew, with the cold certainty that had been settling in him since the bundle at the door, that Elias Marr had died in a squall that never happened.

"The ninth of January, '89," Vesper said one afternoon, apropos of nothing, staring into his untouched beer. "Fine weather that week. My father used to say you could read the sky over the shoals better than any almanac. Calm as a church, the whole week. Then the wind came up on the fourteenth and blew itself out by the sixteenth."

Pell's glass stopped halfway to his mouth.

It was a small thing, that pause. A hitch no longer than a heartbeat. But Vesper had spent three years learning to read ledgers, and a ledger was only ever a record of hesitation—every crossed-out figure, every corrected entry, every number written twice was a moment when someone's hand had not been sure. He watched the pause travel through Pell like a swallowed stone.

"Clerks shouldn't talk about weather they didn't see," Pell said at last, and finished the drink.

"No," Vester agreed mildly. "They shouldn't."

* * *

On the fifth day, Pell bought *him* a drink.

It was late afternoon, the fog so thick the window had become a gray wall, and the Salt Barrel was nearly empty—a fisherman asleep in the corner booth, the barman reading a newspaper from two months ago. Pell stared into his glass for a long time, turning it slowly, and when he spoke his voice had changed, lost the honey, kept the gravel.

"You know why they call me Convenient Pell?"

Vesper said nothing. He had learned that silence was the load-bearing wall of every confession.

"Because I was convenient." Pell laughed, and there was nothing in it. "Eleven years. Do you know what a coroner does in a company town? Nothing. That's the job. You sit in a nice office above the chandlery, you get a stipend that's triple what the mainland pays, and when they bring you a body you sign what's put in front of you. *Lost to squall. Lost to squall. Misadventure. Misadventure. Lost to squall.*" He recited them like liturgy, like a rosary of small betrayals. "Sometimes they didn't even bring the body. Sometimes just the paper, already filled out, and a space at the bottom for my signature. Neat little space. They always left room for my signature."

Vesper kept his breathing even. Inside his chest something was pounding like surf.

"Who dictated?" he asked quietly.

Pell's eyes came around slowly. For a moment the drunkard fell away and Vesper saw the man underneath—young once, ambitious once, a mainland boy who'd come out for the stipend and told himself the papers were probably accurate anyway, told himself that right up until the telling stopped being possible and became merely habitual.

"You're not a clerk," Pell said. "Clerks don't listen like that."

"I'm an indentured clerk. There's a difference. I belong to the company the way the buoys belong to the harbor. Nobody asks the buoys what they think."

Something moved across Pell's face—recognition, maybe, or pity, or the memory of his own younger self standing in some office being handed his first pre-filled certificate. He drained his glass and set it down with exaggerated care, aligning it with the bar's edge.

"Halloway," he said. "Not Augustin—he was still at school in the capital then. The father. Old Silas Halloway himself would come down to my office some evenings, smelling of bay rum and cigars, and he'd sit in my chair, my own chair, and read me the cause of death like he was reading scripture. And I'd write it down. And I'd sign it. Eleven years." He held up his hands, turned them over, studied the palms as though expecting to find ink still ground into the whorls. "You know how many certificates? Two hundred and forty-one. I counted once, drunk, in '88. Two hundred and forty-one signatures. Maybe thirty of them were honest."

The number hung in the fog-thick air between them.

"Elias Marr," Vesper said.

Pell flinched. Actually flinched, a full-body twitch, and his hand knocked his glass and he steadied it with both hands like a man saving a child from falling.

"What about him?"

"January ninth, 1889. Recorded as lost to squall. The harbor log shows calm seas for six days either side. His body was recovered by beach divers off the north shoal with rope burns on both wrists and a weight tied at his waist that wasn't company issue." Vesper had rehearsed none of this; it simply came out of him, all the nights of ledger-reading compressed into a single exhaled breath. "You signed that certificate. Didn't you."

The barman had drifted to the far end of the bar, suddenly very interested in his newspaper. Whether he'd heard nothing or everything, Vesper couldn't tell, and decided it didn't matter. The die was in the air now, spinning.

Pell stared at him for a long, long moment. Then he began, very quietly, to cry.

It was awful to watch—not weeping but leaking, tears running down the red map of his face while his expression stayed perfectly composed, as though the crying were happening to someone else seated nearby. "He had a wife," Pell said. "They brought the paper and the wife's name was on it, for the pension denial, and I wrote *lost to squall* knowing—" His voice cracked apart. "Knowing what the beach boys pulled up. Knowing. I signed it and I went home and I drank and I have drunk every day since, do you understand, every single day, because the drinking is the only thing between me and the remembering—"

"And now?" Vesper said softly. "What's between you and the remembering now?"

Pell looked at him with those drowned-blue eyes, and in them Vesper saw the terrible arithmetic of a man totting up eleven years of cowardice and finding the sum larger than he could carry.

"You," Pell said. "God help us both. You're between me and the remembering now."

* * *

They met twice more before Pell sobered.

Vesper chose the meetings carefully—a bench behind the net-drying sheds where the fog muffled voices, the seawall past the last lamp where the dark came right up to the stones. He brought paper and Pell talked, haltingly at first, then with the terrible momentum of a man opening a wound he'd kept bandaged for years. Names. Dates. The system of it laid bare: how the company insured its divers against loss, policies taken out quietly through a mainland underwriter, premiums paid from the pearl accounts; how a diver's death paid the company three times what his lifetime of hauls would; how the *accidents* clustered in lean seasons, when the beds ran thin and the ledgers needed balancing. How the coroner's signature was the final seal on each transaction, converting murder into weather.

"It's elegant," Pell said on the seawall, and vomited the word like something rotten. "That's what I could never say aloud. It's *elegant*. Old Halloway built it like a machine, and every part does its work, and the parts are men, and the men are paid, and the ones who can't be paid—" He gestured vaguely at the black water below the wall. "The sea keeps their books."

"He'll testify," Vesper wrote that night in the hidden notebook, by candlelight in his attic room, the words cramped and fast. *Pell, former coroner. Willing. Names, dates, the insurance scheme. Two hundred forty-one certificates.* He read the entry back and felt something he hadn't felt since the note at the door: hope. Not the vague kind. The structural kind, the kind with dates attached, the kind that could bear weight. With Pell's testimony, the casualty rolls stopped being suspicious and became evidence. With the insurance pattern, the deaths stopped being tragic and became profitable. He was building something, page by page, name by name. A counter-ledger. A true one.

He let himself believe it for four days.

* * *

On the fourth day, Vesper came to the Salt Barrel and Pell was not there.

The barman shrugged. "Saw him at dawn, walking straight as a churchwarden. First time in years." Something in the barman's tone suggested he found this ominous rather than encouraging.

Vesper found him at dusk, in the room Pell rented above the chandlery, because Vesper had followed him—had waited across the street through the whole gray length of the afternoon watching the window, and seen the curtain twitch, and seen the shape of a man pacing, pacing, pacing.

Pell opened the door before he knocked. Sober, Pell was a different animal entirely: taller somehow, the softness gone hard, the washed-out eyes sharp and terrified. He looked down at Vesper the way a man looks at a debt collector.

"You have to burn it," Pell whispered. "Whatever you wrote down. Burn it."

"Pell—"

"*Burn it.*" He grabbed Vesper's sleeve and hauled him inside, bolted the door. The room was stripped nearly bare—a cot, a chair, a cold stove—and on the table lay a bottle unopened and beside it, incongruously, a razor strop, coiled like a sleeping snake. "I woke up clear-headed for the first time in five years and I remembered what I am. I'm not a witness. I'm an accomplice. Two hundred and forty-one signatures, boy. You don't turn accomplice into witness. You turn him into corpse. That's how the machine works. That's the *only* way it works."

"They need you alive. Killing you proves—"

"Proves what? To whom?" Pell's laugh was a dry rattle. "Drunken ex-coroner drowns in the harbor. Third one this year. The commission isn't here yet, boy, and when it comes it'll dine with the Hallows and sign what's put in front of it, same as I did, same as everyone, because that's what paper does in this town—it signs." He pressed his palms against his eyes. When he lowered them, he looked ancient. "I meant it, on the seawall. Every word. God forgive me, I meant it. But I meant it drunk, and drunk is another country. Sober, I know exactly what they did to the last man who wobbled."

"Who was the last man?"

Pell's mouth opened. Closed. He walked to the window and stood with his back turned, a big shape going grayer in the failing light.

"There was a customs officer," he said finally. "Years back. Found questions in a manifest. They fished him out of the harbor and I signed the certificate myself. *Misadventure.* My own hand." His shoulders shook once. "I signed my own future that day and didn't know it. Every signature since has been practice."

"Pell." Vesper stepped closer. "Elias Marr had a widow. She has her sister-in-law's child now, a baby left on the company step with a note telling me to forget its face. Forget the *face*, Pell. They're erasing people down to the skin. You're the only man alive who can put the pen back in the record. If you go quiet, they win. Not the Hallows—*it*, the machine. The machine wins forever."

For a long moment Pell didn't move. The fog outside the window had thickened to wool; the harbor bell sounded distant, drowned, like a warning sent from underwater. Then he turned, and Vesper saw that he had wept again, silently, without changing expression—that terrible trick of his body, grieving in secret even in front of witnesses.

"Give me a week," Pell said hoarsely. "A week to put my affairs in order. There's a niece in the capital. If I testify, she needs to be away from anything with the Halloway name touching it. A week, and then I'll swear it all, every certificate, every evening old Silas sat in my chair. I'll give you the machine entire." He gripped Vesper's arm, and his hand was shaking, but his grip was strong. "One week. And boy—if anything happens to me in that week, if I float, if I fall, if I hang myself with my own bootlaces—know that it wasn't me. Whatever they write on the paper, *it wasn't me.*"

"I'll know," Vesper said.

"Write it down," Pell said. "Tonight. Write down that I said it. Write down that I was afraid, and that I was willing anyway. Put it in your book, whatever book you keep, because paper is the only thing in this town that outlives a man—and they've had a monopoly on mine long enough."

Vesper walked home through the fog with the words repeating in his head like the harbor bell. At his attic room he lit the candle stub, opened the hidden notebook, and wrote it all: the confession, the promise, the fear, the week. He dated it. He signed beneath it as witness to the witness, which was a thing he suspected no clerk had ever had to do before, and which felt, horribly, like practice for something worse.

Then he blew out the candle and sat in the dark listening to the fog, and tried not to count the days.

He had gotten to four when the knock came at the counting floor's side door, before dawn, and one of the beach divers stood there streaming wet, hatless, his face the color of the fog itself.

"You'd best come," the diver said. "There's a man in the harbor. By the customs steps."
