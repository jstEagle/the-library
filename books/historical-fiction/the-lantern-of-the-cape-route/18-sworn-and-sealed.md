## 18. Sworn and Sealed

The commission had taken over the old customs house on Harrow Street, and the fog that morning seemed to have been sent for the purpose of hiding it. Vesper stood across the road beneath the dripping eave of a chandler's shop, the oilcloth satchel pressed against his ribs like a second heartbeat, and watched clerks in colonial blue carry ledgers up the steps two at a time. Eleven days, they said. The *Meridian* would weigh anchor on the twelfth, and whatever was not sworn and sealed by then would belong to Halloway's lawyers forever.

He had dressed as invisibly as he knew how—grey coat, grey waistcoat, the collar of a man who carries papers and is not asked about them. Three years of being overlooked had taught him the costume better than any tailor could. But his hands betrayed him. They would not stay still against the satchel's buckle. Inside it lay Bram Tull's duplicate ledger, its pages soft as cloth from thirty years of secret keeping; the keeper's final page, folded twice, naming old Halloway himself as the architect of the drowned; and the copied manifests from the lighthouse, forty-one names in Vesper's own careful hand, each one a man the company had insured against itself and then collected upon.

Forty-one. He had counted them three times in the night, by candle, with Sera reading aloud beside him while baby Elias slept against her shoulder. Forty-one men written out of existence in twenty years, and every entry balanced to the penny.

The fog horn sounded off the mole, long and low, like something grieving underwater.

Vesper crossed the street before he could think better of it.

* * *

The chamber smelled of wet wool, lamp oil, and the particular staleness of government rooms—the smell of decisions made far from where their consequences fall. A clerk at a standing desk looked him over with the flat courtesy of a man paid to be unimpressed.

"Business?"

"I wish to be heard," Vesper said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "As a witness to the record."

The phrase was Sera's. She had given it to him two nights ago, sitting at her kitchen table with the swaddling cloth spread flat between them like a map. "Don't ask them for justice," she'd said. "Ask them for the record. Justice is what they decide afterward. The record is what you put down while they're still deciding." He had written it in his notebook, underlined it twice, and read it aloud until the words stopped trembling.

The clerk consulted a printed schedule. "Petitions are heard at four o'clock. Written statements may be lodged at the table by the window."

"Not a petition." Vesper set the satchel on the standing desk and untied the strings with fingers that had finally gone quiet. "Testimony. Sworn testimony, taken down by your recording secretary, certified copies made before the commission departs. I have documents that will not survive another fire."

That word did what no argument could. The clerk's pen stopped moving. Everyone in Cape Route knew about the annex fire; it had been in the *Gazette* for two days, blamed on a defective flue, mourned in exactly one paragraph.

"Name?" the clerk said.

"Vesper Blackwood. Clerk, Halloway Trading Company." He let that sit a moment. "Formerly indentured. Still, technically, until my papers say otherwise."

Something shifted behind the clerk's eyes—not sympathy, but arithmetic. Indentured men did not walk into commissions accusing their owners. Either this was theater arranged for the commission's benefit, or it was the most dangerous thing to happen in Cape Route since the harbor dredging. The clerk picked up a small brass bell and rang it once.

* * *

They put him in a side room with a recording secretary named Miss Aldous, a thin woman with ink-stained cuffs and the patient face of someone who had transcribed a thousand lies and learned to hear around them. A commissioner came in after—Mr. Ferrow, silver-bearded, half-lidded, the kind of official whose skepticism had calcified into a facial expression.

"You understand," Ferrow began, settling into the chair across the trestle table, "that this commission concerns labor conditions. Wages, hours, safety provisions. It is not a court."

"No, sir." Vesper laid Bram's ledger on the table between them. "It's an inquiry. So inquire."

Miss Aldous's pen paused. Ferrow's half-lidded eyes opened fully for the first time.

Vesper opened the ledger to the first marked page. "This is the private record of Bram Tull, senior clerk of the Halloway Trading Company, kept in duplicate against the company's own books for thirty-one years. The company's casualty rolls record divers lost to squall, to equipment failure, to misadventure. Mr. Tull's record records what actually happened—and who ordered it." He turned pages with the deliberation of a man laying cards. "Diver Elias Marr, entered 'lost to squall,' October 1888. The harbor weather log for that week reads calm throughout. Diver Tomas Reeve, 'lost to equipment failure'—the failure being that his air line was cut, per the deposition of a beach witness whose name is here. Diver Ansel Coe, 'lost to misadventure.' Mr. Tull notes the misadventure was scheduled."

Ferrow had picked up the ledger. His thumb moved along the columns the way a man's thumb moves along a scar. "Where is Mr. Tull?"

"Dead, sir. Eleven days ago. Of his heart, which was true enough—it was everything else about him that failed. He gave me this ledger knowing he was dying, and knowing what giving it would cost me." Vesper drew out the folded page, the keeper's last, and placed it beside the ledger. "And this is from the decommissioned light on Gull Point. The former keeper kept manifests the company believed destroyed. Every diver listed as lost was insured by the Halloway Trading Company against precisely the accident that killed him. Forty-one policies. Forty-one payouts. The premiums were charged against the divers' own accounts—they paid, out of their pearl shares, for the insurance that made their deaths profitable."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a door slammed; the fog outside pressed against the high windows like a listener.

Ferrow read the keeper's page. Vesper watched the commissioner's face do what faces did when they encountered old Halloway's name in a dead man's handwriting: nothing, carefully, and then something anyway, at the corner of the mouth.

"These are copies," Ferrow said at last. "Duplicates. Transcriptions."

"Yes, sir. The originals are hidden where the company cannot burn them, and they will remain there until certified copies exist in more than one place. I'm told your commission leaves on the twelfth." Vesper met the half-lidded eyes. "I am asking you to make the record permanent before the victors can bleach it clean. That's all. Not justice. The record. What you decide afterward is your affair—but decide it looking at these numbers, not at Mr. Augustin Halloway's smile."

Miss Aldous wrote that down. Vesper saw her write it, and understood that some sentences pass from the spoken world into the permanent one without ever meaning to.

Ferrow sat back. "You'll be sworn tomorrow morning. Nine o'clock, main chamber. And bring—" he tapped the ledger— "whoever else can speak to what's in here. Sworn witnesses, not paper alone. Paper burns. You've proven that yourself." He almost smiled. "Paper also floats, if it gets aboard the right ship. But we'll come to that."

* * *

Sera Marr wore her good dress, the dark green one with the mended hem, because she said the mend should be seen—that was the whole point of a mend. She sat in the witness chair in the main chamber the next morning with her hands folded in her lap, and when the oath was administered she took it in a voice that carried to the back benches where the company's lawyers sat in a row like well-fed crows.

Augustin Halloway was there too. Vesper had seen him arrive—gloved, unhurried, exchanging pleasantries with two of the commissioners on the steps as though the inquiry were a garden party he had graciously agreed to attend. Now he occupied the front bench with his attorney, and his expression was one of mild, courteous interest, the same expression he'd worn offering Vesper a promotion that was really a leash. Watching Sera, he looked like a man listening to weather.

"State your name for the record," said the presiding commissioner.

"Sera Marr. Widow of Elias Marr, diver, lost October the eighth, 1888."

"And how did you learn of your husband's death?"

"A boy came to the door with a company envelope. Inside was a note saying he was lost to squall, and a figure—a sum against his account. Not owed to me. Owed by me. They said his gear was company gear, and gear lost at sea was charged to the diver." Her voice did not shake. Vesper, watching from the gallery bench, thought: she has rehearsed this not in words but in years. "I was charged eleven pounds for drowning my husband."

A murmur moved through the chamber. One of the crows on the back bench leaned toward another.

"The weather that week," the commissioner prompted, consulting a paper. "You recall it?"

"I recall it because the whole town recalls it. It was the week of the regatta. There wasn't wind enough to fly the bunting. My husband went down on the fourth—he came up, he ate supper, he complained about the price of lamp oil. On the eighth they told me the sea took him." She unfolded a scrap of cloth from her reticule, small and worn, needlework faded but legible. "This is from his kit bag. His sister-in-law sewed it. It came back to me tangled in kelp three weeks later, from the shallows off Gull Point—not the open water where a squall takes men, but the rocks, fifty yards from shore, where the company's salvage divers worked that autumn. Elias didn't drown in weather. He drowned in a place, and the place tells you why."

"Why?" the commissioner asked, though Vesper suspected he already knew; the manifests were on the table before him.

"Because Elias found something down there he wasn't meant to find. A wreck the company was working quietly. Cargo they weren't declaring. He talked about it at the beach fires—just talk, just wonder. A week later he was squall-lost in a flat sea." Sera turned in the chair, deliberately, and faced the front bench. "And when the company sent its men to ask after a certain foundling last spring, they were asking after his sister's child. Because his sister knew what he'd found, and she died too, and the child was left on your doorstep with a note saying forget the face." She held up the swaddling cloth now, the other piece, the one Odile had kept. "I don't forget faces. That's the whole of my testimony. I don't forget them."

The recording secretary's pen scratched. In the gallery, Vesper found he was gripping the bench edge hard enough to hurt, and made himself let go, finger by finger.

Augustin Halloway rose, unhurried, buttoning his glove. "Commissioners—with respect, this is grief speaking, and grief is moving but it is not evidence. The widow repeats beach gossip and kelp. My family has traded honestly in this port for sixty years. We will, of course, answer every specific allegation in due course—" he smiled, and the smile reached the commissioners like lamplight reaching a dark room— "in due course, with counsel present, and with the understanding that anonymous ledgers and burned buildings make poor foundations for ruining honest men."

"Then answer one thing now," Sera said, before the commissioner could rule. She was still standing, still holding the cloth. "One question, Mr. Halloway, and then sit down. Your company insured my husband against death by squall, premium charged to his own account. Is that in your books? Yes or no."

The chamber went still as held breath. Halloway's smile stayed exactly where it was, but Vesper—who had spent three years learning to read the small movements of powerful men—saw the pause. Half a heartbeat. The pause of a man checking a locked drawer in his mind and finding it empty.

"That is a matter for counsel," Halloway said pleasantly, and sat down.

But Miss Aldous had written the question down. And she had written the pause too, in her way—*witness declined to answer*—and Vesper understood that the record had just grown a tooth.

* * *

They adjourned at noon. Vesper walked out through the side door into a fog gone thin and pearlescent, and Sera fell into step beside him, still in the green dress, breathing the way people breathe after swimming a long way.

"You saw his face," she said. "When I asked."

"I saw it."

"He didn't know about the insurance." She said it slowly, testing the shape of it. "The son didn't know. Which means either it stopped before him—or it never stopped, and the father never told him what he inherited."

"Or he knows perfectly well and is a better actor than any of us." Vesper looked back at the customs house, its windows fogged with sea damp, the colonial flag hanging limp above the door. "Eleven days. We've used two."

Ahead, at the corner of Harrow Street, a company carriage waited. Its driver was not looking at the customs house. He was looking at Vesper—at Vesper specifically, with the fixed professional attention of a man memorizing a face for later—and beside him on the box sat Pell's former assistant, the young coroner's clerk who had signed the drowning certificate that ruled Pell's death accidental.

The carriage did not move. It simply waited, patient as tide, while Vesper and Sera stood in the thinning fog and understood, together and without speaking, that the hearing was only half the battle—that somewhere between this chamber and the steamer's deck, the company intended to make sure the record ended with them.

Sera's hand found Vesper's sleeve. "Nine days," she said.

"Nine days," Vesper agreed, and did not let go of the satchel.
