## 4. The Commandant's Offer

They came for her at the hour when the gulls went quiet, which Ansel had learned was the hour before anything bad happened on the waterfront. Gulls were honest that way. They screamed at storms, at barges, at soldiers — and then, just before the true trouble arrived, they stopped, as if even they found it unspeakable.

Two grenadiers stood in the doorway of the counting house with the particular politeness of men who had been told to be polite and resented it. The taller one held a paper. The shorter one held his musket in a way that suggested the paper was a formality.

"Ansel Yarrow," the tall one read. "Diver. Clerk. You are requested."

"Requested," Ansel said. She set down her pen carefully, blotting the last entry so it wouldn't smear. It was a small habit of hers, finishing things properly even at the end of the world. "By whom?"

"The Commandant." The soldier's mouth did something that was almost a smile but hadn't finished the journey. "You'll want your coat. It's cold in the map room."

It was cold everywhere now. The army had requisitioned the coal, along with the warehouses, the salt stores, the good rope, two of the three taverns, and — though no order said so — every ounce of comfort within a day's walk of the river. Ansel took her coat, the diving coat with its deep pockets, and followed.

The streets had changed their character in nine days. Before the requisition, the town had smelled of fish, tar, and Varga's cheap tobacco. Now it smelled of horses and woodsmoke and the sour iron smell of too many boots on frozen mud. Soldiers lounged where fishermen used to mend nets. A dragoon was teaching a corporal to whistle through his fingers outside the chandlery, badly, and both of them laughed like boys, and Ansel hated them a little for it, because they looked so ordinary. Monsters, she had decided long ago, ought to look like monsters. It made the arithmetic easier.

The commandant's office had been the customs house, back when customs meant something gentler than confiscation. The map room was upstairs, behind double doors, and it was warm — the only warm room in the town, she would have bet her mother's pearl on it. A stove ticked in the corner. Maps of the Danube covered the walls from floor to ceiling, the river rendered in blue ink as it wound from the black forests down to the sea, annotated in a dozen hands: depths, fords, sandbars, the little crosses where barges had gone down.

And there, on a table beneath the window, lay the Varga ledger.

It was chained to the table leg. An actual chain, iron, run through the binding and padlocked — as if the book might get up and swim away. Ansel felt something twist in her chest that she refused to name. That book had been her whole life for six years. Every debt, every wage, every diver's name in her careful hand, every secret in the margins written in the code Master Varga had taught her after he caught her forgery. Seeing it chained like a criminal prisoner — seeing it *kept* by someone else — was worse than seeing it burned would have been.

"You are Yarrow." The man at the window didn't turn around. He was small, which surprised her; she had imagined Commandant Haller large, the way you imagine an avalanche. He was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, with the neat, economical build of a man who wasted nothing, including words. When he did turn, his face was unremarkable except for the eyes, which were pale and patient, like a fisherman watching a line.

"I am, sir."

"They tell me you keep the company's books."

"I kept them. The army has them now." She nodded at the table.

Something flickered across his face — not quite amusement, but a cousin of it. "So I do. Sit down, Yarrow."

There was one chair, placed opposite the ledger, and she understood immediately that this was deliberate. He wanted her looking at it while they talked. He wanted her wanting it.

She sat.

"Do you know why I'm here?" Haller asked. He poured coffee from a pot on the stove — real coffee, another requisitioned luxury — and handed her a cup without asking if she wanted it. It was very good. She hated herself for noticing.

"To inventory the company's assets, sir."

"To hang Aurel Varga." He said it the way other men said *good morning*. "The Emperor's commission has evidence enough to ruin him. What we lack is evidence enough to hang him, and hanging requires precision. Ruin is easy. Anyone can ruin a man. Hanging him properly — that takes a clerk."

Ansel drank her coffee and said nothing. Silence was a tool she'd learned at the bottom of the river: hold still, don't spend air, wait.

Haller waited too. He was better at it than she was, which frightened her more than the muskets had.

"Page forty-one," he said finally. "The grain shipments. Four hundred measures of wheat entered as spoiled and written off, autumn of 'forty-six. Spoiled grain doesn't get sold downstream at full price, Yarrow. Someone sold it. Someone was paid. And someone recorded the payment somewhere that isn't page forty-one."

*In the margins,* Ansel thought. *In my hand. In code I can read and you cannot.* Her face did what faces on the waterfront learned to do: nothing at all.

"You're the keeper of those books," Haller went on. "Not the author — I know Varga wrote his own orders. But the keeper. Which means you know where everything lives in them. Which means you can testify to what each entry *means*, not merely what it says." He set down his cup. "Here is what happens next, and I'll say it once, plainly, because I find plainness saves time.

"You will authenticate the entries. You will explain the codes, the abbreviations, the little private language the Varga Company used to steal from the Crown and starve its own divers. You will swear to it before the commission. And when Varga hangs — and he will hang, Yarrow, whether you speak or not, the only question is whether it's for theft or for murder — you will receive a certificate of immunity. Full immunity. Your debts voided. Your indenture dissolved. You will be free."

The word landed in her like a stone dropped into deep water. *Free.* She had been indentured since she was eleven years old, since her father drowned and the company absorbed his debts into hers with the smooth efficiency of water filling a lung. Freedom was a thing she had calculated about the way she calculated weights — always for other people, never for herself.

"And if I refuse?" she asked.

Haller shrugged, a small, tidy motion. "Then you are a witness who declined, which makes you either a fool or an accomplice, and the commission will decide which. Accomplices to grand theft under wartime regulation share the principal's fate." He let that sit. Then, softer, almost kindly: "I don't think you're an accomplice, Yarrow. I think you're a girl who survived a hard place. There's no shame in it. But survival leaves marks, and marks can be read, and I am paid to read them."

He turned back to the ledger, unlocked the chain with a key from his waistcoat, and opened it to page forty-one. Just like that. The lock clicked and her heart clicked with it.

"Read it to me," he said. "Tell me what it says. Only what it says — we'll come to meanings later."

She read. Her own handwriting looked strange to her, as if it belonged to a girl who had died sometime in the past week. *Four hundred measure wheat, spoiled, written off, per instruction A.V.* The letters sat on the page like well-behaved children, saying exactly what they'd been taught to say and not one word more.

"Good," Haller said. "Again tomorrow. And the day after. We'll go through it all, slowly. I'm told you're thorough." He relocked the chain, and the sound of it was final as a coffin nail. "You're dismissed."

Ansel rose. At the door, she paused — not from courage, she told herself, but because the light from the window fell across the wall of maps, and she had always been unable to stop herself reading things.

* * *

What she saw there, in the four seconds she allowed herself, she folded away the way she folded pearls into her cheek when surfacing from a dive — quickly, painlessly, against the teeth.

The guard rotation was posted beside the door in a neat officer's hand. Two sentries on the stairs, relieved at the change of the moon watch, midnight and dawn. One man on the rear stair, which was less a stair than a ladder, and which was guarded — she noted with a diver's appreciation of currents — by a single soldier who spent most of his shift visible through the window of the guardroom below, warming himself at the stove. The map room itself was locked between sessions; the key lived on Haller's belt during the day and, according to the duty roster pinned beneath the rotation chart, in the adjutant's drawer at night. The adjutant, she had seen him in the street, was a young man with a cough who took brandy at the Crooked Pike every evening at sundown and left the drawer key with the innkeeper while he drank, because the innkeeper was his cousin.

All of this passed through her in the time it took Haller to say, "Yarrow?"

"Sir." She turned. Kept her face empty as ballast.

"You understand what's being offered."

"Immunity," she said. "Yes, sir."

"It's a genuine offer. I want you to know that. Whatever else you think of me." His pale eyes held hers, and there was something in them that was almost weary. "Men like Varga make the river filthy. I've spent twenty years dredging men like Varga out of it. Help me, and you'll never owe anyone again."

*Never owe anyone again.* It was beautifully put. It was also, she thought, walking down the cold stairs past the two sentries who did not look at her, precisely backwards. Immunity was not freedom. Immunity was a rope with a collar already fitted, held by a man in Vienna who could pull it whenever testimony needed refreshing, whenever a witness needed reminding. Haller wasn't offering to cut her loose. He was offering to *own her outright*, debt-free, the way the company owned the warehouse chains.

But God help her, some part of her had wanted to say yes anyway. Some part of her had sat in that warm room with real coffee and heard the word *free* and felt it pull like the current pulls a diver who lingers too long on the bottom, sweet and fatal.

Outside, the wind came off the river carrying the first soft rotten smell of thawing ice. Early this year. Too early. The old divers said an early break meant a hard spring.

Tomas was waiting near the fish market, pretending to examine a barrel of salted herring with the intense scholarly interest of a man who had never once cared about herring. He didn't ask how it went. He just fell into step beside her, and after half a street he said, quietly:

"Well?"

"He wants me to hang Varga," Ansel said. "He's offered me immunity."

Tomas was silent for three steps. Then: "Will you take it?"

She thought of the chain on the table. Of the adjutant's drawer, and the cousin innkeeper, and the ladder-stair with its one stove-warming sentry. Of the word *free*, sitting in her chest like a swallowed pearl.

"No," she said. "But I know now how to get what I came for."

Down at the water, far out where the channel ran dark, the ice groaned — a long, low sound, like something enormous turning over in its sleep, beginning to wake.
