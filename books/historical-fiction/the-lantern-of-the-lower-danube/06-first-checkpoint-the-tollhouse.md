## 6. First Checkpoint: The Tollhouse

The dead of the lower Danube traveled better than the living. This was a fact I had learned young, watching the burial barges slide past our mooring in the gray mornings — flat-bottomed, unhurried, flying no flag and answering no questions. Grief, like grain, moved by water here, and nobody taxed it twice.

I had not expected to make use of this knowledge. But then, I had not expected most of what the winter had made of me.

The pages rode against my ribs, sewn into the lining of my diving coat in four flat packets, each one wrapped in oilcloth and waxed thread. Forty-one entries copied in my own careful hand, plus the margin codes that Master Varga had taught me at his own table, feeding me plum brandy and calling me *his clever little abacus*. The coat was stiff with river grease and old shell-mother, and it smelled of me — of tallow and cold water and the particular sourness of a girl who had not been warm since November. No soldier would want to search it closely. That was the theory.

The practice was that I would have to lie down among corpses to prove it.

* * *

The burial barge belonged to Ferenc Molnár, who had ferried the town's dead downstream for thirty years and had buried three wives and, he liked to say, most of his good opinions. He was a squat man built like a mooring post, with a beard that seemed to be attempting an escape from his face in several directions at once, and he owed the Varga Company eleven florins for coffin nails, which is how I knew him. Everyone on the waterfront was in the ledger somehow. That was rather the point of the ledger.

"You want to travel as cargo," he said, when I found him loading at the fish stairs before dawn. "Among the dead."

"Among the coffins," I said. "There's a difference."

"There is not, girl, and you'll forgive me if I'm particular about it, being as I share a boat with them regular." He spat thoughtfully into the shallows. "Why?"

Because there were soldiers at the tollhouse now, and they were stopping every living soul and turning out every bag, and because Commandant Haller's men had begun asking after a diver's daughter with quick hands and a ledger-shaped absence where her conscience used to sit. But I said: "Because the dead don't get searched."

Ferenc considered this with the gravity of a man deciding whether to have another sausage. "No," he admitted. "They don't. It's one of the few kindnesses left in the world." He looked at me a long moment. "You're Yarrow's girl."

"I'm nobody's girl."

"You're Yarrow's girl," he repeated, which was worse, because it meant he'd already decided, and men like Ferenc did not un-decide things. "He pulled my boy out from under the ice once. Whole town knows what happened to him after. Whole town knows what the ledger says about it, too, or will, when the right people read it." He held out a hand like a knotted rope. "Lie down between the third and fourth boxes. Keep your arms crossed over your chest like you're modest. And if you sneeze, girl, I swear on all three wives I'll roll you overboard myself and save the army the trouble."

* * *

Lying among coffins teaches you things about yourself. Mostly it teaches you that your nose itches, that your left knee has opinions, and that your breathing is far louder than you ever believed. The coffins were plain pine, some of them new, some of them scrounged and re-nailed, and they gave off a smell of sawdust and resin that was almost pleasant, almost like the carpenter's shop where I'd bought my first pair of diving shoes with money I had earned and immediately owed back.

Above me, Ferenc hummed. He hummed badly — a funeral psalm rendered in something closer to a drinking song — and poled us out into the current while the mist still lay on the water like a sheet drawn up to the town's chin.

I thought about my father. I tried not to, but the coffins made it unavoidable. There was a version of events in which he was in one of these boxes somewhere downstream, under a stone with his name spelled wrong, and there was a version in the ledger, in Varga's tidy hand, that said *lost through his own carelessness*, and I had copied that line myself, years ago, without thinking anything of it at all. Ink is obedient stuff. It writes whatever you tell it.

The tollhouse appeared out of the mist the way tollhouses do — too soon and all at once — a squat stone building with a chain across the channel and a fire burning on the bank, and around the fire, six soldiers in imperial gray who were very much alive and very much bored, which is either the best or worst thing a checkpoint can be.

"Hold!" someone shouted, though we were barely moving. "Barge! Hold!"

Ferenc brought us alongside with the exaggerated slowness of a man who had done this forty times that month. "Burial detail," he announced to the air generally. "Downstream to the consecrated ground at Orsova. Eleven souls, God rest them, and none of them inclined to hurry."

A sergeant came down the steps. I heard his boots before I saw him, and then I saw his boots, which stopped directly beside the third coffin, close enough that I could have counted the stitches if I'd opened my eyes, which I did not.

"Eleven," the sergeant said.

"Count them yourself if you've the stomach for it," said Ferenc cheerfully. "I'd offer to help but my back's gone and my faith's not far behind."

There was a pause with a smile in it. Soldiers always like Ferencs; they are the human equivalent of a warm stove. Then boots moved along the deck, and a gloved hand thumped a coffin lid — once, twice — and I lay beneath my crossed arms and performed the deepest stillness of my life, deeper than any dive, while my heart carried on like a fish in a bucket.

"They're nailed shut," the sergeant observed.

"Naturally they're nailed shut. You want them traveling open? In March?" A second voice, younger, offended on principle. "Sir, last week at Silistria they opened one and —"

"We do not speak of Silistria," said the sergeant, with feeling.

"No, sir."

Boots retreated. I allowed myself one breath, shallow, through my mouth. Cold triumph settled over me like the mist itself — smooth, silver, complete. *They're afraid of the boxes,* I thought, with a contempt I hadn't known I possessed. *Good. Be afraid. Fear is the only honest tax anyone collects on this river.*

And then the boots came back.

"Wait." The sergeant's voice had changed — lost its boredom, gained something sharper. "What's that on the stern line?"

* * *

I knew before he touched it. Of course I knew. My father's boat tag — a lead disc stamped with the company mark and his diver's number, 114, worn smooth by thirty years of rope — hung off the stern where Ferenc had tied my bundle, because I had asked him to tie my bundle there, because I had not had the wit to think that a dead man's tag might still speak.

It spoke.

"Yarrow," the sergeant read aloud. "Diver, number one-one-four. There was a notice posted. Diver's kin, the commandant wants word of." Paper rustled. "Girl, seventeen, dark hair, scar on the left thumb. Travels light. Answers to Ansel."

The silence that followed had weight. I felt it press down on my chest like deep water.

"Well," said Ferenc, into that silence, with the magnificent calm of a man who has decided to be surprised by nothing ever again, "half the river answers to Ansel, son. It's a common enough name among the kind of people who name their daughters after saints and then can't afford the icon."

"It's not a common name," said the sergeant.

"It's a *river* name," said Ferenc, warming. "Like Danube. Like Mud. Now are you going to open my coffins over a piece of lead, or are you going to let the dead get to their ground before Easter? Because I'll tell you what happens if you keep them past Sunday. The priest at Orsova writes to the bishop. The bishop writes to Vienna. And some clerk in Vienna who has never seen a corpse in his life writes back asking why the imperial army delayed Christian burials to go poking at pine boxes in the mud, and that letter, son, lands on somebody's desk above yours. Whose desk is above yours?"

Another silence. This one had fear in it, which I recognized professionally; I had spent my life around men weighing debts against consequences, and the arithmetic never changed.

"I should look in the bundles," the sergeant said at last, grudgingly, which was the sound of a man negotiating himself downward.

"The bundle's mine," said Ferenc. "Spare shirt. Bottle. A man gets thirsty on the water."

The bundle was indeed Ferenc's, and it contained a spare shirt and a bottle, and also — because Ferenc Molnár understood commerce the way I understood ink — nothing else whatsoever. The sergeant went through it with two fingers, found the bottle, held it up to the light, and sighed the sigh of a man whose whole war consisted of other people's bottles.

Then he came toward the stern. Toward me. Toward the third and fourth coffins and the space between them where a girl lay with forty-one crimes sewn against her ribs.

"Sir," said the young soldier quickly, "the mist's thickening and the captain wanted the chain log signed by noon, and —"

"*Fine.*" The word cracked like ice. Boots receded, paused, returned once more just long enough for the sergeant to say, quietly, so that only Ferenc — and the dead, and I — could hear:

"The tag stays with me. And the next barge through pays the usual rate, Molnár, same as everybody."

"The usual rate," Ferenc agreed comfortably, "has always been reasonable."

Something small and hard changed hands. I did not see it. I felt it anyway, the way you feel a hook set — a small bright pain behind the breastbone, exactly where the pages lay.

We slid under the chain. The mist took the tollhouse back. Only when the fire was a smudge and the voices were gone entirely did Ferenc let out a breath that seemed to come up from his boots.

"He took the tag," I whispered.

"He took the tag and half of what I'd have charged him anyway, the thief." Ferenc spat over the side. "That pearl of yours bought us the channel, girl, and don't think I didn't see it leave your hand. Cheap at the price."

"It was my mother's."

"I know whose it was. Half the waterfront knew your mother." He poled in silence a moment. Then, gruffly, almost kindly: "She'd have called it well spent. She was the sensible one."

I lay back among the coffins and watched the mist, and the cold triumph I had felt at the tollhouse curdled slowly into something else — not grief exactly, and not fear, but the specific chill of a girl who had just learned that her father's number could still be spoken aloud by strangers, that a stamped disc of lead could find her faster than any description, and that somewhere upstream a sergeant was carrying that disc to a commandant who kept ledgers chained in map rooms.

"Ferenc," I said. "How far to the ford?"

"Ford?" He laughed, short and humorless. "Girl, you'd best pray you reach it before the ice does. They're saying it's breaking early this year. Early as tomorrow." He shook his head. "And they're saying Haller's posted men along the whole near bank. Looking for someone."

The river carried us on. Against my ribs, under the waxed thread and the oilcloth, the pages waited — and ahead, where the mist ended, the ice was already groaning like something waking up.
