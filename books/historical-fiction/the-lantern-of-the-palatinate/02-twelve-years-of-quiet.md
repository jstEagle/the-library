## 2. Twelve Years of Quiet

The workshop smelled of brass filings and linseed oil, and Edmund Fairweather had learned to love it the way one loves a room one has never been permitted to leave.

It was Tuesday, and the morning light came through the south window in a long slanted blade that caught the dust and held it suspended, golden and slow, like a congregation of small patient souls. Edmund sat at the bench with a fusee chain pooled in her palm, feeding it link by link through her fingers, checking each one for wear. The chain belonged to a bracket clock commissioned by a wine merchant in Ladenburg, and it was old work, older than she was, and it demanded a particular kind of attention — not the sharp attention of diagnosis but the long, even attention of maintenance, the attention one pays to something one intends to outlive.

Her hands were steady. They were always steady. That was the first thing anyone said about Edmund Fairweather, the clockmaker's son: *steady hands, that boy, like his father.* People said it in the market, in the guild hall, in the pews of the Heiliggeistkirche where the Fairweathers sat in the third row from the back because Jakob Fairweather believed that a man who made instruments of precision should not presume upon God's attention. *Steady hands.* They said it as a compliment, and Edmund received it as a sentence.

Twelve years. She had been five when the rules were made, and she could not remember a time before them, though she could remember — in fragments, in flashes, the way one remembers a dream by its edges — a time when no one had watched her at all.

She fed the chain through her fingers. Link, link, link.

* * *

The winter her mother left — *left*, that was the word her father used, always *left*, never fled, never was driven, though Edmund had long since understood the architecture of the lie and chosen not to disturb its foundations — the winter her mother left, the snow came early to Heidelberg and stayed like an unwelcome relative.

Edmund was five. What she retained was not narrative but sensation, stored in the body the way the body stores all its earliest knowledge. She remembered the smell of tallow candles burning at an hour when candles were never lit, because wax was for Sundays and tallow was for desperation. She remembered her mother's hands — Anna's hands, quick and chapped, a seamstress's hands with a permanent ink stain on the right index finger from marking cloth — moving through a chest of drawers with terrible speed, not packing but *selecting*, and the difference between those two motions was a lesson Edmund would not understand for years.

She remembered her mother kneeling in front of her in the blue room, the small back room that smelled of lavender and lamp oil, and the kneeling was wrong, mothers did not kneel to daughters, and Anna had held Edmund's face in both her chapped hands and said, in the Palatine dialect she fell into only when the world was too large for High German:

*"Vergiss mein Gesicht nicht. Aber vergiss, dass ich's geliebt hab — das darfst du."*

Forget my face. But forget that I loved you — that you may forget.

Even at five, Edmund had known this was a strange sentence. It had the shape of a riddle, and she had turned it over in the years that followed the way one turns over a coin found on a road, looking for the stamp, the date, the proof of what it was. It was only much later — eleven, twelve, sitting awake in the narrow bed above the workshop while her father's breathing slowed below — that she had understood. Her mother had not been asking to be forgotten. She had been granting permission. *Forget that I loved you, so that when they ask, your face will tell them nothing.* Her mother had been trying to make herself into a wound that did not show, so that her daughter could pass an examination she did not yet know she would face.

The persecutions of 1743 had not come to Heidelberg like an army. That was what no one understood about them, what the pamphlets got wrong, what the edicts posted on the church doors could not convey. They came like water. They came the way water comes into a cellar — not through the door, not in a single dramatic flood, but seeping, finding the low places, the cracks, the joints in the masonry. A name read aloud in a consistory meeting. A shop whose license was suddenly *under review*. A neighbor who stopped returning greetings, then stopped nodding, then crossed the street. A summons — not an arrest, never an arrest, arrests required charges and charges required proof, and the beauty of the thing, the exquisite efficiency of it, was that no charges were ever necessary. A summons to *clarify one's standing*. And the men who received such summons did not come back to their shops for some time, and when they came back they were different, quieter, and their shops closed within the year.

Anna Fairweather had been born Anna Reiss, and the Reiss name was on a list. Edmund never learned the full contents of the list. It was not the sort of document that was read aloud in full. But she knew that her mother's name had traveled from that list to a lesser list, and from the lesser list to a conversation between two men who came to the workshop on a Thursday in November and drank Jakob's wine and admired his regulator clock and used the word *emigration* in the tone other men used for weather.

They left on a Sunday. Not mother and daughter — Edmund understood even then, in the wordless way of five-year-olds, who understand everything and can articulate nothing — that the plan had a shape, and the shape had a hole in it, and the hole was the size of a small girl. Anna would go. Anna had cousins, or friends of cousins, or friends of friends, a chain of names that stretched down the Rhine and out to sea and across an ocean to a place called Pennsylvania, which to Edmund was not a place but a sound, a word like a bell rung in an empty room. Anna would go, because a woman alone could vanish, could become a widow, could become a servant, could become anyone, because no one looked closely at women, that was the terrible gift of being a woman, invisibility was thrust upon you whether you wanted it or not.

But a daughter — a daughter was a tether. A daughter had a name on a parish roll, a baptism recorded, a face that would be looked for. A daughter was evidence. And evidence, in the winter of 1743, was a thing that got people summoned.

So the hole in the plan was filled with a fiction, and the fiction was made of paper.

* * *

Edmund set the fusee chain down and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw constellations. The bench before her swam back into focus: the merchant's clock movement, disassembled and laid out on baize in strict order, each component in its appointed place, a small metal city at rest.

Her father had made her a promise once, when she was old enough to ask questions and young enough to believe answers. He had said: *The papers are true. Whatever they say, that is what is true. The paper is the truth.* He had said it with such force, such a desperate, hammering force, that she had understood even then that he was not informing her but persuading himself, and she had loved him for the weakness of it, and she had never believed it for a moment.

The apprenticeship paper was dated the third of February, 1744. Edmund knew the date the way she knew her own pulse. It was the date on which Edmund Fairweather, son of Jakob Fairweather, master clockmaker of Heidelberg, was bound apprentice to his own father for a term of seven years, according to the forms of the guild. It was a true document in the way her father meant: it existed, it bore seals, it was filed where such documents were filed, and anyone who consulted it would find it and be satisfied.

It was dated the third of February because the daughter of Jakob Fairweather had been recorded dead on the last day of January.

Edmund had been six when her father finally told her that. She had gone looking for herself — that was the only way to describe it, she had gone looking for herself, for the girl in the blue room, for the child in the parish roll — and she had found a burial entry. *Anna's daughter, aged five years, of a fever, buried the last of January in the year of our Lord 1744.* A small grave in the record of the world. She had carried the knowledge of her own death the way one carries a stone in the shoe: a small wrongness, always present, shaping every step.

Her father had found her with the parish book's memory still in her face — he always knew, he read her like a dial — and he had sat her down on his work stool and told her the truth in the language he trusted best, the language of mechanism.

*"A clock that is seen,"* he said, *"is a clock that is asked about. And a clock that is asked about must be opened. And a clock that is opened must be found to be correct. Do you understand? We are not hiding you, mein Schatz. We are regulating you."*

Regulating. She had carried that word too. It was a clockmaker's word, and it meant something very specific: not the stopping of a clock, not the slowing of it, but the endless small corrections by which a timepiece is kept in agreement with the heavens. A regulated clock was not a free clock. It was a clock in servitude to the stars.

* * *

The rules, then. She had never written them down — writing a thing down was to make it evidence, and evidence was death, she had learned that before she learned long division — but they existed, complete and inviolable, in the chamber of her mind where she kept such things, and she could have recited them the way other children recited catechism.

The first rule: bind the chest. She had begun at eleven, when her body committed its first betrayal, and her father had said nothing, only left a roll of linen bandage and a small jar of the salve that kept the skin from chafing, on the corner of her workbench, wrapped in brown paper as if it were a delivery of parts. That was his way. He legislated by object. The bandage went on every morning before the shirt, wound tight from below, two turns, three, four, until the breath sat high and shallow in the top of her lungs and her reflection in the workshop glass showed her the flat silhouette the world required. She had learned to bind so well she could do it in the dark, half-asleep, in the time it took the kettle to boil. She had learned to breathe the way a bound woman breathes — small breaths, rationed breaths, the breath of someone carrying a full cup of water up a ladder. And she had learned, without ever deciding to learn it, to move as though her body were slightly heavier than it was, to plant her feet, to let the shoulders carry, because a body that moved like water invited the eye, and the eye was the enemy.

The second rule: never sing.

This was the rule that cost the most, though she could not have explained why to anyone, because it was the least necessary rule and therefore the truest. Her voice had not broken — would never break — and she knew it, and her father knew it, and so the voice was locked away entire, the way one locks a room in a house being shown to buyers. She had loved to sing. That was the thing. Before — before the winter, before the papers, before the regulation — she had sung constantly, tunelessly, the little Palatine songs her mother sang while pinning cloth, songs about millers and maidens and the Rhine running to the sea, and her mother had said she had a voice like a blackbird, and Edmund had been five and had believed this was the finest thing anyone would ever say about her.

Now she sang only in the one place no one could hear: in the stairwell of the observatory tower, at midnight, when the master clock's tick filled the whole stone throat of the stairwell and drowned a human voice the way the river drowns a pebble. She sang there, quietly, under the tick, and the tick took her voice and scattered it, and no one was the wiser, and she descended the tower with her throat aching as though she had wept, though she had not, because that was the third rule.

Never weep in daylight.

This rule had been self-imposed, and she knew its origin precisely: she had been eight, and a wheel blank had slipped the graver and cut her thumb to the bone, and she had cried — loudly, childishly, in the high bright voice that was still hers then, unregulated — and her father had come across the workshop like a man running into a burning house and put his hand over her mouth, not cruelly, but with a terror so complete that it frightened her more than the blood had. And he had knelt, as her mother had knelt, and said: *"Not that voice. Never that voice where anyone can hear. If you must cry, cry at night. Cry into the ticking. The tick will hide you. The tick is the only witness we can trust."*

So she wept at night, into the tick, and the tick hid her, and it had hidden her for twelve years, and she had come to believe — she needed to believe — that the clock was not merely a witness but a confessor, that all the sound she had ever swallowed had gone somewhere, had been taken up into the escapement and converted, somehow, into that patient, impartial, eternal *tick, tock, tick*, which asked nothing of anyone and told nothing to anyone and kept faith with the heavens regardless.

* * *

The morning wore on. The blade of light crossed the floor and climbed the wall. Edmund reassembled the merchant's movement — train, escapement, the delicate marriage of barrel and chain — and it came alive under her hands, the balance wheel waking with its small shiver, and she felt the brief, uncomplicated joy that the work always gave her, the joy that was the one payment the fiction made regularly and in full.

She was good. That was the joke of it, the joke only she and her father were in on, the joke that was not a joke at all: the papers were forged, but the craft was real. Edmund Fairweather, son of Jakob, was one of the finest young clockmakers in the Palatinate, and every skill on that apprenticeship paper had been earned, hour by hour, file-stroke by file-stroke, at a bench where no master had ever actually taught a boy named Edmund. The document lied about everything except her competence. Sometimes she thought that was the most obscene thing about it — that the lie had been made *true* by her own hands, that she had spent twelve years manufacturing the evidence of her own fiction.

She was reaching for the oiler when the workshop door opened and her father came in from the street, and she saw at once — she always saw, she read him like a dial — that something had happened. He was carrying a paper. He was carrying it the way he carried bad news, flat against his thigh, as though it weighed more than paper should.

"Edmund." He said the name carefully, the way he always did, placing it like a part into a movement. "There was a woman here. From the magistrate's office. Asking after the household. The census."

"What did you tell her?"

"The truth." He set the paper on the bench. "The truth as it is written."

Edmund looked at the census form, at her father's careful hand — *Jakob Fairweather, master clockmaker, widower, and son, Edmund, apprentice, aged seventeen* — and she felt, beneath the ribs that were bound flat every morning before the shirt, the first small tremor of a mechanism that had run true for twelve years and was, for the first time, showing wear.

Her hands, when she took up the oiler, were not quite steady.
