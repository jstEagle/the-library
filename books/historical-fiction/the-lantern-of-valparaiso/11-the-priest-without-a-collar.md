## 11. The Priest Without a Collar

The chapel had no name anymore. It had been San Rafael de las Cuevas once, carved into the hillside above the Quebrada de Verde in the years when the city below still believed in the patience of stone, but the diocese had struck it from the rolls the same day it struck Tomás Arriagada from the rolls of the living clergy. A chapel without a name could not be closed, he had reasoned at the time. A priest without a collar could not be forbidden to pray. The logic had a certain Jesuitical elegance to it, and Tomás had learned to live on elegance the way other men lived on bread — thinly, and with a great deal of chewing.

He was sweeping the nave when the knock came.

Three raps, spaced like a man measuring his own courage. Not the knock of the poor, who came to his door the way water comes to a low place, without ceremony and without shame. Not the knock of the sailors, who pounded as if the door owed them money. This was the knock of a man who had rehearsed on the stairs.

Tomás set the broom against the wall. The broom was the only thing in the chapel he kept in perfect order, because sweeping was the only sacrament the Church had left him. Everything else — the cracked tiles, the salt-eaten plaster, the altar cloth he had hemmed himself with a sailor's crooked stitch — everything else was allowed to decay at its own pace, honestly, the way all things decayed when no one lied about them.

He opened the door.

The young man on his step was thin the way a plumb line is thin: vertical, taut, and built to hang something heavy from. He had ink on his fingers and the particular pallor of a man who worked indoors among brass and oil, and he held his hat in both hands as though it were an offering he wasn't sure the altar would accept.

"Señor Arriagada," the young man said. Then, catching himself, with a wince: "Or — forgive me. I don't know what one calls you."

"Tomás," Tomás said. "The collar went. The name stayed. Come in before the hill decides whether it's fog or rain."

* * *

The young man's name was Edmund Pemberton, and he did not sit so much as perch, on the very front of the bench, as though the wood might at any moment be reclaimed. Tomás lit the two candles that were permitted to burn — tallow, cheap, guttering — and put the kettle on the spirit stove behind the vestry curtain, because there was no situation in this city, in his long experience, that was not made more survivable by tea, and no confession that could not wait the four minutes it took the water to boil.

"You know who I am," Tomás said, when he came back with the chipped enamel mugs. "Which means someone told you. Which means I'd like to know who, because the list of people who still speak my name in the present tense is short enough to memorize."

"Doña Inés Ferrer. Of the observatory."

"Ah." Tomás sat down across the aisle from him, an old habit — he no longer felt entitled to sit beside anyone. "The Doña. Is she well?"

"She's angry. Mostly. She's well the way a drawn bow is well."

Tomás laughed — a short, rusty sound, the laugh of a hinge that hadn't been oiled in years. "That's the truest description of Inés Ferrer I've heard since the earthquake. Now. You've climbed a very long hill to find a man the Church has thrown away, and you've brought nothing with you but your hands and your fear. I can smell the fear. It smells different on every man. On sailors it smells like rum. On the poor it smells like sweat. On you—" he leaned forward slightly, "—it smells like ink and clock oil. So. What is it you're afraid of, Edmund Pemberton, and why does it need a defrocked priest?"

The young man looked at his tea. He looked at the altar. He looked, for a long moment, at the place on the wall where a crucifix had hung until Tomás had taken it down himself, the day of his expulsion, rather than let the diocese's men touch it — and where now there was only a paler rectangle in the plaster, a ghost of a ghost.

"There's a ledger," Edmund said.

Tomás had swept this chapel for six years. He had buried, in the unconsecrated ground behind it, eleven people the parish cemeteries would not take: two strikers shot on the pier, a woman dead of fever in a room the police would not enter, children whose names no one could prove. He had thought his heart had been packed in salt and stored somewhere above the reach of ordinary weather.

"There's a ledger," the young man said again, "and a child."

* * *

He told it badly and well — badly in the ordering, well in the detail, the way honest men always tell things, because honesty has no rhetoric. A hidden compartment in the master clock at the observatory. Entries in dockworkers' shorthand. Ships owned by Aurelio Vargas that sailed with families aboard and came back light. And then the child, Lucía, seven years old or nearly, left at his lodging with a note in a hand that shook: *forget the face.*

"She's with a laundress now. Mercedes Fuentes. Off the Calle del Almendro. She's safe for the moment — Mercedes is the safest person in Valparaíso, though she'd deny it with a rolling pin in her hand. But safe for the moment isn't safe. Vargas's men are asking questions at the guild. And when they stop asking questions—"

"They'll start making house calls," Tomás finished.

Edmund nodded. His hands had gone still around the mug, which Tomás noted was the opposite of what fear usually did to hands. This was a young man who controlled his fear the way he probably controlled a lathe: precisely, and at some cost.

"Doña Inés said you'd help," Edmund said. "She said — I'm sorry, I'll say it exactly, because it sounded like something she'd been carrying a long time. She said: *tell Tomás the crypt is still his to give, and it is time he gave it.*"

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere up the quebrada a donkey complained about the weight of the world, which was, in Tomás's experience, the donkey's most theologically sound observation.

"She said that, did she." He set down his mug. "Six years, and that woman still knows which of my locks to knock on."

* * *

He took Edmund down the stone steps behind the altar with a candle, because the chapel had no other light to give and because some things deserved to be seen by flame.

The crypt had been a burial vault once, in the chapel's first century — twelve niches in the rock, long emptied, their occupants moved to the municipal cemetery when the diocese still acknowledged this place existed. Now it held crates of mission wine that Tomás sold to no one and drank almost none of, a store of flour and dried fish, two blankets, a lantern, and a cot he had built himself from ship's timber. It was dry. That was its single great virtue and he had defended it for years against the damp with lime and tar and stubbornness, the way he defended most things.

"Twelve hours of candlelight down here if the door stays shut," he said. "Water in the cistern, though I'd boil it. The door is oak, and the lock—" he touched the iron plate, "—the lock is the one mercy of my former life. I learned smithing in Concepción before I learned Latin. No one picks this lock. They'd need powder, and powder brings the whole hill down on them, and even Vargas's men know the quebrada hates them enough already."

Edmund ran his fingers along the niche shelves, the way a clockmaker runs his fingers along anything — reading it, Tomás realized, reading the stone the way he read brass.

"You've hidden people here before."

"Strikers, in 'eighty-seven. A printer the police wanted for pamphlets. A family from the north once — no, don't ask me which family, I've promised God I'd forget the details and God and I have an arrangement: He forgets my pride, I forget my guests." Tomás held the candle higher. "The point is that it holds people, and it holds secrets, and the two have always been the same work in this country."

"Why did they take your collar?" Edmund asked. It came out of him sideways, the way questions do when they've been sitting in a man's chest since the stairs. "I mean — I know why. The strikers. But — you knew it would cost you. You did it anyway. Why?"

Tomás leaned against the cold rock and considered the question, which was not, he suspected, really about him.

"Because I was there," he said. "That's the whole of it, and men have written fat books saying less. The diocese wanted the Church to be a lighthouse — beautiful, distant, and shining only on its own terms. But I had spent twenty years being a lamp in a window, Señor Pemberton. A lamp in a window is a different thing. It says: *this house is awake. This door opens. Come in out of the dark and we will deal with what you are in the morning.* The lighthouse tells ships where the rocks are. The lamp in the window tells them where the harbor is." He straightened. "When they came to the chapel to drag out the men who'd been on strike — men whose only crime was asking to be paid in money instead of company scrip — I stood in the doorway and I did not move. And when they asked me to choose, I chose. That's all a collar ever was, in the end. A promise to choose. They took the cloth. The choosing they could not take."

Edmund was quiet for a while. The candle flame leaned and recovered, leaned and recovered, like a small faithful heart.

"The child," he said at last. "Lucía. She hasn't spoken. Not a word since she came to my door. Mercedes says she will, when she's ready, and Mercedes is usually right about people. But if Vargas's men come for her — if she has to be moved, moved quickly, moved at night—" He stopped. "She's seven. She's seen her father vanish. I need to know that the place I'd put her is a place that has held frightened people before and not failed them."

Tomás looked at the young man for a long moment — at the ink stains and the careful hands and the fear that smelled of clock oil — and understood that he was not being asked for the crypt. He was being asked for something older than the crypt.

"Bring her," he said. "Bring her tonight, if you like, and let her see it in daylight first, so the dark is a room and not a mouth. Let Mercedes come too — a child should not learn a hiding place without her keeper learning it beside her. And tell the Doña—" he smiled, and it pulled at muscles that had forgotten the shape, "—tell the Doña that the crypt is given. Freely. Tell her the account is not settled, but it is open, and I intend to run up a very large balance before God calls it in."

* * *

They climbed back into the nave, and Edmund paused at the pale rectangle on the wall where the crucifix had been.

"You took it down yourself?"

"The day they defrocked me. I carried it home under my coat like a stolen loaf." Tomás went to the vestry and came back with it — a small thing, dark wood, the corpus worn smooth by a century of thumbs — and held it out. "I keep it in a drawer. Not out of bitterness. Out of precision. A crucifix on the wall is a claim. In a drawer, it's a memory. I wasn't entitled to the claim anymore, and I refuse to lie with furniture."

"And now?"

Tomás weighed the little cross in his palm. Somewhere below them, faint as a pulse, the city's bells rang the quarter hour — the cathedral's bells, the diocese's bells, ringing over a city where families boarded Vargas's ships and did not come back, ringing for everyone and no one.

"Now," he said, "I think I'll hang it again."

He found the nail still in the plaster, exactly where he'd left it, because he had never been able to bring himself to pull it out. He set the cross on it. It hung a little crooked, the corpus tilted, as if leaning forward — as if, Tomás thought, listening.

"There," he said. "A chapel without a name, a priest without a collar, a cross hung crooked. It's not a church. But it's awake."

Edmund looked at it, and then at Tomás, and something in the young man's face unclenched — not trust exactly, not yet, but the ground trust is planted in.

"There's one more thing," Edmund said. "The ledger. I've been reading it. The ships, the dates, the families. And there are entries I can't make sense of — columns of shorthand that aren't cargo. Doña Inés thinks they're coordinates, or dates, or—" he hesitated. "She thinks someone at the observatory helped record the disappearances. Years ago. She thinks it might have been someone she knew."

The candle between them had burned down to its last inch, and the light it threw made the crooked crucifix's shadow climb the wall behind the altar, long and dark and leaning, like a man stepping out of a doorway he had stood in for six years.

"Then you'd better tell her," Tomás said quietly, "that whoever it was — whatever they did, and whatever it cost them to write it down — they wrote it *down.* And in this city, Señor Pemberton, in this city, the pen has always been braver than the bell."

He walked the young man to the door. The fog had won its argument with the rain, and the hill was dissolving into white, and somewhere down in the port the lamps of Valparaíso were coming on one by one, small and stubborn, a lamp in every window that refused to be a lighthouse.

Tomás stood in his doorway until the footsteps faded, then went back inside, took up his broom, and began — for the first time in six years — to sweep toward the altar.
