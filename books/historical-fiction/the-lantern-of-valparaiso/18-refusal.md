## 18. Refusal

Mercedes Araya had learned to read a man's silence the way other women read letters. Twenty-two years of washing other people's linen had taught her that the truth of a household lived not in what was said over the table but in what was carried in on the shoulders—damp, heavy, folded tight. So when Edmund Pemberton came through her door that evening, she knew before he had removed his hat that something had happened on the Cerro Alegre, something that had gone through him like a fishhook and was still lodged there.

He was pale in a way that had nothing to do with the fog rolling off the bay. His hands, those precise, careful hands that could coax a dying escapement back to life, were not quite steady as he hung his coat on the nail by the door. And his eyes went first—not to her, not to the pot of beans simmering on the stove—but to the corner where Lucía sat on the floor with a spill of wooden buttons, sorting them by color with the grave concentration of a child building a cathedral.

"Sit," Mercedes said, in the voice she used for men who needed telling. "The beans are nearly done."

"I can't stay long."

"You can stay as long as I say you can, and I say you'll sit and eat, because no man makes good decisions on an empty stomach, and by the look of you, you've got the worst decision of your life sitting in your chest like a swallowed stone."

He almost smiled. It was a small thing, that almost-smile, but Mercedes had learned to count the small things. In the weeks since the girl had appeared at his lodging with her terrible little note, Mercedes had watched this young man learn to count them too—the way Lucía ate her bread crust-first to save the soft part for last, the way she hummed when she thought no one was listening, the way she still flinched at the sound of heavy boots on the stairs. Small things, counted daily, like a miser counting coins that could not be spent.

Lucía looked up from her buttons. "Señor Edmund."

"Señorita Lucía." He crouched, and Mercedes saw him make himself smaller, gentler, the way he did. "What color are you building tonight?"

"Red. Red is the hardest. There's only four."

"Then red must be worth more than the others."

"Everything red is worth more," Lucía said with the flat certainty of the very young, and went back to her sorting, and Mercedes saw the fishhook sink deeper into Edmund's flesh at the words, though she did not yet know why.

She ladled beans into three bowls, set bread and the last of the hard cheese on the table, and waited. Waiting was a weapon she had refined over decades. Her late husband, Esteban, God rest his stubborn soul, had been a man you could not pry open with a crowbar, but set a bowl of food in front of him and stay quiet long enough, and the truth came out of him like steam from a kettle. She used the same method on Edmund now. She ate. She let the silence do its work.

It took him until the bowl was half empty.

"She offered me London."

Mercedes set down her spoon. "Who offered you London."

"Vargas. Aurelio Vargas. He came to the workshop this afternoon—came himself, not his men, not his agents. He stood in the middle of Harrow & Sons among the clocks and he talked to me for twenty minutes as if we were old friends." Edmund's voice was level, too level, the voice of a man holding a door shut against a flood. "He offered me a full apprenticeship in London. Not an apprenticeship—a partnership. A place with the finest horological house in Europe. Wages, lodging, a future. And he offered—" the level voice cracked, just once, and Mercedes heard what was underneath it, "—he offered to clear my father's name. To buy back the debt. To restore the family. Everything my mother wept over for ten years, everything I've spent my whole life trying to live down, erased with a signature and a bank draft."

The stove ticked. Lucía's buttons clicked softly against the floorboards, red against brown against white.

"And what did you tell him?" Mercedes asked, though her heart had already answered for him, had already begun to pound with the particular terror of a woman who knows what a refusal costs.

"I told him no."

The words fell into the room and lay there. Mercedes found that her hands had gone cold around the spoon.

"Tell me exactly," she said. "Every word. If we are to be hanged for this, I want to know the rope."

* * *

He told it plainly, the way he did everything—no embellishment, no drama, a clockmaker's account of the day's work. Vargas had arrived at four in the afternoon, when the workshop was emptying, when the apprentices had gone and old Harrow was upstairs with his gout and his brandy. He had come without escort, which Edmund said was more frightening than a dozen men would have been, because it meant he did not consider Edmund a threat worth escorting against.

"He knew everything. My father's name. The debt. The scandal—how my father signed notes he couldn't cover, how the family lost everything, how I came to Harrow & Sons at fourteen and made myself invisible so no one would remember the name Pemberton meant anything at all." Edmund turned his bowl slowly on the table, not eating now. "He'd done his research. That's what I kept thinking—he'd done his research on a nobody, on an apprentice clockmaker, because a nobody had seen a ledger he wanted buried. And then he made the offer, and he made it kindly, Mercedes. That's what I can't stop thinking about. He wasn't cruel. He was generous. He talked about my father's craftsmanship—said he remembered a Pemberton regulator in his father's house, said it kept better time than anything made since. He said talent like mine shouldn't rot in Valparaíso repairing parlor clocks for widows."

"Cheek," Mercedes muttered. "I pay promptly."

"And then he said the other thing." Edmund looked at the corner, at the small bent head among the buttons. "He said the child would be better off in a proper institution. A Catholic orphanage in Santiago. He said she'd forget. He said children forget everything." His jaw worked. "And I told him no. I told him the ledger was gone, that I'd never seen any ledger, and that the girl was none of his concern and never had been. And he smiled at me—Mercedes, he smiled like a man watching a clock run down—and he said, 'I admire loyalty, Señor Pemberton. It's rare. It's also expensive.' And he left."

Silence. The beans cooled. Outside, the fog had thickened until the harbor bells came through it muffled and strange, like sounds from another country.

"He'll strike," Edmund said at last, and now the level voice was gone entirely, and Mercedes heard the young man underneath, the one who had spent his whole life being small and was only now learning what it cost to stand up in a room. "Not tonight. Not tomorrow, maybe. But soon. A man like Vargas doesn't make an offer like that and walk away when it's refused. The offer was a courtesy—the kind of courtesy you extend to someone you hope will make this easy. When the courtesy fails, the next thing isn't another offer. It's men at the door in the dark. It's fire. It's—" He stopped, because Lucía had looked up, and some instinct had caught the word *fire* before it reached her ears and swallowed it.

"Lucía," Mercedes said, rising, "go to the chest at the foot of my bed and bring me the blue shawl. The wool one. It's cold in here tonight and you'll want it."

The girl went, obedient, her bare feet quick on the boards, and the moment the bedroom door had clicked shut behind her, Edmund leaned forward and spoke low and fast.

"It's days. Three, four at the most. He'll move before the consul ship sails—he has to, or the ledger's beyond his reach forever. He'll come for the girl first, because the girl is the witness and the ledger is only paper. And when he can't find the girl, he'll come for everyone who might know where she is. You. Me. Doña Inés. Tomás. Everyone." He passed a hand over his eyes. "I should never have brought this to your door. I knew that the night I stood on your step with her asleep against my shoulder. I knew it and I did it anyway, because there was nowhere else, and I have hated myself for it every day since."

Mercedes let him finish. Then she went to the bedroom door, took the blue shawl from Lucía's arms, wrapped it around the girl's shoulders herself, and sent her back to her buttons with a kiss on the crown of her head. Only then did she sit down across from Edmund and fold her hands, the way she did before saying something that was not negotiable.

"Edmund Pemberton. Look at me."

He looked.

"Six years ago I buried my husband in the paupers' row at the Cementerio General, and I stood at that grave with hands still raw from other people's washing, and I asked God a question I have never asked Him before or since. I asked Him what I was for. Because I had no children, and no family left in this country, and a house that echoed, and arms that had nothing in them to hold. Six years I washed other people's linen and listened to other people's lives through the walls and told myself that was enough. That being useful was the same as being loved." Her voice did not shake. She would not permit it to shake. "Then you came to my door with a child on your shoulder who smelled of the sea and of fear, and she looked at my house—at my *house*, this narrow little house on a hill where the stairs creak and the roof leaks—and she looked at me, and she did not flinch. Do you understand? Everyone in this city flinches from me. The rich women whose sheets I wash, they look through me. The priests give me alms the way you'd toss a bone to a dog. But that child looked at me and saw a person, and she took my hand, and she has not let go of it since except to sort her buttons."

"Mercedes—"

"I am not finished." She had not raised her voice in eleven years, not since Esteban's funeral, but she raised it now, quietly, the way a wave raises itself. "You say you should never have brought her here. You say you have hated yourself. Well, I am going to tell you what I told the Virgin the night you left her sleeping in my bed and went back down the hill alone. I thanked God for you. I thanked Him for the first time in six years, because He had finally answered my question. *This* is what you are for. This child. This ridiculous, brave, foolish boy who cannot lie properly and repairs clocks and has carried a burden that would break a bigger man. You brought her to my door, and whatever comes through that door after her, I will meet it with a boiling pot and the grace of God, and I will not regret one hour of it. Do you hear me? Not one hour."

Edmund's eyes were wet. He did not wipe them, which Mercedes counted as a kind of courage.

"She calls you Mamá Mercedes," he said. "When she thinks I can't hear. She told Doña Inés she has a mamá again, and Doña Inés cried, and Inés never cries, she says it fogs the lenses."

"Then we are all fools together," Mercedes said, "and fools together are stronger than wise men alone. Now. Tell me the plan. Because you did not come up this hill only to refuse a magnate and frighten an old laundress. You came because you need us, and thank God, you finally have the sense to say so."

* * *

They talked until the candle had burned down to a thumb's width and Lucía had fallen asleep on the cot behind the curtain, the blue shawl pulled up to her chin, one hand still curled around a single red button she had smuggled to bed like contraband. Edmund laid it out in his careful, methodical way: the consul ship, the *Andromeda*, sailing Saturday's morning tide. The checkpoints between here and the harbor gate—the customs house, the funicular, the gate itself, each one watched by men on Vargas's payroll. Tomás's crypt as a waystation. Doña Inés and her lamp. The coat, and the shorthand sewn into its lining, stitch by stitch, by candlelight.

"Saturday," Mercedes said. "Four days."

"Three and a half, if the tide's early."

"Then we have three and a half days, and we will need every one of them, and you will need feeding, because no one forges documents or outwits customs men on an empty stomach." She was already making the list in her head, the way she made lists for everything—linen to be washed, bread to be bought, a word to be passed to the fishwife on the corner whose brother worked the harbor gate and owed her for the winter she'd washed his children's clothes without charge. "And Edmund. The offer he made you. The London apprenticeship. Your father's name." She reached across the table and put her rough hand over his fine one. "I want you to hear me say this, because you will not say it to yourself. What you gave up tonight was not nothing. It was everything you have wanted since you were a boy. And you gave it up for a child who cannot thank you properly and a ledger full of dead people's names. That is not foolishness. That is the finest thing I have seen a man do in this city in twenty years, and I have seen everything this city has to show."

He bowed his head over their hands. For a long moment the only sounds were the fog-bound bells and the child's slow breathing behind the curtain.

"I was so tempted," he whispered. "God forgive me, Mercedes, for one whole minute I was so tempted I couldn't feel the floor."

"That minute is why I trust you," she said. "A man who cannot be tempted cannot be trusted. A man who is tempted and stands anyway—that is the only man worth standing beside."

He left near midnight, down the creaking stairs and into the fog, and Mercedes stood at the window and watched until the dark took him, and then she stood there longer, listening to the city below—the port that ate people, the hills that kept their secrets, the great dark mass of the customs house where she had once, years ago, waited six hours with a dead husband's papers and been made to feel like a beggar in her own country.

Three and a half days.

She turned back to the room. Lucía slept on, small and warm, the red button held fast in her fist. Mercedes knelt beside the cot, as she did every night now, and tucked the shawl higher, and felt the old terror move through her—the fear she had accepted on the first night, the fear she would carry until Saturday and past it, the fear of boots on the stairs and fire and the particular cruelty of men who lose at cards and take it out on children. She let the fear come. She let it fill her like cold water fills a basin. And then, as she did every night, she set it aside—not gone, never gone, but put in its place, on the low shelf where it belonged—because on the table beside the cold beans sat a child's wooden buttons sorted into piles, and one of the piles was red, and red, the child said, was worth more than all the others.

She blew out the candle.

She was just turning back the coverlet when she heard it: a sound from the street below, faint through the fog. Not boots. Not voices. Something smaller. The soft, deliberate scrape of a match being struck, once, and the brief bloom of light through the gap in the shutters—there and gone, like a signal, like a lamp on a hill.

Mercedes stood very still in the dark, her hand on the curtain, and listened to the fog, and counted the seconds, and waited to hear it again.
