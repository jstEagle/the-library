## 20. Rosa's Choice

The rain had stopped by the time Rosa finished the last of the guild's ledgers, but the damp had worked its way into everything—the stone walls of Harrow & Sons, the wool of her shawl, the marrow of her bones. She sat in the counting room with a single lamp turned low, because lamplight cost money and her father counted money the way other men counted sins: obsessively, fearfully, as though one day the sum would come due.

She was copying figures into the fair book when she found the discrepancy.

It was small. That was what made it terrible. A withdrawal of forty pesos marked against the apprentices' tool fund, entered in her brother's hand, dated the twelfth of October. Then another, thirty pesos, the twenty-ninth. Then another. The entries were neat—Mateo had always had beautiful handwriting, their mother used to say he'd wasted it on a boy's hands—and they were wrong in a way that only someone who had kept the guild's books for six years would notice. The tool fund column did not reconcile. It never reconciled anymore. She had assumed it was sloppiness. Mateo was many things, but sloppy with numbers had never been one of them.

Rosa set down her pen.

She looked at the ledger for a long time without seeing it, the way you look at a familiar street and suddenly cannot remember whether you have always lived there. Forty pesos was not nothing. Forty pesos was two months of a laundress's wages, or a coffin, or a passage north to Santiago for a man who owed money to the wrong people. Thirty more was a debt paid twice. And there were eleven entries like it going back to March.

She thought, first, of the simplest explanation. He had borrowed against the fund and meant to return it. Men did that. Her father raged about it every quarter, and every quarter some journeyman or other sheepishly restored what he'd taken, and the world went on turning. That was the story she could live inside. She reached for it the way a drowning woman reaches for driftwood.

But Mateo had not restored anything. Eleven entries. Seven months. And the withdrawals had grown larger as the year had gone on, which meant whatever hole he was filling was getting deeper, not shallower.

Rosa closed the ledger and pressed both palms flat against its cover, as if she could hold the truth shut inside it like a hand pressed over a wound.

Outside, the bells of La Matriz rang the half hour, and somewhere below in the callejon a dog barked at nothing, and the city went on being the city—smoke and gulls and the groan of ships—and Rosa Whitlock's brother was stealing from their father's shop, and she had just become the only person in Valparaíso who knew it.

* * *

She did not confront him that night. She wanted to; her whole body wanted it, the way a tooth wants to be touched by the tongue. But she had learned from watching her mother die slowly and her father grow old angrily that the first thing you said in a moment like this was the thing you could never take back. So she banked the lamp, climbed the stairs past Mateo's door—he was out again, third night this week, and the knowledge of where he might be sat in her stomach like swallowed glass—and lay awake in her narrow bed listening to the harbor working through the dark.

Her family had been part of Harrow & Sons for longer than most families had been part of anything. Her grandfather had apprenticed under old Harrow himself, back when the shop was two rooms and a reputation. Her father had given it his spine. And Mateo—Mateo had been born with gold dust in his fingers, everyone said so, the natural heir, the one who could feel an escapement's fault through the case. Rosa had gotten the ledgers instead, and the customers' tempers, and her father's silences. She had made her peace with that years ago, mostly.

What she could not make peace with, lying there, was the memory of Mateo at fourteen, caught skimming coins from the till—not much, a few reales—and how their father had taken the strap to him while Rosa stood in the doorway and Mateo had looked at her over their father's shoulder with an expression she had spent twenty years trying to forget. Not remorse. Appeal. *Say something*, that look said. *Tell him I'm sorry before he asks me to be.* And Rosa had said nothing, because she was fifteen and afraid, and the strap had come down, and something in her brother had quietly closed like a shop shutter at the end of a bad day.

She had told herself since that it was nothing, that boys forgot, that men were not made of single moments. But now, in the dark, with seven months of theft sitting downstairs in a locked cabinet, she wondered if she had been watching a slow leak all this time and calling it weather.

He came home near three. She heard his boots on the stair, careful the way drunk-careful men are loud, and then his door, and then nothing. Rosa stared at the ceiling until the window went grey.

* * *

She followed him the next evening.

She hated herself for it even as she buttoned her coat, told herself it was not spying, it was—what? Due diligence. Verification. All the dry words for the wet, shameful act of trailing your own blood through the streets of your own city to catch him doing the thing you already knew he was doing.

Mateo left the shop at half past six, dressed better than a journeyman clockmaker ought to dress. New boots. A coat with a velvet collar that Rosa had never seen before, cut in the English style, the kind of coat that announced itself in rooms. He walked down toward the Almendral, not toward the taverns on the hill where the guild men drank, and Rosa kept half a block behind him in the crowd of dockworkers and fishwives and sailors spilling ashore with their pay, and her heart beat the way it had beaten when she was a girl running from the schoolmistress's cane.

He stopped at a coffee house on the Calle de los Serranos—one of the new ones, gas-lit, with mirrors behind the counter and prices that kept out the wrong sort of people. Rosa could not go in. She stood across the street beneath the awning of a shuttered milliner's shop, close enough to smell the rain coming off the sea, and watched through the fogged glass as her brother took a table in the corner.

And then a man joined him.

Rosa knew the face before she knew the name, the way you know a storm cloud. Aurelio Vargas's factor—a smooth, grey-eyed Chilean named Ossandón who handled the magnate's unpleasant business the way other men handled correspondence. She had seen him once at a guild function, gliding between conversations, laughing at things that were not funny. Now he sat across from her brother with a leather folio open between them, and Mateo was talking, hands moving, and Ossandón was nodding, and at one point the factor slid something across the table—an envelope, thick, the thickness of folded banknotes—and Mateo covered it with his hand and looked, briefly, around the room.

Rosa stepped back into the shadow of the awning. Her breath had gone strange and shallow.

They talked another ten minutes. She could read none of it through the glass, only the shapes: Mateo leaning forward, urgent; Ossandón still as a lizard on a warm wall; the envelope disappearing into Mateo's coat. Then they rose, shook hands like businessmen, and parted—Ossandón out the front door toward the customs house, Mateo up the hill toward home with the walk of a man whose pockets have been recently lightened of guilt and heavy with its price.

Rosa stood under the awning long after both men were gone, letting the cold come up through her shoes, trying to make the arithmetic work.

Forty pesos from the tool fund. A velvet-collared coat. An envelope from Vargas's factor. And underneath it all, like a watermark held to the light, the thing the whole port had been whispering about for weeks: the observatory clock, the repair, the apprentice Pemberton who had been seen going up the hill four times in one week. The guildmaster's son would know all of it. Would have been asked all of it, casually, over brandy, by men who knew how to ask.

*Oh, Mateo,* she thought, and the thought was not anger. Anger would come later, she suspected, in great hot waves. What she felt now was older and worse: the specific grief of watching someone you love walk into deep water with his eyes closed.

* * *

She waited up for him. This time she did not pretend otherwise. When his key turned in the lock past midnight, she was sitting at the kitchen table with the lamp lit and the guild ledger open in front of her, turned to the page, and she watched his face do three things in the space of a second: surprise, calculation, and then the shuttered blankness he had worn since he was fourteen years old.

"You're up late," he said.

"So are you." She turned the ledger around so it faced him. "Eleven entries. Since March. Don't insult us both by asking what I mean."

For a moment he actually considered lying to her. She saw it happen, saw the lie assemble itself behind his eyes—*I was going to pay it back, it's complicated, Father miscounted*—and she watched it fail, because she was his sister and she had been reading his face since before either of them could talk.

"How much do they know?" she asked quietly.

"Who?"

"Don't." Her voice cracked on it, and she hated that too. "Don't do the innocent voice. I stood outside the coffee house on Serranos tonight. I watched you take money from Ossandón. Vargas's Ossandón." She pushed the ledger aside and leaned forward. "What did you sell him, Mateo?"

The silence stretched. Down in the callejon a cart rattled past, iron-shod wheels on cobblestones, and the sound seemed to pass right through the house.

"Nothing that matters," he said finally, and it was the worst possible answer, because it confirmed everything and confessed nothing, and because she could hear in it that he believed it—that he had already performed the little surgery on his conscience that let a man sleep after such work. *Nothing that matters.* As if the question of what mattered belonged to him alone.

"Is it the Pemberton boy?" she pressed. "The observatory? They're saying Vargas has been asking about him all over the port."

"Rosa—"

"There are families missing, Mateo." She hadn't meant to say it; it came out of her like something escaping. "Whole families off those ships of his, and no one will say where they've gone, and you're taking his money in a coffee house like it's a business meeting. What did he buy from you? Tell me exactly, because I need to know how bad this is."

"It's gambling," he burst out, and then, horribly, the rest came with it, the dam going all at once. "It started as gambling, cards, the tables on the Cerro Alegre, I was winning, everyone wins at first, that's how they—" He stopped, dragged a hand down his face. "I owe four hundred pesos to men who broke Ferreyra's arm last month for less. You don't understand what they're like. Ossandón came to *me*. He knew. He always knows. He said all he wanted was information. Names, movements. Who goes up to the observatory, when, how often. Nothing that hurts anyone. Paper, Rosa. He's paying for paper."

"Paper is how it starts," Rosa said. "Paper is how it always starts. And then one day the paper has a name on it and the name gets crossed out."

"He's a clockmaker's apprentice!" Mateo's voice rose, defensive, and she heard beneath it the note that frightened her most: conviction. He had built himself a cathedral of justification and he was living in it. "A nobody from nowhere with a disgraced father. Whatever Vargas wants with him, it isn't—it can't be worth—"

"Worth what?" Rosa stood. The chair scraped. "Worth a child? Because that's what they say Vargas is looking for, Mateo. A child. A girl. Have you sold him that too? Did you tell him where she sleeps?"

"I don't know anything about any child." But he said it too fast, and his eyes went to the wall, and Rosa felt something in her chest turn over and begin, very slowly, to break.

* * *

After he had slammed out—she heard him on the stairs, then the street door, then nothing—she sat alone in the kitchen until the lamp burned low, and she did the only thing left to do, which was to think.

Blood was blood. That was the first law of her life, written before any scripture, older than the guild, older than the city. Your brother steals, you cover the shortfall and shame him privately. Your brother gambles, you sell what you must and stand beside him in the ruin. Every family in Valparaíso ran on that law the way ships ran on tide. Her father would tell her, if she brought this to him, to handle it inside the walls. Her dead mother would have said the same. The whole weight of her childhood leaned on her, whispering: *he is yours, and yours is yours, and the world outside the family is a cold place.*

But the world outside the family contained a girl of perhaps seven years old with a shawl Rosa had seen described in the market gossip, a girl whose father had copied a ledger and vanished for it. It contained a quiet apprentice who fixed clocks and kept his head down and had done nothing to anyone. It contained Mercedes Fuentes, who washed for half the hill and had buried a husband and still laughed at funerals. It contained Doña Inés up in her tower, and a priest without a collar, and all of them, she now understood, standing in a line between Vargas and whatever he meant to erase.

And it contained Mateo—her Mateo, who had taught her to whistle, who had carried her on his shoulders through the 1868 floodwaters, who cried at the death of their dog and denied it for thirty years—standing on the other side of that line, holding the door open.

If she stayed silent, she was complicit. If she spoke, she destroyed him. There was no third door. She walked the corridor between those two doors all night, back and forth, and every time she reached for one, the other grew heavier.

Near dawn she took a clean sheet of paper and wrote two letters, neither of which she sent. In the first she wrote to her brother as he had been at ten years old, and it said: *come home from wherever you have gone.* In the second she wrote nothing at all, because there was no letter that could hold what she had decided, and what she had decided was this:

She would not save him from himself. No one could; she saw that now; the water was already over his head. But she would not let him drown anyone else. If the choice was between her brother's ruin and a child's life, then God forgive her, God judge her, the child. The child, the child, the child. Let the whole port call her a traitor to her blood. Blood was what you were born into. Justice was what you chose.

Rosa banked the fire, put on her coat, and slipped out into the grey morning with the address of a certain laundress written on a scrap of paper in her glove, and the bells of La Matriz ringing for early mass behind her like a warning—or, she thought, climbing the wet streets toward the cerro, like permission.
