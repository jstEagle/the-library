## 22. Interrogation

The room they put him in had once been an office. Edmund could tell by the ghost of a shelf bracket on the wall, by the pale rectangle where a calendar had hung for years and been taken down in a hurry, by the way the afternoon light came through a single high window barred with iron that someone had painted over so many times the bars looked swollen, diseased. A table. Two chairs. A lamp with a cracked glass chimney that gave off more smell than light.

He was made to sit facing the door. He understood the intention of it—every man who entered would come to him out of the glare of the corridor, while he sat blinking in their shadow—but he found he did not mind. In fact, he was grateful. His whole life had been spent watching doors. Doors told you things. The customs house door was oak, older than the building's pretensions, and it had settled on its hinges the way all old doors settle, which meant it opened with a particular drag at the bottom, a sound like a low cough, and no one could enter this room without announcing himself three seconds in advance.

Edmund Pemberton sat in a hard chair in the Customs House of Valparaíso with his left eye swelling shut and blood drying stiffly at his collar, and he counted seconds.

It was what his hands wanted to do anyway. They were trembling—not from fear, or not only from fear, but from the beating, which had been brief and professional and aimed, he suspected, less at hurting him than at reminding him what hurting was—and trembling hands wanted occupation. So he occupied them invisibly. He tapped out the tick of a deadbeat escapement against his thigh, two beats and a rest, two beats and a rest, the rhythm Doña Inés kept in her tower, the rhythm of the master clock he had wound and oiled and coaxed back to life with his own fingers. *Two beats and a rest.* As long as he could keep time in his body, he told himself, he could keep himself. A clockmaker who loses count is only a man in an apron. A clockmaker who keeps count is still, somewhere, at his bench, no matter what room they have put him in.

The door coughed. Three seconds later, a man entered.

He was not one of the ones who had beaten him. Those men had been dock muscle, thick-necked and incurious, doing work they did not think about. This man thought about his work. He was perhaps fifty, silvering at the temples, dressed in the gray-black of a customs functionary rather than the silk of a Vargas lieutenant, and he carried a leather folder under his arm the way a priest carries a breviary. He set it on the table, sat down opposite Edmund, opened the folder, and said nothing for a long moment. He let the silence do its labor. Edmund recognized the technique. It was the same one used on apprentices who had broken a movement: say nothing, and let the boy fill the quiet with confession.

Edmund had been an apprentice. He knew how to wait out a master.

"Señor Pemberton." The man's voice was mild, almost apologetic. "My name is Roldán. I am an inspector of manifests. I want you to understand that this is not—" he gestured vaguely at Edmund's face, at the room, at the whole apparatus of the evening— "*this*. This was not my arrangement. Someone panicked, and panicked men are clumsy. I am sorry for your eye."

"You're welcome," Edmund said, before he could stop himself.

Something flickered at the corner of Roldán's mouth. Not quite a smile. An appraisal. "You are the apprentice sent up to Cerro Alegre. To the observatory. To repair the master clock."

"I am."

"The guild records say you are twenty-three years old, four years into your articles, unremarkable in your examinations, and noted by your master as—here is the phrase—'*quiet, careful, and without ambition*.'" Roldán read it as though tasting each word. "Without ambition. That is a rare thing in this port. Everyone here wants something. The Chileans want passage out, the English want cargo in, the Germans want both, and Señor Vargas—" he closed the folder gently— "Señor Vargas wants everything. And you, señor, want nothing. A man who wants nothing cannot be bought. It makes him very difficult. It also makes him very interesting."

Edmund kept his breathing even and his eyes on the inspector's tie knot, slightly left of center, because looking a man in the eye during an interrogation was a young man's mistake and he had decided, somewhere between the blows, that he was done making mistakes born of wanting to seem braver than he was. He was not brave. He was precise. Precision would have to serve.

"I repaired a clock," he said. "I don't know what else there is to tell you."

"No," Roldán agreed pleasantly. "Of course not. Tell me about the clock, then. I confess I am curious. It is a remarkable instrument, they say. A regulator of the highest grade, made in Liverpool, keeping time for the whole hill—the port authority sets its chronometers by it, the ships set their watches by the port authority, and every cargo manifest, every bill of lading, every arrival and departure recorded in this building—" he laid one finger flat on the table— "is timestamped, ultimately, by that machine. You see why a customs inspector might take an interest. That clock is, in a sense, the honest witness of everything that happens in this harbor. When it runs true, we know when things happen. When it stops—" the finger lifted— "things can happen that leave no mark at all."

*He knows,* Edmund thought, with a coldness that felt almost like clarity. *Not everything. But he knows the shape of it.* A clock that stops is a clock that can be lied around. Somewhere in the last years, ships had sailed and returned empty, families had vanished between one timestamp and the next, and the timestamps themselves had been managed. He thought of the ledger hidden in the clock's housing, written in dock shorthand, recording exactly those gaps. He thought of Lucía's coat lining, sewn now with ciphered copies. He thought: *they stopped the clock to make me come back, and they caught me, and now the man across the table is telling me, in his careful way, that time itself was the first thing bought.*

"Then you'll be glad to hear it runs true again," Edmund said. "Within two seconds a day. Better than it has in years."

"Better than it has in years," Roldán repeated softly. "Yes. My predecessor let it drift. Deliberately, I sometimes think. A drifting clock is a useful clock, if you are in the business my predecessor was in." He turned the folder around and slid a paper across the table. "But we are not here to discuss horology, are we, señor. We are here to discuss this."

Edmund looked down. It was a copy of the guild's work order—his summons, transcribed. Beneath it lay something else: a sketch. A pencil drawing, quick but competent, of a girl. Perhaps seven years old. Dark hair. A shawl drawn with a few economical strokes.

His heart did something complicated and painful, and he made his face do nothing at all. Four years of being invisible had taught him at least that much: the face is a mechanism too, and it can be regulated like any other. Grief, terror, love—all of them produce motion. Motion can be damped. You damp it the way you damp a pendulum's overshoot, with weight, with friction, with deliberate resistance applied at the exact moment of swing.

"Who is she?" Roldán asked.

"A child," Edmund said. "There are many in Valparaíso."

"There are many in Valparaíso," Roldán agreed. "This one was seen. On Cerro Alegre. Near the observatory road. Seen by a man who sells information to people who pay him, and one of the people who pays him pays very well indeed, and the information he purchased was simple: *find the child, find who shelters her, and find the apprentice who came down from the clock tower carrying more than tools.*" He folded his hands. "You were seen, señor. On Tuesday night. Coming down from the observatory after dark, moving quickly, with your coat held closed in a manner that suggested weight. Where is the ledger?"

And there it was, plain at last, the word spoken aloud in a government room, and Edmund understood that the question was not really a question. Roldán already believed the ledger existed. The question was a test of a different kind—a weighing, like putting a movement on the poising tool, seeing where the imbalance lay. Did Edmund deny? Denial confirmed value. Did Edmund bargain? Bargaining confirmed fear. Every answer moved the needle; only perfect stillness read true, and perfect stillness was impossible, because the needle always trembled a little, and a skilled reader could learn from the trembling itself.

So Edmund chose the third path, the one Roldán had not priced: he answered the wrong question, thoroughly, tediously, and with complete technical sincerity.

"That depends entirely on which ledger you mean," he said. "Because a clock of that grade generates documentation the way a ship generates wake. There's the original maker's record from Liverpool—forty-one pages, if the observatory still has it, though half of it will be in a hand nobody living can read. There's the maintenance log, which should have been kept monthly and wasn't, which means the oiling history is a guess at best. There's the rate book, where daily time comparisons are supposed to be entered against the transit observations, and I'll tell you frankly, señor, whoever kept that book before me understood stars better than escapements, because half the entries conflate sidereal and mean time, which is forgivable in an astronomer and catastrophic in a clockmaker. Then there are the parts invoices, the guild's certification papers, and the insurance valuation from 1881, which valued the movement alone at more than this entire building." He paused, and let his swollen eye do some honest work for him. "So. Which ledger?"

Roldán regarded him for a long moment. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below them, in the guts of the customs house, a bell rang twice—some internal signal, some shift change—and Edmund counted the interval automatically, *four seconds, a ship's bell, doubled*, and hated himself a little for how much comfort the counting gave him.

"You are either the most innocent man in this city," Roldán said at last, "or the most disciplined. I genuinely cannot tell, and I will admit that I do not enjoy being unable to tell."

"My father used to say a similar thing about me," Edmund said. "Usually with disappointment."

"Your father." Roldán opened the folder again, and this time what he drew out made the floor tilt very slightly under Edmund's chair. It was an old newspaper clipping, yellowed, soft at the folds. He knew it before he read it. He had known it since he was nine years old. THE PEMBERTON AFFAIR, the headline said, and beneath it a woodcut of a man with Edmund's own deep-set eyes, older, heavier, ruined. *Clockmaker to the shipping houses accused of falsifying chronometer certificates—fled the country—whereabouts unknown.*

"They tell me you do not speak of him," Roldán said. "That at the guild you are careful never to be asked. That you took your mother's name in the apprenticeship rolls, or nearly enough of it. A man builds a small life, a quiet life, a life *without ambition*, and he builds it that way on purpose, because of a father." He tapped the clipping. "Here is what interests me, señor Pemberton. Aurelio Vargas signed the complaint against your father. Twenty-two years ago. Your father refused to certify a shipment of chronometers—one shipment, one—and Vargas ended him. Ruined him in a week. And now, twenty-two years later, the son of that man is summoned by name to the observatory, repairs the clock that timestamps this port, and comes down the hill at night with his coat full of something heavy." Roldán leaned forward, and for the first time his mildness dropped away, and Edmund saw the actual man underneath, tired and sharp and possibly even honest. "I do not work for Vargas, whatever my salary says. I work for the revenue, and the revenue has been bleeding through this port for a decade, and I have known it and proved nothing. So I will ask you once, plainly, man to man, and you may lie to me if you wish, but know that I will know it: *are you the son settling a debt, or a thief selling what he found, or something else?*"

The room was very quiet. Through the high window, far off, Edmund could hear the harbor—the slap of water, a winch groaning, the gulls—and beneath it all, faint as memory, the ten-second gun from the fort that fired at noon, long past now, its echo gone but its interval still fixed in him like a rivet.

He thought about lying. He was good at it now; the week had taught him. He thought about the truth, and what the truth would cost, and who would pay it—Mercedes with her fierce gray eyes, Tomás in his collarless black, Inés alone in her tower with the great lamp, and Lucía, Lucía asleep somewhere he had to believe was safe, wearing a coat lined with the names of the dead.

And then he thought about his father, whose face he had spent his whole adult life refusing to look at, and heard himself say:

"He didn't falsify anything. My father. He refused to certify chronometers that had been tampered with—bimetallic compensation springs swapped for cheap ones, so the ships' masters would think they had instruments accurate to a second a day when they had junk that drifted a minute a week. He told the truth about it. One shipment. One act of honesty, and it cost him everything, and I have spent four years apologizing for it by being small." He looked up, and met Roldán's eyes, and let the needle swing all the way over. "I'm not settling a debt, inspector. I'm finishing his certification. That's all any of this is. Somebody has to sign the truth, eventually. It might as well be a clockmaker."

Roldán sat back slowly. For a moment he looked older, and wearier, and something in his face that had been an inspector's face became briefly just a man's.

"Do you know," he said quietly, "in nineteen years in this building, that is the first time anyone has answered a question of mine with an answer." He gathered the clipping, the sketch, the work order, squared them, and returned them to the folder. Then he stood, and buttoned his coat, and did something Edmund had not expected: he crossed to the door and spoke to the guard outside in a low voice, and when he came back he set a key on the table between them.

"The corridor door locks from the inside. The window is thirty feet above the courtyard and the courtyard is watched, so I advise against romance. You have until morning. At dawn, Señor Vargas's legal representative arrives with a warrant of detention, and after that, señor, neither my manners nor my key will protect you." He paused at the door, hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "For what it is worth—the clock on Cerro Alegre. I checked it myself, this afternoon, against my own watch. Two seconds a day. It has not run that true in eleven years." The door coughed open. "Someone taught you well. Whoever it was—they'd be proud."

Then he was gone, and the lock clicked, and Edmund was alone with the lamp and the key and the enormous dark arithmetic of the hours until dawn.

* * *

He worked the problem the only way he knew how: as a mechanism.

Every system has constraints. The constraint here was time—he had perhaps nine hours, maybe fewer, before Vargas's lawyers arrived and the soft machinery of Roldán's decency was replaced with harder instruments. Within that constraint, the components were these: himself, detained; the ledger, whose location he must assume was compromised or soon would be; Lucía, hidden with Mercedes, protected by distance and Mercedes's ferocity and nothing else; Inés, in the tower; Tomás, in his chapel; and the consul, whom Inés had sworn she could reach if it came to the worst, through channels she would not name, through a friendship she called *the only useful thing I ever did with my vanity.*

The question—the only question—was whether Inés knew he had been taken.

They had planned for capture; that had been Tomás's insistence, weeks ago, in the crypt, pacing among the old bones with his candle. *"If one of us goes, the others hold. No rescue. No negotiation. The package moves whether we move or not."* Edmund had agreed. Everyone had agreed. Agreement was easy in a crypt with candle stubs and no guns visible. Now the agreement sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. *No rescue.* Fine. He did not want a rescue. What he wanted was smaller and colder: he wanted Inés to light the lamp tonight in the pattern they had chosen, the triple occultation, three flashes, three darknesses, repeated—the signal that meant *taken, proceed, trust no one official*—and he wanted that signal to reach whichever sympathetic officer Doña Inés had spent forty years of starlit patience cultivating. He wanted the machinery, all of it, every gear he had helped assemble, to keep turning without him. That was the whole point of a train of wheels: remove any single one, and the motion continues, transmitted onward, because that is what a train is *for*.

But a train needs a driver, and at dawn the driver's seat would be empty, and the man arriving with the warrant would not be Roldán.

He got up, tested the corridor door—it locked, truly, the key turning with a satisfying, well-machined click, Roldán had not lied about that—and then he did the thing he had been resisting since they threw him into the chair. He went to the high barred window and looked out.

Valparaíso at night. The bay a smear of reflected lamplight, the ships at anchor riding their lights like lanterns on black water, the hills rising in tiers of small fires—every window a flame, every flame a household, thousands of them, and somewhere among them one small girl sleeping, and one widow sitting up with a poker across her knees, and one defrocked priest pretending to pray and actually planning routes through colonial cisterns, and one old woman alone in a stone tower with the largest lamp on the coast at her command.

He searched the skyline for Cerro Alegre. Found it. Found the observatory dome, a pale shoulder against the stars.

And then—so faint he doubted it, so faint he made himself count before believing it—the light atop the tower blinked. Went dark. Blinked again.

One. Darkness. Two. Darkness. Three.

Three flashes, three darknesses, steady as a heartbeat, beginning again.

*Taken, proceed, trust no one official.*

Edmund pressed his forehead against the cold bars and let out a breath he felt he had been holding for four years, or perhaps his whole life. She had seen. She had understood. The old woman in the tower had looked down at the customs house—of course she had; she looked at everything, she had spent fifty years reading light that had traveled centuries to reach her, and one small signal from one small window was nothing—and she had answered him across the whole dark city, punctual as astronomy.

*All right,* he thought. *All right. Then my job is simpler.* His job was to survive until dawn. His job was to be, tomorrow, in front of Vargas's lawyers and whatever magistrate they had purchased, exactly what he had been tonight: tedious, technical, immovable, a man who answered questions with answers until decent men like Roldán began to wonder whose side the law was actually on. Wit as escapement—releasing the tension one measured tooth at a time, never letting the spring down all at once, never letting the whole weight of him drop and smash.

He returned to the chair. He turned it to face the door, because doors told you things, and tomorrow's door would tell him a great deal. He set the key where he could reach it. He touched his swollen eye once, gingerly, and decided it was not broken, merely adjusted.

And then, because there was nothing else to do and nine hours to do it in, Edmund Pemberton folded his hands in his lap and kept time. Two beats and a rest. Two beats and a rest. Somewhere above him the stars turned on gears older than any clockmaker's dream, and somewhere across the city a lamp flashed its patient three, and somewhere a child slept with the names of the dead sewn against her ribs, and the night moved forward, tick by measured tick, toward dawn—

—when the door would cough open, and the men who came through it would not be inspectors, and the first of them would say, with terrible pleasantness, "Señor Pemberton. Señor Vargas wonders if you would prefer to speak with him directly," and Edmund would understand that the offer had already begun.
