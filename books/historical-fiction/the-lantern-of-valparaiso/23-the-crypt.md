## 23. The Crypt

Mercedes Araya had washed the clothes of half of Valparaíso, and in doing so she had learned to read people the way other women read prayer books—by what they left in the fabric, by the stains they tried to hide, by the weight of a garment that should have been light. She knew fear when it came through her door. She had smelled it on Edmund Pemberton the night he arrived with a sleeping child in his arms and a note crumpled in his fist like a dead moth.

But she had never smelled fear like this morning's.

It came up the hill from the customs house before any word did—the way bad news always traveled faster than its messenger, seeping through doorways and market stalls, changing hands like counterfeit coin. By the time Rosa Herrera came pounding up Cerro Alegre with her shawl flying behind her, Mercedes already had the kettle on and Lucía's small bundle packed beneath the bed, because a woman who had buried a husband and raised herself alone learned early that trouble kept no appointments but always arrived punctually all the same.

"They've taken him," Rosa said at the threshold, not crossing it, as if grief were something you could catch from a doorstep. "Edmund. Two men in customs house gray walked him out of Harrow & Sons an hour ago. Mateo saw it." Her voice caught on her brother's name, and Mercedes marked the catch, filed it away. "He didn't struggle. He *let* them take him."

"Of course he did." Mercedes poured the tea she would not drink. "A man who hides for twenty years learns every way there is to hide. And one way is to walk into the open and let them think they've found everything."

"But if they make him talk—"

"He'll stall them. He told us himself—he can talk about escapements until the ears bleed off a bishop." She set down the cup harder than she meant to. "The question isn't whether Edmund holds. The question is what happens in the next six hours, while they hold him and search for what they believe he's hiding."

Her eyes went, without her permission, to the corner where Lucía sat cross-legged on the floor, mending a tear in her doll's dress with stitches too fine for a seven-year-old, stitches her father must have taught her before the sea or Vargas took him. The girl had not looked up. Children who had lost parents learned to listen with their whole bodies while pretending their ears were elsewhere. Mercedes recognized the skill because she had invented it herself, at six, in a house where her father drank.

"Lucía," she said gently. "Fetch your coat, mi vida. The one we fixed last week."

The girl's hands went still. She understood. That was the worst of it—that she understood—and Mercedes felt something rise in her chest that was not quite rage and not quite grief but some alloy of both, forged hot enough to bend iron.

*They will not have her,* Mercedes thought, with a clarity that frightened even her. *Not this one. Not while I have two hands and a tongue and the good Lord's grudging permission.*

* * *

The plan had been made weeks ago, in the low murmur of after-supper hours, the way all dangerous plans are made—not as plans at all, but as possibilities, floated and half-denied, so that when the hour came no one could say precisely when they had decided to become criminals.

Tomás Rivera's chapel sat below the cemetery on Cerro Panteón, a squat stone thing abandoned by the diocese when its priest was stripped of his collar for the sin of feeding striking dockworkers. No one went there now except Tomás and the ghosts he claimed kept better company than bishops. Beneath its floor lay a crypt older than the republic, colonial vaults where the founders of the city had been laid in niches stacked like drawers in a cabinet, and above those niches, Tomás swore, was a passage—a sealed stair down to the cistern tunnels that threaded the hills like veins under skin.

"You want to put a child in a tomb," Mercedes had said, when he first proposed it.

"I want to put her in the one place in Valparaíso where Aurelio Vargas has never once thought to look," Tomás answered. "Men like him fear death too much to visit it."

Now, walking the winding streets with Lucía's hand gripped in hers and a basket of washing balanced on her hip—because no soldier in Chile stops a laundress carrying laundry; laundresses are furniture, furniture with legs—Mercedes rehearsed the plan the way she once rehearsed arguments with her dying husband: over and over, smoothing each seam, hunting each weak stitch.

The soldiers were already in the hills. She could see them from the corner of Calle Papudo—six men in gray moving up the zigzag paths toward the observatory, bayonets catching the grey afternoon light, poking into doorways, questioning washerwomen. Searching for a girl. For papers. For whatever Edmund Pemberton had refused, so far, to give them.

"Señora," Lucía said quietly, "if they ask you whose child I am, I'm your sister's daughter. From Santiago. Come to help with the washing because your eyes are bad."

Mercedes stopped walking. She knelt in the street, heedless of the mud, and took the girl's face in her wet-roughened hands.

"Who taught you that?"

"Papá said—" The girl's chin trembled, and then steadied, the way a young tree steadies after wind. "Papá said a lie you prepare is armor. A lie you stumble into is a wound."

"Your papá," Mercedes said, and her voice cracked clean down the middle, "was a wise man, and I am going to be proud to be your aunt for as long as God allows it." She kissed the girl's forehead, stood, and adjusted the basket. "Come. We walk slow. Slow women are invisible."

* * *

Tomás met them at the chapel door with a lantern already lit, which meant he had been watching the road, which meant he had known since before Rosa ran up the hill. Word traveled through Valparaíso's poor the way water traveled through its hills—downward, silently, finding every crack.

"In," he said. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a beard he no longer bothered to trim and hands that had once held communion wafers and now held smuggled bread. He looked at Lucía and his whole rough face gentled. "Niña. You remember me? I gave you the sugar bird."

"The one with the crooked wing." Lucía almost smiled. "I still have him."

"Well then." Tomás cleared his throat and busied himself with the lantern, and Mercedes saw that the defrocked priest was fighting tears, and loved him a little for it. "Down we go, then. Mind the third step. It's a liar."

The crypt swallowed them by degrees. First the smell—cold stone, old wax, the mineral breath of earth that had not seen sun in a century. Then the cold itself, settling into Mercedes' bones like a debt collector taking a seat. Then the dark, pushed back only as far as the lantern's reach, beyond which it waited, patient as tide.

The niches lined the walls in rows, some sealed with marble slabs bearing names worn to whispers, some open and empty, waiting with the terrible courtesy of graves prepared in advance. Lucía's hand tightened in hers.

"It's all right, mi vida."

"It's very quiet," Lucía said. Not afraid—observing. "Papá said quiet places keep secrets best. That's why he whispered everything. Even jokes."

"Then he'd approve of this place." Tomás lifted the lantern toward the far wall, where a slab leaned crooked against the stone, concealing a gap barely wide enough for a child. "Through there, past the old stair, there's a chamber the founders used for storing wine before it was storing anything else. Dry. Air comes up through the cistern vents. I've put down blankets, candles, bread, water enough for three days. More if it must be."

"Three days," Mercedes repeated. "And Edmund?"

"Is either stalling them beautifully or—" Tomás stopped himself. Priests, even defrocked ones, knew when not to finish a sentence. "Doña Inés will signal us. If the lamp burns steady tonight, he lives and holds. If it goes dark—"

"If it goes dark, we run the cisterns to the harbor and pray the consul's ship hasn't sailed."

"Amen," said Tomás, who probably wasn't supposed to say it anymore, and said it anyway.

Lucía squeezed through the gap without being asked twice. Mercedes followed, folding her long frame sideways through stone that scraped her shoulders, and emerged into a low chamber where the ceiling pressed close and the candlelight made the walls breathe. It was a tomb. There was no honest way around it. And yet someone had laid a blanket folded with care, and set a chipped cup beside a jug, and pinned to the stone wall—she saw it and her throat closed—a paper bird with a crooked wing, a second one, cut from a hymnal page, so the girl would have a flock.

"Tomás," she said, when he'd come through after them, "you soft old ox."

"Don't tell anyone," he said. "I have a reputation as a hard case among the clergy I'm no longer speaking to."

* * *

The first night was the longest of Mercedes' life, and she had sat through the night her husband died, holding his hand while it cooled by degrees, counting his breaths the way she counted spoons.

She did not sleep. She sat against the wall with Lucía asleep in her lap—the girl weighed nothing, nothing, when had she last eaten properly, Mercedes catalogued sins against herself—and listened to the crypt speak. Stone ticked and settled. Water moved somewhere far below, in the cisterns, a sound like slow applause. Once, overhead, boots crossed the chapel floor—many boots, unhurried, official—and her heart slammed so hard she was sure it would wake the dead, and perhaps it did, because the silence afterward felt crowded.

The boots paused directly above them. A voice, muffled: "Nothing here. Old Rivera keeps an empty church and a dirtier floor."

Another voice, younger, nervous: "Orders say search everywhere. Even churches."

"Especially churches," the first voice agreed dryly. "That's where sinners hide. But there's nothing here but dust and a smell of incense somebody's ashamed of. Move on."

The boots receded. Mercedes discovered she had been holding her breath long enough to see lights at the edges of things, and let it out slowly, and felt Lucía stir.

"Are they gone?" the girl whispered.

"For now."

"Señora Mercedes." Lucía turned in her lap, and in the candle stub's guttering light her eyes were enormous and ancient. "If they find Papa's book—the copy sewn in my coat—we should burn it, shouldn't we? Before they take me?"

Mercedes went very still. "No, mi vida. Never."

"But it's just paper. Paper isn't worth—"

"That paper is worth more than paper has ever been worth." She smoothed the girl's hair back from her forehead. "Listen to me, Lucía, because I will only say this once, and then we will never speak of fear again. Your father wrote down the names of families that got onto ships owned by a rich man and never got off anywhere. Do you understand? Somewhere in this country there are grandmothers who don't know their grandchildren died at sea, or worse than sea. There are mothers who still set a place at the table. Your father's ink is the only voice those families have left. And you—" her thumb traced the girl's cheekbone, "—you are the only person alive who can say how that book came to be. You are not carrying paper, niña. You are carrying forty voices. And I would burn this whole city down before I let them silence one of them."

The words came out of her with a force that surprised her own ears—low and fierce and absolutely certain—and she realized, hearing herself, that this was what mothers were for. Not the washing and the feeding, though God knew she'd done decades of both. This. Standing between a child and the dark and telling the dark, in a level voice, exactly what it could expect.

Lucía stared at her. Then she nodded, once, the grave nod of a much older woman, and burrowed back against Mercedes' chest, and slept.

Mercedes did not sleep. She sat in the tomb of strangers and kept watch over a child who was not hers, and understood finally, completely, with the simplicity of arithmetic, that the child was hers. Had been since the night on her doorstep. Blood was a river, but choice was the sea, and she had chosen.

* * *

Near dawn—or what she judged was dawn, down where no dawn reached—footsteps came again on the chapel floor above. Lighter ones. Hurried. Then Tomás' voice, low, calling down through the gap:

"Mercedes. Inés' lamp burned steady all night. Steady till four o'clock, then it flashed the pattern—the one we agreed. Edmund's alive. They've released him."

Released. Her mind seized on the word and worried it like a stain that wouldn't lift. Men like Vargas did not release. Men like Vargas *baited*.

"Released," she called back, "or thrown back like a fish too small to eat?"

There was a pause. "Rosa's with him now. He's hurt—beaten, but walking. He says—" Tomás' voice dropped further, reluctant. "He says they let him go so he'd lead them to the girl. And he says he means to do exactly that. On his terms. At the harbor gate, Friday, when the consul's ship takes on passengers. He wants us all there. He wants to end it."

Mercedes looked down at Lucía, asleep, one fist curled around the hem of her coat—around the lining where forty voices slept in ciphered thread. Above them, the city was waking: carts rattling, gulls screaming over the fish market, the great indifferent machinery of a port beginning another day. Somewhere in it, Edmund Pemberton, a small careful man who had spent twenty years learning to be invisible, was standing fully upright for the first time in his life, and asking her to walk a child straight into the guns.

"Tell him," Mercedes said at last, and her voice did not shake, because she would not permit it, "that we'll be there. And tell him if he gets my daughter killed, I will wash his sheets in cold water for the rest of his natural life."

Above her, in the dark, Tomás laughed—one short, startled bark—and then went silent, and she heard him climbing the worn steps toward the daylight, carrying her answer up out of the tomb like a man carrying fire.
