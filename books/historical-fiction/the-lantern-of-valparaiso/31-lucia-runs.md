## 31. Lucía Runs

The world broke apart all at once.

One moment Lucía was holding Mercedes' hand, and the next the funicular car was screaming, and men were shouting in voices that did not sound like voices but like doors slamming, and someone grabbed her other arm, and Mercedes said her name — *Lucía* — in a way she had never said it before, high and cracked, and then Rosa was standing up in the middle of everything with her arms spread wide like a bird, and Mercedes' hand was gone.

That was the worst part. Not the shouting. Not even the man's fingers digging into her arm. The worst part was that Mercedes' hand was gone, and Lucía's own hand was still curled around nothing, still shaped like it was holding on, and she could not make her fingers close on anything at all.

Then the man's grip tore loose — someone had shoved him, or the car had lurched, she did not know — and the door was open, and the hill was there, steep and gray and full of stones, and Lucía did a thing she had never done in her whole life.

She jumped.

* * *

She ran.

She did not know where. Her feet knew, maybe, or maybe they only knew *away*, which was not a place but was better than the car, better than the man's hands, better than the shouting that followed her down the slope like smoke follows a fire.

The hill was steep. The stones rolled under her shoes. She fell once, on her palms, and the gravel bit into them, and she did not cry. She thought about crying. It was right there, in her chest, big as a bread loaf. But crying made noise, and Papa had told her once — Papa had told her, in the kitchen of the house that was not a house anymore, his voice low the way it got when he was saying something important — *a girl who is quiet is a girl who is safe, Lucía. Loud girls get found.*

She got up and ran again.

The streets at the bottom of the hill were narrow and full of smells. Fish. Smoke. The green-rotten smell of the harbor that lived in everything in Valparaíso like salt lives in the sea. Lucía knew these smells. She did not know these streets, but she knew the smells, and knowing the smells was like knowing a person's voice even after their face changes. She had walked streets like these holding Papa's hand, a long time ago, back when hands were just hands and not things you could lose.

She ducked into an alley between a wall and a stack of barrels. The barrels smelled of wine gone sour. She pressed her back against the wall and made herself small, the way she made herself small under the bed at Mercedes' house when the boots came up the stairs, and she breathed through her mouth, quiet, quiet, quiet.

Boots went by the mouth of the alley. Two pairs. Then three. Men's voices, clipped and angry, the kind of voices that did not ask questions because they already had all the answers they wanted.

*The girl. Small. Dark hair. A coat with —*

Lucía's hand went to the coat. Papa's writing was in it, sewn flat against the lining like a second skin, all those little marks that were not letters but were letters, that Edmund had spent whole nights copying while Doña Inés watched the door. She had slept in this coat. She had eaten in this coat. The coat was Papa, in the only way Papa could still be anywhere, and the men wanted it, and they wanted her, and both of those things were the same thing.

She waited until the boots faded. Then she waited more, because Papa had also said, another time, *wait longer than you think you need to, mija. The extra waiting is the part that keeps you alive.* She had not understood it then. She was seven. She understood it now.

When she finally moved, she moved along the wall, in the shadow of the barrels, and she did not run. Running made noise. Walking made almost none.

* * *

The alley let out onto a street that went down toward the water, and Lucía stopped at the edge of it because there was a church there, small and crooked, with a bell tower that leaned a little, like a tired man leaning on a fence.

She knew that church.

Not the church itself. The church was strange to her eyes. But Papa had drawn it. She was sure of it — sure the way you are sure of your own name. In the bad weeks, after Mama was gone, when Papa could not sleep, he used to sit at the table with a stub of pencil and draw on the backs of envelopes, and Lucía would sit across from him and watch the lines come. He drew ships. He drew stairs going up the hill, so many stairs. And he drew a crooked church with a leaning bell tower, and he had tapped the drawing with his finger and said, *if you are ever lost, you find this. From the church, you go downhill until the street bends like a snake, and there is the door of a friend.*

Lucía had not known then who the friend was. She was not sure Papa had known either. But he had drawn the church, and here the church was, leaning its tired lean, and her chest went hot and tight all at once.

*Downhill,* she thought. *Until the street bends like a snake.*

She went.

The street downhill was busier, and busy was good and bad. Good because a small girl alone in a crowd was like a drop of water in the sea. Bad because there were eyes in crowds, and some eyes were paid to look. Lucía kept her head down and her hands in her pockets and she matched her steps to the steps of a fat woman carrying a basket, so that anyone watching would see the woman and not the small shadow beside her. She did not know where she had learned that trick. It felt like something Papa had said, or maybe something Edmund had said, or maybe something she had made up herself and it had simply always been true. It did not matter. It worked.

The street bent. It bent like a snake, a long slow curve to the left, and at the curve there was a wall with a door in it, a blue door, and the blue was peeling so that it looked like the door was shedding its skin.

Lucía stood in front of the blue door and did not knock.

Because now she had to remember the rest. *There is the door of a friend.* But Papa had said more, hadn't he? He had said it another night, or the same night, the nights all ran together now like wet ink. He had said something about the door. About how you would know it. About — 

*Knock like the tide,* she remembered. *Two and then a pause and then one. The sea asks twice and answers once.*

She had laughed at that. She had been small, smaller than now, and she had said, *Papa, the sea doesn't knock,* and he had said, *everything knocks, mija. You just have to be patient enough to hear it.*

Lucía raised her fist. Her knuckles were still scraped from the fall, and they stung when she curled them. She looked up the street. She looked down the street. A dog was sleeping in a doorway. A man was mending a net. Nobody was looking at her, which was either very good or very bad, and she had no way to know which.

She knocked. Two. Pause. One.

The knocks sounded very loud in the quiet street. Louder than the shouting on the hill. Louder than her own heart, which was going so fast it felt less like a drum and more like a bird trapped in a shirt.

Nothing happened.

She counted to thirty, the way Papa had taught her to count when the thunder came. At twenty the dog in the doorway opened one eye. At thirty she knocked again. Two. Pause. One.

And then, from behind the door, a sound. A shuffling. A bolt, heavy and old, sliding back with a sound like a bone cracking.

The door opened a crack, and an eye appeared in the crack, one brown eye under a gray eyebrow, and the eye looked at Lucía, and Lucía looked at the eye, and neither of them said anything for a long moment.

Then the eye went wide, and the door went wide, and a hand shot out — an old hand, brown and spotted, with a ring on one finger — and pulled her inside, and the door shut, and the bolt went home again with its bone-cracking sound.

* * *

The inside of the house was dark and smelled of oil and candles and something cooking, onions maybe, and Lucía stood in the little entryway with her heart still going like the trapped bird and looked up at the person who had opened the door.

It was an old woman. Very old. Old the way the hills were old, old in a way that made her seem like part of the city itself, something that had been here before the houses and would be here after. She wore black, all black, and her hair was white and pinned up with what looked like a pencil.

The old woman crouched down, slowly, her knees cracking like the bolt had cracked, until her face was level with Lucía's face.

"So," the old woman said. "The tide knocks at my door at last."

Lucía did not say anything. She had learned, in these weeks, that not saying anything was often the best thing. Words were like coins. You did not spend them until you knew who was buying.

The old woman studied her. Her eyes were not unkind, but they were not soft either. They were the eyes of someone who had looked at a great many things and believed very few of them.

"You have his hands," the old woman said. "Your father's. I would know them anywhere. He had the hands of a boy who grew up counting." She reached out and took Lucía's scraped hand and turned it over, gently, looking at the gravel-bitten palms. Then she looked up at Lucía's face again, and something in her face changed. "And his eyes when he was frightened. Which was more often than he let anyone see."

"You knew my papa," Lucía said. It was not a question, but her voice made it half one, wobbling at the end.

"Knew him? Child, I fed him. He sat at that table" — she pointed with her chin at a table in the next room, a table with a cloth on it and a candle burned down to a puddle — "and he ate my soup and he copied his papers by that candle, and he told me, if anything happens to me, if the sea ever knocks at your door, you open it. And I told him, nothing will happen to you, you worry too much, you young men all worry too much." The old woman's voice went rough on the last words. She stood up, slowly, holding on to the wall. "And then the sea knocked. And here you are. Smaller than I expected. But the sea always sends what it sends."

Lucía's chest was doing something complicated. It was full and empty at the same time, the way it got sometimes when Mercedes brushed her hair, or when Edmund let her hold the little brass gears in her palms and told her what each one was for. Full of a feeling that did not have a name she knew. It was almost like missing someone and being found by them at the same time.

"Mercedes," she said suddenly. "And Edmund. They were in the car. The men — the men took —" Her words tangled. She pulled at them. "Rosa stood up so they would look at her and not at me. And then Mercedes' hand was gone."

"The laundress," the old woman said. "And the clockmaker's boy. And the priest's people. Yes. Your father wrote me names, near the end. He was careful, your papa. Careful as a man can be, which is never careful enough in this city." She went to the window, a small window with a curtain of sacking, and put her eye to a gap in the weave. She looked for a long time. Then she let the curtain fall.

"They will search downhill," she said. "They always search downhill first, because downhill is where the poor hide, and they think everything that runs hides among the poor. So you and I, we are going to do the opposite of what they think. We are going to stay very still, and drink soup, and let the city forget us. And when it is dark —"

"When it is dark, what?"

The old woman turned and looked at her, and for the first time she smiled, and the smile changed her whole face the way the sun changes a gray street.

"When it is dark, child, we are going to the one place in Valparaíso where no man who works for Aurelio Vargas would ever dare to set his boots." She held up one crooked finger. "Do you know why?"

Lucía shook her head.

"Because," the old woman said, "there are places in this city that belong to the dead, and the dead keep better watch than any guard. Your father knew it. That is why he gave me the papers to hold, before he gave anyone anything. The crypt of Father Tomás. The chapel on the hill where the defrocked one keeps his little flock." She said it lightly, but her eyes were not light. "And I think — I think that is where your Edmund will look first, too. A boy who repairs clocks knows that everything broken goes back to its maker. And that child" — she tapped Lucía's coat, right over the lining where Papa's writing lay flat and secret — "that child is going to need every one of us now."

* * *

The soup was lentils, with too much salt, and it was the best thing Lucía had ever eaten, or at least it was in the top three, competing with Mercedes' sopaipillas and the bread Edmund once bought her from the cart on the corner when he thought she was asleep and she was not.

She ate at the table with the candle puddle, and the old woman — whose name was Doña Fermina, and who told Lucía to call her that and nothing else, no *doña*, *doña is for people who have earned it and I earned nothing but age* — sat across from her and did not eat, only watched, with her chin on her hand.

"Tell me about the coat," Doña Fermina said.

Lucía put down her spoon. She put her hand flat on the table, the way Edmund did when he was going to say something serious, because she had seen him do it and she had practiced it in the mirror of Mercedes' room.

"There is writing in it," she said. "My papa's writing. It is not letters. It is marks. Dock marks. Edmund says it is shorthand that the men on the docks use so the bosses cannot read it, but my papa could read it and write it because he was a clerk and the clerks learn everything." She took a breath. "It says the names of the ships. And the names of the families. And it says which ships came back empty."

The kitchen was very quiet. The candle flame stood still.

"And Vargas wants it," Lucía said. "And Vargas wants me. Because I am the only one who knows where Papa hid the real book. Edmund copied it into my coat, but the real book, the one with everything — Papa hid it, and he told me where, and he made me say it back to him four times, and I have never told anyone, not even Edmund, because Papa said, *you tell no one until you are with people who cannot be bought, mija, and you will know them because they will not ask.*"

Doña Fermina had gone very still. Still the way the candle flame was still. When she spoke, her voice was different — quieter, and older, as if it were coming from a long way off.

"And have you found them? The ones who cannot be bought?"

Lucía thought about Mercedes' hand, and how it had held hers every night at prayers, and how it had been torn away on the funicular. She thought about Edmund's hands, always ink-stained, always careful with the little gears. She thought about Doña Inés, who had shown her the stars through the great telescope and told her, *the stars cannot lie, and neither shall we.* She thought about Father Tomás, who had no collar but who prayed like a man who had given up everything else and kept only that.

"Yes," Lucía said. "I found them."

"And they did not ask."

"No," Lucía said. "Not once."

Doña Fermina closed her eyes for a moment. When she opened them, they were wet, but her voice was steady as a keel.

"Then tonight," she said, "we go to the crypt. And you will tell them what your father made you remember. All of it. Because child —" she leaned across the table, and the candlelight moved in her old eyes — "a book that is hidden is only a book. But a book with a witness who can find it — that is a key. And keys, mija, are the most dangerous things in the world. More dangerous than guns. That is why they will never stop looking for you. And that is why, tonight, we stop running."

Lucía looked at her. Outside, far off, down toward the harbor, a bell began to ring — the customs bell, the one that rang when ships came in, the one that had rung the day her papa's ship left and never came back.

The bell rang, and rang, and Lucía sat very straight on her chair, with her scraped hands in her lap and her father's writing flat against her ribs, and for the first time since the funicular car had screamed, she was not afraid.

She was ready.
