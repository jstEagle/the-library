## 35. Vargas Unbound

The fog came off the bay before dawn, the way it always did in July, rolling up from the cold Humboldt current and swallowing the masts of the harbor until the ships seemed to float on nothing. Aurelio Vargas had lived sixty-one years beside this water. He knew its moods the way other men knew their wives' faces—the gray mornings, the gold afternoons, the treacherous green swells when the south wind rose. He had built an empire on his knowledge of it, on the certainty that the sea kept no ledgers and told no stories.

He was dressing by candlelight when his steward knocked twice, which meant bad news delivered quietly.

"The *Andaluza* is coaled and provisioned," the steward said. "But there are men on the quay. More than usual for this hour."

"Dockworkers begin early."

"Not like this, señor. They're not working. They're standing."

Vargas tied his cravat with steady fingers. His hands did not shake; they never had, not through two bankruptcies survived and one rival drowned, not through the warehouse fire whose smoke still hung in the city's memory like a stain on linen. "Then they'll disperse," he said. "They always do."

The steward did not answer, and that silence was the first true thing Vargas had heard in weeks.

* * *

Tomás Aguirre had not slept. He lay in the crypt beneath his chapel with Tomás's own blood—no, his blood, he kept correcting himself, the wound was his now, it belonged to him as surely as his name—seeping through a fresh bandage, listening to Mercedes hum to Lucía somewhere above. The bullet had gone through the meat of his shoulder, and the naval surgeon at the consul's house had pronounced him lucky with the flat indifference surgeons reserve for miracles.

Lucky. He had stood between a gun and a child, and the gun had chosen his shoulder instead of his heart, and men called that luck. Tomás thought it was something else. He thought it was arithmetic—the long sum of a man's failures, and one payment finally made against the principal.

He could not fight. That was the surgeon's verdict and his body's. But a man without a collar still had a voice, and a voice was a kind of weapon that did not bleed its bearer.

By four o'clock he was standing on the wet stones of the customs quay, wrapped in a fisherman's poncho, speaking low and fast to the men who gathered around him out of the fog. He knew these faces. Some had struck with him years ago, before the strike broke and the defrocking came. Others were younger, sons of strikers, dockmen who had loaded Vargas's ships and watched them sail heavy with families and return light as lies.

"They will say it is not your affair," Tomás told them. "That the tribunal is the navy's business, the consul's business, anyone's but yours. Do not believe it. The ledger names your brothers. The warehouse fire killed Esteban Reyes, and Esteban Reyes carried crates on this quay for nineteen years." He let that name sit in the salt air. "A ship cannot leave a port that refuses to let go of it."

An old stevedore named Fuentes spat into the water. "They'll bring soldiers."

"Perhaps. But soldiers must be paid, and paymasters must be found, and every man who touches this has begun to count what it will cost him when the verdict comes. We are not the ones who need to be afraid anymore." Tomás smiled, and it hurt his shoulder, and he smiled anyway. "That is new. Let us enjoy it."

* * *

Mercedes had wanted to keep Lucía away from all of it. She had said so plainly, standing in the consul's parlor with her arms crossed like a fortress: the girl had lost enough, seen enough, run far enough through cisterns and checkpoints. Let the grown men settle their accounts.

But Lucía had looked up at her with those grave dark eyes—seven years old and already fluent in the language of watching—and said, "My papá wrote the book so people would know. If nobody stands there, then nobody knows I'm the one."

Mercedes had argued. She had argued the way she scrubbed, thoroughly and without mercy. And in the end she had lost, because you cannot argue with a child who has already buried more than most widows, and because some small voice inside Mercedes—the voice of a woman who had washed other people's linens for eleven years while her own life went unlaundered—knew that hiding Lucía forever would be just another way of erasing her.

So they stood together at the edge of the crowd, Mercedes' hand closed around the girl's small fingers, both of them watching the great black hull of the *Andaluza* rise above the quay like a cliff that had decided to move.

"Hold my hand tight," Mercedes murmured.

"I always do," Lucía said.

* * *

Edmund Pemberton arrived last, and he arrived limping.

The beating in the customs house had left its signatures across him—a swollen eye gone yellow-green at the edges, two ribs that announced themselves with every breath, a left wrist bound in a splint he had fashioned himself from clock-case offcuts and linen, because if a man was going to be broken he preferred to be repaired properly. The tribunal had released him the evening before, "pending testimony," as though he were a document to be filed. He had walked out of that building upright, and he intended to keep walking that way.

Doña Inés found him at the crowd's edge. She looked smaller than she had at the observatory, or perhaps he had simply stopped being small enough to look up at everyone. Her eyes, behind the smoked spectacles she wore even in fog, were bright and terrible.

"He'll try to run," she said. It was not a question. She had spent forty years reading the movements of planets, and men were only planets with worse manners. "The tide turns at half past six. A ship that misses it waits twelve hours, and twelve hours is a lifetime now."

"Then we have until half past six."

"We have less than that. Look." She nodded toward the gangway, where Vargas's own men were assembling—not loading cargo, Edmund saw, but forming a line, a thin human fence between the ship and the growing crowd. "He means to push off through us if he must."

Edmund flexed his splinted hand inside his coat pocket. Somewhere in the city behind them, a bell began to ring—not the observatory's chimes, not yet, but the chapel bells, one after another waking across the hills, and he understood that Tomás had sent word through every confidant and former penitent the priest possessed. The city was ringing itself awake.

"Then we stand very still," Edmund said, "and we make him be the one who moves first."

* * *

Rosa Mateo—no, Rosa Delgado, she had been reclaiming her mother's name hour by hour since the cell door closed behind her—came down from the funicular with the taste of jail bread still in her mouth and her brother's confession folded in her breast pocket like a coal that would not cool.

Mateo had told her everything. The gambling debts, the agent with the soft voice, the money passed in the back room of the Café Universal, the names he had sold. *They only wanted to know about the clockmaker,* he had wept. *I didn't know about the girl.* And Rosa had looked at her brother, whom she had raised after their mother died, whom she had defended at every table in every pulpería in Cerro Alegre, and she had felt the terrible freedom of a loyalty snapping clean.

She had spent three days in a cell for blocking a funicular car with her own body. It had been, she reflected now, the easiest decision of her life. Harder decisions waited on the quay.

She found Fuentes and the old stevedores near the bollards, and she said only, "Where do you need me?"

Fuentes grinned through his beard. "Everywhere, muchacha. Everywhere."

* * *

Vargas came down to the quay at twenty past five, and the crowd saw him coming the way a field sees weather.

He wore black, as he always wore black, and he walked with the unhurried gait of a man who had never once needed to hurry, because the world arranged itself around his pace. Two of his men flanked him. Behind them, the gangway rose to the *Andaluza*'s deck, and the ship's engines breathed below the waterline like a sleeping animal.

He had expected to walk through a scattering of idlers. Instead he walked into a wall.

They lined the quay from the customs house to the harbor gate—two hundred men, perhaps three hundred, dockworkers and carters and fishermen and women with market baskets set down at their feet, and they did not shout. That was the thing that crawled up Vargas's spine and settled between his shoulders. Mobs shouted. This was not a mob. This was a jury.

At their center stood the defrocked priest, arm in a sling, and beside him the old woman astronomer, and the young clockmaker with his ruined face, and a laundress with a child's hand in hers, and a girl just released from custody, and old Fuentes who had loaded Vargas's ships since before the *Andaluza* had a name.

"Gentlemen," Vargas said, and his voice carried the way it carried in boardrooms, smooth as varnish. "You are impeding the departure of a lawful vessel. I will ask you once to clear the quay."

Nobody moved. Somewhere in the crowd a man began, very softly, to sing—it was an old loading chant, a counting song, the rhythm dockmen used to time their lifts—and one by one the others took it up, not loud, not angry, just present, a hundred voices keeping time like so many pendulums.

Vargas felt the sound enter his chest and find his heartbeat and correct it, slow it, drag it down to their tempo, and for the first time in thirty years Aurelio Vargas understood that he was not the metronome.

"My ship sails on the tide," he said.

"No," said the priest, gently, the way he might correct a boy's catechism. "Your ship sails when this port lets go of it. And this port remembers."

* * *

Lucía remembered the song.

Her father had sung it, sometimes, walking her home along the waterfront with her hands in his coat pockets, teaching her the numbers in the verses—one for the crate, two for the rope, three for the wave that carries you home. She had thought she'd forgotten it. Grief does that; it packs whole rooms of a life into boxes and nails them shut. But the song came out of the crowd and opened the boxes, and suddenly her papá was walking beside her again, warm and smelling of ink and rope, and she was crying without any sadness in it at all.

Mercedes knelt beside her. "What is it, mi vida?"

"It's his song," Lucía said. "Papá's song. Everybody knows it."

And Mercedes, who had come prepared to shield the girl from the sight of Vargas, from the guns, from all of it, found instead that her hand was being pulled forward, gently, toward the front of the crowd, and she went, because there are currents stronger than protection.

When they reached the line, Lucía let go of Mercedes' hand, stepped forward onto the wet stones directly in the path of the gangway, and began to sing the third verse in a small clear voice that cut through two hundred grown voices like light through fog.

The singing faltered. Then it deepened. Every dockworker on that quay was looking at the child of the clerk who had copied the ledger, the child whose face someone had once ordered forgotten, and the song became something none of them had ever sung before: a vow.

* * *

Vargas looked at the girl and understood, in one long falling moment, exactly what he was looking at.

Not evidence. Evidence could be bought, burned, buried at sea. This was a face—the face, the one his agent's note had commanded erased, standing on his own quay in front of three hundred witnesses and a naval patrol boat that had appeared, at some point in the last ten minutes, at the mouth of the harbor, its guns run out with the unhurried confidence of an institution that had finally made up its mind.

His men looked to him. That was the horror of it. For thirty years they had looked to him and known what to do, and now they looked to him and saw only a man doing arithmetic of his own: the patrol boat, the tribunal summons already served, the crowd, the child, the tide turning in forty minutes against a ship that would not be cleared to sail by any authority in Chile or Britain.

"Señor," said his first officer, at the top of the gangway. "Shall I cast off?"

Cast off to where? Every port would close. Every consul would remember. He would be a pirate in his own paint, hunted by the machinery he had spent a lifetime oiling.

"Stand down," Vargas said.

The words cost him something structural, like a keel timber giving way. He said them again, louder, because his men had not moved. "Stand down. All of you. Get off my ship."

And then, because he was still Aurelio Vargas, still the man who had never once hurried—he straightened his black coat, descended the gangway step by measured step through the wall of singing dockworkers, and walked the length of his own quay like a man going to inspect cargo, except that the cargo was himself, and the hold was a cell at the naval station, and the manifest read *one count of conspiracy, seventeen counts of disappearance, one warehouse, one witness, one child.*

As he passed the girl, he stopped.

The crowd tensed. Edmund's splinted hand closed into a fist inside his pocket. Mercedes stepped halfway between them and stopped herself, because the girl had not stepped back.

Lucía looked up at the most powerful man in Valparaíso, and she did not sing, and she did not speak, and she did not forget his face. She simply looked, the way her father had taught her to look at entries in a ledger—carefully, completely, writing him down.

Vargas held her gaze for three seconds. Then he took out his pocket watch, consulted it as though the tide were a matter of personal inconvenience, snapped it shut, and walked on to the patrol boat's launch without another word.

* * *

The *Andaluza* stayed at her moorings. By noon she had been seized under warrant, her papers impounded, her holds opened to investigators who found, sealed in the master's cabin strongbox, correspondence that made even the hardened naval clerks go quiet.

By evening the story had climbed the cerros on foot, as stories do, passing from pulpería to kitchen to workshop, growing in the telling and needing no embellishment. They sang the loading chant in places it had never been sung. Someone painted the girl's shawl—blue with white stars—on a wall near the customs house, and by morning it had been copied on six more walls, and no one painted over any of them.

Edmund stood at the harbor gate as the sun went down, Inés at his side, and watched the lanterns come alight along the mole, one by one, the way stars come out over an observatory dome.

"She looked at him," Edmund said. "Seven years old, and she looked at him until he couldn't bear it."

"Do not mistake it for innocence alone," Inés said. "It was observation. Rigorous, sustained, unblinking. The finest kind." She smiled faintly. "I intend to propose her for the astronomy society."

Behind them, bells began to ring across the hills—not alarm bells now, not warning, but the plain ordinary evening bells of a city returning to itself. And somewhere among them, Edmund was almost certain, he could hear the chapel above the crypt where Tomás lay healing, ringing longer and brighter than the rest, as if it had something to say.

The tribunal convened in nine days. And Edmund Pemberton, apprentice clockmaker, son of a disgraced man, keeper of a dead clerk's ledger, would stand before it and tell the time.
