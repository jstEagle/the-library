## 36. Testimony

They came for him before dawn, but gently this time — a naval lieutenant and two marines, boots soft on the cobbles of Cerro Alegre, as though they had learned that Edmund Pemberton startled easily and that startling him cost everyone something. The lieutenant carried a folded paper he did not ask Edmund to sign. He simply said, "The tribunal sits at nine, señor. You'll want a clean collar," and waited on the stair while Edmund dressed in the dark, Mercedes' hand steady at his back, Lucía still asleep on the cot with the coat — the coat with its ciphered lining, already surrendered to the naval office — pooled around her shoulders like a small gray wing.

"You don't have to say your father's name," Mercedes murmured, buttoning his collar with the efficiency of a woman who had dressed a hundred men for funerals and knew the difference between those and this. "They only need what you saw. The ledger. The ships. The girl."

"I know what they need," Edmund said.

He did not tell her the rest — that he had lain awake half the night rehearsing not the testimony but the sentence that would precede it, the one he had avoided saying aloud for twenty-three years. It sat in his chest like a mainspring wound too tight, and he was afraid that if he spoke it in this small kitchen, with its smell of soap and yesterday's bread, it would spring loose and take the walls down.

* * *

The tribunal convened in the naval commandancy on the plaza, a stone hall built for empire and now borrowed for justice, its ceiling painted with allegories of Commerce and Navigation that had watched a great deal of commerce and very little navigation of conscience. Edmund was escorted through a corridor of marines and into a room that smelled of ink, tobacco, and the particular salt-and-brass smell of the harbor that came in through high windows and never quite left.

The tribunal sat three officers behind a long table draped in bunting, and beside them a civilian assessor with a face like a closed ledger. A court stenographer sat at a small machine of keys, and Edmund — despite everything, despite the bruised ribs and the sleeplessness and the knowledge that Aurelio Vargas was at that moment in a cell or a suite depending on whose money spoke loudest — Edmund looked at the stenographer's machine and thought: *a clock of words.* Every craft, it seemed, could be bent to the recording of time. It steadied him. Things that could be recorded could be repaired. Things that could be repaired could be made true.

Doña Inés sat in the second row, upright as a mast, in her formal astronomy society dress with its faded silver braid. She had dressed as if for an observatory opening, and Edmund understood: she had dressed for the last public act of her career, whatever it proved to be. Tomás was not there — he was still in the naval hospital, alive, the surgeons said, by the width of a finger's breadth and the stubbornness of a man who had spent years apologizing to God and had no intention of dying before the apology was accepted. Mercedes sat beside Inés with her hands folded, and when Edmund passed she did not touch him, did not speak, only looked at him the way she looked at stains she had decided were coming out.

And in the third row, between a marine and a court clerk, sat Lucía, because the tribunal had ruled — over the objection of Vargas's lawyers — that the witness whose evidence gave the ledger its meaning had a right to hear it spoken. She wore the dress Mercedes had sewn her, blue with white at the collar, and her hair was braided with a ribbon that had been, Edmund happened to know, cut that morning from one of Mercedes' own petticoats. She caught his eye and did not smile. She nodded, once, small and grave, the way her father must have nodded over the ledger, and Edmund's throat closed, and he sat down, and the president of the tribunal called the session to order, and the clock of words began.

* * *

"State your name for the record."

Here it was, then. The sentence. He had rehearsed it in the dark of the lodging house, in the crypt where Tomás had hidden them, in the cisterns with candlelight sliding along wet stone, in the hospital corridor where he had waited to learn whether the priest's heart would keep its time. He had rehearsed it so many ways. *Edmund Pemberton, apprentice.* *Edmund Pemberton, formerly of London.* *Edmund Pemberton, of no particular family.*

He stood. His ribs ached where Vargas's men had worked him over in the customs house, and the ache was oddly useful — a metronome, a proof that he was in his body and not above it watching.

"Edmund Alistair Pemberton," he said. "Apprentice clockmaker, formerly of the house of Harrow & Sons, Valparaíso. Son of Alistair Pemberton, master clockmaker, of Clerkenwell, London."

The stenographer's keys clattered. Somewhere behind him a chair creaked. The name of his father hung in the painted air of the hall — *Alistair Pemberton* — and for a moment Edmund heard, beneath the creak and clatter, the other sound it always carried: the courtroom in London eleven years ago, the foreman's voice, the word *fraud*, the way his father had stood in the dock with the stillness of a man watching his own escapement fail, and the way the crowd had murmured *Pemberton* as though it were a disease. They had sailed for Chile within the year. His father had died in Valparaíso within two, of a fever the doctors named and Edmund had never believed in, because he had watched that heart for years and knew it kept better time than any movement he had ever serviced. Grief, he understood now — grief with a name like *shame* bolted to it — could stop a clock no doctor could restart.

"Your father," said the president, a Captain Reyes with a gray mustache and eyes that missed nothing, "is the Alistair Pemberton convicted of falsifying chronometer certifications in London?"

The hall went very quiet. Mercedes' hands tightened in her lap. Inés did not move.

"Yes," Edmund said. "He is."

"You understand the tribunal is obliged to note the connection. The defense may argue it bears on your credibility."

"I would be disappointed if they didn't," Edmund said, and heard, to his own surprise, a dry note in his own voice that he did not recognize — his father's voice, actually, the voice Alistair Pemberton had used with difficult customers. "But since we are establishing credibility, Captain, I'd like to establish it properly. My father was convicted on the testimony of a firm that was itself falsifying its rate records — a fact that emerged two years after his sentencing, in a case he was too dead to benefit from. The pardon was drafted. It was never issued, because there was no one left to issue it to." He paused. "I have carried the name as a man carries a stopped watch — knowing it is not broken, only unwound, and waiting for the day I could wind it in public. This is that day. So yes. Note the connection. I insist on it."

Captain Reyes regarded him for a long moment. Then he inclined his head a degree — a small motion, but Edmund, who had spent his life reading the small motions of mechanisms and the men who owned them, read it perfectly. *The record will reflect,* the inclination said, *that this tribunal is not Vargas's customs house.*

"Proceed," said Reyes.

* * *

They took him through it hour by hour, and the hall became, in Edmund's telling, a machine laid open on the bench — every gear identified, every escapement traced to its maker.

He told them of the summons to the observatory, and Doña Inés' request for him by name. He told them of the master clock's failing, and how the fault had been no fault of the movement at all but a loosened mounting bracket, and how — reaching deep into the case to reseat it — his fingers had found the compartment behind the strike-train, a space no repair manual would ever account for, and inside it the ledger.

He described it the way he would have described a movement to a fellow craftsman: the dockworkers' shorthand, the columns, the cipher of initials and ship's names. *The *Mercedes del Sur*, cleared for Callao with forty-one steerage passengers, entered with thirty-three. The *Santa Rosalía*, cleared with fifty-six, entered with forty-nine. The *Esperanza* —* he made himself say the names, all of them, because Lucía's father had copied them out at the cost of his life and it seemed the smallest possible courtesy to say them aloud in a room with a stenographer. The names of ships. The names, where the ledger had them, of families. *Rojas, the family of five, third entry, April. The widow Quintero and two children. The man they called only Chileno, who signed with a cross.*

He explained, in the flat patient voice he used with customers who believed their clocks were haunted, what the numbers meant. Ships of the Vargas line departing Valparaíso full and arriving empty — or arriving with fewer souls, the difference entered in the ledger in a neat dock clerk's hand with dates and tally marks, the surplus passengers logged as having disembarked at intermediate ports where no port records confirmed any disembarkation. Passage sold twice. Families sold onward — to the guano islands, to plantations north, to whatever buyer the coast offered — their contracts voided, their names struck. And the ledger's final pages, in a different, hurried hand: notations of payments, and the initials that the naval investigators had spent two weeks confirming belonged to Aurelio Vargas himself.

"Could the ledger be forged?" the defense counsel asked — a sleek man named Ossa who smelled of good tobacco and better fees.

"Anyone can forge a page," Edmund said. "No one forges a *movement*. The entries interlock. Dates align with tide tables, with the observatory's own weather logs — which Doña Inés kept, and which the tribunal has. A forger would have had to falsify two independent records in two hands across eleven years and never once slip a gear. I've examined fraudulent work for a living, señor. This is not it. This is the truest thing I have ever held."

Ossa tried another angle. "You admit you deciphered this shorthand with suspicious ease. How does an apprentice clockmaker read dock clerks' cipher?"

"Because my father taught me cipher as a boy — it was a game, a way of teaching me to look for structure. And because the man who wrote the ledger, Tomás Rojas —" the hall shifted; in the third row Lucía went rigid, and Edmund gentled his voice without lowering it — "Lucía Rojas' father, a dock clerk in Vargas's employ, used the same system, and I have spent weeks with his hand. He was careful. He was frightened. And he was better at his craft than the men who killed him will ever be at theirs."

"Objection," said Ossa. "The clerk's death is not established."

"No," Edmund agreed. "Disappearance is so much tidier. That is rather the whole of the case, señor."

Captain Reyes did not quite smile. The stenographer's keys did not stop.

* * *

They brought Lucía in the afternoon, and Edmund asked — was permitted — to stand near her while she testified, because the tribunal's own doctor had said the child spoke better to a known face, and because Edmund had asked in a voice that made the doctor reconsider arguing.

She stood on a chair so the tribunal could see her, and she told it the way children tell everything: out of order, absolute, unadorned. Her father copying pages at night by a candle shielded with a plate. The knock at the door that was not a knock but a signal — two and then one — and the night the signal came wrong, three and then two, and her father going pale and putting the pages in the clock case, *the big clock, señor, up the hill, he said the big clock would keep them better than God, because God forgives and clocks only remember* — and Edmund, hearing his own trade spoken by a seven-year-old with a dead father's faith in it, had to look at the floor for a moment and let the moment pass through him.

Then the men came. Then the boat. Then the note pinned to her, and the door of a lodging house, and a man with ink on his fingers who had looked at her shawl and gone white.

"Forget the face," Lucía said, when the president asked what the note said. She said it in the careful voice of a child reciting scripture. "But I remembered it anyway. That's what Papá said faces were for."

The president asked her to look around the room and tell the tribunal whether she saw the man who had left her at the door. She looked. She found, in the row where the witnesses for the defense sat, a broad man with a broken nose whom Edmund knew from the customs house, from the night of his interrogation, from the harbor gate. Lucía looked at him for a long, level moment, a child's gaze with an old man's weight in it.

"That one," she said. "He held the lantern. He said my father's name wrong on purpose, so I'd know not to trust him."

The man's counsel rose to object to a child's identification. Captain Reyes overruled it with three words and a look that closed the subject like a case lid.

* * *

It was near dusk when Ossa rose for the defense's last thrust, and Edmund, exhausted, bruised, hoarse, saw it coming the way you feel a mainspring near its breaking point — a thinning of the air.

"Mr. Pemberton," Ossa said, silkily, "you have told this tribunal you are the son of a convicted forger, dismissed from your position, harboring a fugitive child, allied with a defrocked priest, a disgraced astronomer, and a laundress of no standing. You have no fortune, no title, no standing of any kind. Why should this tribunal weigh your word above the word of the most substantial citizen of this port?"

Edmund was quiet for a moment. In the second row, Mercedes' hands had gone white. Inés sat like stone. And Edmund found — this was the miracle of it, the thing he would carry the rest of his life — that he was not afraid. The smallness he had worn for twenty-three years, the careful invisibility, the head down and the name half-spoken — it was not gone; it had simply turned out to be a chrysalis and not a shell, and he had walked out of it on the harbor gate stones with Tomás's blood on them, and there was no going back in.

"Because of exactly what you've listed, señor," Edmund said. "I am nobody. I have nothing. I stood to gain everything by selling that child and that ledger — a London apprenticeship, my father's name cleared, a life. Aurelio Vargas offered me all of it, in his own house, over his own brandy, and I have told the tribunal where and when. You may call him to deny it." He let that sit; Vargas, in custody, had declined to testify. "I have nothing, señor Ossa. Which means I am the only man in this affair with nothing left to protect. Ask any craftsman in this port what that makes me."

"What does it make you, Mr. Pemberton?"

"Accurate," Edmund said. "A clock with nothing to gain runs true."

* * *

The president adjourned for deliberation, and the hall emptied slowly, the way water leaves a lock. Edmund stood at the rail of the public gallery while the marines rearranged themselves and the stenographer closed her machine with the gentle finality of a woman putting a day to bed.

Mercedes came and stood beside him. Inés came and stood on his other side. For a while none of them spoke. Through the high windows the harbor was turning gold and then copper, and the masts of the fleet made their black script against the light, and somewhere below a bell marked the hour — six, by the commandancy clock, which Edmund had serviced in another life, three weeks and one eternity ago, and which was, he noted professionally, running two minutes slow.

"You said your father's name," Mercedes said at last. Not a question. She had heard it; the whole hall had heard it.

"Twice," Edmund said. "Maybe three times. I lost count."

"And the sky didn't fall."

"It's stone," Edmund said. "It never was going to fall. I think I was waiting for permission from a man who's been dead eleven years, and I finally understood he'd given it to me long ago. He taught me cipher. He taught me that a thing is true whether or not anyone is brave enough to say it. He was waiting too, I think. In the gears." He looked at his hands — ink-stained, bruised, steady. "I'm done waiting."

Inés touched his sleeve, two fingers, the precise gesture of a woman adjusting an instrument. "Tomorrow they deliberate fully," she said. "And there is a thing you should know before they do. The naval officer I signaled — Lieutenant Ferrer — came to the observatory this evening. He asked for the original weather logs. All of them. Eleven years." Her old eyes held something Edmund had never seen in them and had not thought to see: fear, and beneath the fear, a fierce bright satisfaction. "He also asked whether I would be prepared to testify. I told him I had been prepared for thirty years and had only been waiting for someone to ask the question properly." She paused. "There is more, Edmund. Ferrer would not say what. But he went from here to the harbor, and there are ships moving out there tonight that were not moving an hour ago, and one of them flies the Vargas house flag, and her crew is aboard, and her papers are being stamped."

Edmund felt the old cold move up his spine — the checkpoint cold, the chase cold — and then, deliberately, set it aside, the way he would set down a tool he no longer needed. Whatever came, it would come to men who had already testified, already named themselves, already stood upright. Let it come.

"Then we'd best get home to Lucía," Mercedes said, gathering her shawl. "And pray the verdict finds us before the harbor does."

They descended the commandancy steps together into the blue Chilean evening, three small figures against the great stone facade, and out in the dark harbor, unseen, a ship began, quietly, to raise steam.
