## 7. The Orders Come

The lists came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Catastrophe should have the decency to arrive on a Sunday, when the churches are full and the bells can at least be said to have warned you. Instead it came on a Tuesday, folded into the ordinary weather of the city, and by Wednesday morning the Szklana Street bakery was dark, and the Weitz family's shutters were closed from the inside, and nobody could tell me where the Kohns had gone because nobody had seen them go.

I learned it the way I learned everything: through paper.

Miriam had me drive her to the municipal office under a pretext so thin it barely qualified as clothing—she needed a stamp for an import permit, she said, though Miriam Kessler had never in her life imported anything heavier than an idea. She went inside wearing her good coat and her bad temper, and she came out twenty minutes later with both intact and something new underneath them, a stillness I had learned to read the way sailors read a drop in the wind.

"Drive," she said. "Slowly."

I drove slowly. Vilna did what Vilna does in autumn, which is to look beautiful while quietly dying—the chestnut leaves going gold along Antokol, the river doing its patient silver work, students arguing philosophy outside the university as if argument were a form of shelter. And between all of that, gaps. A doorway where a flower seller used to stand. A window with no curtain behind it. A name chalked off a doorpost as if the building itself had been corrected.

"The census," Miriam said at last, not looking at me. "Brozek's pages. They've been cross-referenced. The lists are drawn from his columns—I saw one on the clerk's desk, and I recognized the handwriting of the margins before I recognized a single name." Her gloved hands were perfectly still in her lap, which is how I knew they wanted very much not to be. "He counted us, Vesper. That was the whole of it. Someone asked him to count, and he counted, and now the counting has grown teeth."

I said nothing. There is a discipline to saying nothing; I had spent thirty years perfecting it. But my hands on the wheel had gone cold, because I remembered Brozek's notebook, the neat entries about a workshop on Pylimo Street, and old Tomas's laugh—*bureaucrats are weather, boy, you wait them out*—and Ruta copying page numbers into the back of a primer as if numbers could be argued with.

Numbers, it turned out, could be executed.

* * *

The workshop that evening was not the workshop I had left that morning. Firelight still moved in it, the lanterns still burned, but the quality of the room had changed, the way a house changes after a death in it even before anyone speaks. Tomas sat at his bench with his spectacles pushed up into his white hair, holding a letter he wasn't reading. The children had been sent home early—all but Ruta, who stood at the copying table with her chin set at the angle she reserved for arguments she intended to win.

"They took the Fajns," Tomas said, without preamble. He never wasted a greeting on me; we had passed beyond greetings sometime around my second year of sleeping above his lanterns. "Whole family. The boy left his cap on the hook. His mother left the bread rising. You understand? They left *bread rising*, Vesper. Nobody leaves bread rising unless they believe they'll come back to punch it down."

"Where?"

"That's the question, isn't it." He set the letter down with exaggerated care, the way a man handles something when he doesn't trust what his hands might otherwise do. "Nobody knows where. That's the design of it. You can't mourn what you can't locate, and you can't organize around a hole in the air."

Ruta spoke without looking up from her copying. "The list has forty-one names on it. I copied it off the notice board by the post office while the clerk was eating his lunch. He eats slowly. It's a flaw in their system."

"Tomas," I said, and then stopped, because the next word was *we have to move the archive*, and saying it aloud would make it real, and there is a coward in every careful man who will negotiate endlessly with a sentence rather than speak it.

Tomas looked at me over his spectacles, and for once there was no laughter in his face at all. "Say it," he said gently. "You've been rehearsing it since you walked in. Say it badly. It's allowed."

"We have to move the archive."

"There." He nodded, almost approving. "And now the world knows, and the world has declined to end. Remarkable." He rose, joints cracking like small fireworks, and laid his hand flat on the bench where thirty years of documents had passed beneath it—letters, certificates, forged permits, love notes, death notices, the whole murmuring congress of a city that had never suspected it was being transcribed. "We don't move it all. We can't. There isn't a cart in Vilna that could carry it, and if there were, a cart draws eyes. We choose."

"Choose how?"

"The way you always choose, boy." His eyes found mine, and they were terribly kind. "By weight. Not the weight of paper. The other weight."

* * *

We worked until three in the morning, and I will tell you what choosing feels like: it feels like amputation performed by lamplight, by people who love what they're cutting.

Ruta read names aloud. If a document named a living person—a family, a debt, a marriage, a birth—it went into the iron box. If it named no one living, or someone already gone past finding, it stayed. She was fourteen and she made these decisions faster than I could, without flinching, and twice I caught her pausing over a page with her thumb pressed white against the paper, and twice she caught me catching her, and neither time did either of us say anything, because some mercies are only possible in silence.

"What about yours?" she asked at one point, not looking up.

"Mine?"

"Your letters. The ones you keep. The copies you think nobody knows about." She turned a page with terrible calm. "The ones in the false bottom of your trunk, which I found in March, which I have never mentioned, because I was waiting to see how long you'd pretend."

I stood there with an armful of other people's lives and absolutely nothing to say, which for me is roughly equivalent to standing naked in the town square.

"They go in the box," she said. "All of them. Because they're the only things in this building written in your hand, Vesper Dravenmoor, and if the workshop burns, they're the only proof that you ever—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, and finished differently. "They go in the box. That's not a discussion."

It was not, as it happened, a discussion.

* * *

Miriam's network mobilized the way water mobilizes—finding every crack, asking no permission. By Friday there were couriers I'd never seen before moving through the city with packets sewn into hems and hidden in hymnals, and safe houses opening like eyes, and a schedule whispered to me over tea that mapped Kaunas in seven stages. Miriam gave me my route herself, in her study, with the curtains drawn at noon.

"You'll wear the grey coat," she said. "Not the brown. The brown has been seen at two checkpoints already. The grey is boring. Be boring, Vesper. Boring is armor."

"And the pages?"

She lifted the packet from her desk—thin, deceptively thin, forty sheets at most, the distilled marrow of everything we'd chosen—and watched me with those clear, unfooled eyes as I understood what she was asking without asking.

"Sewn into the lining," I said. "Against the ribs."

"Left side. Over the heart, if you insist on the poetry. The needlework needs to be invisible—can you do invisible needlework?" She almost smiled. "Of course you can. You've done nothing else your whole life."

There it was again, that vertigo, that sense of standing on a ledge I hadn't known I was standing on. She saw me. She had always seen me. I took the packet and the needle and thread she offered, and that night in the workshop, by lantern light, with Ruta pretending to sleep two feet away and fooling no one, I sewed forty pages of other people's existence into the lining of my own coat, stitch by stitch, until the coat weighed exactly what a man weighs when he is carrying the evidence that his neighbors existed.

* * *

The checkpoint on Subačiaus was the third of five, and by the third I had perfected the art of being furniture.

The trick, I have found, is not confidence. Confidence attracts attention the way a raised voice attracts ears. The trick is a mild, permanent apology—shoulders slightly stooped, eyes slightly down, the whole body arranged into the shape of a man whose life is too tedious to inspect. I had a delivery manifest for a stationer on Didžioji. I had a yawn I could produce on demand. I had, against my left ribs, the only true thing in the car.

The soldier was young. They were all young now; the war had eaten the older ones first and was working its way down. He looked at my papers, looked at my face, looked at the papers again, and I felt the pages against my ribs the way you feel a bruise—constantly, privately, in rhythm with my heart.

"Purpose in Kaunas?"

"Deliveries. Invoices. My employer's accountant is a man who believes in doing things twice." I let a flicker of long-suffering boredom cross my face, and the soldier—God help us all, the soldier *sympathized*. I watched it happen. I watched him decide I was a man suffering under paperwork, same as everyone, same as him, and he waved me through with the weary generosity of one bore toward another.

I drove. My hands shook only after the mirrors showed the checkpoint small behind me, which I considered a professional triumph.

But at the fifth checkpoint, the last one before the open road, there was a table set up under a tarpaulin, and at the table sat a man with a ledger, and the man had a familiar way of squaring his shoulders before writing, a bookkeeper's little ritual of readiness, and my stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world.

Brozek looked up from his column of figures.

Our eyes met across ten meters of mud and wire, and I watched recognition arrive in his face—not alarm, not yet, just the dull click of a fact fitting into a slot: *the driver from the Kessler house.* His pen hovered. Somewhere behind him, a truck idled, loaded, its canvas flaps tied down in a way that made my skin crawl, because I had begun to know what trucks like that were for.

He looked at me. He looked at his ledger. And then—slowly, deliberately, in a manner I would turn over in my mind for many nights afterward—he turned the page, and wrote something at the bottom of the *next* one, and waved the next car forward without looking up.

I drove past him close enough to touch. He did not raise his eyes. But as I rolled by, low, almost lost under the engine noise, I heard him say it—not to me, not quite to himself, somewhere in the ruined country between:

"Forty-one names. I only counted households."

Then the road opened ahead of me, gray and long, forty pages warm against my ribs, and I understood that whatever Brozek had decided at that table, he had decided it alone, in ink, in a ledger that could hang him—and that I would have to come back for him, or bury what was left of whatever he was trying to become.
