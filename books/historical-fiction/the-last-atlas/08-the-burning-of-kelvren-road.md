## 8. The Burning of Kelvren Road

The order arrived at dawn, written in the careful hand of a staff clerk who had never seen a refugee column and signed by a man who had never seen one either.

*Burn the Kelvren road. All crossings. All traffic. Let nothing pass.*

Marchen read it twice, standing in the doorway of the farmhouse that served as his headquarters, while the winter light came up gray over the frozen fields. Somewhere behind him, his adjutant was boiling coffee on a stove that smoked too much. Somewhere beyond the hedgerows, three thousand people were moving along that road in the cold—farmers with carts, women carrying children, old men walking because their horses had been eaten months ago.

He folded the order once, precisely, and put it in his coat pocket instead of burning it.

"General?" Adjutant Sorin looked up from the stove. "Shall I signal the engineers?"

"No."

Sorin waited. He was a good adjutant, which meant he knew when waiting was the whole of his duty.

"The order stands for now," Marchen said. "I'll review the crossing positions myself this morning."

It was not a lie, exactly. It was a delay wearing a lie's clothes, and he had gotten very good at those in the past year. He put on his greatcoat and went out into the cold to look at the road he had been ordered to destroy.

* * *

The Kelvren road ran west from the river lowlands through country that had been fought over so many times it had stopped resembling anything but itself—stumps where orchards had been, ditches where lanes had been, a church with no roof standing like a broken tooth against the sky. The ice on the Sorrel had begun to groan in the nights now, a sound like slow grinding teeth. Thaw was weeks away, maybe less. Everyone on both sides of the river could feel it coming, the way animals feel weather.

He walked the road's length as far as the picket line, and the pickets let him pass without saluting, which he preferred. A colonel who is saluted sees only soldiers. A general who walks alone sees what the soldiers see: the column strung out along the frozen ruts, half a mile of it, then another half mile, gray figures pulling gray carts, a child's red cap the only color in the whole landscape.

They were fleeing toward Kelvren because Kelvren still had walls, and walls still meant something to people who had watched every other meaning burn. And his government—the one across the river, the one that paid him and promoted him and had just declared him indispensable—had decided that the road to Kelvren was a military asset, and assets were to be denied to the enemy, and the people on it were, in the arithmetic of the general staff, a rounding error.

*Let nothing pass.*

He stood at the picket line a long time. A woman with a handcart stopped twenty paces off, watching him warily, her son's collar gripped in her fist. Marchen realized he was staring at the boy—at the red cap, at the thin wrists—and he made himself look away, because staring at children on roads was how a man began to confuse himself with someone merciful, and mercy was not what he felt. What he felt was something colder and more useful: recognition. He recognized this road. He had built orders like this one. He had burned crossings before, in the first year, when the war still had a shape and he still believed the shape meant something.

A lieutenant named Voss came up beside him, breath steaming. "Engineers are asking again about the demolition charges, sir. They want daylight to place them under the bridge arches."

"Tell them the ice isn't safe to work on."

"It isn't," Voss said, surprised.

"Then it's true. Tell them tomorrow we'll reassess." He turned back down the road. "And Voss. The column moves through tonight. Full passage. No interference."

Voss's face did something complicated. "Sir, the order—"

"I've read the order."

"Yes, sir." Voss hesitated, and then said the thing adjutants say when they have decided to be loyal rather than obedient: "There will be talk."

"There is always talk. That's what armies are made of."

* * *

The talk came that evening, as he knew it would, in the farmhouse kitchen where his senior officers gathered around the map table with its little wooden flags. Colonel Brandt spoke first, because Brandt always spoke first—he was a broad man with a farmer's patience and a butcher's instincts, and he had carried Marchen's flanks in two campaigns and never forgiven him for surviving them.

"They're saying you've gone soft, Ilya." Brandt used the first name deliberately, an old intimacy deployed like a knife. "Soft in the head, soft in the heart. The staff sends one order. One. And the great Marchen sits on it like a hen."

"Do they say why the order was sent?"

Brandt shrugged. "Deny the enemy the crossing. Standard doctrine."

"The enemy has no need of the crossing. Their supply lines run north. The order isn't doctrine—it's punishment. Kelvren's garrison commander wrote a letter criticizing the War Ministry, and this is the answer. Burn the road, starve the city, and when it falls, blame the enemy." He laid his hand flat on the map, over the little wooden flag that marked the bridge. "I've executed orders like this before. I know their smell."

"So you admit you're refusing."

"I'm delaying."

"For how long? The thaw won't wait for your conscience, and neither will the Ministry."

Marchen looked around the table. Voss, young and frightened and trying not to show it. Major Ilves, who counted everything—shells, days, debts—and whose silence now was itself a calculation. Two captains who would follow whoever seemed likeliest to win. He had commanded men for twenty years and had never once, until this winter, asked himself what they saw when they looked at him. Now he understood: they saw power. Not virtue, not wisdom—power, and the question of which way it would point.

"You want to know how long," he said. "Until the column is through. Three days, perhaps four. Then the engineers can have their bridges, and the Ministry can have its ashes, and I will write whatever report keeps the rest of you out of it."

"And if the column takes longer?"

"Then I'll delay longer."

Brandt leaned back. "You know what happens after. Court-martial, or worse. They've shot generals for less than this—I've drunk with the men who pulled the triggers."

"I know."

"Then say the true reason, Ilya. Not the garrison commander's letter. Not doctrine. Say it." Brandt's voice dropped, and the room went quiet enough to hear the stove ticking. "Say why a man who has burned ten roads refuses to burn the eleventh."

Marchen held his gaze. The true reason was a boy with a locket, a courier he hadn't seen in nine years, intelligence reports he'd read forty times—the words *his son was seen alive near the Halfway House* copied into his memory until they wore grooves. The true reason was that he wanted, before the republic finished dying and took him with it, to perform one act that corruption could not explain and could not use. One uncorrupted thing. If it happened to be a road full of strangers, then the strangers were lucky, and he was not fooled about his own motives, and that changed nothing.

"The true reason," he said, "is that I gave an oath to defend these people, and the Ministry has decided they're cargo. I intend to keep my oath. You may report me for it."

No one moved. Then Ilves, the counter of things, said quietly: "Three of the officers at second battalion are already drafting a petition. They mean to go over your head. They're calling it mutiny prevention."

"Let them draft it. Paper is cheap."

"And if they act?"

"Then we'll learn what this army is made of," Marchen said, "before the enemy learns it first."

* * *

He slept badly, in the chair by the window, and woke before light to the sound of the river.

The ice was talking. Not groaning now—talking, long cracking syllables rolling up the valley, the sound of a thing deciding to end. He stood at the window and watched the dark, and thought about Petra Hartwell, whom he had loved in a garden in peacetime, in another country that no longer existed except in the two of them. He had made her promise to guard what he loved, and she had promised, and then the war had taken his name and hung it on walls as an enemy's name, and he had assumed the promise had died of exposure like everything else.

But the report said his son had been seen. Alive. Near the Halfway House, on the far bank, in the care of someone.

In the care of someone.

He pressed his forehead against the cold glass. There was no time in this war for hope; hope was a luxury item, requisitioned only by men who expected to survive. But the thought would not leave him—that somewhere across the black river, in the last days of a dying republic, a woman he had loved was keeping his promise for him, and the ice between them was already deciding to break.

At dawn, the first refugees of the morning column came down the Kelvren road, and Marchen went out and stood at the picket line and did not move, and the road stayed open.

Behind him, in the farmhouse, a telegraph key began to click. He did not turn around. He knew what it was saying. Orders confirmed, inspectors dispatched, a name being written down in a capital that was running out of names to write.

Three days, he told himself. Hold the road three more days.

He did not yet know that the courier was already on the ice.
