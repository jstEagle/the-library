## 3. What the Workshop Kept

Wren had learned to read her mother the way other children read weather. A certain set of the shoulders meant rain coming in from the southwest; a particular silence at breakfast meant a ship lost on the Manacles; and when Bram Ashgrove went down to the boathouse with a lantern and did not come back for an hour, it meant her father's name had been spoken somewhere, somehow, and the wound beneath fifteen years of scar tissue had been pressed again.

So when Wren saw the lantern light moving under the boathouse door on Thursday evening — long after her mother should have been climbing the tower stairs for the first watch — she did what any sensible daughter of a keeper would do.

She waited until the light went out. Then she took her own candle and followed.

* * *

The boathouse smelled of tar and old rope and something else beneath it, something mineral and cold, like the inside of a cave that had never seen sun. Wren's candle found the stone floor, the upturned dinghy, the coiled lines hung from pegs — all the ordinary furniture of a Cornish boathouse — and then found the thing that was not ordinary at all: a hatch in the floor, its iron ring polished bright by use.

Her mother had left it unlocked.

That was the part that made Wren's chest go tight. Her mother locked everything. Locked the oil store, locked the medicine chest, locked the drawer where the letters from the Trinity House were kept. Fifteen years of locks, and now this — an unlocked hatch over whatever her mother had spent two evenings weeping about down here in the dark.

Wren lifted the ring. The hinges made no sound at all, which told her they were oiled regularly, which told her someone came down here often, which told her everything her mother had never said.

Stone steps descended into a room that should not have existed beneath a fishing village's forgotten boathouse. Dry, somehow — she could feel the dryness on her skin, the careful engineering of it — and warm, faintly, as though the walls themselves remembered hands at work. Her candle flame leaned and steadied.

And there was the clock.

It stood against the far wall like a man waiting to be introduced: a regulator case of dark walnut, taller than Wren, its glass door reflecting her candle back at her in a long pale smear. Through the glass she could see the movement — or most of it. Wheels and arbors and a great brass pendulum rod hanging motionless, dead as driftwood. The works were beautiful even unfinished, each wheel cut and crossed with spokes so fine they looked spun rather than filed. But there were gaps. Empty places where components ought to sit, holes drilled in plates with nothing yet mounted in them. A body with organs missing.

Wren set her candle on the workbench and put both hands flat on the wood, the way you'd steady yourself against a boat's gunwale.

Her father had built this. Her father, who had been dead since she was three years old, who existed for her as a photograph on the mantel and a grave behind the church and a shape in her mother's silences — her father had come down into this room, night after night, year after year, and built a clock under the ground while his wife kept a light above it and his daughter slept ten feet overhead and knew nothing, nothing, nothing.

The anger arrived first, hot and familiar. It was the anger she'd been carrying since she was old enough to understand that other children had fathers who taught them knots and fish names and how to whistle through their fingers. She'd been angry at God, at the sea, at the storm that took him. Now, standing in his secret room, she felt the anger turn and find a new target: him. He had been *here*. All those years she'd grieved an absence, he'd been twenty feet below her bed, alive in every way but breathing, keeping company with brass and steel instead of his daughter.

She picked up the nearest object — a small hammer with a hornbeam handle worn glossy — and very nearly threw it at the clock case.

She didn't. Because the handle fit her hand. Perfectly. As though the tool had been waiting.

Wren stood in the lamplight-less dark with her candle guttering and her father's hammer in her fist, and the anger did what anger does when it has nowhere left to go. It curdled. It cooled. It became something hungrier.

Fascination.

* * *

She came back the next afternoon, and the one after that, and by Sunday she had stopped pretending to herself that she wasn't stealing time. Her mother slept mornings after the middle watch; Wren had always used those hours for eggs and bread and the long walk along the cliff path. Now she used them for the stairs beneath the boathouse, taking care to leave the hatch exactly as she'd found it, taking care to be home before the first stirring above.

The workshop gave itself up slowly, grudgingly, like a wreck giving cargo to a diver.

There were ledgers — seven of them, bound in marbled boards gone soft at the corners — and Wren worked through them in order, sitting on her father's stool with her knees drawn up. The earliest ones were almost disappointingly innocent. Lists of commissions. *Regulator, deadbeat escapement, for Mr. T. Penhale, Truro — £14.* *Marine chronometer, repair, s.s. *Cornubia*.* Her father's handwriting was small and exact, each letter formed with the same precision he must have given his wheels. She traced the letters with one finger and felt she was touching his hand.

But the later ledgers changed. The neat entries thinned out, and between them appeared pages of figures that were not money and not dates — columns of numbers in groups of four and five, sometimes with small symbols above them: a crescent, a cross-hatch, a circle with a dot. Codes. She recognized the structure without understanding it, the way you recognize a locked door without having the key. Some of the coded pages were dated; some carried single words in the margin. *Penzance. Falmouth. Delivered. Held.*

And once, in the last ledger, written larger than anything else in his hand, almost as if he'd needed to shout it onto the page:

*Not clocks.*

Two words. Nothing else on the page. Wren sat with them a long time, her candle burning down, the pendulum rod hanging dead in its case beside her. Not clocks. Then what? What had he been delivering to Penzance and Falmouth, holding and handing on, measuring in numbers that weren't prices?

She thought of her mother's face when the letter had come — Wren had seen it only sideways, in glimpses, the way you see lightning without looking directly at it — and thought: *she knows. She's known all along. That's what the silences are made of.*

The thought should have made her angrier. Instead it made her feel strangely less alone. There were two of them standing in this dark, then — her mother below, all these years, and Wren above, asleep — both of them guarding the same secret, one knowingly and one not, and neither of them ever saying a word to the other. What kind of family was that? What kind of love?

The question didn't have an answer. But it had a photograph.

She found it on the third visit, tucked inside the back board of the fifth ledger, loose and curling. A cabinet card, sepia going silver at the edges. Four men stood on a quay — she knew the quay, it was Porthmellon, she could see the net-loft roofline behind them — and one of them was her father.

He looked younger than the mantel photograph, leaner, with his shirtsleeves rolled and his waistcoat unbuttoned, and he was *smiling*. Not the stiff mantel smile. A real one, crooked, caught mid-laugh, his head tipped toward the man beside him as if that man had just said something outrageous. Wren stared at that smile until her eyes stung. She had never once imagined her father laughing. Grief had frozen him solemn, and here he was, thawed, alive, amused.

The other three men she studied the way she'd study a chart before an unfamiliar passage.

The man nearest her father was big, barrel-chested, with a beard like a gorse bush and an arm slung familiarly across her father's shoulders — the posture of an old friend, or a partner. The second stood slightly apart, narrow and neat in a dark coat, his hat held in both hands in front of him like a schoolboy's, his face turned three-quarters away so that the camera caught mostly jaw and ear. The third man stood at the edge of the frame, half cut off by it, and he was not smiling and not looking at the camera at all. He was looking at her father. There was something in the angle of him, in the set of his mouth under a drooping mustache, that Wren had no word for except *watching*. He looked like a man memorizing a face for later use.

On the back, in pencil, in her father's hand: *Porthmellon, '86. J., Silas, —*

The third name was unfinished. A dash where a name should be, as if her father had started to write it and thought better of it, or as if he'd never known it and hadn't wanted to admit that on paper.

Wren turned the card over, then back again. *J., Silas, —*. Three strangers. One named, one half-named, one erased. And her father in the middle of them, laughing, with a stranger's arm around his neck.

She should take it to her mother. That was the correct thought, arriving on schedule, wearing her mother's own voice: *we do not keep things from each other, Wren.* Except they did. They had done nothing else for fifteen years. And besides — and this was the thought underneath, the true one, the one she examined only sidelong — if she gave the photograph up, it would vanish into her mother's silence the way everything else had vanished. It would become another sealed room. Whereas if she kept it—

If she kept it, it was hers. Her father's laugh, hers. His mystery, hers to solve.

She slid the photograph into the inner pocket of her jacket, where it lay against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and told herself she would give it back. Soon. When she understood more. When knowing wouldn't hurt her mother worse than not-knowing already did.

She was sixteen years old, and she was already lying to herself with her mother's fluency. She noticed it, distantly, the way you notice weather changing. She did not stop.

* * *

On the sixth day, she found the notebooks.

They were in a drawer beneath the bench, wrapped in oilcloth — eleven of them, small, cheap, the sort sold in stationers' shops for a penny, filled edge to edge with her father's precise hand. These were not ledgers. These were *him*: notes on escapements, sketches of wheel-tooth profiles, calculations marginated with complaints (*Penhale wants it louder — a regulator is not a church bell, T.P., I've told him twice*), recipes for gilding fluid, a pressed sprig of sea pinks between two pages like a bookmark, and — on the flyleaf of the fourth — a line that stopped Wren's breath:

*For B., who keeps the light so I can keep the time.*

She read it five times. Then she closed the notebook and held it against her chest and let herself cry, briefly and furiously, in the dry underground room where her father had spent years being a stranger. For B. For Bram. He had loved her mother. Whatever else the ledgers hid, whatever *not clocks* meant, he had loved her, and had said so in pencil on a penny notebook, and had hidden it under the ground instead of saying it aloud at supper.

Why? Why hide anything? Why build beauty in a cellar and write tenderness in code and live half a life?

Because of the other half. Because of *J., Silas, —*. Because of the watching man at the edge of the frame.

Wren wiped her face on her sleeve, opened the fourth notebook again, and began, methodically, at the beginning, to read everything her father had written. She had her mother's patience and none of her mother's reasons for silence. She had time, and a locked room that had finally been left open, and a hunger that fifteen years of not-knowing had grown enormous.

She read until the candle was a thumb's width from drowning, then climbed the stairs, closed the hatch, and walked home through the grey afternoon with her father's secrets warming her pocket.

Her mother was awake. Wren saw the light in the kitchen window from the top of the lane, saw the shadow move behind the curtain — checking the path, checking for her — and felt the old irritation rise and then settle into something colder and more useful.

Her mother had her letter, whatever it said. Wren had the notebooks, the ledgers, the photograph. Between them they owned the whole of Thomas Ashgrove's double life, and neither of them knew what the other was carrying.

*Let her keep her silence,* Wren thought, turning the cabinet card over once more in her pocket, feeling the watching man's gaze through the paper. *I'll keep mine. And between us, we'll find out who Silas is — and why the third name was never written at all.*

She did not yet know that the third man had a name, and that within the week she would see it — not in pencil on the back of a photograph, but written in her father's last ledger beside the word *Held*, and beside a date: the twelfth of November, 1893.

Three days from now.
