## 4. The Man from Penzance

He came up the cliff path on the third morning after the storm, when the gulls had only just begun to reclaim the air and the sea still wore its bruised and grudging calm. Bram saw him first from the lantern gallery, a dark figure picking his way over the wet granite with the careful steps of a man who did not know the ground and did not trust it. Not a fisherman. Fishermen walked this coast like dancers, half-drunk on familiarity, leaning into the wind as if greeting an old adversary. This man walked like an accountant.

She watched him from above while her hands went on with their work, trimming the wick, wiping the lens, the small liturgies of the morning that fifteen years had worn smooth as sea glass. He stopped at the foot of the tower and looked up. Even at that distance, she felt the weight of his looking — a man measuring, not admiring.

By the time she came down, he had arranged himself at the gate with his hat in his hands, a posture of courtesy that fit him like a borrowed coat. He was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, with the ruddy, wind-scoured face of a man who had spent years in coastal towns and none of them on the water. His boots were wrong. That was the first thing she noticed, and she hated herself for noticing it, for the ease with which the noticing came. His boots were town boots, Penzance boots, polished at the toe, and he had walked a mile of coastal mud in them without a mark to show for it. Either he had come by boat and changed his shoes, or he cared more about appearances than a shipping agent ever had reason to.

"Mrs. Ashgrove?" His voice was warm, unhurried, the voice of a man who had practiced putting people at their ease. "Trelawny. Penzance shipping office. Merrick and Trelawney, coastal freight."

"We get little freight," Bram said. "As you can see." She gestured at the village below, at the two boats hauled up beyond the tideline, at the empty racks where the pilchards used to hang in silver rows. "The sea's given most of it up."

"All the more reason to look after what trade remains." He smiled. His teeth were very even. "I'm making a survey of the coast, you understand. Accounts to reconcile. And your name has come up."

"Has it."

"In the way names do. Deliveries, you see. Consignments that passed through this station in years past. I'm told your husband handled certain items for the office. Before his — " a small, gentle pause, the pause of a man who had rehearsed sympathy — "his accident."

The word *accident* sat in the air between them like a stone on a table. Bram looked at him and felt something in herself go very quiet and very clear, the way the light felt in the minute before it swung across a ship's bow.

"My husband hauled oil and stores," she said. "Fifteen years ago. Whatever accounts you're reconciling, Mr. Trelawny, you'll want the office in Penzance, not a widow's kitchen."

"Of course. Of course." He was not looking at her face while she said it. He was looking at her hands, and then past her shoulder, at the boathouse below the tower, its door shut, its paint peeling, the most unremarkable building on the whole grey headland. She saw his eyes rest there the way a man's eyes rest on a woman at a dance — briefly, and then not briefly at all. "Only, there was a particular item. A consignment of clockwork, if you can credit it. Heavy brasswork, going upcountry. We've lost the paperwork, you see. Terrible filing. And the office would take it kindly if you could say whether anything of the sort ever came ashore here. Whether anything of the sort might still be — about."

The wind moved through the grass behind him. Somewhere below, the sea folded itself against the rocks and unfolded again.

"Clockwork," Bram said. And she heard her own voice do it — the thing she had not known she could still do — heard it come out flat and faintly puzzled, the voice of a woman to whom clockwork was a word from another country. "You've walked a mile in the rain for clockwork?"

"For accounts, Mrs. Ashgrove. The clockwork is only a line in a ledger." He smiled again, and this time the smile reached all the way to his eyes, and that was the worst thing about it. "But ledgers are how men like me keep the world from coming apart. You'd be surprised what a missing line can do."

"I wouldn't know," Bram said. "I keep a light. Lines of a different sort."

He laughed, a good laugh, well-made. He thanked her for her time. He said he'd be in the village another day or two, asking after the fishing, and that if anything came to mind — any old crate, any forgotten delivery — the Ship Inn at Porthcarrick had a room with her name on it, so to speak. Then he put on his hat, touched its brim, and went back down the cliff path the way he had come, walking carefully, like a man carrying something fragile that no one could see.

Bram stood at the gate until the headland swallowed him.

Then she became aware that her heart was going like a hammer, and that her hands, which had not shaken once in his presence, were shaking now.

* * *

She did not go straight to the boathouse. That was the discipline, and she was frightened by how easily it rose in her, how the old machinery of concealment engaged at the first pressure like a clock's train taking up its slack. She made bread. She mended a sail thumb. She ate the midday meal with Wren and spoke of the weather and the price of coal and whether the girl had finished her arithmetic, and all the while some cold clerk in the back of her skull was transcribing every detail of the morning: the boots, the teeth, the pause before *accident*, the way his gaze had settled on the boathouse door.

It was Wren who gave her the excuse, finally, pushing back her plate. "There's a gentleman at the Ship Inn asking questions," the girl said, in the offhand way she had learned from her mother. "Tamsin Vellacott says he's been asking after the Ashgroves all morning. Whether we'd always lived here. Whether Da kept much company."

"Did he now," Bram said.

"He told the Vellacotts he's a shipping agent." Wren looked at her, and there was a new sharpness in the girl's eyes that Bram did not like and could not fault. "You knew."

"I met him at the gate this morning."

"And?"

"And he asked about old deliveries. I told him I wouldn't know a consignment from a codfish." She said it lightly, and watched the lightness fail to land, watched her daughter study her the way the man had studied the boathouse. Fifteen years, she thought. Fifteen years of *I wouldn't know*, and now the habit had turned on her, and the person it was deceiving was sitting across the table with her father's grey eyes.

"Ma," Wren said. "Is there something in the boathouse I ought to know about?"

"There's a boat in the boathouse," Bram said, "with a hole in her you could drive a cart through, and a smell of tar you can't wash out of wool. That's what's in the boathouse."

Wren held her gaze a moment longer. Then she gathered her plate, and said she had eggs to collect, and went out, and did not collect any eggs. Bram heard her boots on the path going the wrong way — down, toward the shore — and let her go. There was no way to stop her without telling her why, and the why was a door Bram had nailed shut so long ago she had half forgotten there was a room behind it.

She washed the dishes slowly. She put the kitchen to rights. And all the while the clerk in the back of her skull wrote on: *he asked whether we'd always lived here. Whether your husband kept company. He has been in the village a day and he knows your daughter's name is Wren, or he will by nightfall.*

When the light was down to two hours left in the sky, she took the storm lantern and went down to the boathouse, because a lie is only as strong as what it's built on, and she needed to know whether her foundations had been dug into while she stood at the gate making conversation about codfish.

* * *

The workshop smelled as it always smelled — oil, brass, cedar, and beneath it all that faint mineral coldness of the sea coming up through the floor. The regulator stood where it had stood for fifteen years, under its dust sheet, in the corner where the lamplight fell best, as if the room had been arranged around it. Bram had not uncovered it since the day she found it. She had told herself that leaving it covered was grief. Standing there now, with Trelawny's even teeth still hanging in her memory, she admitted what it actually was. It was evidence. She had been keeping it the way you keep a body.

She lifted the sheet.

The clock's skeleton gleamed in the lantern light — the great wheel, the barrel, the frame plates drilled and tapped and waiting. Her eye went down the train the way it had gone down it a dozen times in the last fortnight, since the letter: great wheel, intermediate, second wheel, escape —

The escape wheel was gone.

Bram stood quite still. She made herself look again, wheel by wheel, the way she checked the mechanism each night, because panic invents what it cannot find. The great wheel, present. The intermediate, present. The pallets, wrapped in their oilcloth, present. The escape wheel — the small toothed heart of the whole machine, the piece that doled out time tooth by tooth, that made a clock a clock and not a heap of brass — its place on the arbor was empty. Clean. Not broken, not fallen. The retaining screw had been backed off and set neatly on the bench, the way a workman leaves things, the way *he* had always left things. Whoever took it had known exactly what it was and exactly where it lived.

She went through the workshop like a woman searching a drowned house. The ledgers in their hiding place — untouched. The notebooks — untouched. The photograph of her husband and the three strangers — untouched. Only the one piece, only the one, and she understood the arithmetic of it with a coldness that made her sit down on the workman's stool her husband had worn smooth with fifteen years of sitting.

Trelawny had not come for the clock. If he'd wanted the clock he'd have taken the clock, or burned the boathouse, or done whatever men do when they want a thing and don't care who knows it. He had come to *confirm it existed*. And someone — someone with the knowledge to dismount an escape wheel without scratching the arbor — had been here before him, or after him, or while she stood at her gate being smooth and lying well.

The letter had demanded *the finished work*. The clock could not be finished without its escape wheel. Whoever held that wheel now held the only thing that could make the machine run — and the only proof, once it ran, of who had ordered the deaths. They had not stolen the evidence. They had stolen the *keystone*, and left the arch standing, and left her standing under it.

She thought of her own voice that morning, flat and faintly puzzled, *I wouldn't know a consignment from a codfish*, and how good it had sounded, and how the goodness of it had frightened her even as she spoke. She had spent fifteen years learning to lie so well that the lying had become the truest thing about her. And now the lies had brought her nothing. The man in the town boots had walked away knowing everything he needed to know, because he had not needed her words at all. He had needed only to see whether a widow's face could hold a secret — and hers had held it like a lantern holds a flame, plainly, in a glass case, for anyone standing in the dark to read.

Bram sat in her dead husband's workshop with the lamp burning low and the empty arbor glinting where the escape wheel should have been, and the fear that had lived in her since the letter finally changed its shape. It stopped being the fear of what the past might do to her. It became something with a spine in it, something that stood up.

They wanted the clock finished. That was the whole of it, she saw it now — the letter, the agent, the theft, all of it one sentence: *finish the work, and finish it for us, or be finished with it.* They believed she could not finish it without them. They believed the missing wheel was a leash.

She looked at the notebooks stacked on the bench, at her husband's small, exact hand, at the drawings she had barely begun to learn to read.

They had made one mistake. They had assumed the wheel was the only one.

Bram took up the notebook, turned to the page headed *ESCAPEMENT — SPARE, CUT FROM STOCK, IN CASE OF WEAR*, and read the line beneath it twice. Then she rose, lifted the lantern, and began, methodically, to pull out the drawers her husband had built into the bench — the false-backed one, third from the top, that she had found only a week ago and had not yet had the courage to empty completely.

The back of the drawer gave under her thumb. Behind it, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, something small and toothed shifted against her palm like a sleeping animal.

She did not open the cloth. Not yet. She stood holding it in the lamplight, listening to the sea, and to the sound — faint, above, on the cliff path — of footsteps coming down toward the boathouse in the dark.
