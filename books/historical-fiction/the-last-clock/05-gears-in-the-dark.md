## 5. Gears in the Dark

The notebooks were bound in oilcloth, seven of them, stacked beneath the false floor of the workbench like scripture hidden from the wrong congregation. Bram had found them on her third night in the workshop, after she'd stopped flinching at every sound the boathouse made and started listening to it instead — the tick of cooling timber, the sea worrying the pilings below, the small domestic noises of a dead man's room.

She carried them up to the cottage two at a time, wrapped in her shawl as though they might catch cold.

Wren was asleep. That was the arrangement now, unspoken and therefore binding: her daughter slept while Bram worked, and neither of them named what it was they were working toward. The letter still sat in its drawer, wax seal broken, its demand — *the finished work* — written in a hand too elegant for threats. The stranger from Penzance had not returned, but Bram felt him the way sailors felt weather coming: a pressure behind the ears, a change in the taste of the wind.

She cleared the kitchen table, set the lamp where the light fell cleanest, and opened the first notebook.

Her husband's handwriting met her like a stranger wearing his face.

*Train calculation, going side. Escape wheel, 30 teeth. Pinion 8 leaves.* Numbers marching down the page in columns so neat they looked printed, and between them — this was what undid her — little drawings. A gull. Wren's boot, aged four, with the lace coming undone. Her own hands, sketched once, quickly, holding a mug, steam rising off them in two curling lines. He had drawn her hands the way other men drew saints.

"You were here," she said aloud to the empty kitchen, "all along. Right under my hands."

Fifteen years she had believed grief was the worst thing the sea could give a woman. She had been wrong. Grief at least was honest. This was worse: grief with a trapdoor in it, grief that opened onto more of him than she had ever been permitted to hold.

She read until the lamp burned low. Then she trimmed the wick and read on.

* * *

They had married in April of 1877, in the chapel at Zennor, because it was the only church within walking distance that didn't ask questions. She remembered thinking, even then, even young and half-drunk on him, that it was an odd thing for a man to care about — which churches asked questions. He'd laughed when she said so, kissed her temple, said, *Clever girl. You'll do.*

He had come to Porthmorra with no history anyone could vouch for and a crate of tools nobody asked about. The village was kind that way; the sea took so many men that a man who arrived whole, wanting work, was welcomed before he was known. He mended clocks first — the church clock, the inn's, the great barometer at the Ship Inn that had sulked for a decade. Then watches. Then, quietly, things with no name: a mechanism delivered to a farm cart at midnight, a sealed box he carried to Penzance himself and came back from without the box but with money enough to buy the boat.

She had asked him once, early — lying in the dark of the keeper's cottage, his heartbeat under her ear — *What's in the boxes, Jem?*

And he had said, *Springs. Springs and wheels and the insides of time,* and she had laughed, because it sounded like poetry, and poets don't smuggle.

That was the marriage. Love, real love, the kind that survived winters and stillbirths and the year the fish failed — braided through with these soft places where he simply wasn't. She had learned not to step on them. You learned, married to a man with locked rooms inside him, to walk around the furniture in the dark. She had told herself it was respect. Privacy. A wife's discretion.

Now, reading his notebooks by lamplight, she understood it had been something else entirely. It had been practice. Fifteen years of walking around the furniture had made her fluent in silence, and silence, it turned out, was a language the men in the letter spoke too.

* * *

By the fourth night she could read his shorthand without the key page. By the sixth she had taken apart the dead clock from the cottage mantel — a cheap thing, tin-plated, good for nothing but parts — and laid its organs out on the table like a surgery.

The mainspring defeated her twice. It wanted to uncoil like a serpent and take her fingers with it, and both times she sat back, heart hammering, furious, and heard him in memory: *Respect the spring, Bram. It holds everything you've wound into it, and it doesn't forgive.* He used to say it about clocks. She had thought, at the time, that he was only talking about clocks.

Third attempt she let the spring talk first — watched how it wanted to move, gave it a wooden letting-down tool she'd cut herself from a broom handle — and it came free into her hands, coiled and obedient, humming faintly like something alive.

She sat there with it in her palm and wept, briefly and without drama, the way you cry over a letter found in a coat pocket years late. Not for the spring. For the fifteen years of evenings when he had sat exactly here, doing exactly this, and she had been one room away, mending nets, believing she knew him.

Then she wiped her face, and put the spring in, and it worked.

* * *

The regulator waited below in the workshop, patient as a saint under its dust sheet. She uncovered it each night now and stood before it the way she imagined a priest stood before an altar — knowing the ritual, not yet worthy of it.

It was beautiful. That was the obscenity of it. Even unfinished — the going train assembled, the escapement fitted but not adjusted, the striking work a scatter of parts in labeled drawers — it was the finest thing she had ever seen built by human hands. A long-case regulator, seconds-beating, the pendulum rod of seasoned pine with brass bob, the movement plates pierced and chamfered so the light fell through them like water. On the back plate, half-engraved, a design she hadn't noticed until the seventh night: a lighthouse. Small, precise, its beam rendered in radiating cuts, and beneath it letters she could trace with a fingertip:

*For B., who keeps the light.*

She pressed her hand flat against the cold brass and held it there a long time.

He had been making it for her. All those midnights in the boathouse, all those deliveries, all that double life — and threaded through it, this. A clock that would keep true time for a woman who kept a light. She did not know whether to be moved or murdered. Both, probably. Marriage, she was learning from his notebooks, is a train of gears: every wheel drives another, and you cannot see the whole of it from inside any single tooth.

* * *

The flashbacks came hardest in the small hours, between pages. She stopped fighting them.

There was the winter of '81, when Wren had fever, and Jem had walked to Penzance through the snow rather than wait for the carrier, and come back before dawn with medicine that cost more than three months of lamp oil. Where did the money come from? She hadn't asked. She had been too busy watching her daughter breathe.

There was the night in '86 when a man knocked at the cottage door past midnight, wet to the shoulders, and spoke to Jem in the garden in a language that wasn't words so much as cadences — and Jem had come inside gray-faced and held her so hard she'd complained about her ribs, and he'd said, *If anything ever happens to me, burn the boathouse. Promise me. Burn it and never look back.*

She had promised. She had thought it was a figure of speech, the way men said things.

There was the last morning — God, the last morning, ordinary as bread — when he'd kissed her at the door and said, *Keep the light, my love. I'll mind the time.* And she had laughed at the little joke of it, their old joke, and he had looked back at her from the path with an expression she had spent fifteen years failing to decode and now, standing over his notebooks, decoded in a single cold instant.

He had known. Whatever was coming had already been coming, and he had walked down to his boat anyway, because the alternative was a conversation he couldn't bear to have.

"Damn you," she told the notebook softly, turning a page. "Damn you, Jem Ashgrove." But her thumb smoothed the paper flat, gentle, the way you touch a sleeping child.

* * *

On the ninth night she attempted the escapement.

It was the heart of the thing, he wrote — *everything else is plumbing; the escapement is the soul* — and his notes on it ran to eleven dense pages with diagrams shaded like engravings. The pallets must enter the wheel's teeth at the exact angle; the impulse must be given evenly, or the clock would run fast, slow, dishonest. *A regulator that lies,* he had written in the margin, underlined twice, *is worse than no clock at all.*

Bram read that line four times.

*A regulator that lies is worse than no clock at all.*

He had left it underlined for her. She was certain of it now — certain the way you are certain of tide tables. The whole workshop, the unfinished clock, the notebooks with their careful progression from simple to impossible: it was a curriculum. He had built her a school and then gone out to die, trusting that if the letter ever came, if the wolves ever smelled the work, she would find the school and enroll.

Or he had trusted her to burn it, and some stubborn engine in her had chosen otherwise. Perhaps both. Perhaps he had known that too — known his wife well enough to know she'd choose the harder road just because someone told her not to.

She fitted the pallets. Adjusted. Listened. The escape wheel ticked against them, caught, released — a heartbeat, arrhythmic, sick. She adjusted again. Again. Past midnight, past one o'clock, her eyes burning, the lamp hissing, until —

Tick. Tick. Tick.

Even. Honest. Steady as surf on a calm day.

Bram sat back and listened to it the way you listen to your child breathing in the next room. Somewhere in that small sound was her husband, alive in brass and steel, keeping time at last in a house where he had kept so many secrets.

"I know you now," she whispered. "Better late. But I know you."

The pendulum hung waiting in its case below. The striking train lay in its drawers. Weeks of work remained, and somewhere out beyond the point, she could feel the stranger from Penzance feeling his way toward her along the dark.

She banked the fire, closed the notebooks, and climbed the tower for her middle watch, her fingers still smelling of oil and brass.

From the lantern room, with the beam sweeping its long white arm across the black water, she saw it: a light out at sea, low and green-white, where no fishing boat should be at two in the morning. It showed once. Twice. Then it was gone, swallowed by the dark, running without a lamp toward the cove.

Bram's hand went cold on the rail.

Someone was coming ashore tonight.
