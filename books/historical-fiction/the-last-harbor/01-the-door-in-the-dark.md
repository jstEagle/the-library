## 1. The Door in the Dark

The storm came off the sea like something with intent.

Astrid Winterbourne had kept the light at Grayharrow Point for fifteen years, and she had learned to read storms the way other women read letters—by their weight, their temper, what they meant to take from her. This one arrived early, three weeks before the solstice, and it arrived angry. By dusk the wind had torn the last of the gorse flat against the headland, and by full dark the tower itself was shuddering under blows that felt less like weather than like fists.

She climbed the ninety-three steps anyway. She always climbed them.

At the lantern room she trimmed the wick, wound the clockwork that turned the great lens, and watched the beam swing out over water the color of wet slate. The light went and came, went and came, a patient heartbeat against the noise. Below her, the harbor town of Grayharrow huddled behind its breakwater, its windows lit yellow and few. Fifteen years of watching those windows had taught her their grammar: which ones burned late on market nights, which went dark when the revenue men came down from the north road, which never lit at all.

She had made herself a woman the town looked through. That had been the work of half a lifetime—the plain coats, the unadorned hair, the habit of answering questions with fewer words than they required. Edmund used to laugh at her for it. *You vanish so well, Astrid, you'll forget where you put yourself.* He had been dead eleven years now, and she still caught herself listening for his laugh over the wind.

The lamp needed nothing more. She went down.

In the kitchen she set water to warm and opened the logbook to record the evening: *Wind NNW, rising. Sea heavy. Light burning regular.* Her handwriting was small and even, a hand that gave away nothing. She had just dipped the pen a second time when the sound came—not through the door, but beneath the storm, a rhythm wrong for the wind. Three knocks. A pause. Three again.

No one knocked at the lighthouse. That was the first law of her life here. Fishermen signaled with lamps; the harbormaster sent his boy only in daylight; and no one else came up the cliff path at all, not in fifteen years, not in any weather.

She stood very still, one hand flat on the logbook, and listened to her own heart count the silence between the knocks.

They came again. Weaker now.

She took the poker from beside the stove before she thought better of it, then set it down again, ashamed of the instinct and unable to name why. The bolt was stiff with salt; it fought her, then gave all at once, and the door swung inward into chaos—wind and rain and a swirl of dead leaves—and into the middle of that chaos, small and soaked and swaying, stood a child.

He could not have been more than six. He was wrapped in a blanket far too large for him, wool gone gray with spray, wound around his body the way one wraps an infant—as if whoever left him there had known no other way to carry a child, or had wanted, in some desperate arithmetic, to make him smaller than he was. His hair was plastered black to his skull. His lips were blue. And his eyes, enormous in a face drained white, were fixed on her with an expression she had no name for—not fear exactly, and not pleading. Recognition, perhaps. Or its opposite: the look of someone who has been told to look at a thing and is afraid of what looking will cost him.

"God's teeth," Astrid said, and pulled him inside.

* * *

She worked without thinking, the way she did everything that mattered. Wet clothes stripped away, blanket peeled back—beneath it a boy in a sailor's jersey cut down to fit him, fine wool, someone's careful hands in the mending. She rubbed him down with rough cloth, wrapped him in her own blanket from the bed upstairs, sat him close to the stove with a cup of warmed milk laced with a spoonful of the honey she kept against winter colds. He held the cup in both hands but did not drink. He watched her.

"Where is your mother?" she asked. "Who brought you up the path?"

Nothing. Not a shake of the head, not a shrug. Just those eyes, following her as she moved about the kitchen banking the fire, setting the kettle back, doing ordinary things because ordinary things were the only raft available.

"You're safe," she told him, and heard how strange the words sounded in her own mouth, unused to tenderness, rusted at the hinges. "Do you understand me? You're safe now."

His gaze dropped—to her hands, to the floor, to the fire—and she saw then what she should have seen at the door. His right fist was clenched tight against his chest, knuckles bloodless, and he had been holding it that way since she pulled him in, guarding whatever it held the way a drowning man guards air.

"Let me see," she said gently, kneeling. "What have you got there, lad?"

He flinched. Then, slowly—so slowly it seemed to cost him something physical, some inner battle fought and lost—he opened his fingers.

A locket lay in his palm. Silver, old-fashioned, its chain snapped. It was dented along one edge as if it had been dropped, or trodden, or pried open once by force. Astrid reached out and turned it over with one fingertip, and the world went quiet in a way the storm could not account for.

She knew the locket. She had worn it every day for six years of her married life, until the clasp broke and Edmund promised to mend it and never did, and after he died she had put it away in the bottom drawer of the dresser because wearing it hurt too much and throwing it away was impossible. Hers had held two portraits: Edmund on one side, and on the other—

On the other side of this locket, behind glass gone slightly cloudy with age, was her own face.

Younger. Twenty-five, perhaps twenty-six. Hair pinned up the way she had worn it in the year of their marriage, before she learned to bind it plainly and keep it so. The miniature was painted, not photographed—a good one, delicate work, the kind that cost money—and she remembered the sitting. A traveling artist had come through Grayharrow the spring after her wedding; Edmund had commissioned it as a surprise; she had been furious at the expense and secretly, shamefully, pleased.

There were perhaps three people alive who had ever seen that portrait. Edmund was dead. The artist had drowned off the Skerries a decade ago.

And this boy was holding it in a fist like a relic, in a storm, at her door.

Her hands were steady. She noted that distantly, the way one notes the behavior of a stranger. She turned the locket over. The back was engraved—she had forgotten the engraving, or buried it—but there it was in Edmund's slanted hand, cut deep: *A.W., 1878.*

"Astrid," she said aloud, to hear something human. "My name is Astrid."

The boy's eyes lifted to hers. Something moved behind them—understanding, or terror, or both—and he pressed his lips together so hard they went white.

That was when she saw the paper. It had been tucked inside the wrapping of the blanket, folded small, and the heat of the stove had begun to dry it so that one corner curled open like a leaf. She drew it free with two fingers.

One line, in a hurried hand, ink smeared by rain:

*forget the face*

Nothing else. No name. No explanation. No plea for care or word of return. Five words—or four, depending how you counted—and a boy who would not speak, carrying her own young face against his heart through the worst night of the autumn.

Astrid sat back on her heels. Outside, the wind found some new purchase on the tower and the whole structure groaned, a long iron complaint, and the flame in the stove bent sideways and righted itself. The boy watched her across the small bright room, and she watched him, and between them the locket lay open on the floorboards with her face turned up to the ceiling like something drowned.

Fifteen years. Fifteen years of being no one, of keeping the light and keeping her mouth shut, of letting Grayharrow believe the widow Winterbourne was a dull, dutiful woman with no history worth stealing. She had built that invisibility stone by stone, and she had believed—right up until this moment—that it was load-bearing. That it held up her life.

Now a six-year-old had walked through it in one night, carrying her past in his fist.

"Who sent you?" she asked softly.

He didn't answer. But at the question his whole body changed—a tiny contraction, shoulders drawing in, chin tucking, the flinch of a creature that has learned what questions cost. Whoever had coached him on the journey here had coached him well. *Forget the face.* As if a face could be forgotten. As if she could look at her own twenty-six-year-old self and simply put it down.

She gathered the locket up, closed it, and closed the boy's fingers back around it, folding his grip shut with both her hands.

"Keep it safe, then," she said. "We'll say nothing tonight."

Something in him loosened—one degree, no more. He drank the milk at last, all of it, greedily, and when she led him up the winding stair to show him the great lens turning overhead he stared at it with his mouth open, and for one moment he was only a child seeing a marvel, and her heart cracked along a seam she had thought long sealed.

She made him a bed on the settle by the stove. He lay down obediently, eyes closing, but his hand stayed closed around the locket even in sleep, even when his breathing went deep and even, even when the storm beat its hardest against the glass and the light swept round and round above them like a slow white searchlight looking for something it had lost.

Astrid sat at the table with the note before her and did not sleep.

*Forget the face.* The handwriting nagged at her—not the words, the hand. There was something in the loop of the f, the hard downstroke of the t, that scratched at the back of her mind like a name remembered a half-second too late. She had seen that writing before. Somewhere in another life, in another house, in the years when she had still opened other people's letters.

And the boy's jersey—fine wool, carefully mended, cut down from something larger. She had seen mending like that before too. She had seen it on crates that came off Harrow ships, on sacks in her husband's workshop in the years when the workshop still stood, in the time before the fire took it and everyone agreed it was an accident and no one, least of all Astrid, had ever asked too loudly what exactly had been stored there besides lamp-oil and brass.

Outside, the wind screamed around the gallery rail. Above her the light turned, faithful and blind.

Astrid looked at the sleeping child, at the fist curled around her own young face, and understood that the fifteen years were over. Whatever had come up her cliff path tonight had come from the past, and it had not come to be forgotten.

It had come because someone was desperate enough to spend a child to save him—and in Grayharrow, desperation wore one family's name.

She snuffed the lamp, and in the dark she began, silently, to make a list of everything she had spent fifteen years pretending not to know.
