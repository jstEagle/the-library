## 4. What the Constable Knows

The knock came at half past nine in the morning, which was, I would later reflect, the most civilized hour anyone had ever come to ruin my life.

I knew before I opened the door that it was trouble. Not because of the knock itself — a knock is a knock, and ours came often enough, delivering paper stock and unpaid bills in roughly equal measure — but because of the quality of it. Three raps, evenly spaced, patient. The knock of a man who is paid by the hour and knows the hour is his. Bram knocks like a landslide. Sef knocks like an apology. Corvin doesn't knock at all; he simply appears in doorways like a draft with opinions.

This knock belonged to the city.

"Madam Vane." He was a broad man in a coat too good for a constable's pay and too bad for a gentleman's, with a face like a ledger — everything entered, nothing forgiven. "Constable Harn. Vellmar City Constabulary." He produced a leather wallet and opened it just long enough for me to see a badge and a great deal of official boredom. "Routine inquiries."

"We're a print shop," I said. "Nothing here is routine except the smell of ink and my temper before noon."

He smiled the way a man smiles when he has been instructed to smile and has located the relevant muscles. "May I come in? It's about a missing person."

Now. Here is what you must understand about me, in the winter of 1848: I had never lied to an officer of anything in my life. Not to the tax assessor who suspected our press of being worth more than we declared (it wasn't; it was worth less than my father's memory of it). Not to the excise men. Not even to the charity sisters who came round every Michaelmas collecting for orphans, though I maintained then, as now, that a woman who runs a print shop has no spare coin and no spare sentiment. My father raised me on two commandments — keep your accounts true and your type cleaner — and lying to a constable sat somewhere below counterfeiting on the ladder of sins he'd have climbed down from his grave to box my ears for.

So when I heard myself say, "Of course, Constable," and step aside to let him in, I want it noted that some part of me was already standing outside my own body, watching, appalled, taking notes for a confession I hadn't yet decided to commit.

* * *

Odile was upstairs.

This was not a plan. Plans are things you make on purpose, with forethought, and I had done nothing of the kind. What had happened over the four days since the child appeared on our step — frozen, silent, clutching her note like a hymnal — was less a plan than a sedimentation. She had settled into the household the way silt settles into a riverbed: gradually, invisibly, until one morning you notice the channel has moved and you cannot say when.

She ate at our table now. She slept in the loft above the composing room, in a bed made of proof sheets folded into a pallet, because Sef had declared — with the absolute authority of a runaway compositor who owns three shirts — that no child should sleep on straw in winter. She had begun following Corvin around the shop like a small shadow with questions, and Corvin, who pretends to be carved from exile and disappointment, had begun answering them. Yesterday she had sorted a case of quads for him, correctly, and he had looked at her with an expression I'd never seen on his face and couldn't name and didn't try to.

And this morning, when the knock came, Bram had looked at me — one look, freighted, the look of a man who spent thirty years reading my father's silences and had learned to read mine — and lifted Odile onto his shoulders as if showing her the presses, and carried her up the back stair to the loft, moving with a slowness that fooled no one and everyone.

So when Constable Harn settled into the customer's chair in the front room, brushing snow from his sleeves with the delicacy of a man who did not wish to appear to notice his surroundings but noticed all of them, there were four of us below and one of us above, and the floorboards between us had better manners than any of us did.

"A missing person," I repeated. "In Vellmar. In winter. I imagine the list is long."

"The list is always long." He set his hat on his knee. His eyes went around the room with the unhurried appetite of a customs inspector: the framed specimen sheets, the account books on their shelf, the doorway to the pressroom where the old Albion stood under its dust sheet like furniture in a dead man's house. "But this inquiry concerns a particular family. A family of consequence. They've lost a child — a girl, seven years of age. There's a reward."

"There's always a reward," said Corvin from the doorway of the composing room, where he had materialized with a composing stick in his hand as though he'd merely paused en route to somewhere else. He leaned against the frame. "For information leading to the recovery of property."

Harn's gaze moved to him and stayed a moment longer than comfort allowed. "You'd know about rewards, sir?"

"I know about printers," Corvin said pleasantly. "We're often mistaken for property ourselves."

I could have strangled him. I could also have kissed him, which tells you something about how the last four days had rearranged me. Instead I said, "My journeyman is exiled from Karst, Constable. He finds the constabulary of every nation equally fascinating. Pay him no mind. You said a girl of seven. What family?"

Harn turned back to me with the air of a man resuming a document at his marked page. "That's rather the point of discretion. A certain family. Names aren't given out in these inquiries — you understand. If the child were recognized and returned by someone with a grievance against the family—" he let the sentence hang, and in the hanging of it I heard the shape of the thing he wasn't saying: *if someone is hiding her out of spite, we will make that spite expensive.*

"And what makes you think she'd come through here?" I asked. "We're printers, not foundlings' home."

"No," Harn agreed. "But you're on Lantern Row, and Lantern Row is on the river, and the river quarter sees everything the rest of the city prefers not to. Boats come in at night. Doors open." He unfolded a paper — an actual printed notice, and I confess my eye went first to the typography, which was wretched, set by someone who thought a wide measure and a fat face were the same thing — and read aloud. "'Girl, aged seven, dark hair, answers to no name presently known. Last seen wearing a grey coat.' Grey coat." He looked up. "Might she have passed this way?"

Four days ago I had burned that coat in the kitchen stove, piece by piece, while Odile watched me with those enormous grave eyes, and Sef had cut the buttons off first because — he said, not meeting anyone's gaze — metal keeps its history, and there was no sense leaving it lying about. I had told myself it was practicality. Cold children need warm coats, and a grey coat with a fine lining is a coat that gets remembered.

It had not felt like practicality. It had felt like the first stroke of a lie being set in type, one letter at a time, and me holding the stick.

"She might have passed anywhere," I said. "Half the city wears grey. It's the color of the decade."

Harn almost smiled at that. Almost. "It is indeed." He rose, tugged his coat straight, and put his hat on with the care of a man closing a ledger he intended to reopen. "You'll send word to the station house if a child answers the description. There's a reward — five crowns, and the gratitude of a family that repays services rendered. And—" he paused at the door, hand on the latch, and gave me the full weight of the ledger-face, "—the family also repays the reverse. Concealment is a crime, Madam Vane. harboring, they call it. It carries a term."

"I'll bear the statute in mind," I said, and my voice held, which surprised me more than the visit had.

He let himself out. The bell over the door gave its small tin commentary, and then the street swallowed him, and I stood in the middle of my father's front room listening to my own heart conduct an unscheduled rehearsal.

* * *

Bram came down the stairs with Odile still on his shoulders, which was either excellent theater or a terrible one; a child carried openly is a child hidden in plain sight, or so I told myself while my hands shook so badly I had to grip the case edge.

"Well?" he said.

"He wants a girl of seven in a grey coat. No name given. 'A certain family.'" I looked at Odile, perched up there among the rafters with her chin on Bram's bald head as though it were a cushion designed for the purpose. "Did you hear any of that, little one?"

"Yes," said Odile.

"Then you understand why you were upstairs."

"You said I was helping count the type," she said. "I was counting the type. There are a hundred and fifty-four e's in the case and one is cracked."

There was a silence of the kind that households have when everyone is privately recalculating everything. Corvin set his composing stick down with exaggerated gentleness, the way a man puts down a weapon he'd very much like to use.

"One is cracked," he agreed gravely. "Which means the count is ruined and we must begin again tomorrow. Possibly every day for some time."

"That's not how counting works," said Odile.

"It is exactly how counting works in this shop," said Sef from the kitchen doorway, flour on his hands, bread dough abandoned mid-knead. He had the look of a man who had heard everything through the floor and had opinions he was choosing, for once, to knead instead of speak.

Bram lowered the child to the floor. She went to the type cabinet — her cabinet now, by unspoken treaty — and began sorting quads with the fierce concentration of the recently frightened, and the four of us drifted toward the far end of the pressroom, out of her hearing, which required no discussion because we had already become the kind of household that drifts without discussing.

"Harboring," Bram said quietly. "That's a prison term. That's her—" he nodded at the small bent head across the room, "—taken away, and you in the dock beside her. Mirabel. Your father built this shop with clean hands. Thirty years, not one libel suit, not one seizure. Do you know what that cost him? Restraint isn't cowardice. It's craft."

"My father," I said, "is dead, and the shop is mine, and I have not yet decided anything."

"You've decided," said Corvin. "You lied to a constable. In your own front room. You, who balance the accounts to the farthing and once made me re-set an entire pamphlet because a widow was charged a ha'penny too much." He said it without malice, which was worse. "People don't learn to lie in an afternoon, Mirabel. They learn it in a moment, and spend years discovering why."

I wanted to argue. I opened my mouth to argue and found the argument had already left the building, along with my detachment, my doctrine that revolutions were ink for other people's newspapers, and quite possibly my ability to ever again look at a five-crown reward notice with a steady eye.

Because here is the thing I could not explain to them, and barely to myself: I didn't lie for strategy. Strategy would have said give the child up, take the reward, keep the hands clean, let the law do what law does. I lied because when Harn said *a certain family*, something in my chest had closed around Odile like a composing stick closing on a line — firm, final, locking every letter in place — and I had understood, with the total clarity of the newly damned, that no one was taking that child out of this shop. Not for five crowns. Not for fifty. Not for the gratitude of any family in Vellmar, certain or otherwise.

I didn't know why. That was the honest terror of it. Love, if that's what it was after four days, ought to announce itself, ought to come with reasons attached, itemized, invoiced. This arrived unpriced and unsigned, like a parcel left on a doorstep in the night.

"Forget the face," I said aloud, mostly to myself.

"What?" said Bram.

"The note. *Forget the face.* Everyone assumed it meant whoever left her was hiding from someone. But what if it means the opposite? What if the danger isn't whoever brought her — what if it's her face itself? What if there's something in that child's face that a certain family needs forgotten?"

Sef had gone very still, the way he does, folding himself smaller as though stillness were shelter. "Like a likeness," he said softly. "Like someone she looks like."

The stove ticked. Across the room, Odile sorted type, e's from everything else, patient as winter.

"Tomorrow," I said, "while she's at her letters with Corvin, I'm going through that coat's replacement — the one Sef bought her. Every seam. Children's clothes carry things. Coins, notes, keepsakes. Mothers sew things into linings when they're afraid." I heard my own voice and did not recognize it: low, certain, already committed. "If someone went to the trouble of carrying a child across a frozen city at night and leaving her with strangers and four words of instruction, then those four words are the smallest part of what they left us."

"And if you find something?" Bram asked. "What then, Mirabel? You can't unknow it. Whatever it is, you'll be holding it, and holding it makes you part of it. That's how it always goes. First you hide a child. Then you hide a paper. Then you're hiding a person, and then a press, and then it's you in the cart."

"Bram," I said, "you've described my entire week."

He didn't laugh. None of us laughed. Outside, the river wind worried at the shutters, and somewhere down Lantern Row a watchman called the hour, and upstairs in the loft lay a grey coat's ashes and downstairs sat a child who counted e's because counting was the only thing anyone had promised her would happen again tomorrow.

I looked at her small bent back, at the careful pile of quads growing under her hands, and felt the last plank of my old position — *history happens elsewhere, ink belongs to other people* — go soft and rotten under my feet, like ice on the Vell in late February.

"First thing tomorrow," I said again, and this time it sounded like a vow, and vows, my father taught me, are debts entered in a ledger that death does not close.

Corvin picked up his composing stick. Sef went back to his bread. Bram stood a moment longer, looking at the press under its sheet, at the child, at me — weighing, always weighing, a man whose loyalty was poured in two molds and cracking down the middle of both.

And Odile, without looking up, held one small metal letter high in the air and asked the room:

"What does this one say?"

It was a lowercase *g*. I knew, even then, that whatever answer I gave her, I would spend the rest of this story living inside it.
