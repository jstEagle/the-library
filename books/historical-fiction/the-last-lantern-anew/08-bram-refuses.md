## 8. Bram Refuses

I have been a compositor for forty-one years, and in that time I have learned that a man can tell everything about another man by how he handles type. The careless ones pinch. The proud ones hold each piece up to the light like a jeweler, wasting the day. And the frightened ones — the frightened ones set their letters too tight, as if closeness were safety.

Corvin handles type like a man asking forgiveness.

I watched him from the stairs that evening, down in the composing room where he thought no one could see him, laying out the first lines of that pamphlet of his with the tenderness of a man dressing a wound. *The Lantern Anew*, he called it. Words enough to hang us all twice over, and he touched every letter like it was his firstborn.

Mirabel stood at the stone beside him, sleeves rolled, ink to her elbows. She had that look on her face she gets when she thinks she is being practical. It is the same look her father used to get, which is precisely why I have spent eleven years pretending not to notice it.

I came down the stairs the way I always do, slow, one hand on the rail, because my knees stopped being young around the same year old Vane stopped being alive, and I put myself between them and the press.

"No," I said.

Mirabel looked up. "No what? We haven't asked you anything."

"You were about to." Corvin had gone very still, the way men do when they've been caught and know it. "And the answer is no. Not in this shop. Not on this press. Not while I draw breath and can stand between you and it."

"Bram—"

"The shop was your father's life." I said it plainly, because there was no other way to say it. "Every beam of this roof he cut himself or paid for with money earned letter by letter. He built Vane's into something the city respected. Something the magistrates brought their ordinances to. Something safe. And you mean to turn it into a munitions works."

"It's paper and ink," Mirabel said.

"It's treason with a colophon," I said. "They hang printers, girl. Do you know what they did to Aldous Renk, over in Hessel? Set him up with a rope and made his own apprentices watch. His press they broke and threw in the canal, piece by piece, so the water would carry the lesson downstream. That was for printing a sermon two degrees too warm. This—" I nodded at Corvin's galley, at the neat rows of accusation sitting there in twelve-point, "—this is a declaration of war against General Maraval himself, signed with our font."

"Nobody signs it," Corvin said quietly. "That's rather the point."

"Nobody signs it until somebody hangs for it," I said. "They don't need signatures. They need an example, and a print shop full of misfits is the most convenient example in Vellmar."

I expected Mirabel to argue. She is her father's daughter; arguing is the family trade, practiced alongside the other one. But instead she just looked at me, arms crossed, ink drying black in the creases of her knuckles, and said, "You've known. How long?"

"Since the night you pulled that child out of the snow and didn't send for the constabulary," I said. "A man who keeps a print shop learns to read a form before it's locked. You'd already decided something. I could see it in how you looked at the press."

"And you said nothing."

"I hoped it would pass," I admitted. "Hoping is most of what an old man does. The rest is oiling things."

"Well, it hasn't passed." She stepped forward, and I will grant her this — she has never once been afraid of me, which is either a compliment to my character or an indictment of it. "Bram, listen to me. There are soldiers in the river quarter. Sef saw a boy shot in the street for standing near a barricade. The armistice is days away, and if it's signed as written, Maraval walks away from the massacres at Calvet Ford with a pension and a parade. Corvin's pamphlet might be the only thing that stops it."

"Might," I said. "A fine word. Might stop the armistice. Might also bring a company of dragoons through that door at four in the morning. And then what happens to the child sleeping upstairs? What happens to Sef, who is seventeen and has a face the constables already know? What happens to you?"

"What happens to any of us if we do nothing?" she shot back. "What happens to Odile if her grandfather signs away the last people who could testify for her mother?"

"That is not our fight."

"Whose is it, then?" Her voice cracked on it, just slightly, and I saw the thing underneath the anger, the thing she keeps wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of herself like a mold nobody's supposed to find. "Somebody else's. It's always somebody else's. That's what you taught me, isn't it? Keep the accounts, mind the press, let history happen to other people."

"I taught you nothing of the kind," I said, and my own voice came out rougher than I intended. "Your father taught you exactly that, God forgive him, and I stood by and let him do it because he paid my wages and because grief makes cowards of everyone standing next to it."

The composing room went quiet. Even the stove seemed to hush. Corvin took one careful step backward, a man retreating from a fire he'd helped start.

"What did you say?" Mirabel asked. Softly. Which from Mirabel is worse than shouting.

I should have stopped there. Forty-one years in the trade teaches you when a line of type is set wrong — you feel it in your hands before your eyes confirm it — and I felt it then. But I am an old man and tired, and tired men finish the sentence.

"He left you this shop instead of a childhood, and you've spent eleven years dusting the altar of it," I said. "You cut his type. You keep his hours. You wear his apron, though it hangs off you like a sail. You won't even change the sign in the window, though the paint's peeling, because heaven forbid the city think anything at Vane's has changed since Edmund Vane died. And now, when something finally matters — when the world comes knocking with its face half-frozen on our doorstep — you want to protect the shop. Not the people in it. The shop. Because the shop is all he ever gave you, and you're still trying to earn it."

The silence afterward had weight, like wet snow sliding off a roof.

Mirabel's face did something complicated. Anger first, quick and bright, and I braced for it. But under the anger was the other thing, the true thing, and it looked less like rage than like a woman finding a bruise she'd forgotten she had.

"You don't know anything about it," she said.

"No," I agreed. "I only swept the floor while he worked eighteen-hour days and sent you to bed without supper when the orders ran late. I only heard him tell visitors, more times than I can count, that the shop would be his monument. I only watched a girl of fourteen learn to set type faster than any journeyman I ever trained, and watched him tell her it was adequate. Adequate." I laughed, and it came out bitter as alum. "I set that word in my head that day, Mirabel. Metal type. It's never come out. Some sorts stick in the hand forever."

"Stop," she said.

"He loved you," I said. "Don't mistake me. He loved you the way he loved the press — fiercely, and entirely on his own terms, and without once asking what it cost the thing he loved. And you have become him. That's the tragedy of this house. Not the soldiers. Not the pamphlet. You, standing there in his apron, about to make the same bargain he made — trading the living for the legacy."

"The legacy kept a roof over four people's heads," she said, but her voice had lost its footing.

"The legacy is a chain," I said, "and you've been wearing it so long you call it an heirloom."

She turned away from me. Stood looking at the press — old Vane's press, the iron lady, bought secondhand the year Mirabel was born, so that in a sense the two of them had grown up together, and I suppose that was the whole trouble from the beginning. Two children in one house, and the father only had hands for one of them.

"I'm not doing this to honor him," she said at last, to the press, not to me. "I'm doing it because there's a child upstairs who was told to forget her own face, and because a boy I've fed for three years watched another boy die in the street, and because Corvin carries guilt around like a sack of lead type and I'd rather carry the risk than watch him carry that. If Father disapproves, he can haunt me. I'll set his complaint in eight-point and run it as a correction."

Despite everything — despite the fear sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone — I almost smiled. She has her father's stubbornness, but thank God she got her mother's wit, wherever that woman went.

"It's not about approval," I said. "It's about arithmetic. One pamphlet against five of us. One truth against five lives. I've done that sum before, Mirabel. In 'thirty-one, in the north, in a press circle you've never heard of because none of them lived long enough to be heard of. I was the youngest then. I carried the forms to the river at night and I told myself the words mattered more than the men, and then the men were gone and the words were gone too, burned with the shop, and the only thing that survived was me, remembering. That's the whole harvest of resistance, sometimes. A survivor with a memory."

"Then you remember," Mirabel said, turning back to face me, "which is more than the dead got. Bram. Please. I'm not asking you to set type. I'm asking you not to stand in front of the press."

And there it was — the fork in the road, laid out plain. Stand aside, and be the old man who let it happen. Stand firm, and maybe be the old man who saved them, or maybe just the old man who was right too late. Or stand with them, and hang beside them, and at least hang in good company.

I looked at my hands. Ink in every crack, the way it's been since I was nine years old. Old Vane used to say you couldn't get it out, only wait for new skin. He said it about ink. He meant it about everything.

"I need to think," I said.

"We don't have much time," Corvin murmured.

"Then I'll think quickly," I said, and climbed the stairs, slow, one hand on the rail, feeling every one of my sixty-three years and several I haven't reached yet.

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat in my room above the shop with the lamp turned low and listened to the sounds of the house — Sef moving about below like a ghost practicing to be solid, the tick of the stove, the wind worrying the sign in the window. Once, past midnight, I heard small footsteps cross the landing above me, light as falling type, and pause outside my door. Odile. She stood there a moment, breathing, and moved on. Children always know which doors hide worry. They can smell it through the wood.

I thought about old Vane. Eleven years dead and still the loudest man in the building. I thought about the day he hired me, a northern boy with a warrant's shadow behind him, and asked no questions except whether I knew my cases. I thought about the day Mirabel was born, and how he held the baby for exactly one minute before handing her to the nurse and going back down to check on a job. I was there. I saw it. I told myself for years it was the pressure of the work. It was not the pressure of the work.

And I thought about Aldous Renk swinging in Hessel, and about the boy Sef saw shot, and about a seven-year-old whose own grandfather's men are hunting her likeness through the streets of this city.

Somewhere toward morning I realized I had made my decision somewhere in the middle of the argument and had simply been too proud to admit it. That is the way of decisions, mostly. We don't make them; we notice them.

But before I could go down and say so — before I could take my place at the case like a man coming home — there came a knock at the street door below. Firm. Unhurried. The knock of someone who expects doors to open for him.

I went to the window and looked down through the frost-feathered glass, and my stomach dropped through all eleven years of the shop's foundations.

A man stood on the step in a fine dark coat, no uniform, no colors, hat in hand like a gentleman calling on a friend. But he stood the way officers stand, weight even, chin level, and across the street two more figures leaned against the wall of the chandlery with the studied idleness of men being paid to lean.

He wasn't there for the pamphlet. He couldn't be — no one knew of it yet. No, I knew that walk, that patience. He was there for the girl.

I turned from the window and went down the stairs as fast as my knees allowed, calling ahead in a whisper, "Mirabel. Mirabel — someone's at the door, and he's not selling anything."
