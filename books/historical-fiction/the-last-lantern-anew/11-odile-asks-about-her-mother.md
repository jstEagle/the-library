## 11. Odile Asks About Her Mother

There is a particular quality to a print shop at four in the morning that I imagine the inside of a church must share, if the church were honest about itself. The smell of oil and metal. The sense of having been trusted with something older than you are. The quiet that isn't really quiet — the settling of the building, the tick of the stove cooling, the small sounds of a child breathing in the loft above.

I had been up since two, chasing an error in the forme for Corvin's pamphlet. A reversed *n* where an *u* belonged — the kind of mistake that would have made my father take off his spectacles and clean them very slowly, which was how he shouted. I found it on the third pass, corrected it, and was re-inking my fingers for no reason other than habit when I heard the stair creak and turned to find Odile standing at the bottom step in her nightdress, bare feet blue-white against the boards, holding her coat bunched against her chest like a shield she'd decided to carry rather than wear.

"You should be asleep," I said.

"I had a dream," she said, in the tone of a person announcing weather. "About Mama."

Every adult in this household has developed, in the weeks since she arrived, a small repertoire of deflections. Bram goes gruff. Sef goes busy. Corvin goes scholarly, as though childhood were a subject he'd read about once. I had been relying on a fourth method: simply not being present when the questions came. It is remarkable how much of parenting consists of scheduling.

But it was four in the morning, and there was no one else awake to schedule around me.

"Come here, then," I said, and pulled the stool out from the stone — the composing stone, waist-high, scarred white by forty years of setting type. She climbed onto it the way she now climbed onto everything, with the unselfconscious competence of a child who has learned that furniture is for using. I put my coat over her shoulders because the stove had gone cold and I hadn't the heart to build it up yet.

She looked at the galley of type in front of us. She can pick out her letters now; we've been teaching her without quite admitting we're teaching her, the way you teach a child anything you're afraid to name. "What does it say?"

"It says that men who order massacres should not be permitted to sign peace treaties," I said, and then, hearing myself, added, "in nicer words."

"Mama said words could be weapons."

The stove ticked. Somewhere above us a beam settled with a groan like an old man changing his mind.

"When did she say that?"

Odile considered the question with the gravity she brought to everything, chin tucked, eyes fixed on some middle distance only she could see. "In the house with the smell." A pause. "Before the bad house."

"The house with the smell."

"Ink," she said. "And the hot metal. Like here." She looked up at me then, and I felt something shift under my ribs, the way a forme shifts when you don't lock it properly — everything still in its place, technically, but nothing secure anymore. "Mama knew printers."

* * *

Here is what I had told myself until that hour, and I tell it now so you understand precisely how far I fell: I had told myself that Odile was a stray. A misfortune delivered to the wrong door, a burden assigned by accident or malice to a household already overfull with strays — an exiled typesetter, a runaway compositor, a foreman too loyal to retire. Children appear at doors in hard winters. Constables ask after them. Officers in civilian dress offer coin for them. None of it, I had maintained, needed to connect. The world is full of disconnected cruelties; that is practically its defining feature.

Corvin had not believed in the disconnection. Corvin, who carried his guilt the way other men carry a bad back — stooped, compensating, refusing to discuss it — had looked at the note (*forget the face*) and at the locket I'd shown him and said, quietly, "Printers, Mirabel. Someone trusted printers with this child. Ask yourself why."

I had declined to ask myself why. Asking myself why was how one ended up setting seditious type at four in the morning, and I preferred to believe I did that for abstract reasons — justice, principle, the general irritant of watching armed men decide other people's futures. Abstract reasons can be put down. They can be walked away from, if it comes to it.

A child's mother cannot be walked away from. A child's mother is a specific woman, possibly alive, possibly in a military prison, and she handed her daughter to strangers with three words scratched on paper because she had nowhere else to put her hope.

"Tell me about the houses," I said. I kept my voice level, the way you keep your hand level when you're distributing type — because if it shakes, everything spills.

"There was the big house first." Odile's fingers found the edge of the galley and traced it, careful not to touch the type itself; someone had taught her that too. "Grandfather's house. It had a lot of mirrors. Mama didn't like the mirrors." A frown. "She said they only showed you what other people wanted to see."

I thought of the miniature portrait sewn into her coat lining — the general's face, and beside it a young woman with Odile's same stubborn jaw. I thought: *she fled a house full of mirrors.* I thought: *of course she did.*

"And then?"

"A smaller house. With the smell. There was a man who let her sleep in the back room and he had ink on his hands all the time, even his face sometimes, and Mama laughed more there." Odile said this without accusation, without wonder — as fact, the way children report the geography of their lives. "He taught me letters. He had a cat named Comma."

"Comma," I repeated. My voice did something embarrassing on the second syllable. I covered it by reaching for the ink slab and wiping it, though it didn't need wiping.

"He said every sentence needs somewhere to breathe." She recited it like scripture, which, in our trade, it essentially is.

"What happened to the man with the cat, Odile?"

The tracing finger stopped. And here is the thing about children that adults spend their whole lives failing to learn: they know exactly what they are doing when they go quiet. Odile went quiet the way a compositor pauses before justifying a line — measuring, deciding how much space the truth required.

"Soldiers came," she said finally. "At morning. Mama woke me up and put me in a cupboard and said, whatever you hear, count to five hundred. So I counted. I got to four hundred and twelve and then it was quiet and I counted the rest anyway because she said to." Her voice stayed flat, factual, terrible. "Then she wasn't there anymore. And a lady I didn't know took me away, walking, walking, and then it was snowing and she knocked on your door and gave me the paper and went."

"Gave you the note." My mouth was dry. "Did she say anything? Anything at all?"

"She said, 'Give it to the ink people.'" Odile shrugged, seven years old and matter-of-fact. "So I did."

*Forget the face.* Three words in a frightened hand. I had read them a hundred times since that night, turning them over like a suspect coin, and I had built from them a dozen small theories — a warning, a superstition, a madwoman's riddle. Sitting there with the child warm under my coat, the truth assembled itself with the ugly efficiency of a locked forme: not a warning about danger in the abstract. An instruction. *Destroy the likeness.* Because the likeness — the general's face, the mother's face, the proof that the hero of Vellmar had a daughter who ran and a granddaughter who could be found — was worth more to certain men than the child herself. Because if the soldiers searching the city found the locket, they would find not a stray but a bloodline, and bloodlines get erased at the root.

And the mother — the mother who hid with printers, who taught her daughter that words were weapons, who counted her escape in cupboards and borrowed rooms — the mother had been caught. Was caught. Might be sitting this very hour in a cell in the garrison, alive for exactly as long as she remained useful, or perhaps alive for no reason anyone would ever be required to explain.

I had spent six weeks telling myself history happened elsewhere. It had happened in a cupboard, four hundred and twelve counts long, to a girl whose feet were cold against my floor.

* * *

"Do you remember the lady's face?" I asked. "The one who brought you?"

Odile shook her head. "It was dark. She had a scarf." Then, with the unnerving precision of children: "Her hands smelled of onions. And she was crying, but quietly, the way you do when you don't want the child to hear."

I filed *onion hands* away next to *house with the smell* in the growing ledger of details that might someday matter. I am my father's daughter in this if nothing else: I keep accounts.

"Odile." I turned on the stool so we were eye to eye. Her gaze held mine the way it always did — direct, unblinking, a little disconcerting, the gaze of someone who has already seen the worst thing and is waiting to see whether you'll lie about it. "Your mother. Do you want to find her?"

Something moved behind her eyes — hope, maybe, or its exhausted cousin. "Is she lost?"

"I think," I said carefully, "that she may be in a place where people are kept. A prison. Soldiers' prison."

"Because of Grandfather?"

The question landed harder than any blow I've taken in my life, and I include the time Bram dropped a chase of type on my foot. Because of Grandfather. Seven years old, and she had already worked out the shape of it — that the man whose portrait slept against her heart was also the reason her mother ran, and hid, and was taken. Children do the arithmetic we refuse to do. We hand them the numbers and act surprised at the sum.

"Yes," I said. "Because of him. Because her father is a powerful man, and she disagreed with him, and powerful men don't like to be disagreed with by their own family. It makes them look fallible."

"What's fallible?"

"Able to be wrong." I smiled, and it felt like lifting a heavy form. "Which he is. Very."

She absorbed this. Then she said, in a small clear voice that I will hear until I die: "If we make the pamphlet, will it help her come back?"

We. Not *you*. Not *the grown-ups*. We. Somewhere between the cupboard and my composing stone, this child had concluded that she was part of the work — that the pamphlet with the beautiful damning sentences was hers too, that the letters she was learning were ammunition she'd be old enough to fire someday. Corvin would have called it consciousness. Bram would have called it corruption of a minor. I called it, in that moment, the end of every excuse I had left.

"I don't know," I told her, because I had decided, somewhere in the last ten minutes, that I would never lie to this child again — that lying to her was the family inheritance I refused, the one thing I would not take from my father's shop along with the press. "I don't know if it helps her. But I know that staying silent definitely doesn't. Silence has never once brought anybody home. I've checked. It's in all the records."

That earned me half a smile, the ghost of one, quickly folded away. Children ration their smiles in winter.

"Then we should finish it," she said. "Can I set my name? Sef said when I can do all my letters, I can set my own name."

"Sef," I said, "is a menace and a flatterer." But I lifted her down from the stone, and I did not send her back to bed, and when she asked — she asked, God help me, she asked *me*, as though I were the sort of person one asks — whether her mother would be proud of the pamphlet, I said yes. I said it without hesitation, without hedging, with the full authority of a woman who had just that moment stopped pretending to be a bystander in her own life.

Yes. She would be proud. She hid you with printers because printers are the people who fight with the only weapons that can't be confiscated, and she would be proud, and when we find her — *when*, I said, *when*, and heard myself commit to the word — she can set her own name in type too, and no one will ever erase it again.

* * *

She went back up to the loft eventually, trailing my coat, and I stood alone in the cold shop with the corrected forme gleaming faintly in the lamplight and understood that I had crossed a line I couldn't uncross.

Not the printing. The printing, I could still have told myself, was Corvin's crusade, Sef's fever, Bram's argument — a household quarrel I was arbitrating. What I had crossed was quieter and deeper. I had promised a child her mother. I had said *when* out loud. And promises, unlike type, cannot be redistributed once set; they lock into the forme of a life and print themselves on everything you do afterward.

I thought about the garrison across the river, its lit windows, its ledgers of prisoners. Somewhere in it, if Odile's fragments were true — and they had the ring of truth, the specific grainy texture of things actually witnessed — a woman sat who had trusted strangers with her daughter because strangers-with-a-press were the last institution left in Vellmar that answered to conscience instead of command. She had chosen us. Not the church, not the constabulary, not some rich cousin with a cellar. Us: a dead man's daughter in denial, a traitor doing penance, a boy running from a barricade, an old man guarding a legacy like a dragon on a hoard. She had looked at that ragtag inventory and decided it was hope.

Well. If we were to be hope, we would have to become it properly.

I banked the stove. I hung my apron on its nail — my father's nail, my father's apron, and for the first time the inheritance felt less like a chain than a tool, which is to say something you pick up on purpose. Upstairs, through the floorboards, I could hear Odile turn over in her sleep, and beneath that, fainter, the sound of Sef coming down the cellar stairs to his blanket among the paper stores, and I thought: we are all sleeping in the places we feel safest, and none of those places will survive what's coming unless we make it survive.

At dawn I would wake Corvin and tell him what the child had told me. That the mother lived — probably, possibly, *when* — and that she had been taken from a printer's house, which meant the garrison had a list, and the list had names, and one of those names might be a man with ink on his face and a cat named Comma who had vanished from the record years ago and whom Corvin, when I said it aloud, would go white about.

I didn't know that yet, standing in the dark. I only knew that the denial was finished, burned off like fog in sun, and what remained was simple and enormous: a promise, a press, and however many days remained before the armistice table convened and decided, without us, whether any of it mattered.

Outside, the river ice boomed once, far off — the sound it makes when it begins, at last, to break.
