## 21. Armistice

The news came to us the way all important news comes to Vellmar — secondhand, shouted, and slightly wrong.

Sef burst up from the cellar at midday with his sleeves still dusted in type-metal grime, shouting that the war was over, the general was dead, the city was burning, pick whichever you like. It took Corvin, who had gone out to the square and come back pale and quiet, to give us the true shape of it: the armistice was signed. And attached to it, in ink that would be copied into every gazette from here to the coast, was a clause no one had dared hope for — the resignation of General Aldous Verrane, effective immediately, pending inquiry into "conduct during the river campaigns."

*Conduct.* What a small, polite word for what we had set in ten-point Caslon.

I stood in the doorway of the shop — or what had been the shop, the black ribs of it still smoking faintly three days after the soldiers left it — and I confess my first feeling was not triumph. It was not even relief. It was the peculiar flatness of a woman who has been holding her breath for six weeks and has just been told she may exhale, only to discover she has forgotten how.

"They'll want to know who printed it," Bram said behind me. He was leaning against the surviving doorpost, arms crossed, watching the street the way he always watched the street, as if it owed him money.

"Half the city knows who printed it," I said. "The other half is wearing the pamphlet under their coats."

"That's not the same as knowing."

No. It wasn't. But for the first time since the night Odile arrived on our step with her note and her frozen hands, I found I didn't much care about the difference. Let them know. Let them write it down. My name was on the last pamphlet — *printed by Mirabel Vane, Vellmar* — in letters I had cut myself, and if that signature was a rope, then it was also, for once in my life, a name I had chosen to sign rather than one I'd inherited.

Odile was sitting on the overturned stone trough that used to hold our quench water, swinging her legs, eating an apple Sef had charmed off a market cart. She had heard the word *armistice* and understood it meant no more soldiers would come through the door. Beyond that, I think, the politics of it were simply weather happening to adults. When Corvin told her that her grandfather had resigned, she considered this with the gravity of a magistrate and said, "Good. He was mean to Mama."

Out of the mouths of seven-year-olds, the entire case for the inquiry, delivered in four words. Corvin flinched as though she'd struck him, which, in fairness, she had, without knowing it.

* * *

We spent the afternoon doing the arithmetic of ruin.

The press was gone — smashed in the raid, its iron screw bent into something that looked like a piece of modern sculpture, if modern sculpture were made by vandals with bayonets. The roof was half open to the sky, letting in a gray Vellmar drizzle that fell with great patience onto everything we owned. The composing room had burned hottest; the frames were charcoal, the floor was charcoal, the poster of my father's founding date above the door — *VANE & SONS, 1801* — had burned down to *VANE &*, which felt less like vandalism than editorial comment.

But the type. The type had survived.

It survived because of Bram, who in the last sane hour before the raid had carried cabinet after cabinet down into the cellar while the rest of us argued about whether there would even *be* a raid. It survived because of the false back he'd built into the largest cabinet years ago, back when my father was alive and smuggling was a rumor rather than a profession. And it survived because when the soldiers dragged Bram out the door in irons, he had looked at me across the wreckage of the shop and said nothing at all, because there was nothing that needed saying. The type was safe. That was the whole conversation.

Now I knelt in the cellar among those cabinets, opening drawer after drawer, and it was like visiting the dead and finding them breathing. The 12-point body text, unharmed. The italics. The little fount of ornaments my father had bought in a fit of optimism in 1830 and never once used. Each drawer slid out with its familiar wooden whisper, each compartment held its silent congregation of letters standing shoulder to shoulder, waiting to be asked what they thought.

"You're petting the type," Sef observed from the stairs.

"I am assessing inventory."

"You're petting it."

I was. There are worse things a woman can love. My father loved this shop more than he loved me, or so I spent thirty years believing, and here I was on my knees in its ashes loving the pieces of it, and I could no longer tell whether that made me his daughter or his fool. Perhaps the categories had collapsed. Perhaps they'd never been separate.

Corvin came down and stood among the cabinets with his hat in his hands, which he did when he was about to say something difficult. Corvin had a great many hats, metaphorically speaking, and took most of them off before hard sentences.

"The widow's people can get us a press," he said. "From Lisse. Within the month, if the roads stay open."

"We have no roof."

"A press doesn't care about roofs. Neither does type." He ran one finger along the edge of a cabinet, leaving a line in the ash. "Mirabel. We won. Do you understand that? Not cleanly, not completely — but the armistice has a clause in it that exists because a handful of fools in a cellar refused to let a general retire quietly. Whatever else happens, that clause is ours. We put it there."

I wanted to feel it the way he said it. I reached for the feeling the way you reach for a warm coal, and got some of it, enough. "And Bram?"

The silence that followed had a shape, and the shape was the empty chair by the cold stove upstairs where Bram had sat every evening for twenty-two years, complaining about the price of lead and the morals of apprentices.

"He'll come back," Corvin said. "Exchanges have started. The commission lists printers separately now — did you know that? There's a category for us. We've become a class of prisoner, like officers or priests." He almost smiled. "Bram will be insufferable about the rank."

* * *

He came back nine days later, on a gray morning in early March, walking up the Rue des Fountaines on his own feet with a soldier's blanket over his shoulders like a cape he hadn't asked for.

I saw him from the doorway and my whole body did something embarrassing — lurched, is the honest word, lurched like a press taking a bad sheet — and then I made myself stand still, because Bram Halloway had carried our type down into the dark under fire and had endured three weeks of a military prison without giving up a single name, and the least I could do was receive him with dignity instead of weeping in the street.

Dignity lasted four seconds.

He stopped in front of me. He was thinner, grayer at the temples where he'd been merely silvered, and there was a new stiffness in his right hand that I noticed at once and knew he'd want me to pretend not to. He looked past me at the gutted shop, at the sky showing through the roof, at the *VANE &* hanging scorched above the door.

"Well," he said. "You've redecorated."

"Bram—"

"Hold on." He raised the stiff hand, and I waited. He looked at the ruins a long moment. Then he said, quietly, in the voice he used for correcting a forme that was set wrong, "Your father built this place to outlive him. I told him once it wouldn't. I owe you an admission, girl: I'm glad to be wrong."

Then Odile hit him at knee height like a small determined cannonball, and dignity was concluded for the day.

He ate two bowls of the soup Sef made — Sef's soup being less a dish than an apology — and told us about the prison in the flat, unhurried tone of a man reading a shipping manifest. The cell. The questions. The officer who had shown him a copy of *The Lantern Anew* and asked, with genuine bewilderment, why men would die for paper. And Bram had answered — I made him repeat it twice, because I didn't believe it the first time — *"Because paper is how the dead keep talking."*

"They didn't like that," he added.

"I imagine not."

"They gave me bread anyway. Prisoners' bread is terrible, but they gave it." He turned his damaged hand over, flexing the fingers slowly, testing them like new type. "They asked me who set the last form. I told them the truth. I said a dead woman set it, and a dead man's daughter, and a couple of exiles, and a child learning her letters. They wrote it down. I don't think they knew what to do with it."

Corvin laughed — a short, cracked sound, the laugh of a man who had confessed his worst sin to all of us in the cellar and was still learning how to sit inside his own skin afterward. "That's the best colophon I've ever heard."

"It's accurate," Bram said. "Accuracy matters." And then, because he was Bram, and because grace from Bram always arrived disguised as criticism, he looked at me and said, "Your spacing on the final page was too tight. You were rushing."

I was. I had been rushing toward the dawn and the soldiers and the fire, and every em-square on that page knew it. "Noted," I said. "For the next edition."

The table went quiet. Because *the next edition* was a sentence none of us had earned yet, spoken over a shop with no roof, in a city with half its printers jailed and the other half hiding, in a country whose armistice was signed but whose peace was, at best, a proof not yet pulled.

* * *

That evening we climbed to what remained of the upper floor and watched the lanterns.

They were still appearing — fewer now than on the great night, when the whole river quarter had glowed like a fleet at anchor, but they persisted, one here, one there, in windows where someone had decided the gesture was worth a candle. The city had taken up the habit the way a man takes up a limp: reluctantly, permanently. Somewhere out there, delegates were drafting the inquiry's terms. Somewhere out there, Odile's mother was either alive in a military prison awaiting the general's fall to reach her, or she was not, and no clause in any treaty could tell us which. We had sent inquiries through the widow's network, through the booksellers, through a sympathetic clerk Corvin swore was trustworthy. So far: nothing. The machinery of mercy moves slower than the machinery of war. Everyone had known that. Knowing it didn't help.

Odile stood between Sef's knees at the broken window, counting lanterns in a voice that grew sleepier by the number. At forty-one she lost the thread and leaned back against him, and he wrapped his arms around her and kept counting silently, his lips moving, long past the point where anyone was keeping score.

"She should have a surname," Corvin said suddenly. "For the register. If she stays with us — and she stays with us, I want that minuted — the constabulary will want papers eventually. Papers want names."

"Vane," Sef said immediately, not looking up from the sleeping child.

"She's not a Vane," I said.

"No," Sef agreed. "Neither are you, really. You're a Mirabel. The Vanes were your father and his press and that ridiculous poster. You could be anything." He shrugged. "So could she."

I looked at the child asleep against his chest, this girl who had arrived at our door as a note and a locket and a pair of frozen hands, who had learned her letters at our stone, whose face I had refused to spend even when spending it might have bought us everything — whose face, thank God, the crowd had swallowed into anonymity on the night of lanterns, one face among thousands, unremarkable, safe. Her mother's face was in my memory and nowhere else now; I had burned the portrait and kept the testimony, and I would make that trade again in every life I was offered.

"We'll ask her," I said. "When she's older, if she wants another name, or her mother's, or her own invention — it's hers. Until then—" I hesitated, and found the hesitation itself was the answer. "Until then she's Odile. Of the household. That's a name. It's the truest one any of us has."

Bram, from his chair by the cold stove — we hadn't the fuel to light it, and he sat by it anyway, on principle — grunted the grunt that in the old grammar of the shop meant *motion carries.*

Below us the cellar held its cargo of rescued letters. Above us the roof let in the stars, which seemed, all things considered, a fair exchange. The armistice was signed. The general was falling. Bram was home, changed but alive, his hand stiff and his tongue unchanged. We had lost the building and kept the meaning, which is, I suppose, the only trade this profession ever really offers anyone.

I went downstairs before bed and opened one drawer of type, just one, and stood there in the dark with my hand resting on the cool brass edge. Tomorrow — or the day after, or whenever the snow gave out and spring got around to arriving, which in Vellmar is a matter for negotiation — we would need a name for whatever we rebuilt. A sign. A line of type to set above a door.

I found myself thinking about ampersands.

*Vane & Sons* had been a lie for thirty years; there had never been sons, only me, uncounted. Whatever came next would have to be honest from the very first letter. I stood in the dark among the salvaged alphabet and began, helplessly, to compose.
