## 1. The Weight of Unfinished Lines

The lamp had to be trimmed before anything else. That was the rule.

Oscar Almeida had learned it from Master Corvino in his first week as an apprentice, when he was fourteen and all elbows and ink-stained cuffs: *A cartographer who works by bad light makes maps that lie, boy. The coastlines go soft where the shadows go soft. Trim the wick. Respect the line.* Corvino had said it while eating a plum, which rather undercut the gravity, but the lesson stuck. Seven years later — three years after the master's death — Oscar still trimmed the wick first, every morning, before he so much as looked at a plate.

He trimmed it now, in the grey hour before dawn, and the lamplight steadied into a clean gold circle on the workbench, and the workshop assembled itself out of the dark around him: the map press with its great iron screw, the drying racks hung with proofs like strange laundry, the drawers of pens graded by nib width and labeled in Corvino's cramped hand — *fine, finer, do not touch unless you are me.* Oscar had never touched that last drawer. Not once. It felt less like disobedience than like conversation; somewhere in the arrangement of those pens there was still a voice, and opening the drawer carelessly would be like interrupting.

He put the kettle on the spirit stove. He ground his ink — lampblack and gum arabic, a little clove oil because Corvino had sworn by it and because the smell made the workshop feel inhabited. And then, at last, he sat down at the bench and took up Plate Forty-Seven, the harbor approaches of Cape Serrat, and began to ink the soundings.

This was the work. This had been the work for seven years, and would be, God willing and the Crown permitting, for perhaps two months more. *The General Atlas of the Southern Reaches* — forty-eight plates, every coastline from Vessa to the Salt Marches, drawn from surveys Corvino had spent thirty years collecting and Oscar had spent seven years fair-copying into copper. When it was done, it would be the finest atlas produced in the empire in a generation. When it was done, Oscar would have no idea what to do with himself.

But not yet. There were still lines to finish, and finishing them was the only prayer he knew how to say.

* * *

The trouble with grief, Oscar had discovered, was that it did not keep hours. It arrived whenever it liked — mid-sounding, mid-meal, mid-laugh at some joke only he could hear — and it arrived most reliably in the quiet places between tasks. So he had learned to leave very few quiet places. His days were built like a seawall against the tide: trim the lamp, grind the ink, kettle, plate, walk to Mirela's print shop with the day's proofs, walk back, supper, plate again until the candle guttered, bed. Every hour claimed. Every silence filled before it could fill itself with the memory of a heavy man laughing at his own plum.

It worked. Mostly. He had gotten very good at it, the way one gets good at anything through seven years of practice, and if there was something faintly ridiculous about a grown man scheduling his sorrow the way a harbormaster schedules tides — well. Ridiculous things kept a person alive. He'd seen enough of the alternative.

There were other silences too, though. Those were different. Those he kept on purpose.

The locked drawer, for instance. Bottom left of the desk, iron-bound, key on a cord around his neck under his shirt where it lay warm against his sternum all day like a second heartbeat. Corvino had locked it the night he died — or rather, Oscar had found it locked afterward, among the wreckage of the workshop, and had never opened it. He told himself this was respect. Some nights he suspected it was fear. Either way, the drawer stayed shut, and whatever the master had wanted hidden stayed hidden, and that silence, Oscar was fairly sure, kept someone safe. Possibly himself.

Then there were the unasked questions. These he kept the way other men kept debts — a ledger, always open, never settled. *Who killed you? Why did you burn your letters the week before? What were you doing in the drowned quarter at midnight, Master, with your good coat on and your survey case empty? Who is the woman who leaves bread on the step and takes nothing?* Seven years of questions, each one noted and set aside, because asking questions in Vessa, in these last years of the empire, was how people stopped being seen. The chandler's boy had asked questions. The chandler's boy was now a name spoken quietly in taverns, the way one speaks of weather that has already sunk ships.

So Oscar did not ask. He inked his coastlines. He kept his silences like lamps in windows — small, steady, and pointed away from home.

* * *

By midmorning the fog off the harbor had burned off, and the sounds of Vessa came up through the shutters: gulls arguing over something dead, the fish-bells, a barrel rolling somewhere with the particular rhythm of a barrel being rolled badly. Oscar worked through it all, pen whispering across the copper, laying in the depth-numbers along the Serrat channel. Six fathoms. Four and a half. Three — careful here, the sandbar shifted with the storms, and a wrong number on this plate could drown honest sailors as surely as a reef.

That was the thing about maps nobody appreciated, he thought, dipping the pen. A map was a promise. Every line said *this is true, trust me, steer by me.* Corvino used to say an atlas was just a book of promises bound in leather, and that was why he took eleven years over plates other men rushed out in eleven weeks. *Anyone can draw a coast, boy. The work is making certain the drawing deserves to be believed.*

Oscar missed him the way one misses a wall one has been leaning against. Not dramatically. Just — constantly, in the small ways. He still caught himself turning to say something and finding only the empty chair, its cushion worn into the shape of a man who had sat like a sack of grain and refused all correction. He still made tea for two some mornings before remembering, and drank both cups rather than waste them, which Mirela said was either devotion or gluttony and she'd decided it was devotion because it was kinder.

Mirela. He should take her the proofs by noon. She'd want to see the Serrat plate — she always wanted to see everything, Mirela, with her quick eyes and quicker tongue, standing amid her printing frames like a general among siege engines. *Show me the numbers, Oscar. Show me the margins.* She was particular about margins in a way that had always struck him as slightly excessive, even for a printer.

He set the plate down, flexed his cramping fingers, and allowed himself the morning's one indulgence: standing at the window with the shutter cracked, watching the harbor.

Vessa in the last year of the century wore its decay the way an old admiral wears his dress uniform — threadbare, but buttoned with pride. The empire's banners still flew from the customs house, though the dye had faded to the pink of an old scar. The grain fleet came thinner every year. Soldiers drilled in the market square to audiences of pigeons. And everywhere, quietly, the Crown's men watched: at the gates, at the quays, outside print shops especially, because paper, the empire had finally understood, was more dangerous than powder. Powder killed a street. Paper killed a certainty.

A hundred years ending. People spoke of it in the taverns with a strange doubled feeling — celebration and dread in the same breath, like a wedding held during a storm. The new century would come whether the empire was ready or not. Whether anyone was ready or not.

Oscar found, watching the gray water, that he was counting. Not the days to the atlas's completion — he always counted those, greedily, hungrily — but something else. The turn of the year sat out there in the future like a sandbar under dark water, and he could not have said why the thought of it made his hands cold. Nothing would change. Numbers didn't govern anything; he was a cartographer, he knew better than most that lines on a page were agreements, not laws. And yet.

And yet the master had died in the last month of a decade, and something in Oscar's bones insisted the arithmetic meant something.

He closed the shutter. Back to work. Silence filled, tide held back.

* * *

The afternoon went to the marginalia, which was the part of the work he loved best and trusted least. Corvino's margins were a country unto themselves — compass roses with faces drawn in them, sea-serpents chasing each other around the scale bars, tiny jokes in Latin that were usually rude. Oscar had spent years learning their grammar. On Plate Twelve, the serpent wore Corvino's own hat. On Plate Thirty-One, the compass rose was weeping, and Oscar had never had the heart to ask why, and now never could, and had added it to the ledger of unasked questions, where it glowed like a coal.

Today's task was simpler: finish the border ornaments on Serrat, check the title cartouche, verify the scale bar against the field notes. Routine. Ritual. Pen, ink, breath, line.

He was halfway through the cartouche flourishes when he noticed it — a mark in the margin of the proof, low in the corner where the border met the neatline. Small. Deliberate. Not Corvino's style at all.

A circle, crossed by a single vertical stroke. Like a lantern seen edge-on. Or a door.

Oscar sat very still.

He knew this proof. He had fair-copied this plate himself, four years ago, from Corvino's original drawings, and there had been no such mark then. Which meant it had been added after — added to the proof, in this workshop, by hands he knew or hands he didn't, sometime in the years since. Sometime since the murder.

He turned the proof toward the lamp. The ink was brownish, old. Someone had stood exactly where he stood, bent exactly over this exact corner, and pressed a small secret into the margin of a map of harbor approaches — a map of ways in and ways out.

His heart was doing something unprofessional.

*Don't ask,* said seven years of discipline. *Don't look. Fill the silence. Keep the lamp trimmed.*

But the mark sat there, patient as a lit window, and Oscar Almeida — who had kept every silence, honored every locked drawer, swallowed every question whole for seven long years — heard himself think, for the very first time, a thought shaped like a key:

*I wonder what else is hiding in my margins.*

Outside, the light was failing early, the way it did in the last weeks of the year, and far off across the rooftops the harbor bell rang the hour — five strokes, thin and clear, like something beginning to count down.
