## 6. The Harbor of Small Betrayals

Mirela Voss had three rules for her print shop, and she recited them every morning the way other women recited prayers: *Never print what you can hand-copy. Never copy what you can memorize. Never memorize anything worth hanging for.*

She broke all three before breakfast most days.

The morning fog off the harbor had that particular gray thickness that made Vessa look like a charcoal sketch of itself, and Mirela stood in her doorway watching a gull argue with a fishmonger over something unspeakable, when she saw Oscar Almeida crossing the square with his collar up and his shoulders doing that thing they did — that careful, apologetic hunch, as though he were sorry to take up space in the world and would fold himself away shortly if it was inconvenient.

He was carrying a wrapped parcel under his arm, and he was carrying it wrong.

That was the thing about Oscar Almeida that had irritated Mirela for seven years: he could not lie with his body to save his life, which was fortunate for him, because he lied with his face almost as badly. A man carrying paper carried it flat against his ribs, protected, angled from the rain. A man carrying bread slung it. Oscar was carrying that parcel like a relic — close, high, tilted so the rain couldn't find a seam — and he was walking toward her shop, which meant one of two things. Either he needed ink, or he needed absolution.

She sold both, though only one honestly.

"Master Almeida." She stepped back to let him in out of the wet. "You're up early. The dead don't set their deadlines, I thought."

"The dead set the only deadlines that matter," Oscar said, and then winced at himself, because it was the kind of thing you said when you'd been alone too long with your grief and it had started talking back. "Good morning, Mistress Voss. I need — " He stopped. His eyes had already gone past her shoulder to the press, the way they always did, cataloguing her type cases like a man checking another man's arithmetic.

"Ink," Mirela supplied. "You always need ink. You go through it like a drunkard goes through excuses."

"Lamp black, if you have it. And the good gum arabic. And — " he hesitated, and here it came, the second thing, the real errand wearing the first one's coat — "and I wondered if you still had use for an apprentice's hands. Just for a few days. Proofing work. Nothing finished."

Mirela let the silence sit between them long enough to grow uncomfortable. It was one of her few talents, letting silence do the heavy lifting. In the Lantern, they called her the patient one, which she knew was code for *the old one*, but patience had kept her alive through two purges and one marriage, and she was fond of it accordingly.

"Proofing work," she said. "For the atlas."

Oscar's face did the thing. The terrible, honest thing. A flicker of alarm chased by a determination to appear unalarmed, arriving roughly half a heartbeat too late, like a guard who hears the bell after the thieves are over the wall.

"What atlas?" he said.

"Oh, forgive me." Mirela pressed a hand to her breast in mock contrition. "I must have dreamed seven years of Corvino coming in here every month, ordering paper by the ream, complaining about the cost of vellum, showing me proofs I never asked to see because the man couldn't hold a secret any better than you can hold your shoulders straight. I dreamed the whole atlas. It's a condition. The physician says I should eat more fish."

"Mistress Voss — "

"Mirela."

"Mirela." He said her name carefully, the way you'd step onto ice you suspected of being thin. "I don't know what you're suggesting."

"I'm not suggesting anything." She turned toward the back room, calling over her shoulder. "Lamp black I have. Gum arabic I have. Sit down, you're dripping on my floor, and if you ruin my floor I'll add it to your bill along with the insult."

* * *

She made him tea because that was what you did with frightened men — you gave their hands something hot to hold so they couldn't wring them. Oscar sat at her worktable among the trays of sorted type, enormous and careful, a big man trying to be furniture, and Mirela studied him while pretending to study her ledger.

Seven years since Corvino died. Seven years since the Lantern had lost its cartographer, its mapmaker, its quiet genius who used to come into this shop and talk about coastlines the way other men talked about lovers. Seven years, and the network had heard nothing from the workshop except through Mirela's own small reports: *the apprentice keeps the shop open. The apprentice buys ink. The apprentice does not ask questions.*

*Does not ask questions.* She had written that line so many times it had worn smooth, and somewhere in the last year it had stopped sounding like reassurance and started sounding like a warning. A man who didn't ask questions for seven years was either a saint or a grave, and Oscar Almeida had the complexion of neither.

"You know what I did before I owned this shop?" Mirela said, pouring.

"You were a printer."

"I was a printer's wife. Different trade entirely. My husband printed pamphlets. Honest ones, mostly — grain prices, tide tables, the occasional sermon nobody read. And then one autumn a man came in with a manuscript about the Crown's tax farms in the eastern provinces, and my husband read it and went white and said, *we cannot print this, we will all hang,* and I said, *you're right, give it to me, I'll set the type myself.*" She handed him the cup. "He wept. Big man, my Tomas. Wept like a child. And then he helped me lock the door and we printed forty copies in one night, and that is how I learned that courage is mostly just fear that has decided to be useful."

Oscar looked into his tea. "Is there a point to this story?"

"There's always a point. Points are cheap; stories are how you smuggle them." She sat across from him. "The point is that I have spent thirty years learning to recognize a man carrying something dangerous, and you walked into my shop this morning carrying it wrong."

"I'm carrying ink."

"You'll carry ink home in a sack, like always, slopping it on your boots. That parcel under your coat is proofs. Fresh proofs. Heavy stock, the good rag paper, and there are enough sheets in there to make the shape of folio pages, and you asked me for proofing help, which means the atlas is nearly done." She leaned forward. "And I am going to ask you a question now, Oscar Almeida, and I want you to understand that everything depends on how you answer it, though not in the way you fear."

His knuckles had gone pale around the cup. "Ask it."

"Who is the atlas *for?*"

* * *

Here is what Mirela knew, and what she could tell no one, and what had been sitting in her chest for seven years like a swallowed stone:

Corvino had been the Lantern's mapmaker. Not a sympathizer, not a funder — the mapmaker. Every safe route, every hidden landing, every cellar and culvert and smuggler's stair from Vessa to the border passes, drawn in the master's beautiful, disciplined hand and distributed in pieces so no single courier ever carried the whole. When Corvino drowned in the flooded quarter — officially an accident, unofficially the Crown's work, though the network had never proven it — the Lantern lost not just a man but its memory of its own geography. Routes had gone dark. Contacts had gone silent, some fled, some worse. For seven years the Lantern had been a body feeling along walls in the dark, waiting for word of the atlas, and the word had never come, because the only man who knew where it was — the apprentice — reported nothing, asked nothing, and gave nothing.

The council had argued about him for years. *Leave him be, he's grieving. Watch him, he's hiding something. Take the workshop, he's a liability.* Mirela had argued for patience, because she remembered Corvino's face the last time he'd sat where Oscar sat now, drinking tea, saying: *My boy isn't ready. Not because he lacks the skill. Because he hasn't yet learned that a secret kept out of fear is a debt, and a secret kept out of love is a gift, and the Lantern runs on gifts.* She had thought, at the time, that Corvino was describing himself. She had begun to suspect he'd been describing a test.

And now here was the apprentice, seven years late, with finished or nearly finished proofs under his coat, asking to borrow her hands without asking her anything at all.

"Well?" she said, when the silence had done its work. "Who is it for?"

"It's a commercial atlas," Oscar said. "Coastal charts, trade routes, city plans. For booksellers. For — collectors. It's for whoever buys it."

"That's who it's *sold* to. Who is it *for?*"

"Mistress Voss — Mirela — I don't know what you want me to say."

"I want you to say the true thing, whatever it is. Lie to the Crown, lie to the harbor master, lie to your own reflection if it helps you sleep. But not to me, and not today, because today I am asking as more than a woman who sells ink." She kept her voice gentle, which took effort, because gentleness had never come naturally to her; her natural register was the one she used on tardy apprentices and dishonest wool merchants. "There are people — good people, careful people — who believe that atlas contains things that could save lives or end them. They've believed it for seven years. They've waited politely for seven years. And polite, Oscar, is running out."

Something moved behind his eyes — not guilt exactly, but its cousin, the one that looks like a man doing arithmetic he hates.

"If there were such people," Oscar said slowly, "I'd tell them what I'm telling you. The atlas is unfinished. There are plates missing. There are — gaps. Until it's complete, it's worthless. Worse than worthless — it's wrong. A wrong map kills faster than no map at all." He lifted his chin, and for one moment the apologetic hunch straightened and she glimpsed the man Corvino must have seen underneath. "So whoever these careful people are, they can wait a little longer. Or they can come take it from me. But I won't hand over something broken and call it a gift."

Mirela sat back.

It was, infuriatingly, a good answer. It was the answer of a craftsman, and it might even have been the truth, and it told her nothing whatsoever about whether the man understood what he was sitting on. That was the cruelty of it — the atlas might be the Lantern's salvation, and its keeper might be a loyalist, a coward, a fool, or simply a grief-stricken apprentice who genuinely believed he was finishing a book about coastlines. She could not tell. Seven years of reports and she could not *tell*, and the loneliness of that settled over her like the fog outside: belonging to a network whose trust had to be rationed like wartime bread, loving people you were forbidden to know, serving a cause that required you to suspect everyone, including — especially — the ones you liked.

She liked Oscar Almeida. That was the worst part. She liked his terrible lying face and his careful manners and the way he'd once spent an entire afternoon helping her chase a runaway proof sheet down the street without asking why a print-shop owner cared so much about one sheet of paper. And she had just threatened him, more or less, in her own shop, over tea she'd sweetened herself.

"Unfinished," she said. "Gaps. Missing plates."

"Yes."

"And when will it be finished?"

"When it's finished." He almost smiled. "You of all people should respect that. You don't sell half-set type."

"No. I burn it." She rose, went to the shelf, and began wrapping his order — lamp black, gum arabic, a tin of the good varnish she pretended not to keep for special customers. Her hands worked while her mind ran ahead, the way hands do when they've been trained by necessity. If the atlas was unfinished, then the missing piece mattered more than the whole, and if the missing piece was what she feared it was — the master plate, the rendezvous chart, the heart of the Lantern that Corvino had died rather than surrender — then every day it sat in that workshop was a day it sat within reach of the Crown. And the Crown, she'd heard, had sent someone. A new inquisitor. Patient, they said. Courteous. The worst kind.

"Oscar," she said, tying the parcel. "A piece of advice, free of charge, worth more than the ink."

He looked up.

"When a man asks you for nothing and watches you anyway — when strangers start finding reasons to buy maps they don't need — when the chandler next to your shop suddenly has new locks and new men who don't smell of tallow —" she put the parcel in his arms, deliberately, so he had to carry it right, against his ribs, like a man protecting something — "then the questions you haven't been asking for seven years will start asking themselves. And you'll wish you'd practiced your answers on a friend first."

"I don't have friends," Oscar said. It wasn't self-pity. It was inventory.

"No," Mirela agreed. "Not yet. That's the trouble with our trade. Everyone you'd want beside you is someone you're obliged to doubt." She opened the door for him. The fog had thickened; the gull had won its argument; somewhere across the square a church bell counted the hour with the smugness of institutions. "Come Thursday. Bring the proofs if you like. I'll show you how to proof properly — you've been doing it wrong for years, I can tell just by looking at your sleeves."

"My sleeves?"

"Ink on the heel of the hand, never the palm. Corvino never taught you that. He was saving it." She smiled, and found, to her surprise, that the smile was real. "Men like him always save one lesson too many. Go home, Oscar. Finish your book. And when the questions start asking themselves —"

"I'll come find you."

"*I'll* come find *you*," Mirela corrected. "You'd get lost. You're a mapmaker's apprentice with no sense of direction whatsoever. It's the most reassuring thing about you."

He laughed — short, startled, rusty, like a hinge remembering its purpose — and went out into the fog, carrying the parcel correctly now, and Mirela watched him until the gray took him, and then she closed the door and stood with her hand flat against the wood.

Well, she thought. Either I just recruited him or I just terrified him, and with that boy the two look identical.

She went back to her ledger and, beneath the day's orders, wrote the line she'd written a hundred times before: *The apprentice keeps the shop open. The apprentice buys ink.* Then, after a moment, she added a new one, and hated how much her hand wanted to shake:

*The atlas exists. Unfinished. He knows more than he says. God help us, so do I.*

Outside, the fog pressed at the windows, and down at the harbor a ship's bell rang twice — the signal she'd been taught twenty years ago and had never once needed until now, the signal that said: *a stranger has arrived asking old questions.* Mirela blew out her lamp, sat in the dark of her shop among her obedient letters, and began, very quietly, to count the hours until Thursday.
