## 9. Rell's Gallery of the Broken

Inquisitor Rell believed that a room could be tuned, the way a lute is tuned, and that most of his colleagues played their instruments badly.

The room he had been given in the Vessa garrison was a former counting-house on the second floor, with a good window that looked out on the harbor — not for the view, he explained to the nervous garrison clerk who had shown him up, but because people spoke more honestly when they could see water. Something about water made a man aware of the size of the world and the smallness of his own secrets. Rell had arranged the furniture himself, over the clerk's mild objections: two chairs, a small table between them, a decanter of wine, two glasses. No desk. Desks were barriers, and barriers invited performances. A lamp with a warm shade, because cold light made people squint, and squinting made faces lie.

The prisoner would arrive within the hour. Rell had read his file three times, and then, out of habit, a fourth time, looking not for facts but for the shape of the man behind them.

His name was Dario Fell, aged thirty-one, a courier of the smuggling network the Crown's records called the Lantern, though Rell had never cared for the name — names given by enemies were always a little too flattering, a little too poetic, as though the bureaucracy needed its villains to come with atmosphere attached. Fell had been taken at the toll bridge outside the city with a satchel of tallow candles, each candle hollowed and packed with rolled papers. The papers were shipping manifests, mostly dull, mostly innocent, which was itself interesting: a courier carrying mostly-innocent papers was a courier whose real cargo was elsewhere. In his head, most likely. Or in his habits. Or in the particular way he said good morning to a particular fishwife.

Fell had said nothing for two days. This was standard, and Rell did not mind. Two days of silence told him the man had been trained, and trained men had trainers, and trainers could be mapped.

Rell poured himself a glass of the wine — a Vessan white, unremarkable but honest — and sat in the chair facing the door, and waited, and was content. People imagined that patience was a virtue. Rell had long ago stopped thinking of it as a virtue. It was simply the only reliable form of power. Empires rose on armies and fell on ledgers, but in between, in the long middle where actual history happened, power was the patience to sit in a warm room with a decanter of wine while other men broke themselves against their own fear. He had never in nineteen years of service raised his voice in an interrogation. He had never needed to. Voices raised were voices spent.

The knock came at the appointed hour, because Rell had requested the appointed hour, because a prisoner who knew when you were coming had time to compose himself, and a composed man was a man performing, and a performance, watched long enough, always dropped a stitch.

"Come in," Rell said, though the door was already opening.

* * *

They brought Fell in with his wrists bound in front of him, which Rell corrected immediately.

"Untie him, please. If he runs, the door is locked and Sergeant Brakk is outside it, and Sergeant Brakk has been hoping all week for something to do." He said it lightly, and the guards laughed the way men laugh when they are not sure whether the joke includes them, and the ropes came off.

Fell stood in the middle of the room and looked at the wine and the window and the two chairs, and Rell watched him do the arithmetic every trained man did — *this is not the room I was promised* — and then watched him decide, wrongly, that the arithmetic was a trick.

"Sit," Rell said, and poured the second glass, and slid it across. "It's not drugged. If I wanted to drug you I'd have done it while you were unconscious after the arrest, which would have been cheaper and saved me the price of the wine. It's a Vessan white. I'm told it's local. I confess I can't tell one white from another; I once drank vinegar for a week before someone corrected me. So if it's terrible, the fault is mine and not the vintage's."

Fell did not sit. He looked down at Rell — the man was a head taller, broad through the shoulders, with the sunburned neck of someone who had worked decks or roads — and said, "I know what you are."

"Of course you do. I'm the Inquisitor Rell. You've been told stories about me, I expect. I'm told the stories are colorful." He smiled, and it was a genuine smile, which unsettled people more than any performance of menace. "Sit down, Master Fell. I'd like to talk about candles."

Something flickered across the courier's face — not fear exactly, but the recognition of fear arriving from an unexpected direction.

"Candles," Fell said.

"You were carrying forty of them. Tallow, cheap wicks, the kind a chandler sells by the dozen to kitchens and poor households. Except that cheap tallow candles are never uniform, and yours were — same weight, same hollow, same rolling of the papers inside, to the finger's width. Which means they weren't made by a chandler. They were made by a workshop. A careful one." Rell turned his glass a quarter-turn on the table, idly, the way a man turns a thought. "I've spent a very dull week with those candles, Master Fell. I've learned that whoever makes them uses a mold from the Salt Quarter, because of a flaw in the base — a little seam, like a scar. I've learned the papers inside are cut from a particular stock that three stationers in this city sell, and only one of them sells it in the quantities involved. I've learned that your manifests are genuine, which means someone with access to genuine manifests is helping you, and that person is more valuable than you are, though I'm sure that's no comfort."

Fell sat down. Not because he had decided to — Rell could see the moment the chair simply became more sensible than standing, the body overruling the training. He took the wine but did not drink it. He held it the way a man holds a shield.

"You've had a dull week," Fell said. "Sounds like you should find better entertainment."

"I find this entertaining." Rell leaned back. "Tell me about the fishwife."

"I don't know any fishwife."

"You do. Or you knew one, or you were told to know one. On the morning of your arrest you crossed the toll bridge at the second bell, which is late — the couriers we've caught before all cross at first bell, when the guards change and the ledger is being written and no one looks up. You crossed at second bell. Which means you were told to cross late, which means the schedule changed, which means someone changed it, and the only reason to change a courier's crossing is if the courier himself is being watched." Rell let that sit for a moment, and watched it work. "You were told to be late, Master Fell, by someone who already suspected you might be taken. Someone was protecting the network from you. I'd like to know what you did to make them suspicious. In my experience it's almost always something small. A question asked too directly. A kindness out of season."

For the first time, Fell looked afraid — not of Rell, but of the sentence Rell had just described, the sentence that had already been passed on him somewhere in the city's dark, by his own people, before Rell had ever laid eyes on him.

"They didn't trust me," Fell said quietly. It was not a question.

"They were right not to. You're sitting in my room." Rell refilled the man's untouched glass, though it needed nothing, because the gesture itself was the point. "Here is what I think, and you may correct me. I think you were recruited young. I think there's a debt involved — a family debt, or a favor owed, the kind of thing the Lantern is very good at collecting, because it recruits with gratitude, which is cheaper than coin and holds better. I think you've been a courier for four years, maybe five. I think you're good at it. And I think that about a month ago you began to be afraid, not of me — you'd never seen me — but of the work itself. Because the work has been getting heavier. Am I right?"

Fell said nothing, but his thumb moved along the stem of the glass, once, a small unconscious stroke, and Rell had his answer, and noted it, and did not press.

This was the whole art, as Rell practiced it. Not to ask the question that mattered. To ask the question beside the question, and let the prisoner's own mind carry him the rest of the way, because a man will defend a secret to the death but he cannot defend himself from his own conclusions. Rell had once spent eleven days with a fortress quartermaster and never once asked about the missing arms shipment; they had talked about cheese, at length, and the quartermaster had wept on the ninth day, and by the eleventh he had given Rell the shipment, the buyer, and a recipe for cheese that Rell had, in a moment of weakness, actually tried.

* * *

They talked for three hours. About candles, mostly. About tallow and wicks and the economics of the Salt Quarter mold-makers. Fell was careful, and Rell was patient, and the wine got drunk, and a second decanter was sent for, and somewhere in the second hour Fell began to forget, in small increments, that he was performing. That was the window. It always opened. Not wide — a hand's width, for a few minutes — but Rell had built a career in hand's-widths.

It opened on the subject of children.

Not because Rell had steered there. He had learned never to steer toward children; the mind guards that door too fiercely, and a guarded door tells the guard. It opened because Fell, relaxing, talking about the chandlers of the Salt Quarter, mentioned — in passing, in a list, a man listing ordinary things — that the mold-shop's owner had a grandson who ran errands, "a sharp little lad, good with his feet, knows the lanes better than the watch does," and then stopped, and went pale, because he heard what he had said.

Rell did not pounce. Pouncing was for amateurs and playwrights. He simply continued, in the same warm murmur, about mold-makers and their families, and let the silence do its work, and two minutes later Fell was talking again, and the door had closed, and Rell knew what he knew.

There was a child in the network. Not a child of the network — the network had children the way any quarter of any city had children, running errands, carrying nothing, knowing nothing. A child *of* the network. A child being kept, or moved, or protected. A child whose existence was worth changing a courier's schedule over.

Rell had a file, three inches thick, on the Lantern, assembled over a decade by men less patient than himself, and in all those pages there were ships and warehouses and codes and forty-one dead agents and two turned ones, and there was no child. Which meant the child was new. Which meant the child mattered enough, and recently enough, to have rearranged the habits of a network that had not rearranged its habits in ten years.

"Tell me about the boy," Rell said, much later, when the light through the window had gone amber and Fell's carefulness had worn down to a fine, tired edge.

"What boy?"

"The sharp little lad. Good with his feet." Rell smiled and shook his head, self-deprecating. "No. Forgive me. I'm being clumsy, and I dislike being clumsy. I don't mean the mold-maker's grandson. I mean the boy the network is carrying. The one whose existence was worth your life, Master Fell — because that's what the changed schedule was. Someone decided you were worth less than his safety. I'd like to know what a child could possibly be, that he's worth more than a trained courier."

Fell's face did something complicated. Rell watched it the way a cartographer watches a coastline — not for any single feature, but for the shape of the whole.

"I don't know any boy," Fell said.

"Of course." Rell nodded, agreeably. "You don't know any boy. But you know *of* one, and here's how I know: when I said *the network is carrying him*, you looked at my left hand. People look at the thing they're afraid of, and you're afraid I already know more than I do. Which tells me the boy is real, and tells me something better — it tells me you don't know where he is." He leaned forward, and for the first time in three hours his voice lost a little of its warmth, not into cruelty but into something plainer, almost kind. "Master Fell. You think your people suspect you, and you're right, and that means that when this is over — however it's over — no one from the Lantern is coming to help you. I am the only person in this city who has any use for you at all. I'm not threatening you. I'm describing your situation. It's a lonely one. I've sat where you're sitting, in a manner of speaking, and I know the shape of it."

Fell looked at the window. The harbor was going gold and gray, the fishing boats coming in, the gulls doing their idiot arithmetic over the masts. When he spoke, his voice was rough.

"He's about eight," Fell said. "Maybe younger. Small for his age, I think — I never saw him, I only heard. Dark hair. There was a mark — they said he carried a mark, under his coat, on the inside. The Lantern's mark." He swallowed. "I heard one of the older ones say they'd cut it out of his coat so no one would see it, and someone else said no, leave it, it's safer where it is, because anyone who finds it will know what he is and be afraid to touch him." Fell's hands had gone still on the table. "They said he doesn't talk right. That he talks in — pieces. Routes and names and things. Like a ledger that got up and walked. And they said—" He stopped.

"Go on," Rell said, very gently.

"They said he's the only one left who knows all the true names. Every agent. Every route. In his head, and nowhere else. That the network burned everything else, and what's left—" Fell looked up, and there was something in his face that Rell recognized, because he had seen it before, in other rooms, in other years, in men who had finally understood the size of the thing they had been carrying a piece of. "What's left is him."

Rell sat back. He did not let his face change, because faces were instruments too, and this was no moment to play a wrong note. But inside the warm, patient machinery of his mind, something turned over with a sound like a lock.

A ledger that walked. Every true name. Eight years old, dark-haired, small, marked under the coat, somewhere in a city of ninety thousand people, in the keeping of a network that had just demonstrated it would spend a trained courier to keep the boy's schedule quiet.

"Thank you," Rell said, and meant it, and poured the last of the wine.

"You're not going to—" Fell began, and stopped, because he did not know how to finish the sentence, and neither, Rell reflected, did he. The law had its answer. Rell had long ago stopped confusing the law's answer with justice, and justice with mercy, and had found that the three were usually strangers to one another.

"You'll be transferred tomorrow," Rell said. "To the provincial facility at Corvane. It's not a bad place, as such places go. There's a garden. Write to me, if you like. I answer my letters."

Fell was taken out. Rell remained at the window a long while, watching the boats, doing his own arithmetic.

* * *

That night, in the rooms the Crown had rented him on Lanternmakers' Row — the Crown had a sense of humor, or its clerks did — Rell sat at his desk with a single lamp and a fresh sheet of paper, and wrote, in his small exact hand, the beginning of a new file.

*Subject: male child, approx. 7–9 years. Dark hair, small stature. Carries the network's mark, concealed. Speech: fragmented; recites routes, names, harbor data. Assessment: living register of Lantern personnel. Status: location unknown. Priority: absolute.*

He sat back and looked at the words, and then at the window, beyond which the street lamps of Vessa were being lit one by one, a slow constellation, each flame a small defiance of the dark.

A decade of patience, he thought, and the network's whole secret turns out to be a child who cannot even say his own name. There was a poetry in it that Rell distrusted. Empires did not fall to armies; they fell to ledgers. And somewhere out in that spreading constellation of lamps, a ledger with a boy's face was walking through the dark, and every hour he remained unfound was an hour the Crown's whole case against the Lantern hung by a thread no thicker than a child's memory.

Rell dipped his pen and added a final line to the file, and underlined it once.

*He will be moved soon. Networks protect what they cannot replace. Find what they cannot replace, and you have found everything.*

He blotted the page, and blew out the lamp, and in the dark he began, patiently, to make a list of every print shop, mapmaker, and chandler in the Salt Quarter — because a ledger that walked would need someone to walk beside it, and someone to hide it, and hiding, in Vessa, was a trade, and trades had addresses.

By the third bell he had forty-one names. He would begin, courteously, at the first one in the morning.
