## 4. The Census Taker's Second Visit

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that a census taker learns in his first winter, and it has nothing to do with numbers. It is the arithmetic of doors. Some doors open before you have finished knocking — those belong to people who are lonely, or frightened of being thought poor, or both. Some doors never open at all; you count the house from the chimney smoke and move on. And some doors open halfway, just wide enough for a face, and you learn to read that face like a ledger entry: solvent, insolvent, hostile, grieving.

The Marchetti door had opened fully, both times, and I had not yet decided what that meant.

I told myself, walking up the Kirchgasse on the second Tuesday of February, that this was simply diligence. The magistrate's office had flagged four households in the print district for "verification" — a word that in Hahn's office meant exactly what a noose means when someone calls it a rope. Verification. As though a family were a sum that might not add up, and the state merely wished to carry the one.

The truth was less administrative. The truth was that I had not slept well since my first visit, and that a man who does not sleep well begins to believe that if he can only look at the thing troubling him once more, squarely, it will resolve itself into something ordinary.

It did not.

* * *

Let me explain what I knew about Lena Marchetti, because everything else follows from it, the way water follows the slope of the land.

Twelve years ago, before the borders closed and the words *reform* and *sedition* were welded together by decree, I was a clerk in the customs house and she was the most inconvenient woman in Velden. Inconvenient because she was right about things. She ran a reading circle out of the back of the print shop — six people, then nine, then fourteen, until the magistrate's office began to take an interest in attendance figures the way magistrates do. Tomas Brandt came to speak twice. I came to carry chairs, which is what young men did in those days instead of admitting they had opinions.

The last time I saw her alive was on the steps of the prison, eleven years ago this spring. She had been held three weeks for questioning — long enough for her hands to go cold, not long enough for them to break anything in her that mattered — and when they released her without charge, because there was nothing to charge, she stood on those steps in the rain and looked at me as though I were the last honest figure in a falsified column.

"Ivo," she said. "You count things. That's your gift and your curse. So count this." She took my wrist. Her fingers were freezing. "One day they will come for the people around Brandt. Not him first — he's too useful to them as a monster. They'll come for the printers, the booksellers, the ones with ink under their fingernails. When that day comes, I want you to remember every name you ever heard in my parlor, and lose them. Lose them completely. Can a man who counts for a living learn to miscount?"

I said something dutiful and stupid, the sort of thing clerks say. She smiled — she had a smile like a door opening all the way — and said, "Good. Then you're one of us after all."

She died two winters later. Fever, the doctor wrote. I have always suspected the fever had help, in the form of grief and poverty and a city that had stopped looking at her.

Her daughter answered the door.

* * *

Astrid Marchetti had been eleven years old at her mother's funeral and twenty-two now, and the transformation had apparently consisted of becoming her mother with the warmth filed down. She looked at me the way one looks at weather — something to be endured, possibly useful later.

"Herr Kessler. We've already been counted."

"Verification," I said, and produced the folder, and watched her decide whether to be bored or wary. She chose bored, which is the more intelligent choice, and stood aside.

Otto Marchetti was at the press, doing the slow, squinting work of a man whose eyes are leaving him ahead of schedule. He greeted me with the elaborate courtesy of the guilty, offering coffee he clearly could not spare. I declined. There is a rule in my trade: never accept hospitality from a household you may be required to burn. I invented that rule on the spot, standing in their shop, and have kept it since.

The verification itself was theater. Household members present and accounted for. Occupation: printer. No lodgers, no apprentices beyond the one who had left in autumn — I noted the vacancy aloud and watched Otto's hands still over the forme. Everyone lies on a census; my job was officially to catch the lies that mattered, and unofficially to notice which ones didn't.

Then I asked the question. I had promised myself I wouldn't, and then I did, because a man who cannot sleep will sabotage himself just to feel something decisive.

"The drawer in the corner," I said. "Locked. What's kept there?"

"Overstock," Astrid said. Too quickly, and evenly enough afterward to know she'd caught the quickness herself.

"Of?"

"Job work. Invoices. Old forms."

I could have let it lie. God knows I wanted to let it lie. But there was a copy of the flag list in my folder, and Brandt's name was on it, and beneath Brandt's name, in Hahn's own hand, a note: *Velden distribution believed active. Print shops. Priority.* If I left that drawer unexamined and someone else examined it later, my negligence would hang beside theirs.

So I said, "May I?" and Otto Marchetti went white in a way I recognized, because I had seen men go that color on the prison steps, and Astrid set down her composing stick very carefully, the way one sets down a thing one is deciding whether to use as a weapon.

And then — I still don't know why, except that cowardice and mercy sometimes wear the same coat — I looked at the drawer, rattled the lock, pronounced it "old stock, nothing current," and closed my folder.

"You'll want to clear out that overstock," I said. "Paper attracts damp. And inspectors."

I let myself out. On the step I turned back and found Astrid watching me through the glass, and I gave her nothing — no wink, no nod, none of the theatrical signals conspirators imagine are invisible. I simply looked at her the way I imagine her mother once looked at me, and hoped the resemblance would do the translating.

* * *

That night I burned my notes.

Not all of them — I am not an idiot, whatever Hahn's junior clerks think of me, and a census man who burns his files wholesale might as well hang a sign. But the Marchetti folio, the one with my marginalia — *drawer locked, father evasive, daughter watchful, press warm* (the press had been warm; they had been printing within the hour) — that folio went into the stove in my rooms above the chandlery, page by page, and I sat and watched each one curl.

Here is what nobody tells you about treachery, when you finally commit some: it is quiet. I had expected, in the abstract, to feel like a man in a ballad — drums, torchlight, a dramatic vow. Instead I felt like a clerk staying late. The fire ate the paper. The chandlery smelled of tallow. Somewhere below, the chandler's dog dreamed audibly. History, I thought, watching the last corner glow and die, is mostly bookkeeping done badly by frightened men.

Except that isn't quite true either, and I resented myself for the comfort of it. Because what I had done was not merely fail to record a crime. I had warned the criminals. *Clear out that overstock.* Twelve words, and any competent investigator reading my file would find the file conveniently thin, and thin files have a smell, and Hahn had a nose like a customs hound.

I took inventory of myself, as one does at such hours. Assets: a modest salary, a widowed mother in Basel whom I sent money to and visited rarely, a reputation for dullness so consistent it was practically armor. Liabilities: one promise made in the rain eleven years ago to a dead woman, currently accruing interest.

My mother used to say that a man's face is the one ledger he cannot cook. I caught my reflection in the dark window over the stove and did not care for the entries. Something had moved into my expression and begun furnishing the rooms. Worry, probably. Or its respectable cousin, diligence. Either way, it showed, and showing is dangerous, and knowing it shows is more dangerous still, because a man who knows he looks guilty begins performing innocence, and performance is the one art Hahn can spot at forty paces.

Three days later the summons came.

Not a summons — I flatter myself. A note, delivered by a runner, in the round bureaucratic hand: *Herr Kessler to call upon Magistrate Hahn, Thursday, four o'clock, regarding district verification matters.* Regarding verification matters. My own department. My own files. Which meant either routine review — possible, though Hahn did not do routine — or someone had noticed the thin place in my ledger where the Marchetti folio used to be.

I dressed carefully. I chose my dullest tie. On the walk to the magistrate's office I rehearsed answers to questions I hadn't been asked yet, which is the national pastime of Velden that winter, practiced in kitchens and counting houses and, presumably, in the print shop on the Kirchgasse, where a locked drawer was perhaps even now being emptied into somewhere safer, on the advice of a tired man who counted things.

Hahn received me in his office with the window shut against the cold, though the room was already hot from the stove, and offered me tea, which he had never done before.

"Herr Kessler," he said pleasantly, "tell me about the print district."

I sat down, and folded my hands, and prepared to miscount.

Behind him, on the wall, hung a map of the city with small pins in it, and I noticed — because noticing is my curse — that three new pins had gone into the streets around the Kirchgasse, placed close together, like stitches closing a wound.
