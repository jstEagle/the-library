## 6. The Magistrate's Tea

There is a particular quality to being summoned by Magistrate Hahn: you learn of it twice. First from the runner who arrives at your office with a card so heavy with embossing it could anchor a boat, and then again from your own stomach, which drops an hour early and stays down through lunch.

The card said *four o'clock*, and beneath that, in Hahn's own hand — he was a man who believed in the authority of handwriting — *informal*.

Informal. In my experience, when a magistrate writes "informal" on a summons, what he means is: *I will be watching how you hold your teacup.* Hahn had never once in his career asked a question he didn't already know the answer to, and the tea was merely the stagecraft that let him watch you discover this.

I dressed carefully, which is to say I dressed like a man with nothing to hide, which is to say I dressed like every man in Velden who has something to hide. Grey coat, unremarkable. The census office coat, actually, because there is no costume in the world more invisible than bureaucracy. A man in a clerk's uniform could walk through a burning building and people would only apologize for the mess.

My father had taken tea with Hahn. That was the thing sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone as I crossed the old town in the grey afternoon light. They had been young men together, before Hahn traded his ideals for a magistrate's seal and my father traded his for a pension and an early grave. There were photographs somewhere — my mother kept them in a biscuit tin — of two thin young men squinting into a sun that no longer exists, standing in front of a university hall that has since been renamed three times.

One of them grew up to count citizens. The other grew up to decide which ones counted.

I arrived at four o'clock precisely, because arriving late is a confession and arriving early is an enthusiasm, and I wished to be neither guilty nor eager.

* * *

Hahn's rooms above the courthouse smelled of bergamot and old paper, which is the smell of power in this country: things steeped too long, things filed away. He received me himself, without his secretary, which was either an honor or a warning, and with Hahn it was always both.

"Kessler." He said my name the way a man tests a coin against his teeth. "You have your father's shoulders."

"You have his memory, sir," I said, and hated myself for the smoothness of it even as I watched it land well.

"I have everyone's memory. It's the burden of the job." He waved me into a chair across from him and poured the tea himself, another honor-or-warning. His hands were steady. Magistrates' hands are always steady; it is the rest of us who shake. "Sit. Drink. This is not an interrogation."

"No, sir."

"It is a conversation between two men whose fathers would be appalled at what we've become." He smiled when he said it, which made it worse, because Hahn smiling at the truth was Hahn filing it away for later use.

I drank. The tea was excellent, which I resented.

He let the silence do some work first. Hahn understood silence the way printers understand white space — as the thing that gives the letters their meaning. I occupied myself cataloguing the room. Ledgers along one wall, spines cracked from use. A map of the city districts, updated recently; I could see the new ink where the river quarter boundaries had been redrawn. And on the corner of his desk, squared neatly as a place setting, a stack of pamphlets.

I knew them the way you know your own reflection in a shop window. Cheap paper, bad ink, the distinctive cramped setting of the Marchetti press. Brandt's essay, third edition. Someone had collected them off the streets and carried them up four flights of marble stairs to lay at the magistrate's elbow like a dead bird brought in by a cat.

"You've been through the river district," Hahn said. Not a question. He had my route schedules; of course he did. "Door to door. Names, trades, lodgers, loyalties."

"Loyalties aren't on the form, sir."

"Everything is on the form, Kessler. Most clerks simply lack the eyes to read it." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, aligning the handle with his wrist. "Tell me about the print shops."

"The print shops."

"There are eleven of them in your district. You'll know this better than I. Which of them prints more than they declare?"

And there it was, laid out on the table between the cups like a card he wanted me to pick up. I set my own cup down — carefully, because a rattling saucer is also a confession — and gave him the honest face I'd been practicing in the window of a glover's shop on the way over.

"They're tradesmen, sir. They print wedding announcements and funeral notices and price lists for the corn exchange. If any of them printed sedition, they'd be doing it badly — half of them can't spell 'citizen.'"

"Mm." He opened a ledger — of course he had a ledger; the man probably kept accounts of his dreams — and ran a finger down a column. "The Marchetti shop. Bridge Street. German father, Italian name, a story there I've never bothered to untangle. Declared output last year: broadsheets, hymnals, one run of school primers. Yet." He lifted one of the pamphlets from the stack and held it up between two fingers, the way a physician holds a specimen. "This says otherwise. Or rather, its typeface does. My examiner tells me the sorts used for the word 'liberty' — twice on this page — show a chipped serif on the lowercase *b*. Distinctive. Like a limp. You can disguise a man's face but not his walk."

Somewhere behind my ribs, my heart performed a small, disciplined maneuver, the kind you learn in childhood when your mother asks who broke the window and you understand that the answer must be constructed, not remembered.

"That sounds like thorough work, sir."

"It is. The examiner is thorough. The examiner is not, however, a census man." He looked at me over the rim of his cup. "You went into that shop. Twice, by my count. You spoke to the Marchettis. You saw their cases, their stock, their hands."

"I saw a dying trade, sir. A blind printer and a daughter who does the work of three men for the wage of none."

"And the drawer?"

The word went through me like a nail through cloth. I had mentioned no drawer in any report. But then, I hadn't needed to; Kessler the younger had stood in that shop with his ledger open while old Marchetti's hand rested on a locked drawer the way a man rests his hand on a dog that might bite, and apparently I had worn my noticing on my face where Hahn's network of eyes could read it later.

"What drawer, sir?"

"Ah." He set the pamphlet down and laced his fingers. For a long moment he simply looked at me, and I understood that I was now past the point where lies could help me and into the country where only performance remained. There is a difference. A lie is a thing you say. A performance is a thing you are, continuously, under examination, and it is far more exhausting.

"My examiner believes there is a drawer in that shop containing several hundred unsold copies of this essay," Hahn said. "He believes it because the Marchetti girl ordered more ink in February than her declared work could consume. Ink leaves a trail, Kessler. Everything leaves a trail. That is the whole art of government, when you come down to it — reading trails." He poured himself more tea, unhurried. "What I want from you is simple. You are in those houses. Those people open their doors to you because you carry a form and a dull expression and they mistake you for furniture. Tell me what is in the drawer."

I could feel the shape of the trap now, and it was elegant, the way all his traps were elegant: if I said *there is no drawer*, and a search later found one, I was a fool or a liar. If I said *there is a drawer*, I had signed the death warrant of a print shop and whatever promise it contained. Either way, Hahn learned what he wanted, which was not the drawer's contents but my usefulness.

So I chose the third road, the one my father would have recognized, God rest him, because he'd walked it his whole career: the truth, bent just enough to pass through the keyhole.

"There is a drawer," I said, and watched Hahn's eyebrows rise a fraction — the reward of surprise, rare currency with that man. "Locked. Old Marchetti keeps his accounts in it, and his late wife's papers, and I would guess a quantity of stock he hasn't the heart to pulp. Printers hoard. It's a sickness of the trade — ask any of them, they'll show you drawers full of job work twenty years dead. Whether anything seditious is among it, I couldn't say. I'm a counter of heads, sir, not a reader of drawers."

"Hm." He considered this, turning his cup again. "You defend them."

"I describe them. Defense requires an opinion, and opinions are above my pay."

That got a short laugh out of him, dry as a file stroke. "Your father said nearly the same thing once, in this room, about a different matter. He was wrong then, too. Being wrong cost him nothing. Being wrong costs more these days." He stood, and I stood, and the audience was evidently over, except that Hahn, walking me to the door, paused with his hand on the frame and delivered the actual message of the afternoon, the tea having been merely the envelope it came in.

"A search warrant is being drawn up. Not today. Not tomorrow. When it comes, it will be executed properly — by men who are not you, so that whatever is found cannot be blamed on a census man's sentimentality. You understand."

"I understand, sir."

"If the drawer is empty when they open it," Hahn said pleasantly, "then there was never anything in it, and the Marchettis are exactly what they appear to be, and you will have done your father's memory credit. If it is not empty—" He smiled his filing-cabinet smile. "Then we shall all learn something about one another. Good afternoon, Kessler."

* * *

I walked back to the office the long way, along the river, because I needed wind on my face and the river provides the only honest weather in Velden.

Empty the drawer. That was the instruction, wrapped in silk. Hahn hadn't asked me to report; he'd asked me to arrange. He was giving me a rope and calling it a ladder: empty the drawer, save the shop, prove yourself clean. And if I emptied it, I proved there had been something in it. And if I warned them to empty it, I became a courier for the other side. Every branch of the tree bore the same fruit.

A colleague was waiting for me when I got back — Vogel, at my desk, ostensibly returning a form he'd borrowed, actually sitting in my chair with his boots on my footrest, which was his way of saying *I live here now, get used to it.*

"Long tea," Vogel said.

"Magistrates pour slowly."

"Heard he poured for you personally. No secretary." He handed me the form without getting up. "When Hahn pours for you himself, one of two things is happening. Either you're being promoted, or you're being weighed." He smiled. Vogel's smile was the smile of a man who checked other men's pockets for a living and enjoyed the checking more than the finding. "Which is it, Kessler?"

"I'm a census clerk, Vogel. Nobody promotes us. It's against policy — we might start counting ourselves."

He laughed, but he didn't leave, and he didn't take his boots off my footrest, and when I sat down at the neighboring desk and began copying names into a register I wasn't reading, I felt his attention on the side of my face like weather coming off the mountains.

Vogel had joined the office two years ago, after the reorganization, and he was good at the work in the way that dogs are good at hunting — no philosophy in it, just appetite. He'd noticed, weeks ago, that my route logs showed longer dwell times at certain addresses than the forms justified. He'd noticed it and said nothing, which from Vogel was louder than saying it. Men like Vogel don't spend their observations; they invest them, and wait for the interest to compound.

"Bridge Street," he said now, conversationally, examining his fingernails. "You spend a lot of time on Bridge Street."

"People on Bridge Street have complicated households. Lodgers, widows, a boarding house that changes tenants like socks."

"Mm." He stood, stretched, took his time about it. "Funny thing about complicated households. They're always where the interesting papers turn up." At the door he paused. "Enjoy the tea, Kessler. Not many get poured for twice."

Twice. So he knew about the first visit too, or guessed at it, or had simply said *twice* to watch my face. I kept my face doing nothing, which is the hardest work I do, and after his footsteps faded I sat for a long while looking at the register, at the columns of names I had entered in my neat clerk's hand, each one a person reduced to a line, and understood that I was now a line in someone else's column.

* * *

That evening I did something I had not done in years. I went to the biscuit tin.

My mother died believing my father had lived an uncomplicated life, and I had seen no reason to correct the record; grief is heavy enough without adding archives. But the tin was where she kept everything, and I sat on the floor of my rooms with it open on my knees like a child, and dug past the photographs to the letters underneath.

Two young men in front of a university hall. On the back, in my father's hand: *E.H. and me, the day we swore the republic would arrive before our hair did.* And beneath the photograph, folded small, a letter from E. Hahn to my father, dated thirty years ago, full of words like *liberty* and *press* and *brothers*, written in a hand identical to the one that had that morning embossed a card summoning me to tea.

They had been the same man. That was the thing I sat with in the lamplight, the thing that made the loyalty-to-the-dead pull so hard against survival among the living: Hahn had not betrayed my father's generation. He had become what my father's generation feared they'd become, and buried the evidence in ledgers, and gone on pouring tea.

I thought of Astrid Marchetti — the daughter, sharp-eyed, ink-stained, watching me across her father's shop with the look of someone deciding whether I was weather or climate. I thought of the locked drawer, and the chipped serif on the lowercase *b*, and the promise I had made on prison steps to a woman who is now a folder of papers in a magistrate's memory and a sorting tray in a daughter's hands.

Three days, perhaps less, before the warrant came. Perhaps fewer, if Vogel's investments matured early.

I put the photograph back in the tin. Then I took out the letter from young Hahn, read it once more by the lamp, and burned it in the stove — not because it was dangerous, though it was, but because some debts should only ever exist on one side of the fire.

Tomorrow I would walk Bridge Street again, form in hand, dull expression in place, and I would need to find a way to tell a blind printer and his daughter that the tea was finished, the cup was drained, and the leaves at the bottom spelled a search party.

The lamp guttered. Outside, in the square, soldiers were drilling by torchlight, and their boots kept a rhythm like a press at speed — strike, lift, strike — printing something on the cobbles that no type case could ever set right.
