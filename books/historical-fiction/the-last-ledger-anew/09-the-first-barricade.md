## 9. The First Barricade

I have kept ledgers for a living, so let me begin the way a ledger begins: with an entry.

*Tuesday, the fourteenth of March. Weather: unseasonably warm. Disposition of the city of Velden: overturned.*

That is the whole of it, and no clerk alive could make it more accurate. You can count doors, I have counted thousands of them. You can count heads, names, trades, the number of pigs kept in a courtyard on Fischer Lane (two, and both ill-tempered). But there is no column in any census for the moment a city decides it has had enough. It arrives the way weather arrives, and by the time you notice it, your hat is already in the river.

I noticed at half past six in the morning, because a paving stone came through the window of the census office and landed on my desk, precisely where I had been about to set my coffee.

The stone was accompanied, as stones often are, by shouting. I went to the window and looked down at the Rathausplatz, and what I saw there I will describe as plainly as I can, because plainness is the only honesty left to me this week.

There were perhaps four hundred people in the square. Not the usual four hundred — not the shuffling, patient four hundred who queue for flour or wait to petition the magistrate about a disputed fence line. These four hundred had pulled up the cobbles behind them as they came, stacking them into a low, ugly wall across the mouth of the Kramergasse, and they were adding to it with benches, cart wheels, a baker's sign, and — I noted with the professional admiration that has been the ruin of my character — an entire cast-iron stove, which three men were wrestling into place with the tenderness of midwives.

Someone had raised a red cloth on a broom handle. Someone else was hammering planks into the windows of the tax office. A boy of no more than twelve stood on the fountain's rim reading from a pamphlet, and the crowd was repeating the lines after him in a low rolling chorus, the way a congregation repeats a psalm.

I knew the pamphlet. Of course I knew it. I had counted its printer's address among my omissions for the better part of two years, and I had watched the words on it march from the Marchettis' back room into the city's bloodstream one handbill at a time, the way water works through plaster — invisibly, and then all at once, and then the wall comes down.

The wall came down. Or rather, several walls came down, and one went up, and by seven o'clock the bells of St. Ulrich were ringing the alarm and the garrison was turning out in the square with fixed bayonets, and I was standing at my window holding my coffee — which had survived, I am pleased to report, though the stone had not spared my inkwell — understanding with perfect clarity that everything I had spent two years carefully not writing down was now being written in cobbles and blood across the entire city.

There is a particular sensation that comes to a man at such a moment. It is not fear, or not only fear. It is the sensation of a ledger balancing — the awful satisfaction of seeing every entry you have falsified come due at once, with interest.

* * *

Captain Brenner found me at eight.

I should describe Brenner, because he matters to what follows. He is a large man built along the lines of a wardrobe, with a face like a closed door and a voice that he uses the way other men use a crowbar. He is not stupid. I want to be fair to him, insofar as fairness is available to either of us. He is a man who believes in order the way my father believed in the church: completely, comfortably, and without ever once examining what it cost to maintain. In another life he would have made an excellent bridge. In this one, he makes an excellent instrument, and instruments do not choose what they are used for.

"Kessler." He dropped a folded order on my desk, on top of the paving stone, as if the two were colleagues. "The magistrate wants the print district searched. Every shop, every cellar, every attic. There are arms coming in through the printers — that's the intelligence — and seditious material by the cartload. You know the district. Every door, every name, every back stair. You'll walk point for my squad."

"Every shop," I said.

"Every shop." He looked at the paving stone on my desk and then at me, and something moved behind his face — not suspicion, exactly. Brenner does not suspect; suspicion requires imagination. It was more the look of a man noticing that a familiar room has been rearranged. "You look tired, Kessler."

"I've been counting the city for six years, Captain. Tired is the occupational condition."

He grunted, which from Brenner is a eulogy, and left.

I sat for a moment with the order in my hands. It was a proper document, stamped and signed, with Magistrate Hahn's seal at the bottom — that neat, precise seal, like a wax coin, the seal of a man who believes that a city is a ledger and every entry can be corrected if you are firm enough with the pen. I had drunk tea in that man's office. I had lied to him about a name while he watched me with his father's eyes, and he had believed me, or believed in me, which is worse.

And now he was sending me through the print district with forty soldiers and a list, and the list had one name at the top that I could read without unrolling it, because there is only one print shop in Velden with a cellar deep enough and a family stubborn enough.

I did the arithmetic I have been doing since the day I stood on the prison steps and let a dying woman's friend squeeze my hand and say, *Ivo, you count everything. Count them safe.* Two years of arithmetic. It had always resolved to the same sum. It resolved to it now.

The only question — and I confess I sat with it longer than I am proud of — was whether I would be hanged for the answer.

* * *

The print district at nine in the morning looked like a district that had been expecting us.

Shutters closed. Doors barred. In the gutters, the evidence of a hurried morning: a burned smell, as though papers had gone into stoves all down the street at the same hour, a general confession by smoke. The Kramergasse barricade had been extended overnight — or rather, since dawn — and now stretched across both ends of the print quarter, and behind it stood the kind of men who do not run when soldiers appear. Carpenters, tanners, two of the boatmen from the river yards. A woman with a rolling pin stuck through her belt like a saber, which I mention because it is the single most Velden thing I have ever witnessed.

Brenner surveyed it with the satisfaction of a man who has finally been given something to kick.

"Kessler. The houses first. We'll deal with the barricade when we've done our work."

*Our* work. I have thought about that word since. It is remarkable how a man can spend years inside a lie and still be wounded by the pronouns.

I led them up the Kramergasse's side streets, and here I will set down what I did, plainly, because a ledger that hides its own entries is worthless. I knew every door in that district. I knew which shops printed wedding invitations and which printed something warmer. I knew that the Fischbein bindery had a cellar full of nothing but ledgers — the irony there being of a quality I cannot improve upon. And I knew, with the terrible precision of six years of counting, exactly which addresses could absorb a search without anyone hanging from a rope at the end of it.

So I gave Brenner the district in the wrong order.

It is not so difficult as it sounds. You begin with the shops nearest the square — the ones the crowd can see, the ones where a search is a *performance*, and performances want audiences. We spent forty minutes in the stationer's on Adlergasse while Brenner's men turned over reams of cream writing paper and found, in the cellar, a crate of pamphlets that the stationer had been using to prop up a leaking barrel. Brenner confiscated the pamphlets and arrested the barrel. The stationer wept, convincingly, and was released by noon, because Brenner is thorough but not imaginative, and a man who prints psalms does not fit the shape of the enemy he has been given.

From the stationer's we went to the bindery, and from the bindery to the engraver's, and at each stop I made a small error. Nothing a court could hang. A wrong turning — *forgive me, Captain, the lanes all look alike in this quarter*, which is a lie only a man who has never walked them could tell. A name misremembered. A cellar door I described as bricked up when it was merely painted, so that we stood in the engraver's yard for ten minutes while a soldier tried to find a seam that I assured him did not exist.

Each small error bought an hour. I have never in my life been so grateful for my own meticulousness, nor so ashamed of it. Six years of learning every door in Velden, and the whole education had been in service of this: knowing precisely where to point forty men so that they would look everywhere except where it mattered.

There is a version of this story in which I am a hero. I want to say, for the record, that I do not believe in that version. A hero chooses courage over safety. What I did that morning was choose a dead woman's promise over my own neck while wearing the uniform of the men I was betraying, and I did it with sweaty hands and a heart going like a drum in a parade. Complicity and courage, I have learned, are not opposite things. They are the same act, viewed from different windows, and the view depends entirely on which side of the glass the firing squad ends up standing.

* * *

We came to the Marchetti shop at half past two in the afternoon.

I had saved it. That was the design — save it for last, when the light was going and the men were tired and the day's harvest of confiscated psalms and leaking barrels had dulled the edge off Brenner's certainty. I had planned it the way I plan everything, in columns, and the plan was sound.

The plan did not account for the smoke.

It was rolling up from the direction of the river when we turned onto the Marchettis' street — not the smoke of burned papers this time but the smoke of burned timber, black and fat, and beneath it the sound that I had been hearing all day at the edges of the city and had managed, until that moment, to keep at the edge of my mind as well: the sound of a barricade being stormed. Musketry in ragged volleys. A bell ringing not the alarm but the *toque*, the fast panicked striking. And under all of it, a crowd's roar, which is not like any other roar. It has joy in it, and terror, and they sound identical from a distance.

"Kessler." Brenner had stopped in the middle of the lane, and for the first time that day he looked uncertain, and his uncertainty looked like a wardrobe that has been asked to dance. "The garrison's engaged at the Fischer Gate. We're to fall back and hold the Rathaus."

"Captain, the search—"

"The search is concluded." He said it the way a man closes a ledger. "The magistrate's order stands for tomorrow. We'll come back with proper numbers."

Tomorrow. I turned that word over as the squad formed up behind him, and I understood that I had won the day and lost the war, because *tomorrow* was not a promise I could misdirect. Tomorrow there would be no barricade to hide behind, no crowd to perform for. Tomorrow the city would belong to whoever held it at dawn, and if that was the garrison, then the Marchettis' cellar would be opened with bayonets instead of keys.

I gave Brenner the route back to the Rathaus — the long way, past the fountain, where the crowd was thickest and slowest to part, because every minute I could steal from him was a minute someone else could spend carrying paper down a cellar stair — and then I did the one thing I had promised myself I would not do.

I went back.

* * *

The lane behind the Marchetti shop is a cart lane, six feet wide, walled on one side by the shop itself and on the other by the cooper's yard. I have walked it perhaps a hundred times in six years, in the course of counting, and I had never once seen anyone in it, because it leads nowhere and everyone in Velden knows it.

Astrid Marchetti was in it.

She came out of the shop's back door with a crate in her arms — a type crate, I knew its weight by the way she carried it, low and close, the way you carry a thing you cannot drop — and she saw me before I could decide whether to be seen, and she stopped, and for the length of four heartbeats we stood in that lane with the smoke rolling over the rooftops and the musketry going at the Fischer Gate like a giant knocking on a door that would not open.

She was not afraid of me. That is what I remember most. I have been afraid of plenty of men with my face and my uniform, and I have watched fear do its work on people's faces — the quick arithmetic of *how much trouble am I in* — and Astrid did none of it. She looked at me the way you look at weather. She assessed me, and she went on.

"Kessler." Just my name, flat, the way you'd read it off a form. Which, in fairness, is what I am.

"They're coming back tomorrow," I said. "The whole district. Brenner's squad, full numbers, magistrate's order. Whatever you're doing in that cellar — finish it tonight, because tomorrow there won't be a tonight."

She shifted the crate on her hip. "And you'll be with them."

"I'll be with them."

"Then why are you telling me?"

Because your mother made me promise. Because I have been keeping a ledger of omissions for two years and it is the only honest book I own. Because you are twenty-two years old and you are carrying a crate of small metal letters through a burning city and you look, God help me, exactly like her.

"Because I count everything," I said, "and I've counted you safe. I'd like the sum to hold."

She looked at me for a long moment, and behind her eyes I watched her do her own arithmetic — the same sum, run in the other direction, a daughter weighing a stranger's word against her father's neck — and I understood that she did not believe me, and that she had decided to use me anyway, and that both of those things were correct.

"Then count the back stair," she said. "It's the third door past the cooper's, and the hinge screams. Oil it tomorrow, if you're walking point. You'd know how."

And she went past me, up the lane toward the river side, with her crate of letters, and I stood in the smoke and said nothing.

That is the entry I keep coming back to. Not the misdirected squad, not the falsified route, not the lies I told Brenner with a straight face. *He passed Astrid in the smoke and said nothing.* A whole confession, compressed into a silence. If they hang me, that is the line I would want read out. It is the truest thing I have ever not said.

* * *

I walked back to the Rathaus the long way, alone, because Brenner had gone ahead with the squad, and the city came apart around me as I walked, gently at first and then all at once, the way a wall comes down.

By the Fischer Gate the crowd had held — I saw the barricade from a distance, the stove still on top of it, absurd and magnificent, and a red cloth flying above it all, and men on the rooftops passing ammunition hand to hand like buckets at a fire. By the river, boats were loading in the dark with no lights. On the Kramergasse, someone had already chalked on the census office wall — *my* wall, the wall of the building where I have spent six years turning human beings into columns — a single line of figures, and I stopped to read it, because I cannot pass figures without reading them, it is the whole of my affliction.

It was a number. The number of the census district. And beneath it, in a different hand: *We were counted. Now we count ourselves.*

I stood there until the smoke made my eyes water, and I am prepared to swear, to any court that asks, that it was the smoke.

Tomorrow the soldiers come back with full numbers. Tomorrow there is a name at the top of the list that I can read without unrolling it. Tomorrow I will walk point past a back stair whose hinge I intend, very quietly, to oil.

And somewhere in that cellar tonight, by candlelight, Astrid Marchetti is setting type — I know she is, because it is the only thing she knows how to do with fear — and the letters she is setting are the ones that will hang us both or free us all, and there is no column in any ledger for which.

I went home. I sharpened my pencil. I began, for the first time in six years, to keep two books.

The second one I will burn, if it comes to that. But not before it is finished.
