## 11. The Informer

There is a particular smell to a government office in winter: cold stove, wet wool, and the faint sweetness of sealing wax. I had come to know it the way a man comes to know his own heartbeat — without wanting to, and without any hope of forgetting it. On the morning the denunciation arrived, that smell was stronger than usual, because Magistrate Hahn had ordered the stoves lit early and the windows shut against the river fog, and forty clerks were breathing the same recycled air and pretending it was patriotism.

I was at my desk before seven, as always, reconciling the district rolls. My hands were steady. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only here, in the privacy of my own skull: on the morning everything broke, my hands were steady. Whatever else they say about me afterward — and they will say things; Hahn keeps files the way other men keep grudges — let it be noted that Ivo Kessler could hold a pen.

The boy was brought in at half past eight.

I knew him before anyone said his name. Wendel Furrer, seventeen or thereabouts, apprentice compositor at the Adler Press — Brandt's old rival in more ways than one, though the rivalry these days consisted mostly of printing funeral announcements and tax notices with marginally better kerning. I'd seen him at the trade fair last autumn, all elbows and ink-stained cuffs, showing off a new chase he'd machined himself. A clever boy. Clever boys are the most dangerous kind, because they have not yet learned what their cleverness costs other people.

He sat across from Hahn's desk with his cap in his lap like a churchgoer waiting for the sermon to end, and he talked.

I will not reproduce the whole of it. Denunciations have a grammar of their own, and after four years in this office I know it by heart: the careful opening (*I come forward not from malice but from duty*), the rehearsed middle (dates, names, the locked drawer), the trembling close (*I ask that my name be kept from the parties concerned*). Wendel Furrer performed all three movements adequately for a first attempt. He said the Marchetti shop printed pamphlets at night. He said he had seen the type forms through a window during a delivery — *forms set and locked, sir, not job work, the measure was wrong for invoices* — and that he recognized the style of the headpiece ornament from copies of the Brandt essays circulating in the wine markets. He said the word *Brandt* three times, each time a little louder, as if volume were evidence.

Hahn listened the way he listened to everything: with his chin propped on two fingers and his eyes doing slow arithmetic over the boy's shoulder. Hahn is not a cruel man, which is worse than cruelty in an official. Cruel men are predictable. Hahn is thorough. He believes in the state the way my late father believed in the Catholic Church — completely, gratefully, and with a suspicion of anyone who seemed too eager to prove their own devotion.

"Kessler," he said at last, without turning. "You did the print district census yourself, didn't you."

"I did, Herr Magistrate."

"And your notes on the Marchetti establishment?"

"In the district file." I kept my voice at the pitch of a man discussing drainage. "Small operation. Father and daughter. Mostly jobbing — broadsheets, labels, the theater bills for the Wintergarten. Nothing political came through my count."

"Nothing political," Hahn repeated, tasting it. He looked down at Wendel's statement, then up at me, and for one long moment I understood exactly how a mouse feels inside a snake's attention: not yet eaten, merely measured. "You'll lead the search party, Kessler. You know the premises. You know where a printer would hide stock." A pause, delicate as a paper cut. "You counted every room, after all."

"Yes, Herr Magistrate."

"Three days. The garrison can spare men Thursday morning, not before — they're drilling for the parade review, God help us all." He said *God help us all* the way he said everything, mildly, as though Providence were a slightly incompetent junior clerk. Then he dismissed the boy, who left with the lightness of a man who has just sold something heavy, and turned back to his papers, and that was that. Three days. Signed, sealed, and already smelling of smoke.

* * *

I walked home the long way, along the river, because the short way passed the Marchetti shop and I could not yet trust my face.

Let me explain something about my position, since no one else will ever bother to. A census taker is a nobody. That is precisely why I chose the work, six years ago, when Lena Marchetti stood on the prison steps with her wrists still marked and made me promise — made *me*, a frightened clerk with a rubber stamp and a conscience she could see straight through — to watch over her people. Nobody watches a nobody. I have spent six years being invisible in plain sight, counting doors, memorizing rooms, knowing which cellar in the print district connects to which, which dockmaster drinks, which gate guard owes whom. It is astonishing how much a man may protect with nothing but a ledger and a reputation for dullness.

But protection from inside has a shape, and the shape is a circle drawn around you by other people's ignorance. As long as no one looked closely, the circle held. Now Hahn had handed me the search warrant itself. There would be no losing papers this time, no misfiled report, no convenient clerical error. He wanted me personally to walk through the Marchetti shop and find what was there — or find nothing, and explain the nothing, with Wendel Furrer's statement sitting on the magistrate's desk like a stone in a shoe.

And there was worse. There is always worse; the state is generous that way.

Because I knew why the boy had done it. Not the official reason — duty, citizenship, all the words that cost nothing. I knew the real one, or I suspected it, because I keep census records, and census records are confessions people don't know they're making. The Furrer household: mother dead, father a dyer with lungs full of indigo, and a sister. Marta Furrer, fifteen, arrested in October outside the university gates, charged with distributing seditious leaflets. Still in the women's wing at the citadel, awaiting a hearing that Hahn's own docket showed scheduled for — I checked twice that night, by candlelight, hating myself for hoping — the week after next.

A sister in a cell. A magistrate with the power to move the hearing either direction. And a boy with sharp eyes and a compositor's knowledge of what locked type looks like.

They hadn't even needed to threaten him. That was the elegant part, the part that made me want to put my fist through a wall. They had simply let him understand that hearings go better for families that cooperate. No signature required. No bargain on record. Just a boy walking out of the citadel with his sister's name in his mouth and the Marchettis' address in his pocket, telling himself he'd chosen, because the alternative was to know he'd been used, and seventeen-year-olds cannot afford to know that. None of us can, really. We'd stop functioning.

I stood on the bridge in the fog and watched the barges go downriver toward Basel, black shapes with lanterns at bow and stern, and I thought: *three days*. Somewhere south of the city, an old man with failing lungs was writing letters no postal service would carry. Somewhere north of it, a girl was setting type by lamplight, believing — because I had told her so, because I had promised her mother so — that I could still stand between her and the machine.

The machine had just asked me to operate it personally.

* * *

Thursday morning, then. Which gave me Wednesday night, and Tuesday, and whatever hours remained of today, to perform a miracle without witnesses.

I ran the arithmetic on the walk home, because arithmetic is what I have instead of prayer. Option one: warn them to run. Astrid Marchetti does not strike me as a woman who runs, and even if she did, a printer fleeing with a press leaves a trail a blind man could follow, and her father cannot walk to the end of the street unaided. Also — and I am honest enough to admit this even now — running confirms everything. A family that flees is a family that confesses. Hahn would have them within the week, and this time there would be no hearing at all, not with the city the temperature it is, not with the garrison drilling in the square and every officer hungry for a treason conviction to wave at his superiors.

Option two: make the evidence vanish before Thursday. Burn the stock, break the type, scatter the formes. But Wendel had seen locked forms through a window; a shop suddenly stripped of its type would be stranger than a shop caught with it. And I would be the one searching. If I found nothing, Hahn would conclude one of two things: either the boy lied, or Kessler did. Hahn does not believe boys lie. Boys are simple; boys want what boys want. Clerks, on the other hand, are capable of anything.

Which left option three, the one I kept circling like a moth around a lamp I knew would kill me: find a way to be searched and survive it. Plant doubt in Wendel's testimony. Discover some procedural flaw — a wrong form, a missing countersignature — that would buy a delay, and pray the delay outlasted the boy's usefulness or the regime's patience, whichever expired first. The city was a kettle with the lid rattling; everyone in this office knew it, everyone pretended otherwise. Revolutions are inconvenient for informers too. Sometimes the whole ledger burns, and every debt in it with it.

It was thin. All of it was thin. But then, so was the ice on the Velden every January, and every January someone walked across it, and every year it was a different sort of person — the desperate, the foolish, the ones with no other road. I had been walking on it for six years. I suppose I had simply forgotten the sound it makes, that soft groaning underfoot, right before it decides.

* * *

Wednesday evening I went to the shop.

I tell myself I went to warn them properly — face to face, no notes, nothing that could burn into evidence. That is true. It is also true that I needed to look at Astrid Marchetti's face and ask myself whether I could do what Thursday required, and the only place to ask that question was across a counter from her, under the hanging lamp, among the smells of ink and oil and cold metal that I suddenly realized I associated with her mother more than with any living person.

She opened the door herself, before I finished knocking, as if she'd been standing behind it listening for footsteps. Perhaps she had. People in our situation develop ears like bats.

"You shouldn't be here," she said.

"No," I agreed. "May I come in anyway? I've come to be unwise in person rather than by letter."

Her father was asleep in the chair by the stove, a proof sheet open on his chest, rising and falling. She led me past him to the back room, where the composing stone gleamed under the lamp, and I saw — I couldn't help seeing — the locked forms stacked along the wall under dust sheets, disguised as job work, and I felt the floor tilt slightly beneath me. So much type. Enough type to hang a parish.

"There's been a denunciation," I said. "An apprentice from the Adler. Furrer. He saw the forms. He named Brandt." I gave it to her plainly, because she deserved plainness and because I had no strength left for cushioning. "Search Thursday morning. I'm leading it. I couldn't refuse without making it worse."

I expected fear. What I got instead was a stillness, a settling, like water finding its level. She looked at the dust sheets, then at me, and something moved behind her eyes that I recognized from her mother — that terrible calm of a woman recalculating the world and finding the new sum acceptable.

"Three days," she said. "You mean two."

"I mean two nights. Yes."

"Then we won't waste them." She said it quietly, so as not to wake her father, and reached up and took down the first dust sheet as though she were airing linens. "Thank you for coming, Herr Kessler. You should go before you're seen. And Ivo—" She never called me Ivo. The use of it stopped me at the door like a hand on my chest. "Whatever happens Thursday. You were my mother's friend. That was never a small thing. Don't let it become a large one."

I did not understand her until later. By then the clock had started, and its ticking sounded, to my ear, exactly like a stick of type dropping onto a stone floor — once, twice, and then all of them at once.
