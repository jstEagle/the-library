## 16. The Burning of the Marchetti Shop

They came at dawn, which I have always considered a coward's hour. If you are going to destroy a family's life, you ought at least to have the decency to do it in daylight, when the neighbors can see your face and remember it.

I was awake anyway. I had been awake most of the night, as I had been awake most nights that week, my hands still smelling of ink and lamp oil from the work in the cellar — the second copy, the locked forms, the pages of Tomas Brandt's manuscript disguised as a job lot of funeral notices for a coffin-maker who did not exist. Father knew now. He had known since the hymnal arrived sewn shut with its cargo of contraband truth, and he had said nothing except to move his cot downstairs, closer to the press, the way a man might sleep nearer a sick child.

So when the knocking came — not knocking, really; pounding, the kind of sound that splinters wood before it opens doors — I was already dressed, already standing in the dark of the composing room with my hand on the frame of the Albion press like a woman touching the shoulder of someone she loves who is about to be taken from her.

"Astrid." Father's voice from below, low and terribly calm. "The back stair. Now."

"I'm not leaving you."

"That wasn't a request."

"It wasn't an answer either."

There was no time for more. The front door gave way with a crack like a type case dropped on stone, and boots came into the shop — many boots, heavy ones, the boots of men who had been told they were heroes and half believed it.

* * *

What strikes me now, looking back across the years and the ash, is how ordinary it began. A lieutenant with a list. Two soldiers posted at the door. The smell of their pipe tobacco mixing with the smell of our ink, two smells that never belonged in the same room and yet were forced together by history, which has no nose.

"Marchetti," the lieutenant read off his paper, pronouncing it wrong, as everyone official always had. "Print shop. Licensed premises. We have a warrant for search and seizure of seditious materials."

My father stood in the middle of his own shop with his hands at his sides, an old man in his nightshirt with a coat thrown over it, and he said, "You are welcome to look. You will find job work, almanacs, and the parish register we printed last autumn. Nothing else."

It was true, in the way that all our best truths had become true: technically accurate, arranged like type in a form, every element justified so the lie would hold under pressure. The pamphlets were gone — distributed, burned, or turned into other things. The manuscript was lead now, locked in chases, labeled in my own handwriting as coffin notices. Even the magistrate himself could stand in front of those forms and see nothing but death announcements.

But soldiers do not read type. Soldiers follow lists, and somewhere on a list — written by a boy with a sister in prison, a boy whose face I could not stop seeing whenever I closed my eyes — was the word *press*, and the word *cellar*, and perhaps the word *daughter*.

"They'll want the press," Father murmured to me, barely moving his lips, while the lieutenant's men began pulling drawers open upstairs. "Presses get confiscated. It's procedure. Let them take it."

"You've spent thirty years paying for that press."

"And I intend to spend thirty more complaining about it. Let them take it, Astrid."

I wanted to believe him. I want it noted, wherever such things are recorded, that I wanted very much to believe him, and that for nearly an hour I did.

* * *

They searched the way armies do everything: thoroughly, loudly, and without understanding what they were looking at. One of them held up a composing stick and asked his corporal if it was a weapon. Another found the drawer where we kept the ornaments — fleurons, rules, a woodcut of the river bridge we'd used on a hundred trade cards — and seemed disappointed that beauty was not also evidence.

Upstairs, drawers emptied onto the floorboards. Downstairs, the lieutenant walked the length of the shop with his hands behind his back, reading spines, tilting his head at the framed specimen sheets Father kept on the wall — alphabets, ascending in size, the whole architecture of language displayed like a family portrait.

"You print well," the lieutenant said, and it was almost a compliment. "For this district."

"We print accurately," my father said. "That is the whole of the trade."

"Mm." The lieutenant stopped at the cellar door. It was locked. It was always locked; it had been locked since the night Ivo Kessler first stood in this shop and asked one question too many. "And down there?"

"Storage. Paper stock. Damp gets in; we keep it locked against the weather."

"Open it."

Father produced the key with the unhurried movements of a man who had rehearsed this moment in his head so many times that rehearsal had worn grooves, and the grooves held him upright. I watched him fit the key. I watched the lock turn. And I understood, with a clarity that felt almost like peace, that whatever happened next had already happened somewhere in his imagination, months ago, and he had simply been waiting for reality to catch up.

The soldiers went down the stairs ahead of us, torches raised, because the cellar had no lamp lit and these men traveled with fire the way other men traveled with prayer books.

I remember thinking: *they brought torches into a paper cellar.*

I remember thinking: *of course they did.*

* * *

They found the forms within minutes. Of course they did — I had hidden the manuscript in plain sight, which is the best hiding place there is until someone reads what you've written. Coffin notices, the labels said. But a soldier held a page proof to his torch, sounding out letters the way a schoolboy does, and his face changed, and he called up the stairs in a voice too excited for the smallness of what he'd found.

The lieutenant read. The lieutenant read again. Then he looked at my father with something that was not quite triumph and not quite disappointment — the look of a man who has caught a fish smaller than the one the story promised.

"Seditious matter," he said. "Under the emergency decrees. The press is forfeit. The premises are forfeit. You will come with us, both of you, for questioning."

"Forfeited," my father repeated. "You mean burned."

"The decree provides for destruction of materials—"

"My daughter and I will come with you," Father said, over him, gently, the way you speak to a child repeating a lesson, "and you will do what you were sent to do. But you will not pretend it is law that makes you do it. Law is typeset, young man. I know law. I have set it. What you are holding is orders."

I saw the lieutenant's jaw tighten. I saw the torch in the nearest soldier's hand dip lower, toward the bales of paper stacked against the cellar wall, and I saw my father move.

I will spend the rest of my life seeing it.

He moved the way he had never moved in my memory — fast, decisive, the slowness of his age falling away like a coat shrugged off — and he put his body between the soldier and the press. Not between the soldier and the paper. Between the soldier and the *press*. Thirty years of his life in iron and walnut, the machine that had fed us and named us and printed my mother's wedding announcement and my mother's obituary and everything between, and when the moment came he did not reach for the manuscript, or for me, or for the door.

A torch is a stupid instrument in a close space. Everyone knows this. The soldier knew it; he flinched back, and the flame swung wide, and it should have missed everything.

It caught the edge of the paper stack instead. Dry stock, winter-dried, waiting to become almanacs and receipts and the ordinary paperwork of ordinary lives. Flame went through it the way rumor goes through a frightened city.

"Out," the lieutenant shouted, suddenly no longer bored, suddenly just a man in a cellar filling with smoke. "Out, out —"

They shoved past us. In the crush my father stumbled, and a burning bale — lifted, God knows why, carried rather than left, some soldier's idiot instinct to remove the fire by holding it — came down against his side, against his arm, against the press he was still somehow touching, and then we were on the stairs and the cellar behind us was orange and roaring, and the last thing I saw of the inside of my home was the Albion press standing black and patient in the firelight with my father's hand still resting on its frame.

* * *

Outside, the street filled the way streets do — slowly, then all at once. Neighbors in nightclothes. The baker with his apron still on. Frau Lindqvist from the corner house, who had not spoken to us in two years and who now stood with her hands pressed to her mouth saying *oh, oh, oh* as though the syllables were all she had left in any language.

The soldiers formed a line and kept everyone back, and the fire climbed our staircase and ate our bedrooms and licked out the windows like a tongue tasting the morning, and I stood in the crowd holding my father's arm and watching thirty years go up in a column of black smoke that the whole city could read.

He was burned along the right side, forearm and ribs, where the bale had struck and rolled. Not deep — the surgeon later said the word *mercifully* as though mercy had anything to do with it — but he was fifty-eight years old and he had breathed smoke, and by the time the bell carts came he was gray-lipped and shivering, leaning on me with his whole weight, which is a thing fathers do not do, a reversal so complete it frightened me more than the fire.

"Your hands," he said, as I steered him toward the Lindqvists' doorway, because Frau Lindqvist had opened her door and was beckoning with both arms and crying without noise. "Astrid. Your hands. Show me your hands."

I showed him. Ink-stained, unburned. He nodded, once, and closed his eyes, and I understood that he had not been asking after my skin. He had been asking whether the hands that set the second copy — the copy that was not in the building, the copy that lived now in locked forms in a coffin-maker's consignment three streets away — whether those hands were still whole, still mine, still capable.

"Yes," I said. "They're fine."

"Then nothing of consequence burned today," he whispered, and fainted against my shoulder, and I screamed for the surgeon with a composure I did not feel and have never since located.

* * *

They took him to the hospital on the Ringstrasse, and I sat in the corridor outside the ward for six hours, watching nuns go in and out with basins, doing sums I could not stop doing. The shop: gone. The stock: gone. The press: gone, or worse than gone, dragged out of the cellar by soldiers and hauled away as *forfeit*, which meant it would print proclamations now, decrees, the lieutenant's lists. Our alphabet, conscripted.

The original manuscript: gone. Burned in the cellar with the paper, with the hymnal's twin, with the bed I had slept in since I was born. Somewhere in that ash were pages in Tomas Brandt's own hand, the only pages in his writing left in Velden, and I sat in that corridor and grieved them specifically, separately, the way you grieve one dead person in a war full of them.

And underneath the grief, patient as water finding a crack, another thought moved:

*The forms survive.*

Locked chases, wrapped in sacking, labeled in my own steady hand as coffin notices, sitting in a consignment shed by the river gate because my father — because my mother, years ago, who had taught him the trick of it — had built us a road out of this exact disaster before I was old enough to imagine needing one.

I did not let myself think it fully. Thinking it fully felt like tempting God, and I had seen enough of God's handwriting that week.

* * *

Near evening, a nun touched my sleeve and said I could go in for five minutes.

Father was propped on pillows, bandaged from wrist to elbow, his right side wrapped, his eyes clear and tired and entirely his own. He looked, absurdly, better than he had in months — as though the fire had burned away something else along with the shop, some residue of fear he'd been carrying since the census taker's first visit.

"They'll question you tomorrow," he said quietly. "Be dull. Be boring beyond endurance. You are a printer's daughter who lost everything; give them nothing but inventory."

"And you?"

"I am an old man who tripped in a cellar." His mouth twitched. "Even Hahn cannot make sedition out of a stumble."

I took his good hand. Through the window behind him, across the rooftops, I could see the smoke still rising from the Ring of Printers — thinner now, gray going to white, the city's wound closing over our particular piece of it.

"Papa," I said. "The consignment."

His fingers tightened on mine. He didn't answer. He didn't need to. In his eyes was the same expression I had seen on the press's iron frame a hundred times: the patience of a thing built to hold, letter by letter, against exactly this.

Somewhere across the city, in a shed by the river gate, a coffin-bound crate waited with a dead man's book sleeping inside it in lead.

All I had to do was get it out of Velden alive.
