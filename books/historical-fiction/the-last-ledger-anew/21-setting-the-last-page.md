## 21. Setting the Last Page

The Basel print shop smelled of ink and river damp, and it was the most beautiful thing I had ever known.

I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the whole point of what I did there. For eleven years I had loved a smell without knowing its full meaning — the oil and lead and lampblack of my father's shop in Velden, the smell of my childhood, of my mother's ghost in every sorting tray. But that smell had always been mixed with something else: fear, thin as vinegar, worked into everything. In Velden we printed with our shoulders hunched. We printed like thieves even when printing receipts.

Here, on the third floor of a building owned by a Swiss printer named Herr Vogel who asked no questions and charged fair rates, the same smell stood alone, unashamed. Ink was just ink. Paper was just paper. My shoulders, for the first time since the soldiers came to Velden, began to unknot themselves.

* * *

Herr Vogel was a broad, slow-moving man with ink permanently blackening the creases of his knuckles, as though he'd dipped his hands in the trade up to the wrist and never bothered to wash them off. He spoke German with a Basel accent thick enough to spread on bread, and when I arrived at his door with a coffin-bound consignment of locked type and a story I'd rehearsed for two hundred kilometers, he looked at me for a long moment and said:

"You're Lena Marchetti's daughter."

Not *who are you*. Not *what is this freight*. Her name. He knew her name before he knew mine.

"She's dead," I said, because I didn't know what else to say.

"Yes," he said. "So are half the people I've ever loved. Come upstairs. The press is cold but it warms quick."

That was all. No questions about the border, no questions about the soldiers, no questions about why a nineteen-year-old girl was traveling with a coffin full of set type disguised as a merchant's shipment of mourning goods. Later I understood: Vogel had been one of Mother's correspondents too. The network she and Brandt had built wasn't broken by her death or his exile. It was only sleeping, waiting for someone to walk through the right door with the right cargo.

My mother had been dead four years, and she was still arranging things for me. I sat down on a crate of French paper and cried until Vogel awkwardly patted my shoulder with an ink-stained hand, leaving a black smudge on my collar that I never washed out. It's on the coat I'm wearing now, if you must know. Some stains you keep.

* * *

The manuscript — Brandt's final work, the one he could not publish while living under any name at all — existed now in exactly two forms. The original pages had burned with the Marchetti shop, along with nearly everything else I loved. What survived was the copy I had set myself in the last desperate days, working by candlelight while soldiers drilled in the square below: the entire text composed in metal, locked into forms, hidden in plain sight among the ordinary job work of a print shop, then smuggled out in a coffin because my father, in one of his last lucid acts before the fever took him that winter, had arranged the consignment years earlier through a Basel undertaker who owed him a favor.

A book hidden inside a coffin. You may laugh. I have earned the right to laugh about it now, mostly.

The type was safe. The type was whole. And standing in Herr Vogel's shop on my first morning, looking at those heavy galley trays stacked like cordwood, I understood that I was not looking at a manuscript at all.

I was looking at a press run waiting to happen.

* * *

Brandt himself was dying in a room above a watchmaker's shop across the city, and I went to him before I touched a single letter. That chapter belongs elsewhere — the old man propped against pillows, his hands too shaky now to hold a pen, asking me for the one thing I hadn't expected. Not to publish him. To publish *her*.

"Your mother wrote half of it," he told me, his voice like paper being torn slowly. "Everyone assumes the essays were mine alone. Tomas Brandt, the great exile, the great voice." A cough that seemed to come from somewhere below the floorboards. "A voice needs a throat, child. She was the throat. She was the hand. Put her name on it. Let them say what they like about a dead woman writing sedition — she can't be arrested twice."

He died three weeks after the first copies came off Vogel's press. He never held one. I have regretted that every day since, and I have also decided it doesn't matter, which I suspect would please him more.

* * *

Now let me tell you about setting a title page, because you cannot understand what I felt unless you understand the mechanics, and I will not have my mother reduced to sentiment. She was a craftswoman. Sentiment was not her medium. Type was.

Every page of a book begins as a mirror. The compositor sets each letter backwards — *reversed*, we say, though reversed isn't quite right; it's *turned around*, made to face the way it will face once ink meets paper and the sheet is pressed and pulled away. You read the world left to right; you build it right to left. Every word you set is a word you cannot read until the moment of truth, when the form is inked, the paper laid, the bar pulled, and the impression revealed.

For eleven years I had set other people's words backwards. Receipts. Playbills. Wedding announcements for couples whose names I misread daily because my father's eyes were going and he'd dictate from memory, getting letters wrong, and I'd correct him without telling him. Sermons. Auction notices. Once, memorably, an advertisement for a laxative tonic that required me to set the phrase "gentle and thorough" forty times, which remains the funniest thing I have ever done for money.

But this page was different. This page I was setting forwards and backwards at once — setting it in the present with my hands and in the past with my heart, because every word of it had passed through my mother's mind first, twenty years ago, in letters she'd written to a man in exile, arguments refined over months of correspondence until neither of them could say anymore where her thinking ended and his began.

The title page. The largest type in the case.

In Velden we kept a font of 36-point roman that Father called "the monument," used only for funeral notices and the occasional civic proclamation nobody read. I had no such font here — Vogel's largest was 28-point, a handsome old-faced roman with serifs like small confident feet — and so the title would sit slightly smaller than I'd imagined. This troubled me for exactly one afternoon, and then I understood how little it mattered.

Because it was not the size of the type that mattered. It was the name.

I stood at the case with the stick in my left hand and the composing rule in my right, and I set it letter by letter, the way I'd been taught at seven years old, the way she'd been taught before me by her own father in a shop in Trieste:

L-E-N-A M-A-R-C-H-E-T-T-I

Backwards, in the stick. Each letter a small cold weight. Each letter facing away from me, toward the future.

I had set thousands of names. Names of brides, names of the deceased, names of aldermen and auctioneers and one memorable dog (a prize-winning spaniel; the owner insisted). Never once had my hands shaken. Compositors do not shake; shaking makes pie the type, mixing the sorts into an unreadable jumble, and a compositor who pies a form is a compositor looking for new work.

My hands shook now. I set the M wrong — upside down, its serifs pointing at the ceiling like a startled cat — caught it, corrected it, breathed, started again. L-E-N-A. Four letters. Fourteen years since anyone had spoken that name aloud in a print shop. The apprentices in Vogel's employ glanced over curiously; to them it was just a name, foreign-sounding, feminine, unremarkable.

To me it was the sound of a sorting tray sliding open at dawn. The smell of her shawl. The particular way she said *mind your quads, Astrid, loose spacing is loose thinking* — advice I have repeated to every apprentice I've trained since, usually while they roll their eyes, usually while I smile.

Below her name, smaller, the subtitle Brandt had dictated to me in his last weeks, his voice failing, making me lean close: *Being a true account of the rights of persons and the duties of power, set down by one who believed that the people who make history are the people who make the record.*

Set down by one who believed. Not *written by*. Set down. As in type. As in a compositor's work. He had chosen the verb deliberately, knowing I'd be the one to read it, knowing I'd understand that the book was claiming its own authorship as an act of printing, not merely of writing.

Tomas Brandt, dying, had written my mother a love letter in the grammar of our trade, and hidden it in a subtitle where the censors would never think to look.

* * *

It took six days to lock the full form of the first signature. Six days of Vogel's apprentices casting curious glances, of me sleeping on a pallet behind the paper stock, of eating bread and hard cheese and drinking coffee so strong it could have set type itself. On the seventh morning we inked the press — Vogel's iron handpress, older than either of us, its bed scarred with a century of work — and pulled the first proof.

You pull a proof quickly, carelessly, to check for errors. The impression comes up grey and uneven, and you squint at it sideways in the light, hunting for turned letters, for spaces too tight, for the thousand small failures a form can hide.

I found none. Not one. Eleven years of my father's training, of my mother's discipline inherited secondhand through his teaching, and the form was clean.

Vogel read the proof standing beside me, moving his lips slightly the way careful readers do. When he finished, he was quiet for a long time. Then he said, "Your mother argued this better than Brandt did."

"Sir?"

"The middle section. Everyone credits him for the argument about assemblies. That's her construction — you can tell by the structure, the way it builds from the particular to the general. He thought in thunderclaps. She thought in architecture." He tapped the proof with a blunt finger. "I published two of her pamphlets in 'thirty-nine, under a false imprint. Best-set copy I ever received from across the border. I always wondered whose hand it was."

"Mine," I said, before I could stop myself, and then corrected: "Hers. Ours. She taught my father, who taught me. Or maybe she just taught everyone and let us believe otherwise. She was good at that."

Vogel smiled for the first time since I'd met him. It transformed his face entirely, the way a pressed sheet transforms a form — suddenly legible, suddenly alive.

"Then we'll print five hundred," he said. "And when they're gone, five hundred more."

* * *

Five hundred became a thousand within the month. The pamphlet crossed the border folded inside bales of cloth, sewn into the linings of coats, tucked into the false bottoms of cheese wheels — Basel's smugglers were poets of concealment and charged accordingly. From there it moved hand to hand through Velden and beyond, copied by hand where copying was safer than printing, read aloud in kitchens with the shutters closed, passed from person to person like a coal passed carefully between cupped palms so it won't go out.

I know some of what happened next because letters reached me, and I know some of it because years later people told me to my face, and I know some of it I will never know, which is the fate of everyone who makes a thing and sends it into the world. You lose control of it at the moment of release. That is the price and the glory of the work. You set the type; you do not set the consequences.

But I heard this: that in Velden, in the spring after the burning, a copy was read aloud on the steps of the Rathaus by a student who was arrested for it, and that his arrest brought three hundred people to the square, and that the magistrate who ordered the arrests — not Hahn; Hahn was gone by then, swept out in the first wave of the upheaval he'd spent his life predicting wrongly — found that his own clerks refused to draw up the charges. I heard that the pamphlet was read in the barracks. I heard that a colonel's wife copied it out longhand and mailed it to her sister in the capital, which did more for its circulation than all of Vogel's presses combined.

And I heard — this from a Velden bookseller who found his way to Basel two years later, hat in hand, asking if I really was who people said — that in certain houses along the river, when the authorities weren't listening, people had begun calling it simply *her book*. Not Brandt's. Hers. Lena Marchetti's book, the one the dead woman wrote, the one the daughter set.

The daughter. People said that too. *The daughter set it herself.* As if I were a footnote. I resented it briefly, and then I stopped, because I understood that the footnote was the whole story. History is not written by the victors, whatever they tell you. History is *set* by whoever holds the stick and the rule, and the victors are simply the ones who got their forms locked first.

* * *

On the last night of the press run, after the final sheet had dried and the forms had been distributed — the type would be melted and recast, as all type eventually is; nothing in this trade is permanent except the impression — I stayed late in the shop alone. Vogel had gone home to his supper. The apprentices were gone. The press stood silent, its bed still faintly smelling of ink, and outside the window Basel glittered down at the river, and beyond the river, invisible in the dark, lay the border, and beyond the border, Velden, where my father's shop was ash and my mother's grave was tended by neighbors and a census taker I had not seen since the smoke cleared.

I took one last sheet from the stack. The best impression of the run, almost — not quite perfect, a slight kiss of extra ink on the L of her name, so that the first letter stood a shade darker than the rest, like a door held open.

I sat down at Vogel's desk with the sheet in front of me and read my mother's name in the largest type on the page, reading it forwards for the first time instead of backwards, reading it as strangers would read it, as history would read it, as *she* would have read it, standing in a shop doorway wiping ink from her fingers, squinting at a fresh proof with that critical tilt of her head she had, the tilt that meant *acceptable* — the highest praise she ever gave anything, including me, including herself, including, I believe, the entire institution of government.

"Acceptable," I said aloud to the empty shop.

Then I folded the sheet carefully, put it inside my coat, over my heart, where the ink stain from Vogel's consoling pat still marked the collar, and I blew out the lamp.

Somewhere south of the border, in a prison cell in Velden, a man who had once counted every door in the city was sitting in the dark keeping a promise. I owed him a visit. I owed him more than a visit.

I owed him a book.

And I had just finished printing it.
