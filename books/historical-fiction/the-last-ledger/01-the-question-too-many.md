## 1. The Question Too Many

The census came to Bellhaven on a Tuesday, as it had come every five years, and it came the way rain came to the harbor — inevitable, indifferent, and falling hardest on those with the least cover.

Wendell Okonkwo had been rehearsing for this day since he was fourteen. Not the way other boys rehearsed, dreaming of speeches they'd never give, but the way a clockmaker rehearses: precisely, silently, over and over, until the mechanism of the answer ran so smooth that no one thought to look inside it.

*Name: Wendell Okonkwo. Age: nineteen. Trade: apprentice clockmaker, guild-certified, third year. Residence: the shop and rooms above it, Quayside Row, belonging to Master Igeoma. Parents: deceased. Siblings: none living in the city.*

*None living in the city.* That was the sentence. Five years he had carried it, turning it over like a coin worn smooth, until it had no edges left to catch on anything. His sister Adaeze had not lived in the city for five years. The official record said she had been deported — put on the *Coriolis* with three hundred others and sent east to the resettlement provinces, where the government said the displaced were given land and purpose and a future. Wendell had learned to say it without flinching. *Deported.* A clean word. A word with paperwork behind it.

He was at the workbench when the knock came, three measured raps, and Master Igeoma opened the door to a man in a grey coat with a leather satchel and a face like a closed ledger.

"Census," the man said. "Aldous Vane, Bureau of Enumeration. I'll need a quarter hour of the household's time."

"Of course," Igeoma said, in the tone she used for everything — even, level, giving nothing away. She was a small woman with iron-grey hair pulled back so tightly it seemed to hold her thoughts in place, and she had run the clockmaker's guild's finest shop for thirty years by being exactly as warm as a brass fitting. "Wendell. The table."

Wendell cleared the table of its parts — a carriage clock in a hundred pieces, each wrapped in its own cloth, because you never left a mechanism open to strangers — and set out two chairs. His hands were steady. He noticed that they were steady and was grateful, the way you're grateful for a fuse that holds.

Vane sat, opened the satchel, and produced a folio of forms, a bottle of ink, a pen with a nib worn to a particular slant. He wrote left-handed, Wendell noticed, and he noticed it because noticing things was his trade. You could not repair a clock without first seeing how it was wrong.

"Name of household head?"

"Igeoma. Master Igeoma, guild register 4-118."

"Occupation?"

"Clockmaker. Master of the guild."

"And the apprentice?"

"Wendell Okonkwo. Third year."

Vane wrote. His hand was small and fast, and he did not look at the forms while he wrote them; he looked at the people. Wendell had expected a clerk's eyes — dull, moving down columns, already thinking of lunch. Vane's eyes were not dull. They were the eyes of a man reading a document in which he suspected an error, and Wendell felt, for the first time that morning, the small cold weight of attention.

"You'll answer for yourself, I think," Vane said to Wendell. "The Bureau prefers first-person testimony where the subject is of age. It reduces — transcription error."

"Of course," Wendell said.

"Name?"

"Wendell Okonkwo."

"Age?"

"Nineteen."

"Trade?"

"Apprentice clockmaker. Third year."

"Where were you born?"

"Bellhaven. The Harbor District."

"Parents?"

"Deceased. My father in the fever year, my mother after."

"I'm sorry," Vane said, and the strange thing was that it did not sound like form language. It sounded like a man who had said it ten thousand times and had not yet stopped meaning it. He made his note. "Siblings?"

Here it was. The coin, worn smooth. Wendell let the pause happen naturally — not too long, not too short, the pause of a young man doing ordinary arithmetic of family — and said, "One sister. Adaeze. She was deported in the resettlements. Five years ago."

"Deported," Vane repeated. He wrote it. Then he looked up. "On which ship?"

The question was not on the form. Wendell knew the forms; Igeoma had made him memorize them, every field, in the same way she made him memorize the strike trains of every clock they serviced, because *you cannot mend what you cannot map*. There was no field for the ship. There was name, age, disposition — deported, relocated, deceased, resident — and a registry number if the subject had one.

"The — the transport," Wendell said. "I don't recall the name. I was fourteen."

"Most people remember," Vane said mildly. "Ships have names the way people do. It's easier to grieve a name."

"I was fourteen," Wendell said again, and heard, a half-second too late, how the repetition sounded. Like a door shut too hard.

Vane's pen had not moved. It rested above the form, and his eyes rested above the pen, and for the length of three of Wendell's heartbeats the shop was very quiet — quiet in the particular way the shop was always quiet, which was not silence at all but the sound of forty clocks breathing. Wendell had grown up inside that sound. He knew its every texture. And he knew that Vane, sitting in the middle of it, had gone still in the way of a man listening for a tick that has gone missing.

"The *Coriolis*," Igeoma said, from the doorway, in her level voice. "It was the *Coriolis*. Wendell was a child; the paperwork fell to me as guardian. Ship's registry 7741, eastbound, spring convoy. It's all in the relocation notice, if the Bureau has mislaid it."

Something in the room loosened. Vane smiled — a small, correct smile, the smile of a man accepting a repair — and wrote *Coriolis*, and asked his remaining questions: wages, hours, whether the household kept firearms (it did not), whether it harbored persons not listed on the form (it did not), whether it had knowledge of sedition (it did not). He asked them all in the same mild voice, and Wendell answered them all in the same steady one, and by the time Vane packed his folio the moment by the table had been folded away, smoothed flat, filed.

But Wendell had watched the man's hands, and the man's hands had not been hurried.

* * *

He thought about it that evening while he worked, because he thought about everything while he worked; his hands needed occupation for his mind to run free, and the carriage clock needed its escape wheel polished, and polishing was the kind of work that left the mind unattended.

*Most people remember.* Why would a census taker say that? A census taker wanted the form filled. A census taker wanted the column complete and the household signed and the next door knocked on before dark. Vane had wanted the name of a ship.

Wendell set down the rouge cloth and looked across the workbench at Igeoma, who was doing the accounts, because she did the accounts every evening with the same pen and the same slanted hand and the same expression of a woman settling debts with the world one entry at a time.

"He asked about the ship," Wendell said.

"I heard."

"Why?"

"Because he's thorough." She did not look up. "Thorough men are the easiest kind to survive. They ask the question, you give the answer, they write it down, and they never think of you again. It's the lazy ones you want to fear — the ones who guess."

"It wasn't on the form."

"No." She made an entry. "It wasn't."

Wendell waited. Waiting was a skill Igeoma had taught him in his first year, along with filing and bluing screws: *the apprentice who talks to fill silence tells the master everything and learns nothing.* He waited, and the clocks breathed, and finally Igeoma set down her pen and looked at him with the expression she reserved for mechanisms that had come in broken by their owners' own hands.

"You did well today," she said. "You gave the answers. You gave them cleanly. And then he asked one question too many, and you flinched — not in your face, your face was fine — in your *timing*. You answered too fast the second time. People who are hiding something answer fast, because they've rehearsed, and people who are telling the truth answer slow, because they have to remember." She picked the pen back up. "Five years, Wendell. You've had five years to wear that coin smooth, and you flinched on the one question that wasn't on the form. Do you know why?"

"Because I didn't rehearse it."

"Because you rehearsed the *lie*," Igeoma said, "and not the *grief*. The lie is smooth. The grief is raw. He touched the grief, and the whole mechanism showed through the case for half a second." She went back to the accounts. "Go to bed. The observatory wants their striking train looked at Thursday, and you'll be going up with me."

"The observatory," Wendell said. "The master clock?"

"The master clock." She turned a page. "It's been losing time. The city sets itself by that clock — the docks, the courts, the newspapers, every church that's too poor to keep its own regulator. If it drifts, the whole city drifts with it. The Astronomer Royal wrote to the guild personally." A pause, one entry long. "Personally, Wendell. That doesn't happen for a dirty escapement."

* * *

He dreamed about the harbor that night, which he had not done in months, and woke with the taste of salt and coal smoke in his mouth.

Five years ago, on the night of the riots, he had been fourteen and locked in the cellar of this same shop while Igeoma stood upstairs with the door barred and a poker in her hand, and the city outside had made a sound he had no word for then and still had no word for now — not fire, not screaming, not bells, but all three braided into a single rope of noise that went on until near dawn. In the morning the smoke had sat over the harbor like a lid. In the morning the soldiers had come down Quayside Row in their clean grey coats, and by noon the newspapers had the story: a riot, an insurrection, foreign agitators, order restored, casualties regrettable but few, and the deportation ships already loading at the east quays.

Adaeze had been at the docks that night. She was nineteen then, five years older than Wendell, and she worked the tally house on the east quays, counting bales for the shipping lines, and she had gone to the rally the way everyone went to the rally — because the dockowners had cut the tonnage rates again, and because everyone she knew would be there, and because she was nineteen and the world was supposed to be argued with.

The official record said she was deported. The notice had come eleven days later, printed on Bureau paper: *Adaeze Okonkwo, detained under emergency provisions, transported east aboard the resettlement convoy. Correspondence may be directed through provincial channels.* Igeoma had written through provincial channels. Four letters over two years. Two came back unopened, marked *addressee relocated*. Two were never returned at all, which Igeoma said was worse.

But Wendell had been at the harbor that morning. He had slipped the cellar door while Igeoma slept her one hour of the night, and he had gone down to the water because fourteen-year-olds are built for exactly one kind of foolishness, and he had stood in the crowd of the living looking at what the tide was giving back. He had not found her. He had found other people's dead, laid in rows on the fish-slate, and men with clipboards moving among them, and he had heard — he had never in five years managed to unhear it — a tally clerk say to a soldier, not unkindly, wearily, the way a man says a thing he has said all night: *put her down as transported, the ships are still loading, it's cleaner for the paperwork.*

He had not known if *her* was Adaeze. That was the wound he lived inside: not a wound but a locked room, a case he could not open, a striking train that had never been allowed to finish its hour. He did not know. The record said deported. The tide had said nothing. And so he had done what the city did, what the whole country did after that year: he had taken the official word and built his life on top of it, the way you build a foundation over bad ground, and you never dig, and you never look down, and you call the building safe.

He lay in the dark above the shop and listened to the clocks, all forty of them, keeping their separate times, and he thought about a man with a worn pen nib who had asked the name of a ship.

*Most people remember.*

*It's easier to grieve a name.*

* * *

Thursday came grey, with a wind off the water that smelled of the fish market, and Wendell carried the tool roll up Observatory Hill while Igeoma carried the keys and said nothing, which was her way of saying that the climb was for thinking.

The observatory sat at the top of the hill like a chess piece — white dome, black railings, and beneath it the clock room, where the master clock lived in a case of black walnut two stories tall, a regulator of the guild's own pattern, built sixty years ago by a master whose name was on a plaque and whose escapement was still, the guild liked to say, the truest thing in Bellhaven. The Astronomer Royal met them at the door: a dry, courteous man with ink on his fingers, who shook their hands and said, "It's been losing eleven seconds a day for three weeks. I've regulated the sidereal work around it. I'd be obliged if you'd find out why it's lying to me."

"Clocks don't lie," Igeoma said. "They're the only things that can't."

"Then find out what's making it lie anyway," the Astronomer Royal said, and left them to it.

The case was locked; Igeoma had the guild key. Inside, the great mechanism rose through the dark like the skeleton of something patient — the weight lines running up into the tower, the barrel, the going train, the striking train with its hammers and its graduated bells, and the long pendulum swinging its slow, indifferent arc, one second out and one second back, the heartbeat of the city.

Wendell climbed the maintenance ladder into the works with a lamp, and for an hour he was happy. This was the truth of him, underneath everything the census touched: he was happy inside a clock. The world above the works was forms and grey coats and questions with edges, but in here there was only cause and effect, and every wrong thing had a reason, and every reason could be found. He checked the suspension. He checked the pallets. He checked the crutch, the impulse, the beat. The going train was clean.

It was the striking train that was wrong.

He found it at the top, where the warning wheel ran, and he held the lamp close and went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the draft from the tower. A washer had been added to the arbor — a brass washer, unmarked, not guild stock — and it was the wrong thickness, and it was placed so that it would let the strike train drift out of count, losing a beat every third hour, accumulating error like debt. It was elegant. It was small. It was the work of someone who understood the mechanism completely.

"Master," he called down. "Come and look at this."

Igeoma climbed, looked, and was silent for a long time.

"That's not wear," Wendell said. "That's not a repair. That's — someone did this. Recently. The brass is bright."

"Yes."

"Why would anyone sabotage a clock to lose eleven seconds a day? If you wanted to stop it, you'd stop it."

Igeoma took the lamp from him and held it closer, and the light moved across the striking train, across the wheels and the levers and the count wheel, and then it stopped, because Igeoma's hand had stopped, and Wendell watched her face do something he had never seen it do in five years of apprenticeship. It went carefully, deliberately blank — the face of a woman closing a case door.

"Hand me the long tongs," she said.

He handed them to her. She reached past the warning wheel, deep into the case, into the space behind the count wheel where the light of his lamp had not quite reached, and she drew out — with the tongs, delicately, the way you draw out something that might be live — a slim book.

It was bound in grey board, the color of the Bureau's coats, and it was thin, and it was wrapped in an oilcloth that had kept the clock's oil and the tower's damp off its pages. Igeoma held it in both hands and did not open it. The pendulum swung behind her, one second out, one second back.

"Master," Wendell said. "What is it?"

"I don't know," Igeoma said, and Wendell knew, from the sound of her voice, that she was lying, and that she was lying badly, and that she was a woman who had just found something she had been afraid of finding for a very long time.

She opened it anyway. The lamp was in Wendell's hand, so it was Wendell who raised the light, and Wendell who saw the pages: columns, ruled in a careful left-slanted hand — the same slant as the pen nib worn at a particular angle, the same hand that had written *Coriolis* on a census form two days before.

Names. Dates. And in the third column, where the official forms said *deported* or *relocated*, a different word, written over and over, in a hand that had begun controlled and grown cramped and urgent down the years:

*Drowned. Drowned. Shot. Drowned. Missing, vessel lost. Drowned.*

"Master Igeoma," Wendell said, and his voice came out in the small, steady tone he used for answers he had rehearsed, except that this one he had never rehearsed, and it shook anyway. "Whose handwriting is this?"

Below the lamp, on the open page, a name in the left-hand column caught the light. A name he knew. A name that was not his sister's — not yet, he had not got that far — but a name from the east quays, from the tally house, from the world before the lid of smoke.

Igeoma closed the book.

"Get your coat," she said. "We're going to the census office."
