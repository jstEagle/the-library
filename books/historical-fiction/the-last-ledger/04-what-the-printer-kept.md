## 4. What the Printer Kept

The cellar under Ademola Press smelled of ink and wet stone, and Sefu Ademola had learned to read both the way other men read weather. Ink told him what he could still say. Water told him what the city was trying to drown.

He was setting type by lamplight — a funeral notice, innocuous, for a woman who had not died and whose name would mean nothing to any soldier who kicked the door in — when the knock came. Three slow raps, then two quick ones. The signal Vane had taught him, back when Vane still came himself.

Except Vane was dead. Drowned, they said. Fished out of the harbor with his ledger book missing and his boots on, which no census taker ever wore into water.

Sefu set down the composing stick and stood very still.

Three slow raps again. Patient. Not a soldier's knock. Soldiers knocked like they were owed something.

He opened the door on the chain and saw a boy — no, not a boy, a young man, narrow-shouldered, ink-stained to the elbow, holding his hat against his chest like it might protect him. Wendell Okonkwo. The Igeoma apprentice. Sefu knew the face from guild functions he'd attended only to count who else was counting.

"You shouldn't be here," Sefu said through the gap.

"Mr. Vane sent me." The young man's voice cracked on the name. "Or — he didn't send me. He's dead. But I have something of his."

"Show me your hands."

Wendell turned them over, palms out, like a man proving he hadn't stolen bread. They were clean except for the oil under the nails that every clockmaker carried like a birthmark.

Sefu closed the door, worked the chain off, and let him down the stairs into the dark.

* * *

The boy held the ledger the way you hold a sleeping child you're afraid will wake screaming. Small, slim, bound in gray board with no title. Sefu had seen its twin — or half of it — a hundred times over the past year, but seeing the original in the flesh made his mouth go dry anyway.

"Where did you get this?"

"The observatory clock. It was wedged inside the case, behind the striking train." Wendell's eyes were red-rimmed and steady at the same time, which took a particular kind of courage. "The mechanism was sabotaged. Deliberately. Someone wanted the clock stopped so someone else would be summoned to find it."

"And they chose you."

"They chose whoever the guild would send. Master Igeoma sent me because I'm cheap and expendable." He said it without self-pity, just as fact, and Sefu decided in that moment he liked him. "The sabotage was recent. Fresh tool marks. Whoever hid it wanted it found before the anniversary."

Sefu pulled a stool over. "Open it."

Wendell did, and there it was: the hand Sefu knew better than his own father's. Aldous Vane wrote like a man drawing maps of a country he loved and could never live in — small, exact letters, each name given its full dignity, dates ruled straight without a guide.

"I've seen this writing," Wendell said quietly. "On my census form. Five years running. He asked about my sister once." His jaw tightened. "I lied to him."

"He expected you to lie. Everyone lies to the census. That's why he built this one." Sefu turned pages with a care that surprised even himself. Names. Dates. Causes. *Deported, ship *Meridian*, manifest 44 — actually drowned, harbor, 12 October.* *Relocated, district nine — actually died in custody, Bellhaven gaol.* Page after page after page, the official record peeled back like rotten plaster to show the wall underneath.

"How much do you know about Mr. Vane?" Sefu asked.

"That he counted people for a living. That he drowned."

"That he counted people," Sefu said, "and couldn't sleep afterward."

* * *

He made tea on the spirit stove because his mother had taught him that no hard conversation should happen empty-handed, and because his hands needed something to do while he decided how much truth a stranger could carry.

"My mother worked the docks thirty years," Sefu said. "She buried my father after the first crackdown, when I was nine, and she spent the rest of her life keeping a list. Not on paper — in her head. Every neighbor taken, every boat that went out heavy and came back light. She'd recite it some nights like prayers. When she died, the list died with her, and I swore that would never happen again."

Wendell wrapped his hands around the tin cup and waited. Good listener. Clockmakers probably had to be.

"Aldous Vane came to my shop fourteen months ago. Bought a pamphlet he clearly had no intention of reading — some tract about tariff reform, chosen specifically to bore anyone who searched his effects. Then he came back. And back. He'd sit where you're sitting and drink tea and tell me things he wasn't supposed to know. Which families on the deportation lists had actually boarded. Which ships sailed under weight. Which parish registers had been 'corrected' by order of the commission." Sefu sipped his own tea. "I thought at first he was testing me. A government man, sniffing out sedition. But he kept coming, and he kept paying for the pamphlets, and eventually he put a page on my counter and asked if my press could duplicate a hand this fine."

"He was building a copy here."

"Half of one. We divided the work. He gave me entries in batches — never the whole book, never all at once. If either of us was taken, the other would hold half the truth, enough to matter, not enough to hang the city on. I set the names in type and printed sheets. Two hundred copies of everything, hidden in—" he gestured at the cellar walls, the paper stock, the bales of rag waiting to be pulped, "—places a soldier doesn't look because a soldier doesn't read."

"Two hundred copies," Wendell repeated. Something in his face loosened and reassembled. "So it isn't only the one ledger."

"It was never only the one ledger. That's what Vane understood that nobody else in this city has understood yet. You can burn a book. You can drown a man. But if the truth is set in type, it becomes a thing that makes more of itself. Like life." Sefu smiled without much humor. "That's why they'll come for printers first, when they come. They always know."

* * *

Wendell set down his cup. "Then we should assume they're already looking. The clock was sabotaged to be found. Someone wanted the ledger moving again — or wanted to see who moved it."

Sefu had thought the same thing and hated it. "Vane hid it in the clock years ago. I know that much — he told me once that the safest archive in Bellhaven is the one the city winds every day. But then he started pulling documents together for the inquiry testimony he hoped to give. Shipping manifests. A naval order he wouldn't describe to me, only say that when I saw it I'd stop sleeping. He was going to come in from the cold at the anniversary. Read the whole thing into the record himself, with his government credentials standing behind it, so no one could call it sedition."

"And instead he's floating in the harbor."

"Instead he's floating in the harbor wearing boots." Sefu watched the boy absorb that. "Which means either he slipped, or someone helped him slip, and either way the people who killed him don't know where all his papers are. If they knew about the ledger, they'd have torn the clock apart themselves. So they're searching blind. And now—"

"Now I've gone and moved it," Wendell finished.

"You've gone and moved it." Sefu leaned forward. "Tell me exactly who knows you have it."

"Master Igeoma. She told me to return it and forget it."

"She's right, and you won't, and neither would she in your place, whatever she says now." Sefu had met Igeoma twice; she had the look of a woman who had already survived one purge by being careful, and being careful was a skill he respected even as he planned around it. "Anyone else?"

"No one. I came straight here. Your knock — the signal — I found it written in the ledger's back cover. Vane left instructions. Names, addresses, a code. Yours was first."

Sefu went cold in a way the cellar damp couldn't account for. "Show me."

Wendell turned to the back board. There, in pencil, faint as breath: a sequence of knocks, an address on Corder Street, and beneath it a single word in Vane's hand: *Ademola — trust.*

"He trusted you," Wendell said. "Before he died, he trusted you with the map of everything."

"And I'm going to honor it," Sefu said, "by tearing that page out and burning it tonight. Instructions die with their author. From here on, we keep nothing written that isn't set in type and multiplied, and we keep nothing spoken that can be repeated under pressure. New rule. Learn it fast."

Wendell nodded slowly. "You've done this before."

"I've been raided twice and burned out once. My father taught typesetting; the government taught me the rest." Sefu stood and crossed to the far wall, where a rack of standing type reached nearly to the ceiling. He pressed a panel that looked like every other panel, and it swung inward on oiled hinges onto a cavity the width of a coffin. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay bundles of printed sheets, hundreds of them, each headed with a line Sefu had composed himself:

*A True Census of the City of Bellhaven, Being a Record of Those the Official Count Erased.*

Wendell made a sound low in his throat, half reverence, half grief.

"Half a duplicate," Sefu said. "Vane's batches, through last spring. Everything since — the winter deportations, the executions after the uprising — exists only in his hand, in the book you're holding. That's why he sabotaged the clock. He was out of time to finish the copying himself, so he passed the debt to whoever came next." He looked at the young clockmaker steadily. "It happened to be you. I'm sorry for that. And I'm glad of it. Both things are true."

"What do we do now?"

"Now? Now we finish it. You bring me the new entries in installments, small pieces, different routes, never the whole book on the street. I set them and print them. And then—" Sefu tapped the ledger's cover, "—we find out what Vane meant to do at the anniversary, because whatever it was, it was going to be loud, and loud is the only thing this government hears."

* * *

They worked until the lamp burned low. Wendell read entries aloud in a voice that grew steadier with each name, and Sefu set them in type, letter by letter, the way his mother had recited her list — each name given its full weight, none rushed, none skipped. Around three in the morning the boy stopped mid-name and sat with his head in his hands.

"Amara Okonkwo," he said finally. "Page forty-one. Deported aboard the *Meridian*, manifest forty-four. Actually drowned, twelfth of October, the harbor riots." He looked up, eyes wet and furious. "My sister. I told Vane she was deported. I gave the safe answer, five years of safe answers, and he wrote down the true one anyway. He knew. He must have known, and he never said a word to me, just put her in the book where she belongs."

"That was his kindness," Sefu said. "He collected the truth and let the living keep their lies until they were strong enough to need the truth. It's how most of us survive. You lie in the daytime and grieve at night, and someday the two halves meet." He reached across the workbench and closed the boy's fingers around the ledger. "They've met for you now. That's what this is. Not a book. A meeting."

Wendell held on.

When the sky over the harbor grays toward morning, Sefu walked him out through the coal chute into the alley, checked the street both ways, and pressed a folded proof sheet into his pocket — a single name, freshly printed, as a token and a test.

"One more thing," Sefu said. "There's a woman you need to meet. Widow named Okafor — her husband's in your book too. She keeps the parish records the commission hasn't gotten to falsifying yet, because she sleeps in the vestry and the curate owes her money. Vane's second source. And there's a sailor who jumped ship rather than crew a deportation run — he remembers faces, dozens of them, boarding the *Meridian*. Between the three of us and your master's tower, we might just build something Vane would recognize."

"A congregation," Wendell said, and Sefu laughed despite everything, because the boy had no idea yet how literally he'd spoken.

"Go home by the long way," Sefu told him. "And Wendell — if anyone follows you, don't come back here. Come to the clock tower. If the city's going to wind a hiding place every day, we may as well use it."

Wendell vanished up the alley with the ledger flat against his chest, and Sefu barred the door, fed the pencil page to the stove, and watched the instructions curl and go dark.

Through the cellar's high window, above the rooftops, the observatory tower stood black against the graying sky. In a few hours its bell would ring the hour across the whole city, the way it always had, the way it always would.

Sefu looked at it for a long moment, thinking about clocks, and towers, and how many ears a bell had — and then he went to pull type for the next name.
