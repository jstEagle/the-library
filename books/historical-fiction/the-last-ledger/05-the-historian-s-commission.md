## 5. The Historian's Commission

The letter appointing Cordelia Marsh to the Historical Commission arrived on a Tuesday, in an envelope so fine she could feel the watermark through her fingertips. She read it twice at her kitchen table before dawn, coffee going cold beside her, because the second reading was where the work always lived — not in what a document said, but in what it had been made to say.

*By order of the Provisional Authority for the Restoration of Civic Order,* it began, and already she was taking notes. *Provisional.* Eleven months after the quelling of the dockworkers' uprising, and the government still could not bring itself to call itself permanent. *Restoration.* As though something had been broken that they intended to fix, rather than something seized that they intended to keep. Language was the first archive. She had built her career on knowing that.

The substance was simple. Dr. Cordelia Marsh, formerly of the Royal Institute, author of two monographs on municipal record-keeping in the northern provinces, was hereby commissioned to produce *a definitive and authoritative account of the events of last autumn in the port city of Bellhaven*, to be completed and sealed by the anniversary of the uprising. She would have access to all state archives, a stipend, four clerks, and rooms in the Custom House.

She would also have instructions.

They came separately, delivered by a man who did not give his name — a gray functionary with gray eyes who sat in her parlor with his hat on his knees and spoke as though reciting a shopping list. The account should establish that the riots were instigated by foreign agitators. That the loss of life, while regrettable, was confined to the disturbances themselves and to necessary measures taken to restore order. That the total number of deaths was forty-one, a figure already published and therefore beyond revisiting. That the deportation program — he used the word *resettlement* — had been conducted humanely and in accordance with statute.

"And if the documents contradict this?" Cordelia asked.

He looked at her then, the first time his eyes had left his hat. "Then the documents are wrong."

Most people would have heard a threat. Cordelia heard a methodology. He was telling her precisely how the official history would be constructed: not by lying about the sources, but by ruling on them. A historian did not need to burn a document. She needed only to declare it unreliable, and burning became unnecessary. The archive would do the work itself, quietly, forever.

"I accept," she said.

The gray man blinked. She suspected he had prepared arguments. It was one of the small disappointments of working for governments that they so rarely required you to be persuaded — only to be competent.

* * *

Her first week in the Custom House rooms, she did nothing but read.

This was the part her clerks found maddening. They were soldiers' clerks, mostly, seconded from the Provost's office, and they expected a purge: carts brought round, ledgers hauled out, flames in the yard. That was how previous regimes had handled inconvenient paper, and it was why previous regimes' histories were so easy to unpick. Ash left a shape. Anyone who dug into the charred edges of an archive could reconstruct what had been removed, the way a man's silhouette remained burned into a wall behind him.

Cordelia did not intend to leave silhouettes.

"Bring me the accession registers," she told her senior clerk, a dry woman named Hobbes who had served three administrations without visible change of loyalty. "Every register, every year, back thirty years. And the finding aids. I want to know what the archive says it holds before I look at a single item."

Hobbes raised one eyebrow, which Cordelia would learn was her version of open rebellion. "You're cataloguing, ma'am? I understood we'd be removing things."

"We will be. Eventually." Cordelia spread the first register across her desk. "But think, Hobbes. If we remove a document, what happens?"

"It's gone."

"It's *absent*. And absence is legible. A gap in a run of shipping manifests tells any future scholar exactly where to dig. No — the document stays. What changes is its classification." She tapped the page. "A manifest recording the departure of the transport *Meridian* becomes a manifest recording the departure of the transport *Meridian*, cargo miscellaneous, condition at loading unverified, provenance doubtful. We don't destroy the truth. We bury it under qualifications until no honest reader can lift it out. The lie isn't in the archive. The lie is in the difficulty."

Hobbes wrote that down. Cordelia pretended not to notice, but later she would find the sentence copied into Hobbes's private notebook, underlined twice, and she would wonder for a long time whether it had been recorded as doctrine or as evidence.

By the end of the month she had reclassified eleven hundred documents. Not destroyed — *recontextualized*. Parish burial registers relating to the harbor districts were moved to a restricted shelf pending "verification of ecclesiastical authority." Shipping manifests from the autumn convoys were flagged for "tonnage inconsistencies requiring reconciliation," which no clerk would ever be given time to reconcile. Three boxes of coroner's inquests were consolidated with unrelated files from a decade earlier, so that anyone searching for them would find them technically present and practically unreachable.

It was, she admitted to herself on the fourth night, walking home through streets still scarred with musket pocks, masterful work. If she had encountered this archive as a scholar, she would have spent two years fighting through its fog, and she might — *might* — have surfaced with fragments. Most scholars would surface with nothing, and worse, with the conviction that there was nothing to find.

That was the horror of it, and she did not look away from the horror, because looking away was a luxury she had decided she could not afford. She was not a fool. She knew what she was building. She had stood in the Institute's library at twenty-four and sworn, over her dissertation, that history was a debt owed to the dead, and here she was at thirty-one refinancing that debt on behalf of the men who had created the corpses.

But there was her brother's pension to consider, and her mother's house, and the plain fact that commissions went to people who said yes and gallows went, eventually, to people who said no. She had chosen competence over courage years ago, in smaller ways, each one reasonable, and a life was nothing but a ledger of small reasonable entries summing to a total you never agreed to sign.

She worked. It was what she was for.

* * *

The trouble began, as trouble always did for Cordelia, with an inconsistency.

She had requested the maintenance logs of the observatory clock almost as an afterthought — a completeness exercise. The master clock governed the harbor bells; the harbor bells had marked the hours of the uprising; a definitive account would naturally establish the timeline against the city's most reliable instrument. She drafted the request herself, expecting a routine reply.

What came back was a single sheet from the Bellhaven Clockmakers' Guild, signed by one Master Igeoma, and it was wrong in three ways.

First, it was late. Guild records showed the observatory clock serviced quarterly, without exception, for forty years. The last service entry predated the uprising by five weeks. Nothing since.

Second, it was thin. Every other log in the guild's series ran to pages — parts replaced, weights rewound, escapements adjusted, initials of the apprentice assigned. This one was a paragraph. *Mechanism inspected. Regulation performed. No defects noted.* Signed with initials that matched no apprentice listed in the guild rolls for that quarter.

Third — and this was the detail that kept her at her desk past midnight, lamp trimmed low, the Custom House empty around her — the ink was different. She held the page to the light the way she'd been taught to examine charters, and the signature had been written in a fresher black than the body text. Someone had taken an old log page and added a new entry to it. Recently. Within the last month, perhaps within the last fortnight.

Someone was maintaining the appearance of maintenance on the most public clock in Bellhaven, and doing it badly enough that Cordelia could see the seams.

Now, a charitable reading existed. Wartime disruption. A dead or reassigned apprentice. Clerks everywhere had been improvising since the quelling. She wrote down the charitable reading in her working notes, in fair hand, because her notes might someday be read by others, and then beneath it, in pencil, she wrote the uncharitable one: *someone needed the clock to appear untouched.*

Why would a person need a clock to appear untouched?

Because they had touched it.

She sat back and looked at the ceiling beams of the Custom House, and let her mind do the thing it did best, which was to refuse comfort. The observatory clock was not merely a timepiece. It was the city's reference instrument — every other clock in Bellhaven was set by it, every bell timed to it, every almanac and shipping schedule derived from its strike. Whoever controlled the master clock controlled, in a sense too subtle for most people to notice, the city's agreement about when things happened. An account of the uprising rested on a timeline. A timeline rested on bells. And the bells rested on whatever was inside that case on the tower.

If someone had altered the mechanism itself — shifted the strike, delayed the chime, even briefly stopped it — then every timestamp in every deposition taken that night was negotiable. Witnesses swore the shooting began at the ninth hour. Change what the ninth hour sounded like, and you changed the testimony without touching a word of it.

Or — and this possibility arrived quietly, the way the worst ones did — the clock case was simply a good hiding place. Large, dark, opened only by guild hands, visited rarely. If a person wanted to conceal papers in Bellhaven, in the weeks after the quelling, when houses were searched and presses smashed, a hollow among the gears of the master clock was nearly perfect. Who searches a clock? Who dares stop it long enough to try?

Cordelia wrote both hypotheses side by side and disliked them equally, because both meant the same thing: there was something in that tower that someone believed worth hiding from her commission. And things worth hiding from a commission whose conclusions were already fixed were, by definition, things that contradicted those conclusions.

She was being handed, free of charge, a map of her own employers' lies. All she had to do was follow it.

* * *

"You want the logs again," Hobbes said the next morning. It was not a question. She stood in the doorway of Cordelia's office with the day's requisitions, and her face had the particular flatness of a woman deciding how much to say.

"I want the clock itself examined. By our own man, not guild hands. A full inspection of the case, the movement, the weight channels. Quietly."

"That'll cause friction with the guild. They're particular about their tower."

"The guild can be particular at the anniversary ceremony, when the commission formally rededicates the instrument. Until then, I'd like my inspector to go up on a maintenance pretext. A regulation check. Nothing that requires stopping the works." Cordelia looked up from her desk. "Is there a reason the guild would object, Hobbes?"

The older woman was quiet a moment. Thirty years of serving administrations had taught her exactly how much silence was safe, and she spent none of it carelessly. "There's talk," she said finally. "Guild talk. That Vane's death sits oddly."

"Aldous Vane. The census taker."

"Drowned off the north pier in the spring. Body never recovered, which happens, but—" Hobbes set the requisitions down on the corner of the desk, aligned them square, a habit of hers. "Vane was a careful man. Careful men don't slip off piers. And the census office was sealed the same week, records impounded, and half the impounded records have gone missing from the Provost's store since. My cousin's husband is a storekeeper there. He counts the boxes every month. The count changes."

Cordelia felt the familiar cold pleasure of a pattern assembling itself — the same pleasure she imagined a clockmaker felt when a train of gears suddenly meshed. A dead census taker. A sealed office. Vanishing records. A clock maintained in fiction only. Somewhere in Bellhaven, someone had gathered truths the government needed buried, and someone else had begun killing and sealing to keep them buried, and now her commission — her beautiful, well-funded, officially sanctioned commission — was going to walk straight into the middle of it with lanterns lit.

She should have felt afraid. She noted, distantly, that she ought to feel afraid, the way one notes a legal formality. What she actually felt was narrower and more shameful: interest. After years of writing about archives instead of inside them, the hunt had come to her door wearing official seals, and some part of her — the part that had sworn oaths over dissertations, the part she kept locked in the same restricted shelves where she put everything else inconvenient — that part was awake for the first time in years.

"Draft the inspection order," she said. "And Hobbes — find me the name of the apprentice who last genuinely serviced that clock. Not the initials on the log. The real one."

"There may not be a real one."

"There's always a real one. That's rather the point of me."

Hobbes went out. Cordelia turned back to her working notes and, after a moment, added a line beneath the two hypotheses, in pencil, in her smallest hand:

*If the clock has been touched, find out by whom, and ask yourself what they knew that was worth the risk. People hide paper for two reasons: to sell it later, or because the paper protects someone. Watch which kind these are.*

Below that, after a pause long enough that the lamp guttered once, she wrote a third line, which was not a hypothesis at all:

*Forty-one dead. Begin there. Count backward.*

* * *

The inspector went up the tower on Thursday. He came down in under an hour, reporting the mechanism sound, the case clean, nothing amiss — and Cordelia listened to his report with full attention and complete disbelief, because he had climbed sixty feet, spent fifty minutes alone with the most important clock on the coast, and returned with nothing but assurances, and no honest inspection in history had ever produced nothing but assurances.

Either the clock was genuinely clean, or her inspector had been gotten to, or he simply hadn't known where to look — and a man who didn't know where to look inside a clock case was a man who had never opened one, which meant she had sent a soldier to do a craftsman's work.

She dismissed him courteously, thanked him for his diligence, and waited until his boots faded on the stairs before opening her notebook.

Under the day's date she wrote: *I need someone who understands mechanisms. Someone who has had their hands inside that clock. Find out who services the observatory works — the true name, the apprentice, whoever touches those gears. Then decide whether they are an obstacle or a witness.*

She underlined *witness* twice, closed the book, and sat for a while in the lamplight, listening to the distant sound of the harbor bells marking the hour — bells governed, at this very moment, by whatever was hidden in the dark above the city, keeping a time she did not yet know.

Somewhere in Bellhaven, someone was counting the dead in secret. Cordelia Marsh intended to find the count. What she had not yet asked herself — what she would not ask herself until it was far too late to be anything but an answer — was what she would do when the count turned out to include a name she knew.
