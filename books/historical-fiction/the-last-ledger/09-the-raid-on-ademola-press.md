## 9. The Raid on Ademola Press

The knock came at half past four in the morning, and Sefu Ademola knew before he opened the door that it was not a customer.

Customers did not knock at half past four. Customers came at noon with wedding announcements and funeral notices, at dusk with pamphlets they pretended to be embarrassed by, sliding coins across the counter with their eyes fixed anywhere but his face. Nobody honest knocked in the dark. His mother had taught him that, back when she was alive — *the soldiers come in the dark, boy, and so does grief* — and she had known both by their footsteps.

He was already moving when the second knock landed. Not toward the door. Toward the cellar.

The duplicate lay under the floorboard beneath the composing stone, wrapped in oilcloth where he'd hidden it three nights ago after Wendell's visit, after the two of them had sat up until dawn cross-checking names against parish registers until the candles drowned in their own wax. Half a counter-census. Four hundred and eleven entries in Vane's cramped hand, verified against everything Sefu could reach: baptismal rolls, burial fees, harbor manifests, the wage books of shipping firms that thought paper was safe because it was boring. He had been a printer's son before he was anything else, and he knew what paper could do. It could outlive empires. It could also burn.

Boots on the cobbles outside. Many boots. The particular rhythm of men who had rehearsed this.

Sefu pried up the board, took the ledger bundle, and hesitated one full second over the question every printer dreads: what do you save? The type was his father's. Thirty years of his family's labor sat in those cases — Baskerville and Caslon, the small caps his mother had cut herself, the ornaments she'd cast from melted candlesticks. The press itself, the old Albion, had printed his first word and his mother's last pamphlet.

He left all of it.

Not because he didn't love it. Because Vane hadn't died for type. Because somewhere in the harbor mud there were people whose names existed now in only two places in the world, and one of those places was under his feet, and the other was in the hands of an apprentice clockmaker who didn't yet understand what he was carrying.

He took the bundle down into the cellar as the front door came off its hinges above him.

* * *

They announced themselves, which surprised him later. He had expected silence, or the pretense of a warrant read too fast to hear. Instead a voice like wet gravel called through the shop: "Ademola! By order of the Commission on Public Records, open in the name of the inquiry!"

Cordelia Marsh's commission. Of course. She would want it done properly. That was the thing about her that Sefu had learned from the dockworkers who watched her office — she was not a looter. She was worse. She was a believer. Looters take things and sell them; believers take things and burn them clean, with paperwork.

He heard them moving through the shop above. A case of type went over with a sound like rain on tin. Someone laughed. Then the voice again, closer, muffled by the floorboards: "Check the cellar."

There was no time for cleverness. The cellar had one way out — a coal chute that fed the alley behind the print shop, half-blocked since winter, and beyond it the drainage culvert that ran under Tanner's Row toward the harbor. His mother had used it twice during the uprising, ferrying pamphlets and once a bleeding union man whose name Sefu still said to himself some mornings, the way other men said prayers.

He went through the chute headfirst, the ledger bundle clutched against his chest, and the coal dust took his breath and gave it back tasting of a decade of dead fires. Behind him, the cellar door splintered. Light swung down the stairs in a long yellow blade.

He was in the culvert when they found the empty hiding place.

* * *

The burning started an hour later, and Sefu watched it from the roof of the rope-walk on Tanner's Row, close enough to feel the heat push against his face like a hand.

They did it in daylight, which told him everything. Night raids were shame; day raids were instruction. They brought the type out in crates and stacked it in the street, and then an officer — Sefu would remember his face forever, young, bored, careful — poured lamp oil over thirty years of his family and struck a match, and the Caslon went up in a bloom of orange that turned the morning air the color of the inside of a bell.

His hands hurt. He looked down and understood why: he had burned them coming through the culvert, or after — grabbing the edge of a steam pipe in the dark, hauling himself past a junction where the drain glowed near some boiler main. The palms were striped red and white, blistering along the lines of them, and he realized he had not felt it. Grief does that. It takes the body's messages and marks them *return to sender.*

A neighbor stood beside him on the roof — old Mrs. Ferro, who had bought her obituary notice from him in advance two years ago because she liked to be organized. She said nothing. After a while she put her shawl around his shoulders, and they watched the smoke go up straight in the windless morning, a black column you could see from the observatory hill, and Sefu thought: *they've made me a signal fire.* Let the whole city read it.

In the street below, soldiers carried out the partial duplicate they'd found — the false trail he'd left in the flat file drawer, forty pages of deliberately corrupted entries, wrong dates, shifted parishes, a decoy built in three sleepless nights. Let Cordelia Marsh spend a month discovering that the dead of St. Brigid's had supposedly perished in a parish across town. Let her doubt her own sources. Every hour she spent on lies was an hour the true ledger stayed hidden.

It was a small victory. It cost everything he owned. He held both truths at once, the way you hold a hot iron, and went to find Wendell Okonkwo.

* * *

The clock tower smelled of oil and cold brass and, faintly, of pigeons — a smell Sefu decided, climbing the stairs behind Wendell in the blue dark of evening, that he could live inside for a while.

Wendell had met him at the guild's side door without questions, which told Sefu the apprentice had already grown past the boy who'd come to his shop a week ago clutching a dead man's ledger like it might detonate. There were questions in his face — there would be time for them — but he had looked at Sefu's bandaged hands and simply stepped aside and let him in. Master Igeoma had cleaned and dressed the burns herself, her fingers precise and unsentimental, and said only: "Hands heal. Type doesn't. You did the arithmetic correctly." From Igeoma, Sefu suspected, that was a eulogy and a knighthood in one sentence.

Now they sat in the room behind the clock faces, among the great gears' slow breathing, and Wendell lit a single lamp and turned it low.

"They took the decoy," Sefu said. "Forty pages of rubbish. If Marsh is half as good as they say, she'll know within weeks it's false. But weeks is what we bought."

"And your real copy?"

Sefu unwrapped the oilcloth from around his chest, where he'd carried it all day beneath a borrowed coat, and set it on the workbench between them. Four hundred and eleven names. The room felt smaller with it uncovered, the way a room feels smaller when someone you mourn walks into it.

Wendell looked at it for a long moment. "They burned your shop for a lie," he said quietly. "And the truth was under your coat the whole time."

"That's the trade now." Sefu flexed his bandaged fingers, wincing. "Everything we own, for days. That's the exchange rate. My mother's press bought us maybe three weeks. The next purchase will cost something else — maybe a person. Maybe one of us." He met Wendell's eyes. "I need you to understand that. I chose the decoy over the type. I'd choose it again. But the price goes up every time we move, and we have to keep moving, because Marsh doesn't stop. She requests maintenance logs. She interviews witnesses. She is building our obituary right now, page by page, in beautiful handwriting, and the only question is whether we get ours written first."

Wendell was silent. In the dark behind them, the master clock's escapement ticked with the patience of something that had watched centuries and intended to watch more.

"Then we stop thinking like a man with a secret," Wendell said finally, "and start thinking like a guild."

Sefu laughed — short, surprised, painful. "Go on."

"A secret is one man carrying one thing until he drops it. A guild is many hands, each holding a little, none holding enough to hang them all." Wendell touched the ledger's cover, lightly, the way you touch a wound. "Vane worked alone. Look what it got him. We don't verify these names alone anymore. We find the widows. The sailors. The clerks who falsified the manifests and can't sleep. Every name gets a witness, every witness gets a place to stand. And no one stands in the same place twice."

"You're describing a conspiracy."

"I'm describing my sister's census," Wendell said, and his voice did not break, but it bent, and Sefu let it bend. "Four hundred and eleven people the city says never happened. Somebody has to happen for them."

Above them, distantly, the clock began to strike six — twelve bells rolling down through the tower's bones and out over Bellhaven, over the burned lot on Cooper Street where Ademola Press had stood for thirty years, over the harbor where the water kept its own records and gave up nothing.

Sefu listened to the last bell fade, and thought about his mother's type melting in the heat, every letter returning to the metal it came from, ready someday to be cast again.

"All right," he said. "Guild it is. But Wendell — the decoy buys weeks, not months. And there's something else. One of the pages Vane copied referenced source documents. Shipping papers. Naval orders. He hid them somewhere, and his notes point to the harbor — the customs house lockers." He watched Wendell's face go carefully still. "If we're going to make the city believe four hundred and eleven names, we'll need more than faith. We'll need the paper that tells them *who gave the order.*"

Wendell looked at the ledger, then at the dark stairwell leading down into a city that had burned one printer today and would burn more tomorrow.

"Then tomorrow," he said, "we go to the harbor."
