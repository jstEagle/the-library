## 11. The Anniversary Approaches

The official history of the Bellhaven Dock Riots was to run forty pages.

Cordelia Marsh had been told this on her first morning in the commission offices, by a deputy minister who did not look up from his tea when he said it. Forty pages, printed on good stock, bound in government blue, distributed to every school and garrison and guildhall in the province before the anniversary. Forty pages was not a suggestion. It was a container, and her task — the word *task* had been used twice, the word *duty* four times — was to pour the approved facts into it without spilling.

She had accepted the commission because refusing it would have accomplished nothing except her own irrelevance. She told herself this each morning as she climbed the stairs of the former customs house, now draped in bunting for the anniversary preparations, and each morning the sentence felt thinner, like a coat worn past its season.

Her office had once belonged to the chief surveyor of the harbor. The windows looked out over the water where, eleven months ago, men had drowned within sight of the quays while the garrison's rifles did their work. Cordelia had chosen the desk facing the window deliberately. She wanted to see what she was writing about. It was a scholar's discipline: never write about a place you have not looked at.

It was also, she was beginning to understand, a form of penance she had no right to perform.

* * *

The draft lay before her in its third iteration, forty pages exactly, each paragraph numbered in her own hand against the master outline the ministry had provided. The outline was a marvel of compression. *Causes: economic agitation fomented by foreign agitators. Events: regrettable violence initiated by striking dockworkers. Response: proportionate restoration of order. Casualties: nine.* Nine. She had written the number herself, transcribed from the provost's report, and her pen had hesitated over it for perhaps half a second — an interval no instrument in Bellhaven could measure, but which she felt in her wrist like a caught gear.

Nine dead. She had stood at the harbor rail that morning counting what could be counted from where she stood: the boats that went out, the boats that came back, the interval between them. She was a historian. Counting was the whole of her trade. And the count she had made privately, standing in the cold with her notebook open, had not been nine. It had not been ninety either. But it had not been nine.

The trouble with the official history was not that it lied. Lies were easy; lies sat flat on the page like stones. The trouble was that it was *bad history*, and badness showed. A competent reader — and there would be competent readers, in other provinces, in other years, when the passions of the present had cooled into the raw material of scholarship — a competent reader would find the seams in a week. The casualty figure with no source. The agitators who appeared from nowhere and vanished after. The absence of any named witness below the rank of sergeant. History written to conclusion rather than from evidence has a texture, and Cordelia knew that texture the way a weaver knows flawed cloth, and she was weaving it, yard by yard, with her own hands.

She had raised the problem once, carefully, in her second week. *A record without sources invites doubt,* she had written in a memorandum. *Doubt, unaddressed, becomes revisionism. I recommend annexing selected primary documents.*

The reply came back inside two days, in the deputy minister's own hand, which was itself a message: *The record will be believed because it is the record. Annex nothing.*

She had read that sentence perhaps fifty times since. *Because it is the record.* It was, she recognized, a coherent theory of history — power as provenance, authority as evidence. It was even, in its way, an old theory, older than any archive she had ever catalogued. Kings had ruled by it. Churches had burned by it. And it had one flaw so large that only someone trained to find flaws could see it: records outlive regimes. The blue binding would fade. The ministry that guaranteed the text would fall or drift or simply forget. And then some future scholar would open the book, feel the seams pull, and begin — as Cordelia herself had begun a hundred times in other archives, with other governments' careful fictions — to dig.

She was building the very document she had spent her career taking apart.

She dipped her pen and wrote: *The disturbances of last autumn, though localized, revealed weaknesses in municipal administration which the Crown has moved decisively to correct.* Decisively. There was a word doing heavy labor. She underlined it, then struck the underline. Then she sat looking at the harbor until the light changed.

* * *

The clerk's slip arrived on a Thursday, folded into a stack of requisition forms, and it should have meant nothing at all.

His name was Pell. He was nineteen, freckled, conscientious to the point of tedium, and he had been assigned to inventory the census office's papers following the death of Aldous Vane — drowned, the file said, an accident, the harbor full of such accidents that autumn. Pell's job was to list documents for transfer to the provincial archive, and Cordelia's job, among her many duties, was to approve the transfers. He came to her desk with his stack and his list and his habit of narrating everything he did, and halfway through the narration he frowned at a page.

"This one's odd, ma'am. Vane's own hand, but it's not a census schedule. It's a note to himself, looks like. About a delivery." Pell turned it toward her. "He writes about a 'second census.' Do you think that means the follow-up enumeration? We never did a follow-up."

Cordelia took the slip.

It was a fragment, a quarter-page in a small, disciplined hand — the hand of a man who had learned to write quickly and legibly under pressure, the hand of a clerk, an accountant, a surveyor. *the second census cannot go to the parish copies again — too many eyes. if anything happens to me it stays with the mechanism, where no one reads but the man who winds.* Below that, a string of figures that might have been dates or might have been ledger references, and beneath those, in letters smaller than the rest, almost apologetic: *nine is not the number. the number is kept elsewhere.*

The room did not change. Pell went on talking about the transfer schedule. Outside, a crane swung a crate of anniversary bunting across the window. Cordelia read the fragment three times and her face, she was fairly certain, expressed mild bureaucratic puzzlement, because she had learned long ago that her face was a document she could edit.

"Probably a private project," she said. "Some enumerators keep parallel tallies. Set it aside for me, would you? I'll want to check it against the schedules."

Pell set it aside, relieved to have a decision, and moved on down his list, and Cordelia returned to page thirty-one of the official history — *Casualties: nine* — and found that she could not write another word that afternoon.

* * *

That night, in her rooms above the chandler's shop on Rope Walk, she laid out what she had on her writing table like a surgeon laying out instruments.

The fragment. Her copy of Vane's personnel file — she had pulled it that evening from the commission's own holdings, properly signed for, properly logged; whatever else she was, she was scrupulous. His service record was dull and complete: twenty-two years an enumerator, punctual, accurate, commended twice, passed over for promotion three times. A man who stayed. Men who stayed in minor posts for twenty-two years were either without ambition or committed to something the promotions could not touch. The file could not tell her which.

And beneath the file, her notebook — the one from the harbor, the one she had told no one about, the one in which she had recorded, on the day after the riots, her own count. Not a count of bodies; she had seen none. A count of absences. Boats out, boats back. Names she had begun collecting from dockside talk before she understood why she was collecting them, before the commission existed, when she was still only a historian walking a wounded city with her notebook open because that was what her hands did.

Seventeen names. She had stopped at seventeen because stopping was easier than continuing, and she had told herself the count was unreliable, anecdotal, unsourced — all true — and she had closed the book, and the book had waited eleven months the way books do, patient as debt.

Now she opened it beside Vane's fragment and read her own handwriting as if it belonged to a stranger. *Widow Okafor says her son-in-law went out with the tide crews and did not come back. No name in the provost's list. The provost's list has nine names and six of them are foremen.* Six foremen out of nine dead. Even a reader innocent of statistics would pause at that ratio. She had paused at it, in October, and written in the margin: *ask why.* She had never asked why. She had been appointed instead.

Vane's fragment said: *nine is not the number.*

Her notebook said, in effect: I know.

Two independent records, she thought, reaching toward the same conclusion from opposite directions — hers from the outside, anecdote and arithmetic; his from the inside, from the schedules themselves, from the machinery of enumeration. That convergence was the thing she had spent fifteen years training herself to recognize. Sources that agree without contact are the strongest sources there are. And a source that agrees with your own secret count is stronger still, because you cannot dismiss yourself.

She was aware, sitting there in the lamplight, of the shape of what she was doing. She was beginning an investigation. Not the investigation — not yet, not officially; officially she was a servant of the record, and the record was closed. But a scholar's habits do not ask permission. A discrepancy had entered her field of view, and discrepancies were to her what unfinished sentences were to novelists, what loose rivets were to engineers: intolerable, magnetic, demanding.

*If anything happens to me.* Vane had drowned. The harbor was full of accidents. She wrote both facts in her notebook and drew no line between them, because drawing lines was what prosecutors did, and she was not a prosecutor. She was a historian. She dealt in what could be shown.

But she noticed that her hand was steady in the way it went steady before difficult work.

* * *

Over the following week she pulled threads with a rigor she had not been permitted to use since her appointment, and the rigor itself was a kind of relief, like a muscle finally given load.

She requested Vane's completed schedules for the autumn census — all districts — and compared them against the parish registers, district by district, household by household, working evenings at her own table because the commission office had ears. The work was slow and exacting and it was the first honest work she had done in a year. In three districts the schedules matched the registers perfectly. In two, they matched almost perfectly. In the dock districts — Saltrow, the Weighhouse ward, the Tanner's Flats — they diverged, subtly, in a pattern.

Households listed in the parish books but absent from the schedules. Not many. Never many. Two here, five there, a widow alone, a family gone. And in each case the schedule carried, in Vane's cramped marginal notation, a single abbreviation she could not initially parse: *rel.*, followed sometimes by a date. Relocated, she assumed. Deportation was the ministry's own term; the transports had been public knowledge, even celebrated in the loyal press as mercy — removal rather than punishment. Relocated persons would appear in transport manifests. She ordered the manifests.

They did not appear in the transport manifests.

She checked twice, then a third time, varying her method — by name, by household, by district, by date. The relocated of Saltrow and the Weighhouse ward and the Tanner's Flats had been relocated nowhere. No arrival records. No settlement assignments. No subsequent appearance in any register she could reach: tax rolls, militia lists, burial books, marriage banns. They had left the paper world entirely, and the paper world, which misses nothing, had not marked their going.

There were thirty-one such households. She made the list herself, in her notebook, next to her seventeen names from the harbor, and found — her breath actually caught, a small mechanical failure she noted with detached interest — that several of the names coincided. The absent of the schedules and the absent of the water were substantially the same people.

Thirty-one households. Perhaps a hundred and forty souls, using standard multipliers. Souls for whom the official record offered one word — *relocated* — and no destination, and no date of arrival anywhere, and no grave.

She sat back from the table. The lamp hissed. Down in the street a dray rolled past toward the harbor, loaded with timber for the anniversary platform, and the house shook faintly with its passing.

This was the moment, she understood later, at which she might have stopped. The evidence was incomplete. It always was; that was the nature of evidence. She could have closed the notebook, filed her memoranda, finished her forty pages, and gone on to a comfortable career footnoting conclusions reached by others. Many scholars did precisely that, and slept well, and were remembered politely.

But the seams were pulling. That was the only way she could describe it, and she had tried, in her private notebook, three times, crossing out each attempt. A history with holes does not merely lack information; it exerts pressure on everything around the hole. The missing households deformed the schedules. The false casualty figure deformed the provost's report, which deformed the garrison's orders, which deformed the whole account she was contracted to write. She was not being asked to fill gaps. She was being asked to build a structure across a void and call the void solid ground, and she was a person whose entire life had been an argument that you do not get to do that. Not for convenience. Not for the Crown. Not for forty pages of blue binding.

*The record will be believed because it is the record.* She wrote the sentence in her notebook and beneath it: *No. Records are believed because they can bear checking. This one cannot. Therefore it will be believed only while belief is compulsory. Compulsion expires.*

She looked at what she had written and understood that she had just composed, in her own hand, the indictment of her own commission. It was the most honest paragraph she had produced in a year. She did not cross it out.

* * *

On the ninth day she went back to the fragment — *it stays with the mechanism, where no one reads but the man who winds* — and sat with it for a long time.

Mechanism. The word had seemed incidental, a clerk's metaphor, the way Vane might have said *the works* meaning the bureaucracy. But Vane had not been a metaphorical writer. His schedules were literal to the point of austerity; his margins contained abbreviations, never flourishes. A man who wrote like that used *mechanism* to mean a mechanism.

And there was, she recalled, a famous mechanism in Bellhaven, one that required winding, one tended by a particular guild, one that the city regarded with something close to superstition. She had walked past the observatory hill a dozen times since her appointment. The tower stood above the roofs like a raised finger, and its clock was the city's heartbeat; every bell in every parish obeyed it. When the clock spoke, Bellhaven listened. Everyone knew that. It was the kind of fact so large it became invisible.

*Where no one reads but the man who winds.*

She opened her diary of official engagements and found the entry she had been avoiding: the rededication of the master clock, scheduled for the anniversary itself, the same week the official history would be sealed into the archive. Her name was on the program as presiding officer. She had accepted the duty weeks ago as one accepts all duties, without thought.

She looked at the entry for a long moment. Then she took a fresh sheet of commission paper and wrote, in her best administrative hand, a routine request — the kind of request a thorough historian makes by the dozen:

*To the Guild of Clockmakers: in preparation for the anniversary ceremonies, kindly furnish the maintenance logs of the observatory master clock for the past twelve months, together with a schedule of all repairs performed and the names of all persons admitted to the mechanism housing during that period.*

Routine. Unremarkable. The sort of paper that crossed her desk daily and crossed back answered.

She sealed it, addressed it, and set it in the outgoing tray, and only then allowed herself to notice that her heart was beating quickly, and that she had not felt this — this alertness, this fear braided into purpose — since she had been a young researcher in the provincial archive, before she had learned how much of history is written by whoever holds the seal.

Outside, the harbor lights came on one by one, and somewhere across the dark water the observatory clock began to strike the hour, and the sound came over the roofs the way it had come for a hundred years, and everyone in Bellhaven who heard it believed, without thinking, that it was telling the truth.
