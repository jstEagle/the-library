## 13. Betrayal at the Weighhouse

The weighhouse smelled of hemp and brine, as it had every morning of Sefu Ademola's working life, but this morning it smelled of fear too, and he knew the difference the way he knew the difference between rain coming and rain already fallen.

He had come to collect the pages.

It was a simple exchange, the kind they had run a dozen times now. Tomi Okafor, the stevedore with the forearms like mooring posts, kept a cache of ledger copies in the false bottom of his tool chest, and every third morning Sefu met him at the weighhouse before the harbor masters arrived, took the pages Tomi had collected from the tower congregation, and carried them to the cellar press on Coppersmith Lane. Simple. Reliable. The kind of small, dull ritual that made dangerous work survivable — because danger, Wendell had told him once, was like a clock spring: it only killed you when it was coiled tight. Routine kept the spring slack.

Sefu had believed that. He had built his whole method on it. Print, copy, distribute, verify. Small hands doing small motions, ten thousand times, until the thing was done.

Tomi was late.

That was the first wrong thing. Tomi Okafor had not been late to a shift in nineteen years. The harbor masters set their clocks by him; the cranes seemed to wait for his whistle. Sefu stood by the great brass scales — the weighhouse scales, old enough that the pans had been recast twice, old enough that the chains had a song when the wind moved them — and he watched the door, and he counted seconds the way a printer counts lines of type, and at four hundred seconds past the appointed time he knew.

He did not run. Running was a confession. He walked, unhurried, toward the fish market, and he let his eyes do the work while his body performed the loiterer. Two men by the customs shed who were not customs men — customs men slouched, and these stood like furniture. A woman in a gray coat near the weighhouse steps who had been near the weighhouse steps yesterday too, and the day before, and Sefu cursed himself with a fluency that would have made the dockside preachers blush. He had seen her. He had filed her away as nobody. He had let routine make him blind.

The spring was coiled. It had been coiled for days.

He cut through the fish market, past the stalls where the gulls argued over offal, and out onto the quay, and there he saw the thing that turned his fear into grief.

They were bringing Tomi out of the customs shed.

Not marching him. Supporting him. Two men under his arms, his boots dragging, his head lolling — and then, as they crossed into the light, Tomi lifted his head, and Sefu saw his face, and the face was wrong. Not broken. Wrong in a subtler way. It was the face of a man who had been shown something, and could not stop seeing it.

They put him in a black government carriage, and the carriage pulled away toward the administrative quarter, and the gray-coated woman made a small note in a small book, and Sefu Ademola stood on the quay with his hands in his pockets, pressing his palms flat against his thighs so no one would see them shake, and understood that the net had just closed around a man he had personally vouched for.

Tomi knew the weighhouse. Tomi knew the tool chest. Tomi knew the cellar on Coppersmith Lane.

Tomi knew Sefu's face.

* * *

He did not go to Coppersmith Lane. That was the discipline his mother had beaten into him before the fever took her — *when the house is burning, boy, you do not run back in for the things you love; you run to where the fire is going next.* He went to the tower.

Wendell was up in the mechanism housing, sleeves rolled, hands black with grease, and when Sefu climbed the ladder into the gear room and said Tomi's name, Wendell went still in a way that clockmakers could — absolutely still, the stillness of a man listening to a movement he knows is about to fail.

"When?" Wendell said.

"Twenty minutes ago. They took him from the customs shed. He was walking, mostly. They wanted him walking."

"Then he told them something already, or they wouldn't have bothered carrying him out." Wendell came down the ladder slowly, wiping his hands on a rag that only spread the grease thinner. "What does Tomi know?"

Sefu made himself say it all, because saying it in pieces would be worse. "The weighhouse exchange. The tool chest. Coppersmith Lane. Your face, mine, the widow Amaechi's, the sailor Brannigan's. He sat in on four testimony sessions. He knows the shape of the whole network, Wendell. He was never meant to know the shape. He was meant to know one corner of it. I broke my own rule. I trusted him because he was trustworthy."

Wendell sat down on the gear crate, and for a moment he looked very young, which he was, twenty-three years old and carrying a census of the dead in a clock tower, and Sefu felt the old anger rise — not at Wendell, never at Wendell, at the city, at the victors, at the whole rotten arithmetic that had turned a dockworker with three children into a liability to be carried out of a customs shed.

"We have hours, maybe," Wendell said. "Not days. They'll want to move on Coppersmith Lane tonight, before we can clear it."

"Then we clear it now."

"It's not just the press. It's the people. Amaechi sleeps in the back room. Brannigan's been using the cellar as a bolt-hole since the *Meridian* list came through. And there's the half-duplicate — the only other copy in the city besides what's in this tower."

Sefu was already moving to the ladder. "Then stop listing it and help me carry it."

* * *

They cleared Coppersmith Lane in ninety minutes, and it was the worst ninety minutes of Sefu's life, and he had buried his mother at fourteen and his father's press at nineteen, so the competition was real.

The widow Amaechi went first, wrapped in a fishwife's shawl, walking with Wendell toward the market as if they were betrothed and shopping, which got a snort out of her that Sefu chose to interpret as courage. Brannigan refused to leave until he'd drunk the last of the rum, which nearly killed them all. And the half-duplicate — four hundred pages, Sefu's own typesetting, the work of a year — went into a false-bottomed flour barrel and out through the cellar's coal chute into the arms of a boy who was paid too much and asked too little.

The press itself they could not move. A hand press is half a ton of iron and stubbornness. So they did the thing that made Sefu's chest ache even now, thinking of it: they left it standing, inked, with the forme of the preamble still locked in the chase, and they scattered printer's waste and spoiled sheets around it as if the shop had simply failed — as if Ademola Press had died the ordinary death of small businesses, debts and disappointment, nothing worth a soldier's boot.

At midnight, from the roof of the chandlery across the lane, Sefu watched them come.

Not soldiers first. First the gray-coated woman, alone, standing before the shop door in the lamplight for a long moment, reading the sign — ADEMOLA, LETTERPRESS & JOBWORK — the way Cordelia Marsh's agents must have taught her to read it, as an object of interest. Then the knock, polite, three raps. Then, getting no answer, the door going in, and the boots, many boots, and the lantern light blooming in the windows, and Sefu crouched on the cold roof with his father's press name burning in his throat and did not make a sound.

They were thorough. He gave them that. They took the type they found, the paper stock, the spoiled sheets, the ink. They did not find the flour barrel. They did not find Amaechi or Brannigan. And after an hour they brought out the one thing Sefu had known they would bring out, the thing he had been watching the door for since the first lantern:

Tomi Okafor.

They walked him to the door and stood him there, and a man Sefu didn't know — soft-spoken, well-dressed, the kind of man whose cruelty was administrative — said something to him, quietly, and pointed down the lane, and Tomi turned his head and looked, slowly, at the chandlery, at the fish market, at the dark mouths of the streets, and Sefu understood that they were making him identify the escape routes. The bolt-holes. The places Tomi had promised, in whatever room they'd kept him in, to give up.

Tomi looked at the chandlery roof.

Sefu was flat on the lead before the man's eyes finished the arc. He lay with his cheek against cold zinc and his heart going like a trip-hammer and he counted to three hundred, and when he raised his head the lane was empty and the gray-coated woman was writing in her small book and Tomi Okafor was gone into the black carriage again, and Sefu lay there and thought: *he looked, but did he see?*

And then, because he was his mother's son and his father's printer, he thought the harder thought: *does it matter? Either way, they have him. Either way, he'll give them the tower eventually, because they will show him his children's faces until he does.*

* * *

The council of war happened at dawn in the gear room, among the gears, because the tower was the only place left they trusted, and because — as Igeoma said, arriving with bread and a face like a closed ledger — "if they come for us here, I would rather be holding a wrench."

They were seven. Wendell. Igeoma. Sefu. Amaechi, who had not slept and whose hands would not stop moving, folding and unfolding a scrap of cloth that had been her husband's. Brannigan, the mutinied sailor, who had brought the rum he'd refused to abandon and now drank it like medicine. Old Nwosu, the parish clerk who had falsified the manifests and looked, permanently, like a man awaiting sentencing. And the boy with the flour barrel, whose name was Pip and who had appointed himself to everything.

Sefu laid it out plainly, because plainness was all he had left.

"They have Tomi. Tomi knows the network's shape. Sooner or later — days, I'd say, not weeks — they'll have the tower. We have to assume every hour is borrowed."

"Then we run," Brannigan said. "Ship out. I know captains on the coast run who'd take us. Whole company, gone, safe."

"And the ledger?" Wendell asked quietly.

"The ledger goes with us."

"And then what? It sits in a cellar in some port town while the official history gets sealed on the anniversary, and in fifty years someone opens the archive and finds Marsh's version, all clean and official, and our ledger is a curiosity. The dead stay 'relocated,' Brannigan. Forever. We'd be alive, and they'd be erased, and that's the trade."

"Alive is not nothing," Brannigan said, and there was something raw under it. "I've seen what not-alive looks like. I mutinied over it."

"No one's saying alive is nothing." That was Amaechi, and her voice cracked the room like a dropped plate. Everyone looked at her. She unfolded her scrap of cloth, refolded it. "My husband is in that ledger. Page eleven. *Drowned, harbor riots, body not recovered.* I sat in that cellar and gave my testimony because I thought it would matter. If we run, it mattered for nothing. If we stay, I might matter for nothing too. But I know which one I can live with." She looked at Tomi's empty chair — Tomi's chair, because Tomi had sat in the tower sessions too, before any of this. "And I know what Tomi would say, and it's killing me, because Tomi would say *run, take my children into account, I sold what I sold for them* — and I'd say it back to him: you didn't sell us, Tomi. You sold time. And time is the one thing they can't print."

Silence. The master clock ticked above them, enormous, patient, the way it had ticked through every argument the city had ever had.

"Mercy or security," Igeoma said at last, into the quiet. "That's the choice in front of us, and I want us to be honest that it's a real one. Security says: cut Tomi loose. Assume he's lost, change everything he knew, protect the ledger. Mercy says: he has three children under ten, and the men who have him know it, and everything he did, he did because they made him choose between his conscience and their faces." She looked around the gear room. "I'm an old woman. I've watched this guild and this city choose security my whole life. It has never once made us safe. It has only made us quiet."

"Then what do we do?" Pip asked. "We can't get him out. Can we?"

Everyone looked at Wendell, because Wendell was the one who had started this, and Wendell was looking at the floor with an expression Sefu had learned to read: the look of a man watching gears turn in his head that had no business turning.

"There's a third thing," Wendell said slowly. "Not mercy, not security. Misdirection. They think Tomi has given them the network. Fine. So we let him have given them a network — one that's real enough to check, and wrong in the places that matter. We give Tomi something to tell them that costs us nothing. Amaechi's cousin has a room on Rope Walk — empty, it's been empty since the spring. We make it look used. Papers, wax drippings, a cot. We let them find it. Let them spend a week watching an empty room while we move everything that matters and finish the duplicate."

"They'll still have Tomi," Sefu said.

"They'll still have Tomi," Wendell agreed. "And when they figure out the room was bait, they'll know he lied to them, or half-lied, and that buys him — " he stopped, and Sefu saw him do the arithmetic and hate the answer — "it buys him nothing, maybe. Or it buys him the difference between a man who lied and a man who cooperated. In a room with men like that, that difference is a kind of mercy. It's the only kind we can reach."

"And if it fails," Nwosu said, in his sentencer's voice, "we've spent our last quiet days building a stage set."

"Then we'd better build it well," Sefu said, and heard his own voice come out flat and certain, and marveled a little at it, because inside he was not flat or certain at all. Inside he was nineteen years old, watching soldiers carry his father's type out into the street, and he was promising every god he'd half-believed in that he would never again be the man who lost the press. "We build it well, and we finish the duplicate, and we get the truth into the record before they seal the archive. That's the whole plan. That's all of it. Anyone who wants to run, run now, with my blessing and my share of the rum."

Nobody ran.

Brannigan passed the bottle instead, and even Nwosu drank, and Amaechi folded her scrap of cloth one last time and put it away, and above them the master clock gathered itself and struck seven, and the sound went out over the rooftops like a bell tolling for the city they were about to save or lose.

* * *

They built the stage set in a day and a night. Sefu forged correspondence in three hands — he was, after all, a printer; forging hands was half his trade — and Pip, who turned out to be a natural, aged paper with tea and candle smoke until it looked like months of use. Amaechi's cousin was paid in advance and sent to her sister's in the country, which was the only honest mercy in the whole arrangement.

On the second morning, Sefu walked past Rope Walk at the appointed hour, a man delivering bread, and saw the gray-coated woman across the street with her small book, and saw, behind the empty room's curtain, the shadow they'd arranged to look like a man pacing. She wrote something down. She did not smile. Cordelia Marsh's people, Sefu was learning, never smiled; they only recorded.

It would hold. It would hold for days, maybe a week.

And then, as he turned the corner by the weighhouse — the weighhouse, always the weighhouse, the city's great brass scales weighing everything and balancing nothing — a boy ran up to him, one of the harbor kids who carried messages for coppers, and pressed a folded scrap into his hand and ran.

Sefu opened it. The hand was shaky, written fast, in pencil:

*They moved me. New man, new questions. He doesn't believe the room. He's pulling the shipping manifests — the real ones, the customs copies. Sefu — the locker. They're going to find the locker. You have one tide. — T*

Sefu stood very still. The locker. Vane's locker. The naval order, the scuttling authorization, the paper heart of everything — the one piece of evidence that could not be forged, dismissed, or printed around.

One tide. The tide was turning now.

He was already running for the tower.
