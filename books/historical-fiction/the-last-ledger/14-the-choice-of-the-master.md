## 14. The Choice of the Master

Master Igeoma had buried three husbands, outlived two guild wars, and survived the cholera summer of '81, and in all those years she had learned one lesson above the rest: when a powerful person asks for a small favor politely, it is never small, and it is never a favor.

So when Cordelia Marsh arrived at the guildhall on a gray Tuesday morning — alone, unescorted, her coat still beaded with harbor fog — Igeoma did not offer her tea.

"You'll take it anyway," Cordelia said, settling into the chair across from the workbench as though she had been invited. "You strike me as a woman who keeps good tea and bad company."

"I keep excellent tea," Igeoma said. "And no company at all before noon." She poured two cups regardless. Hospitality was armor too, if you wore it correctly. "You've come about the clock."

"I've come about many things." Cordelia set a leather folio on the bench between them, square and official, stamped with the seal of the Commission on the Bellhaven Disturbances. "But yes. The clock first."

Igeoma drank her tea and waited. Patience was the first thing they taught apprentices, and the last thing anyone mastered. A movement that is rushed is a movement that is ruined.

"The master clock requires rededication," Cordelia said. "Anniversary week. There will be a ceremony, an inquiry — formality, mostly, but formality matters to this government, which is more than can be said for most governments. The guild will want its house in order. Its records in order." She opened the folio. "Its people in order."

"Guild records are guild property," Igeoma said. "Sealed under charter since before your commission existed."

"I know precisely what they are sealed under." Cordelia's voice did not rise; that was the unsettling thing about her. Everything stayed level, like water finding its height. "Which is why I'm here instead of soldiers. Soldiers make such a mess of paper."

She turned the folio around. Inside was a single sheet, written in a fine clerk's hand: *Full pardon, all charges, real or imagined, pertaining to the events of the riots and their aftermath. Restoration of guild standing, senior rank, seat on the governing council.* Below it, a line for a signature.

Below that, a blank space where the consideration would be named. They hadn't even bothered to write it down. That, Igeoma thought, was either arrogance or courtesy. With this government it was usually both.

"And what," Igeoma said, "would a woman of my years have done that needs pardoning?"

"Nothing yet." Cordelia met her eyes. "That's rather the point. This is a pardon in advance. A rare instrument. It means someone believes you're going to do something worth pardoning."

Igeoma set down her cup. The porcelain made no sound against the bench; forty years of handling delicate mechanisms had taught her hands to land softly. "And who believes that?"

"The same someone who believes the observatory clock was sabotaged by dockworkers and repaired by a diligent apprentice with no questions asked." Cordelia folded her hands. "The same someone who has been asking me, weekly, why a census taker drowned within sight of a tower full of clockmakers who see everything. Master Igeoma, I am not a fool, and you are not a grandmother. Let us not spend the morning pretending otherwise."

* * *

There it was, then. Not the accusation — the invitation to confess without accusing. Igeoma had seen interrogators work both ways. The cruel ones broke doors. The clever ones left them open and waited to see what walked through.

She rose, crossed to the window, and looked out over the guildyard, where Wendell was supposed to be cleaning escapements and was instead, she noted, nowhere in sight. Good boy. She had sent him to Sefu's cellar an hour ago with instructions not to return until dark, and he had gone with the particular obedience of a young man who understood that obedience could be a form of protection.

"You say *someone*," Igeoma said. "Not *the commission*. Not *the ministry*. Someone."

Cordelia was quiet for a moment. When she spoke again, something had shifted in her voice — not warmth, nothing so generous, but a kind of professional candor, the tone of one craftsman acknowledging another across a bench.

"I was given conclusions before I was given evidence," she said. "Do you understand what that means? A historian receives her conclusions the way a jury receives its verdict — after the trial, not before. Mine arrived the day I accepted the appointment. Death toll: forty-one. Cause: sedition. Responsibility: agitators and foreign money. My task is not to discover what happened. My task is to make what happened legible in a way that flatters the men who ordered it." She paused. "I am very good at my task. That should frighten you more than cruelty would. Cruelty is lazy. Competence is not."

"And yet you're telling me this."

"I'm telling you this because I have spent six weeks in the archives, and the archives are lying to me, and I know they're lying because I taught half the clerks who falsified them how to footnote properly." Cordelia closed the folio. "Someone above me burned a census. Someone above him scuttled ships. And someone above *him* wants a history with no seams showing, and has hired me to press it flat. I am the iron. You are the cloth. Neither of us chose the arrangement."

Igeoma turned from the window. Here was the thing she had been weighing since Vane's body came out of the harbor: whether the woman across the bench was a hunter or another kind of hunted. She had decided, provisionally, in the way she decided everything — by examining the mechanism.

A hunter would have brought soldiers. A hunter would have named the charge. A hunter would not have said *scuttled* — that word had appeared in exactly one document Igeoma knew of, and it was locked in a customs locker and copied into a ledger hidden among gears. Unless Cordelia had found it herself, in which case the game was already lost, and politeness was just the executioner's gloves.

But if she hadn't found it — if she was guessing, pulling threads with a scholar's stubborn rigor, arriving at the truth from the wrong direction and being stonewalled by her own employers —

Then she was not the enemy. She was the instrument of one. And instruments, Igeoma knew from a lifetime at the bench, could be repurposed.

"I can't give you what you came for," Igeoma said. "Because you haven't told me what you came for. You've told me what *they* sent you for. So let us save the afternoon." She sat back down. "Ask me plainly, Madam Historian. What does the commission believe I have?"

"A ledger," Cordelia said. "A second census. Names of the dead the official count erased. And I am instructed to recover it and destroy it, and I am telling you — because you are sharp enough to hear what I am not saying — that I have not yet determined whether I intend to follow my instructions."

The room went very still. Somewhere below, a lathe started up; the guild went on making time while the women at the top of it stopped keeping it.

"Why?" Igeoma asked. One word. The whole question.

Cordelia looked at the pardon sheet a long moment. Then she slid it back into the folio, untaken, unsigned, and stood.

"Because a record that only contains the victors isn't history," she said. "It's a eulogy with the wrong name on it. And I didn't spend twenty years learning archival method to write eulogies for murderers." She buttoned her coat. "I have until the anniversary to deliver my account. After that, the archive seals, and whatever isn't in it stays buried for fifty years. You should know that. Whoever holds your ledger should know that. The clock runs down on all of us, Master Igeoma. Even yours."

She left the tea untouched. At the door she paused, without turning.

"For what it's worth — the pardon was genuine. They don't offer those twice."

"They rarely need to," Igeoma said. "They usually just send the soldiers."

Cordelia almost smiled. Almost. Then she was gone, and the fog took her, and Igeoma stood alone in the guildhall with two cold cups of tea and the shape of a decision that had, without her quite noticing, already been made.

* * *

She found Wendell at dusk, in the tower, where she had told him not to be and known he would be anyway. He was up in the mechanism housing with an oil lamp, his sleeves rolled, his long fingers resting on the great chime train the way other young men rested a hand on a sweetheart's shoulder.

"We need to talk," she said. "Come down. No — stay there. It's better if we talk here."

He swung down, lamp in hand, face smudged, eyes alert. He had lost weight since the raid on Ademola Press; they all had. But there was a steadiness in him now that hadn't been there in the spring, when he'd come to her shaking, holding a dead man's ledger against his chest like a wounded bird.

"Cordelia Marsh came today," Igeoma said.

Wendell went white. "Did she—"

"She knows about the ledger. Or close enough. She offered me a pardon and my old seat on the council for it." Igeoma watched his face do the arithmetic of catastrophe. "I refused."

"You *refused*—" His voice cracked upward. "Master, if she reports—"

"She won't report. Not yet. And not what you fear." Igeoma took the lamp from him and hung it on its hook, and in the better light she studied the chime train: the hammers, the gongs, the great bronze bells that had marked every hour of every life in Bellhaven for a hundred and forty years. "Wendell. Do you know why the guild built this tower so tall?"

"So the bells could be heard."

"Anyone could build tall. Bells carry anyway." She touched the great wheel of the striking train, felt the teeth like a spine under her palm. "No. We built it tall because the founder — old Aldery himself, back when the guild was young and the city was smaller and meaner — believed a clock should do more than tell time. He believed it should *speak*. Look here."

She led him along the housing to a panel behind the hour bell, one so old and grimed that most journeymen took it for a wall. She worked the hidden catch — a trick of the thumb, taught master to apprentice for five generations — and the panel swung open onto a darkness that breathed.

Wendell leaned in. "Speaking-tubes."

"Lead and copper. Laid inside the tower walls when it was built, running down through the masonry, branching — one to the council chamber of the old guildhall, one to the weighhouse, one to the fish market, one to the harbor steps. Nine branches in all. They were meant for the bell-ringers' calls, once. Then telegraph wires came, and everyone forgot." She smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "Everyone forgets lead pipe. Lead pipe is patient."

Wendell stared into the dark throat of the tube, and Igeoma watched understanding arrive in him the way dawn arrives — not all at once, but irreversibly.

"At the anniversary stroke," he said slowly. "When the whole city is listening for the bells—"

"The tubes will carry anything we put into them. A voice. Voices. Reading." He turned to her, and his eyes were wet and burning at once. "The names. Master — the *names*."

"The names," Igeoma agreed. "Read into the inquiry's own transcript, carried on the guild's own clock, in front of the soldiers and the historians and the men who wrote the lies. You cannot unring a bell, Wendell. That was always the point of bells. And now you know the second point."

He put his hand flat against the tube, reverent, the way he touched everything he loved — clocks, ledgers, the memory of his sister. "How long to rig it?"

"Days. It must be done quietly, at night, between windings. And it must be done perfectly, because we will get one stroke. One." She fixed him with the look she reserved for apprentices on the edge of greatness or ruin, which were, in her long experience, neighboring rooms. "This is the oldest secret the guild keeps, and I am giving it to you. Understand what that means. If this fails, it fails on both our heads, and the guild burns with us."

"Then we won't fail."

"Good," Igeoma said. "Now help me close the panel. And Wendell — tomorrow, send word to Ademola. Tell him we'll need his press ready, and his preamble short enough to read between the first stroke and the ninth."

Wendell grinned — the first true grin she'd seen on him in weeks — and reached for the panel. But before it shut, Igeoma caught his wrist.

"One more thing. Marsh gave me until the anniversary. Which means whoever commands *her* gave us until the anniversary. When the sealing comes, everything ends — the archive closes, the record locks, fifty years of silence." She let him go. "So whatever we mean to do, we do it at the rededication itself. In front of everyone. There is no second chance coming."

The panel clicked home. Above them, unhurried, indifferent, magnificent, the master clock gathered itself and struck the hour — nine deep bronze notes rolling out over the rooftops, down through the hidden tubes, into the sleeping bones of the city.

Somewhere in the dark, Igeoma thought, the pipes were already listening.
