## 5. Aldric's War

The glue pot told the truth before anything else did.

Aldric Venn had kept a bindery for thirty-one years, and in all that time he had learned that every material confessed eventually. The leather spoke of the animal and the tannery. The thread spoke of the spinner's hands. The paper — paper above all — was the most honest thing in the world, because paper remembered everything that had ever been done to it: the rags it was rendered from, the water of the mill, the size brushed into its pores, the press that had squeezed it flat as a held breath. A man could lie with ink. A man could lie with his mouth, with his ledger, with his whole life arranged like a well-made case. But the paper under his fingers could not.

So when he turned the cipher fragment over beneath the lamp on the third night after the child came, and felt the grain of it against his thumb, Aldric Venn stopped breathing for the length of four heartbeats.

He knew this stock.

Not the way a man knows a face in a crowd — vaguely, by category, *fine laid paper, rag content, probably foreign* — but the way a man knows a scar he put there himself. The watermark was faint, meant to be found only at an angle, a small crowned anchor pressed into the left margin. He had felt that anchor ten thousand times. He had bound ledgers of it, dispatch cases of it, tall stacks of ministerial correspondence during the years when the old regime still called itself a government instead of what it had become by the end, which was a machine for feeding itself. The Chancellery bought its paper from the Hessel mills upriver, two hundred reams a quarter, and every sheet bore the crowned anchor because the ministers had wanted their authority to be something you could hold.

Aldric set the fragment down on the bench as though it were hot.

The workshop was quiet around him in the particular way it went quiet after dark — not silent, never silent, but hushed into its nighttime register. The cooling stove ticked. Rain worked at the shutters with the patience of a creditor. Somewhere upstairs the child slept in the narrow cot behind the drying loft, breathing the small even breaths of someone who did not yet know what she carried folded inside her coat lining. And across the room, at her own bench, the apprentice Iversen bent over her work with that stillness Aldric had come to distrust precisely because it was so perfect. Real apprentices fidgeted. Real apprentices sighed, ruined corners, asked questions. Iversen worked like a woman defusing something.

He looked at the fragment again. Then at her back. Then at the fragment.

*Someone inside sent the child.*

The thought arrived whole, the way the worst thoughts always did, fully dressed and already sitting in the chair before you noticed the door open.

* * *

He should have slept. Instead he climbed the stairs to the loft where he kept the one thing in the bindery he had sworn never to look at again, and he took down the box.

It was a good box — he had made it himself, of course, made everything himself in those years, chests and slipcases and solander boxes, because a craftsman's hands will keep working even while the rest of him is deciding whether to be ashamed. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, lay three volumes. Not masterworks. He had destroyed the masterworks long ago, or rather he had sold them, which amounted to the same thing and paid better. These were workmanlike things: a registry of appointments, a correspondence file, a minute book. Ministry bindings, plain calf, gilt rules worn to ghosts. The paper inside them was Hessel stock. The crowned anchor sat in every margin like a small drowned god.

He had bound these in the winter of the last regime. He remembered the winter by the smell of it — the chancellery offices had been cold enough that the clerks wore gloves, and the gloves left gray smudges on every sheet, and he had complained about the smudges, and a junior secretary had laughed and said, *Master Venn, in six months no one will care about your smudges.* The secretary had been right. In six months the city had changed flags, the ministers had scattered or swung, and Aldric Venn had taken down the brass plate with his name and hung out a plainer one, and for thirty years he had bound merchant invoices and prayer books and wedding albums and told himself, nightly, with the discipline of a man saying grace, that a binder serves the book, not the hand that pays.

But he had served the hand that paid. That was the plain grammar of it. He had sewn the ledgers in which the old regime recorded who it arrested. He had covered, in good Nigerian goat, the appointment books that decided which families ate and which families vanished. He had never read the documents — he had made a religion of not reading them, had trained his eyes to slide off the text the way they slid off a title page — and this not-reading had been his absolution, renewed daily, and it had been worth exactly nothing then and worth exactly nothing now, thirty years later, holding a scrap of ministry paper that had arrived at his door sewn into a child's coat.

He sat down on the loft stool, which groaned, and so did he.

The rain thickened. Below, he heard the soft scrape of Iversen putting away her tools — earlier than usual, and too carefully, each bone folder set in its slot with a click he could count. She knew he'd seen the fragment. Of course she knew. He had held it too long under the lamp; he had turned it over twice; he had done the one thing a careful man must never do in front of a careful woman, which is show interest. Tomorrow she would be gone, or the codes would be gone, or both, and the bindery would go back to being a place where nothing happened except the slow honest work of keeping other people's words together.

Except that it wouldn't. Because the paper had spoken, and once paper spoke, a binder either listened or became complicit all over again, in a new key, with new instruments.

He unwrapped the third volume — the minute book — and opened it to no page in particular, because any page would do. The entries marched across the sheets in the clerkish hands of dead men. *Approved. Deferred. Referred to committee.* The vocabulary of men signing away other men's lives without once using an active verb. He had bound this. His thread ran through it. Somewhere in this book, very likely, was the order that emptied the house on Candlemaker's Row where the Ostrava family had lived, though he had never looked, would never look, because looking was the one door he had kept shut, and a man is allowed one door.

Wasn't he?

The question sat with him while the candle burned down. It was not a rhetorical question anymore. That was the change. For thirty years it had been rhetorical, a stone he carried and did not turn over. Now there was a child asleep eight feet above his head whose coat lining held half a key to the city's surrender, and the paper said *ministry*, and the ministry was supposed to be a ruin, and ruins do not send children through the dawn fog to bookbinder's shops on Tanner's Lane. Someone alive and employed had cut that fragment. Someone with access to Hessel stock, access to the cipher, access to a six-year-old they were willing to abandon on a stranger's step with a note that said *forget my face.*

Forget my face. He turned the note over in his mind yet again. Written fast, in a fine clerkly italic, on cheap stock — deliberately cheap, he realized now, the opposite choice to the fragment, a woman who understood paper well enough to make sure the note could never be traced upward. Whoever she was, she had known exactly what she was doing, and what she had chosen to do was give away the only thing she apparently could not bear to lose, along with the only thing that might save everyone else.

And she had brought it here. To him. Or to his shop, at least — perhaps to the apprentice, perhaps to the address itself, which appeared in no directory he knew of. But she had chosen a bindery. In a city full of hiding places, she had chosen the shop of a man who could read paper like a priest reads scripture, and either that was luck — and Aldric had stopped believing in luck somewhere around the winter of the gloves — or someone, somewhere, still remembered what Master Aldric Venn used to be, and what he used to bind, and had gambled that thirty years of silence had left some part of him that wanted, at last, to speak.

His hands, resting on the minute book, had begun to tremble. He watched them tremble with a kind of detached professional interest, the way he might watch a spine crack under too much pressure, noting the failure, cataloguing the cause.

Thirty years of quiet complicity, and it had taken one sheet of paper to find the seam.

* * *

In the morning the fog came off the harbor the way it always did now, gray and smelling of tar and cold iron, and the city got up and pretended. That was the other thing the siege had taught him: cities pretend. The fishwives still argued prices as if prices mattered. The church bells still rang the hours as if time were going anywhere. Boys still played hoops in the lane outside until the patrol wagons passed, and then stood very still, watching the wagons with the flat attention of children who had learned which sounds meant run.

Aldric opened the shop at the usual hour because routine was the last load-bearing wall. Iversen was already at her bench, and the child — Tam — was already awake, sitting cross-legged on the floor amid the paper offcuts, sorting them by color with the grave concentration of a customs officer. The boy had been with them five days and had already learned the workshop's liturgy: don't touch the nipping press, don't shout near the sewing frames, sweep the offcuts into the second bin, not the first. He was a watchable child. Too watchable. He tracked every visitor with his eyes and said nothing, and Aldric had bound enough confidential correspondence to know what kind of household taught a six-year-old that skill.

"Master." Iversen didn't look up from her bench. "I've finished the Kessler ledger."

"You've finished it wrong," Aldric said. "You've rounded the corners on the fore-edge. I round at the head and tail. You know this."

"I know this," she said, and there it was — the flicker, quick as a fish turning — the acknowledgment passing between them like a coin under a table. *You saw. I know you saw. We are both pretending we didn't.*

He crossed to her bench and picked up the ledger and found, tucked into its hollow where a finished job waits for collection, the cipher fragment, returned. She had put it back in his keeping. That was either surrender or a test, and Aldric Venn was too old and too tired to enjoy tests.

"Where did you learn to round corners?" he asked quietly, not looking at her.

"Apprenticeship. Before the war closed the schools."

"In Vellmark?"

"South. A long way south."

"Mm." He set the ledger down. "The paper in your pocket," he said, "is Hessel mill stock. Crowned anchor watermark, laid finish, sized with the yellow gelatin they use because it's cheaper than the white. The Chancellery bought it by the quarter-ream until the regime fell. I know because I bound it. Fourteen years of it. Every dispatch, every registry, every minute book those men used to decide the city's business — my thread is in all of it."

For the first time since she had walked into his shop with her false license and her perfect stillness, Iversen looked genuinely unguarded. It lasted perhaps two seconds. It was enough.

"You're certain," she said.

"A man is certain of his own sins, girl. Everything else is opinion."

She absorbed that. Outside, the hoop boys had started up again, and a gull screamed somewhere over the tile roofs, and the ordinary morning assembled itself around them, indifferent.

"Then someone inside the ministry sent the child," she said. "Someone who's still there. Or still has access."

"Or someone robbing the corpse," Aldric said. "The ministries weren't burned, whatever they tell you. They were locked. There are cellars in the Chancellery annex that haven't been opened since the turnover — I know the man who holds the keys, or knew him, before he learned to be the kind of man who holds keys for whoever's paying. Paper keeps, Iversen. That's the whole tragedy of my trade. We build things to last forever, and then forever arrives, and it's carrying a knife."

Tam had stopped sorting offcuts. He was watching them, the way he watched everything, and Aldric caught the boy's eye and made himself smile, and the boy, after a moment, smiled back — a small, careful, rationed smile, the smile of a child who had learned smiles were currency.

"Go and see if the kettle wants you," Aldric told him gently, and Tam went, obedient as a soldier, and the loft stairs creaked under his small weight, and Aldric waited until the sound faded before he said the rest.

"There's more. And you'll want to sit, though I know you won't."

"I'll stand."

"Of course you will." He wiped his hands on his apron, an old habit, though they were clean. "Fourteen years I bound for the Chancellery. When the regime fell, I kept three volumes I should have burned. Registry, correspondence, minutes. Why? God knows. Insurance, maybe. Evidence, maybe. Maybe just the hoarder's sickness — a binder hates to destroy a binding, even a wicked one. Especially a wicked one." He heard his own voice go dry and bitter and let it. "They're upstairs. If your fragment came out of a ministry cellar, the stock will match mine sheet for sheet. And if it matches, then your cipher wasn't hidden by some patriot in an attic. It was filed. Catalogued. Kept warm in an official drawer by an official hand, right up until the day someone cut it out and sewed it into a child."

Iversen was very still. Stillness, he was learning, was her native language.

"And the person who sent him," she said slowly, "knew this shop would recognize it."

"Knew, or hoped. There's a difference, and it matters." He looked toward the window, where the fog was lifting off the lane and the city showed itself: the leaning roofs, the smoke standing straight up from a hundred chimneys, the harbor cranes motionless as gallows. "I spent thirty years telling myself that serving the book was different from serving the hand. That thread and glue were neutral. That a craftsman's conscience lives in the quality of his work and nowhere else." He shook his head. "Then a woman leaves a baby on my step with half the republic in its coat, and I discover my conscience has been living in the paper all along, waiting. Waiting thirty years for me to pick it up and read it."

"What will you do?"

"What I should have done in the winter of the gloves," Aldric said. "Read the damn documents."

He was reaching for his coat when the knock came at the shop door — not the soft double tap of a customer, but a measured, official knocking, three blows, evenly spaced, the knock of a man who has never in his life been kept waiting.

Through the glass, in the thinning fog, Aldric could see a uniform. Gray-green, immaculate, and above it a face that was smiling already, patiently, the way power smiles when it has all the time in the world.

"Master Venn," said Colonel Vessane, when the door opened, in excellent Vellmarki, "what a charming little establishment. I wonder if I might come in and admire your presses."
