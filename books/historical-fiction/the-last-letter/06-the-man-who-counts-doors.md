## 6. The Man Who Counts Doors

Colonel Vessane had a theory about doors.

He had developed it in the first year of his commission, standing in a burned village whose name he had never bothered to learn, and it had served him through nine cities since. A door, he believed, told you everything a city wished to hide. A door was the one surface a family could not help but maintain — even in famine they oiled the hinges, even in occupation they repainted the panels, because the door was what the street saw and the street was what kept them honest. So he read doors the way other men read ledgers. The fresh paint that concealed a scorch mark. The bolt newly installed on the inside of a house too poor to own anything worth bolting away. The knocker removed from a merchant's home after the owner had been taken for questioning — grief expressed as hardware.

Vellmark's doors were telling him a great deal this morning.

He walked the Salt Row at a pace he had cultivated deliberately: not the march of an officer, which invited salutes and performance, nor the stroll of a tourist, which invited contempt, but something between — the unhurried gait of a man who owned the ground beneath him and saw no reason to prove it. His aide, Lieutenant Brakk, followed three steps behind with the ledger of registered workshops under his arm, and behind Brakk came two soldiers who were mostly furniture. Vessane did not like large escorts. Large escorts made people perform fear, and performed fear was worthless to him. He wanted the unperformed kind — the small tellings, the second glance, the hand that paused a half-second too long on a latch before opening.

The morning smelled of tar and cold iron. Fog had come off the harbor overnight and not yet lifted, so the city existed in strips: a doorway emerging, then a façade, then nothing again, as though Vellmark were being erased and redrawn line by line by some indifferent hand. Vessane found the fog agreeable. It made the city honest. In clear light, everyone arranged themselves; in fog, they could be observed before they knew they needed arranging.

"Salt Row," Brakk said, consulting the ledger without breaking stride. "Fourteen registered workshops. Three have already been inspected under the press ordinance. Eleven remaining."

"And how many presses did we find among the three?"

"One, sir. A lithographer's. Properly licensed."

"A properly licensed press." Vessane turned the phrase over as if tasting it. "In a city that is about to stop existing. You know what that tells me, Lieutenant?"

"That the lithographer is law-abiding?"

"That the lithographer is frightened. There is a difference, and the difference is useful." He let the fog take a moment before continuing. "A man obeys a law because he believes in it or because he fears it. Belief we cannot use. Fear we can spend."

Brakk wrote this down. Brakk wrote everything down; it was why Vessane kept him. Not for intelligence — Brakk had the analytical gifts of a well-trained mule — but because the act of being written about changed people, and Vessane liked to watch the change happen. A citizen who learned that his answers were entering a ledger began, from that moment, to edit himself. And edited men leaked. Unedited men simply lied.

The first workshop on their route was a cooper's. Barrels stacked to the eaves, staves leaning in bundles like sheaves of grey wheat, the whole interior smelling of oak shavings and the vinegar pickle used on the hoops. The cooper himself was a broad man with forearms like mooring posts, and he stood when they entered with the careful blankness of someone who had decided in advance to be no one.

Vessane admired that. It never worked, but he admired the discipline of it.

"Master Hovden," he said, reading the name off the license nailed by the door. "You bind books?"

"No, Colonel. Barrels."

"Barrels." Vessane ran a finger along the nearest stave, leaving a line in the dust. "And yet a barrel is a kind of container, isn't it? Things go into barrels intending to come out somewhere else. Wine. Oil. Gunpowder, in the old wars. Letters, sometimes. I've found letters in barrels." He smiled at the cooper's carefully unchanging face. "Have you ever found letters in your barrels, Master Hovden?"

"No, Colonel."

"No. Of course not." He let the silence do its work — four seconds, five, six; he had timed it once and found seven seconds to be the maximum before discomfort curdled into open hostility, and hostility was a door slamming shut. At five seconds he nodded pleasantly and moved on, examining the lathe, the adzes, the stack of finished casks, pausing once at a barrel with a fresh head where the rest were weathered, and watching — there it was — the flicker in the cooper's eyes. Nothing. Probably brandy hidden from requisition. Everyone hid brandy. But the flicker was the point. The flicker was proof that the man understood containers, and understanding containers made him a participant in the world Vessane governed.

He left the cooper's with a receipt for inspection signed in triplicate, which the cooper would keep for years and show to no one, and which would sit in a file in the customs house meaning precisely nothing. That was the theater of it. Legality was a stage, and Vessane had learned early that the audience mattered more than the play. Every inspected workshop would talk about the inspection by nightfall. Every conversation would add a detail he had never said or done. By the end of the week, half of Salt Row would believe he had found a printing press buried under the cooper's floor, and the other half would believe he had arrested a child. Fear compounded like interest. He only had to make the deposits.

* * *

The tanner's was next, then the rope-walk, then a chandler's where the inspector's lamp caught vats of rendered fat and Vessane stood a long time looking at the wicks — thousands of them, bundled like pale hair — thinking about what a city looks like when its lights are put out one district at a time. The chandler answered questions in a whisper, though there was no reason to whisper, and Vessane noted the whisper in his mind without writing it down. Whisperers were either guilty or grieving, and lately in Vellmark the two categories had begun to overlap.

It was past midday when they came to the bindery.

He had saved it. That was not sentiment — Vessane did not permit himself sentiment — but craft. An inspection was a meal, and one did not eat the best course first. He had heard about Aldric's bindery before he ever set foot in the city; it appeared in the pre-occupation surveys as a point of civic pride, a workshop older than the republic itself, producing restoration work that collectors across two seas paid handsomely for. Old buildings. Older tools. And, according to the survey, a master craftsman who had been binding documents since before the current government's grandfathers had learned to write.

A man like that remembered things. Paper stock. Watermarks. The weight of a ministry's seal wax versus a chancery's. Thirty years ago, such knowledge had been ordinary; now it was nearly extinct, and extinct knowledge was the most dangerous kind, because no one thought to guard against it.

The shop sat where Salt Row bent toward the water, its upper storeys jutting over the street in the old style, its sign — a painted hand holding a closed book — faded to the color of weak tea. The door was oak, original, with iron studs gone soft with age, and Vessane stopped before it longer than he needed to, letting Brakk and the soldiers wait, letting anyone watching from across the street understand that this door interested him particularly.

It was a good door. Honest wear. No new paint. No new bolt. Which meant either that the bindery had nothing to hide, or that whoever kept it was confident enough not to bother hiding anything — and confidence, in Vessane's experience, was rarer than guilt and far more interesting.

He knocked. Not the military rap; a civilian knock, three unhurried blows, the knock of a customer.

The door opened on a narrow passage smelling of glue and leather and something faintly mineral — the alum-water they treated skins with, he supposed. Light came from the back of the shop, gold and dusty, and in it stood an old man wiping his hands on an apron that had been white perhaps thirty years ago and was now the color of parchment itself.

"Colonel Vessane," Vessane said, offering no rank beyond the name, which was a small cruelty he permitted himself — making others supply the fear his title implied. "Liaison for the Provisional Authority. This is an inspection under the press ordinance. You are Master Aldric?"

"I am." The voice was level. Old, but level. "We don't print, Colonel. We repair what others have printed. If it's presses you're hunting, you'll find none here."

"If it's presses I'm hunting," Vessane said mildly, stepping past him into the shop, "I'll find them. What I'm actually hunting is harder to name, and that's why the ordinance is written broadly. Broad laws catch narrow truths." He removed his hat — a courtesy, always, in workshops; craftsmen trusted men who respected their ceilings — and looked around.

The room was beautiful, and he registered its beauty the way a quartermaster registers grain: as resource. Binderies were archives by another name. Every book that passed through a bench like this carried its history in its spine — bookplates, marginalia, receipts tucked into covers, letters used as binding waste two hundred years before anyone thought them valuable. A bindery was a place where paper went to be taken apart, and taken-apart paper confessed things whole paper never would.

Long benches under the deep windows. A sewing frame with a volume stretched taut, cords taut as harp strings. Presses — two of them, wooden, for pressing boards flat, entirely innocent, exactly the kind of machine that gave the ordinance its pretext while having nothing to do with printing. Shelves rising to the ceiling, spines in ranks, leather in every shade from oxblood to ash. And on a side table, laid out with the tenderness of surgical instruments, the tools: bone folders worn smooth as river stones, awls, a plough, brushes, pots of paste covered with damp cloth.

An apprentice worked at the far bench. Young, slight, sleeves rolled, hands stained with adhesive. The boy — girl? Vessane genuinely could not tell at first glance, and the ambiguity itself snagged his attention like a burr — did not look up immediately. Finished the stroke of the brush first. Then rose, wiped hands, and bowed with the correct degree of deference and no more.

"Ines," Master Aldric said. "My apprentice."

"Ines." Vessane tasted the name. It was not a local name. He filed that. "How long apprenticed?"

"Two months," Aldric said.

"Before that?"

"Before Vellmark, Colonel, if it's all the same."

"It isn't all the same. But it can wait." He smiled, and watched the apprentice watch him smile, and noted that the apprentice's eyes went — briefly, naturally, the way any workman's eyes would go — to the bench where the sewing frame stood. To the volume on it. Or possibly merely toward it. Possibly nowhere at all. The trouble with reading people, he reflected, was that the text was always ambiguous, and only fools pretended otherwise. But ambiguity was not emptiness. Ambiguity was a door with the paint still wet.

He walked the shop slowly. He touched nothing at first — that was the ritual, the theater of legality again: the inspector who respects property establishes the standard against which every deviation will later be measured. He read spines instead. Liturgies. A municipal charter of the old regime, rebound. Ledgers from a shipping house defunct for a generation. And here, pulled out an inch further than its neighbors, a slim volume in plain boards that his fingers found before his eyes did.

He drew it out. A ledger of accounts, the label said, for a wine merchant, dated eleven years back. He opened it at random. Columns of figures. He opened it elsewhere. Figures. He held it to the light and flexed the spine gently, feeling the sewing beneath, and the sewing felt — wrong. Too tight for a commercial job. Tight the way a binder sews when the contents matter more than the cover.

"Fine work," he said, and slid it back exactly where it had been, and saw — or imagined he saw — the apprentice's shoulders lower by a fraction. Imagination was also intelligence. Half of what he knew he had imagined first and confirmed later.

"You bound for the ministries once," he said to Aldric, not as a question.

The old man's hands stilled on his apron. "Thirty years ago. Before the republic. Everyone bound for the regime then, or starved."

"Everyone," Vessane agreed pleasantly. "Which means you remember the papers. The stocks, the weights, the watermarks. A man forgets faces, Master Aldric, but a craftsman never forgets paper. It's the one memory your trade won't let you lose." He picked up a bone folder from the table, turned it over, set it down precisely parallel to its original position. "If a document of uncertain origin were brought to you — say, a single leaf, loose, no binding — could you tell me where it was printed? Could you name the office?"

Silence. The good kind. The kind with weight in it.

"I could tell you what I saw," Aldric said at last. "Paper is paper, Colonel. It doesn't carry confessions."

"On the contrary." Vessane turned to face him fully, and let the pleasantness drain out of his voice by degrees, the way heat leaves a stone at dusk. "Paper is the most confessing material God ever made. It remembers every hand that pressed it. It carries its birthplace in its fibers and its purpose in its size. When the history of this war is written, Master Aldric — and it will be written, on paper, bound by men like you — it will not be written from speeches. Speeches evaporate. Paper stays. Paper is how I will know, ten years from now, exactly who in this city lied to me and exactly how."

He let that stand in the glue-smelling air. Then the pleasantness returned, snapped back like a mask on a spring, and he was the courteous visitor again, thanking the master for his time, requesting the ledger of works completed these past three months — "routine, purely routine" — and noting with approval the apprentice's steady hands.

At the door he paused. One last thing. He was good at last things; they were what people remembered.

"The woman," he said. "Your apprentice. Two months in the trade, and the sewing frame already set up correctly, the paste at the right temper, the folder held like a native. Either you found the finest natural talent in Vellmark, Master Aldric—" he settled his hat back onto his head, and the fog outside had thickened, and the street beyond the open door had become a white corridor with no end visible in either direction "—or you are teaching someone very quickly indeed. And men teach quickly only what they need known urgently."

He stepped out. Then, from the threshold, without turning:

"We'll return. Inspections are never concluded, only adjourned. Keep your door unlocked. Locked doors make me curious, and my curiosity, I'm afraid, has a body count."

* * *

That evening, in the requisitioned customs house with its marble floors gone cold as riverbeds, Vessane dined alone on salt cod and dark bread and reviewed the day's harvest by lamplight.

Brakk's transcript lay beside his plate, faithful and useless. Vessane read past it, into his own memory, which was the better instrument. The cooper's flicker. The chandler's whisper. The bindery — the bindery above all. The apprentice's two months. The non-local name. The sewing frame too perfectly arranged for a shop that supposedly did only restoration. The slim ledger with the tight sewing, shelved within reach of a stranger's hand, which no careful man does unless the shelf itself is the message — look here, see nothing — or unless the shelver had been interrupted recently and often.

And underneath all of it, fainter than watermark, something else. The old man had not feared him. Cooper feared him, chandler feared him, the whole trembling length of Salt Row feared him, but Aldric had stood in his own shop like a stone in a stream, and stones in streams have usually been there a long time, and have usually, at some point, done something that required becoming stone.

Vessane poured the thin occupation wine and considered.

There was a cipher missing from the ministry archive. He had known it for eleven days — a fragment, half of an authentication key, vanished from a sealed drawer along with a junior clerk who had likewise vanished, and the drawer's lock undamaged, which meant the theft wore a uniform or a livery of trust. Half a key was a curiosity. Half a key in a port city full of couriers, smugglers, and ministers selling pieces of the future — that was a fuse lying in a powder magazine, waiting for whichever hand arrived first.

He did not yet think the bindery held it. But he thought the bindery held something, and he had learned across nine cities that something was always the shadow cast by the thing he wanted.

He dipped his pen. Against the bindery's entry he wrote a single word in the margin of Brakk's ledger, small, in his private shorthand, the word he reserved for doors worth counting twice:

*Return.*

Outside, the fog had swallowed the harbor whole. Somewhere down in those white streets, lamps were being lit one by one behind doors that had counted him all day, and Vessane sat in the lamplight counting them back, patient as arithmetic, certain that sooner or later every door in Vellmark would open — and that he need only decide, door by door, what was standing behind each one when it did.
