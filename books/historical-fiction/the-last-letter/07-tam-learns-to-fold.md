## 7. Tam Learns to Fold

The inspections began on a Tuesday, though in Vellmark that week the days had stopped meaning much. What mattered was the rhythm of boots on cobbles, and where they stopped.

Ines heard them from the bindery's back room, where she was trimming boards with a slitting knife and pretending the sound didn't make her hands unsteady. Three houses down, then two. The knock at Master Aldric's door came just after the ninth bell — polite, unhurried, the knock of a man who knew no one would refuse him.

"Colonel Vessane," said a voice smooth as tallow. "Liaison office. We're conducting a survey of unregistered presses."

Aldric let them in. Of course he did. Refusal was its own confession these days.

Ines retreated to the workbench and became what she had trained herself to become over eleven years of this trade within a trade: furniture. She wet a sheet of marbled paper, laid it flat, and pressed it with a bone folder, her movements slow and professional and utterly without thought, while through the doorway she watched two soldiers in grey walk the length of the shop trailing their fingers along the shelves like men appraising horses.

Vessane himself she saw only in profile — tall, silvering at the temples, with the particular stillness of a man who never needed to hurry because everything came to him eventually. He paused at Aldric's finishing bench. He lifted a brass finishing tool, turned it in the light, set it down exactly where it had been. He asked questions in a voice pitched for courtesy: How long has the shop operated? Do you take outside commissions? Any government work, historically?

"Historically," Aldric said, "I bound ledgers for the customs house. Nothing more."

"Nothing more." Vessane smiled at the phrase as if it were a small joke he'd allow. His eyes moved across the workshop the way water moves — into every corner, around every obstacle, leaving nothing dry. They passed over Ines once. Paused. Returned.

"The apprentice?"

"My sister's girl," Aldric said, without a heartbeat's hesitation. "Quiet. Good with her hands."

"Mm." Vessane looked at Ines for three seconds longer than comfort allowed. She kept her gaze on the bone folder, on the paper, on the work, and gave him the gift of her own boredom. Boredom was the best armor she owned. Spies were interesting; apprentices were not.

He left a printed notice pinned to the door — HOUSEHOLDS ARE REMINDED THAT POSSESSION OF UNREGISTERED PRINTING APPARATUS CONSTITUTES AID TO SEDITION — and then they were gone, boots fading up the street toward the chandlery, and Ines discovered she had been holding her breath long enough to feel faint.

"They'll be back," Aldric said quietly. He hadn't moved from the doorway. "Men like that don't survey. They *collect*. He's building an inventory of this street, and one day soon he'll come to collect an item from it."

Ines wiped her knife and said nothing, because there was nothing true and safe to say.

* * *

The inspections continued for six days. Soldiers at the corners, a clerk with a ledger taking down names, and always — always — Vessane's carriage passing at some point in the afternoon, slow, the curtains half-drawn, a man reading his city like a proof sheet and marking errors for correction.

So they stayed inside. And a workshop full of paper, with a child in it, cannot stay quiet for six days.

It started, as most dangerous things do, innocently.

Tam had finished sweeping the offcuts — the child had appointed herself keeper of the scrap bin, guarding paper fragments with the ferocity of a dragon on a hoard — and now stood at Ines's elbow watching her fold a sheet into eighths for endpapers.

"You do it wrong," Tam announced.

Ines looked down. Six years old, chin barely clearing the bench, hair cut short and ragged in the way of hair cut by someone in a hurry, in the dark, perhaps. "Do I."

"That's a soldier fold." Tam pointed at the crease pattern. "The ones who came yesterday. They fold their papers like that. Big squares. For counting."

"And how would you fold it?"

Tam reached up with both hands, and Ines, absurdly, found herself handing the child a clean quarto sheet, and then watched something impossible happen.

Small fingers moved with certainty. First a diagonal — but not centered; offset by a precise finger's width, a proportion Ines recognized dimly from somewhere she couldn't name. Then a valley fold aligned not to edge but to the first crease, then a mountain fold turned back on itself, and the paper began to collapse inward the way certain origami forms do, except this wasn't any form Ines knew. It was tighter. More deliberate. Each crease was made twice — folded, opened, refolded — so the pattern would hold when the thing was finally collapsed, and Tam worked with the unconscious fluency of someone who had done this a thousand times before learning to speak properly.

The result was a small dense packet, roughly hexagonal, no larger than a walnut, with a hidden interior pocket formed by the last two folds.

Ines's blood went cold and stayed cold.

She knew that fold. Not from childhood games. From training. Her handler in the courier service — dead now, or presumed so, along with everyone else from those weeks in the capital — had shown her a diagram once, in the context of a single warning: *The ministry nursery teaches its children a compression fold for dispatch packets. If you ever see a civilian produce one, understand that you are looking at someone raised inside the walls. There are perhaps forty people alive who fold that way, and every one of them is either a hostage or an asset.*

She had laughed at the time. Children as cipher-bearers. Paranoia dressed as tradecraft.

Now a six-year-old stood at her bench holding a walnut-sized packet with a hidden pocket, and the diagram lived again in Ines's memory, line for line, proportion for proportion.

"Who taught you that?" she asked. She kept her voice light. Light cost effort.

Tam shrugged, the shrug of a child who doesn't know a question is a question. "Nana Vess."

"Nana Vess." The name landed wrong immediately — too close to the colonel's, coincidence or not. "And where is Nana Vess?"

"Gone." Tam smoothed the packet flat against the bench, then unfolded it — creases springing back into place with eerie obedience, the paper remembering — and began again, faster this time, showing off. "Everyone goes. Mama went. I waited at the door like she said and then the lady brought me here." A pause, small hands still moving. "She said don't tell about the folds. But you do folds too, so it's not telling if it's folding."

Ines sat very still.

*Don't tell about the folds.* So the mother knew. The woman who left the note — *forget my face* — had known her child carried this in her hands the way other children carry a song, and had warned her against it, which meant the mother understood exactly what it was worth and to whom.

And the child had told anyway. Because Ines folded paper too. Because trust, in a six-year-old, is a door with no lock.

* * *

That night Ines couldn't sleep. She lay on the pallet in the back room listening to the harbor foghorn and turned the problem over until it wore grooves in her.

Here was the arithmetic she could not escape: the child reproduced, from muscle memory alone, a fold pattern taught exclusively within the ministry nursery. The cipher fragment sewn into her coat lining was ministry stock — Aldric had confirmed the paper, watermark and all, from a restricted print run. Put together, these facts stopped being sad and started being a map. Someone inside the ministry had used their own child as a courier, because a child was the one vessel no inspector would search, carrying the one cargo no adult could be seen with.

Which meant the fragment was real. Which meant it mattered enough to risk a daughter for.

Which meant that anyone who learned what Tam could do — what Tam *was* — would want her. Vessane would want her as evidence and leverage. Corvain, whoever he truly served, would want her as a key. And neither of those men was famous for returning what they borrowed.

Ines had spent eleven years keeping her own hands clean of other people's wars, carrying things without opening them, delivering without asking. That discipline had a grammar: *you are the envelope, not the letter.* But envelopes didn't teach children to fold paper. Envelopes didn't lie awake listening to a small person breathe two rooms away, memorizing the sound without permission.

She got up, lit the stub of candle she was allowed, and took a fresh sheet to the bench — partly to think, mostly because her hands needed occupation or they'd start shaking.

Tam appeared within minutes, of course. Children hear everything.

"Can't sleep?" Ines asked.

"I sleep better when the candle's on." Tam climbed onto the stool beside her, uninvited, entitled, home. "Are we doing folds?"

"We're doing folds."

They worked for an hour. Ines taught her simple things first — the blintz base, the waterbomb, the crane that every apprentice learns because cranes teach patience. Tam absorbed each one after a single demonstration, her fingers quick and exact, and Ines watched with a feeling she refused to name growing in her chest like mold in a damp wall.

Then, casually — too casually, she knew even as she did it — Ines said, "Show me your special one again. The one Nana Vess taught."

Tam's hands hesitated. Just a moment. Just long enough.

"It's secret," the child said.

"I know. I won't tell. But see—" Ines took the sheet and reproduced the first two folds from memory, the diagonal offset, the valley aligned to crease. "I already know how it starts. Someone taught me a long time ago. Maybe the same someone."

That was a gamble and she knew it. Tam stared at her with an expression far too old for six — the expression of a child weighing whether an adult is safe, an evaluation no child should have to make — and then, apparently satisfied, nodded once and completed the sequence. Fold, open, refold. Collapse. Pocket.

"There," Tam said, setting it on the bench between them like an offering. "But the real ones have writing inside. Nana Vess put writing inside and then you can't see it anymore. That's the whole point." She looked up, suddenly fierce. "You won't tell the grey soldiers?"

"No," Ines said. "Never."

Because now she understood the whole mechanism. The fold wasn't decoration. It was concealment — a dispatch packet that read as blank paper, message hidden in the interior pocket, invisible unless unfolded along the correct memory-locked sequence. The ministry had been using its own nursery children as living dead-drops. And the fragment in Tam's coat lining, the one matching Ines's codes — it hadn't been hidden in the coat by chance. It had probably traveled *inside* such a fold for part of its journey, before someone sewed it into fabric for permanence.

Marta — if that was the mother's name; Ines still didn't know it — Marta hadn't just smuggled a document out. She'd smuggled out a child who was herself a kind of document, written in creases and muscle memory, readable by perhaps forty people alive.

Ines blew out the candle and lay back down and did not sleep at all.

* * *

On the eighth morning the inspections ended — or paused; Ines trusted pauses the way sailors trust calm water — and Aldric reopened the shutters and let the gray light back into the shop, and life resumed its counterfeit shape.

Ines stood at the bench gluing spine linings and watched Tam in the corner, teaching herself variations, folding and unfolding scraps from the bin, and felt the tenderness in her chest finish its slow hardening into something with edges.

She had told herself, in the first days, that she would keep the child out of it. That Tam was cargo to be protected, not evidence to be examined. That was a lie she had needed, and lies, like folds, only hold so long before the paper remembers its original shape.

The truth was simpler and worse: Tam was proof. Proof that the fragment came from inside the ministry. Proof that someone with nursery access had turned against whoever currently held the other half of the key. Proof that could authenticate Ines's own fragment — or damn everyone connected to it. And proof, in a city falling to Colonel Vessane, had a short shelf life. Evidence got destroyed. She had seen it done. Sometimes she had been the one sent to prevent it; once, years ago, she had been the one sent to do it, and she had burned a farmhouse full of correspondence rather than let it fall into the wrong hands, and she had told herself the smoke smelled like duty.

She would not burn this child. Whatever else happened, whatever the arithmetic demanded — that line she drew now, standing at the bench with glue drying under her fingernails.

But protecting Tam and using what Tam knew were not, she was beginning to understand, entirely different acts. The fold pattern authenticated the fragment's origin. The fragment authenticated the armistice key. And the key — both halves of it — was the only currency in Vellmark that could still buy anything worth having.

She needed to reach Deputy Minister Corvain. Aldric had mentioned him once, carefully: the one official still answering his door, the one man with a faction, a following, and a stated interest in the terms of surrender. Stated interests, Ines knew, were like stated prices in a besieged market — the opening position of a longer negotiation.

Still. He was a door, and doors were what couriers dealt in.

"Aldric," she said that evening, as they closed the shutters against the curfew bell. "This Corvain. How would a bookbinder's apprentice get a message to a deputy minister?"

Aldric's hands stopped on the shutter latch. He didn't turn around.

"Why," he said slowly, "would she want to?"

"Because I've finished waiting," Ines said, "and I've started counting what I'm carrying. And I think it's time someone in this city knew what it's worth."

Outside, the curfew bell finished tolling, and somewhere down toward the harbor, a warehouse door slammed like the first page of something turning.
