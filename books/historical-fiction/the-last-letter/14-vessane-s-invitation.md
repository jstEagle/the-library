## 14. Vessane's Invitation

The summons came the way winter comes to Vellmark — not all at once, but in small signs that could be ignored right up until they couldn't.

First there was the soldier at the corner of Binder's Lane, standing with the studied idleness of a man who had been told to stand somewhere and was enjoying it. Then the second one, by the fountain, where the water no longer ran. By the third day Ines had stopped pretending not to count them. Three soldiers on a street that held a bindery, a candle-dipper's shop, and eleven shuttered houses did not mean patrol. Patrols moved. These men had planted themselves like fence posts.

"They're not here for us," Aldric said, though his hands, sorting type, told another story. He had dropped the same letterform twice that morning — a *g*, then an *h*, each clattering onto the stone floor like a small confession.

"No," Ines agreed. "They're here for me."

She said it the way she said everything now: quietly, evenly, with the flat calm she had learned in seven years of carrying other people's secrets through other people's wars. But underneath the calm something was moving, the way water moves under ice — fast, dark, going somewhere.

The summons itself arrived at noon, delivered by a boy too young to know what he carried: a card of heavy cream stock, engraved, absurdly elegant amid the ash-smell of the city. *Colonel A. Vessane requests the pleasure of Master Aldric's apprentice at the Customs House, four o'clock.* The word *pleasure* sat on the page like a fly on cream.

"He knows your name," Aldric said. "Your false name."

"He knows more than my name." Ines turned the card over. Blank. Of course blank. Vessane would never put anything in writing; she had learned that much about him from the letters in the back room, from the thirty-year-old correspondence that proved he had sold this city once already, to the old regime, and was selling it again. A man who dealt in paper learned never to leave any of his own behind.

"You don't have to go."

"Yes, I do." She looked up at him — old Aldric, bent as a book spine, who had destroyed his own masterworks two nights ago to hide her fragments inside ordinary rebinds, whose hands still smelled of the glue he'd used to seal her fate into covers labeled *Parish Records, 1809–1814*. "He invited the apprentice, not the master. That means he wants to talk to me alone. Which means what he has to say is either a trap or a bargain." She set the card down. "Traps don't come engraved."

* * *

The Customs House had been the first building the occupation forces repaired, and they had repaired it lovingly — new glass in the tall windows, fresh paint the color of old bone, a great brass eagle over the door that some quartermaster had polished every morning while children starved three streets away. Ines walked past it with her ledger satchel on her hip, empty except for a stub of pencil and the folded note Aldric had pressed into her hand at the door. *If you are not back by six, I will burn everything and take Tam north.* She had not argued. There was nothing to argue. It was the only sensible plan, and it would fail, because Vessane would follow the smoke.

Inside, the air smelled of floor wax and cold tea. A clerk with a face like a closed ledger led her up a staircase, down a corridor hung with maps of a coastline that no longer belonged to anyone, and knocked once on a door before opening it without waiting for an answer.

Colonel Vessane stood at the window with his back to her, hands clasped behind him, watching the harbor. He was smaller than she remembered from the inspections — a compact man, gray at the temples, dressed with the kind of neatness that took time and a servant. On the desk behind him lay a single object, squared to the desk's edge with geometric precision: a book.

Not just any book. She knew it the way you know your own handwriting. Dark calf binding, plain, unlettered. One of the rebinds. One of Aldric's masterworks, destroyed and reborn as a shell around a secret.

"Sit," Vessane said, without turning.

She sat. The chair was real walnut, upholstered, the first comfortable thing she had touched in four months. That was deliberate too. Everything in this room was deliberate. The window faced the harbor so she would see the masts of the blockade ships. The fire was lit so she would feel warmth she hadn't felt since autumn. The book lay on the desk so she would see it and understand, instantly and completely, how badly things had gone.

"Master Aldric's work," Vessane said, still looking out at the water. "I've admired it for years. Even before the war, when I used to come through on customs business. He bound a psalter for the cathedral once — you may have seen it. Gold tooled into the leather so finely it looked like frost." He turned then, and smiled, and the smile was the worst thing in the room, worse than the book, because it was patient. "A craftsman like that shouldn't die in a fire. Don't you agree?"

"I wouldn't know anything about fires, Colonel."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't. Yet." He crossed to the desk but did not sit behind it; he leaned against its edge, close enough that she could smell bay rum and beneath it something fainter, ink, the ink of a man who wrote many letters and burned most of them. "Let's not spend the whole afternoon pretending. You've been very good at pretending — I'll give you that. Four months in that bindery, glue under your nails, learning the trade like a proper apprentice. I watched you carry water. I watched you sweep. I even watched you teach the child to fold paper." Something flickered across his face at that, quickly gone. "Very domestic. Very convincing. But you hold your awl wrong for someone who's been doing it since childhood, and you read the shipping notices every morning like a woman counting days. Couriers always count days."

Ines kept her face still. Inside, the ice-water was moving faster. *Couriers,* he had said. Not spies. Not agents. Couriers. He knew what she was. The question was how much of what she carried he understood.

"You went to Corvain," Vessane continued. "Three weeks ago. Through the fish-seller on Tanner's Row — clever, that, dead drops inside herring barrels, very old school. Corvain thought he was being so careful. Corvain is not a careful man; he is a greedy man with careful habits, which is different." He picked up the rebound book, weighed it in his hand like a grocer weighing fruit. "Corvain told me you offered him half of something. He didn't tell me what. He didn't have to. Because I already knew what you were carrying when you walked into this city, Ines Iversen."

Her name. Her true name, spoken aloud in a warm room above a conquered harbor. She let herself show nothing, but she felt the floor tilt slightly, the way a deck tilts.

"The republic's diplomatic cipher," Vessane said, turning the book over, running a thumb along its spine. "The authentication key for the armistice terms. Half of it sewn into a ledger binding, if my information is correct — and my information is always correct; it's the only thing I've ever been faithful to. And then, remarkably, a second half arrives at your workshop door in the coat of a six-year-old child." He shook his head slowly, with something that might have been genuine admiration. "Someone inside the ministry trusted a bookbinder's shop more than their own chancery. I've been trying to decide whether that says something about you or something about them. I suspect both."

"Say what you've brought me here to say," Ines said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it.

Vessane set the book down. Squared it to the desk's edge again. And then he told her, in the tone of a man discussing freight rates, exactly what he wanted.

* * *

"There is a ship," he said, "leaving in nine days. Neutral flag, out of Halvard. It carries cotton samples and a diplomatic pouch and twelve passengers who bought their way aboard with money or information, whichever they had more of. There is a berth on it with your name on it — your real name, papers made properly, passage guaranteed. The blockade will open for that ship the way a door opens for a key." He paused. "You'd take the child, of course. I'm not a monster. Two berths. The boy grows up in a country where the bakeries still open in the morning."

Ines said nothing. She had learned, in seven years of this work, that silence was a tool with two edges, and she held it carefully now, letting him fill the room with his offer, letting him hear how generous he sounded to himself.

"In exchange," Vessane said, "you give me the key. Both halves. Complete, functional, unaltered."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I burn the bindery." He said it without heat, without emphasis, the way a man reads a price list. "Not today. Perhaps not next week. But the order will go through, and fires in occupied cities are poorly investigated. An old man asleep above his glue pots. Tragic. Common. No one writes ballads about bookbinders." He straightened from the desk. "And the child goes into the ministry orphanage registry, which, since the ministry no longer exists in any functional sense, means he goes wherever I say he goes. There are families inland. Farming families. They take children for labor. The boy is healthy; he'd live. Probably."

The fire crackled. Out in the harbor, a gull cried once and fell silent, as if embarrassed.

"You should understand what you'd actually be giving me," Vessane went on, softer now, almost confiding, and this was the part she would remember afterward, lying awake, the part that would visit her for years: he explained it to her like a colleague. "With the authentication key, I don't need to alter the terms. Alteration leaves traces; alteration is for amateurs like Corvain. No — with the key, I can simply *choose* which draft gets signed. The original terms, the harsh ones, the ones drafted in the first week when the generals were still angry — those get authenticated as legitimate. The gentler draft, the one your ministers bled to negotiate, dies unsigned and unremembered. The city keeps its buildings and loses everything else. Its archives. Its names. Its ships. Forty years of debt paid by people who weren't born when the debt was incurred." He shrugged, a small, neat shrug. "Or — the reverse. If I give the key to the other faction, the gentle terms stand, and I am the man who saved Vellmark, and I extract my price quietly over twenty years. Either way, I win. The only variable is you."

"Why tell me all this?" Ines asked. "Why explain the whole machine?"

"Because I want you to make an informed choice." His eyes were pale gray, the color of the harbor in February. "I find that people who sell something knowingly sleep better than people who are robbed. And you strike me as a woman who values sleeping well."

* * *

She walked back through streets that seemed to have rearranged themselves while she was indoors. The light was going amber and long; smoke from the evening cook-fires rose straight up in the windless cold, a forest of thin gray columns, a city exhaling. She passed the fountain where the second soldier had stood. He was gone. They were all gone. The message had been delivered; the fence posts had served their purpose.

Tam was in the workshop window when she turned onto Binder's Lane, his face pressed to the glass, watching for her. When he saw her he didn't wave — he had learned not to, these last weeks, learned the economy of visible joy — but his whole body waved, the way children's bodies do, and something in Ines's chest pulled tight and painful, like thread drawn through cloth.

Aldric met her at the door. He searched her face, then her hands, then the empty satchel. "Well?"

"He has the book," she said quietly, once the door was shut and bolted. "One of the rebinds. He knows everything, Aldric. My name, the route, the drop, the child. He offered me two berths on a neutral ship out of Halvard. Passage for me and Tam. Papers, guarantee, the blockade opening like a courtesy call." She unwound her scarf with hands that were, she noticed distantly, perfectly steady. "In exchange for the key."

"And if you refuse?"

"The bindery burns. Tam goes to a labor farm inland." She heard the words leave her mouth and was surprised at how small they sounded, how inadequate, like coins of a debased currency. "He gave me nine days. The ship sails in nine days. The signing is in four."

Aldric lowered himself onto his stool, slowly, the way old men lower themselves when the news is too heavy to remain standing under. For a long moment neither of them spoke. The workshop settled around them — the tick of cooling glue pots, the smell of leather and paste, the small sounds of a place built entirely out of keeping things together.

"Nine days," Aldric said at last. "He's giving you time to think. That's a mistake. Men who give you time to think have forgotten what thinking does."

"It's not a mistake. It's the trap." Ines sat down across from him, at the workbench where she had spent four months learning a trade she would never practice, and laid her hands flat on the wood. "If I run, the key stays hidden, the harsh terms get signed by default, and Tam lives — fed, safe, alive, in some border town, hating a country he can't remember. If I stay and deliver, the true terms might stand, and this city keeps its name and its records and its dead counted properly — and Tam—" She stopped.

"And Tam," Aldric finished for her, gently, "may not."

Through the doorway to the back room she could see him: Tam, curled on the cot with Marta's fragment of cipher hidden in the lining of his coat hanging on a nail above him, asleep with one fist under his cheek, breathing the deep even breaths of a child who did not yet know that adults were deciding the shape of his whole life in the next room. He had learned to fold a crane last week. He slept the way cranes flew, she thought absurdly — one wing tucked, ready.

"There's a third option," Aldric said.

"There's always a third option. It's usually the one that costs the most."

"Then we should start paying early." The old man's eyes, in the lamplight, had lost their usual tiredness and found something older underneath it, something from thirty years ago, from the young man who had bound documents for a regime he despised and told himself he had no choice. "The quarantine quarter," he said. "No soldiers patrol it. The chancery is four days' walk if you go the long way, through the streets nobody wants. You'd carry both halves. You'd carry the boy."

"They'd stop us at the chancery door anyway. A courier and a child—"

"Not a courier." Aldric reached beneath the workbench and drew out a parcel wrapped in oilcloth, and began, with the deliberation of a man performing a rite, to unwrap it. Inside lay documents — parish records, shipping manifests, a guild ledger — the ordinary rebinding jobs, the camouflage. And beneath them, at the bottom, wrapped separately in a scrap of linen: the completed key, both fragments joined, sealed between boards as thin as communion wafers, invisible to any search that wasn't looking for the shape of a nation's surrender. "Not a courier," he said again. "A widow taking her son to the chancery to register his father's death. Grief opens doors that credentials can't. I'll bind you the proof tonight — a death record, a marriage line, a history in paper. I'm good at making histories, Ines. It's the only thing I've ever been good at." He looked at her, and his voice roughened. "It's time I made one worth the name."

Ines looked at the parcel. At the child asleep beyond the doorway. At the window, where the smoke columns rose from a thousand hearths in a city that had nine days, or four, or none, depending on what she chose before dawn.

Outside, far off toward the harbor, a ship's bell rang the hour — eight slow strokes, unhurried, indifferent, marking time for everyone equally: for the colonel with his two berths, for the ministers with their factions, for the child with his folded paper cranes, and for the courier who had until morning to decide which country she belonged to — the one that had paid her, or the one that was about to be erased.

She picked up the parcel.

"Aldric," she said. "Start binding."
