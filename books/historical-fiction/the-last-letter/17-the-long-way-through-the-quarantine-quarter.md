## 17. The Long Way Through the Quarantine Quarter

They left before the fourth bell, when the fog off the harbor was still thick enough to drink.

Ines had dressed Tam in Aldric's smallest apron, turned inside out so the workshop's mark didn't show, and stuffed the coat — both fragments sewn now into its lining, one at each shoulder like a pair of hidden wings — beneath a bundle of spoiled binding leather that smelled of tallow and rot. No soldier would search spoiled leather. Even hungry men had their limits.

"The chancery is on the far side of the city," she had told him the night before, kneeling so their eyes were level. "We're going to walk there. It will take all day."

"Is it far?"

"It's not far," she said, which was true. "The road there is long," she said, which was truer.

Because they could not go by the avenues. The avenues belonged to Vessane now, and Vessane had sent word through every garrison post: a woman and a child, traveling together, to be held for questioning. He hadn't needed to describe her face. There were perhaps forty women left in Vellmark who moved with purpose, and he knew most of them by name.

But the quarantine quarter — the low warren of streets behind the salt-works where the fever had burned through two winters ago and never fully died — no one patrolled there. Not the occupiers, who considered plague a cheaper garrison than soldiers. Not the city watch, which had stopped drawing pay in the spring. The quarter had been sealed with rope and painted crosses and then, in the way of such things, simply forgotten, the way you forget a tooth that has stopped aching because the nerve is dead.

That was the road. Through the dead nerve of the city.

* * *

The painted cross on the barrier gate had faded to the color of old blood, and someone had written beneath it, in charcoal, in a child's uneven hand: *we are still here.*

Ines read it twice and then stepped through.

Inside, the quarter was not dead. That was the first thing she understood, and it rearranged everything she had braced herself for. She had expected silence, ruin, the smell of lime pits. What she found instead was a hush — but a lived-in hush, the hush of a place that had learned to move quietly the way other places learned to sing. Smoke rose from chimneys in thin, disciplined threads. Laundry hung between buildings, gray sheets that had been washed grayer. A man sat on a stoop mending a net with hands that moved without hurry, as though hurry were a luxury from another country.

He looked up when they passed. He looked at her face, then at Tam, then at the bundle of spoiled leather under her arm, and something passed over his expression — not recognition, exactly. Recognition of a *kind*. Of what she was.

"You'll want the Solder's Row," he said quietly, not stopping his mending. "The main lane floods near the tannery. And the well water at the corner is clean; the rest is not." He bent back to his net. He did not ask who she was or where she was going. He did not say good luck, because luck was a word for people who still believed outcomes were undecided.

"Thank you," Ines said.

"Don't thank me," the man said. "Thank whoever taught you to come this way."

No one had taught her. But walking on, Tam's small hand in hers, she began to understand that someone had — that the route through the quarter was a thing known the way routes are known by the forgotten, passed along in the grammar of shrugs and glances, and that she had been permitted into it the moment the net-mender saw what she carried. Not the cipher. He couldn't have seen the cipher. He'd seen the way she walked: like a woman carrying something heavier than luggage, careful of it, willing to be hurt before letting it be hurt.

Tam tugged her hand. "Why does everyone look at us?"

"Because we're strangers."

"No," Tam said, with the terrible precision of six-year-olds. "They look at you like Master Aldric looks at his good knives. Like they hope you don't break."

Ines said nothing. Ahead, the lane narrowed between houses leaning toward each other like conspirators, and somewhere a woman was singing — low, half-voice, a work song worn down to almost nothing, three notes repeated like a heartbeat. The song followed them for two streets and then handed them, note by note, to another voice further on, so that they walked the whole morning inside a chain of singing, passed from throat to throat, and Ines realized the songs were signals. *Strangers moving. Let them pass. They are ours today.*

She had been a courier for eleven years. She had crossed borders in coffins, in bridal processions, in a grain barge with the codes strapped to the small of her back while inspectors probed the cargo with bayonets. She had thought she understood how a message moves: by concealment, by speed, by the courier's solitary cunning.

She had never once been carried.

* * *

At midmorning they came to the Solder's Row, and the quarter showed them its wound.

The fever had taken this street whole. Whole families, whole floors, sometimes whole buildings — and the city, busy dying in its own larger way, had never come to bury properly. So the quarter had buried its own, and it had done it in the only material it had: paper. Every door on the row bore a pasted sheet, names written in ink gone brown, some doors layered so deep with memorial pages that they looked upholstered, soft with grief, like the walls of a reliquary. Names, dates, the occasional line of a life: *wove the finest nets on the east quay.* *Sang at weddings.* *Gave his coat to the Kessler boy, who lived.*

Tam stopped at one door and read, slowly, moving his lips. He was learning letters from Aldric's type cases, and he read the way children read — aloud, possessively, as if naming made the thing his.

"Mira Solen," he pronounced. "Who was Mira Solen?"

"A person," Ines said. "Someone loved her enough to write it down."

"Did the sickness take her?"

"Yes."

"Are we going to get sick?"

"Not today." She crouched beside him. "Tam. Listen to me. Today we are doing the most important work I have ever done, and you are part of it. Not my helper. Part of it. Can you carry that?"

He considered this with the gravity of a foreman. "What's my part?"

"Walking. Being brave about being tired. And remembering everything you see, because someday people will ask me if I imagined all this, and I want to be able to say: no, there was a witness."

"I'm the witness," Tam said, trying the word out.

"You're the witness."

He straightened. For the next hour he narrated the quarter under his breath — *cracked bell, blue door, dog with only one ear* — an inventory of a country that existed nowhere else, and Ines let him run on, because a child's voice reading the world aloud was the only psalm this street would get.

An old woman emerged from a doorway as they passed and pressed something into Tam's hand without breaking stride: a heel of bread, hard as a roof tile. She was gone before Ines could speak. The bread was warm. Someone had baked it this morning, in a quarantined quarter, with flour that must have cost more than the oven, and given it to a stranger's child because the stranger was carrying something for all of them.

Word traveled ahead of them faster than they did. Ines could feel it — the slight change in doorways as they approached, faces appearing and withdrawing, the singing handing them along. By noon she had stopped pretending she didn't understand. At a corner where a shrine to some harbor saint stood black with candle smoke, a boy of maybe ten fell into step beside them, uninvited, and walked them three streets to a gap in a collapsed wall, pointed through it, and left without a word. Beyond the wall lay the last stretch: the salt marsh flats, the neutral ground, and far across the gray water the chancery's dome, pale as a fingernail paring against the sky.

Four hours' walk. Two days until the signing.

And behind them, faint but unmistakable, smoke rising from the direction of the harbor district — not the burning of warehouses this time. Something smaller. Something with a chimney she knew.

* * *

They rested in the shell of a salt-storehouse, and there Ines allowed herself the luxury of fear.

It came on her the way fever comes — not all at once but in waves, heat and chill alternating. She sat with her back against a wall crusted white with salt residue and took stock the way her handler had taught her, item by item, because inventory was the antidote to panic.

Item: both fragments, sewn at Tam's shoulders, complete now, authenticated in her own hand against the ministry paper stock Aldric had identified. The key was real. The original terms — the decent ones, drafted before Corvain's faction got its knives into the margins — could be signed with them.

Item: Corvain expected forged terms to pass unchallenged, and had no idea the authentication key had slipped his grasp. His confidence was her only weapon bigger than the key itself.

Item: Vessane knew. Vessane had offered her passage, and when she refused — she had seen the refusal land in his eyes like a coin dropped down a well — he had begun to close accounts. Aldric was already in custody. The bindery was marked.

Item: the smoke behind them.

She made herself do the arithmetic anyway. If the bindery burned, it burned after Aldric had already given it away, brick by brick, masterwork by masterwork, buying her the night she needed to finish the sewing. A building was a shell. A man in custody was a debt she intended to collect. And a city about to sign either mercy or plunder —

"Your hands are shaking," Tam observed. He was sitting cross-legged opposite her, folding a scrap of paper with the intense concentration Aldric's lessons had given him. Crane, boat, the strange nursery fold Marta must have learned at her mother's knee in the ministry corridors — he did them all in sequence, over and over, a rosary of paper.

"Couriers shake," Ines said. "It's how the body carries what the mind can't."

"That doesn't make sense."

"No," she agreed. "Most true things don't, until afterward."

He held up his latest fold: a boat, slightly lopsided. "For crossing," he said. "When we get to the water."

"We're not crossing the water. Just walking beside it."

"Then it's for later," Tam said, and set it carefully in his pocket, and Ines felt something in her chest turn over — because that was the whole doctrine of the last four weeks in a child's sentence. *For later.* Everything they were doing, every name on every door, every heel of bread, was for later. The republic might die tomorrow at a signing table, but a boat folded today would still be a boat in twenty years, in a pocket, in whatever country came after.

She ate half the bread and gave him the rest, and they slept in shifts, and the salt walls held the day's warmth around them like a held breath.

* * *

They reached the marsh flats at dusk.

The tide was out, and the exposed mud gleamed like hammered pewter under a sky the color of a healing bruise. The chancery stood on its rise across the flats, lit now, windows gold, flags of three nations hanging limp in the windless evening — neutral ground, the one acre in fifty miles where Vessane's writ ran thin and Corvain's faction had to smile in public. Tomorrow the delegates would gather. Tomorrow terms would be read aloud and signed, and whichever words were on the page would become the truth retroactively, the way conquerors' words always did.

Unless a bookbinder's apprentice arrived first with a coat full of contraband honesty.

Ines looked down at Tam, asleep on his feet more than standing, leaning against her leg, his pocket with its paper boat sagging. Then she looked back once at the city — at the smoke columns, at the dark mass of the harbor district where a workshop had stood that morning, at the quarter behind them where a hundred doors wore the names of the dead in brown ink and a hundred living voices had sung two strangers through without asking why.

*We are still here,* the charcoal had said under the painted cross.

Yes, she thought. You are. And tomorrow, if the mud holds and the sentries sleep and a deputy minister's confidence holds one day longer than his ambition deserves — you will still be here, with your names, your nets, your songs, accounted for. Not erased. Never erased.

She lifted Tam onto her hip — he was lighter than he should have been, everyone in this city was lighter than they should have been — and started across the flats, following the line of stakes that marked the safe path through the mud, toward the lighted windows and the waiting table.

Halfway across, a sentry's lantern came alive on the chancery wall ahead of them, swinging toward the flats.

And behind them, from the city, a bell began to ring — not the alarm pattern, not the fire pattern, but a slow, deliberate tolling she had never heard before, one stroke, a pause, one stroke, like a heart beating in a chest too quiet, like a message in a language only the forgotten quarter spoke.

The lantern stopped. The sentry was listening too.

Ines stood in the pewter mud with the child on her hip and the key at his shoulders, and waited to learn what the bell was saying.
