## 22. After the Republic

The town was called Brannholt, and it had the good manners to be unremarkable. It sat where the river bent lazy around a hill, on the border that had been redrawn twice in living memory and would probably be drawn again, and nobody in it cared much about anything except the weather and the price of tallow. There was one church with a crooked spire, one market square where geese were sold on Thursdays, and one bindery — small, narrow-fronted, wedged between a chandler's and a house that smelled permanently of onions.

Tam knew every board of its floor by the sound it made underfoot.

It had been a year since Vellmark. A year since the smoke, since the crowd in the square where the terms were read aloud and the names of the dead were entered into the record instead of burned. A year since Ines had sat up all night writing, her pen scratching like a small animal in the walls, sealing everything into an envelope she had addressed simply: *For Tam, when you are tall enough to carry it.* It lived now in the bottom drawer of the workbench, beneath the bone folders and the spare awls, and Tam did not open it. Not yet. Ines had said *when you are grown*, and Tam had decided — with the fierce private logic of a seven-year-old — that grown meant as tall as the shelf where the ledgers stood, and Tam was not yet as tall as the shelf.

But some mornings Tam stood against it anyway, back flat, chin lifted, measuring.

This morning was a working morning, which most of them were. The shop smelled of wheat paste and leather and the faint vinegar tang of the mordant Ines used for dyeing calf skins. Winter light came through the front window at a slant, catching the dust motes and turning them into slow golden fish swimming through the air. Ines sat at the bench with her sleeves rolled, sewing signatures of a parish register for the church — Father Ellam paid promptly, which made him Ines's favorite client, though she'd never say so aloud.

"Thread," Ines said, without looking up.

Tam fetched it. Linen thread, waxed, the color of pale honey. This was the liturgy of their days: *thread, paste, folder, press*. Tam handed each thing before it was asked for half the time now, and when that happened Ines's mouth did the small thing at the corner that was not quite a smile but lived in the same neighborhood.

"Two more stations and we rest," Ines said. "Then bread."

"Then bread," Tam agreed solemnly, because bread was serious business in Brannholt in winter, and because agreeing with Ines about bread was the easiest treaty either of them ever signed.

Outside, a cart rattled past. The geese complained. Somewhere a door slammed and someone shouted cheerfully about the cold. The ordinary noises of a town that had never been besieged, never burned, never learned what its own harbor looked like alight. Tam had stopped flinching at slamming doors sometime in the spring. Mostly stopped.

Ines's hands moved in the old rhythm — needle through station, pull, tension, needle through again. Her left hand still bore the scar across the knuckles from the night of the workshop fire, a pale seam like a stitch in the skin itself. Tam liked to trace it sometimes when Ines dozed in the chair after supper. Ines pretended to sleep through it. Both of them knew the other knew. It was one of the many things they said without saying.

The bell above the door rang.

Tam looked up first — Tam always looked up first; a year ago looking up had been the difference between safety and soldiers — but it was only the post rider, a red-cheeked boy named Oskar who delivered to the whole river road and considered himself a man of great consequence.

"Brannholt bindery!" he announced, though there was no other bindery within a day's ride. He set two items on the counter: a parcel wrapped in sacking, and a letter.

A letter.

Tam watched Ines look at it. Watched the way her whole body went very still, the way it did — Tam had learned this too — when something mattered so much that moving might break it. The letter was plain enough. Cheap paper, travel-worn, creased from a long journey. But the handwriting on the front —

Tam climbed onto the stool beside the bench, the high one reserved for "observing, not touching," and leaned over Ines's shoulder.

The address was written in a careful, upright hand. *To the binder, Brannholt, by the river bend.* No name. But below it, small, almost hidden in the flourish of the direction:

*Marta.*

* * *

Ines did not open it at once.

She held it the way she held rare books — palms flat, supporting the whole, never pinching the edges. Tam could feel the change in the air of the shop, the way the light itself seemed to lean in. Ines turned the letter over once. Turned it back. Her thumb rested just beside the name without touching it, the way one hovers a hand near a flame to learn if it is warm.

"You knew her face," Tam said quietly. It was not a question. Tam had heard the story in pieces over the year, the way bread is broken — never all at once. The woman at the workshop door at dawn. The note that said *forget my face*. The coat lining, the cipher fragment, the ministry paper stock Aldric had recognized with his old man's guilt rising off him like heat off stones. And later, the last letter Ines had sealed, in which — Tam had been told this much, and only this much — Marta's face was described in loving detail, line by line, so that a child might someday find her in whatever world came after.

"I knew her face," Ines said. "I wrote it down so I wouldn't lose it." She glanced sideways at Tam, and now the corner of her mouth moved properly. "You are allowed to breathe, you know."

Tam realized they had been holding their breath. They let it out slowly, the way Ines had taught them — *when your hands shake, breathe out longer than you breathe in* — and watched Ines slide one finger under the seal.

The paper opened with a soft crackle. Two pages, closely written. Ines read in silence, and Tam read over her shoulder, sounding out the harder words, and neither of them spoke for a long time.

*Marta's letter* — and it was hers, unmistakably, the same upright careful hand that had once written *forget my face* on a scrap left on a doorstep — began without greeting, as if the writer had feared that any word of tenderness might fail her before she reached the middle.

*I have been free since midsummer,* it said. *They exchanged the clerks in the third week of the armistice — the ones who signed nothing and saw everything. I was worth less than a horse to them and so I was let go with the horses. I will not tell you where I am. Not yet. Not because I distrust you, but because I spent a year learning to be no one, and the habit sits deep, and I find I want to arrive somewhere rather than flee toward it.*

*I heard the terms were signed. The true ones. I heard Corvain was named before the delegates, and Vessane with him, and that the city surrendered with its archives intact. I sat in a kitchen in a town whose name I won't write and I wept into the dishwater like a fool. You did that. Whatever else you were paid or not paid, ordered or not ordered — you did that, and my son is alive because of it, and I have run out of ways to say it so I will stop trying and simply say everything else.*

*Is he well? Is he tall? Does he still fold the nursery patterns — the crane with the double neck? I taught him that when he was four. If he has forgotten it, don't tell me. If he remembers it, tell me twice.*

*I am coming north in the spring, when the roads clear. I will come to Brannholt, because you were foolish enough to give your true trade to the chancery registry, and I have always loved you for being bad at hiding. If the child does not wish to see me, I will understand, and I will leave a parcel at the door and take nothing with me but the sight of the roof over his head. If he does wish it —*

Here the handwriting wavered, the upright letters leaning for the first time, and then steadied.

*— if he does wish it, then I will knock, and I will show him my face, and he may forget it or keep it, whichever he chooses. That is the whole of what I ask. I spent a year asking everyone to forget it. I should like to spend the rest of my life being remembered by one person.*

*Tell him the woman at the door loved him before she ever left him, and that leaving him was the largest thing she ever did, larger than the theft, larger than the war, and that she would do it again, and that she is sorry, and that she is not sorry, and that both of these are true the way both halves of a key are true.*

*Yours in debt and hope,*
*Marta*

*P.S. — Tell him also that the cipher fragment was sewn into his coat by my own hand, badly, because I am a clerk and not a seamstress, and that if he ever wondered why his coat hung crooked on one side, that was the republic hanging off his shoulder. He carried it better than any courier I ever watched walk a ministry corridor.*

* * *

Ines set the letter down on the bench, face up, and pressed both palms flat against the wood on either side of it, as if steadying herself against a swell.

Tam read it again. Then a third time. The words went in slowly, some of them needing to be sounded out, some of them needing no reading at all because Tam already knew them — knew them the way you know a song you've hummed all your life without knowing its name.

*Does he remember the crane with the double neck?*

Tam slid off the stool.

The paper drawer was the second one down, beneath the marbled endsheets. Tam pulled it open, dug past the bundles of twine and the tin of brass paper fasteners, and found it: a sheet folded long ago into a shape that had traveled from a ministry nursery in Vellmark to a bindery in Brannholt inside a child's pocket, unfolded and refolded a hundred times until the creases shone like veins in a leaf. The crane with the double neck. Its wings had gone soft as cloth. One neck was shorter than the other because Tam had torn it slightly during the winter in the quarantine quarter, the long cold walk through streets where no soldier patrolled, when the paper bird had been the only thing in Tam's pockets besides hunger.

Tam set it on the bench beside the letter.

Ines looked at the bird. Then at Tam. Then at the bird again, and something crossed her face that Tam had seen only twice before — once at the signing, once the night she finished the last letter and sealed it and sat with her forehead resting on her stacked fists for a long time before she blew out the lamp.

"She asked me to tell you twice," Ines said. Her voice was steady, mostly. "That you remember."

"He can hear it himself," Tam said. "When she comes."

Ines was quiet a moment. "The roads won't clear until the thaw. It could be April. Maybe May."

"That's fine." Tam picked up the paper crane, carefully, by its body, the way Ines had taught them to handle anything old. "We'll need to fix the shop up. She can't see it like this — the glue pot's filthy, and the window's got a crack, and your dye bottles look like a witch lives here."

"My dye bottles look like a professional establishment."

"They look like a witch lives here."

Ines laughed — a short, surprised sound, as if the laugh had gotten out before she could review it — and then she pulled Tam against her side, one arm around the small shoulders, and they stood together in the winter light looking down at the letter and the little worn bird, the two halves of a message finally lying next to each other on the same bench.

"We'll need bread," Ines said into Tam's hair. "More than usual. Women who've been fleeing for a year arrive hungry."

"And tea?"

"And tea. And we'll air the good room. And—" She stopped. Tam felt her go still again, that particular stillness, and followed her gaze to the bottom drawer of the workbench. The envelope lay where it had lain for a year, beneath the folders and the awls. *For Tam, when you are tall enough to carry it.*

"Not yet," Tam said.

"No?"

"When she comes. I'll open it when she comes. So somebody can explain the parts I don't understand." Tam looked up. "You wrote it. Some of your handwriting is terrible."

"My handwriting is celebrated in three countries."

"It's terrible in all three."

Ines laughed again, easier this time, and crouched down so they were eye to eye. Up close, Tam could see the year in her face — softer now around the eyes than it had been in Vellmark, the wariness settling gradually into something that might, in another year or two, become peace. "All right," Ines said. "When she comes. Together."

"Together," Tam agreed, and shook on it, small hand in scarred one, a treaty ratified over the workbench among the wheat-paste pots and the witch's bottles of dye.

* * *

They worked the rest of the day as they always worked, but differently underneath, the way a river runs differently over new stones. Ines sewed the parish register and Tam collated the sections and passed the thread before it was asked for, every time. At noon they ate bread with butter, real butter, bought extravagant with the church's payment, and Ines ate slowly and looked out the window at the frozen river and said, apropos of nothing, "April," and Tam said, "May, if the thaw is late," and neither of them explained further, because no explanation was needed.

In the afternoon a customer came in wanting a family Bible rebound, and Ines quoted him a fair price and talked leather with him for twenty minutes, and Tam swept the floor and watered the paste and straightened the dye bottles, which did not help; they still looked like a witch lived there.

At dusk Tam stood against the ledger shelf, back flat, heels touching the wall, chin lifted as far as it would go.

"How close?" Ines asked, not looking up from her sewing.

Tam measured with a flat hand, marking the air above their head. "One book. Maybe two."

"By April, then."

"By April," Tam said, and came down, and helped close the shop.

That night, after supper, while Ines banked the stove, Tam took the paper crane from the bench and set it on the mantel, upright, facing the door — so it would be the first thing to greet her, wings soft, one neck short, carrying the republic on its back the whole way north.

And outside, far down the frozen road, the wind came off the hills carrying the first promise of the thaw, and the town of Brannholt slept its unremarkable sleep, and in the small bindery between the chandler's and the onion house, a lamp burned late while a woman wrote back across the distance — *Come in April. He remembers. Come twice.*
