## 1. The Silence in the Gears

The summons arrived on a Tuesday, which Zephyrine Hartwell considered a bad omen, because nothing good had ever come to Verenchal on a Tuesday. The baker's ovens failed on a Tuesday. The miller's daughter broke her arm on a Tuesday. And on this particular Tuesday, a garrison orderly stood in her workshop doorway with snow melting off his shoulders and a note in his hand, looking at her the way men looked at her — that is to say, not at all.

"Master clock at the observatory's stopped," he said. "Colonel wants it fixed."

"Does the Colonel say why it stopped?"

The orderly blinked, as though the question were impertinent, which it was, which was why she'd asked it. "Clocks don't need a why. They need a clockmaker."

Zephyrine set down her tweezers with the care of a woman who did not want her hands to tremble where anyone could see. "Tell the Colonel I'll come after the morning frost."

"He said now."

"Then tell the Colonel that a clock stopped for three days will keep for three hours, and a clockmaker with frozen fingers will fix nothing."

The orderly looked at her for the first time — a brief, startled glance, the way one glances at a chair that has spoken — and left.

She gave it two hours. Long enough to seem obedient, short enough to seem merely slow. This was the arithmetic Zephyrine had been performing her whole life: how to be present without being noticed, useful without being remarkable, a woman shaped exactly like the space between other people's attention. Old Perrin, who had raised her, called it her gift. He had meant it as praise. She had long suspected it was a diagnosis.

The observatory sat at the top of the Verenchal road like a stone eye, all dome and granite, with the garrison compound curled around its base. She had been inside twice in her twenty-six years — once as a child, once to oil the turret bearings — and both times she had kept her eyes on the floor and her mouth shut, and both times she had left feeling she had escaped something rather than visited somewhere.

Marta, the cook, met her in the scullery passage.

"You'll want to go up the back stair," Marta said, not looking up from the onions she was butchering. "The front hall's full of officers this morning."

"Thank you, Marta."

"I said the front hall's full of officers." Chop. Chop. "I didn't say anything about who's in it."

Zephyrine understood. Marta was one of the speakers — the small, brave category of servant who communicated entirely in things they hadn't said. There were perhaps four of them in the whole household. The rest had learned the other language, the one made of silence, and Zephyrine recognized the accent because she was fluent in it herself.

The back stair was narrow and smelled of tallow and boiled linen. She passed a chambermaid on the second landing who pressed herself against the wall and studied the stones as though they might be examined later. She passed a footman who did not press himself anywhere at all, who looked straight through her with the polished emptiness of a man who had decided, years ago, that seeing things was a habit best broken early. She catalogued them both, the way she catalogued everything, without meaning to.

The clock room was at the top, beneath the dome, and the master clock itself was a great case of black walnut taller than two men, its face painted with stars instead of numerals because some long-dead astronomer had thought that charming. Zephyrine had always found it a little vain. Clocks were meant to tell time, not to show off about it.

She opened the case and listened.

A stopped clock has a smell, if you know how to listen with your nose — oil gone sour, dust cooked warm, and underneath, faintly, something else. Something like a snuffed candle.

She climbed the step-ladder and put her ear to the movement. Then she took off her gloves, because the truth of a mechanism lived in the fingertips, and began her examination from the escapement down.

The escapement is the heart of a clock. It is the small, cruel genius that lets the wheels move only a tooth at a time, that takes the rush of falling weights and chops it into seconds. A good escapement whispers. This one was not whispering. This one had a dead anchor pallet — jammed — and wedged into the gap where the pallet should swing, packed in with a care that made her stomach turn, was a fold of oilcloth.

Not a rat's nest. Not a wasp's nest, though she had found those before, in tower clocks, and removed them without ceremony. This had been placed. Folded twice, tucked flat, sized to the millimetre to stop the pallet without breaking it. Whoever had done it had understood escapements.

Zephyrine stood on the ladder for a long moment, her fingers resting on the oilcloth, and felt the workshop of her mind go very quiet.

She could put the pallet back in order, rehang the weights, and walk away. She could report that the clock had suffered dust and cold, collect her fee, and return to a life in which she was no one, which was the life she had spent twenty years building, plank by plank, screw by screw.

She unfolded the oilcloth.

Inside were papers. Six of them, folded small, gone soft at the creases from years in the clock's warm dark. She carried them down the ladder to the window, where the light came through the dome's oculus in a single pale column, and she opened the first one with hands that were, she noted with distant professional interest, perfectly steady. Old Perrin had taught her that too. *Your hands are the one part of you that must never lie, little Zeph. Train them.*

They were muster rolls. She knew the format — her town was a garrison town, and garrison paperwork was its true native tongue. Names. Dates of conscription. Dates of desertion, entered in a clerk's neat hand.

Except that she knew some of those names.

Tomas Verel, entered as deserted on the fourteenth of October. Tomas Verel had been the boy who pulled her hair in the churchyard when she was nine, and he had not deserted anything. He had marched east with the conscript company in the autumn two years past, and he had not come back, and the town had been told they'd been reassigned to the far depots, and the town, being a town, had shrugged and gotten on with its winter.

The second paper was not a roll. It was a report, in a hand she did not know, describing — in the bloodless grammar of officialdom — an *incident of refusal* at a place called Kolvary Ford, and the *necessary measures* taken, and the number of men *struck from the rolls as deserters to preserve regimental standing*.

Nineteen names. Nineteen boys who had said no, or run, or simply been in the wrong frozen field on the wrong morning. Nineteen desertions that had never happened, because dead men do not desert. Dead men are made to.

Zephyrine sat down on the ladder's lowest step, because her knees had opinions, and folded her hands, and made herself breathe the way one breathes when regulating a delicate train — slow, even, counted.

*So that's why the clock stopped,* she thought. *Not sabotage. Confession. Someone hid the truth inside the one machine in Verenchal that no one ever opens, and then the truth stopped it, and the Colonel sent for me, because I am the only person in this town who opens clocks.*

She almost laughed. It came out as a small, unlovely sound, and she pressed her knuckles to her mouth.

The question that mattered was not *who hid these*. The question was *who knew the clock was stopped, and why did it take three days to send for me*. Three days was time enough to burn papers. Three days was time enough to remove them. Unless the person who hid them had also been the person who reported the stoppage — had wanted them found, or wanted them *handled*, or had simply lost their nerve and let the machinery of the Colonel's household do the deciding.

Or unless the Colonel himself had sent for her precisely because he knew what was in the clock, and wanted to watch who came, and what they carried out.

The thought settled over her like the dome's cold shadow.

Below her, through the floor, she could hear the household breathing — boots in the front hall, the clink of the officers' coffee things, Marta's knife going steadily through onions. A whole building of people who had each, at some point, decided what they would not see.

Zephyrine refolded the papers along their old creases. She did not put them back in the escapement.

She took a spare anchor pallet from her kit — she carried spares of everything, a habit of Perrin's, *the world is mostly broken, little Zeph; carry spares* — and fitted it, and adjusted it, and listened to the escapement take up its whisper again. Then she set the pendulum swinging and watched the hands crawl forward over their painted stars, and the master clock of Verenchal began, once more, to tell the time.

She wrapped the papers in the oilcloth, and the oilcloth in a strip of clock-cleaning rag, and the whole of it went into the false bottom of her tool case, beneath the hammers, where it weighed nothing and everything.

When she came down the back stair, Marta was waiting in the scullery with a cup of coffee she had not been asked to make.

"Fixed it, then?" Marta said.

"Dust and cold," Zephyrine said. "The escapement was jammed."

"Old clocks," Marta said, and handed her the coffee, and her eyes — dark, tired, older than her face — held Zephyrine's for exactly one second longer than they needed to. "My brother worked in a garrison office once. He always said the truth in this town keeps better time than any clock. It's just nobody ever winds it."

Zephyrine took the cup. Her hands, trained hands, did not tremble.

"Tell your brother," she said quietly, "that someone's thinking of winding it."

Marta went back to her onions. Chop. Chop. Steady as an escapement.

Outside, the snow had begun again, soft and thick, filling in her footprints on the observatory road behind her. By evening there would be no trace that anyone had climbed to the dome at all. Zephyrine walked down into the town with her tool case against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and behind her, above her, the master clock ticked on in its dome of stone — keeping time, keeping secret, keeping watch.

And somewhere below, in the warm rooms of the garrison, Colonel Vasseur stood at his window with his coffee going cold, watching a small grey figure descend the white road, and wondered — not for the first time, and not, he suspected, for the last — why the clock had needed two repairs in one winter, and what, exactly, a clockmaker carried home in her case that she hadn't carried up.
