## 3. A Corpse Who Writes

She didn't scream. I want that on the record, because it's the thing I admired most about her for years afterward and the thing I least expected. I'd rehearsed this moment on the train down from Yorkshire — rehearsed it in the corridor of a hospital ward in France, in a dugout at Passchendaele, on every stretch of track between there and here — and in every rehearsal, the woman who'd spent two years copying a dead suffragist's letters by lamplight did something dramatic. Fainted. Wept. Called me a liar, a madman, a German agent. Threw the letters back in my face.

What she actually did was stand very still on the wet road with my bundle of envelopes in her hands, and look at me the way a bank clerk looks at a man trying to pass a forged note: not with horror, but with professional interest.

"You said Holloway," she said.

"I did."

"Infirmary wing?"

"So I was told."

"And these are dated after." It wasn't a question. She turned one envelope over, examining the postmark as though the date might be a clerical error she could correct with sufficient sternness. "The eleventh of March."

"The eleventh of March," I agreed.

"Verity Ashcombe died on the fourth of February."

"Then somebody ought to tell her," I said. "Because she's been writing letters ever since."

* * *

You'll want to know who I am before you trust anything else I say, and fair enough. Tobias Crane. Twenty-nine years old, or thirty — I lost a year somewhere between Arras and Amiens and never bothered going back for it. Before the war I was nothing much: a hospital orderly at the Middlesex, which is a way of saying I was strong-backed, soft-hearted, and cheap, and could be relied upon to empty bedpans without fainting, a combination rarer than you'd think. The war took me the way it took everyone, in installments. First my civilian clothes, then my brother, then most of my hearing in the left ear, and finally, in the spring of '17, a piece of German shrapnel that lodged itself near my shoulder blade and gave me a permanent souvenir and a ticket home.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. The part that matters starts in the winter of 1916, at a place I only ever knew as the Annexe.

Every prison has an annexe. That's not official language; it's orderly language, the vocabulary of men who carry things. The Annexe at Holloway was where they put the women whose names were too loud for the ordinary wards. You'll have read about the hunger strikes, maybe, if the papers let you — the force-feeding, the tubes, the Home Office's Cat and Mouse Act, which sounds like a joke until you've watched it played out with actual cats and actual mice and no laughter anywhere. The government had discovered that letting a famous suffragist starve to death in prison made bad headlines, so they'd invented a system: arrest them, starve them half-dead, release them to recover, rearrest them the moment they could stand. A revolving door with bars on it.

And when even that became inconvenient — when a woman's name had been in the papers too many times, when her case had become a scandal rather than a lesson — there was a third door. Not the front door and not the revolving one. A door at the back, through which women went in under one name and came out under another, or didn't come out at all, officially speaking.

I didn't understand any of this at the time. I was twenty-four and my ambitions ran to hot meals and dry socks. What I understood was that in November of 1916 I was transferred from the Middlesex to help cover staff shortages — so many orderlies had gone to France that the prisons were running on skeleton crews — and that among my duties at Holloway was carrying trays to a woman in the infirmary wing registered as Mrs. Grey.

Mrs. Grey. I've thought about that name more than any man should think about any name. Grey is what you call a woman when you intend to make her disappear into it. The matron who assigned it probably thought herself clever. Or perhaps it wasn't cleverness at all; perhaps it was just the bureaucratic instinct, the same instinct that files a living woman under pneumonia because a file needs closing and a scandal needs burying, and the paperwork doesn't care which comes first.

Here is what I knew about Mrs. Grey in those first weeks: she had a bad cough, she wrote constantly, and she tipped like a duchess, except that she had nothing to tip with, so instead she paid me in conversation, which was worth considerably more.

* * *

I want to describe her properly, because everything that follows depends on you believing me, and belief is built out of small details the way a wall is built out of bricks.

She was thinner than the photographs — the papers had run her picture often enough in the old days, before her name became unspeakable — and her hair had gone grey at the temples in a way that forty-one is too young for, though God knows the last few years would age anyone. Her hands were the remarkable thing. They were always moving: folding, smoothing, turning a page, adjusting a blanket that didn't need adjusting. Hands that couldn't bear to be idle. And when she wrote — she wrote all day, on scraps, on the backs of forms, on anything the authorities hadn't confiscated yet — those hands moved like they were playing a piano only she could hear.

They took her pens away twice while I was there. Both times she got another one within a day. I never asked how. Some economies run entirely on smuggling, and prison is one of them.

The first time we really talked, she caught me looking at the letter she was writing and I had the grace to be embarrassed about it. Most of the staff wouldn't meet her eyes. You don't look directly at a woman the state has decided isn't there; it's like staring at the sun, or at your own conscience.

"You're new," she said.

"Crane," I said. "Tobias."

"Tobias Crane." She tried the name out like a coin bitten for gold. "Do you know what they're saying about me, Tobias Crane?"

"That you're Mrs. Grey," I said, "and that Mrs. Grey has pneumonia."

Something happened in her face then — not a smile exactly, more the place where a smile goes to die. "Pneumonia," she repeated. "Well. It has a certain dignity. Better than influenza. One can die of pneumonia respectably." She coughed then, badly, and I fetched the water jug, and when I came back she was watching me with an expression I'd learn to know very well: the expression of a woman taking inventory.

"How much do you need?" she asked.

"For what?"

"To be bought, Mr. Crane. Everything in here is for sale. The matron's silence costs a shilling a week. Dr. Fenwick's discretion costs two. Your predecessor sold his entire soul for half a crown and a promise of a reference." She said it without contempt, which somehow made it worse. "I'm not accusing you. I'm asking the price, so I can decide whether to pay it."

And here's the thing I've never been able to explain well, the thing that sounds like a lie when a man tells it about himself: I didn't want anything. My whole life I'd wanted things — a better position, a girl named Ada who married a grocer, enough money to stop counting — but standing in that infirmary ward, being offered a bribe by a woman the government insisted was dead, I found I wanted only one thing, and it wasn't for sale.

"I want to know what happens to your letters," I said. "That's all. Everyone writes in here and nobody's letters go anywhere. It's like posting them into the sea."

She looked at me for a long time. Then she held up the page she'd been writing, and I saw that the ink was still wet, and she said:

"Then you're exactly the man I've been praying for, and I don't even believe in God."

* * *

The arrangement lasted eleven weeks. I can account for every one of them.

It worked like this: she wrote, sometimes three or four pieces a day, on whatever paper survived the searches. I carried them out. The trick was embarrassingly simple — the laundry. Prison laundry went out in sacks twice a week to a contractor in Islington, and no one searched dirty linen going *out*, because no one imagined anything valuable lived in it. I'd learned the trick from a chap at the Middlesex who used it for tobacco; I improved on it by wrapping each letter in oilcloth against the bleach water. Eleven weeks, sixty-odd letters, and not one lost.

Where they went is a longer story. There was a woman in Islington — I'll keep her name out of this, she's earned her quiet — who ran a dressmaking shop above the laundry contractor's, and she forwarded things. Some went to family. Some went to a solicitor. Some, I suspect, went into the growing archive of evidence that various people were keeping against the day the government's arithmetic finally failed. I never opened them. That matters to me, so I want it to matter to you: in eleven weeks of carrying a dead woman's correspondence, I never once read a word. It seemed to me that a man who reads other people's letters will eventually sell them, and I wanted to remain the sort of man who wouldn't.

I know how that sounds coming from a man who smuggled them out in laundry sacks. Hypocrisy is just principle wearing a disguise. But there it is.

In February, my transfer orders came through. France. The army had remembered it owned me. On my last shift, Mrs. Grey — Verity, she'd finally told me her real name, in the low voice people use for contraband — pressed a folded paper into my hand along with the usual oilcloth packet.

"This one's yours," she said. "Not for forwarding. For keeping."

I still have it. I'm not going to tell you what it says. Some things a man carries privately or he's carrying nothing at all. But I'll say this much: it was a promise, and it was addressed to a future neither of us could see, and it began with the words *If I am ever erased entirely—*

Two days later I was on a boat. Three weeks after that, Mrs. Grey was dead of pneumonia, according to the certificate, and the newspapers printed four lines about it, and the world moved on to the business of killing itself on a larger scale.

* * *

Now we come to France, and I'll be quick, because the war is the part of my story everyone has a version of and nobody wants to hear again.

I was at Passchendaele, which means I don't have to describe it; you can supply your own mud. I carried stretcher handles for eight months, then carried a rifle for four, and in June of this year a shell fragment found the gap between my shoulder blade and my spine where the anatomy books promise there isn't one. The surgeons left the fragment where it was — too close to anything important, they said, which struck me as optimistic, since nothing of mine had been important in years. They gave me a Blighty wound and a limp that comes and goes with the weather, and in September they shipped me home to a convalescent hospital in Yorkshire, where I lay in a clean bed listening to other men scream in their sleep and thought, with the peculiar clarity of the morphine, about unfinished business.

Because here's what I couldn't shake, lying there: the promise. *If I am ever erased entirely.* And then the newspaper clipping a nurse showed me, some months back, four lines long: the death of Verity Ashcombe, pneumonia, Holloway infirmary. And the arithmetic that wouldn't come out right. Because I had seen her alive in January. I had carried her letters out in February. Dead women don't write, and the woman I'd known was many things — ill, imprisoned, hunted, magnificent — but she was not, in any sense available to the five senses, dead.

People will tell you shell shock makes a man credulous. I say it makes him precise. When you've watched the ground open and swallow men whole, you develop a professional interest in what actually gets buried. And something in me — the orderly in me, the man whose whole trade was knowing what was in each bed and why — kept insisting that the bed marked *Ashcombe, deceased* had been empty when the certificate was signed. Moved out the night before. I didn't know that then; I only felt it, the way you feel weather coming in a healed fracture.

So I started asking questions. Quietly. A discharged matron here, a laundress there, the dressmaker in Islington who'd forwarded the letters and had, it turned out, been asking her own questions since February. And the answers pointed north, and the questions started getting noticed, and a man I'd approached in a pub in York told me, with genuine kindness, that I ought to stop, because the last fellow who'd asked about Mrs. Grey had lost his job, and the one before that had lost more than that.

Which is how I came to be standing on a wet road outside London in October, having followed a government motor car for three days based on nothing but a hunch and a description — a young woman driver, dark-haired, careful, who collected the mail of a house where a lady lived under guard — and having intercepted her at the one point on her route where the road narrowed and the hedge gave me cover.

Desperate hope seeking a witness. That's the whole of my strategy, if you can call it a strategy. I had letters written by a woman the state had declared dead, and I had no journalist brave enough to print them, no solicitor willing to swear to them, no proof beyond my own word and my own scar. What I needed — what I'd crossed a country with a hole in my back to find — was someone inside. Someone the authorities trusted. Someone with access, and patience, and a reason of her own.

Someone, in short, like the woman currently holding my bundle of envelopes and looking at me as though I were a sum that refused to balance.

* * *

"They'll have copies," she said at last. Her voice had changed — steadier now, or perhaps just colder, the warmth packed away somewhere for safekeeping. "The censors. If she's been writing since February, the prison authorities will have logged every letter. They'll know the handwriting. They'll know it's hers."

"Of course they know," I said. "That's rather the point. They know, and they're doing nothing, because doing nothing is the entire scheme. You can't prosecute a dead woman, Miss Ravensworth, and you can't release one either. She exists in the one place a modern state keeps its problems: nowhere at all."

The rain had started again, fine as sifted flour. She didn't move toward the car. I watched her weigh me — the limp, the service stripe, the bundle of letters, the whole improbable shape of my arrival — and I understood that I was asking her to gamble everything she had on the testimony of a stranger, and that she knew it, and that she was going to do it anyway, because I'd seen the folio-shaped bulge in her coat and I knew what kind of woman carried a secret archive against her ribs for two years.

"If I help you," she said slowly, "and you're wrong — if this is a forgery, or a trap, or a madness —"

"Then we'll both hang separately," I said. "Or whatever it is they do to women nowadays. I honestly can't keep up."

That almost got a smile out of her. Almost. She looked down at the topmost envelope again, and her thumb moved across the direction line, and I saw her stop breathing for a moment — a small, involuntary hitch, the sound a person makes when the ground tilts.

"What is it?" I asked.

She didn't answer straight away. When she did, her voice had dropped to nearly nothing, and the rain filled the silence around it.

"There's an ink blot," she said. "Lower left corner. Every letter she ever sent through my hands had one — same blot, same corner. I used to think it was her pen. I copied it a hundred times." She looked up at me, and her eyes were bright and terrified and absolutely certain. "Mr. Crane. This blot is in the wrong corner."

I stared at her. "What does that mean?"

"It means," said Elodie Ravensworth, "that whoever taught her to fake her own death also taught her to change her pen. Which means she knew they were watching. Which means—" and here she pressed the letters flat against her chest as though they might fly away, "—which means she's been leaving us a trail on purpose. And we're the first ones clever enough to follow it."

Somewhere over the hedgerow, a train whistle sounded, long and lonesome, heading north.

North, I thought. Toward the answers. Toward the house where a corpse sat writing by lamplight, waiting for someone to read her.

"Well then," I said. "Shall we?"
