## 2. The Child at the Door

The rain came down that August night like a debt being collected, hard and indifferent, hammering the slate roofs of the mews until the whole world sounded like applause for something terrible. Rafael Marchetti stood in the doorway of the coach house with a cigarette he had no intention of smoking, watching the water leap off the cobbles, and told himself — as he had told himself every night since the fourth of August — that the war was a thing happening to other men, in other countries, in languages he had deliberately forgotten how to grieve in.

He had been wrong, of course. He had known he was wrong the way one knows a tooth is bad: not by argument but by the low persistent ache of it. Trieste was only a name on a map to England, but to Rafael it was a harbour, a smell of salt and coffee grounds, his mother's hands dusted with flour, his brother Marco laughing too loudly in a language now classified as enemy noise. The map had drawn a line through all of it and called the line *frontier*, and men in London who had never seen the Adriatic spoke of Austria the way one speaks of weather — something to be endured, something foreign, something that could be turned out of the house.

Which was, in its way, exactly what had happened. Three days ago, Mrs. Danvers the cook had gone to her sister in Croydon. Yesterday, the girl who did for the bedrooms had wept over her wages and left before dawn. The Calloway house on Cheyne Walk stood half-empty, its windows going dark one by one like eyes closing, and Edith Calloway had walked through the rooms with her chin up and said, *We shall manage, Rafael,* as though managing were simply a matter of spine. He would have driven her through fire for that voice. He suspected, some nights, that he already had.

The cigarette stayed unlit between his fingers. He did not smoke where the neighbours might see an Italian man loitering in the dark; he had learned to make himself a piece of the architecture, a fixture, a thing that belonged to the house rather than to any nation. Invisibility was a craft. He had been practising it for eleven years, ever since he'd stepped off the boat at Southampton with one suitcase and a name the customs officer pronounced as though tasting something spoiled.

*Marchetti.* Even now, saying it aloud felt like opening a window in winter.

He was about to go inside when he heard it — footsteps on the mews cobbles, quick and uneven, the kind of walk a person makes when they are carrying more than luggage. A figure came around the corner by the far wall, hooded against the rain, and Rafael's body understood before his mind did. There is a way frightened people move. He had seen it in Trieste in '98, in the dock strikes, in the faces of men running from the Austrian police. The body runs ahead of the news.

"Signor Marchetti." A woman's voice, low, accented — German flattened into English by urgency. She stopped three paces away, close enough that he could see rain streaming off her hood, and she did not lower it, which meant she had been crying or was about to. "You are the driver. The Italian."

"I am nobody," Rafael said, which was his first answer to everything now, and then, because the voice went through him like a needle finding an old scar: "Who sent you?"

"A friend from Trieste." She said the city's name softly, the way one touches a wound to check if it still hurts. "Anna Weiss. You will not know me. That is the point. I need you to know nothing at all — do you understand? Nothing. Not my face, not my name, not this." She shifted the bundle in her arms, and Rafael's heart stopped being a heart and became a drum.

It was a child. Six years old, perhaps seven, asleep or pretending to sleep against the woman's shoulder, dark hair plastered to her forehead, wrapped in a wool coat two sizes too large with buttons that caught the lamplight like small wet coins. Behind the woman, dragged by its strap, came a leather satchel, sealed with wax, heavy-looking, the kind of bag that changes lives.

"You cannot mean—" Rafael began.

"The Home Office has published the order. Enemy aliens. Registration, internment, repatriation — they use whichever word fits the mood of the week. I am Austrian. I have cared for this child for four years, and in four days they will come for me, and I will not let them put her in a institution while I sit behind wire in the Isle of Man." Her breath came fast. "Her father is dead. Her mother is dead. There is an aunt in Liverpool. There are papers. Everything is in the satchel, and everything depends on it reaching Liverpool, and I cannot carry both."

"Then take the child and leave the papers—"

"The papers *are* the child." Anna Weiss said it with such ferocity that the sleeping girl stirred. "You don't understand what she is to certain men. What she's worth dead. Signor Marchetti — Rafael." She stepped closer, into the lee of the doorway, and the rain fell behind her like a curtain closing on a stage. "In 1908, in Trieste, there was a young man who printed pamphlets against the police. The Austrian constabulary wanted him very badly. A woman hid him in her cellar for nine nights and told the officers, to their faces, that she had never seen him. Nine nights. Do you understand what I am telling you?"

Rafael's mouth had gone dry. Marco. His brother Marco, twenty-two years old and magnificent and reckless, printing sedition in a cellar that smelled of onions and lamp oil, while their mother knelt before a stranger and lied with her hand on a saint's medal. He had never known the stranger's name. His brother had died at Messina in the earthquake, years later, safe at last from everything except the earth itself, and Rafael had carried the debt of those nine nights like a stone sewn into his coat, unpayable, unnamed.

"You are her," he said quietly.

"I am her." Anna Weiss set the child gently against his chest, and Lucia — for so the woman called her, *Lucia, piccola, dormi* — turned her face into his collar without waking, trusting and boneless as only children and drunkards can be. "And the debt comes due tonight, across your enemy lines and mine. I am sorry. I am so sorry. But there was no one else. Everyone else has already run, or been taken, or sold themselves." She pressed the satchel's strap into his free hand. It was heavier than a child. "Do not open it until she is asleep and the door is locked. And signor — look at me once. Now. So you can spend the rest of your life forgetting my face."

He looked. Brown eyes, a scar through one eyebrow, ordinary, exhausted, human. He looked, and he forgot her even as he watched, because forgetting was the price and he had always paid his debts promptly.

She kissed the child's hair, said something in German too soft for him to catch, and walked back into the rain without hurrying, which was the bravest thing he saw that night. At the corner she paused — he would remember that much, though not the face above it — and then she was gone, and the mews filled again with the sound of water on stone.

* * *

Inside the coach house, with the door bolted and the single lamp turned low, Rafael laid the child on his narrow bed beneath his one good blanket and sat on the floor with the satchel between his knees like a man at confession.

The wax seal broke cleanly. Inside: a oilcloth packet, dry despite the rain; within that, documents in careful order, as though someone had spent their last calm hours arranging them. Shares — certificates, thick paper, embossed — in the Halloran & Voss Shipping Company, registered to one Emil Vogel, deceased. A will, dated 1911, with a codicil dated March of this year, the ink still sharp-edged, the signatures crisp. And a letter, unsigned, in a clerk's precise hand, that Rafael read twice because the first time his eyes refused it.

The codicil disinherited the child. The letter said why that was impossible: the codicil was forged, and one of the witnesses to the forgery — the second signature, the one that made it lawful — was Gerald Calloway. Brother to Edith Calloway. Brother to the woman whose letters Rafael had carried and read for eleven years, whose causes he drove her to, whose voice could make him forget he was a servant and a foreigner and a man made of packed suitcases.

He read the letter a third time. Outside, the rain kept up its indifferent applause. On the bed, the child breathed in the small whistling rhythm of deep sleep, one fist curled under her chin.

Enemy property, the newspapers said. Enemy aliens. A child declared by paperwork to belong to the wrong side of a line drawn by men who would never meet her. And the papers in his lap said she belonged to no side at all — she belonged to herself, to a dead father's honest shares, to an aunt in Liverpool, to a promise made in a cellar in Trieste six years before anyone thought to draw the line.

Rafael Marchetti, who had spent his whole English life ensuring that nothing could be traced to him, understood that everything was now traced to him: the child, the satchel, the crime, the choice. He thought of Edith's face if she learned what her brother had signed. He thought of the Home Office sweep he'd read about in the evening paper, the registration lists, the knock at doors. He thought of his own suitcases, packed these three weeks beneath his cot, waiting.

A small voice broke the silence.

Rafael looked up. The child was awake, sitting up in the blankets, regarding him with grave dark eyes that held none of a child's usual softness — eyes that had already learned to take inventory of adults.

"You're the letter man," Lucia said. Her English was careful, faintly accented, the English of governesses and schoolrooms. "Anna said you would be. You carry the letters, and you keep them safe, and now you'll keep me safe too. Until Liverpool." She recited it like a lesson learned for examination. Then, with the terrible directness of the very young: "Are you going to say yes?"

The lamp flickered. Rain on the roof. Eleven years of careful invisibility, and a six-year-old had seen straight through him in ten seconds flat.

"Yes," Rafael said, and heard in his own voice the sound of a man stepping off a ledge. "I am going to say yes."

Lucia nodded once, satisfied, lay back down, and was asleep almost immediately — the sleep of a child who has been handed over and has decided, provisionally, to trust the hand. Rafael sat on the floor a long while, the forged codicil in his lap, listening to her breathe.

Then he took out pen and paper, and began — for the first time in his honest life — to write a lie: a travel pass for a child travelling north with her uncle, one R. Marchetti, naturalised subject of His Majesty, which he was not, escorting family, which she was not, to a funeral, which had not occurred.

Outside, somewhere toward the river, a whistle blew, long and thin, and did not stop.
