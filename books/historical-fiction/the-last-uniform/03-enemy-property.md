## 3. Enemy Property

*Rafael*

The mews smelled of wet straw and coal smoke, and Rafael Marchetti stood in the doorway of the coach-house with a six-year-old girl's hand folded inside his own, listening to London decide what it thought of him.

It had been three days since Anna vanished into the rain with her face turned away, three days since the satchel came to rest against his boot like something washed up from a wreck. Three days of Lucia sleeping in the groom's old room above the stables, of porridge made on a spirit stove, of teaching her — without meaning to, without deciding to — the small liturgy of his mornings: which floorboard announced visitors, which window showed the lane, how to fold herself into silence when boots rang on cobbles.

She learned fast. Children of the frightened always did. He had been one himself once, in Trieste, counting footsteps outside his father's shop until counting became a kind of prayer.

"Signor Rafael." Her voice was a whisper worn smooth by repetition. "The grey cat is back."

"The grey cat is not a cat," he said, for the fourth time that morning. "The grey cat is Constable Dunn, and he has been standing at the mouth of the mews since Tuesday because he believes no one interesting would ever live above horses."

Lucia considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Does he think you're not interesting?"

"I am an Italian who drives a motorcar," Rafael said. "To him I am furniture with an accent."

But the joke sat wrong in his chest, because it was only half a lie, and the other half was the whole truth of England now: that a man's name could turn him from a person into a category between one August morning and the next. *Enemy alien.* He had read the phrase in a dozen letters before he ever heard it spoken aloud — clerks writing to clerks about men like him, the language of ledgers applied to lives. He had always believed words were the safest place to hide. Now the words themselves had grown teeth.

He looked down at Lucia. She was wearing Edith Calloway's youngest niece's outgrown coat, buttoned to the chin, her dark hair stuffed under a cap so that she might pass, at a distance, at dusk, for a boy. The satchel's contents lay redistributed through the coach-house like a confession cut into pieces: the share certificates sewn into the lining of a horse blanket, the disputed will rolled inside the hollow handle of a carriage brush, and the letter naming Gerald Calloway — the letter that could hang a respectable man or save a child, depending entirely on whose hands held it next — flat against his own ribs, beneath his shirt, where it moved when he breathed.

"Today," he told her, "you are my nephew, Luca, come from Naples to learn English horses. You are shy. You do not speak. Shyness is a coat; wear it well."

"And if someone asks my name?"

"You are Luca Marchetti, and your father drives for Miss Calloway, who is a great lady and too busy to be questioned."

Lucia nodded once, solemn as a small nun, and went back to watching the grey cat.

Rafael climbed the ladder to the loft window and looked out over the mews wall toward the house. Belgrave Square wore its new face this morning: flags where there had been none, bunting in colors he'd never seen hung there before, and beneath the gaiety a stillness, the held breath of a city waiting to find out what it had become. Two constables walked the square's perimeter at a pace designed to be noticed. A motorcar he didn't know idled opposite the Calloway front door, its driver reading the same page of the same newspaper for twenty minutes.

They were watching the house. Which meant they were watching everything that fed it, cleaned it, drove it. Which meant the mews was watched too, if less lovingly.

Edith had gone to the march. Against the advice of everyone, against the advice most loudly of her brother, she had gone to stand in Trafalgar Square with ten thousand women while the country rehearsed its grief for a war it hadn't yet begun to bleed in. Rafael had wanted to tell her not to go — had wanted, more precisely, to tell her why, to open the satchel right there in the hall and let the papers speak — but Anna's instruction sat on him like a stone: *the child must not be known, and those who know her are in danger.* So he had buckled her gloves, checked the Daimler's tires, and said nothing, which was itself becoming a kind of lying, and he was beginning to understand that lies, like debts, compounded.

Below, the grey cat shifted its weight. Somewhere beyond the mews wall, church bells began — not for service, but for war, pealing across London like a fever breaking.

"Hold very still, Luca," Rafael said quietly. "The day is starting."

*Edith*

Edith Calloway came home to find her own house studying her.

She saw it first from the motorcar — Rafael driving, silent since the Embankment, his eyes doing that thing she'd noticed years ago and never named, moving constantly, cataloguing, a man reading a street the way other men read newspapers. There were two constables by the railings. There was a man in a bowler who tipped his hat to her with a fraction too much deference, and whose boots were wrong for his coat. And there was Gerald's car already at her door, gleaming, entitled, parked as though the square belonged to the family rather than to any single address.

"Gerald," she said, when the hall door closed behind her. He was standing in her drawing room as though he owned the light coming through it, and beside him — uninvited, unannounced — a solicitor's clerk with a leather case and the apologetic posture of a man paid to be disagreeable.

"Edie." Gerald kissed her cheek. He smelled of good tobacco and better brandy, and beneath both, faintly, of fear. "Thank God you're back. We've had a devil of a business. The governess — Anna Weiss, the Austrian woman — she's gone. Absconded Thursday night, and she took papers that don't belong to her. Firm papers. Father's estate papers."

"Papers," Edith said. She set down her gloves with great care, one finger at a time. Through the window she could see Rafael at the motorcar, wiping the windscreen that did not need wiping, positioned — she realized now, with the strange slow clarity that had been following her all week — precisely where he could see the drawing-room windows and she could see him. "What sort of papers?"

"Shares. Correspondence. Legal documents relating to Marchetti & Voss Shipping." Gerald smiled, and it was the smile from their childhood, the one that had preceded every raid on the biscuit tin. "You remember Papa's little German connection. Well. In wartime, Edie, enemy property is a matter for the authorities. If that woman left anything here — anything at all — we're obliged to hand it over. It's practically treason to keep it."

"Treason," Edith repeated. "In my drawing room. Before you've even asked me how the march went."

"How *did* the march go?"

"A policeman told me I ought to be home knitting." She turned to the clerk. "And you are?"

"Mr. Prewitt, ma'am, of Hargreaves & Prewitt. We act for the firm." He opened his case as though displaying communion wafers. "If I might ask you directly, madam: did the governess leave any effects? A satchel, correspondence, anything of that nature?"

And here was the moment, Edith thought later, though she could not have said so then — here was the hinge on which something turned. Because she had seen the governess last, briefly, Thursday evening, rain streaming off her hat brim, asking in that careful accented English whether she might collect her wages early, saying she was called away to family, saying thank you, thank you, *gnädige Frau*, and pressing into Edith's hand nothing at all, asking for nothing, looking past Edith's shoulder into the square as though memorizing it.

She had seen her. She had said nothing to anyone. And she did not know why.

"No," Edith said. "Nothing. She took her things when she went."

Gerald's eyes stayed on her a moment too long, and she understood that he had asked the question before, of others, and had received answers he liked better.

"There'll be a search, of course," Prewitt said gently. "Standard procedure. The Home Office is compiling registers of enemy aliens — residence, employment, associations. Your household employs—"

"My household employs an Italian," Edith said. "Italy is not yet at war with anyone, Mr. Prewitt, except possibly with itself. Is my driver to be registered?"

"All aliens will be registered, ma'am. It's really nothing personal."

"Nothing ever is," said Edith, "until it happens to you."

Gerald laughed — too quickly, too loudly — and drew her aside under the pretext of admiring the roses on the mantel. His voice dropped to the register he'd used since childhood when he wanted something and meant to have it. "Edie. Listen to me. This isn't a game. There are people — serious people — who believe those papers could embarrass the firm. Embarrass *Father's memory*. If that woman passed through here, if she left so much as a handkerchief—"

"She left nothing, Gerald."

"Because if you're protecting her—"

"Why would I protect her?" Edith asked, and heard, in her own voice, the exact note of her mother defending some long-ago indiscretion, and hated it, and could not stop it. "I barely knew the woman."

"Good." He squeezed her arm. "That's good. Because Prewitt will want to question the staff, and servants invent things, you know how they are. Especially the foreign ones. Send yours round to me tomorrow — the driver, what's his name, Marsden?"

"Marchetti."

"Marchetti. Have him call at the office. Routine questions." He smiled the childhood smile again. "You'll look after yourself, won't you, Edie? This war's going to make everything simple, one way or another. Us and them. It's a mercy, really."

He kissed her cheek again, and the brandy-and-fear smell of him followed him out, and Edith stood alone in her drawing room listening to the front door close, feeling the house rearrange itself around a question she had not yet agreed to answer.

* * *

*Rafael*

Prewitt the clerk, before leaving, insisted on walking the mews. Of course he did. Rafael met him at the coach-house door with the easy boredom of a man interrupted at chores, hay dust on his sleeves, and behind him in the dimness, invisible in the loft's shadow, a small boy called Luca sat perfectly still upon a hay bale, being shy.

"Foreign fellow, are you?" Prewitt said, not unkindly, the way one asks a horse its age.

"Italian, sir. Ten years in England. I drive for Miss Calloway."

"Ten years." Prewitt peered past him into the gloom, at the carriage, at the blankets, at nothing. "Registration will apply to you soon enough. Name, address, photograph. Nothing to fret over, if you've nothing to hide."

"Nothing to hide, sir," Rafael said, and the words came out smooth as poured oil, and somewhere inside him a man he had been for forty years — a man who had never lied to a landlord, a priest, a customs officer, or a wife — stepped quietly out of the room and closed the door behind himself.

He gave Prewitt the tour: the Daimler gleaming under its canvas, the harness room, the feed store. He talked the entire time, about carburetors and coal prices and the difficulty of finding good oats in wartime, a running commentary of harmless detail, and he understood with a cold, delighted clarity that he had learned this from the letters. From ten years of reading other people's words he had learned that the best lie is not concealment but abundance — give them so much true and tedious truth that they cannot imagine a false thing living underneath it.

Prewitt left. The grey cat resumed its post. And in the loft, Lucia crept to the ladder's foot and looked up at Rafael with Anna's grave, frightened eyes.

"Was that a bad man?"

"He was a man doing a job badly," Rafael said. "There are many of those just now."

"Did I do the shyness properly?"

"You did it beautifully." He crouched to her level. "Luca. Tomorrow I must go into the city and answer questions, and you must stay here, very quiet, with Mrs. Hobbs bringing your meals and forgetting immediately that she brought them. Can you be quiet for one whole day?"

"I can be quiet for two," Lucia said, with the terrible earnestness of children negotiating with fate.

Rafael laughed, and the laugh hurt, because it sounded — God help him — like his brother's laugh, his brother who owed his life to an Austrian woman now hunted across a country that had been kind to her once, and whose debt had just arrived, six years old and hungry, calling him *the letter man.*

That night, after the house slept, he took out the letter from his shirt and read it again by candle stub in the loft, though he knew it by heart. *My husband's signature was witnessed by G. Calloway, who was not present on the date sworn.* Eleven words. He had carried confessions before — lovers' secrets, debts, a suicide note once, undelivered — but never one with this weight, never one that touched the family of the woman he served, the woman whose gloves he buckled, whose causes he drove her toward, whose trust was the only country he had ever truly held citizenship in.

Tomorrow Gerald Calloway would question him. Tomorrow he would lie again, fluently, abundantly, and pray the abundance held.

And below him, in the hay, a child slept who was the answer to a question Edith Calloway had begun, without knowing it, to ask.
