## 4. The Reading of Letters

The mews smelled of wet straw and coal smoke, and in the narrow room above the coach house Rafael Marchetti sat at a table meant for polishing harness buckles, and read.

He had been reading other people's letters for eleven years.

It had begun as an accident of grammar. English was his third language and its formal constructions were a country he crossed slowly, so when Edith Calloway first handed him her post to carry to the pillar box on the corner — *you're going that way anyway, Rafael* — he found himself deciphering each sentence twice: once for the meaning, once for the road. A letter to her sister about a hat. A letter to a Member of Parliament about a woman in Holloway being force-fed through a rubber tube. He had stood at the pillar box with that second one in his hand for a long time, the brass slot cold under his thumb, before he let it fall.

After that he told himself it was practice. After that he stopped telling himself anything at all.

* * *

There were rules to it, rules he had never spoken aloud because speaking them would have made them sins instead of habits. He read only what came through his hands. He memorized rather than kept. He never learned anything he could not use to protect someone — though he knew, in the honest hour after midnight, that this last rule bent like a reed whenever it needed to.

Through eleven years of intercepted lives he had assembled, without meaning to, a portrait of the Calloway family more complete than any of them possessed of themselves. He knew that Edith's mother wrote on Sundays and forgave nothing. He knew that Edith's courage ran ahead of her arithmetic — she committed to causes the way other people committed to trains, without checking the timetable. He knew that Gerald, the brother, signed his letters with a flourish that grew larger in years of prosperity and smaller in years of want, and that by 1911 the flourish had shrunk to something cramped and apologetic, like a man signing inside a shrinking room.

That was how Rafael had learned about the debts. Not from any confession — Gerald Calloway would have swallowed glass before confessing — but from the paper itself. The letters from Messrs. Whitcombe & Rennard, solicitors, arrived quarterly, then monthly, then weekly, and their tone moved from courteous to firm to something with teeth in it. Rafael read them standing in the mews with the horses shifting behind him, and understood that the heir of a great shipping name owed money to men who did not extend credit out of kindness.

And he understood something else, something that had no name until tonight: that firms like Marchetti & Voss — no relation, though he had smiled the first time he saw the name on an invoice — did not merely move cargo. They moved cargo *both ways*. He had carried enough of Edith's correspondence with her father's old partners to know that the shipping houses of London and Hamburg and Trieste were like merchants in a market square who shouted insults across the stalls and lent one another umbrellas. War, when it came, would not stop the ships. It would only change whose flag they flew while the same hands counted the same money.

He had known this the way you know weather. Now the weather had broken over his head.

* * *

In the present, the candle guttered and he trimmed it, and Lucia slept in the loft above on a horse blanket, her breath making a small steady sound like a stitch being drawn through cloth.

The satchel's contents lay before him in the order he had arranged them three nights ago, when Anna's footsteps had faded down the mews and he had stood in the rain holding a child and a leather bag and an instruction to forget a face. Three nights of hiding her — feeding her bread and dripping in the loft, teaching her to be quiet when boots came up the alley — and only now, with the household asleep and the police observation outside having withdrawn to the corner for the night watch, could he do the work the bag demanded.

The shares first. Certificates of the Marchetti & Voss Shipping Company, Hamburg-registered, London-traded, in the name of Emil Vogel, deceased. Enough paper, even discounted by war, to buy a street in Liverpool. Then the will — the London copy, with its witnesses' signatures along the bottom edge like birds on a wire. He had looked at those signatures forty times. Two clerks' names he did not know. And between them, in a hand he knew better than his own mother's:

*Gerald A. Calloway.*

A man did not witness a will dishonestly unless there was something in it for him or someone he feared. Rafael turned the page and read the disposition again: everything to the cousin in Hamburg, the English branch of the family, contingent upon certain "management arrangements" with the firm — arrangements that, per the codicil, vested near-total authority in the London directorate. In Gerald. The child got a annuity of two hundred pounds a year, administered by the same directorate, which is to say she got nothing but a receipt.

And then the letter. Emil Vogel's own hand, written — Rafael judged from the tremor and the ink blotting — in the last weeks of his life, in German, addressed to his daughter, to be given to her when she was grown.

Rafael's German was serviceable; Trieste was a city where four languages fought politely in the streets. But this letter was not written plainly. It was written the way frightened men write: half in plain statement, half in reference, naming no names where names were dangerous, using phrases that meant one thing to a stranger and another thing entirely to someone who knew the ground.

To a stranger it read as a dying man's sentimentality. To Rafael it read as a map.

*"My shares are safe,"* Vogel had written, *"because paper does not bleed. Remember that your uncle's kindness has a price list. Remember that Herr W. keeps two books, and the second book is the true one. Remember that the signature on the fourth page was bought, not given."*

The fourth page. Rafael turned to it. Gerald's signature sat there, small-flourished, dated November 1911 — the very season the solicitors' letters had turned weekly, the very season a man drowning in debts might sell the only thing he owned outright: his honesty.

*"Bought, not given."* Bought with what? With the annuity clause, perhaps — the cousin's faction buying a witness cheap. Or with forgiveness of debt. Rafael thought of the cramped signature, the shrinking room, and felt no triumph at all. He had spent eleven years learning people from their paper, and what he had learned was that almost no one was a villain in his own account. Gerald Calloway was a man who had signed his name once, badly, and had been paying interest on it ever since — and now wanted the document that held his name burned, and the child who inherited it erased or exiled, whichever came quieter.

Rafael set the letter down. His hands were not quite steady.

For eleven years he had lived in other people's words the way a fisherman lives in weather — reading them, profiting by them, never blamed for them. Now the words were his responsibility. Somewhere in this satchel was proof that could ruin a powerful man, save a child, and hang a driver if it were found in his coat. Knowledge, he understood suddenly, was like the shares themselves: paper that did not bleed, until someone made it bleed.

* * *

He thought of the first letter he had ever truly read — not deciphered, but *read* — and it was, of course, one of Edith's.

March 1909. A note to her father, brief and furious, announcing that she had joined the Women's Social and Political Union and that she expected neither permission nor pardon. Rafael had carried it to the pillar box and walked ten steps past it and come back, and stood in the drizzle reading it, and felt something turn over in his chest like a key. Here was a woman who wrote her convictions the way other people signed receipts — flatly, finally, without appeal. He had posted it. He had gone home and, for the first time since leaving Trieste, written a letter of his own: to his brother Matteo, three lines, saying that in England there were women who refused to be afraid, and that he thought he might stay a while.

Matteo had written back: *Stay. But remember, Rafi, that brave people are the ones who need drivers.*

He had remembered. He had driven Edith to rallies in Manchester and Newcastle while eggs burst against the motorcar's paintwork, and he had learned the back ways into every hall in the north of England, and he had learned which constables could be talked to and which could only be outrun. He had thought all of it was devotion. Only now, sitting over a dead man's coded letter with a sleeping child overhead, did he understand that it had also been apprenticeship. Everything he knew — the maps, the sympathies, the reading of hands and flourishes and fear — had been tuition for this term.

* * *

The problem, reduced to its bones, was this: a six-year-old girl with an Austrian name could not travel to Liverpool. Not by train, not by road, not past a single ticket inspector with a Home Office circular in his pocket. The registration sweep was coming — the neighbors had already complained of foreign voices in the mews; he had heard them through the wall, the word *Italian* said the way one says *rat*. When the sweep came, it would take him too, unless his papers were perfect, and Lucia's papers were worse than imperfect. They were evidence.

What she needed was a name that drew no eye. An English name. A child traveling with her guardian — a chauffeur, say, taking his employer's ward to relatives in the north while London was unsafe. Employers sent children away all the time now; the trains were full of them. No one questioned a man in a good coat with a quiet child and a first-class ticket bought with cash.

No one questioned — provided the papers were right.

Rafael took out a clean sheet of good rag paper, the kind Edith used for correspondence, and a pen, and sat for a long moment looking at the blankness. In eleven years he had written perhaps twenty letters of his own. His hand, formed in an Italian schoolroom and polished in an English office, sat neatly between the two. He could imitate a solicitor's clerk; he had read enough of their letters to reproduce their cadence the way a mockingbird reproduces a thrush.

He began to write.

*To whom it may concern — This is to certify that the bearer, Miss Lucy Vogel—* no. *Lucy Calloway.* A dead man's name was dangerous; a living man's name was armor, and Gerald would never publicly claim the connection, which made it safer still. *— Miss Lucy Calloway, aged six, a ward of the household of Mrs. E. Calloway of Kensington, is travelling north under the care of the undersigned, her guardian pro tempore, on account of the present disturbance—*

He chose the words the way he had watched Edith choose hers: flatly, finally, without appeal. He forged the firm's stamp by tracing it in pencil from a delivery docket and going over the trace in ink. He blotted it, aged it against the lamp chimney, folded it soft. It was the first document he had ever created to deceive, and his hand did not shake until the end, when he signed it — and found, absurdly, that he signed it in his own true name. *R. Marchetti.* Because a false pass with a false name was a hanging matter, but a false pass with a true name was only a promise: *I am the man who did this. Come and find me.*

Above him, the small breathing paused, then resumed.

And it was in that silence — pen lifted, candle low, the ink still shining on his own name — that he heard, from the direction of the mews gate, the unmistakable creak of the door he had oiled himself and no one else ever used.

Someone had slipped into the yard. Someone who knew the way.

On the flagstones below his window, wrapped in oilcloth against the rain, lay a letter that had come earlier that evening, pushed under the door in a hand he did not know — Austrian stamps, steam-softened, addressed simply: *To the driver.* He had not yet dared open it.

Now, listening to footsteps cross his yard, he wished he had.
