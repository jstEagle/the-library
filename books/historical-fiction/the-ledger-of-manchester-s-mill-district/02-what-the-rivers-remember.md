## 2. What the Rivers Remember

The letter had her family name on it, and that was the first strange thing, because Freya Whitlock had never been family. She'd been the apprentice who swept the inkstone and fetched the lamp oil and learned to hold a pen so steady it looked printed. Josiah Whitlock had never once called her anything but "girl" or "Freya" depending on how many pipes he'd smoked that day.

Now his handwriting — cramped, urgent, the letters leaning like men in a wind — addressed itself to *Miss F. Whitlock* across a sealed envelope that had spent years at the bottom of his desk drawer, beneath a false bottom she only found because the bailiff's hammer had cracked the wood.

She sat alone in the shop with the shutters drawn. Outside, the picket line moved past Ancoats Bridge in its slow circuit, forty strikers where there had been two hundred, their boots on the cobbles keeping time like a heart that refused to stop beating. The bailiffs had given her until Saturday. It was Thursday.

She broke the seal.

*If you are reading this, girl, then I am dead, and not by accident, whatever they tell you.*

Her hands went cold around the paper. She read it twice before the words stopped swimming.

*I have finished the waterways. Every culvert, every drain, every well from the Irk to the Medlock. The atlas is not an atlas. It is a ledger. Hartley's dye works pipes its waste into the well behind George Street — I traced it myself, three nights running, with a lantern and more courage than sense. The Ashworths did the same at their card room. Forty-one children dead of "fever" last summer in streets fed by those wells. Forty-one, and the inquests signed off by Dr. Pell without one body opened. I have marked it all. The plates carry it. Anyone who reads the rivers as I drew them will read the deaths as plainly as print.*

*Do not sell the shop. Do not let them take the plates. Burn nothing. Trust no man who wears a good coat.*

*You were the finest hand I ever trained, and I was too proud a fool to say so while I could say it aloud.*

*— J.W.*

P.S., smaller, almost shy: *Your name goes on the title page. I decided this in March. Argue with me when we meet again.*

Freya read it a fourth time, because grief is stupid and hope is worse, and then she heard the key turn in the front door.

Not Josiah's key. Not hers.

She blew out the lamp and folded the letter into her bodice in the dark, moving by memory through a shop she knew better than her own heartbeat, out the back stair, over the wall into the tannery yard, while below her three men in good coats began pulling drawers from desks with the unhurried confidence of people who owned the constable and half the town besides.

They wanted the plates. Which meant the letter was true, which meant Josiah hadn't drowned drunk on the towpath after all.

She ran toward the bridge and the picket line, because it was the only place in Manchester where a good coat would be noticed and remembered.

* * *

Elias Barrow had learned to read a crowd the way other men read scripture, and what he read at half past nine that night was a young woman walking too fast along the picket line with her collar up and her eyes doing arithmetic — counting exits, counting faces, deciding which of forty starving strangers was least likely to betray her.

He'd seen that look on men coming off a ship, on women leaving a husband, on anyone carrying something worth more than their life.

"You're the mapmaker's girl," he said, falling into step. Not a question. Everyone in Ancoats knew the mapmaker's girl, the one who'd been seen measuring gutters with a tape and writing down what she found in them, which had struck Elias as odd even before the old man drowned.

"I'm nobody's," she said.

"Fair enough." He offered his tin of tea, cold and stewed, and she took it, which told him she hadn't eaten since morning. "You're looking for someone."

"I'm looking for someone who can't be bought."

"That's a short list in this district. There's me, and there's Mrs. Whitlock, and between us we've got one working lung and half a loaf."

That earned him a look — quick, assessing, almost amused despite everything. Good. Fear that couldn't laugh was fear about to break.

"Mrs. Whitlock," Freya said. "His widow. You know her?"

"She brings bread to the picket line Tuesdays and pretends it's stale so we'll accept it. It's never been stale in her life." He walked her past the mill gates, past the black windows where the looms stood idle and cost the Ashworths four pounds an hour, a sum he recited to himself nightly like a prayer. "Now. What do you carry that makes you walk like the ground owes you money?"

She stopped. For a long moment he thought she'd walk on, and he wouldn't have blamed her — he was a stranger, a Scot, a troublemaker with a price on his organizing and no trade but talk. Then she said, "Can you keep a secret from everyone except people who need it?"

"That's the only kind of secret worth keeping."

So she told him. In the shadow of the bridge, in a voice pitched under the wind, she told him about the wells and the waste pipes and the forty-one children, and about the plates sewn into oilcloth in the shop cellar, and about the men in good coats currently turning her master's life's work inside out looking for them.

Elias listened the way he listened to everything: completely, and with his jaw tightening at intervals like a man chewing iron.

"The inquests," he said finally. "Dr. Pell signed them?"

"All of them. Fever, fever, fever."

"My sister's boy died in June. George Street." He said it flatly, the way you say things you've said to yourself ten thousand times. "My sister believed God took him. She's been praying harder ever since, and it's killing her, because you can't pray your way out of thinking you deserved it." He looked up at the mill, dark against the stars. "It wasn't God. It was a pipe."

"It was a pipe," Freya said gently. "And I know exactly where it runs."

For the first time in a year, Elias Barrow felt something in his chest that wasn't anger. It took him a moment to name it. Hope, probably. Dangerous stuff. It had gotten better men than him hanged.

* * *

Margaret Whitlock opened her door at midnight holding a fire poker, saw her husband's apprentice soaked in canal spray and flanked by a large Scotsman, and lowered it with the sigh of a woman whose evening had been going too peacefully anyway.

"They ransacked the shop," Freya said. "I got the letter. They didn't get the plates — the cellar's false-walled, they'll be hours finding it if they find it at all. But they'll come back with warrants, real ones, and Margaret — they killed him. The letter says—"

"I know what the letter says." Margaret's voice didn't shake. She'd been a printer's wife for thirty years; she knew what ink could prove and what men would do to stop it. "Josiah showed me the George Street plate in February. I've been waiting since March for someone to come to this door needing help." She stepped aside. "Come in before the whole street sees you. And you—" she fixed Elias with a look that had survived both a husband and a press-room strike, "—you'll want tea. Don't argue. Nobody wins."

Over the kitchen table, by candlelight, they made the plan the way maps get made: patiently, and knowing some of it would be wrong.

The plates came out of the cellar wrapped in oilcloth, fourteen of them, copper-heavy and beautiful, the rivers of Ancoats rendered in Josiah's fine obsessive hand — and once you knew, you couldn't unsee it. The wells marked with a small cross. The dates of deaths written along the banks like mileposts. A whole district's grief, engraved.

"We move them tomorrow night," Elias said. "There's a magistrate, Alderman Croft — old friend of Josiah's, and the only man on the bench who's ruled against Hartley twice. If these reach him, he can force an inquiry."

"There are checkpoints," Freya said. "Since the strikes started. Toll bridge, the police post by the mill, and the militia cordon at the gates. They're stopping everyone."

"So we don't look like everyone." Margaret was already fetching her sewing basket. "A wool merchant's clerk carries samples to her employer. Clerks get waved through; clerks are boring. Freya, hold still."

The needle flashed. Freya held still, feeling the plates settle against her ribs one by one, fourteen slabs of proof stitched into her coat lining, heavy as conscience.

"Elias walks ahead as escort," Margaret went on. "I'll follow behind with the market cart. If anything happens to either of you, I'm just a widow selling cabbages, and widows are invisible."

"Margaret," Freya said quietly, "if they catch me with these, they won't arrest me. People who drown in canals don't get arrested."

The room went still. Candleflame leaned.

"No," Margaret agreed. "Which is why none of us gets caught. Pass me the thimble."

Outside, far off toward the mills, a whistle blew — once, twice, then a third time, wrong somehow, urgent. Elias crossed to the window and looked out at the street, and Freya watched his shoulders change.

"That's the signal," he said. "Someone's told the constables. They're watching this house already."

Margaret threaded her needle without looking up. "Then we leave tonight," she said, "and we go the long way round."
