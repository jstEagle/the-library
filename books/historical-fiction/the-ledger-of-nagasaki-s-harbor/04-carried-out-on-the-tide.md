## 4. Carried Out on the Tide

The smoke had a taste now, and Xanthe had learned it the way she'd once learned her mother's dialect—by immersion, by necessity. Char and kerosene and something sweeter beneath, the records office burning like a body, all its paper organs giving themselves up to the night sky.

She ran with the ledger against her ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, bound with the sash Aya had given her three winters ago when the harbor still smelled of salt instead of ash. Her left arm hung wrong at her side where a soldier's rifle butt had found it in the dark of the burning corridor, but pain was a language she had grown fluent in, and she did not let it slow her. Behind her, the records office groaned through its death. Ahead, down the shingle path between the fish-drying racks, the water waited, black and patient.

The boy was already at the tideline. He stood exactly where she had told him to stand, small hands folded, watching the fire paint the harbor orange, and when he saw her he did not run to her—he simply turned his whole body toward her, the way a compass needle turns, and that quiet faith nearly undid her more than the blow to her arm had.

"You remember what I said?" she asked, kneeling in the wet sand despite the cold of it soaking through her skirt. "You say nothing. You hold this." She pressed the ledger into his arms. It was heavier than a child should carry; it was heavier than most men carried their whole lives. "These are people. Every page is a person. You keep them breathing."

He nodded. He had not spoken since the night the aide left him on her step with money and an order to forget his face, and she no longer expected him to. Some silences were wounds. Some were armor. She had stopped trying to tell them apart.

Aya's boat came out of the dark the way Aya herself always did—without announcement, without permission, cutting straight toward you as if the space between you had been a formality all along. The little craft grounded on the shingle and Aya was over the side before it stopped moving, hauling it up by the bowline, her face lit from behind by the burning garrison so that Xanthe could not read her expression, only its intensity.

"You're bleeding," Aya said.

"I'm standing."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have time for." Xanthe lifted the boy into the boat, settled him in the bilge beneath the tarpaulin among the smuggled tins and bales, tucked the ledger inside his coat so it rode against his heartbeat. "There are search parties coming along the shore road. Ishikawa's aide leads one of them. They'll be here within the hour."

"Then you get in the boat," Aya said, "and we are gone before they finish their cigarettes."

Xanthe looked at her—at the woman who had taught her that love was not a shelter but a heading—and made herself say it plainly, because Aya deserved plainness. "I stay."

"No."

"They'll search the boat if I'm on it. They'll search everything, because they'll know I know. But if I'm here—" she touched her wounded arm, let the blood show, let herself look like exactly what she was, a nurse caught fleeing a fire—"if I'm here, half-dead on the beach, I can point them north. Up the coast road. Toward the fishing villages. I can buy you the whole night."

"And if they don't believe you?"

"Then I'll have bought you twenty minutes instead of six hours, and twenty minutes is enough to clear the point." She took Aya's face in her good hand. The skin there was cold from the sea wind and warm underneath, and Xanthe memorized it the way she memorized names: completely, against loss. "You come back for me. That's the bargain. Not protection. Not silence. You come back."

"I always come back," Aya said, and for once there was nothing reckless in her voice, only the terrible steadiness underneath it. "It's the one honest thing about me."

Xanthe kissed her once, brief as a bandage applied, and then stepped back onto the sand and pushed the bow out into the black water with all the strength her ruined arm would give her, which was enough, which had to be enough.

The boat slid away. The boy turned once to look at her, and she raised her good hand—not goodbye, never goodbye, just *remember*—and then the dark took them both, the child and the cargo of names, and the tide carried them past the burning harbor like a secret told to the sea.

She gave herself ten seconds to feel it. Then she lay down in the sand among the fish racks, arranged her arm at an angle that told the truth too loudly, and began to rehearse her lies.

* * *

They found her within the hour, as she'd promised. The aide with the soft voice crouched over her with a lantern, and she gave him a performance worthy of the stage: delirious, half-conscious, mumbling of soldiers gone north, of a boat that had passed hours ago, empty, headed for nowhere. She watched his eyes calculate. She watched him choose, as men in collapsing empires always chose, the version of events that required the least paperwork.

They carried her up the coast road, north, in the wrong direction entirely, and she let them, and above the stretchers and the shouted orders the harbor burned on behind her like a second sunset that refused to end.

**Coda — Forty-one years later**

The memorial hall in Nagasaki smelled of camphor and old paper, and every August the same ritual unfolded: the survivors gathered, the mayor spoke, and then an old man with white hair and steady hands would rise, open a glass case, and lift out a ledger wrapped in oilcloth gone brittle as moth wing.

He read the names aloud. All of them. It took four hours now, and no one hurried him. Deserters and dockworkers, foreign laborers and the unclaimed, mothers and boys listed as traitors—one name, near the middle, inked in a careful hand that had measured everything by what fit in a satchel. His father's name. He had learned to speak it from these pages before anyone alive could teach him.

Afterward, as always, the crowd thinned and he walked down to the harbor, where the water kept none of its old secrets. And as always, she was waiting on the bench by the seawall—smaller now, her burned arm long healed into a crook she called her souvenir, her eyes still measuring the world by what could be saved.

"You're late," Xanthe said.

"The ferry was crowded."

"It's always crowded. That's rather the point of a city." She shifted to make room, and he sat, and together they watched the boats come in—fishing boats now, pleasure boats, none of them carrying contraband or children or the weight of erased men.

"Aya sends her regards," he said. "She says the tide still runs true."

Xanthe smiled, and it was the smile of a woman who belonged to no order but her own, whose registry was the sea and whose garrison was the memory of everyone who had ever been told to forget. "Tell her," she said, "that I counted. Every name. Every year. Not one lost."

Below them the tide turned, patient and black and keeping, carrying its cargo of names out and, always, back again.
