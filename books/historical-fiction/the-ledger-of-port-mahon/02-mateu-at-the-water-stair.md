## 2. Mateu at the Water Stair

The water stair below the shop smelled of brine and tar and, on Tuesdays, of the fish carts that came up from the mole before dawn. Tamsin Kavanagh knew its moods the way she knew her own hands: the way it went silver in the early light, the way the stone steps darkened with the tide like a page drinking ink.

She was on the third step from the bottom when he came, because she had learned that the third step was where the sea left things — driftwood, rope-ends, once a whole crate of oranges from a ship that had lost its argument with the rocks off Fornells. She went down most mornings to see what the water had written. It was a habit she would have denied under oath, this small superstition, this belief that the harbor was a press of its own, printing and reprinting the same message in salt.

He came down the stair from above, not up from the water, which was why she did not hear him until his shadow fell across her shoulders.

"Señorita Kavanagh."

She turned. He stood one step higher than she did, which put them nearly level, and he held a wrapped bundle against his chest the way a man holds bread in a famine year. Mateu Ferragut. Clerk to the customs house by day, though what he was by other hours no one in Port Mahon could say with certainty, and he seemed to prefer it that way. He had the kind of face that made you work for your conclusions: dark-eyed, watchful, with a mouth that looked as though it had been designed for silence and occasionally betrayed itself into smiling.

"Señor Ferragut." She kept her voice flat, businesslike, the voice of the shop and not the girl. "My father is upstairs."

"I know. I have come about the type."

"The type," she repeated.

"For the *Alcúdia Almanac*." He said it smoothly, but his eyes flicked toward the door at the top of the stairs, then back to her, and something passed between them that had nothing to do with almanacs. "There are corrections to be made. Marginal notes. Your father asked me to bring the copy."

Her father had said nothing of the sort. Her father had said, only last night over supper, that the season's work was thin and the winter thinner, and had spent an hour afterward staring into the banked fire as though reading something there. But Tamsin had grown up in a house where men spoke in margins — where the real text ran beneath the printed line — and so she only nodded and stepped aside.

"Then you'd best come up. The stairs are slippery after rain."

"They are always slippery after rain," he agreed gravely, and followed her.

* * *

The shop smelled of lead and oil and the particular dry sweetness of old paper, a smell Tamsin thought of as the smell of safety itself. Morning light came through the south window in a long slanted bar full of dust motes, and in that light the cases of type gleamed like fields seen from a height — every letter a small standing soldier, waiting for orders.

Her father was not there. She had known he would not be; Tuesdays he walked out to Es Castell to see about paper stock, and he was never quick about it, and he was never sorry.

"He will be disappointed to have missed you," she said, hanging her apron on its peg.

"He will be exactly where he intended to be," Mateu answered, setting his bundle on the composing stone, "and we both know it."

Tamsin unwrapped the manuscript herself. It was not an almanac. She had not truly expected it to be. Beneath the printed cover sheet — *Alcúdia Almanac for the Year 1798, containing tables of tides, saints' days, and market prices* — lay twenty pages of close, upright handwriting in Spanish, dense as a hedgerow, and she read the first lines standing in the dust-barred light while Mateu watched her with the stillness of a man who has practiced being overlooked.

It was an argument. That much she understood at once, even skimming: an argument about grain prices, about the corvée labor owed to the garrison, about who ate and who paid and who decided. It was signed with nothing at all.

"This isn't an almanac," she said, though they both knew she would set it anyway.

"It contains tables of tides," Mateu said. "And saints' days. And if one reads the marginal notes with attention, market prices. The truth is a matter of typography, señorita. What you print on the page and what you print around it."

"And the imprint?"

"*Imprenta de la Viuda Sintes,* Ciutadella." He named a printer's widow two islands away who had died the previous spring, whose press had been sold, whose name therefore cost nothing and protected everything. "No one can hang a widow twice."

"No one can hang a widow at all," Tamsin said, "which I suppose is the economy of it."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — the smile escaping custody again. "Your father told me you were quick."

"My father tells everyone I'm quick. It saves him having to admit I'm useful."

"It is possible," Mateu said, taking off his coat and folding it over the back of the proofing press with the ease of a man who had done it many times before, "to be both."

* * *

They worked through the morning, and working beside Mateu Ferragut was, Tamsin discovered, a discipline entirely unlike working alone.

He read aloud — quietly, under his breath, translating the Spanish into English as he went so she could follow the sense of what she was setting — and his voice had a low, even music to it, the voice of a man reading scripture or seduction, and she could not decide which, and suspected he counted on her not deciding. When she misread his hand — his *b* and his *v* fought each other like brothers — he leaned over her shoulder to point, and his breath touched her ear, and she set an entire line upside down without noticing until she pulled the proof.

"There," he said, tapping the galley. "You've invented a new language. *Viva el revés.* Long live the reversed."

"It's a legitimate error," she said, feeling heat climb her neck. "Compositors make them. There's a whole vocabulary for it."

"Teach me the vocabulary."

"Why? So you can flatter me with it?"

"So I can know," he said, and his voice dropped into something less playful, "when a thing is wrong on purpose."

She looked up at him then. He was very close, near enough that she could see the ink stain on his right middle finger, the same stain she carried on hers, the mark of their shared trade, and for a moment neither of them moved and the dust turned slowly in the light between them like a held breath.

"A *kern* that overlaps too far," she said finally, stepping back to the case. "A *widow*, a line abandoned at the top of a page. An *orphan*. A word cut off from its family."

"Orphan," he repeated softly. "You printers grieve everything."

"We notice everything," she corrected. "Grief is just noticing that won't stop."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "Yes," he said, as though she had confirmed something he'd long suspected about the world, and went back to his pages.

* * *

By noon the first forme was locked and they pulled proofs together, her hands on the tympan, his hand over hers on the press bar — a liberty no apprentice would have dared and no clerk should have, and she let it stand because the press was heavy and because she was weak in precisely that one place and knew it. The impression came up sharp and black. Twenty copies of an argument that could get a man transported, wearing the clothes of a dead woman's almanac.

Mateu read the proof standing in the window light, lips moving, and Tamsin watched him instead of the type, which was a compositor's sin and she committed it freely.

"You read like someone who expects the words to change," she said.

"They do change." He did not look up. "Every time the government changes. Every time the price of bread changes. Words are the only honest currency on this island — they at least admit what they're worth."

"That's cynical."

"That's clerical." Now he looked up, and the humor in his face had gone somewhere deeper, the way light goes deep into water. "I copy manifests, señorita. I record what enters this harbor and what leaves it, and in seven years I have learned that everything true arrives disguised as something permitted. Wheat as ballast. Rifles as tools. Arguments as almanacs." He folded the proof along its spine, precisely, a crease like a decision. "Only the disguises are honest. They tell you exactly what the truth is afraid of."

"And what is the truth afraid of now?"

He considered her for a long moment, and she had the strange sensation of being read — of being set in type herself, examined letter by letter for flaws.

"Of lists," he said at last. "Truth is always afraid of lists. A list turns a man into a line, and a line can be struck through." He smiled then, fully, and the depth closed over like water over a dropped coin. "But you didn't ask me that. You asked about the kerning on page four."

* * *

In the afternoon her father returned, smelling of road dust and the cheap wine at the Es Castell tavern, and if he was surprised to find Mateu Ferragut still at the composing stone, eating bread and olives Tamsin had brought up from the kitchen, he showed it only in the smallest settling of his shoulders — the relaxation of a man coming home to find the thing he hoped for already accomplished.

"The corrections?" her father asked.

"Made." Mateu rose and shook his hand. "Your daughter has a fine eye. The spacing on the tables is better than Ciutadella work."

"Ciutadella work," her father snorted. "Ciutadella sets type the way drunkards walk." But he glanced at Tamsin as he said it, and in the glance was a whole unprinted column: *you saw, you understand, you will say nothing.* She had been raised on such glances. She returned it with one of her own: *I saw. I say nothing. I want to know more.*

The three of them stood a moment in the fading light among the cases, and Tamsin felt the shop around her differently than she ever had — felt the presses not as furniture but as instruments, felt the type not as soldiers awaiting orders but as soldiers already marching, quietly, in a direction she was only now being allowed to see.

"Mateu," her father said at the door, "come Thursday. There's a psalm book wants resetting."

"A psalm book," Mateu said solemnly.

"Full of marginal notes," her father said, "for the edification of the faithful."

They exchanged the look of conspirators everywhere, which is indistinguishable from the look of old friends, and Tamsin, drying the composing stone, pretended to notice neither.

* * *

From the window of the customs house, Mateu Ferragut watched the lamplight come on in the print shop above the water stair, one square of gold in a wall of blue dusk, and made himself finish the manifest in front of him before he allowed himself to think.

He was good at this — at the discipline of the deferred. Seven years copying cargo manifests taught a man that everything important travels under bill of lading, including his own heart, which he had entered in no ledger whatsoever and did not intend to.

But he thought it now, briefly, in the privacy of the closing office: *she let my hand stay on hers at the press bar. She could have moved. She knows the weight of a thing left resting.*

He knew more about Tamsin Kavanagh than he had said. He knew that her father had been printing for the cause since before the cause had a name — since the days when it was only grievances and wine, before the grievances organized themselves. He knew the old man's caution, and the daughter's quicker mind, and he knew — this was the knowledge that sat in him like a swallowed coal — that the lists had already begun. Names gathered in Mahón, names copied in Palma, names traveling east in diplomatic pouches toward governments that measured loyalty in tonnage. He had seen the first of them himself, in a minister's anteroom in Barcelona, a clerk's fair copy of a clerk's fair copy, and his own surname had been on it, misspelled, which somehow made it worse.

He had said nothing to her. He had talked of kerns and widows and orphans while the real text waited in his breast pocket: a letter, half-written, that began *When the war comes* — because he no longer believed in *if* — and could not find its way past that phrase. How did one post such a letter? To whom? *Dear señorita, the ships are coming and I am on a list and I find I cannot leave this island while you are on it.*

He blew out the lamp. Below, the print shop light went out too, and the water stair disappeared into the dark, and the harbor breathed its slow tide-breath against the stones.

Thursday, he told himself. There was a psalm book wanting resetting. There were marginal notes for the edification of the faithful. And there was a festival coming at month's end — Sant Joan, fires on the beaches, the whole town drunk on black figs and gin — when a man might find a quiet stair, and a moment, and ask a promise of someone.

He locked the office behind him and went down into the town, whistling very softly, a tune with no words, the way a man walks when he is carrying something breakable and refuses to call it fragile.
