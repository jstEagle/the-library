## 5. Requisition

They came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Wars, Tamsin had always believed, should arrive with thunder — with drums and burning and some grand terrible music. Instead the redcoats came ashore in longboats while the fishwives were still haggling over sardines, and by the time the church bells rang for noon, there were sentries at the Custom House and a corporal nailing a proclamation to the door of Sant Antoni as casually as a man posting a notice about a lost mule.

*By order of His Britannic Majesty's forces, all books of account, registers, rolls, ledgers, and records whatsoever are to be surrendered for inspection.*

Tamsin read it twice, standing in the square with a basket of type-metal she'd been sent to collect from the founder's yard. The words were set in a plain face, no flourishes, the kind of typography chosen by men who wanted nothing admired and everything obeyed. She noticed such things now; she couldn't help it. Her eye had been trained since childhood to see how a page was built, and this one was built like a wall.

"All books of account," she murmured.

"Every debt in Mahon," said old Bartomeu beside her, the rope-seller, squinting at the paper through his one good eye. "They'll know who owes whom. Who paid their taxes. Who didn't. Who signed the petition against the mole tax three years back." He spat, carefully, away from the sentry. "A ledger is just a list of names, girl. And a list of names is just a list of throats."

She laughed, because it was expected, and because the alternative was to feel what was already settling into her chest like cold water seeping into a cellar. Then she carried her basket home through streets that had changed texture overnight. The stones were the same stones. But British boots made a different sound than Spanish ones — heavier, more deliberate, the tread of men who intended to stay — and by evening the whole town seemed to be listening to its own footsteps.

* * *

Her father did not burn anything.

She had expected him to. Every shopkeeper on the street was feeding papers to their hearths that first night; the smell of scorched vellum hung over the quarter like fog, and down by the chandlery someone had thrown a chest of correspondence into the harbor rather than let it be read. But Joan Kavanagh sat at his desk with a candle and a pot of ink and began, methodically, to write.

"What are you doing?" Tamsin asked from the doorway.

"Making an inventory." He did not look up. His hand moved with the same even strokes he used for setting copy — unhurried, exact. "One press, Stanhope pattern, second-hand, bought from Barcelona. Four cases of type, roman, two of italic. A composing stone. Two galley trays. One paper store, quantity approximately nine reams, various weights."

"That's it? That's all you'll give them?"

"That's all that exists," he said mildly, "as far as any inventory can prove."

She understood then, with the slow dread of watching a tide come in, that her father had been preparing for this day for longer than she had been alive. There was no panic in him, no fury. Only the calm of a man who has already decided where he will stand when the water rises. He blotted the page, folded it once, and set it beneath the paperweight — a lump of type-metal cast in the shape of a fist, a joke gift from an apprentice years ago.

"They'll take the ledgers, Papa. All of them. Ours too — the accounts, the customer book—"

"Yes."

"And everyone in them."

He looked up at last. In the candlelight his face was difficult to read, the way a composed page is difficult to read before it's inked — all potential, all shadow. "Names are going to become very valuable in this town, Tamsin," he said. "More valuable than gold, because gold can't tell a soldier who to arrest. Remember that. Whatever happens next, remember what a name is worth."

She remembered. She would spend years remembering.

* * *

They came for the shop on the fourth morning.

Not soldiers — not at first. A clerk first, a thin man with ink-stained fingers and a leather portfolio, accompanied by two privates who stood in the doorway looking bored and slightly ashamed, the way soldiers do when assigned to paperwork instead of glory. The clerk introduced himself as attached to the office of the Provost Marshal, laid a printed form on the counter, and asked to see the proprietor.

"I am the proprietor," said Joan Kavanagh.

The clerk blinked. Behind him, one of the privates glanced at Tamsin — in her smock, her sleeves rolled, metal filings to the elbows — and visibly revised whatever he'd assumed about the pretty daughter of the house. She kept her face blank. Eleven years of being mistaken for an apprentice boy had taught her the value of a blank face.

"The requisition order requires a full cataloguing of the premises," the clerk said. "Presses, type, stock, and all materials pertaining to the printing trade. Also all books of account and correspondence."

"You may catalog," her father said. "I will assist."

It took most of the day. The clerk worked with a dreary thoroughness, measuring the press, counting the type cases, weighing reams of paper on a little brass scale he produced from his portfolio as though producing a rabbit. Tamsin watched him handle a case of long-primer roman and felt something twist in her chest — those letters were hers, cut and sorted and set in order by her own hands over six years, each compartment known to her fingers in the dark. Watching a stranger run his thumb across them was like watching a man put his fingers in her hair.

It was near dusk when the officer arrived.

She heard him on the stair before she saw him — a different tread than the privates', lighter, quicker, the tread of a young man in a hurry to matter. He came into the shop still pulling off his gloves, and she saw at once that he was young indeed: perhaps five-and-twenty, fair-haired, with the kind of precise, narrow face that looks as though it has been set in type itself. His uniform was immaculate in a way that suggested effort rather than vanity. On his collar, the insignia of a captain.

"Captain Voss," he said, to the room in general. "Assistant to the Deputy Quartermaster for Records." He pronounced the title without irony, which somehow made it worse. "You are the printer Kavanagh?"

"I am."

"Then I've read your work, sir, before I ever saw your shop." Voss crossed to the press and ran his palm along the frame — not the clerk's dutiful pawing but something more like a connoisseur's touch. "That broadsheet on the grain prices, last March. Set by your house?"

"It was."

"Beautifully done. The spacing alone—" He stopped himself, as if catching a boy caught admiring a horse at the wrong moment, and turned brisk again. "Your inventory lists four cases of type. I count five."

The silence lasted two seconds. Tamsin counted them the way she counted em-quads.

"Your count is wrong," her father said pleasantly.

Voss looked at him. Then at Tamsin. Then, slowly, around the shop — at the racks of dried sheets, the stone, the ink bench, the little window above the harbor where the light came in gold now, dying over the water. When he spoke again his voice had lost its officiousness entirely.

"Mr. Kavanagh. I am going to say something once, and then we will never speak of it again. This press could be confiscated under the general order. Most will be. The garrison needs printers, however, and the ones they seize tend to be uncooperative, and uncooperative printers produce crooked proclamations and misprinted pay-lists, and misprinted pay-lists cause mutinies." He picked up a printed sheet from the rack — one of Tamsin's, a funeral sermon, and she watched his eyes move across it with involuntary appreciation. "So. Your press will remain. You will print what the army requires, at rates the army sets. In exchange, your name goes on my list as *necessary*, not *suspect*. Do you understand the difference between those two words?"

"No," said Tamsin, before she could stop herself.

Both men looked at her. Voss studied her for a long moment — long enough that she regretted her tongue, long enough that she saw something flicker behind his eyes, curiosity or calculation or both.

"Necessary means I need you alive and working," he said. "Suspect means I need to know everything you've ever done. The lists are kept in the same office." He put his gloves back on, finger by finger. "Pray you stay on the right one, Miss...?"

"Apprentice," said her father smoothly. "My boy."

"Your boy," Voss repeated, in a tone that suggested he was filing the phrase somewhere for later. He bowed — actually bowed, to a printer's apprentice, which no officer in any army had ever done — and went down the stairs, and the privates followed, and the clerk closed his portfolio with a snap like a small execution.

* * *

That night Tamsin could not sleep. She lay in her loft above the shop listening to the harbor sounds change — the new challenge-and-answer of sentries at the mole, boats challenged by voices speaking English, the whole town's rhythm rewritten in a week — and she thought about lists.

She thought about the customer book downstairs, with its columns of names: the priest who bought funeral sermons, the merchant who ordered shipping notices, the widow Ferrer who paid for her almanacs in eggs. Harmless names. Names that had meant only bread and ink. Now every one of them was a fact about a person, filed in a portfolio, available to any officer with a stamp.

And she thought — she could not help thinking, though she tried to push the thought away like a bad proof — of another name. Not in any book of hers. A name written in a hand she knew better than any typeface, slanted and quick, that filled margins with jokes and corrected her father's proofs in pencil so gently the corrections looked like suggestions from a friend.

If they were making lists of everyone, they would have his name. If they had his name—

She got up, lit the stub of a candle, and went down into the shop. The army had left a receipt pinned to the press: *Catalogued and retained, by order, J. Voss, Capt.* She stood looking at it for a long time. Necessary, not suspect. The words sat in her mind like two sorts standing side by side in a case, waiting to be set into a sentence she didn't yet know how to compose.

In the corner, the fifth case of type — the one Voss had counted and her father had denied — sat under its cloth, silent as a held breath.

She lifted the cloth. She did not open the case. She only stood there with her hand flat on the lid, feeling the warmth of the wood, and thought: *somewhere tonight a man is writing his name in a ledger, and tomorrow that ledger becomes a weapon.*

Tomorrow, she decided, she would find out whose hands held it.
