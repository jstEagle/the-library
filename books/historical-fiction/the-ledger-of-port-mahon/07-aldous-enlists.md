## 7. Aldous Enlists

The commission came on a Tuesday, folded inside an envelope that smelled of sealing wax and wet wool. Tamsin found it on the shop counter when she came down before dawn to heat the ink, a rectangle of cream-laid paper lying against the scarred wood as though it had grown there overnight, as though the house itself had produced it in the dark the way oysters produce pearls — around some grit, some irritation, some foreign body.

She did not open it. That was the first strange thing, that she did not open it, because in their family letters were opened the way bread was broken, without ceremony, by whoever's hands were nearest. But the envelope bore the crest of the occupying army — a lion couchant stamped in black — and something about the blackness of it made her leave it where it lay, the way one leaves a spider on the wall and watches instead.

Aldous came down an hour later. She heard him on the stairs, his boots heavier than they had been a year ago, and she heard him stop when he saw it, and she heard the small sound he made — not quite a breath, not quite a word — before he took the envelope into his hands and turned it over twice, as if the answer might be printed on the back.

"From the governor's office," he said. Not to her. To the room.

"I can see that."

"They know our name." He said it flatly, but she heard beneath it the thing he actually meant: *they know our name, and names are currency now, and we are being spent.* Since the requisition, since Captain Voss had walked through the shop with his catalog and his careful, terrible courtesy, their name had existed in a ledger somewhere, sorted and weighed. Tamsin had seen the ledger. She carried its contents in her chest like swallowed stones.

"Open it," their father said from the doorway of the back room. Josiah Kavanagh had ink on his fingers even at that hour, even on a day when there was nothing yet to print, because his hands went to the trade the way a devout man's hands go to prayer — without decision, without thought. He looked at the envelope the way he looked at badly set type: with a craftsman's distaste for what was about to be made of his material.

Aldous opened it. He read it once in silence, and then again aloud, and his voice did something Tamsin had never heard it do, which was to perform.

"'His Excellency the Governor, acting under authority of His Majesty's forces in the Mediterranean, is pleased to offer Mr. Aldous Kavanagh a provisional commission in the civil administration of Port Mahon, with duties pertaining to the registry of records, at a salary of—'" He stopped reading the numbers. The numbers were good. They all heard how good they were in the space where he stopped.

*Registry of records.* Tamsin turned the phrase over. She thought of the master ledger in the governor's strongroom, the columns of names, the small cold annotations — *loyal, suspect, enemy at large* — and understood that her brother had been invited to become a hand that wrote in that book.

"Well," said their father.

"It's a good position." Aldous set the letter on the counter, smoothing it flat with both palms, as though it were a proof he was checking for flaws. "It's a *very* good position, Papa. There are men in this town who would cut off a finger for it."

"There are men in this town who have cut off fingers for less," Josiah said, "and called it the price of staying honest."

And there it was, the fault line, running through the middle of the family like a seam of bad ore through good stone. Tamsin had felt it for weeks without being able to name it — in the way her father watched the harbor from the upper window, in the way Aldous no longer complained about the British billeted along the Mola, in the way neither of them spoke of Mateu Ferragut, whose name she kept folded inside her like a letter she could not risk opening even in her own thoughts.

"I'm going to take it," Aldous said.

* * *

The quarrel, when it came, was three days in the making, and it happened in the composing room among the cases of type, because Aldous would not have it in the living quarters and their father would not have it in the street, and so the shop — the neutral ground, the place where all three of them had always been able to meet as craftspeople if not as family — became the battlefield instead.

"You would take their wages," Josiah said, "and their uniform, and their oath."

"It's not a uniform, it's a coat. It's not an oath, it's a signature. Papa, listen to me—" Aldous's hands were moving the way he moved them when he argued, wide and pleading, the hands of a man trying to hold something visible between himself and the person he was losing. "The Vasseurs lost their warehouse. Old Bartomeu lost his fishing boats, all of them, and he's seventy. The Solers are on the suspect list and their son can't get bread at the market without the baker looking at him like he's a rat. And we—" he gestured at the shop, at the presses, at the racks of type that had been cataloged and stamped and left, *suspiciously* left, in their possession — "we still have all of it. Do you know why?"

"Because we've done nothing wrong."

"Because nobody's *looked* yet!" The words cracked across the composing room. Somewhere below, the harbor water slapped the pilings, patient as a clock. "And one day someone will look, Papa, and on that day I would rather there be a Kavanagh inside the office where the looking happens than three Kavanaghs outside it waiting to be found."

Tamsin stood at the stone with a composing stick in her hand and a half-set line of type before her, and she did not move, because moving would mean choosing a side of the room, and the room was split down its center like a board about to be sawn.

"And what will you write in their registry?" their father asked, and his voice had gone very quiet, which was worse than shouting, because Josiah Kavanagh shouted about money and weather and the quality of Spanish paper, but he went quiet about things that mattered. "When they hand you the book and the pen and ask you to sort your neighbors into columns — loyal, suspect, enemy — what will you write, Aldous? Will you write *Mateu Ferragut, enemy at large*? Will you write the Soler boy down as a rat because a baker looked at him?"

The silence that followed had weight and texture. Tamsin felt it press against her eardrums.

"I would write what was true," Aldous said at last, but his voice had lost its performance, and what was underneath sounded young.

"Truth," their father said, "is a typeface. Everyone thinks they're setting it. Everyone's hand shakes a little."

He turned back to his case and began, deliberately, insultingly, to set type — the small click and slide of metal against metal filling the room, each letter a period at the end of the argument. And Aldous stood there another moment, his ears red, his jaw working, and then he took the commission letter from inside his coat, where he had been carrying it for three days like a wound dressing, and he set it on the stone beside Tamsin's half-finished line, and he left.

* * *

He took the position on the Friday. He wore the coat on the Saturday.

It was not a uniform, he had said, but it might as well have been: a dark blue civilian coat with the crown's button, brass and bright, the kind of button that caught light across a whole square and told everyone whose side of the ledger you stood on. Tamsin watched him from the shop window as he crossed the Placa des Born in it, walking a little too fast, the way a man walks when he is trying to arrive before his own reflection can catch him.

She had tried, the night before, to stand between them as the plan required — not that anyone had made a plan, but she had made one anyway, in the dark, in her narrow bed above the harbor, listening to the two men breathing in their separate rooms like two tides that would not meet. Her strategy had been simple and childish: she would love them both, loudly, in the same room, until the room could not hold a quarrel.

It failed by breakfast.

"He's protecting us," she said, spreading butter on bread that was going stale, because the requisition had taken the grain barges and the bakers had begun to ration with a shrug that said *don't blame me, blame the flag on the fort.* "However it looks, Papa. He looked at the ledger and he did the arithmetic and he decided we were worth a crown button."

"Is that what love is in this family now? Arithmetic?"

"Love is whatever keeps people alive long enough to keep loving."

Her father put down his bread. He looked at her across the table, and for a moment she saw him truly — not the quiet radical of her childhood, the man who burned nothing and hid everything, but an old man in a war he had seen coming for years and had prepared for in the only way he knew, which was to keep his hands moving and his mouth shut and his daughter believing the wars belonged to other people.

"And what does it cost him," he asked, "this arithmetic? Do you know? Does he?"

She didn't answer, because she didn't know, and because some part of her — the part that had seen the master ledger, that had read *enemy at large* beside a name she loved — suspected the cost was already higher than any of them could count, and was being paid in a currency none of them had agreed to spend.

* * *

The registry office occupied the old customs house on the quay, and on Aldous's first day of work Tamsin walked past it twice without meaning to, the way the tongue returns to a broken tooth.

Through the tall windows she could see the desks, the clerks, the stacks of seized ledgers from every counting house and parish and confraternity in the town — the whole written memory of Port Mahon, confiscated and stacked in daylight like firewood. And she could see her brother among them, at a desk near the window, a crown button at his throat, a pen in his hand.

He looked up. He saw her. For a moment neither of them moved, and the distance between the window and the street was the distance between two countries.

Then he raised his hand — not a wave, exactly. More like the gesture a compositor makes when he wants a line held, *wait, not yet, don't distribute that type.* And she understood him, because they had grown up in the same trade, and she lifted her hand the same way back, and they stood there in the grammar of their childhood, saying *wait* to each other across the width of an occupation.

She turned for home along the water stair, and it was there, on the third stair from the bottom, worn pale by two hundred years of feet, that she saw it: a chalk mark on the riser, small and deliberate, the kind of mark only a printer would read — a quoin, the little wedge that locks a page of type into its frame.

*Locked. Held. Not yet distributed.*

Mateu was alive. Mateu had walked these stairs. And somewhere in the town above her, in the town her brother was now helping to catalog, the man whose name sat in the enemy column was leaving her messages in a language only their trade could speak.

She climbed the stairs with her heart going like a press at full run, and she did not look back at the customs house, and she did not let herself think about what her brother's pen was writing, line by line, into the book that would decide them all.
