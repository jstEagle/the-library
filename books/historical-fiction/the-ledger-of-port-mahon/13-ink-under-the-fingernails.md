## 13. Ink Under the Fingernails

My father woke me by the smell of ink, not sound.

That was the way of him. He had spent forty years in a trade where the loudest thing was the press itself — a groaning iron animal that could wake the whole street if you let it run wild — and so he had learned to do everything else in silence. He moved through the dark shop like a man walking through his own memory: no lamp lit until the shutters were closed, no word spoken until he was certain of the walls.

I came down the stairs with my boots in my hand and found him already at the stone sink, washing the composing stick I had left drying there, as though tidiness were the first act of conspiracy.

"You didn't sleep," I said.

"Neither did you." He set the stick down. "Sit. We have perhaps four hours of true dark, and the moon sets before three."

Outside, Port Mahon lay under occupation the way a table lies under a dropped cloth — everything still beneath it, nothing changed on top, and yet nothing the same. There were sentries at the Mole now, and a curfew bell that rang at nine from the old church whose bells had once rung only for fiestas and funerals. The harbor water caught the lanterns of the frigates and broke them into long red shivers. Somewhere across the town a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

We worked by one candle shielded behind the galley of standing type, the flame turned away from the window, its light cupped in brass like something we were smuggling even from ourselves.

"The decoy," my father said, and laid a proof sheet flat between us. "Tell me what's wrong with it."

It was the pamphlet Voss's clerks had been hunting for six weeks — or rather, it was the pamphlet they *believed* existed, because Captain Voss had let slip, in that courteous way of his, that the garrison had intercepted a description of it from an informant in Ciutadella. *A seditious broadside,* the report said. *Set in a small pica, ragged right margin, bearing the imprint of a Barcelona printer.* The real network printed nothing in small pica. The real network did not exist on paper at all anymore; it existed in whispers, in chalk marks on doorposts, in the memory of men who could recite a manifesto and leave no letterform behind.

But the British believed paper. The British believed type. They were a nation of ledgers, and so we would give them a ledger to read.

"It's too clean," I said, turning the proof toward the candlelight. "The spacing is even. A man printing in fear, in a cellar, working fast — he doesn't correct his spacing. He doesn't kern."

My father smiled the way he did when I solved a problem he'd been carrying all evening. "Go on."

"And the e." I held the sheet closer. "You've used our good e — the one from the Fannin consignment, the one with the unbroken counter. If this is supposed to come from Barcelona, it should be worn. It should be a battered face, full of nicks. Every press in Catalonia has been running day and night since the war started. Their type is dying."

He was quiet a moment. "Your mother would have said you think like a forger."

"My mother never met me."

"She met you for eleven days." He said it without sadness; it was simply a fact in the family ledger, entered long ago. "She'd have known anyway. It's in the hands. Now — show me. Build me a page that looks like desperation."

* * *

There is a pleasure in craft that I had never confessed to anyone, because it seemed indecent next to the suffering the craft was now serving. But I will confess it here, to this book, because this book is where truths go when they have nowhere else.

I loved setting type.

I loved it the way Mateu loved the margins of a manuscript — the way a thing unfinished invites you in. To stand before the case with the candle low and the house asleep, and to lift each letter from its compartment — the little brass-and-antimony bodies, warm from your fingers, cool again in an instant — is to hold language itself in pieces and decide how it will stand up in the world. Most people meet words already assembled, already authoritative, already printed. They never know that every sentence they've ever trusted was built by someone's hands, letter by letter, upside down and backward, out of order, out of sight.

Tonight those hands were mine, and the words were lies, and the lies were mercy.

I chose the battered e. I chose a pica that had been dropped on the stone floor in '94 and carried a hairline crack through its stem — a flaw my father had kept out of sentiment and which now became, overnight, a forgery's greatest asset. I spaced the lines unevenly on purpose, letting two words collide where a frightened compositor's thumb would have slipped, leaving a gap where he'd have reached for a space and found the box empty. I set the final line crooked — not visibly crooked, but crooked enough that a careful eye would feel it, the way you feel a picture hanging a half-degree wrong.

"Aldous used to say the eye can't be taught," my father murmured, watching me work. "Only the hand. And the hand teaches the eye eventually."

"Aldous says a great many things."

The name sat between us like a coin neither of us wanted to pick up. My brother wore the King's coat now. He took his supper at the officers' mess and came home smelling of their tobacco, and he had stopped asking Father about the cellar, which was worse than asking.

"He believes he's saving us," my father said at last.

"I know what he believes."

"Do you?" He looked at me over the candle, and in that light his face was all shadows and planes, a woodcut of himself. "Because belief is a kind of innocence, Tamsin, and I find I cannot hate him for his. Only fear him for it."

I said nothing. I picked up the next line of type and set it, and the small click of metal finding metal was the only answer either of us needed.

* * *

By two o'clock the forme was locked in the chase, wedges tightened, quoins snug, and my father ran his palm across the face of the type the way a physician palpates a patient — reading health and sickness through the skin of it.

"Now the ink," he said. "This matters more than anything else you've done tonight."

I had mixed it myself an hour earlier, and badly on purpose. Good press ink is milled for hours, ground fine, married to its oil until it flows like thought. Prisoner's ink — cellar ink, panic ink — is mixed in a bucket with whatever lampblack and linseed the night provides. Mine had grit in it. I had left it uncovered so the air would skin it. When I pulled the first proof, the impression came up patchy, starving in places, flooded in others, one letter doubled where the sheet had shifted on the tympan.

Father studied it for a long time. Then he laughed — a low, startled sound, as if the laugh had escaped custody.

"It's beautiful," he said. "It's perfectly ugly."

"There's more. Look at the fold." I creased the sheet badly, against the grain, so the paper cracked along a wrong axis. "A Barcelona shop folds against the grain because they're rushing and their paper's cheap. Ours folds with the grain because Father is proud."

"I am proud," he said. "It's the cross I bear." He took the sheet, folded it wrongly, and handed it back. "Again. And again after that. If the garrison unfolds this fifty times, the crease must look fifty days old."

So I folded and unfolded, folded and unfolded, until the paper went soft and gray along the crease like cloth, until the fibers broke and fuzzed, until the pamphlet looked as though it had traveled from Catalonia in a smuggler's boot. My fingertips went black. The ink worked down under the nails, into the whorls, into the fine seams of the knuckles, and I understood suddenly why the priests of some countries wash before prayer: because the hands remember everything they touch, and carry it whether you will or no.

* * *

We printed two hundred.

The press ran quiet — Father had greased the ways with tallow instead of oil, and he worked the bar with his whole body rather than letting the spring do it, muffling the great wooden groan to something no louder than a bed creaking. Between pulls we stood in the dark listening. Once, boots passed in the street below, unhurried, two men, a lantern swinging its light along the shutter slats and across the ceiling in slow gold bars. We stood motionless among the cases, my father's hand on my shoulder, pressing me gently down behind the imposing stone, and I watched that light travel the wall over the racks of type — over the letters that had spelled out baptisms and bills of lading and wedding invitations for thirty years — and I felt the strangest calm.

They were looking for a printer. They were looking in a print shop. And yet the light passed over us and moved on, because we were not hiding. We were *working*, and work, done well, done quietly, wears the mask of ordinary life better than any disguise.

When the boots faded, Father exhaled and pulled the next sheet.

Two hundred copies, stacked and folded and bundled in sacking. And then the question I had been waiting for all night, the question that made my stomach tighten even as my hands stayed steady:

"Where do they go?"

"Not here," Father said. "Not within a mile of here. The garrison must find them the way they expect to find things — by accident, by informer, by the sea. If they trace these pages to Mahon town, they'll search every press in it, including ours, and ours has a clean forme and clean type and a daughter with ink under her fingernails." He nodded at my hands. "Wash. Scrub. Use the pumice."

"And the pamphlets?"

"Joan Castell takes them out with the fishing boats tomorrow, before dawn. Half to the north shore, half to Es Grau. They'll surface in taverns, in chapel porches, tucked into bales of hay. Within a week Voss will have twelve copies on his desk and a theory in his head." Father's mouth bent, not quite a smile. "And the theory will be wrong in exactly the way we've built it to be wrong."

* * *

I scrubbed my hands at the stone sink while he damped down the fire and buried the ash. The pumice took the surface ink but not all of it; there was always a shadow left in the cuticle, a gray ghost in the quick of the nail. Printers' hands, my father called them. He had worn that shadow his whole life and had never once tried to hide it — until this year, when he began wearing gloves to market, and I understood that the gloves were not vanity but armor.

"Tamsin." His voice came low across the dark shop. "Come here. I want to show you something before we close the case."

He lifted the forme from the stone and tilted it toward the last of the candle. "Read it."

I read it. Our forged pamphlet — the seditious broadside that would occupy the intelligence of an entire occupying army — was a denunciation of the French, a call for loyalty to Spain, stirring and hollow, full of phrases any patriot might shout and no patriot need act upon. It committed no one to anything. It named no names. It was, in its way, a masterpiece of saying nothing at great volume.

"You see it," Father said.

"It's empty."

"It's *safe*. Every man who reads it will nod and pass it on, and no man who reads it can be hanged for it. That is the whole art, Tamsin — not the type, not the ink, not the fold. The art is writing fire that burns nothing but suspicion." He set the forme down and looked at me, and for a moment he was not my father the tradesman but my father the radical, the man who had spent years printing truth for Mateu Ferragut's network while I cut type upstairs believing history happened elsewhere. "Voss thinks he is fighting ideas. Let him chase this one. Every clerk he puts on deciphering this trash is a clerk not reading the real network's silence. Emptiness, properly set, is the fullest thing we own."

I looked down at my hands, ghost-gray, ink-shadowed, steady.

For nineteen years I had been the invisible one — the girl who cut type while the apprentices assumed she was somebody's younger brother, the Kavanagh nobody counted, the second child, the quiet one. I had thought invisibility was a wound. Standing there in the dead hour with two hundred lies folded soft as linen, I understood it was something else entirely. Invisibility was access. No one watched the person no one watched. The occupiers catalogued the merchants, the clergy, the officers, the men. They did not catalogue me. I could walk through their occupied town carrying anything — a message, a chalk mark, a bundle of sedition — because to them I was furniture.

Furniture with ink under its fingernails.

"Papa," I said. "Next time, let me set the whole page. Not just the body. The headline too."

He raised an eyebrow. "Your headline work is good, but—"

"For a lie, it has to be perfect. For a lie, it has to be better than my best." I heard my own voice and did not recognize its steadiness. "If we're going to do this, we do it as if the gallows were proofreading."

Something moved across his face — pride and grief in the same expression, the way they so often travel together in fathers.

"As if the gallows were proofreading," he repeated softly. "Yes. All right. Next time."

* * *

He banked the candle. I gathered the bundled pamphlets into the fish crate Joan Castell would collect, and my fingers, moving over the sacking, paused on one sheet whose corner had come loose — and I did the thing I had been resisting all night.

I read it once more. Whole. As a reader.

*To the faithful of the island: stand fast; the hour of Spain returns; the invader's ink is not yet dry upon his false promises—*

False promises. My own words, set in my own cracked pica, and even knowing them hollow, even having built their hollowness plank by plank, I felt the heat of them. Words are like that. You build a fire to warm a room and the fire does not care about your intentions; it burns according to its nature. Somewhere in a week's time a fisherman would read this in a tavern and believe it, and his believing would be real even if the pamphlet was not, and his courage would rise on the strength of a thing I had manufactured the way one manufactures shoes.

Was that different from what the governors did? From what Voss did, filing men into ledgers as *loyal*, *suspect*, *enemy* — making people into entries and then treating the entries as the people?

I did not have an answer. I folded the corner down and told myself I would have one later. Later was where printers kept everything.

* * *

Joan Castell came at half past four, before the first gray came into the sky above the Mole. He was an old man with hands like rope and a face that had been weathered past expression, and he asked no questions, which was his entire character. He loaded the crate into the false bottom under his nets, spat over the side of the stair for luck, and was gone into the blue-dark toward the harbor, and the pamphlets went with him, and the lie went out onto the water wearing our ink.

Father went up to bed. I stayed.

I stood a moment in the empty shop with the forme still lying on the stone, and I did what I always did at the end of a job: I distributed the type. Letter by letter, back into the case, each piece to its compartment — the cracked pica home to its box, the battered e back among its wounded brothers. It is the printer's discipline: leave nothing standing, leave nothing to be read. A page that stays set is a confession waiting for eyes.

I was halfway through when I heard the knock.

Three knocks, soft, spaced — and then a fourth, delayed, arrhythmic. Not the pattern of the network. Not the pattern of anyone I knew. My hand closed around the composing stick — a poor weapon, a ridiculous weapon, and the only one in reach — and I crossed the dark shop and put my eye to the shutter slat.

Below, in the street just graying toward dawn, stood a boy of perhaps fourteen, one of the chandlery errand-runners, breath steaming, glancing up and down the empty lane. And pinned to the doorframe beside the knocker, at the height of a passing shoulder, was a scrap of paper.

I opened the door on the chain. The boy thrust a folded note through the gap without meeting my eyes, said nothing — had clearly been paid to say nothing — and walked away fast, his footsteps slapping the cobbles like something fleeing.

I broke the seal with my thumbnail.

The handwriting was hurried, the ink blotted, the spelling of a man writing in a third language by candlelight. But I knew the hand. I had spent months correcting it in proof margins, teasing it toward elegance, and I would have known it blind, by the pressure alone.

*T. — They have someone inside. The two arrested at Es Castell were given a handbill that only the network could have printed. Someone made a copy of our marks and fed it to the Spanish side. Whoever it is knows the presses. Whoever it is knows us. Burn this. Trust no paper. — M.*

I stood in the doorway with the dawn coming up cold off the harbor, and the note trembled in my hand, and the first thing my mind did — the terrible, automatic, craftsman's thing — was to begin analyzing the *paper*.

It was a stock I knew. It was our stock. The same wove sheets Father bought from the factor at Es Grau, the same weight, the same faint ribbing at the edge.

Which meant Mateu had written it on paper from our own shop.

Which meant he had been here. Recently. Close enough to steal a sheet from the rack by the door — or been given one.

Or was given one by someone who wanted me to believe exactly this.

I looked down at my hands, gray to the quick, ink in every seam, and for the first time since I had learned the trade I looked at them as a stranger might: as the hands of someone who could make any document, forge any name, counterfeit any truth. Hands that could save a town.

Or betray it.

Behind me, upstairs, my father's floorboard creaked once, and went silent — and I could not tell whether he had heard the knock, or was awake watching, or whether the house itself had simply settled around the secret we had printed into it.
