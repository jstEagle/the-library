## 4. The Hand That Keeps Writing

The room above the print shop smelled of cold glue and lamp oil and, beneath it all, of paper — the particular sweetness of wood pulp waiting to become someone's opinion. Ilse had been in a hundred rooms like it across four years and three borders, and she knew the taxonomy by heart: the partisan shops with their barricaded windows, the collaborationist shops with their brass fittings and their fear polished thin as varnish, the dead shops where the presses stood under sheets like furniture in a house of mourning. This one was none of those. This one was alive in the way a hare is alive — motionless, ears back, every nerve listening.

She sat on a stool the daughter had given her, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of another woman's soap, her wet boots steaming faintly by the stove. The letter lay on the table between them, face up, because hiding it now would be worse than useless. They had already seen it. The father had seen it too, before he'd gone out into the dark on some errand he hadn't named, and his face when he read the handwriting was a thing Ilse intended to think about later, carefully, the way one thinks about a wound only after the bleeding stops.

For now there was only the daughter. Wendell. She stood at the far end of the table with her sleeves rolled and her hands flat on the wood, and she looked at Ilse the way Ilse imagined a customs officer looks at a passport: not hostile, exactly, but weighing.

"You said you'd tell me who you are," Wendell said. "Before my father comes back."

"I said I'd tell *you*," Ilse answered. "Whether I tell him is a separate negotiation."

"He's my father."

"He's also a man who faked a drowning," Ilse said, "and has kept it from you for two years, which means he is capable of keeping other things, larger things, from you for longer." She saw the words land, saw the flinch the girl suppressed — badly; she was too honest a face for this work — and softened her voice by exactly one degree. "I don't say it to wound you. I say it because we are about to decide whether to trust each other, and trust that begins as politeness gets people shot."

* * *

Her name was Ilse Brandtner, though the name had been worn smooth by use, like a coin passed through too many hands, and half the men who had known her by it were dead or had sold it. She had been born in a river town whose name changed twice during her childhood — once by treaty, once by army — and she had learned early that a place could be occupied without a single soldier entering it, occupied by language, by paperwork, by the slow replacement of one set of names with another. Her father had been a schoolteacher who taught maps. He had been made to teach the new maps each time, and each time he had done it, and each time something in him had gone quieter, until the last year he barely spoke above a whisper even at home, as if the walls had been deputized.

She had come to Anton Vasilescu at seventeen, carrying nothing but a letter of introduction from a cousin and a hand that could copy any script set in front of it. It was the hand that got her in. Anton had tested her the first day by putting three documents before her — a commercial invoice, a love letter, an official summons — and asking her which was forged. She had studied them for a long time. Then she had pushed the invoice aside and said: the invoice. The love letter was genuinely written by a man in love, clumsy and excessive. The summons was genuinely bureaucratic, right down to its small corruptions. But the invoice was too correct; no merchant wrote numbers that beautifully unless he was showing off, and merchants showing off were usually merchants lying.

Anton had laughed — a short, surprised bark, as if the laugh had escaped custody — and hired her on the spot.

"I was his apprentice," she told Wendell now. "Officially, a compositor. Unofficially, a courier. There were eleven of us who moved his letters, and I was the youngest and therefore the most useful, because nobody searches a girl carrying bread." She turned her cup slowly on the table, watching the tea leaves settle. "I carried his correspondence for three years. Upstream, downstream, across the ice when the ferries stopped. I learned the checkpoints the way a priest learns psalms. And I learned his handwriting better than my own, which is why, when your father opened that letter tonight and went grey, I understood him completely. That hand does something to people. It's the hand of a man who was supposed to have stopped writing."

Wendell had not sat down. She remained standing, and Ilse noted it — the girl guarded the room's geometry, kept herself between Ilse and the stairs, between Ilse and the shop floor below. Good instincts. Wrong war.

"Tell me about the drowning," Wendell said.

"Which version?"

"There's more than one?"

"There are always more than one." Ilse counted them off against her fingers, a habit from her father's classroom. "There's the official version: Anton Vasilescu, printer of seditious material, attempted to flee arrest by night boat, capsized in the current near the mill race, body never recovered. There's the version the gendarmes tell over drinks: that they chased him onto the water themselves and watched him go down, and some of them still can't sleep. There's the version his enemies believe: that he's alive and laughing at them, which keeps them paranoid and therefore careful. And then there's the true version, which I will give you in pieces, because pieces are all anyone should carry at once." She paused. "Your father built the false one. The official one. He wrote the coroner's report — or rather, he printed it, and taught the coroner what to write, and supplied the witness statements, and set the type for the newspaper notices, so that the story of Anton's death went out into the world wearing the shop's own clothes. Nobody questions a death notice. A death notice is the one document everyone believes."

"And you? Where were you when he drowned?"

Ilse looked at her for a moment. Outside, the wind worried at the loading door, and somewhere below a shutter knocked in its frame, patient as a pulse.

"I was the one who reported seeing the body," she said. "A woman's testimony, weeping, at the water stair. Very convincing. I had practiced the weeping for a week." She let that sit. "So you understand the shape of me, Wendell Oyelaran. I am not a girl who stumbled here. I am one of the architects of the lie you grew up inside. If that makes me untrustworthy, good. You should find me untrustworthy. Everyone worth trusting in this business is."

* * *

The letter itself she explained last, because the letter was the point, and points should arrive after the ground has been prepared to receive them.

It was written in Anton's hand — not his public hand, the bold slanted script of his manifestos, which half the literate population of the river towns could identify on sight, but his working hand, the small compressed cursive he used for accounts and private notes, the hand that leaned hard to the right as if hurrying. Only six people alive could distinguish the two. Ilse was one. She suspected the old man upstream was another.

"It is not a letter," she said. "It is a summons. Read it again — not the words, the structure. 'Regarding the estate of A.V., deceased, the undersigned requests the presence of the holder of certain effects, at the mill at Sfântu Gheorghe, before the feast of St. Nicholas.' Estate language. Probate language. It reads like a lawyer's circular. But the feast of St. Nicholas is nine days away, and the mill at Sfântu Gheorghe burned in the spring of the second year of the war, and dead men do not hold estates. Every sentence in it is a lock, and the key is knowing that the man who wrote it is alive."

"And the effects?" Wendell asked. "What effects?"

"The ledger." Ilse said it plainly, watching the girl's face. She had decided, somewhere in the last hour, on the edge of the stove's warmth, that this daughter would learn everything eventually anyway — that households like this one leaked truth the way old boats leaked water, steadily and from below — and that it was better she hear it from Ilse than from a gendarme. "You've heard the word. Don't pretend you haven't; your whole street whispers it. A list of names. Men who gave the orders for the reprisals along the marshes, and the men who signed the requisitions, and the officers who countersigned, and the civilians who informed — names with dates, names with places, names with the ink still on them. Anton spent two years collecting it while the world believed him at the bottom of the river. Now the armies are converging and the armistice is coming, and when it comes, everything will be frozen — the borders, the blame, the story. Whoever writes the history in the first month after the signing will have written it forever. Anton intends to be the one who writes it. Or at least the one who makes it impossible to write it wrong."

Wendell was quiet for a long time. The stove ticked. Downstairs, faintly, the great press seemed to breathe in its sleep, iron cooling, and Ilse thought: *she listens to machines the way I listen to rooms.* It was a thought that gave her a strange, unwelcome comfort, and she distrusted comfort on principle.

"My father," Wendell said at last, very evenly, "helped kill him. On paper."

"Your father helped keep him alive. On paper. It amounts to the same act viewed from opposite banks." Ilse rose, stiff, her wet clothes finally surrendering their chill, and crossed to the window, easing the curtain back a finger's width. The street below lay blue with snow-light. A patrol passed — two soldiers, collars up, rifles slung, the bored walk of men counting the hours until relief. She counted them out of sight before she spoke again. "Three factions in this city, your father's shop prints for all of them, and none of them trusts him, and he survives because each believes the others need him more. I know the arithmetic. I ran the same arithmetic for Anton. But arithmetic fails, Wendell. It always fails at the same moment — the moment someone decides that winning matters more than balancing. That moment is coming. It's coming with the armistice. Which is why the summons says *before* the feast of St. Nicholas, and why I am standing in your kitchen instead of sleeping in a barn."

* * *

They circled each other for another hour, and Ilse let her, because the circling was the negotiation.

She laid out what she needed: passage upstream, discreetly, within days; access to the shop's back channel of barge traffic, which she knew existed because Anton had used it twice before the drowning and had praised it once, drunk on plum brandy, calling it "the Oyelaran miracle, freight that carries nothing and arrives everywhere." In exchange she offered what she had: herself, her knowledge of the checkpoints, her fluency in the three occupation passes and their tells, and — she held this back until the end, watching the girl's eyes — the fact that whoever met Anton at the mill would need someone who could authenticate him. Because there would be others summoned, others arriving with their own letters in that unmistakable hand, and at least one of those letters, Ilse was nearly certain, would be false. Forged. Bait. The gendarmes had been running a trap like that since autumn; she'd seen its edges through a courier who never came back.

"You want me to help you travel to meet a dead man," Wendell said, "in a burned mill, based on a letter I can't verify, delivered by a stranger who admits she's a professional liar."

"Yes," Ilse said. "Put that way, it sounds almost reckless. Let me put it the other way. In nine days, the armies finish their arithmetic, and every name in that ledger becomes either evidence or embarrassment, depending entirely on who holds the printing presses when the ink dries. Your family's shop will be offered a commission — I promise you, it will be offered; shops like yours always are — and your father will have to choose whether to print the victors' history. You can spend the rest of your life setting type for other people's versions. Or you can spend nine days finding out whether the true one exists." She paused, and then, because honesty between professionals was the only currency that held value, added: "And understand this too. I don't trust you either. Not yet. Your father buried my master for two years and told his own daughter nothing, and a man who can lie that thoroughly to the people at his own table can lie to anyone. I am here because I have no better door, not because I believe in your hospitality. We are allies the way two swimmers in cold water are allies. We hold on to each other because letting go means sinking alone."

Something moved in Wendell's face at that — not warmth, not yet, but recognition, the grim pleasure of meeting a person who refuses to pretend. Ilse had seen it before, in trenches and kitchens and border offices: the relief of the undeceived.

"Then we understand each other," Wendell said.

"We understand each other perfectly, which is different, and safer."

Below them, the loading door scraped — the specific scrape of its bolt, the sound Ilse had memorized in her first hour in the house — and footsteps came up the stairs, heavy, slower than they had gone down. The father returned smelling of river fog and candle wax, and he stood in the doorway looking at the two of them across the lamplit table, at the letter lying open between them like a third person, and Ilse watched him assemble his face piece by piece, the way a compositor assembles a line of type, and thought: *he is deciding what to tell us, and how much of it will be true.*

He closed the door behind him and turned the key.

"The barge leaves tomorrow night," he said. "If you're going upstream, you'll need to know what cargo you're pretending to be."
