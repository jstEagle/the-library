## 17. The Forger's Signature

Madame Kertész had a way of holding paper the way other people held birds — firmly enough that it could not escape, gently enough that nothing in it would be bruised. She held the commission's draft history up to the window now, the winter light coming through it like water through cloth, and Wendell watched her face for the verdict she already knew was coming.

"Look at the chain lines," Kertész said. "You see them? The vertical shadows in the sheet. Every mill lays its moulds in its own rhythm — the wires are spaced by hand, by habit, by the length of a workman's forearm and the patience of his master. This paper claims to have been made at the state mill at Turnu two years ago." She turned the page so the light caught it just so. "But Turnu changed its wire spacing in the spring of that year. Wider. I know because I catalogued their output for eleven years before they decided I was an embarrassment. This sheet is old stock. Or new stock made to look old. Either way, it did not exist when it claims to have existed."

Wendell wrote it down. She had learned, in these weeks of lessons in the archivist's cold rooms above the disused customs house, that the hand that writes is slower than the eye that sees, and that this slowness was not a weakness but a discipline. Her father had taught her silences; Kertész was teaching her proofs.

"And the ink," Kertész went on, setting the page down on the scarred table between them. "Iron gall darkens with age. It goes from blue-black toward brown, toward rust, toward the color of dried blood, if you want to be poetic about it, which I do not. But look here—" her cracked thumbnail hovered over a date at the head of the page, *the fourteenth of October* — "this ink is uniformly brown. Aged. And here, in the same sentence, the same line, written presumably in the same sitting—" the thumbnail moved half an inch — "this stroke is still blue at its heart. Two inks pretending to be one hand on one day. Someone wrote this document recently and then bathed parts of it to suggest age, and they were careless, or hurried, or both."

"Or they never expected anyone to look," Wendell said.

"No one ever expects anyone to look." Kertész sat down heavily, her joints announcing themselves one after another like a printer counting type cases. "That is the whole art of forgery, child. It is not making a lie that cannot be caught. There is no such thing, not forever. It is making a lie that no one will *want* to catch. The commission's history will be bound in fine calfskin and placed on shelves behind glass, and every man who opens it will already know what it says, because he helped decide what it should say. Who among them will hold it to the window?"

"I will," Wendell said.

Kertész looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the river ice groaned — a sound Wendell had lived beside all her life and still could not hear without feeling it in her teeth, as though something enormous were turning over in its sleep beneath the city.

"Yes," Kertész said at last. "I believe you will. So let us make certain that when you do, no one can call it a daughter's loyalty or a conspirator's spite. Let us make it arithmetic."

* * *

The arithmetic took three days.

Wendell worked at the customs house by day and at her father's shop by night, and the two kinds of work began to bleed into each other until she could no longer say where the scholarship ended and the conspiracy began — if indeed there was any difference left. In the mornings she learned authentication: how to date a watermark against a mill's known production, how to read the direction of laid lines under raking light, how a forger's pen betrays him in the joins between letters, where the living hand hesitates and the copied hand glides too smoothly across the very places where a real writer would have paused to think.

In the evenings she set type.

Her father had been taken twelve days ago, and the shop ran strangely without him. The workers came because Wendell told them to come, and because the commission's contract required the Oyelaran press to deliver five hundred copies of the official history by the armistice signing, and because a man with children does not ask questions about contracts in wartime. They set the commission's text by day under the eye of a liaison officer named Brandt, a gray, punctual man who smelled of pencil shavings and watched them with the mild vigilance of a shepherd who has been assured the wolves are all accounted for.

And by night, when Brandt went home to whatever gray household contained him, Wendell unlocked the back room where Ilse slept on a cot among the reams of waste paper, and the two of them worked by a single lamp turned down to a bead of flame.

"The beauty of it," Wendell said one night, standing over the forme they had built in secret — a locked chase of type hidden inside the frame of a legitimate job, the way a forged letter hides inside a genuine envelope — "is that we are not counterfeiting anything. That is what Kertész kept saying. We are doing the opposite of counterfeiting. They counterfeit the past; we are only printing the evidence that it is counterfeit."

Ilse looked up from the galley she was composing. In the lamplight her scars stood out pale along her jaw, the map of some old violence that she had never explained and Wendell had stopped waiting for her to explain. "Say that again more slowly," Ilse said. "Because it sounds like the kind of sentence that gets people hanged, and I want to make sure I've memorized it properly."

Wendell almost smiled. "They print a lie. We print the proof of the lie, and we bind it into their own book, in their own edition, under their own imprint. Their calfskin. Their title page. Their seal. Five hundred copies delivered into their own hands, distributed to their own delegates, shelved in their own archive. And somewhere in the middle of each copy — sewn in, not tipped in, so it can't be removed without tearing the binding — the true ledger pages, and the analysis, and the comparison plates."

"They'll burn them."

"Some of them. Immediately. But burning takes time, and books travel faster than bonfires, and by the time the first copies burn, the second copies will be in Brăila and Galați and Vienna, because Anton's network didn't die when he did — didn't die when he *died the first time*, I mean — and those people have been waiting two years for exactly this." Wendell pressed her palms flat against the cold iron of the press bed. "Kertész says a document doesn't need to survive forever. It only needs to survive long enough to be copied by someone who isn't us. After that, it belongs to everyone, and everyone is very hard to kill."

Ilse was quiet for a while. Then she said, in the flat voice she used for the things that cost her most, "Anton used to talk like that. About paper being patient. He'd say soldiers win wars but paper decides what the war was." She set another line of type, her fingers moving with the blind certainty of someone who had learned the case in a language she couldn't speak aloud anymore. "He also said the man who taught him everything — Voss, the chief historian now, the man who trained the forger who trained himself — he used to say the same thing. Same words, nearly. Paper decides what the war was." Her jaw tightened. "Truth and lie share a workshop, Wendell. Remember that when you're proud of this plan. We're using his tools. His methods. His typography, even — look at your own title page. You set it in his face because it will pass his inspection. We are lying our way to the truth, and I want us both to understand that clearly before dawn, so that neither of us gets to be surprised later by what we've become."

Wendell thought about her father's name in the margin of the ledger page. *Witness.* Not victim. Witness to something he had spent two years refusing to say aloud, and which she would now set in metal and ink and send out into the world attached to his name forever.

"I understand it," she said. "I think I understood it the night I hid Ilse in the loading dock and watched Papa's face come apart. Everything since then has just been the slow work of admitting it."

* * *

The comparison plates were Kertész's idea, and they were the heart of the whole audacious structure.

"You cannot argue with a committee," she had said. "Committees are armored against argument; that is what committees are *for*. But you can put two documents side by side and let anyone with eyes do the arguing themselves. So: photographs, or better, facsimiles printed directly from the originals — the commission's page and the ledger's page, mounted together, with the discrepancies marked in red. The chain lines. The ink. The pen lifts. The watermark dated wrong by fourteen months. And then a plain statement, in type so ordinary it looks like a railway timetable, listing what each discrepancy proves."

It was Wendell who found the flaw that made the whole case unanswerable, and she found it, as Kertész had promised she would, not through brilliance but through slowness — through the discipline of looking at the same page for the eleventh time instead of the tenth.

The commission's history contained a document reproduced in facsimile: an order, allegedly issued two years ago, authorizing the reprisals along the lower river — the reprisals whose victims' names filled Anton's ledger. The order bore a date, a signature, a seal. It was the cornerstone of the official version: the killings were authorized, therefore lawful, therefore the responsibility of the regional commandant who had conveniently died in battle and could bear the blame alone.

But the facsimile was printed from a photograph, and the photograph showed the original document resting on a surface — and in the corner of the photograph, half cropped away, was the edge of a printed form. A requisition form. With a serial number.

Wendell tracked the serial number through three days of archives and one terrifying afternoon in the commission's own records room, where Ilse's translator's badge got them past a sentry who looked at Wendell's ink-stained fingers and saw only a clerk. The requisition forms with that serial sequence had not been printed until seven months *after* the date on the photographed order. The photograph of a two-year-old document had been taken on a desk that did not possess that piece of paper until a year ago.

Someone had staged the photograph. Recently. Carelessly.

When she brought it to Kertész, the old woman held the two references side by side for a long time without speaking. Then she laughed — a short, dry sound like a type stick being rapped on stone.

"There it is," she said. "The forger's signature. Not his name — forgers never sign their names. Their signature is the seam. The place where the lie was stitched to the world and the thread shows. Voss taught Anton everything, and Anton told me once — I interviewed him years ago, when he was merely a radical and not a corpse — that Voss's one law was *never forge what you can obtain*. Never fake a document when a real one can be altered. Because alteration leaves the original's bones intact, and bones are hard to fake." She tapped the serial number. "But this — this is a fabrication entire, and a fabricated photograph needs a *setting*, needs a desk, needs props, and props have histories. He reached for the nearest requisition pad on his own desk. Seven months too late. The most careful man in the empire, undone by a piece of stationery."

"Will a tribunal see it?"

"A tribunal?" Kertész shrugged. "A tribunal will see what it is paid to see. But a typesetter will see it. A clerk will see it. Some student, thirty years from now, working through the archive on a wet afternoon — *he* will see it. That is who we print for, child. Not the tribunal. The wet afternoon."

* * *

On the last night of preparation, snow began falling around nine o'clock, the fat silent flakes that meant the river would freeze solid by morning, and Wendell stood at the loading door watching it cover the courtyard where Ilse had arrived soaked and desperate all those weeks ago, carrying a dead man's handwriting.

Inside, the secret forme waited in its hiding place — locked into the bed of the old hand press, the one her father had always called the widow because it outlived every other machine in the shop. Under the pretext of repairing it, they had built the true edition inside its iron body: sixteen pages of ledger facsimile, eight pages of Kertész's analysis, four comparison plates, and a single title page that Wendell had composed and recomposed eleven times before she let herself lock it up.

It read, simply:

*The Ledger of the Lower Danube. Being a true account of the reprisals of the winter of ——, with documentary proof that the official history commissioned by the Armistice Committee is a forgery. Printed by W. Oyelaran & Co., by arrangement.*

No manifesto. No denunciation. Just the word *true*, set in the same dignified roman face her father had used for twenty years of proclamations, invoices, and lies.

Ilse came to stand beside her at the door. Snow gathered on her shoulders and did not melt.

"Tomorrow we interleave," Ilse said. "During the day run, while Brandt watches the front of the shop, we sew the signatures into the commission's copies at the bindery stage. Marta and the boys can do forty an hour if the folds are right. By evening, five hundred books, each one carrying its passenger."

"And tomorrow night—"

"Tomorrow night the armistice is signed, and the delegates receive their copies at the banquet, and Voss opens his own book and finds his own forgery staring back at him from between his own covers." Ilse's breath fogged and drifted. "Anton should have seen this. He planned the ledger for two years and he won't see a single bound copy."

"He saw the plan," Wendell said. "The letter that started everything — that was the plan. He wrote to my father knowing what it would set loose. Maybe that was the only ending he needed to see."

She did not say the other thing, the thing that had been sitting in her chest for days like a stone in a shoe: that tomorrow, when the books moved, the protection would be over. That the moment the first delegate opened the first copy, every finger would begin pointing backward down the chain of custody — to the bindery, to the shop, to the widow press, to the girl who signed the delivery note. Kertész had taught her that documents decide who lives. Tomorrow her own name would be on the most dangerous document in the city.

From the street below, footsteps in the snow — unhurried, deliberate, one set, stopping directly outside the loading door. Wendell's hand found Ilse's arm. Through the gap in the shutters they watched a figure stand in the falling snow, motionless, facing the shop. Then he struck a match, lit a cigarette, and the small flame lit for one instant the gray, punctual face of Herr Brandt, liaison officer of the Armistice Commission, who looked — for exactly as long as the match burned — directly at the loading door.

Then the match died, and the footsteps went on through the snow, and Wendell stood in the dark counting the hours until dawn.
