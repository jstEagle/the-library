## 1. Ink Under the Fingernails

The smell came first, always the smell, before the light of the lamps, before the groans, before the wet rustle of bandages being unrolled: carbolic and blood and the sweetish rot of wounds left too long in the summer heat. Freya Delacroix had learned to breathe through her mouth. It was the first thing she taught the younger girls, before how to fold a compress, before how to hold a man down when the surgeon's saw came out. *Breathe through your mouth, and your stomach will forgive you.*

It was the fourth of August, 1870, and Landau had become a mouth full of broken teeth.

She moved down the ward with a basin in her hands, steam rising off it, her sleeves pinned to the shoulder because the heat in the converted refectory was a living thing. Forty-one cots where the nuns had once fed the poor. Forty-one men, and more arriving on carts every hour from the direction of Weissenburg, their uniforms a mingled testimony that no one in this room had asked for: the blue of French line infantry beside the grey-blue of Bavarians, both of them bleeding the same color onto the same stone floor.

"You're burning," she said to the boy in the third cot, and put her palm flat against his forehead the way her mother used to, back when the worst thing in the world had been a fever.

The boy—Bavarian, sixteen at the outside, his drummer's hands too small for the rifle someone had put in them—tried to salute her and failed, his arm flopping against the blanket. "Fräulein," he managed. "The drum. Did anyone—"

"Your drum is safe." It was a lie. The drum had come in with him, splintered, one stick still strapped to his wrist, and Sister Anselma had burned it in the courtyard because there was nothing else to do with it and the cook needed fuel. "Sleep now. You'll want your strength for the letter."

The letter. They all wanted letters. She had a stack of them in her apron pocket, folded small, addressed in her careful hand to mothers and wives in places she pronounced silently to herself like a rosary: *Zweibrücken. Kaiserslautern. Haguenau. Strasbourg.* Half the men she wrote for would be dead before the letters arrived. She wrote them anyway. A letter that outlives its writer is still a letter, Sister Anselma said. God reads the address, not the postmark.

She wrung out a cloth, laid it across the boy's brow, and moved on.

At the far end of the ward, near the windows that looked out on the herb garden, lay the Frenchmen. Sister Anselma had insisted on the arrangement—Germans by the door, French by the garden—and when a Prussian sergeant had bristled at it two days ago, she had looked at him over her spectacles with an expression that could have curdled milk at forty paces and said, "The dying prefer the view. Would you prefer they didn't?" The sergeant had not preferred anything, visibly, and had left.

Freya knelt beside Corporal Aubert, whose leg had come off above the knee at Weissenburg and who was fading in the slow, dignified way that men sometimes chose, as though dying were a correspondence he intended to finish properly. His lips moved. She leaned close.

"Water," he said. "And—my wife. Léonie. In Colmar. Tell her the garden—"

"I'll tell her about the garden," Freya said, and meant it, and wrote it down later, word for word, because that was the other thing she did, the thing her hands did when no one was watching them.

* * *

The ledger lived in the bottom of the linen cupboard behind the fevered sheets, wrapped in oilcloth, and she went to it twice a day: once at the changing of the night watch, once at the gray hour before dawn when even the dying slept and the convent held its breath.

She did not write what the reports wrote. The reports—the ones the military authorities compiled with their neat columns and their rubber stamps—counted men the way a butcher counts carcasses: *Enemy losses, approximately. Friendly losses, approximately. Civilians: negligible.* Freya had seen the word *negligible* once, in a captured French dispatch a Prussian officer had dropped in the courtyard, and it had lodged in her like a splinter.

Her ledger counted differently.

She wrote by candle stub, hunched over the page with her back to the door, and the ink got under her fingernails no matter how she scrubbed, so that she carried the evidence of it into the wards each morning like a woman wearing her crime openly. Nobody looked at a nurse's hands. That was the mercy of being nobody: you could stand in any room in this war and be furniture.

*August 3rd,* she wrote. *Karl Weis, drummer, 6th Bavarian, aged perhaps sixteen. Mother: Anna Weis, née Hofmann, of Regensburg. He asked twice for his drum. Died at 11 in the evening. He was not counted in the Bavarian returns because his regiment's muster roll burned with the baggage train, and so officially he was never here, and so his mother will receive nothing, not even a date to mourn on.*

*Marie Ott, laundress, of Landau, aged thirty-four. Killed by shellfire in the rue de la Gare on the 2nd while carrying washing. Her daughter, aged six, is with the Sisters. The shell was German. The report of the bombardment lists no civilian deaths in the rue de la Gare.*

She wrote the names the way she changed dressings: thoroughly, gently, without flinching. A name written down is a splint set against forgetting. She had not always believed this. She had believed, a month ago, that the war would be reported honestly by someone whose job it was, that the columns in the newspapers would fill with true figures, that grief would be given its proper weight somewhere official. Then she had watched a Prussian staff officer dictate a casualty return that omitted an entire field station, and she had understood that the paper war was already being fought, and that it was being fought against the dead.

So she kept the ledger. She kept it the way the nuns kept their hours: faithfully, secretly, at fixed times, with a small flame.

* * *

Morning brought the carts.

She heard them before she saw them, the grind of iron-rimmed wheels on the cobblestones of the convent yard, and she was at the gate with her sleeves still unpinned when the first one came through, drawn by a horse that had gone blind in one eye and did not care. The men on the cart were Bavarian and French both, collected from the field at Weissenburg by a burial detail that had run out of shovels and started collecting the living instead.

"Fourteen," the driver said. "Two might last the day."

"Then we'll make sure they do," Freya said, and waved the cart around to the ward door, and the machinery of her morning took her: lifting, turning, washing, feeding, holding men's heads while they vomited, holding their hands while they confessed things they'd never told their wives. A Bavarian sergeant wept and told her he had shot at a boy in French uniform who had turned out to be no older than his own son. A French lieutenant with a belly wound recited the names of his company like a litany and asked her, each time he finished, whether she had written them down.

"Yes," she said. "All of them."

He looked at her for a long moment with fever-bright eyes. "Then someone will know we were men," he said, "and not a number."

"Numbers are men too," she said. "They're just men with the names taken off."

She said it without thinking, and then heard it, and carried it with her all day like a coin she'd found on the road.

* * *

In the afternoon she sat with Aubert while he finished his correspondence with death. He was quieter now, and the garden light came through the window and lay across the blanket like a hand.

"Colmar," he said. "The garden had pear trees. Tell her the pears—this year—the war came before the pears."

"I'll write it exactly."

"You are not a nun," he said. It was not a question. His eyes were clear in a way that frightened her more than the fever did.

"No."

"Then what are you?"

Freya looked at her hands. The ink was under the fingernails again, three days' worth, ground in at the quick. She thought about the question honestly, the way he deserved, and found that the answer had changed without her noticing.

"I'm a record," she said. "Someone has to be."

He smiled, a small movement, a door opening a crack. "Then write the pears."

She wrote the pears. She wrote them that evening, at her candle stub, with the oilcloth unwrapped and the ledger open to a fresh page, her pen moving in the small, upright hand that no one in this convent but she and God could identify:

*Corporal Étienne Aubert, 2nd Zouaves—no, line infantry, 2nd of the line—of Colmar. Died at four in the afternoon with the window open. He asked that his wife be told about the pears. The war came before the pears.*

She blotted the page. She was wrapping the oilcloth back around the covers when Sister Anselma's shadow fell across the doorway, and Freya's hand went still over the knot.

The old nun did not ask what she was hiding. She looked at Freya's ink-stained fingers, and at the shape of the book beneath the cloth, and something passed over her face that was not suspicion but recognition—the look of a woman who had spent forty years watching girls grow into their purposes.

"There is a Prussian column on the Speyer road," Sister Anselma said. "Artillery, wagons, a field hospital of their own. They will be in Landau by tomorrow night."

Freya's hands went cold around the ledger. "The fighting's east of us. Weissenburg is taken. What would they want here?"

"The wounded will be requisitioned first," the nun said. "Then the stores. Then, knowing soldiers, everything else that stands still long enough to be stamped." She paused at the threshold, and her voice, when it came again, was quieter. "Whatever you keep in that cupboard, child—decide tonight how much of it you can bear to lose."

She went. The candle flame leaned and righted itself. Down in the yard, the blind horse stamped once, and the wheels of another cart began to grind against the cobbles, and Freya stood in the linen cupboard with the war's true names in her arms, and began, for the first time, to think about hiding them.
