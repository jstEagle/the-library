## 7. Two Hands, One Page

The lamp had a cracked chimney that threw a hairline shadow across every page, and by the third night Elias Brandt had stopped noticing it. He had stopped noticing his own hands, too—the way they moved now without instruction, dipping the pen, blotting, turning the leaf of the original ledger with a care he had never once extended to the ten thousand documents of his official career.

Freya sat across the table from him in the infirmary's linen room, where the smell of carbolic and boiled cotton masked the smell of lamp oil and ink. Between them lay two books: the original, her ledger, its boards swollen from damp and handling; and the duplicate, a blank register they had liberated from Vogel's own requisitioned stores, which struck Elias as a justice so neat it was almost theological.

"Again," she said.

He wrote it again. *Johann Reiter, drummer boy, 2nd Bavarian Jäger, age fourteen. Died 6 August. Wound to the abdomen. Buried in unconsecrated ground behind the tannery because the chaplain would not come.*

His hand was better than hers at the formal hand—the upright, clerical script that Prussian record-keeping demanded—but hers was better at everything else. She could reproduce the small hesitations, the ink blots where her pen had paused over a name she'd known alive. A forgery, Elias understood, was not a copy. It was an act of memory performed twice, and the second performance had to believe itself the first.

"You're pressing too hard on the descenders," Freya said. "You write like a man being graded."

"I was graded for thirty years."

"And look what it made you." She reached across and turned his wrist, adjusting the angle of the nib, and did not let go immediately. Her fingers were cold. Everything about her was cold these days—since the fire, since Anselma—except her eyes, which had gone hot in a way that frightened him more than Vogel ever could.

* * *

The stamps were the problem.

Every document that survived into the century's archive would be weighed against its authentication marks: the round black seal of the field records office, the date stamp, the initials of the examining clerk. The original ledger bore none of these, which was precisely why it was dangerous—it existed outside the empire's system of truth, and anything outside the system could be dismissed as invention. So the duplicate would have to enter the system. It would have to be born already certified.

Elias had spent four days studying the seals before he dared cut one. He used a piece of cork from a wine bottle, a surgeon's scalpel borrowed from the operating theater and returned boiled, and a pot of printer's ink requisitioned under a false entry in his own manifest—a small treason, logged neatly, like everything else.

"The trick," he told Freya, holding the cork seal up to the lamp, "is not perfection. The trick is wear. A stamp used daily has a chipped edge here—" he pointed to the ring of the seal, "—and a faint double-strike when the clerk is tired. If I give you a perfect impression, any examiner will know it was made yesterday by someone with something to hide."

"So you've broken your own seal."

"I've broken my own seal," he agreed, "so that it can vouch for a lie."

She looked at him for a long moment. "Do you hear yourself?"

"Every hour."

"No—I mean it as a compliment." She took the seal from him and pressed it experimentally against her thumb, leaving a black circle on her skin like a brand. "You've spent your whole life making the empire's paper true. Now you're making truth out of the empire's paper. There's a word for that."

"Treason."

"Vocation," she said.

* * *

They worked in shifts timed to the hospital's rhythms. When the night rounds came, Freya went out to change dressings and hold dying men's hands, and Elias sat alone with two ledgers and one conscience. When she returned, smelling of carbolic and other people's blood, they compared pages.

It was during those comparisons that the lying became something else.

"Read me yours," she said on the fifth night. "I want to hear if it sounds the same."

He read. *Margarethe Falk, laundress, killed by shellfire 5 August while hanging washing in the convent yard. No military record exists. No pension will issue. Witnessed by Sister Anselma and this writer.*

"Again," she said, though there was nothing wrong with it.

He read it again, and heard what she meant: the first time it had been transcription. The second time it was testimony. Somewhere between the two readings, the duplicate had stopped being a forgery and become a second original—as if the truth were not a thing written once but a thing that insisted on being written again, by whoever was willing.

"Elias." She said his name rarely, and each time it landed like a document entered into evidence. "When this is over—if we live—what will you do?"

"I don't know that I'll be permitted a afterward."

"Answer anyway."

He considered the lamp, the crack in its chimney, the shadow it laid across her face. "There's a town called Zweibrücken," he said. "And after that, perhaps, a life in which no one asks me to file grief in alphabetical order."

"That isn't a life. That's just an absence of filing."

"It's more than I planned for in August."

She almost smiled. Under the lamplight, with ink on both their hands—his black, hers the faded blue-black of the original entries—he thought: we are two liars telling the same truth, and there is no confession required, because neither of us believes anymore that the lie is ours.

Then boots in the corridor, and the lamp went out between one heartbeat and the next, her hand finding the flame before his found the shutter.

* * *

Captain Vogel announced it at morning muster, in the courtyard, in a voice pitched to carry through the convent walls: the records would ship to Berlin early. Within the week. All crates, all registers, all death rolls and parish books and confiscated papers—sealed, manifested, and gone.

Elias stood among the clerks with his hands folded and his heart performing a slow, deliberate catastrophe.

Early. The word rearranged everything. Their schedule had assumed three weeks; their forgery needed six days more; and Vogel, standing at the courtyard's center with his gloved hands clasped behind him, wore the particular satisfaction of a man who has moved a chess piece and is waiting to see who flinches.

It was a trap. Elias felt it the way one feels weather changing—in the teeth, in the old wound of his disgrace. Vogel had no logistical reason to accelerate the shipment. Berlin had not asked. The rail schedule from Landau did not favor it. Which meant the announcement existed for one purpose only: to make whoever cared about these records do something careless.

That evening Vogel appeared in the archive room himself, unannounced, and stood watching Elias work for eleven minutes by Elias's count.

"You've been thorough, Brandt."

"I try to be, Captain."

"Yes. Thoroughness was always your problem." Vogel picked up a parish register at random, opened it, closed it. "Tell me—do you find much of interest in the convent's papers? Anything... personal?"

"The dead are personal, Captain. Otherwise the paperwork is dull."

"Mm." Vogel set the register down exactly where it had been, which was its own kind of message. "A fire in a building full of records. A nurse who spends her evenings near the archive. A disgraced archivist who once falsified casualty figures." He smiled without warmth. "You see how a suspicious man might arrange those facts into a sentence."

"I see how a suspicious man might. I also see a burned wing, a dead sister, and a war. Sentences arrange themselves around whatever a man already wants to believe."

For a moment something flickered behind Vogel's eyes—not doubt, but the recognition of an opponent worth disliking properly. "The crates are inventoried Friday," he said. "Every book accounted against the seizure list. Every page counted where counting is possible." He paused at the door. "Count carefully, Brandt. You have a gift for numbers. It would be a shame to waste it on a cell."

* * *

Afterward, in the linen room, Freya listened to the report with her arms crossed, and Elias watched her do the arithmetic faster than he could.

"Friday," she said. "Six days. We need four for the remaining pages and one for the drying. That leaves us nothing."

"It leaves us one day. One day is either enough or it is the whole design."

"He wants us to rush. Rushing men make errors."

"Or he wants us to freeze. Frozen men miss deadlines." Elias laid the two ledgers side by side. "We cannot know which trap it is. So we walk between them: we finish on time, we make no error, and we assume every hour is watched."

Freya uncrossed her arms. In the lamplight her face had the stillness he had learned to read as decision. "Then we finish tonight. All of it. And Jakob carries the last pages out tomorrow, before the inventory, hidden in the medicine run to the annex."

"He's twelve."

"He's twelve and no soldier searches a child carrying bandages for dysentery cases. Vogel's own regulations forbid it—hygiene exemptions, section nine." Her mouth curved, thin and fierce. "Let the empire's paper protect us for once. We've been using it for everything else."

Elias looked down at the open ledgers, the two hands alternating across the pages—her blue-black certainty, his upright clerical script—and understood that whatever happened Friday, this part was already finished. Not the copying. The other thing. Two liars telling the same truth had stopped being two somewhere around the fourth night, and there was no entry in any ledger, imperial or otherwise, that could unmake it.

"One condition," he said.

"Name it."

"When it's done—when the cart goes, whatever happens—we stop calling it a forgery. Even between ourselves."

Freya reached across the table and pressed her inked thumb beside the fresh black seal, a print like a signature the empire had never asked for and could not refuse.

"Witnessed," she said, "and entered."

Outside, the sentry changed the watch, and somewhere below, in the ward, a man began to die quietly while a boy carried bandages past a guard who did not look up. Four days remained. On the sixth crate, stamped and waiting, the seizure list had already been drawn up in Vogel's hand—and at the bottom of it, in fresh ink, a line Elias had not seen before:

*One item unaccounted. To be identified at inventory.*
