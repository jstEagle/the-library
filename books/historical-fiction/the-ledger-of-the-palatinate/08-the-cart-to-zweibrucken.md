## 8. The Cart to Zweibrücken

The coffin was too small. That was the first cruelty of it, and Freya had made herself look at it straight, because a lie you refuse to examine is a lie that will betray you at the worst possible moment.

Jakob had built it himself, planed smooth, the pine still smelling of resin, and he had wept while he worked—not for anyone dead, but for the boy whose name they were borrowing: little Anton Reiss, seven years old, carried off by the fever in March, before the Prussians came, before any of this. The parish register that would have recorded Anton's burial was crated somewhere in Vogel's archive, which meant that on paper, as far as the occupation could prove, no one had buried him yet. Elias had found the gap in the records himself, and had gone very quiet when he showed it to her, the way a man goes quiet when he realizes how easily his own tools can be turned.

"The dead are patient," Sister Anselma used to say. "It's the living who rush." Freya pressed her palm flat against the coffin lid one last time, feeling through the wood the weight of what lay beneath it—not a body, never a body; they had not dared that blasphemy—only the duplicate ledger wrapped in oilcloth, sewn into a false bottom Jakob had fitted with a carpenter's patience, plus the ink samples, the affidavits, the letters she had spent three sleepless weeks assembling. Everything. The whole truth of Weissenburg, of Hartmann, of every name the official history intended to forget.

Above it, layered in straw and lime, lay the actual remains of an actual child, exhumed that morning from the paupers' row with the gravedigger's silent cooperation and a coin that had once belonged to Freya's mother. If Vogel opened the coffin—and he might; he was not a stupid man—he would find a dead boy, and grief, and nothing else. Grief was the one document no archivist knew how to read.

"You'll go with her?" Freya asked Jakob.

He nodded. He was eleven now and had stopped crying easily, which she hated, because it meant the war had taken something from him too, quietly, the way it took everything. "I'm the mourning cousin," he said. "Family from Zweibrücken come to fetch him home. I practiced looking sad."

"You won't have to practice."

* * *

They left before dawn, when the fog off the Queich lay so thick the town seemed dissolved in milk. The cart was old Gruber's, hired for bread money and a promise, and the horse was half-blind and gentle, which suited Freya fine; she wanted nothing about this morning quick or clever or memorable. She rode beside the coffin in her blackest dress, her hands folded, her face arranged into the particular blankness of bereavement—the face she had worn so often over these two years that it no longer required arranging.

At the Landau gate the fog thinned, and Captain Vogel stood in it like something the fog had produced.

He was alone except for two sentries, and he held a paper in his gloved hand, and Freya felt her heart perform a single enormous contraction and then resume, steady, obedient, a nurse's heart, trained in crisis. *Busy hands,* she reminded herself. *No one asks about busy eyes.* But today her hands were empty, folded on her lap, and there was nothing between her and the captain's attention but a coffin and a lie.

"Frau Delacroix." He touched his cap. He had always been correct with her; correctness was its own kind of menace. "An early departure."

"A burial, Herr Hauptmann." Her voice came out low and even, and she marveled at it from a great distance, as if it belonged to someone else. "A child. His family in Zweibrücken wishes him home."

Vogel walked the length of the cart slowly. He looked at the coffin. He looked at Jakob, who sat with his head bowed and his knuckles white on his knees, playing his part so well that Freya wanted to put her hand on his shoulder and tell him to breathe. Vogel looked at the straw, at the lime, at the shovels lashed to the side, and then he looked at her, and in his eyes was the particular arithmetic of a man who suspects everything and can prove nothing.

"There is a regulation," he said, "regarding the transport of documents out of occupied territory."

"I am transporting a child, Herr Hauptmann. Unless your regulations have annexed the dead as well."

The sentry nearest the cart shifted. Somewhere behind the fog, a bugle sounded the morning call, thin and far away. Vogel's jaw tightened, and Freya understood suddenly that he had been waiting for her—that this was not chance but design, that someone had talked, or he had simply watched her long enough to know that the nurse with the quiet hands was the center of whatever moved beneath his tidy occupation. He raised the paper in his hand.

"All conveyances leaving the town are subject to inspection by order of—"

"That order will not be necessary, Captain."

Elias Brandt stepped out of the fog.

Freya had not known he would be there. They had agreed he would stay away—his presence would only confirm suspicion—and seeing him now, walking toward the gate with a leather satchel under his arm and the gray morning light making him look older than his years, she felt terror and love arrive together, indistinguishable, twins.

Elias handed Vogel a manifest without being asked. "Conveyance number forty-one, burial transport, cleared yesterday under my seal. Section twelve, sanitary regulations—I signed it myself." He said it in the flat bureaucratic German of the archive, each clause a small brick in a wall. "You'll find my stamp below the counterstamp. If you wish to override it, Captain, you will need to file the objection through the commission, which sits in session until Thursday, and the child will wait in the ground here until then. In the fog. In the lime." He paused, delicately. "I assume the commission has no objection to a Christian burial being delayed for paperwork. But I would hate to be the officer who has to explain it to the clergy. Or to Berlin. The clergy write letters, Captain. Clergy write *many* letters."

Vogel read the manifest twice. Freya watched his eyes move and understood, with a cold clarity, what Elias had done: the manifest was forged, or half-forged, backdated, stamped with the archive's authentication seal in a use that would take any auditor ten minutes to unravel. Elias had not merely covered her. He had written his own confession into the record and handed it to the one man most likely to read it closely.

"Elias Brandt," Vogel said softly. "The archivist who questions figures."

"The archivist who completes them," Elias said. "Someone must, Captain. You'd be astonished what falls between the cracks otherwise. Children, for instance."

For a long moment nobody moved. The horse stamped. The fog drifted between them like a curtain deciding whether to close.

Then Vogel folded the manifest along its crease, very precisely, and put it in his breast pocket, and Freya knew—he was keeping it. Keeping it the way a man keeps a knife he intends to use later. "Go," he said. "The cart passes. But it will be remembered that it passed, Frau Delacroix. And it will be remembered who let it." He turned his gaze on Elias, and there was almost pity in it, which was worse than anger. "You should have stayed in Berlin, Brandt. Men who file grief eventually become it."

He stepped aside. The sentries lowered their rifles.

"Drive on," Freya said to Gruber, and her voice did not break, though everything inside her did.

* * *

The road to Zweibrücken ran west through wet fields and leafless orchards, and for the first hour none of them spoke. The fog burned off slowly, revealing a country scarred but going on anyway—crows rising from a plowed furrow, a woman beating a carpet over a fence rail, smoke from chimneys where people were baking bread as if empires were not falling on their heads.

Jakob was the first to laugh. It started as a shudder and became real, helpless, eleven-year-old laughter, and when Freya looked at him he pointed back down the road, at the gate already lost from view, and said, "He kept the paper. He *kept* it," as if this were the funniest thing in the world, and perhaps it was, because keeping things was all any of them had done for two years, and today, for once, the keeping had worked.

Freya laughed too, and then found she was crying instead, silently, the tears running into the collar of her black dress, and she did not wipe them away because there was no one on this road to perform composure for. She cried for Anton Reiss, who was really in the coffin and really going home. She cried for Anselma, ash in the west wing, who would never see this. She cried because it was finished and because it was not finished at all—it had only just begun, and the beginning would cost more than the smuggling had.

Elias rode up alongside on the borrowed mare and did not touch her, did not speak, only matched his horse's pace to the cart's, close enough that his sleeve brushed hers at every rut in the road. That was enough. That was, she thought, the truest language either of them had.

"They'll arrest him within the week," Elias said finally, nodding back toward Landau. "Vogel will audit the manifest tonight. By tomorrow he'll know the stamp was mine. By Thursday—" He shrugged, a small, terrible shrug. "By Thursday I'll be a case file myself. Filed under treason, probably. Alphabetically it comes quite early. I checked once."

"Then don't go back."

"And leave the archive unguarded? Leave the original ledger sitting in a crate with my forged stamp three pages from it, waiting for some clerk to connect them?" He shook his head. "No. Someone has to stay and misfile things properly, Freya. There's an art to it. Besides—" and here his voice changed, went quieter, went the way it went when he spoke of the work itself, the only altar he had ever served, "—if they take me, they take me *after* the copy is safe. And a copy in safe hands is worth more than an archivist. Even this one."

She reached over and gripped his wrist, hard, once, and let go. Neither of them said anything else. There was nothing left to say that the road didn't already know.

* * *

They buried Anton Reiss at dusk in the churchyard at Zweibrücken, properly, with the priest who owed Gruber a favor and did not ask questions, and Jakob laid flowers on the small grave with genuine tenderness, because whatever else the coffin had carried, the boy beneath the stone deserved his name spoken aloud at last. Freya stood with her bare head bowed and committed him to God along with everyone else the ledger held, the drummer boy, the laundress, the men Hartmann had left to bleed at Weissenburg, and she felt the strange lightness of a woman who has finally set down a thing she has carried so long she had forgotten its weight.

The duplicate was safe now, hidden in the cellar of a widow in Zweibrücken who had lost two sons at Sedan and asked no questions beyond "Is it true?" and "Will it matter?" Freya had answered yes to both, and the widow had believed her, because widows believe the dead.

They lodged that night above a cobbler's shop, and Freya was washing in a basin of cold water when the knock came—a courier, mud to the knees, a telegram folded twice. She opened it standing, water dripping from her wrists onto the floorboards.

It was addressed to Elias. She read it anyway. There were no secrets left between them worth the price of them.

*BRANDT STOP WARRANT ISSUED LANDAU STOP HARTMANN COMMISSION MOVES TO DECLARE LEDGER FORGERY STOP VOGEL REQUESTS YOUR RETURN UNDER ESCORT THURSDAY STOP THEY ARE ASKING ALSO AFTER THE NURSE.*

She carried it downstairs to where Elias sat sharpening a penknife by candlelight, and she put the telegram in his hands, and he read it without surprise, the way a man reads a diagnosis he has suspected for months.

"They're asking after me," she said.

"Yes."

"Then they'll come to Zweibrücken."

"Yes." He looked up at her across the candle flame, and she saw in his face neither fear nor despair but something she recognized from her own mirror these past two years: the calm of a person who has stopped calculating whether to act and begun calculating only how. "Freya. When they come for you—don't run."

She had been afraid he would say *run*. She had prepared arguments against running all evening, rehearsed them in the dark. Now he had taken the argument away from her entirely, and she stood in the cobbler's kitchen with wet hands and a dry throat and understood what he was telling her: that hiding had bought them the copy, and the copy was safe, and the only weapon left was herself.

"No," she said. "I'm done running. I've been invisible for two years, Elias. Invisible women get erased." She took the telegram back, folded it along its crease, precisely, the way Vogel had folded the manifest, and put it in her bodice over her heart. "Tomorrow I'm going to find out where the commission sits in this town. And I'm going to walk in the front door."

Outside, the church bell rang nine, and somewhere down the dark street, boots were already asking questions at doors.
