## 3. The Baptismal Page

The storm had spent itself by morning, and Valparaíso lay rinsed and dripping in a light the color of pewter. From the shop window I could see the harbor working itself back to order — fishing boats going out with their sails still furled, gulls arguing over whatever the night's wind had torn loose from the fish market — and it seemed impossible that only hours before, a man had stood on this same threshold with rain running off his coat and a child in his arms.

Impossible, and yet there she was.

She had slept most of the night in the type cabinet drawer I'd lined with my father's old shirt, waking twice to cry with a fury that seemed far too large for so small a body, and both times I had paced the length of the shop with her against my shoulder, whispering nonsense, feeling like a burglar in my own life. Now she slept again, and I sat at the workbench with the folded page from the naval officer's pocket spread flat beneath the lamp, and I told myself I was only looking.

Only looking. As if looking had ever been a neutral act in this family.

* * *

My father used to say that paper forgets nothing and forgives nothing. He said it while wiping ink from his hands, always, as though the thought were a residue the trade left on him along with the lampblack. A printed page is a confession, he'd say. Every letter accuses somebody. You may as well know who before you set it.

I had heard the sermon a hundred times and believed it the way one believes weather — abstractly, until it rains.

The page was good stock, rag paper, the kind used for parish registers, and it had been folded twice and carried long enough for the creases to soften into cloth-like ridges. The header read *Libro Primero de Bautismos — Parroquia del Espíritu Santo, Valparaíso*, and below it, in a careful ecclesiastical hand that was not nearly as careful as its writer wanted me to believe:

*En doce de Marzo de mil ochocientos noventa, bautizé solemnemente á una niña de tres días, hija legítima de Don Fermín Undurraga y de Doña Mercedes Undurraga: púsosele el nombre ALMENDRA.*

A legitimate daughter. Born the twelfth of March, 1890. Named Almendra — almond, the sweet kernel inside the bitter shell — and entered into the book of the faithful with all the proper flourishes, the abbreviations, the little superscript letters that made a lie look like liturgy.

Except that I knew the hand. Not the priest's hand — that was competent forgery, the kind any clerk with a steady wrist could produce after an evening's practice with a borrowed register. I meant the type.

The entry was not written at all. It was printed — set letter by letter in metal, inked, and pressed onto the page to imitate handwriting, then gone over with a pen in a few strategic places where a register would show corrections or blots. It was a trick as old as printing itself, and it was done well. But the letters themselves betrayed it, because letters are like voices. You can disguise your speech, but the shape of what you say remains yours.

And these letters were cut in my father's shop.

* * *

I want to be precise about what happened in the next hour, because precision was my inheritance too, and because what I found on that page rearranged everything I understood about the man who raised me.

I took down the specimen books first. Every type foundry keeps them — bound volumes of printed samples showing each face at each size, the way a tailor keeps swatches. My father had kept three, going back to the founding of the shop, and after his death I had continued them, pressing a fresh sample whenever we cut a new matrix or re-cut a worn one. They were the shop's memory in metal and ink. They were also, I realized as I turned the pages with the baby's blanket draped over one arm, the shop's conscience.

The body text of the forged entry was set in a face called — my father's joke — *Escribana*, our imitation of a chancery hand, cut in 1879 for a client who wanted circulars that looked personally penned. We had sold it perhaps six times. Nothing remarkable. But the capital A in *ALMENDRA* stopped my breath.

Every cutter has tics. You cannot help them; they come up through the graver like veins through marble. My father's tic lived in the apex of his capitals: he cut the peak of the A with a minute flat facet, a facet no wider than a hair, where other cutters would leave a point or a serif. He claimed it caught the light better on the press. I claimed it was vanity — that he liked knowing his letters were identifiable, the way a painter signs a canvas in a corner no one looks at. We argued about it pleasantly for years. He died before either of us won.

There it was. Under the loupe, magnified ten times, the apex of the A in *ALMENDRA* wore its tiny flat facet like a signature. And beside it, in the specimen book, the A from our own *Escribana* matrices — cut by my father's hand, pulled from our own press — wore the identical facet, identical to the width of the hair, because a graver does not make the same mistake twice unless the mistake is a man.

Someone had taken type out of this shop and printed a false baptism.

* * *

The child woke while I was still bent over the loupe, and cried, and I fed her the milk I had bought before dawn from the widow Contreras two doors down, telling her I'd taken a fancy to custard. She had looked at me the way Cerro Concepción looks at strangers — kindly, and without believing a word — but she sold the milk, and the city being what it was, she would sell her silence just as cheaply if anyone asked.

I held Almendra — *her* name now; the page had given it to her and I could not unknow it — in the crook of my left arm, the way I had seen laundresses hold babies while wringing sheets one-handed, and with my right hand I turned pages and matched letters.

It was slow work. It was, I confess, the happiest hour I had passed since my father died, and I am not proud of how much that admission costs me. There is a particular communion in matching type — the world reduced to a question of forms, of whether this curve answers that one, of weight and counter and the white space a letter carries inside itself like a held breath. For an hour the questions were answerable. The baby slept. The lamp burned. Letter answered letter, and every answer was the same: *this came from us.*

But the answers accumulated into a larger answer, and the larger answer was terrible.

The date on the page was March 1890. My father died in November of 1889. So whoever printed this forgery had done it within four months of his death — which meant they had done it while his matrices still sat in their cases, unguarded, in a shop whose master was freshly buried and whose only remaining soul was a nineteen-year-old apprentice who kept to himself, spoke little, and was never, ever seen undressed.

They had not needed to steal anything. They had needed only to wait for the funeral, walk in through a door that grief had left unlatched, and use my father's letters to commit their crime.

Unless.

Unless they hadn't waited. Unless they had come earlier, when my father was alive — when he was ill, in those last gray months when the cough kept him abed and the shop ran on my hands alone, and callers were received upstairs, and I was downstairs setting type and hearing footsteps cross the floor above and thinking nothing of it, thinking nothing, nothing, nothing.

I set the loupe down. My hands were not entirely steady, and I watched them not be steady with a kind of clinical disgust, because a compositor's hands must be steady, must be trustworthy, must never tremble over someone else's name.

* * *

Here is what I did next, and why.

If the page was forged — and it was; the facet proved the type provenance, and the priest's hand proved the rest — then it had been forged for a purpose, and purposes in baptismal registers are narrow. Either someone wanted a child to appear legitimate who was not, or someone wanted a record to exist that did not, or — and this was the possibility that sat coldest in my chest — someone wanted a record to *disappear behind*. A forged page is a door. You print it to close another one.

The officer had said: *forget my face.* He had paid me in gold, more gold than the child's keep would require for a year, and men do not pay a year's keep for an errand of charity. Men pay that way to purchase silence, or to discharge terror, or both.

And the name on the page — Undurraga. I knew it. Everyone in Valparaíso who could read knew it. Don Fermín Undurraga, deputy for Valparaíso, the congressman whose speeches against President Balmaceda were printed in *El Mercurio* and read aloud in cafés by men who then argued until the glasses broke. A patron of the shop, too — that was the part that mattered to me, the part that made the name land in my stomach rather than merely in my ear. Don Fermín had ordered pamphlets from my father for years. Election broadsides, a memorial edition when his wife died — Doña Mercedes, the register said, mother of the legitimate Almendra — and my father had spoken of him with the particular warmth he reserved for clients who paid promptly and argued about kerning. *An honest man,* my father had said once, *which in Congress is a professional deformity.*

So the forged page named a living, prominent, dangerous man as the father of a legitimate daughter. And a naval officer had carried that page through a storm to my door along with the daughter herself, and begged me to forget his face.

You did not need to be a compositor to read that sentence. You needed only to know that in Chile, in the winter of 1891, with Balmaceda and Congress circling each other like dogs over a bone, a document proving the parentage of a congressman's child was not paper. It was ammunition. It could save him. It could hang him. Depending on whose hands closed around it.

And I was holding it in a shop full of stolen letters.

* * *

I worked until the light failed, and what I assembled, piece by piece, was this:

The entry was printed on our *Escribana* type, our matrices, my father's facet. The ink was lampblack-based, our own mixing — I knew the recipe's slight greasiness under the loupe the way a cook knows her own stew. The paper was genuine parish stock, which meant someone had obtained blank leaves from a register, or from the stationer who supplied the parish, and that narrowed the conspirators to men with money or office. The corrections in pen were added afterward, by a different hand than the main forgery — a right-handed writer imitating, poorly, the hesitations of an old priest.

And one thing more. In the lower margin, invisible unless held to the lamp at an angle, there was a blind impression — a mark pressed into the paper with no ink, the ghost of a letter that had been set, proofed, and then removed. I tilted the page against the flame for a long time before I could raise it. A single character, struck hard enough to emboss the rag paper permanently.

It was an S.

Not my father's S — the facet was wrong, the spine too upright. A different S, from a different case, but cut in the same tradition, the same school of graver-work. Someone had set an S on that line, changed their mind, distributed the type, and pressed the sheet anyway — carelessly, or in haste, or in some private ceremony I could not begin to guess at.

An S. I sat with that a long while, the lamp guttering, the baby breathing in her drawer, the harbor fog coming up the street like something poured.

Sebastián. My name, the name I had worn for two years like a coat cut slightly wrong. Or — and here the cold went deeper — the initial of whoever had composed the forgery, sitting in this very room, standing at this very stone, inking these very letters while my father coughed upstairs or lay already dead below the floorboards.

I had been so busy proving the page came from my father's hand that I had not let myself ask the question underneath: *who else was in this shop?*

* * *

Almendra woke at dusk and did not cry this time. She simply opened her eyes and regarded me with the grave, unfocused solemnity of the very young, as though she had been listening to all of it and had reached conclusions.

"You have a name," I told her. My voice sounded strange in the empty shop, rusted from disuse. "Almendra. It seems you're a congressman's daughter, and it seems the whole government might someday want to know that, and it seems someone committed a crime with my father's letters to make it so." She blinked. It was not a satisfactory audience, but it was the only one I had, and saying it aloud made it real in the way that setting type makes words real — one letter at a time, irrevocable, each one locking the previous into place.

I should have burned the page. I knew that even then. A sensible woman — a sensible *apprentice*, I corrected myself, feeling the old reflexive flinch — would have fed it to the stove and told no one, kept the coins, kept the child quiet, kept the secret small enough to hide inside an ordinary life. That was the arithmetic of survival, and I had been doing that arithmetic since I was seventeen years old, since the morning I bound my chest and cut my hair and buried my father and became Sebastián Brightwater, apprentice, a young man of few words and fewer friends.

But my father had also taught me the other lesson, the one underneath the sermon about paper. *Ink is a witness,* he said. *And a witness that burns is worse than one that speaks.* He meant that destroyed evidence breeds suspicion, but I have wondered since whether he meant it more largely — whether he was telling me, in his sideways way, that truth has a physical weight and cannot finally be melted down, only misplaced.

I did not burn the page. I wrapped it in oilcloth, and I hid it where no agent of any government would think to search, because I hid it in plain craft: I pasted it, face inward, beneath the lining of the drawer where the *Escribana* matrices slept, so that the forged letters lay skin-to-skin with the true ones, the lie pressed against the metal that proved it. If they wanted the page, they would have to dismantle the type to find it. Let them try. No soldier on earth knows a type case well enough.

Then I picked up the child, because the milk was finished and the night was coming, and because tomorrow I would need to carry her up the hill to Cerro Alegre, where widows take in laundry and ask no questions, and I could not set type with an infant in the house and a sergeant's knock always possible. But that was tomorrow's composition.

Tonight there was only the lamp, and the drawer, and the small warm weight of a girl named for a nut — the sweet thing inside the shell — sleeping above a hidden page that tied her blood to Congress, her christening to a crime, and my father's dead hand to a plot I could not yet see the shape of.

I blew out the lamp. In the dark, the harbor foghorn sounded once, long and low, like a voice deciding what to say.

And I thought — with the clarity that comes only when it is far too late to be useful — of Don Fermín Undurraga's last visit to the shop, three weeks before the bombardment rumors began, when he had stood exactly where the officer had stood, and looked at me exactly as long, and said, in a voice carefully stripped of everything:

"Your father's letters are the finest in Chile, Señor Brightwater. I hope they will be in good hands, when they are needed."

I had thought he meant the trade.

I was no longer sure what he had meant. But I knew one thing with a compositor's certainty, letter locked to letter: within the week, that man would walk back through my door — or send someone to demand it — and I would have to decide, with the page warm against the matrices, whose side my father's letters had been cut to serve.
