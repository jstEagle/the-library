## 5. Sergeant Roldán Calls

The rain had stopped by nine, and Sergeant Emilio Roldán decided that the morning had a shape to it — the shape of a man deciding to be reasonable before he was forced not to be.

He stood across the calle from the Brightwater type foundry for eleven minutes, long enough to be mistaken for a clerk waiting out the weather, and watched the shop do what shops do: breathe. Smoke from the stovepipe chimney, thin and gray against the wet sky. A boy — no, the apprentice, the dead founder's boy, Sebastián — hauling a crate of type metal up from the cellar with a hand truck, moving with that particular economy of someone who has done a chore ten thousand times and resents it only mildly. Down at the harbor mouth the fog was lifting off the water in rags, and somewhere beyond it the fleet sat at anchor like dogs pretending to sleep.

Roldán had read the file on the shop twice. It was a thin file. José Brightwater, English by blood, Chilean by thirty years of ink under the fingernails, dead of his heart eight months past. The son — or the apprentice; the papers were ambiguous in the way that small businesses are always ambiguous — kept the trade going. Paid his municipal dues. Printed playbills, shipping manifests, labels for the wine merchants, wedding invitations for families who could afford copperplate but settled for letterpress because Brightwater's hand-cut faces were prettier than anything imported. No politics on record. No pamphlets. Nothing seditious, nothing loyal, nothing at all.

That nothing was what interested him.

In Roldán's experience, printers who printed nothing political were either cowards or liars, and Valparaíso in the spring of 1891 did not have room for many cowards. The city was full of talk the way a hull is full of bilge water — you didn't see it until you opened the wrong valve, and then it came up all at once. Congress against the President. The fleet against the forts. Every café on Cerro Alegre running two conversations at once, one aloud and one beneath it, and every printing press in town a potential cannon if the right man loaded it.

He crossed the street.

* * *

The bell over the door of the Brightwater foundry made a sound like a coin dropped into a church plate, and Roldán noted, with professional pleasure, that the apprentice looked up from the composing stick before the bell finished ringing. Good ears. Or a guilty conscience. In his experience the two were difficult to tell apart and rarely worth distinguishing.

"Buenos días," Roldán said, and removed his hat, which he did not need to do, and let the water drip from its brim onto the clean floorboards, which he also did not need to do. "Sergeant Roldán. Policía Fiscal. I'm making a survey of the print trades. Routine."

The apprentice set down the composing stick with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down a knife when a stranger enters the kitchen. Up close, the boy was younger than Roldán had guessed from the street — seventeen, perhaps eighteen — with ink worked so deep into the knuckles that no pumice would ever get it out, and a face that was doing an admirable job of being blank and a poor job of being quick about it.

"Routine," the apprentice repeated. "We haven't had a visit from the police since—" He stopped, recalculated. "Since never, actually."

"Then I'm honored to be first." Roldán smiled the smile he practiced in the mirror above his washstand, the one that said *I am a tired public servant and you are a bore to me*, and produced his papers. The apprentice read them longer than necessary, which told Roldán the boy could read well and was using the time to arrange his face. Also good. Also bad.

"Of course," the apprentice said finally. "Look wherever you need. Though I'll warn you the cellar floods when it rains, and today it has very much rained."

"I've been warned by worse than weather." Roldán stepped past him into the body of the shop, and let the shop speak to him.

It spoke fluently. Printers' shops always did; they were the most honest rooms in any city, because type cannot lie about itself. Along the left wall, the cases — California job case, lower case, the deep banks of matrices racked like organ pipes, each drawer labeled in a careful hand: *Brevier, Long Primer, Pica, Great Primer*. Above them, the private faces. That was the thing about Brightwater, the thing that had put the shop in the file at all despite its thinness: old José had cut his own punches, dozens of them, faces that existed nowhere else in Chile or the hemisphere. A roman with calligraphic terminals. An italic so slanted it seemed to be running. A display face the apprentices called *la Viuda* — the Widow — because of the black teardrop serifs. Distinctive as a fingerprint. Beautiful, Roldán admitted, even to his uneducated eye. And therefore dangerous, because beauty is memorable, and memory is evidence.

"Your father's work?" he asked, nodding at the racks, though he knew the answer.

"Yes, señor. All of it." Something moved behind the apprentice's eyes — grief, still fresh enough to be involuntary. "Every punch in this room, he cut himself. Took him twenty years."

"He must have been patient."

"He was stubborn," the apprentice said, and then, as if hearing how the word might land in a police sergeant's ear, added quickly, "in the good way. With the steel. Not with people."

"With people he was soft?"

"With people he was fair." The correction came fast and smooth, and Roldán filed it: *the boy is used to defending the dead man's character. To whom? Creditors? Customers? Someone has asked these questions before.*

He wandered. He let his fingers trail along the stone sink where the rollers were washed — the water in it was cloudy and smelled of turpentine and linseed — and paused at the press itself, a Columbian of respectable age, its eagle lever polished bright by hands. On the imposing stone lay a half-finished form, locked in the chase: a shipping manifest for the firm of Williamson, Balfour & Co., forty copies, due Thursday. Utterly innocent. He leaned over it the way a man leans over a sleeping child, and felt the apprentice's attention settle on his back between the shoulder blades like a hand.

"You're expecting me to find something," Roldán said pleasantly, without turning.

"No, señor."

"But you've thought about what I might find. You've spent the last minute cataloguing this room through my eyes. The manifest. The label stock. The drawer of ornaments." Now he turned, and held the boy's gaze, and saw the pulse jump once in the throat before the face closed like a ledger. "That's not guilt, young man. That's just sense. Only guilty men relax around the police."

"I don't think I'll manage the relaxing part, señor."

Roldán laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him — and saw the boy almost smile in response before catching it. Almost. There was something in the apprentice's face that resisted him, some load-bearing wall of caution that no joke would breach, and Roldán had been doing this work long enough to know that resistance of that quality was not fear. Fear wobbles. This was discipline. Somebody had taught this boy to be afraid carefully, systematically, as a craft.

Interesting.

* * *

He inspected the way he liked to inspect: slowly, silently, letting the silence do the work. Silence makes honest men nervous and dishonest men inventive, and both reactions are useful.

The cellar was indeed half-flooded, an inch of harbor-smelling water over the flags, and the paper stock had been raised onto pallets with the anxious foresight of a household that had lived through hard times. He counted the reams without appearing to count them. Enough paper for a great deal more than shipping manifests.

Back upstairs, he drifted along the wall of specimen books — bound volumes showing every face the shop offered, each sample line printed in three sizes — and opened one at random. *La Viuda*, in display size: **EL PUERTO HABLA** — *the port speaks*. Old José had had a sense of humor. Roldán wondered idly whether the son shared it, and whether it would survive the next six months.

It was on his third circuit of the room that he began collecting the small things, the things that were not evidence yet but were patiently becoming evidence, the way sediment becomes stone.

The first small thing: the hands. The apprentice had gone back to work while Roldán prowled — a sensible move, work as armor — and was setting type again, and Roldán positioned himself at the angle where he could watch the fingers move. They were quick, certainly. Trained. But they were wrong. Too fine at the tips. The nails too neat, the skin too unscarred for a trade that ate men's hands — castings misfired, hot metal spat, matrices bit. His own cousin had been a compositor in Santiago and his thumbs looked like walnut shells. These hands looked like they had been protected from the world for a long time and had recently begun lying about it.

Gloves, Roldán thought. Or a mother's care. Or—

He put the thought down gently, the way one puts down a full glass, and picked up the second small thing instead.

The milk. On the shelf beside the stove, half-hidden behind a tin of ink solvent, stood an earthenware jug, and the jug was not empty. He lifted it as if admiring the glaze. Fresh milk, still cool, cream beginning to separate at the neck. A pint, perhaps less. Milk in a print shop. Milk for coffee, certainly — but there was no sugar bowl, no cup dirty or clean, no sign that anyone in this room drank coffee at all. The stove had a flat space swept clear for cooking, and on that space a single saucepan, scrubbed, sized for porridge or for warming milk, and nothing else. No plates. No bread box. Men who live in their shops eat like men: badly, standing up, out of paper. This shop fed someone who drank warm milk.

A cat, he supposed. Shops kept cats for the paper-mites. But cats got milk in saucers, lapped, spattered — and the rim of the jug was clean, wiped, tended.

The third small thing arrived while he was setting the jug back: a sound from above. Faint. A floorboard shifting in the room overhead, where the file said the late Mr. Brightwater had kept his living quarters. Then another. Then — Roldán held his breath and gave the silence its full courtesy — a third, rhythmic, small, the unmistakable cadence of a person pacing a short distance and turning around. Pacing, or rocking. Not the tread of a grown man.

"You live alone?" he asked the apprentice, conversationally, not looking up.

"Yes, señor."

"The room above — your father's?"

"It's storage now. Type furniture, paper, the old presses that don't run." The answer came without hesitation and without breath, delivered at the exact speed of truth, which is precisely why Roldán disbelieved it. Liars rehearse their lies until they come out level; the innocent stumble; but the trained — the trained deliver everything at the same tempo, and the tempo itself becomes the tell. Someone had drilled this boy. Or the boy had drilled himself, nightly, for years.

"Storage," Roldán echoed. "You keep your late father's room for type furniture. Sentimental trade, yours."

"We're practical people, señor."

Above them, the floor creaked once more — softly, softly — and went quiet, as though whoever paced up there had heard the word *practical* and frozen.

Roldán turned his head and looked directly at the ceiling for a long moment, and then looked back at the apprentice, and smiled his tired-public-servant smile, and said nothing at all, because saying nothing was the loudest thing available to him.

* * *

He finished the inspection with the cellar drains, the coal store, and a theatrical examination of the privy, and then, finding nothing he had expected to find and several things he had not, he returned to the center of the shop and put his hat back on, which in Roldán's private grammar meant the interview was over and the visit had not been.

"Well," he said. "Nothing seditious here. Manifests and wedding invitations. You may tell your customers their secrets are safe from the government, such as they are." He took out a card — plain, printed cheaply, his name and the office on the Calle de la Aduana — and laid it on the imposing stone, weighting it with a spare quoin as if it were a piece of type awaiting composition. "If anyone offers you work that seems — ambitious. Political. Pamphlets, broadsheets, anything unsigned. You'll send for me before you accept it. Consider it a professional courtesy. From me to you."

"And if I don't?"

The question hung in the air, and Roldán admired it even as he registered its danger. Most boys would have said *of course, señor*. This one had asked the real question, the only question, with the flat honesty of someone who had already calculated the cost of both answers.

"Then I'll hear about it anyway," Roldán said, "and the next visit won't be routine, and I won't admire your composure, because I will already have stopped believing in it." He let that sit. Then, softer, almost kindly: "This city is going to come apart, young man. Perhaps quietly, perhaps the other way. When it does, the government will remember who helped and who watched. I'd rather remember you kindly. You seem like someone worth remembering kindly."

The apprentice looked at him — a long look, older than seventeen, older than the shop — and said, "I'll send for you, señor."

"Liar," Roldán said cheerfully, and touched his hat brim, and went out into the washed light of the morning.

* * *

On the corner opposite, he stopped and lit a cigarette he did not want, and made himself wait, because patience was the whole of his method and he had built a career on out-waiting other men's nerve.

Two minutes. Three. The bell over the Brightwater door rang, and the apprentice emerged with a covered basket on one arm and a shawl — a shawl, folded over the basket, wool, gray, feminine in its folding if not its weave — and walked quickly up toward Cerro Alegre with the basket held against the chest in the manner of every woman and nursemaid in Chile carrying something alive.

Roldán smoked his cigarette down to the cork tip and did not follow. Following was for gendarmes. He had what he needed, which was not proof — proof could wait, proof ripened — but pattern. A shop that fed milk to no one. A room that paced. Hands too fine for the trade. A shawl folded like a secret. And underneath all of it, humming like a live wire, the fact that mattered most and proved least:

Brightwater's private faces. Dozens of them. Faces that could set a page that no one could prove had come from anywhere else — or, conversely, faces whose every curve could be identified by any expert in the country. The same quality that made them beautiful made them witnesses. Somewhere in that shop, in steel and lead, lay the means to forge a document that would fool a bishop — or to expose a forgery already made.

And Roldán happened to know — because knowing was his trade, as cutting type was the Brightwaters' — that somewhere in this city there existed a baptismal record, entered years ago in a parish ledger, in type that certain gentlemen in the Ministry had recently developed a passionate interest in proving authentic or false, depending on which way the war broke. A record concerning a congressman named Fermín Undurraga, whose name had begun appearing this week on lists, and whose daughter, the story went, had died in infancy.

Died in infancy. Roldán blew smoke at the harbor and considered the milk jug, the pacing floor, the shawl.

He flicked the cigarette end into the gutter, put on his hat properly, and went to write his report — a short one, deliberately, omitting the milk and the shawl and the hands, keeping those in the drawer of his own mind where he kept everything that was valuable precisely because it proved nothing yet.

Some drawers, he reflected as he walked, you lock. And some you leave ajar, and wait to see who comes to open them.
