## 7. The Wet Nurse of Cerro Alegre

The decision, once made, arranged itself into a list, because lists were how I kept terror at arm's length. My father taught me that. *When the type case jams and the deadline burns, Sebastián, make a list. Fear cannot read.* I stood at the workbench at four in the morning with Almendra asleep against my shoulder in a sling of flour sacking, and I wrote on the back of a spoiled galley proof:

1. Milk.
2. A woman to give it.
3. A story no one will test.
4. A route no one will watch.

Four lines. It did not look like the shape of my whole life bending, but it was.

The milk I had managed for nine days now, and badly. There was a goat kept behind the chichería on the next street, and I had bought milk from the chichera's daughter with a story about a sick cousin, and I had boiled it in the type-casting pot after scrubbing it, which would have made my father weep, and fed the child with a rag dipped in it while she screamed at the insult of it. She was gaining weight the way a leaky boat gains water. I could feel her ribs under my palm like the staves of a barrel not yet hooped. Nine days of a stranger's goat and a rag, and the rag was not enough, and both of us knew it.

A woman to give it. That was harder. A wet nurse in Valparaíso in the spring of 1891 was not hired, she was *arranged* — through priests, through midwives, through the quiet economy of women who knew which households had lost a mother and which mothers had lost a child. I could not go through priests. A priest would ask who the father was, and the mother, and why an apprentice compositor with ink in the creases of his knuckles was keeping an infant in a print shop above the harbor. Every question was a nail, and enough nails make a cross.

But Doña Peta Quintanilla was not a priest. Doña Peta was a laundress on Cerro Alegre who had washed my father's collars for eleven years and had buried a husband in the smallpox year and a daughter — this was the part that mattered, and I hated myself for how much it mattered — a daughter-in-law, seven months past, dead of childbed fever, along with the infant. I had sent flowers. My father had sent money. Neither of us had gone, because my father did not climb hills in his last year and I could not climb them as a woman.

I knew her schedule the way I knew the harbor tides. Tuesdays and Fridays she came down to the Almendral with her basket to collect work from the shops along the flat, and she always, always stopped at our door for a cup of hot water and a bit of conversation, because my father had been kind to her once in a way she never forgot and never explained, and kindness like that outlives the man who gave it.

It was Tuesday. I had until ten o'clock.

* * *

I fed Almendra at half past four so she would sleep through the approach, and I cleaned the shop the way a man cleans a shop — badly, in the corners, thoroughly in the middle. I put away the goat's milk rag. I burned the list. I put on my father's spare waistcoat because it made my shoulders read wider in the grey light, and I bound myself the way I had bound myself every morning for three years, and I looked in the cracked mirror behind the door and saw Sebastián Brightwater, journeyman, twenty-two years old, tired.

The mirror did not show me what Roldán's kind would eventually see. I told myself that. I had been telling myself that for three years.

Doña Peta came at nine, her basket on her hip, her grey hair pinned with what I had long suspected was a fork. She was a small woman built like a capstan, all of her strength in the forearms, and her face had the particular geography of a woman who had done her grieving in public and her weeping in private and now had no use for either in company.

"Sebastián." She always said my name as though tasting it for flaws. "You look like your press ran over you."

"The press and I have an understanding. Sit. There's hot water."

"There always is." She sat on the bench by the door, the one my father had built too low for any chair-loving Chilean and exactly right for a laundress resting her feet. She looked around the shop with the frank inventory of a woman who had washed every rag in it. Her eyes snagged, as they always did, on the type wall — the hundred small drawers, each labeled in my father's hand, each holding a voice. "Your father's shop still smells like him."

"That's the ink. Ink smells like everyone who's ever touched it."

"Mm." She accepted the hot water. I had put the last of the sugar in it, which she noticed, and which cost me, and which I did not begrudge.

I had rehearsed the story on the walk to the goat and back, and rehearsed it again at the workbench, and now that it was time I found I could not deliver it as rehearsed, because Doña Peta's eyes were on me and lies, I have since learned, are like type: they set best when you stop trying to force the justification.

"Doña Peta," I said, "I need your help, and I can't tell you the whole truth, and I'm sorry for both."

She drank her water and waited. Women like her had been waiting out men like me since before the Spanish came.

"My sister had a child," I said. "My sister is dead."

It was not even entirely a lie. There had been a sister, once — Marisol, dead of the same fever that took my mother, before I could walk. I had borrowed a dead girl's grave to stand on, and the ground held.

"The child is three weeks old. There's no one. There's — the circumstances are bad, Doña Peta. The father can't be known. If it's known the child is in this shop, it will go badly for the child, and worse for me, and I don't have milk, and I don't have a wife, and I don't have—" I stopped, because the sentence had a cliff at the end of it and I could see the sea below. "I have coins. I have work I can do in trade. I have nothing else."

"The child is here?" she said. "In this shop?"

"In the back."

She set down the cup. She stood, and she did not ask permission, which I had known she would not, and she walked through the composing room past the press with its great iron wing, and she lifted the blanket off the drawer where Almendra slept — a type case drawer, lined with clean sacking, the compartments empty beneath her, and I had told myself the symbolism of that meant nothing — and she looked down at the child the way a woman looks down a well she once fell into.

Almendra woke, and did not cry. She looked up at Doña Peta with the grave, unfocused astonishment of the very young, working her small mouth like a fish testing the surface.

"Oh," said Doña Peta. Just that. A single syllable with a whole drowned family underneath it.

"She's not mine," I said quickly. "I want that understood. I'm keeping her for—"

"I don't care whose she is." Doña Peta's voice was flat and hard as a washboard. "I care that she's three weeks old and light as a confession. Who's been feeding her?"

"Goat's milk. Boiled. On a—"

"On a rag. Dios mío. You've been feeding a baby like she's a sick cat." She turned and the full weight of her regard came off me like a wave off a breakwater. "How long?"

"Nine days."

"Nine days." She said it the way my father used to say *widow* — a word for a thing already lost. Then she unbuttoned her bodice with the unsentimental efficiency of a woman who had fed children in doorways and ditches, and she sat on the crate by the press, and she took my whole future out of the drawer and into her arms.

I looked away. Then I looked back, because I found I couldn't not.

Almendra rooted, found, and latched, and the sound she made — the small, furious, satisfied sound of a body finally getting what it had been owed for nine days — went through me like a spike through a forme, and I understood, standing there in my dead father's waistcoat in my dead father's shop, that I was in a great deal more trouble than I had planned for.

* * *

The arrangement took an hour to make and the shape of a confession to describe, and I made it in logistics, because logistics were the only language in which I could afford to be fluent.

Almendra would go up the hill to Doña Peta's house on Quebrada de San Juan — two rooms, a courtyard with a washing copper, a view of the harbor that cost nothing and was worth everything. Doña Peta would say the child was her daughter-in-law's, born before the fever took her, smuggled out by a midwife and now come home. There were women on the cerro who would believe it and women who would not, and the women who would not would also not say so, because Doña Peta had washed for half of Cerro Alegre and knew what was in everyone's linen, figuratively and otherwise.

I would come up twice a week — Tuesdays after dark, Saturdays at midday — as Sebastián, the print apprentice, delivering wages. Coins in an envelope. A man visiting a laundress who kept his sister's orphan was a story with a door in it; a woman visiting would be a story with a window, and windows get looked through.

"And the father?" Doña Peta asked, while she packed — she had apparently decided in the first five minutes, and had begun folding her spare clothes around a basket as though my consent were a formality already notarized. "The true father. You know who he is."

"I know who he isn't. That's all that's safe."

"Safe." She snorted. She wound a blanket around Almendra with three turns and a tuck that I could not have performed in a year of practice. "Nothing in this city is safe, muchacho. Not the harbor, not the hills, not the government. You don't keep a child safe. You keep her *hidden*, and you pray the hiding holds longer than the war."

"What war?" I said. "There's no war."

She looked at me with something close to pity. "You live above the harbor and you read the papers you print and you think there's no war. The *Blanco* has been saying for a month that the fleet will never fire on Valparaíso, and the *El Chileno* has been saying the opposite, and one of them is printed by fools and the other by liars, and I wash for the wives of naval officers, Sebastián, and I can tell you that the officers have stopped bringing their uniforms home to be cleaned. They're keeping them aboard." She hefted the basket. "Men keep their clothes where they expect to sleep. Think about that."

I thought about it for a long time after she climbed the hill out of sight, the basket on her hip and the blanket-wrapped bundle riding above it like a second, smaller cargo, and the fog took her at the corner of the Quebrada del Almendro the way the fog takes everything in this city eventually — not gone, just withheld.

* * *

The rumors came the next day, and the day after, and they came the way rumors come to a print shop: first as paper, then as talk, then as a change in the quality of the silence.

Paper first. The *El Chileno* we pulled for proofing carried a dispatch from Santiago — Congress had fled, the President had declared their acts null, the words *civil war* set in a type size usually reserved for earthquakes and royal deaths. I stood with the proof in my hands and read it three times and felt nothing, which is how I know now that I felt everything. Then Tomás — my apprentice-mate, nineteen, all elbows and appetite — came in from the street with his cap in his hand and his eyes too wide.

"They're saying at the fish market the fleet's going to bombard the forts," he said. "The *Cochrane* and the *Blanco Encalada*. Against the *Esmeralda* and the shore batteries. They're saying the *Esmeralda*'s captain told the harbor pilot that if the government puts guns on the hills he'll shell the city itself."

"That's talk."

"That's what I said. But the fish market's emptying anyway. The English ships are taking on coal like it's going out of fashion, and the *Imperial* German one has moved to the outer roads, and my cousin says the consuls have told their nationals to keep their papers ready." He looked at the press, at the type wall, at me, with the naked question of the young: *what do we do?* "Sebastián. What do we do?"

"We print," I said. "Whatever happens, someone will need handbills. Someone will need the truth, or something near it, and it will have to be inked by hand. We print."

It sounded brave. It was mostly true. Both things can be said in the same voice; my father taught me that too.

* * *

Saturday, midday. I climbed Cerro Alegre with coins in my left boot and a pound of rice in my coat and my heart going like a platen.

The house was the second past the washing copper, blue-washed, with a door that stood open to the courtyard because doors on the cerro stand open in daylight — a fact I had not considered until I was in front of it, a man in a waistcoat arriving at an open door in a street where every neighbor was a window. Doña Peta saw me before I could arrange my face.

"You'll come in," she said, "and you'll take off your hat, and you'll hold her, because she's been fed and she's clean and there's no reason on God's hill you shouldn't."

"I don't—"

"Sit down, Sebastián."

I sat. She put Almendra into my arms with the same unsentimental efficiency she did everything, and my hands, which can set twelve hundred ems an hour without a misprint, did not know where to be. The child weighed more than she had nine days ago. That was the first thing. She had a double chin arriving like a rumor of better times. That was the second. She looked up at me, and focused — actually focused, those dark eyes finding my face and *staying* — and something in my chest performed a maneuver I had no name for.

"She knows you," Doña Peta said, folding laundry, not looking at me. "Nine days in a drawer with you, she knows your smell. That's a bond, muchacho, whether you signed for it or not."

"She's not—"

"If you say *she's not mine* one more time I'll put the flatiron where the sun doesn't reach. Here's what you are: you're a man who took in a stranger's baby because no one else would, which is more than her father did, whoever the coward was." She snapped a sheet straight. "Now. The neighbors believe my story, mostly. Señora Vergara across the way believes it completely, because she wants to, because believing it lets her be charitable without cost. But the fisherman's wife on the corner asked me yesterday who the father was, and I told her a sailor lost at sea, and she said *ah, like the papers say, so many lost*, and Sebastián — she said it *looking at me*. Looking the way women look when they already know and are checking whether you know they know."

"The story will hold."

"The story will hold as long as nothing shakes it. So here is what shakes it: war. If the fleet bombards the harbor, every woman on this cerro will be in the courtyard with her children and her chickens and her whole biography, and stories get compared the way linen gets compared. Yours is a good story. Good stories survive weather. They don't survive *scrutiny*." She set down the sheet. "So. How long?"

"How long what?"

"How long until you take her back? Or until you stop pretending you're going to?"

I looked down at Almendra. She had seized my forefinger in her whole fist, with the tremendous, unearned confidence of the infant, and was holding it the way a typesetter holds a quoin — tight, essential, load-bearing.

"I don't know," I said, which was the truest sentence I had spoken in a week.

Doña Peta nodded as though I'd said something wise. "That's the first honest thing you've brought up this hill. The coins I'll take. The rice I'll take. The lies, I'll take the useful ones and return the rest." She went back to her folding, and the copper bubbled, and the harbor below us lay flat and glittering and innocent as a coin at the bottom of a well, and somewhere out on those outer roads the warships sat with their clothes aboard.

* * *

I came down the hill at dusk and the city had changed in the six hours I'd been above it, the way a sky changes before it commits to weather. There were more soldiers on the flat than there had been at midday — government troops, red-striped trousers, bayonets catching the last light — and they were not marching anywhere. They were *standing*, in pairs, at the corners of the Almendral, and one of them was outside the chichería where I bought goat's milk, and he looked at me as I passed with the flat professional attention of a man who has been told to look at everyone.

I did not hurry. Hurrying is a confession. I walked to my door at the pace of a tired apprentice, and I went in, and I lit the lamp, and I stood in the middle of my father's shop and listened to the harbor breathe.

The drawer where Almendra had slept was still lined with clean sacking. It smelled of her — of milk and of that particular warm-flour smell of new people. I stood there longer than a man with work should, and then I took the drawer out entirely and set it on the bench where the light fell on it, and I looked at the empty compartments, each one the exact size of a single letter of type.

*E*, *L*, *M*, *N*, *D*, *R*, *A*. I set her name in my mind, in my father's first-cut roman, the letters standing in their little cells like a crew at muster. Almendra. Almond. A thing that is sweet inside a hard shell, and blooms before it should, in the cold, on faith.

A knock at the door, two raps, soft.

Through the frosted glass: a shape in a naval greatcoat, and under the coat's collar, the pale line of a face I had seen once before, nine days ago, in fog and rain — and my hand went to the bolt before my mind had finished the arithmetic of *he came back*, and behind him, down the street, the sentry at the chichería had turned his head, and was looking at my door.
