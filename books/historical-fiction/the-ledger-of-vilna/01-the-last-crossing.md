## 1. The Last Crossing

The bridge closed on the last day of the pearling season, and the company liked its accounts sealed before the river sealed itself.

Cyrus Amberdale had carried the winter packet across the plank-and-pontoon span of the Vilna crossing for six years now, ever since she'd grown tall enough that Master Ellery stopped saying *the child can do it* and started saying *the girl is quick.* Six years of the same errand, the same wind off the water, the same ritual at the far end: the factor's clerk on the north bank would break one seal, count the folios, reseal, and sign her receipt, and she would stand with her hands folded and her eyes down and say nothing at all, which was the most valuable skill the Vilna Trading Company had ever taught her.

She had come to them at eight years old, an orphan with a debt attached — her father's debt, though no one had ever explained to her what a man who mended nets could possibly have owed a trading company that he hadn't already paid with his hands. The indenture said she belonged to them until her twenty-first year. The indenture said a great many things. Cyrus had learned early that papers in Vilna said whatever the person holding the pen wanted them to say, and that the only safety was in not being the person who asked why.

So she did not ask why. She was good at not asking. She had made a quiet art of it, the way other people made quiet art of embroidery.

"Mind the wind," Master Ellery had told her that morning, pressing the packet into her arms. It was wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with the company's grey wax, three seals, heavy as a fist. "And mind the clerk. He'll try to talk to you. Clerks always try to talk to you. It's how they find out things."

"Find out what things, master?"

"Whatever things there are." He'd coughed then, the wet rattle of a cough that had been getting worse since the first frost, and waved her off with a hand that trembled more than it used to. "Go. The ice won't wait for your questions, and neither will the river."

The bridge was already crowded. It always was, on the last crossing day — the whole town of Vilna seemed to have decided that if it must be trapped on one side of the water or the other for four months, it would at least spend the final hour of freedom jostling elbows with everyone it would soon be sick of. Carters with their wheels wrapped in sacking. A woman carrying a goose under each arm, both geese objecting in unison. Two children racing each other along the planks until their mother's voice caught them like a net.

And below, on the south shore, the diver families were unloading the season's catch.

Cyrus stopped at the middle of the span, where the railing was worn smooth, and let the traffic flow around her the way water flows around a post. She told herself she was only resting the packet, which was heavy, which was true. But she knew the other reason too, and she was honest with herself in the small ways that were safe.

The boats came in low in the water, gunwales nearly kissing the river, heaped with oyster shells in long baskets. The divers themselves came up the landing stairs gray-lipped and shivering, ears still ringing from the pressure of the deep water, and their wives met them with wool blankets and something hot in tin cups, and the children ran the baskets up the shingle to the drying racks, where the shells would be cracked and sorted through the winter. The pearls went into the company's strongbox. The shells went to the workshop. The divers went home with a slip of paper that said how much of their season's wage remained after the company's deductions, and every year Cyrus noticed that the slips seemed to say less than the year before, and every year she noticed that she noticed, and every year she put the noticing away in the same quiet drawer where she kept everything else that didn't reconcile.

There was Tam Voss, up on the landing stage with a basket on each shoulder, arguing cheerfully with the tally-clerk about the weight of the third basket. He was nineteen, broad through the shoulders in the way that divers' sons got by sixteen, with his father's dark curls plastered to his forehead and his mother's stubborn chin. Even from the bridge Cyrus could see him making the tally-clerk laugh while refusing to move an inch on the count — that was Tam all over. He could charm a stone into apologizing, his mother said, and then get it to carry a basket too.

He looked up, as if he'd felt her watching — he always claimed he could, and she had never once managed to prove him wrong — and raised one hand off his basket-pole. Not a wave, exactly. A salute. *Still there?*

*Still here,* she raised back, packet and all, and saw him grin before the crowd swallowed the space between them.

Six years of that. He'd been fourteen and she'd been thirteen when he first came to the workshop window with a cracked shell and an outrageous story about the oyster that had made it, and she'd been supposed to send him around to the yard door, and instead she'd leaned on the counter and listened to the whole story and told him his pearl was worth exactly one honey-bun, and he'd said *done,* and neither of them had ever really closed the account since. It was the one ledger in her life she kept gladly, and never balanced, because balancing it would mean it was finished.

The wind came up the river, sharp with the smell of coming ice. Cyrus shifted the packet and went on.

* * *

The factor's clerk on the north bank was named Hobb, and he was exactly the kind of man Master Ellery had warned her about: friendly, chatty, and curious in the way of a man who had been promised that curiosity would be rewarded.

"Cyrus Amberdale, isn't it?" He broke the first seal with a bone folder, unhurried, watching her face as much as the folios. "Ellery's girl. He's not long for the work, they say. Lungs like wet paper."

"The master's season has been hard," Cyrus said, which was true, and said nothing, which was safe.

"Hard seasons make for hard arithmetic." Hobb counted the folios with one finger, lips moving. "You do the copying now, do you? The man must trust you."

"The man trusts the seals," Cyrus said, and Hobb laughed as though she'd said something clever, and signed her receipt, and she took it and folded it away and walked back out into the wind with her heart beating in the steady, unremarkable rhythm of a girl who had just told the truth in a way designed to end a conversation.

The return crossing was emptier. The last of the divers were tying down their boats for the winter, and the bridge planks rang hollow under her boots, and somewhere behind her the pontoon-keepers were already beginning their long argument about which floats would need re-caulking before spring. On the south shore, Tam Voss had come down to the water's edge with his father, and the two of them were hauling the family boat up onto the rollers together, the old man steering and the young man pushing, and Cyrus watched them the way you watch something you are afraid to want: the boat going up the shingle, the season's work done, the family whole, the debt — whatever the slip said the debt was — waiting for spring like everything else in Vilna.

She was nearly to the workshop door before she saw Master Ellery.

He was standing at the yard gate, which was wrong, because Master Ellery did not stand at gates. Master Ellery stood at desks, and had done for forty years, and the yard was the domain of the shell-crackers and the carters and the boys who swept, and an old man in a ledger-keeper's coat had no business there at all. But there he was, hunched into his coat against the wind, watching the last boats come up the shingle, and there was something in the set of his shoulders that stopped Cyrus where she stood.

She had spent her whole life in the counting house learning to read people the way she read columns: not the numbers themselves, but the spaces between them. She knew what it looked like when a carter padded his load by one basket. She knew what it looked like when a clerk rounded a sum in his own favor. And she knew — with the cold, immediate certainty of someone who had been raised on the company's kindness and never once been fooled by it — what it looked like when a man was doing something he did not wish to be seen doing.

Master Ellery had his hand inside his coat.

Not adjusting it. Not warming it. Holding something flat against his chest, the way you hold a folio you do not want creased. And as she came through the gate he turned, and for half a heartbeat his eyes met hers, and she watched him do it — watched him decide, in the space of that half a heartbeat, whether to take his hand out of his coat or leave it where it was.

He left it where it was.

"Girl." His voice was rough. "You gave it to Hobb?"

"Sealed and signed for, master."

"Good. Good." He coughed, turned his head to the side, and Cyrus saw, in the gray light, how thin he had gotten — the coat that had hung on him all autumn now hung on him like a flag on a windless day. "The workshop will want you at the sorting frames. The shells came in heavy this year."

"Heavy is good," Cyrus said. "Heavy means the divers ate well."

"Heavy means the shells came in." He said it flatly, without looking at the shingle, without looking at the boats, without looking anywhere at all except at a point just over her left shoulder, which was where a man looked when he was holding a page inside his coat and did not want to think about what was on it. "Go on, then. And Cyrus."

She stopped. He had never used her name before. In six years she had been *girl* and *you there* and, once, memorably, *the quick one,* but never Cyrus.

"Master?"

He looked at her then, properly, and his eyes were the color of weak tea and full of something she could not name — something that was not quite apology and not quite warning and sat in the uncomfortable country between them.

"When the ice comes," he said, "and the workshop goes quiet, and the nights get long — you'll keep the ledgers well, won't you? Every line true. Every figure where it belongs."

"Every line true, master. That's the whole of the work, isn't it?"

"Yes," said Master Ellery. "That's the whole of the work."

He took his hand out of his coat at last, empty, and walked past her into the yard, and if there was still a flat rectangular weight somewhere about his person, in the breast of the coat or the lining or the space where a man keeps the things he is not ready to show anyone, then Cyrus Amberdale did not see it, and did not say she saw it, and filed it — where else — in the quiet drawer.

But that night, in her narrow bed above the workshop, with the wind worrying the shutters and the river groaning its first ice-song out in the dark, she found that the drawer would not stay shut.

She lay awake and turned it over, the way she turned over a column that wouldn't reconcile. A man who had kept the company's books for forty years. A man who did not stand at gates. A man who had watched the divers' boats come in and held something flat against his chest and then asked her, of all people, whether she would keep every line true.

*Every line true,* he'd said. As if there were lines that weren't.

In the morning she would report to the workshop, and Master Ellery would set her to the copying, and the winter would close over Vilna like the ice closing over the river, and she would learn — though she did not know it yet — exactly what a man keeps warm against his heart when he has decided he is dying.

The last crossing was done. The bridge would not open again until the ice broke.

And somewhere in the dark above the counting house, the season's true accounts were waiting to be read.
