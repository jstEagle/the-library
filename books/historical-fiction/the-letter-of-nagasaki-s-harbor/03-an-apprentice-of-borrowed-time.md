## 3. An Apprentice of Borrowed Time

The winter her father died, the frost came early to London and stayed like a creditor.

Oscar had been seventeen that November, and she had spent most of it in the workshop's back room, mending watch mainsprings by lamplight while her father coughed in the bed above. The cough had started in September as a small dry thing, a tick in his chest like a escapement gone slightly out of beat. By October it was a hammer. By the first week of November he could not climb the stairs to the shop, and by the second week he could not sit up to see the work she brought him.

"Show me your hands," he said one evening, when she brought his broth up untouched because he'd fallen asleep before she reached the landing.

She held them out. They were stained with oil at the knuckles and nicked along the left thumb where a slip of the graver had caught her that afternoon. He turned them over with fingers that trembled — not from weakness, she told herself, only from cold — and read them the way he read a movement: back plate, train, balance.

"They'll do," he said. "Better than mine did at your age."

"Your hands are fine," she said. "You'll be back at the bench by Christmas."

He smiled at that, the small private smile he kept for things they both knew were lies but agreed not to charge interest on. "Bring me the ledger," he said instead. "The green one. Bottom shelf, behind the spare pendulum bobs."

* * *

The green ledger was the shop's memory. Every repair since 1889 was entered there in her father's narrow hand: the customer, the piece, the fault, the remedy, the fee. Some entries were a line. Some were a page. There was a whole entry on the vicar's bracket clock that read simply, *Mr. Ellery says the chime is haunted. It is not. It is a worn pallet stone and a guilty conscience.* Oscar had laughed over that entry a hundred times. That night she carried it upstairs with both hands, as if it might break.

Her father propped himself against the pillows and opened it to the last written page, then turned past it to the blank ones.

"Now," he said. "We're going to write an entry together. You'll hold the pen. I'll tell you what it says."

"I don't understand."

"You will." He closed his eyes for a moment, gathering breath the way a clock gathers fall before it strikes. "When I'm gone, this shop closes. That's what happens. A workshop is a man's name over a door, and without the name—" He stopped. Started again. "Unless the name keeps working."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the regulator clock in the shop ticked on, indifferent, keeping the time of a world that was about to change its mind about everything.

"Papa—"

"Hear me out. I've been thinking on this since September, so don't waste my breath arguing before you've heard the whole of it." His voice had the flat steadiness he used with difficult customers, the ones who wanted their grandfather's clock made young again for the price of a cleaning. "Your mother's people have money enough to keep you in a boarding house until some solicitor marries you or some mill works you dead. I won't argue which is worse; they're both doors into the same dark room. Or—" he laid his palm flat on the ledger, "—the shop stays open. Blackwood & Son, Clockmakers. And the son turns out to be a slow grower, quiet, keeps to himself, learned everything at his father's knee. Which will be true, every word, if anyone asks."

"That's fraud."

"That's arithmetic." He said it without heat. "I've done the sums, Oscar. I've been doing them since the doctor looked at me the way he did. One way, you lose everything and become someone's burden. The other way, you lose your name — my name, which I lend you gladly — and keep your hands, your trade, your roof, and the only thing I ever made worth leaving anyone." He tapped the ledger once. "This. The record. Everything we've ever fixed, everyone who trusts us. Names don't mean anything except that people know them. So let them go on knowing Blackwood."

She looked down at the blank page. The lines were ruled faintly blue, waiting.

"What would the entry say?" she asked, and heard her own voice go strange and small.

He dictated it slowly, pausing for breath between phrases, and she wrote it in her best imitation of his hand, which was already good — she'd been practicing it since she was twelve, signing repair tickets when his eyes were tired:

*November 14th. O. Blackwood, apprentice, assumes full charge of the workshop. All work guaranteed as before.*

"There," her father said softly, when she finished. "Now it's true. It's in the book."

"It isn't true. I'm not your apprentice. I'm your daughter."

"You're both," he said. "You've been both since you were six years old and took apart my good lever escapement to see where the ticking lived. The book doesn't lie, Oscar. People just don't read all of it." He leaned his head back against the headboard, exhausted by the effort, and for a while the only sound was the regulator downstairs, striking eight in its patient bronze voice.

"Come here," he said finally. "Let me show you the trick of it. Not the disguise — the disguise is nothing, clothes and a lowered voice and dirt under your nails, any actress on Drury Lane can manage that. The trick is smaller than that and harder." He held up one finger. "Never sign anything twice the same way. Never let them see you count money — women count differently, thumbing the notes, and men notice without knowing why. Walk like your boots are too heavy. And never, never be alone in a room with another tradesman's wife, because kindness is the thing that unmasks us faster than suspicion ever will. Suspicion looks for evidence. Kindness looks for company."

"Why are you telling me this now? You'll be here for years yet. You'll teach me properly."

"Yes," he said, and looked at her with such gentleness that she understood, finally and completely, and had to put the pen down because her hand had begun to shake. "Yes, I will. Every day I have. Now listen to the rest."

* * *

He lasted nine weeks.

They were the strangest weeks of her life — half nursemaid, half apprenticeship conducted at speed, her father propped in bed issuing instructions like a general directing a battle from a hilltop. How to true a wheel. How to talk price with a man who thought grief entitled him to a discount. How to close the shop at exactly six, because a clockmaker who kept irregular hours was a clockmaker whose clocks couldn't be trusted. She wrote it all down at night in the margins of the green ledger, filling the blank spaces around the entries, until the book became two books woven into one.

The neighbors helped without knowing it. Mrs. Petchey next door sent up pies and asked no questions, because grief was a wall even the curious respected. Mr. Danvers at the ironmonger's extended credit without being asked, saying only, "Your father stood surety for me in 'ninety-one, lad. We're square when I say we're square." The town of Ashcombe had known Blackwood & Son for thirty years, and the Son had been seen in the shop window since he was small enough to stand on a stool — a thin, shy boy, always bent over the bench, always oily-fingered, always, conveniently, facing away.

That was the thing she learned in those nine weeks, the thing that sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone: nobody looked. People saw what the sign over the door told them to see. She bound her chest, wore her father's old apron, cropped her hair herself with the sewing scissors and a hand mirror, and the town adjusted its picture of the world to fit. Only once did the mask nearly slip — a traveling dealer named Croft, sharp-eyed, wine-flushed, who watched her across the counter for a long moment and said, "Funny. Old Blackwood had bigger hands."

"He did," she said, heart slamming. "I take after my mother."

Croft laughed, bought a carriage clock, and never gave her another thought. But she lay awake that night listening to the regulator strike the hours, counting them like a rosary, and understood that she would spend the rest of her life one remark away from ruin. Not caught — *unmasked*, which was worse, because being caught meant you'd done something, and being unmasked only meant you were something.

Her father died on the twenty-third of January, in the hour before dawn, quietly, between one tick of the regulator and the next. She was holding his hand. She felt it go light.

She buried him on a Saturday, in rain that froze as it fell, under a stone that read *EDMUND BLACKWOOD, CLOCKMAKER, AND HIS SON OSCAR* — because she had ordered the stone herself, and because the mason had raised an eyebrow at the son's absence, and because a lie, once started, must be fed on schedule like a clock. She stood at the graveside in her father's black coat, which fit her shoulders almost well enough, and listened to the vicar say words about a good man and his promising boy, and felt the strangest sensation: that she was standing at the funeral of someone who hadn't died. That Edmund Blackwood's son was alive and well and standing right there in the wet grass, and would go on living as long as she could hold her shoulders the right way and keep her voice down where the coal smoke hung.

Afterward, back in the shop, she took down the green ledger and turned to the entry they had written together. *O. Blackwood, apprentice, assumes full charge of the workshop.* Below it, in her own hand, she added the week's repairs: Mrs. Tallow's carriage watch, cleaned; the church turret clock, oiled and regulated; one alarm clock, new balance staff, Mr. Hobbes, paid.

Then she sat down at her father's bench — her bench — and began to cry, silently, the way she had taught herself to do everything, until the regulator struck seven and she washed her face, lit the lamps, and unlocked the door for the day's first customer.

* * *

Three years passed. Then four.

The mask grew skin. Her voice settled naturally into its lower register, or perhaps she simply forgot there had ever been another one. Her shoulders broadened with the work — filing, turning, carrying movements up ladders to regulate the church clock — until one day, catching her reflection in the shop glass, she saw a young man looking back at her, thin and serious, with her father's frown and her father's stoop, and felt something between grief and triumph that she never found a name for.

She got good. That was the armor no one could pierce. When your hands could do what hers could do, customers forgave a great deal of oddness. Oddness in a craftsman was practically a credential. *Blackwood's boy doesn't talk much,* they said, *but look what he does with a broken repeater.* She let them say it. She built the legend the way her father had taught her to build a fusee — patiently, turn by turn, each coil bearing a little more weight.

But armor is heavy, and she wore it to bed. She wore it in dreams. Once, drunk on half a glass of sherry at a client's Christmas gathering, she nearly hummed along to a song her mother used to sing — caught herself with the note half-formed in her throat and converted it into a cough, and went home shaking, and did not sleep. Another time a girl in the market, a stranger passing through, looked at her a moment too long and smiled, and Oscar walked home the long way with her heart going like a detached balance wheel, and did not go back to that stall again for a year.

That was the tax. Nobody spoke of it, but she paid it daily, in small coin: the friendships not risked, the laughter kept low, the whole warm animal country of being known that she had renounced at seventeen in exchange for a bench, a roof, and a name that wasn't hers. Some nights she took out the green ledger and read her father's marginal notes just to hear his voice, and once — only once — she allowed herself to write, in pencil, in a corner of a blank page, a single sentence she erased the next morning with bread crumbs:

*I am so tired of being no one's daughter.*

Then, in the spring of her fourth year alone, the letter came from Nagasaki.

It arrived in a foreign envelope, postmarked through a shipping agent in London, addressed to *Mr. Oscar Blackwood, Clockmaker, Ashcombe.* Inside, on paper thin as onion skin, a formal request: the observatory at Nagasaki harbor required a master clockmaker. Their own horologist was dead. The English firm that had supplied the mechanism had dissolved. A colleague of her father's — a name she half recognized from an old correspondence file, a Swiss-trained man named Brandt who had emigrated east decades ago — had recommended, before his own death, *the Blackwood workshop, whose work I have seen and can vouch for.* Passage would be arranged. Terms enclosed. An answer requested within the month.

She read it four times at the bench, the lamplight trembling, and felt the mask press against her face from the inside like a living thing.

Japan. The other side of the world, where no one had ever seen her, where the legend of Blackwood & Son would arrive ahead of her as pure paper — a letter, a recommendation, a name. Where she could be, completely, the person the documents said she was. It should have been freedom. It was freedom, of a kind, and she understood with terrible clarity that it was also the same trap, rebuilt larger: she would land in a harbor town full of strangers, carrying a forged identity into a place where discovery wouldn't mean scandal and a moved shopfront. It would mean something she didn't have words for yet. Prisons, perhaps. Or ships home. Or worse, in a country she didn't understand, with laws she couldn't read.

She should refuse. Any sensible person would refuse.

She looked around the shop instead — at her father's tools hanging in their painted outlines, at the regulator ticking its faithful bronze hours, at the green ledger lying open on the desk with thirty years of names in it, everyone the town had ever trusted with its time. She thought about how a clock, once stopped, is very hard to persuade to start again, and how a life is the same.

On the last blank page of the green ledger, she wrote the entry in her best imitation of her father's hand, though by now the imitation had drifted into a hand that was entirely her own, and she knew it, and wrote it anyway:

*April 3rd. O. Blackwood accepts engagement abroad, Nagasaki observatory, master clock repair. Duration unknown. Shop closed, tools conveyed with apprentice. All accounts settled.*

All accounts settled. She blotted the page and sat looking at it for a long time, because some part of her understood that she was not closing an account at all. She was opening one, in a currency she couldn't yet count, and the debt would come due in a harbor on the far side of the world, among strangers, under a name that was a loan she could never repay.

She packed her father's tools that night, wrapped the green ledger in oilcloth, and set the regulator going one last time before she locked the door — not because it needed winding, but because she could not bear to leave it stopped.

Six weeks later, watching Nagasaki rise out of the sea haze — green hills, red roofs, a forest of masts, and somewhere above it all a tower with a clock that had stopped — she touched the forged letter of apprenticeship inside her coat and made herself a promise she did not fully believe: that this time would be different. That this time she would find a way to be, if not herself, then at least honest about the borrowing.

The harbor whistle blew as the ship came in — a long, mournful note that rolled across the water and shook something loose in her chest. On the quay, a crowd waited: dockhands, customs men, and a small gray clerk holding an umbrella against a sun that hadn't quite decided, who stepped forward as she descended the gangway with her father's tool case in her hand.

"Mr. Blackwood?" the clerk called. "Welcome to Nagasaki. I am Ito, harbormaster's office. We have been keeping your time for you — such as it is." He smiled apologetically. "I'm afraid you'll find it has been running badly indeed."
