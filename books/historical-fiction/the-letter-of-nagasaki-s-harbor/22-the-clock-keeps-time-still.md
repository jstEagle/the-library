## 22. The Clock Keeps Time Still

The key was where it had always been, which is to say nowhere and everywhere at once — in the drawer that stuck, behind the tin of spare pallets, wrapped in the same oilcloth I had used twenty-two years ago when the tower smelled of gun oil and fear instead of beeswax and patience.

I climbed the ninety-one steps the way I always did now, slowly, one hand on the rail Sanae's father had installed after the war, when he decided that if the town was going to keep its clockmaker she ought not break her neck keeping it. The stairwell still bore the scorch mark from the lamp I'd knocked over during the inspection, the night the auditor came and I hid the copied pages inside the case of the going train. The carpenters had offered to plane it out. I told them no. A tower should remember what happened in it.

At the top, the master clock stood where it had always stood, taller than me by half again, its walnut case dark with decades of hands. The pendulum swung in its long slow certainty — tick, and tick, and tick — the sound I had once thought of as the town's heartbeat and now understood to be something humbler and stranger: the town's agreement with itself. Whistles, tides, shifts, school bells, funerals. All of it hung off this one escapement, off this one old woman's stubborn wheelwork, and none of it would have noticed if we stopped. That was the joke I finally understood about timekeeping. You are never keeping time for the people who notice you. You are keeping it for everyone who doesn't.

I opened the case.

Inside, the movement gleamed. I had rebuilt it twice since the war — once in '49 when the salt air got into the pivots, once in '58 when the whole train had to come out for re-bushing — but the heart of it was still my father's design, still his wheels, his escape lever, the counterweight hook he'd filed himself in a workshop in another country before either of us had ever heard the word Nagasaki. My father, whose name I had worn like a borrowed coat for eleven years. Oscar Blackwood, apprentice clockmaker, dead man walking through a living port.

Oscar Blackwood, clockmaker, alive and sixty-one years old, took up the winding crank.

* * *

Winding is a liturgy. Four turns on the great barrel, feeling the spring take tension through the crank like a muscle flexing; two turns on the strike weight; a check of the oil in the bearings, though I had done it Tuesday and it was fine and I checked it anyway because checking is half the trade. Through the louvred windows the harbor lay below me in the morning light, and I did what I always did, which was read it like a page.

The new breakwater, gray concrete where the old timber had been. The container cranes standing over the pier like patient herons, lifting boxes my father could never have imagined, full of things nobody needed a harbor ledger to record anymore. The ferry terminal with its digital board clicking through departures in three languages. And beyond it all, the sea, unchanged, indifferent, keeping its own accounts in water.

They had asked me, a few years back, if I wanted the clock electrified. A committee. There were sandwiches. A young man from the prefecture explained, kindly, as to someone elderly, that a synchronized system would be more accurate, more efficient, cheaper to maintain. I let him finish. Then I opened the case and showed him the pendulum and said: this has not lost more than four seconds in any year since 1945, including the year it was shot at. He looked at the movement the way people look at things they have been told are obsolete and discovered are alive. The committee kept the sandwiches and dropped the motion.

Accuracy was never really the argument, of course. It never is. What they wanted was a clock nobody had to know. What they got was a clock everybody knew, because a machine you must climb ninety-one steps to wind is a machine somebody loves, and love is the only synchronization that holds.

I finished the winding. I closed the case. And then, because it was the first of November, I went down the narrow stair to the gear room — my old room, my lamplight room, the place where I had sat night after night copying names by a light hidden behind a brass reflector so the soldiers on the quay would see nothing but shadow — and I unlocked the flat iron box that lived there under the floorboard.

The ledger.

Not the original. The original had gone into the hold of a neutral mail steamer in the autumn of 1945, sewn into a mail sack by a trembling girl who was not yet sure she existed, and from the steamer into the consul's vault, and from the vault into the national archive, where it sits to this day in an acid-free folder with a catalog number, available to anyone who asks and read by almost no one. That was the copy of the truth that survived the fire of official history — the quiet contradiction, Sanae called it, the stone in the shoe of every tidy account.

But a ledger is not only paper. A ledger is a practice. And so in 1946, when the port reopened and the town began, haltingly, to keep itself again, Ito-san had started a second book. *Continuation of the Harbor Register,* the cover said, in his careful hand. Same columns. Same rules. Foreign residents, ship arrivals, shelter given, debts owed and forgiven, births, deaths, the small mercies and small crimes of a working harbor. He kept it until his hands shook too badly to write, and then I kept it, and now Sanae's daughter kept it, and every entry in it was true, or as true as human beings can make a thing, which is to say: argued over, corrected in margins, occasionally wrong and always trying.

It was the only ledger in Nagasaki that had never been requisitioned, because no army had ever thought to want it, and that, Ito-san told me near the end, laughing from his sickbed, was the whole secret of preservation: be uninteresting. History devours the important documents first. It chokes on the ordinary ones.

I carried the continuation ledger up the ninety-one steps, set it on the little shelf beside the clock case, and opened it to the current page.

* * *

There is a discipline to writing in a register, and Ito taught it to me the winter before he died. Ink, never pencil. Full names, never initials — *a person is not an abbreviation.* Date the entry even if nothing else is certain. And write while the knowing is fresh, because memory is a bad clerk: it falsifies gently, without meaning to, and a gentle falsehood is harder to catch than a bold one.

I dipped the pen. Below me the harbor went on with its morning — a tug backing off the breakwater, gulls arguing over something at the fish market, the ferry horn sounding the ten o'clock, which sounded because somewhere under all the electronics and synchronization the old relay still listened for my clock's chime, a courtesy the engineers had never gotten around to removing and I had never reminded them of.

I wrote the date. I wrote the weather, because Ito had always written the weather — *harbor fog till nine, then clear* — insisting that a record without weather was a skeleton without flesh. I wrote the tide. And then I sat for a long moment with the pen above the line, the way I had sat above this same kind of line once before, in a different life, when the consul's clerk had told me that a deposit required standing, required a name, required testimony, required risk — and I had understood that the trap of anonymity closes from the inside.

That day I had chosen my father's name, because it was the only door open to me, and I had paid for it, and others had paid for it — Ito with his career, Hayashi with everything, Sanae with a night in a cell that she spoke of afterward only as *the cold hotel*. I had spent twenty-two years as the keeper of other people's entries. Names in, names preserved, names read aloud on the wharf every first of November while the freed townsfolk stood in the wind and Sanae's voice carried down the water. But my own name — the true one, the whole one — I had kept off the page. A clockmaker can hide inside her work. The work is public; the maker needn't be. I had told myself for two decades that this was humility. It was not. It was the same fear, grown older, wearing better clothes.

She stopped believing history happens to other people — that was the sentence Sanae had said to me once, years ago, teasing, after I'd given some speech at the wharf about memory and witness. *You talk like a historian, Oscar. When did you stop thinking history was something that happened to other people?* And I had laughed, and gone home, and lain awake until three in the morning hearing it.

I put the pen to the line.

*Blackwood, Oscar Margaret, clockmaker, resident of the harbor district since 1945. Entered into this register by her own hand, this first day of November, being the anniversary of the reading of the names. Occupation: repairer and keeper of the harbor master clock. Services rendered to this community: recorded elsewhere, and sufficient.*

Then, because Ito's ghost was looking over my shoulder and would have insisted, I added:

*Character of residence: permanent.*

I blotted the page. My hand was steady. Ninety-one steps and sixty-one years, and the hand was steady.

* * *

Sanae found me at noon, as she always did on the first of November, carrying two thermoses and the particular expression she wore when she had already guessed what I'd done and was waiting to see whether I'd admit it.

"You've been crying," she said.

"I've been winding the clock."

"You've been crying *and* winding the clock." She poured the tea onto the gear-room table, between the spare pallets and the tin that had held my father's letter of apprenticeship, forged in another country by a dying man's hope, now kept empty as a relic. "Show me."

I turned the ledger toward her. She read the entry twice. She was seventy now, Sanae, white-haired and straight-backed, with her father's whistle-lungs and her mother's talent for silence, and when she finished reading she did not say anything kind, because we had long ago agreed that kindness between us was assumed and therefore wasted breath.

"Permanent," she said.

"Permanent."

"It took you twenty-two years to write six words."

"Some entries," I said, "require the ink to age."

She snorted, and drank her tea, and looked out the gear-room window at the harbor she had grown up listening to. Down on the wharf, people were gathering already — early ones, the faithful, the families of the named, children too young to understand dragging elders who remembered everything. At three o'clock she would stand where she always stood and read the names aloud, all of them, from both ledgers, the rescued and the continued, the smugglers and the shelterers and the castaways and the clerks, the whole unruly population of a harbor that had once been ordered erased and had declined. Her granddaughter would hold the book for her this year. The voice would pass down, the way the winding passed to me, the way the register passed to whoever loved the town enough to write it honestly.

"Hayashi's students sent a card," Sanae said. "For today. They're publishing his papers next spring. The university gave them a proper archive." She smiled slightly. "An honest one, they claim. We'll audit."

"He'd have hated the ceremony."

"He'd have secretly loved it and publicly complained for a decade." She stood, brushing off her skirt. "Come down at three. Read your own name with the rest of us. It's on the list now — I saw to it last week."

I looked at her. "You put me on the list?"

"I put you on the list in 1961," she said. "You're the slowest entry in the whole register, Oscar Blackwood. But you're in it." She paused at the stair, and for a moment she was seventeen again, furious and frightened on a pier with stolen pages in her coat. "History isn't finished," she said. "It never is. That's the point. That's why we keep climbing these stairs."

She went down ahead of me, her footsteps quick on the treads her father had built.

I stayed a moment longer. The pendulum swung. The escapement released its tooth, and caught the next, and released it — the oldest promise in the world, kept and kept and kept. Somewhere below, the town ran on its shared time, and the harbor wrote its ordinary entries, and the sea kept its own counsel, and the consul's stone-in-the-shoe slept in its acid-free folder waiting for whoever might someday need it.

I wound the strike train one extra turn — for luck, for Ito, for my father, for the girl with the forged letter who climbed these stairs believing she was borrowing a life — and I left the tower unlocked, as I always did, because a clock that keeps a town's time should never be the property of one pair of hands.

Then I went down the ninety-one steps, out into the clear afternoon, toward the wharf, toward the voices, toward the reading of the names —

— where, at the edge of the crowd, a young woman stood holding a battered suitcase and a letter of introduction, watching the clock tower the way I had once watched it, a stranger measuring herself against a community that runs on shared time, and I recognized the look, and I started toward her.
