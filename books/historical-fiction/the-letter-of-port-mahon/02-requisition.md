## 2. Requisition

The notices went up on a Tuesday, in the hour before dawn, when the town belonged to gulls and milk carts and no one else.

Rosalind knew this because she was awake for it — she was always awake for it, her body tuned to the observatory clock as a tuning fork is tuned to its note. The great regulator in the tower above her struck four, and she lay in her narrow bed under the eaves of the harbor master's row listening to the sound travel down through stone and timber into her chest, and then she heard, beneath it, the softer rhythm of boots. Not one pair. Many pairs, moving in step, moving with purpose, fanning out along the quay the way water fans when a dam breaks.

She did not go to the window at once. That was the first lesson her father had ever taught her, though he had taught it without words: *when something changes, count first.* Count the bells, count the seconds between them, count the footsteps. A clockmaker who flung herself at every irregularity would never find what was truly broken. So Rosalind lay still under her blanket and counted, and by the time she reached two hundred the boots had stopped, and there came instead the small wet slap of paste brushes against brick, over and over, up and down the harbor quarter, like the town being licked by some enormous patient animal.

Only then did she rise and put her face to the frosted glass.

There were six of them visible from her window, red coats gone almost black in the blue pre-dawn, working their way along the fish market wall. One held a lantern; one carried a stack of broadsheets under his arm; two brushed paste; two smoothed the paper flat with long wooden paddles, working from the center outward the way she herself smoothed gold leaf onto a clock face. Even half-asleep, even afraid — though she did not yet know she was afraid, that knowledge arriving later like a delayed bell — Rosalind noticed the technique. Soldiers drilled in everything, even in the pasting of notices. Somewhere a sergeant had taught them the center-outward stroke, and somewhere an officer had decided the notices must be legible, and somewhere above that officer someone had signed an order, and so on upward, rung upon rung, until the whole weight of it came down at four o'clock on a Tuesday morning on a fishing wall in Port Mahon, and a girl of nineteen watched through glass while the machinery of the world began, with terrible patience, to turn toward her.

She could not read the notices from her window. She dressed anyway — wool stockings, the gray dress, her father's old coat with the sleeves turned twice at the cuff — and she went down the stairs as quietly as she could, because Mrs. Aldersey slept behind the ground-floor door and woke like a cat.

* * *

Mrs. Aldersey was not asleep.

Rosalind knew it the moment she reached the bottom stair, because the smell reached her first: tallow candle and burning paper. The landlady's parlor door stood open a hand's width, and firelight shivered in the gap, and inside, hunched over the hearth like a woman warming her hands at a funeral, sat Mrs. Aldersey feeding letters into the flames.

They were good letters. Rosalind had seen them once, years ago, when she'd been sent down to fetch the spare key: a bundle tied in ribbon, correspondence from a sister in Falmouth, from a husband long drowned, from a son who had gone to sea at fourteen and written home every port until, one year, he hadn't. Mrs. Aldersey had never spoken of the son. But she kept his letters the way Rosalind kept her father's last watch — wound, polished, touched but never opened where anyone could see.

"Mrs. Aldersey." Rosalind stood in the doorway. "It's four in the morning."

"I know what time it is." The old woman did not look around. Her voice had the flatness of a struck bell after the ring has died. "I have known what time it is for fifty years, girl, before your clocks were ever built. I know it in my knees." She fed another letter to the fire. Rosalind saw the handwriting flare — a strong slanted hand, ink going brown at the folds with age — and then it was light, and then it was nothing.

"What are they saying? The notices."

"Now that's the question, isn't it." Mrs. Aldersey wiped her hands on her apron, though they were clean; it was what her hands did instead of shaking. "I couldn't read them from my window either. But I know why men post papers in the dark, Rosalind Nakamura." She used the full name rarely, and always like this — carefully, as if setting down something fragile on a high shelf. "They post them in the dark because they don't want anyone reading them until it's too late to do anything but obey."

"They're requisitioning." Rosalind said it before she had fully thought it, the word assembling itself out of the night's evidence: the boots, the paste, the landlady burning forty years of love in her own hearth. "The harbor quarter. For winter works."

Mrs. Aldersey finally turned. In the firelight her face looked carved, all planes and shadow, and her eyes were wet but furious, which was worse than weeping. "My sister's letters," she said. "Forty years of letters. You burn a letter, you can't unburn it. There's no clerk in London who can give me back my sister's voice." She looked back at the fire. "But I'll not have soldiers reading how I loved people. That's mine. That stays mine."

Outside, the paste brush slapped against the wall of the chandlery next door. Slap, smooth, smooth. The machinery turning.

"Get your coat properly on," Mrs. Aldersey said. "And take your father's tools out of the shop and keep them on your person. Whatever those papers say, the first thing they'll want is lists. Lists of premises. Lists of goods. Lists of names." She said the last word looking directly at Rosalind, and held the look a moment too long, and Rosalind felt the first true cold of the morning move through her, because Mrs. Aldersey knew. Of course she knew. She had rented the room above the clockshop to a dead man's daughter for three years and had asked, in all that time, exactly one question — *can you pay?* — and had never asked the second question whose answer hung off the first like a pendulum.

"I'm entered in the ledger," Rosalind said. It came out steady. She was proud of that, later, and ashamed of it too.

"Mm," said Mrs. Aldersey, and fed another letter to the fire, and said nothing else at all.

* * *

By seven the harbor quarter had gathered in front of the fish market to read what had been done to it in the dark.

The notice was nailed now as well as pasted — someone had thought of the wind off the river — and it was headed with the royal arms and dated from the office of the commanding engineer, and it ran to four hundred words of the most beautiful, most terrible prose Rosalind had ever read, though she did not appreciate its beauty until much later, standing before it a third time, committing it to memory the way she memorized gear trains.

*Whereas it has been represented to Us that the harbor and approaches of the town of Port Mahon are insufficient to the defense and provisioning of Our forces in the coming season; and whereas certain persons have taken up residence within the said harbor quarter without regular title, tenure, or entry of record, to the prejudice of good order...*

*Iregular settlement.* Four words, and inside them, folded like a knife in a cloth, every family on the waterfront who had ever paid rent in coin rather than parchment, every widow who had inherited a mooring from a husband whose papers had gone down with him, every sailor ashore, every foreigner, every child born between censuses. The notice went on to announce that the harbor master's ledger — *the register of residency and moorage for the said quarter* — was confiscated forthwith to the office of the requisitioning commissioner, there to be examined as evidence, and that all persons claiming lawful residence should present themselves to make formal claim upon proof of entry of record.

Proof of entry of record. Rosalind read that phrase five times. Around her the crowd read it too, or had it read to them, and the sound the crowd made was very quiet — not a roar, nothing so useful as a roar, but a low shifting murmur, the sound of many people each privately doing arithmetic and each disliking the sum.

Old Ferren the netmender spat into the cobbles. "Entry of record. Half the boats on this quay are recorded in that ledger under names me grandfather made up because the tax man couldn't spell the real ones."

"Then you'll be wanting to make your claim," said the fishwife beside him, sharp with fear dressed as scorn.

"With what proof? The ledger's took. They've took the book and told us to prove what's wrote in it. That's like—" Ferren groped for it, hands knotting in his net. "That's like locking the church and telling us to pray louder."

Rosalind did not laugh. Something in her chest had gone very still and very cold, the way the escapement goes still when a tooth catches wrong — everything stopped, everything waiting, the whole mechanism of the morning suspended on a single point of failure.

Because she had seen the ledger. She had seen it a hundred times, had stood beside her father's stool while he made his weekly entry, moorage fees, wharfage, the names of the quarter entered in his careful upright hand. And she remembered — she had remembered it idly a thousand times and never once let the memory become a thought — that the entries ran in columns, and that her father's own line read *Nakamura, J., clockmaker and adjustor of marine chronometers, premises at the sign of the Escapement,* and that below it, where other fathers' lines continued with *and wife* or *and children,* her father's line simply ended. Full stop. Ink dry. As if the shop had no one in it but the clocks.

He had registered himself and not her. She had never asked why. There had been a year — she was fourteen — when he had grown careful about documents, about names, about the shuttered window facing the customs house; a year when he had begun keeping the shop books in two hands, one for the guild and one, smaller and finer, for himself. She had understood without understanding, the way children understand weather. And then he had died in the spring of '43, between one tide and the next, and she had buried him and shortened the coat sleeves and become, to all the town, *the clockmaker's apprentice at the sign of the Escapement* — a fiction so old and so well-oiled that it had run for three years without her once hearing its tick.

Now the army had come to wind that particular spring.

"Girl." Ferren was looking at her. Several of them were looking at her; she realized she had been standing before the notice longer than anyone, lips moving faintly, and that a nineteen-year-old apprentice who read the King's English fluently was itself a kind of spectacle. "What's it say, the middle part? Tam Ossler's asking. His eyes are shot."

She read it aloud for them, evenly, in the voice she used for explaining a repair to a customer — level, precise, stripped of anything that might be mistaken for opinion. *Confiscated forthwith. Examined as evidence. Formal claim upon proof of entry of record. All persons unable to establish such entry shall be deemed irregularly settled and removed from the said quarter as the exigencies of the service require.*

"As the exigencies of the service require," repeated Tam Ossler slowly, tasting it. He was seventy if he was a day, mended nets on the third step of the salt house with his hands wrapped in tarred twine, and had a face like driftwood. "That's a fine long way of saying *out.*"

"It's a fine long way of saying a lot of things," said the fishwife.

Rosalind stepped back from the notice. The crowd closed into the space she left, pressing toward the paper as if warmth came off it. She walked back along the quay toward the shop with her father's tool roll heavy in her coat pocket, and the morning came up gray-gold over the river, and the ice at the edges of the current — early this year, everyone had remarked on it, early and thin — caught the light and broke and caught it again.

Early ice. She noted it the way she noted everything, automatically, a datum filed: *ice forming upstream earlier than any year since '39.* She did not yet know what the datum would grow into. Data never does, at first. It only waits.

* * *

The shop was cold. She lit the stove, swept the floor she had swept ten thousand times, uncovered the movement on the bench — a tower-clock remontoire she was rebuilding for the guild, her finest work, the work that had begun to make people forget to call her *the boy* — and tried to lose herself in it, and could not.

Her hands wanted work. Her hands always wanted work; it was the family inheritance, more surely than the tools. When her father had not wished to speak, he had polished. When grief had come to the shop, as it came to every shop on a waterfront, he had cleaned pivots. Rosalind had learned early that feeling was something the hands could process so the mind need not, and so she sat down and picked up the remontoire and began to work, and for perhaps twenty minutes the world contracted to the width of a pivot and the song of the file, and she was safe.

Then the knock came.

Two knocks, official spacing. She knew before opening it. Everyone in the quarter knew the spacing by noon; it traveled faster than the notices had.

The officer on her step was young — younger than she was, or hardly older — with a red coat too new and a face trying to arrange itself into authority. Behind him a private shifted from foot to foot with a leather-bound book under his arm, and the book was the size of a Bible and the color of dried blood, and Rosalind's stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world.

"Miss." The officer consulted a paper. "Premises known as the sign of the Escapement, clockmaker's shop, harbor quarter. Registered occupant..." He paused. Something flickered across his face — the small human confusion of a man encountering a name his tongue had not been drilled for. "Nakamura. J. Nakamura."

"My father," Rosalind said. "Dead these three years. I'm his apprentice. I hold the shop under the guild."

"Under the guild." He wrote it. His pencil scratched; she hated how loud it was. "Name?"

"Eastwick." The lie came out worn smooth as a bearing, three years of turning in its race. "Rosalind Eastwick."

"And you reside on the premises."

"I reside above the premises. My landlady is Mrs. Aldersey, of the harbor row."

More scratching. Then the young officer looked up, and for a moment his eyes were not official at all — they were tired, and apologetic, and frightened in a way he clearly believed himself alone in being. "I'm to inform all registered occupants that the residency ledger has been removed to the commissioner's office. Claims of residence are to be presented in writing within fourteen days, supported by entry of record. After that period—" He stopped. He looked at his own paper as if the next words were printed there and he disliked the print. "After that period, the quarter passes to the service entire. I'm sorry. I'm required to say it exactly."

"You've said it exactly," Rosalind said.

He hesitated on the step. She watched him decide not to say whatever it was he wanted to say — watched the decision happen behind his eyes like a lever thrown — and then he touched his hat, and the boots moved on down the row, and the private's blood-colored book swung at his side, and Rosalind stood in her doorway in the cold and listened to the machinery recede.

Fourteen days. Entry of record. Her father's line, ending in a full stop.

She went inside and did not touch the remontoire again. She sat at the bench with her hands flat on the wood, feeling the grain, feeling the shop hold its breath around her, and she made herself think it plainly, without ornament, the way she would write out a fault report:

*The ledger is gone. My name was never in it. In fourteen days, the law of this town will look for me and find no one.*

Somewhere across the roofs the observatory clock struck nine, unhurried, indifferent, keeping perfect time over a town that had just been told its time was running out. Rosalind listened to all nine strokes, counting as her father had taught her, and when the last one faded she stood up, and rolled her father's tools tighter in their leather, and began — with the same center-outward care the soldiers had used on their paste — to plan.
