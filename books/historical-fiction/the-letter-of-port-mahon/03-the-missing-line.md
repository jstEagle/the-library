## 3. The Missing Line

The clerk's office smelled of tallow and damp vellum, a smell Rosalind had come to think of as the smell of power — the particular must of paper that decided things. She had passed the door of the harbor registry a hundred times in her life, delivering her father's invoices, fetching stamped copies of the shop's license, and she had never once thought of the building as anything but a kind of clock itself: slow, indifferent, turning out its small increments of yes and no. Now the door was barred with a soldier's notice, and she stood across the street in the grey light of a Tuesday morning, watching a man she had bribed before breakfast through the crack of a shuttered window.

His name was Pell. He was a thin, stooped creature of perhaps fifty, with ink stains to the second knuckle of his right hand and the watery, apologetic eyes of a man who had spent his whole life writing down what other people did. She had found him three days ago, after two evenings of watching the registry's back door, and she had found him the way her father had taught her to find anyone: by learning what he lacked.

Pell lacked money — that was the obvious thing, the thing every clerk lacked, because the Crown paid its copyists in the coin of their own insignificance. But money alone would have been crude, and crude frightened men like Pell. What he lacked more deeply, she had seen in the way he lingered at the fish market, was the small dignity of being consulted. Of being asked. So she had asked him — asked him about the old registers, the pre-requisition copies, the duplicates struck before the army's men came with their canvas satchels and their wax seals — and she had watched his chest expand with the pleasure of being the man who knew. Only then had she mentioned, delicately, as one mentions weather, that she could make it worth his while.

Now she waited across the street while he worked, and she counted the bells of the observatory to keep her hands from trembling. Nine. Ten. The town's time ran through her fingers like everyone else's, but she was the only one in Port Mahon who knew that the ten o'clock bell had been ringing four seconds late since the frost came into the tower's east wall, and that she had not yet been permitted to go and correct it, because the observatory's commission — like everything else — was under review.

*Under review.* The army's phrase for the world. Her shop, her father's shop, was under review. The harbor quarter was under review. The residency of every family between the salt house and the customs stairs was under review, which was a way of saying that it might not exist, that it might never have existed, that the whole quarter could be folded up like a chart and carried away.

The back door of the registry opened. Pell came out with his coat collar up and a ledger — a thin one, a duplicate — folded flat against his ribs like a stolen loaf. He did not look at her. He walked north, past the chandler's, past the cooper's yard, and turned down the alley behind the ropewalk, exactly as she had told him to, because a man walking somewhere with purpose attracted no attention, while a man standing still attracted every eye.

She followed at a distance, the way her father had taught her to follow a gear-train: not looking at the tooth in front of you but at the space it was about to occupy.

* * *

The ropewalk's alley ended at a disused tar shed that still held the ghost of its trade in every plank. Pell was waiting inside with the ledger open on a barrel head, and he had lit no lamp, which she appreciated — light was a debt, and they were both already in debt enough.

"Duplicate register," he said, without greeting. His voice had the flatness of a man reciting from habit. "Struck in '43, when old Marlowe was harbor master. Marlowe kept duplicates of everything. The army took the originals Monday. This one was in the loft with the mooring charts." He pushed it toward her across the barrel. "I never touched it. You understand. It was already here."

"Of course," Rosalind said, though they both understood the fiction for what it was, and both understood that the fiction was part of what she was paying for.

"Page forty," Pell said. "The harbor quarter, commercial registrations, M through R." He hesitated, and in the hesitation she saw something she had not expected: pity. It moved across his face like a cloud across a clock face, briefly shadowing the numbers, and then it was gone, and she understood that he had already looked. That he had known, before he came, what she would find, or at least what he feared she would find. "I'll wait outside," he said, and left her alone with the book.

She did not turn to page forty at once. She stood in the tar-dark and let her hands do what her hands did when her mind could not be trusted with itself: she took out her loupe, her father's loupe, brass and glass worn smooth at the rim by thirty years of his thumb, and she polished it on her sleeve, and she screwed it into her eye socket, though there was nothing yet to magnify. The ritual steadied her. *Hands first,* her father used to say. *The hands know the way before the head does. That is why God gave apprentices fingers.*

Then she opened the ledger.

The duplicate was a fair copy, written in Marlowe's own round hand, and the pages smelled faintly of the loft, of pigeon dust and old rope. She found page forty and ran her finger down the column of names — *Mancini, boatwright; Marchetti, sailmaker; Osei, chandler; Peralta, cooper* — each entry with its date of registration, its witnesses, its little world of proof. Her finger moved with the steadiness she used for setting jewels in clock plates, and her heart moved with none of that steadiness at all, hammering somewhere up under her jaw.

*Nakamura, Ichiro. Clockmaker and repairer of instruments. Registered 1731. Premises: Tower Lane, the third house from the observatory wall. Witnessed: Marlowe, harbor master; Voss, T., mariner.*

Her father's name. Her father's shop. The line was there, whole and beautiful, in ink that had dried before she was old enough to hold a file. She read it three times. She read the witnesses twice — Tam Voss, she thought; old Tam Voss, who used to bring her father brandy and sea-stories and once, memorably, a quadrant with a cracked arc that her father had repaired for free because Tam had wept over it, and she had been seven and had thought grown men did not weep — and she felt the strange comfort of seeing her father's life fixed in the record, certified, witnessed, real.

Then she looked for her own name, and it was not there.

She looked again. She went through the line character by character, the loupe making each letter large as a coin. *Nakamura, Ichiro.* No second entry. No *Rosalind, daughter, residing above the shop.* No *apprentice.* Nothing. The line ended with her father's name and a small ink flourish, and beneath it began the next entry, a rope-maker named Quist, and the space between the two names was blank paper, and the blank paper was her.

She had known. That was the terrible thing — she had known since the soldiers posted the notices, since the word *irregular* began to circulate through the quarter like a fever, that her father had been careless in one particular way, the way of a man who thought he had all the time in the world. He had registered the shop. He had registered his tools, his apprenticeship papers from Amsterdam, his letters of guild standing. He had registered everything that was his. But he had never, in fourteen years of living above that shop, in fourteen years of her answering the door and winding the regulators and cutting her teeth — literally, her first tooth-marked file was still in the third drawer — on the trade, gone down to the registry and said: *and there is a girl. There is a daughter. Write her down.*

She pressed her palms flat against the barrel head and breathed the tar smell until the hammering under her jaw slowed.

There were reasons. She knew the reasons the way she knew the escapement's geometry, intimately, from the inside. A foreign name on a registry drew eyes. A Japanese name drew more. Her father had spent his whole life in the space between being seen and being overlooked, and he had made a craftsman's calculation: the shop was the legal thing, the shop was the shield, and the girl who lived behind the shop was covered by the shop the way a movement is covered by its case. He had meant to formalize it. She remembered him saying so, once, when she was twelve — *when you're fourteen we'll enter you properly, an apprenticeship is a fine thing to have written down* — and then the winter had come that took him, quick as a snapped mainspring, and the entry had gone unwritten, and the unwritten thing had waited fourteen years in the blank paper like a flaw in a wheel, invisible, until the day the whole mechanism was seized and turned over and examined by men looking for exactly such flaws.

"Miss Nakamura." Pell's voice, low, from the doorway. "Someone's coming. The sergeant does his rounds at half past, and I must be at my desk when he does."

She closed the ledger. Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady; it was the rest of her that had come apart somewhere in the tar-dark, quietly, the way a clock comes apart when a single screw is missing — no drama, no noise, just the slow cessation of everything working.

"How much?" Pell said. "You said you'd—"

She paid him. She had counted the coins out that morning, wrapped in a scrap of chamois, and she put them in his hand without looking at his face, because she did not want to see the pity again. Pity was worse than contempt. Contempt you could fight.

"Miss." He had not moved. "There's a way, you know. There's always a way. A petition to the commission. A witness — Voss, the mariner, he witnessed the entry, he could swear to your residence. Fourteen years, the whole street would swear to it. The law isn't—"

"The law," Rosalind said, and heard her own voice come out flat and precise, her father's voice, the voice he used for quoting prices to customers who tried to haggle, "is a ledger, Mr. Pell. I have just spent a quarter hour reading it. It does not swear. It does not remember. It does not know that I exist." She wrapped her loupe away in its chamois. "You cannot appeal to a page that has no line for you. You can only ask it to add one. And the men who hold the pen now—" she nodded toward the registry, toward the canvas satchels and wax seals, "—are not adding lines. They are striking them out."

Pell stood in the doorway with the coins in his fist, and for a moment he looked like he wanted to say something else — something about his own name, perhaps, and where it was written, and what held him to his desk at half past every morning — but the sergeant's boots were audible somewhere up the alley, or perhaps they were only in his head, and he went.

* * *

She did not go home. Home was Tower Lane, the third house from the observatory wall, and home was under review, and she could not bear the sight of the notice nailed to her own door with the Crown's stamp bleeding slightly in the damp, the way it had bled on every door in the quarter, a red flower blooming on each household like a slow epidemic. Instead she walked. She walked the harbor quarter the way a watchmaker walks around a movement he cannot yet open, looking at it from every side, learning where the strain had entered the works.

The quarter was quieter than she had ever known it. Not empty — not yet — but quiet in the way of a room where someone has just said the unsayable thing. The fishwives at the market were not shouting prices; they were standing in knots, talking low, and the knots parted and reformed around her as she passed, and she caught fragments: *the salt house, they say the army will let the salt house stand* — *my mother was born on that stair, born, and they say she's irregular* — *the Voss man is taking people in, old Tam, down by the*— and then the knot closed, because she was the clockmaker's daughter, and the clockmaker's daughter was half of her, and everyone in the quarter knew which half, and no one in the quarter had ever minded, which was the whole of what she had, and the whole of what she was about to lose.

At the customs stairs she stopped. The harbor lay grey and ridged under a sky the color of pewter, and out at the mouth of the river the water was already wearing the dull, leaden look it got before the ice. Early this year. Everyone said it. The fishermen said it, and the fishermen were never wrong about water.

She thought: *I have lived here fourteen years. I have kept the master clock that keeps this town's time. Every ship in that harbor has come in on hours I have certified with my own hands. I have repaired the alarum that wakes the pilots, the chronometers the captains trust their lives to, the church clock that tells the fishermen when the tide turns. The town runs on my father's work and my work, on gears I have filed and pivots I have polished, and there is not one line of me in the record of it.*

The thought did not make her weep. She found, examining herself with the same cold attention she gave a broken movement, that it made her something else — something harder and more useful. It made her precise. Fear, she discovered, could be filed into shape, the way a rough casting is filed into a working part. Her father's name was on page forty, and hers was not, and no petition would change that, because petitions were read by men who trusted paper over people, and the paper said she was a blank.

But paper could be made. Paper was made by hands. She had hands that could engrave a clock face so fine that the numerals held their edges for fifty years; hands that could cut a escape-wheel tooth within a hair of true; hands that had spent fourteen years learning the one law her father had left her that no requisition could touch — *that anything made carefully enough becomes true.*

She turned from the water. Down the harbor road, past the cooper's yard, she had heard the knots of women say it twice now, and the third time she had heard it she had let herself listen properly: *the Voss man is taking people in.*

Old Tam Voss, who had wept over a quadrant. Who had witnessed her father's line on page forty with his own hand. Who owed her father — or her father owed him; with Tam it had never been clear, and perhaps it did not matter — a debt fourteen years old and gathering interest.

The river would freeze early. The ledger had no line for her. The army had the originals, the seals, the soldiers, the pen.

Rosalind Nakamura turned her collar up against the pewter wind and started walking down the harbor road toward the salt house, and in her pocket her fingers found her father's loupe and closed around it, and the closing was a decision, though she would not have called it one. The hands knew the way before the head did.

She was going to ask old Tam Voss what a man did when the law's book had no page for him — and she was going to find out what else, besides clock faces, could be engraved by hands as careful as hers.
