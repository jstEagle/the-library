## 4. The Salt House

The salt warehouse stood at the harbor's edge like a great sleeping animal, its stone flanks stained white where decades of brine had wept through the mortar. Rosalind had passed it a thousand times on her way to the observatory — the smell of it had been as constant to her childhood as the smell of her father's oil and brass — but she had never once been inside. Salt was the town's oldest trade, older than the garrison, older than the ledger, and the building held that age in its bones: walls three feet thick, windows set high and narrow like the eye-slits of a helmet, a roof of tarred beams that had carried the weight of ten thousand barrels.

Now it carried people instead.

She came at dusk, when the light off the water turned the color of pewter, and even from the far side of the quay she could hear the sound the crowd made — not shouting, nothing so dramatic as that, but the low continuous murmur of forty families learning to live in one room. A child crying in the flat, exhausted way of a child who has cried past the point of comfort. A woman's voice rising and falling in what might have been prayer or might have been a list of things lost. Underneath it all, the sea, patient against the pilings.

Rosalind stopped at the edge of the lamplight spilling from the great doors and made herself look.

They had come with what they could carry. She saw that immediately, the way she could see at a glance whether a clock's weights were hung true. A man with a feather mattress rolled under one arm and nothing else. An old woman seated on a chair she had evidently refused to abandon, sitting in it now in the middle of the packed earth floor as though the chair were an island and the flood of displaced humanity merely weather around her. Children asleep across barrels that still smelled faintly of herring. A family had chalked their name on the wall above their corner — *Baptiste* — the way a ship's crew marks a berth, claiming space by writing on it, because there was no paper left in Port Mahon that would claim them.

Her chest did something complicated and painful. She pressed her lips together and went in.

The heat inside was astonishing. Forty bodies, cook-fires built in iron rings because no one dared risk open flame near the tarred beams, the breath and sweat and grief of a whole quarter of the town compressed into one echoing hall. The smell was salt and woodsmoke and wet wool. Rosalind moved along the wall, keeping to the shadows the way she always did, hands busy without her permission — straightening a fallen lantern, righting a stack of crates someone had begun to build into a partition. Busy hands. Her father had taught her that much before he taught her anything about clocks: *when you cannot speak, work. Work is honest.*

"Mind the third barrel," said a voice near her elbow. "It leaks. Brine gets into everything."

She turned. The speaker was a small boy of perhaps seven, solemn as a churchwarden, pointing at the barrel in question with the gravity of a harbormaster. She nodded to him as seriously as she would have nodded to his elders, and he nodded back, satisfied, and returned to whatever grave business occupied him.

It was then that she saw Tam Voss.

He sat apart from the others, near the eastern wall where the last of the daylight came down through the high window in a single dusty column. He was old — old the way driftwood is old, silvered and hard and shaped entirely by weather. He sat on an upturned crate with his forearms on his knees, watching the room with eyes the color of shallow water over sand, and he was doing precisely what she was doing: seeing everything, saying nothing.

She knew the face. Of course she knew the face. Every child who had grown up around her father's shop had known Tam Voss, though half of what they knew was rumor. He had run cargo up and down this coast for thirty years before the navy made the coast too hot for him, and for twenty years after that he had been the town's most respectable smuggler — respectable in the way that meant everyone knew and no one said, because Tam never took a shilling from a widow and never ran anything worse than brandy, tobacco, and the occasional fugitive from a debt. Her father had traded with him. She remembered — suddenly, completely, the memory arriving whole like a key turning — evenings when a knock came at the shop door after hours, and her father would go out and speak low in the alley, and come back in smelling of pipe smoke, and say nothing, and she would ask nothing, because that was how it was done.

Tam Voss looked up and found her looking at him.

For a long moment neither of them moved. Around them the warehouse murmured and wept and cooked its supper, and between the two of them stretched a silence that was not empty but full — full of a dead man's name, full of an alley and pipe smoke, full of every question Rosalind had trained herself never to ask.

Then Tam patted the crate beside him. Once. Without a word.

She crossed the floor and sat.

For a while they simply watched the room together, and it was, absurdly, the easiest silence Rosalind had kept in months. There was no need to explain herself to this man. That alone felt like setting down a weight she had forgotten she was carrying.

"You've got your father's way of sitting," Tam said at last. His voice was gravel over which much water had run. "Back straight. Hands quiet. Like you're waiting for a mechanism to finish a thought." He didn't turn his head. "Kazuo Nakamura used to sit exactly so. In my boat, once, in a fog thick enough to chew, while the revenue cutter felt along the coast for us. Never said a word the whole four hours. Just listened to the water and knew where we were better than I did."

"He never told me," Rosalind said. Then, because it was true and because something in her was tired of the other thing: "He never told me most things."

"No," Tam agreed. "He wouldn't have." He shifted, and she heard the small dry music of old joints. "Word came round the quarter that you'd been asking after the ledger. Clerk's office. Tuesday."

Rosalind went very still. In the firelight across the hall, a mother fed her child from a tin bowl, spoonful by careful spoonful, and Rosalind watched her do it because she could not watch Tam.

"I'm not asking what you found," Tam said. "I told you. I'm not asking anything. But I'll tell you what I see, since I'm an old man and old men are permitted to talk to themselves." He nodded slowly at the crowded hall, at the chalked names, at the woman on her island of a chair. "I see forty families in a salt house in November. I see redcoats measuring the harbor quarter for walls nobody asked for. And I see a girl who walks like her father and asks questions like her father, in a town that has just decided paper matters more than people." He finally turned his head and looked at her, and his shallow-water eyes were not unkind, but they were not soft either. "Paper can be made, girl. That's all paper is. Made things can be remade."

Her heart knocked once, hard, against her ribs. "I don't know what you mean."

"No," Tam said placidly. "You don't. Nobody does. That's why I'm talking to myself." He rose, unhurried, joints complaining, and picked up a canvas roll from the shadows behind his crate. "There's a loft at the back, over the old curing racks. Dry, and the beam's sound, and nobody bothers it because the ladder looks rotten. It isn't. I checked." He pushed the roll toward her. Blanket, she saw. Wool, army-gray, but clean. "Take it. Take the loft. It's yours as long as the salt house stands."

"Mr. Voss—"

"Tam." He waved the correction away like smoke. "Everyone calls me Tam. Even the revenue men, God rot them, when they weren't shooting at me." He paused, and something moved behind his eyes — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile lived in a face that had mostly forgotten the geography. "Your father pulled me out of the water once. Off the Mola head, in February, when any sensible man would have let the sea keep me. He rowed out in that little skiff of his with the tide against him and hauled me aboard, and do you know what he said? Not one word. Not one. He wrapped me in sailcloth, put a flask in my hand, and rowed me home, and the next morning he was at his bench as if nothing had happened, and I never mentioned it and neither did he, and that was thirty-one years ago." Tam looked at the blanket in her arms rather than at her. "A man keeps accounts like that. Not on paper. Somewhere better."

Rosalind's throat had gone tight. She looked down at the gray wool and found that her hands, those reliable hands, were trembling very slightly, and she despised them for it.

"I can't pay you," she managed.

"Didn't ask you to."

"I mean—" She stopped. Started again, quieter, in the voice she used for admitting a mechanism was beyond repair. "I may be here longer than you'd want. If what I think is true about the ledger, I'm not — I'm not one of the forty families, Mr.— Tam. I'm less than they are. On paper I'm nobody at all."

The words came out smaller than she intended, and once spoken they seemed to fill the whole vast hall, and she hated that too. But Tam only studied her for a moment with those driftwood eyes, and then he did something she had not seen him do: he reached out and laid one broad, scarred hand briefly on top of her head, the way a man blesses a child or a boat before a voyage.

"On paper," he said, "I died in 'nineteen, off Cape Salinas, along with my entire crew and eleven thousand gallons of French brandy. Yet here I sit." He shrugged his great slow shrug. "Paper's a tide, girl. It comes in, it goes out. What matters is what floats."

* * *

She climbed to the loft that night by the light of a stub of candle, testing each rung of the treacherous-looking ladder and finding it sound, exactly as promised. The loft was low and dark and smelled of salt and old timber, and through the gap beneath the eaves she could see a sliver of the harbor: black water, the riding lights of the fishing fleet, and beyond them the tower of the observatory, where the master clock she had tended for six years was striking nine with its familiar unhurried authority.

*Nine o'clock,* said the bell, to a town that was listening. *Nine o'clock,* to the salt house, where no one had needed telling.

She spread the gray blanket on the planks and lay down and did not sleep. Below her, the great room settled by degrees — fires banked, children stilled, the murmur thinning to breathing — until what remained was the sound she had heard from the quay: forty families existing, together, in the dark. Someone coughed. Someone turned over. Far off, a sentry's boots rang on the quay stones, counting his rounds.

And in the middle of the sleepless dark, Rosalind Nakamura — who had spent six years being the clockmaker's apprentice, the late master's quiet girl, the name that wasn't written anywhere — discovered that she was crying. Silently, the way she did everything, face turned to the eaves, hands folded and still because there was no work for them. Not for her father, not exactly, and not for the ledger line that didn't exist. For the blanket. For the rotten ladder that wasn't. For an old smuggler who had said *take it* and *it's yours* and *that was thirty-one years ago*, and who had asked for nothing, explained nothing, required nothing of her but her presence — the first person in this whole grinding catastrophe to treat her as if she were real.

*What matters is what floats.*

She woke once before dawn to voices below — Tam's gravel among them, low and quick, and two or three others she didn't know, speaking in the clipped cadence of people arranging something they did not wish to name. She caught only fragments through the floorboards: *Thursday's tide... the priest will grumble but he'll come... tell Marta we need her arithmetic...*

Then footsteps moving away, and the salt house quiet again, and Rosalind lying in the dark with her heart going fast and her mind — her precise, mechanism-minded mind — already turning the fragments over, fitting them together, finding that they made a shape.

She did not know yet what the shape was. But she knew the sound of machinery beginning to move.
