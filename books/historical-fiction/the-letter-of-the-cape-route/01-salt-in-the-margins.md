## 1. Salt in the Margins

There are forty-one ways to say *lost at sea* in the Halvard Company's ledgers, and I have copied every one of them.

*Vessel unreturned.* *Crew not recovered.* *Cargo and hands written off against insurance.* That last one is my favorite, if a person can be said to have a favorite way of describing a drowned man. *Written off.* As though the sea were a creditor with poor manners, and the company merely an aggrieved party filing its complaint. The ink never smudges. The clerk's hand stays steady. Somewhere in the harbor, a woman is boiling seawater for salt because she can no longer afford to buy it, and here in the counting house we record her husband as a rounding error.

I am very good at my work. This is not a boast; it is a confession.

My name is Astrid Nystrom, and for eleven years I have sat at the third desk from the window in the Halvard Trading Company's counting house, copying shipping entries into fair hand while the real clerks—the ones with salaries and surnames the comptroller can pronounce—did whatever it is that salaries permit a man to do. I am indentured, which is a word that sounds almost gentle until you remember it has a twin: *indentured servant*, and before that, in older documents I was once allowed to shelve, *bonded laborer*, and before that a word I will not write even now because it would make this account worth burning.

I copy. That is what I do. Entries come to me in the comptroller's crabbed fist or in the rough pencil of a harbor master who never learned where the tails of his sevens go, and they leave me in copperplate so clean that merchants in the capital have complimented the Company on its "excellent clerical staff." The comptroller beamed when the letter came. He read it aloud at supper. He did not mention that the excellent clerical staff consisted of himself, two nephews he'd hired for their handwriting rather than their arithmetic, and me.

That is the arrangement. I make the Company legible, and the Company makes me invisible, and between us we have achieved something like peace.

* * *

The morning I begin this account—for I have decided, God help me, to keep an account of my own—was a Tuesday in the month the herring left early, which is how we mark time here. The calendar says one thing; the fish say another; we believe the fish.

The counting house smells of tallow, dust, and the particular sourness of cheap paper stored too near the sea. Forty years of ledgers line the back wall, spines cracked, titles stenciled in fading gold: *Halvard Trading Co., Inward Cargo, 1711–1714*. *Outward Manifests, Pearl Season*. *Debts & Obligations, Divers' Accounts*. That last shelf sits highest, out of easy reach, which I used to think was an accident of carpentry. I no longer think so, but I will come to that later, or I will try to, or I may find that some habits of silence go deeper than ink.

My desk faces the window, and the window faces the harbor, and the harbor is the whole argument of this town. From the third desk I can see the pearl boats going out at first light, low in the water, their sails patched with canvas the color of old teeth. I can see the drying racks where nets hang like the skeletons of enormous fish. And if I lean—which I do not, because leaning is noticed—I can see the stretch of shingle below the north jetty where the divers come up, gasping, their baskets of shell held high, their lips blue as slate.

My father dives there. Has dived there for twenty-two years, since before I could walk, since before my mother died of the fever that came off the ships one spring and took a tenth of the town with it, including, according to the ledger of that year, "divers' wives, sundry." Sundry. My mother had a name. It was Maren. She sang while she mended sails, badly, cheerfully, and she taught me her letters and then, because she was the daughter of a ciphering woman from across the water, a private shorthand besides—a way of writing that looks like flourishes and squalls but says exactly what you mean. She taught it to me as a game, the way you teach a child to whistle. She never imagined it would matter. Neither did I, for a long time.

But that is later too. Everything important, I am learning, was always later than I thought.

On that Tuesday the comptroller, Mr. Aldous Vane—a man built like a wine cask and about as permeable—stood at the front of the room reading aloud from a circular that had come on the morning packet. His voice has two registers: the one he uses for clerks, which is bored, and the one he uses for superiors, which I have only heard through doors and which sounds like a man swallowing his own flattery.

"Gentlemen," he said, meaning the six men in the room who were gentlemen and pointedly not meaning me, though I was the only one taking down his words in fair hand, "the Company directs all stations to prepare full inventories for the autumn audit. Every manifest reconciled. Every debt current. Every—" here he squinted at the circular "—*irregularity resolved prior to inspection*."

He said *irregularity* the way a man says *rat* in a kitchen. Then he looked around the room, found nothing to fear in any face present, and went into his office to drink the small sweet wine he believes nobody knows about. I know about it. Knowing things is the whole of my employment, though it appears nowhere in my contract.

So: the audit. Which meant more copying, faster, and fewer errors, and by evening my eyes ached in that particular way that comes from making small marks precisely, over and over, until the world itself seems composed of small marks.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is apparently the project now. I did not mind the work. There. I have written it, and I will not strike it through. I did not mind it. There is a real pleasure in a page brought to order—in taking the harbor's chaos, its drownings and debts and bargains struck over wet shells, and reducing it to columns that balance. When the sums agree, something in the chest goes quiet. I believed, for eleven years, that this quiet was contentment. I had even begun to believe it was mine.

It was resignation wearing contentment's coat, and I wore it so long I forgot whose coat it was.

* * *

At midday I carried the morning's fair copies to Vane for signature, as required. He signed without reading, as also required—he reads nothing he signs, which is why his signature is worth less than the paper it bleeds onto—and dismissed me with a flick of two fingers. On my way back across the room I passed the high shelf. The *Divers' Accounts*. I did not stop. Stopping is noticed. But I looked, the way you look at a door you have walked past ten thousand times without once wondering what was behind it, and I felt something shift in my chest, small and cold, like a pebble rolling in a pocket.

Here is what I knew, and what I made myself not know, in equal measure.

I knew that divers were entered in the Company books not as men but as accounts. Each diver had a number. My father's was 114. He has been 114 longer than I have been Astrid, as far as the Company is concerned. Advances against catch were debited; gear issued was debited; the boat share, the salt, the lamp oil, the doctor's visits when the bleeding wouldn't stop after a long bottom—all debited. Against these stood credits: pearls delivered, weighed on the Company's scales by the Company's weigher, recorded by the Company's clerk. If the credit exceeded the debit, the diver was paid in scrip redeemable only at the Company store, where prices were set by the same hand that set the debts. If the debit exceeded the credit—and it always, always did—the difference rolled forward into the new season, growing like coral, patient and hard and impossible to dislodge.

My father has worked twenty-two years and owes the Company four more than he owed it the day he started. He knows this. He does not need to read to know it; the arithmetic lives in his shoulders, in the way he moves his arm on cold mornings, in the fact that he is fifty-one and still goes down thirty feet on a single breath to pry shell from rock for men who sleep above the tideline in dry beds.

What I did not let myself know was whether the numbers were true. Whether the pearls weighed in were the pearls written down. Whether Elias Voss—that is, whether a certain young man I will speak of properly when I can bear to—whether the entries beside his number told anything resembling the truth of him. I did not check. Checking is a kind of asking, and asking is a kind of trouble, and I had arranged my life, carefully, brick by brick, into a shape that invited no trouble whatsoever.

You will despise me for this. I despised myself, though I called it prudence at the time, and prudence is such a respectable word that it can cover a great deal of cowardice, the way a tarpaulin covers cargo. Underneath, of course, the cargo rots just the same.

* * *

That evening I walked home along the shingle, because the cliff path was washed out in places and because I wanted, though I would not have admitted it, to see the boats come in.

The tide was dropping. Gulls stood in the wet sand like clerks at their posts, patient, rapacious. The light did what the light does here in the late afternoon—it turned the water to hammered pewter and set every sail edge alight, so that even the Company's grimmest pearl boat looked briefly like something out of a ballad. Appearances are the sea's oldest swindle. Sailors know it. Clerks forget it, because our sea is paper, and paper lies flat.

My father came up the beach with the last basket, and I watched him the way I always watch him: counting his breaths without meaning to. He surfaced, spat, hauled the basket up the shingle, and grinned at me with teeth stained brown from the kelp-chewing that keeps his ears clear on descent. He is not a melancholy man. That is the thing people get wrong about the divers—they imagine bitterness, and mostly what they are is tired, and tiredness looks like calm from a distance.

"Evening, little clerk," he said. He calls me that. It is not affectionate, exactly, and not mocking either; it is simply accurate, the way he is accurate about weather and depths and the weight of a shell in the hand.

"Evening, 114," I said, and he laughed, short and surprised, the way he always does, as if I'd caught him at something.

"Any word from your fine house?" he asked, meaning the counting house, meaning: any change, any mercy, any arithmetic done differently today?

"The audit's coming," I said. "They'll want everything squared."

He nodded slowly, working the meaning of it. Squared. He understood the word in his body before he understood it in his head. "Then they'll be wanting the books pretty," he said. "Well. They've had years to practice."

I should have asked him then. Right there, with the gulls screaming and the light dying and his basket of shell sitting between us like a small heap of moons—I should have said, *Father, show me your tally stick, let me see what you owe and what they say you owe, let me lay the two side by side.* It would have taken ten minutes. Ten minutes, and everything after might have gone differently, or gone worse sooner, which is sometimes the same kindness.

Instead I said, "Supper's at seven," and kissed his salt-cracked cheek, and walked up toward the town with my skirts hem darkening from the wet sand, feeling—as I had felt every evening for eleven years—like a woman walking between two countries that shared a border and no language.

* * *

I lit my lamp at the window of my room above the cooper's shed and took out the day's leftover paper—not the Company's, never the Company's, that would be theft, and I have never stolen anything except, as it turns out, time—and I practiced my mother's shorthand for a quarter hour, as I do most nights, the way other women practice songs. Flourishes and squalls. A system of hooks and loops in which a whole sentence hides inside what looks like a penman's ornament. She called it her *letter-box*, because you could post a letter inside it and no one but family could open it.

Why did she teach me? She was a sail-mender married to a diver in a town the charts barely bother with. Who did she imagine would ever want to read her mail? I used to think it was sentiment—a keepsake of her father's trade, passed down like a ring that doesn't fit. Now I wonder. Now I wonder whether a woman who spent her life mending other people's sails, watching other people's boats come home heavy and her own husband come home owing, had already understood something about records and who gets to keep them. Maybe she taught me the way you teach a child to swim: not because you expect a flood, but because you live beside deep water, and love is preparation.

I wrote that night, in the letter-box, a sentence I remember exactly, because it was the last comfortable thought I ever had:

*Tonight the books balance, and I am safe, and small, and no one's enemy.*

Read it again and notice what I did there, what I did for eleven years: I put *safe* and *small* in the same breath, as though they were the same thing. As though smallness were a shelter rather than a kind of erasure performed voluntarily, daily, with a steady hand and good ink.

No one's enemy. No one's anything, more truly. The captains have their chronicles; the companies have their ledgers; the admiralty has its rolls and its commissions and its gold-stamped histories of glorious tonnage. And the rest of us—we have our names, if we're lucky, spoken for a generation or two at a graveside, and then the sea takes those too, softly, the way it takes everything, without malice and without record.

I blew out the lamp. Below, the tide turned with that long sigh it makes against the jetty piles, and somewhere out in the dark a boat bell rang once, uncertain, like a question no one answered.

In the morning there would be entries to copy. There were always entries to copy. I fell asleep rehearsing the fair hand for tomorrow's manifests, my fingers moving on the blanket, writing names and numbers that belonged to other people, in a script so clean that no one would ever guess a living hand had made it.

I did not yet know that three desks away, in the drawer I dusted but never opened, the master ledger waited with its hidden pages. I did not yet know that among them, in a column dated three winters past, in copperplate very like my own, was written a word I would spend the rest of this story refusing to accept:

Beside a number, beside a name I loved—

*Drowned.*

And that the man it named was, at that very hour, alive, breathing, and nearer than I dreamed.
