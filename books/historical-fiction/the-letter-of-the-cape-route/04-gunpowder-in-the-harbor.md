## 4. Gunpowder in the Harbor

The warships came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were inventory days at the Halvard Company—barrels counted, casks sounded, the small arithmetic of a small port—and I had been halfway through reconciling the salt-fish entries when the first shadow crossed the counting house window and the light in the room went the color of pewter.

I looked up. So did every other clerk, which was itself remarkable; Halvard clerks were trained from boyhood to notice nothing that did not appear in their ledgers.

"Ships," said Pieter, the youngest of us, stating the obvious with the reverence of a man announcing an eclipse.

"Not ships," said old Marten, who had sailed before his knees failed him. "Men-of-war. Look at the lines of her. That's not a merchant's belly."

He was right. Three of them, standing in past the point under full canvas though the wind was foul for it, as if they had somewhere important to be and intended to be there regardless of what God or the weather thought about it. They came around the headland in a stately, arrogant line, took soundings where no honest ship needed soundings—the harbor was charted to the fathom, everyone knew its moods better than their mothers' tempers—and dropped anchor in a sequence so precise it could only have been rehearsed.

By the time the third anchor splashed, the whole town had emptied onto the quay. I watched from the counting house steps, because watching was free and my opinion was worth exactly what I was paid for it, which is to say nothing but expected anyway.

They were imperial men-of-war, gun ports open in a town that hadn't fired a shot in anger since before I was born. The white ensign snapped at each masthead. Soldiers lined the rails—not sailors, soldiers, red coats gone pink at the shoulders from salt spray—and they stood looking down at us the way a man looks into a drawer he intends to empty.

"They're early," said someone near me. "The fleet wasn't due till autumn."

"The fleet," said Marten grimly, "was never due at all. Fleets don't send word ahead. That's rather the point of them."

I should have felt afraid, and part of me did—a cold, animal part that counted the gun ports and did sums. But another part of me, the clerk part, the part that had spent eleven years learning that the world ran on paper, felt something worse than fear. It felt recognition. Whatever those ships wanted, they would want it in writing. And whatever they wanted in writing, someone would have to surrender it.

I had a very good idea whose hands that someone would be.

* * *

The officer came ashore within the hour: a tall man in a blue coat with gold enough on it to buy our whole street, accompanied by six marines and a clerk of his own—young, precise, carrying a leather case the way a surgeon carries his instruments. The harbor master met him at the foot of the customs stairs, sweating through his best coat, and the exchange was brief. Then the party walked up through the town as if they owned the cobblestones, which by nightfall they effectively would.

I returned to my desk because that is what I do. That is what I have always done. When the world convulses, the clerk keeps writing, partly out of duty and partly because a moving pen is harder to arrest than a staring face. I was entering the day's fish weights when the door opened without a knock and the comptroller came through it followed by the officer himself.

I had never seen Herr Grieg move quickly. He was a man built of slow economies, who crossed a room the way he approved an invoice—reluctantly, and only after establishing that someone else would bear the cost. Now he fairly bustled. He pulled off his hat to the officer, which I had never seen him do to anyone, including the company director whose portrait frowned down at us from above the fireplace.

"Herr Lieutenant," Grieg was saying, "the Halvard Company places itself entirely at the fleet's disposal. Entirely. You will find our records in exemplary order—"

"I'm sure," said the officer. His voice was dry and unhurried, the voice of a man reading a document aloud to someone he already knew was guilty. "I am Lieutenant Brandt. Requisition authority, southern squadron." He produced a paper, unfolded it with two crisp movements, and laid it on the comptroller's desk as if setting down a chess piece. "By order of the Admiralty, this port, its wharves, warehouses, stores, boats, and appurtenances are hereby requisitioned for the use of His Imperial Majesty's fleet. Compensation schedules will be assessed against fair market value as determined by—" here the faintest pause, the ghost of a smile that never arrived, "—imperial assessors."

Fair market value, determined by the men taking the goods. I kept my eyes on my ledger and wrote *herring, 14 cask* in a hand that did not shake, because I had learned long ago that shaking hands get noticed.

"Further," Brandt continued, "all commercial records of the port are to be surrendered for audit. Ledgers, correspondence, chart books, manifests, debt rolls, crew lists. All of it. Within one week."

I felt the sentence land somewhere behind my ribs, though it was not addressed to me. All of it. Every entry I had ever made. Every name.

Grieg, to his eternal shame and my eternal lack of surprise, did not even blink. "Of course, Herr Lieutenant. Naturally. We keep meticulous records. You will find everything in order."

"Everything?" said Brandt mildly.

"Everything," said Grieg, and smiled the smile he used on creditors.

I wrote *herring, 15 cask*. My father had dived those pearl beds for thirty years and appeared in the company's books as *labor, native, seasonal, rate fixed*. Somewhere in the pages Grieg was promising so blithely were the true names of every diver in the bay, the debts docked against their breath, the fines levied for short hauls, the advances never repaid because repayment was calculated at rates no one had explained to men who couldn't read the contract. Somewhere in those pages was a line that read *Elias Voss — lost at sea* in a neat clerk's hand that had never once gotten wet.

And this man in the blue coat was going to take all of it away and read it by his own lamplight, with his own purposes, and write whatever history suited the fleet that fed him.

I did not know yet whether that would be better or worse than the company keeping it. I suspected it would be neither. I suspected it would simply be different hands on the same eraser.

* * *

The proclamation went up on the customs house door before dusk, nailed there by a marine while a crowd gathered in the kind of silence that has weight. I read it twice, because the first time I read it as a clerk and the second time I read it as a person, and the two readings disagreed violently.

*All inhabitants will render assistance as required. Boats not registered with the naval purser by Friday will be confiscated. Stores of powder, shot, sailcloth, timber, and provisions are subject to immediate seizure. Harbor pilotage will be conducted under naval direction. Any person concealing property, documents, or information liable to requisition will be tried by tribunal.*

That last clause. *Documents.* Not *stolen goods*, not *arms*—documents. The empire had come for our paper before it came for anything else, and I stood in the crowd understanding, with the particular clarity that comes too late to be useful, that everything I had ever believed about staying invisible depended on the world not caring about people like us. The empire cared now. It cared in triplicate.

Beside me, a fisherman named Aldo muttered, "Compensation schedules. My boat cost eight years of my life."

"It'll cost you four years' worth of herring, by their arithmetic," said Marten. "Assessors always round down."

A woman near the front—I knew her face from the market, a net-mender with forearms like cable—said loudly enough for the marines to hear, "And when they've taken the boats, how are we to eat?"

No one answered her, least of all the marines, who had the professional deafness of men paid to stand still. But I saw the question travel through the crowd like current through water, touching everyone, and I saw something else settle over the faces around me: the look of people recalculating. Fishermen counting their hidden coin. Housewives eyeing their flour barrels. And somewhere in that crowd, I had no doubt, divers thinking about what the navy would make of men who could hold their breath for four minutes and had grievances older than the war.

I went home along the tide line, as I always did, and the harbor at low water looked wrong—too many anchors, too much shadow, the great hulls blotting out the evening stars one constellation at a time. My father would be up from the beds soon. I found myself walking faster.

* * *

He was on the shingle below our cottage, wringing out his diving shirt, and I told him all of it in the flat voice I use for bad news, which is the same voice I use for shipping entries. He listened the way he listens underwater—with his whole body still, saving everything.

"They'll want the divers," he said at last. Not a question.

"For salvage, maybe. For the pearl beds, certainly. The fleet eats, Papa. And pearls buy officers' careers."

"And the ledgers?"

"Surrendered within the week. All of them."

He was quiet a moment, working the water out of his hair. "Then they'll learn things," he said. "About the company. About the men marked lost."

"Yes."

"Will that help anyone?"

I thought of Elias Voss, drowned three years ago on paper, and said nothing, and my silence said more than I meant it to, and my father, who can read the surface of me better than any chart, let it go. That is the arrangement between us: he dives, I keep things, and neither of us asks what the other carries.

"They say accurate records serve justice," I offered. "That's what these empires tell themselves."

"Empires tell themselves many things," he said. "That's why they need so much paper." He hung his shirt on the line and looked out at the anchored ships, black against the last light. "One week, you said?"

"One week."

"Then the company has five days to decide what those books are going to say," he said, "and you have five days to decide what you're going to remember."

I told him I hadn't decided anything. He gave me the look he gives the sea when it lies to him, and went inside.

* * *

I was back at my desk before dawn, because dread, in my experience, is best metabolized through work. The counting house was cold. I lit the lamp, unlocked the entry cabinet, and began making a private list—not of goods, but of records. What existed. Where it lived. Who could reach it.

It was a short list and a terrible one. The master ledger, chained to the comptroller's office. The debt rolls. The crew registers with true names and birthplaces. The burial book, such as it was—half the divers got no better than a marginal tick and a date. The correspondence files, eleven years of Grieg's letters to the directors, which I had filed myself and which contained, among other things, phrases I could recite from memory: *losses acceptable within seasonal projections* and *no recovery attempted, expense unjustified.*

No recovery attempted. Four words for a man. I had filed that letter myself, years ago, and thought nothing of it, because filing is not feeling, and I had built my whole life on the difference.

Now a lieutenant with a leather-cased clerk of his own was coming to take all of it, and I sat in the gray light doing the arithmetic of erasure. If the records went to the fleet, the fleet would audit them for profit and fraud—company fraud, which might even be punished, in the way empires punish theft from themselves. But the names? The debts owed to divers dead and drowned-on-paper? No assessor rounds up for the dead. The victors would keep what served them and seal the rest, and sealed is only one step from burned, and burned is one step from never having been.

Or—and this was the thought that stopped my pen—Grieg would not wait for the week. Grieg, who had complied too quickly, too smoothly, too *entirely*. A man that eager to surrender everything is a man who has already decided what everything contains.

I looked up and found him standing in the doorway, watching me. How long he had been there I could not say. He was fully dressed, though the sun was barely up, and he had the look of a man who had not been to bed.

"Early, Nystrom," he said.

"Inventory," I said. "With the requisition coming, I thought the entries should be current."

"How dutiful." He came into the room and stood looking at the cabinets the way a man looks at livestock he means to sell. "You'll want to prepare the files for surrender, then. All of them. I'll see to the master ledger myself—it requires special handling."

Special handling. I wrote that phrase down inside my skull, where I keep the entries that never appear in any book.

"Of course, Herr Comptroller," I said.

He lingered a moment longer, and I had the distinct impression he was deciding something about me—whether I was furniture or a risk—and then he nodded once and left, and I heard his boots cross the yard toward the warehouse district hours before any honest business hour.

Toward the furnaces, I would have bet my year's wages, if I'd had any.

I sat with my cold lamp and my useless list and understood that the week was a fiction. The confiscation had already begun, quietly, in the dark, by the only man with keys to everything. And whatever he was feeding to the fire right now—letters, pages, names—would be ash by the time the empire's auditors arrived to catalog a truth already curated for them.

I put out the lamp. Through the window the warships rode at anchor, patient as ledgers themselves, waiting for the world to be handed over in writing.

Five days, my father had said. I looked at the gray water and corrected him silently.

Fewer.
