## 6. The Officer Who Reads

My mother taught me to read from a prayer book, but my father taught me to count from a cargo manifest, and between the two of them they settled the question of what I would become. A man who believes in God may be disappointed by Him. A man who believes in arithmetic is never disappointed by arithmetic. Numbers do not lie. They can be made to lie, of course—there is the whole sad history of my profession—but the numbers themselves remain innocent, waiting, like drowned men, for someone honest to pull them up.

I had been at the cape eleven days when I understood that the Halvard Trading Company's ledgers were lying to me.

Not lying in the way ledgers usually lie, which is to say optimistically. Every merchant on this coast rounds his profits upward and his losses downward; I had budgeted for it the way one budgets for weather. No. These books lied in the other direction, and that was what troubled me. They were too clean. A harbor that lands four thousand barrels a year should show spoilage, breakage, pilferage, the ordinary attrition of honest greed. These ledgers showed a town of angels. Or rather—they showed a town of nothing. The entries thinned out at the edges the way a painting thins out where the painter grew tired, and I had learned long ago that the tired places in a record are where the truth goes to hide.

I said as much to Sergeant Ove, who was inventorying powder stores with the enthusiasm of a man who would rather be anywhere else, and he said, "Begging the lieutenant's pardon, but it's paper. It's a wet rock full of fish-smellers and pearl-pickers. What truth is there to hide?"

"Every truth there is," I told him, "if the rock is profitable enough. Empires are not built on gold. Gold is heavy and runs out. They are built on paper, because paper can be made to say anything, and the man who controls what it says controls everything downstream of it." He nodded the way soldiers nod at lieutenants, which is to say with his eyes elsewhere, and went back to counting powder.

I did not mind. I have never required an audience for my work. My work and I understand each other.

* * *

The counting house of the Halvard Company stood on pilings above the harbor mud, and it smelled of tar, tallow, and old ink—the smell, I would come to think, of this whole coast, where every fortune floated on something that rotted beneath it. The comptroller, a man named Vesselink with the polished, apologetic manner of a knife, had surrendered the records with a promptness that should have flattered me and instead insulted me. Honest men grieve over their ledgers the way they grieve over their children. Vesselink had handed mine over like a man returning a borrowed umbrella.

I set up my audit in the company's own back room, which had the advantage of a window, a lock, and a view of the harbor that let me watch the pearl boats come and go while I worked. For six days I reconciled. Cargo in against cargo out. Wages paid against wages owed. Divers licensed against divers listed.

It was the divers who stopped me.

The company kept a roll of licensed divers, as the regulations required, with a column for status: active, retired, deceased. I counted one hundred and forty names. In the last seven years, thirty-one of them had been marked *lost at sea*. Thirty-one men out of a hundred and forty, in an industry where, by naval actuarial tables, a well-run operation should expect perhaps a tenth of that. I am not a sentimental man. The sea takes its toll; that is why the pearls are expensive; that is the whole arrangement. But thirty-one was not a toll. It was a harvest.

And here was the discrepancy that kept me at my desk past midnight, with the lamp turned low and the harbor breathing below me: the *lost* divers were not lost anywhere else. No salvage claims. No widows' petitions for back wages. No bodies recovered, no funerals recorded in the parish rolls I had borrowed from the church on the pretext of confirming deaths for the muster. A man drowns, his body comes ashore, his people bury him, his wife petitions the company for his final season's pay—that is the ordinary paper trail of grief, and it exists in every port I have ever audited. Here there was nothing. Thirty-one men had walked off the page as cleanly as if a hand had wiped them.

I do not like clean pages. Clean pages are where dirty hands have been.

* * *

On the seventh day I sent for the junior clerk.

I had noticed her before, in the way one notices a piece of the furniture that turns out to be load-bearing. The Halvard counting house employed six clerks, five of them men with the prosperous, incurious faces of men whose fathers had bought them their desks. The sixth was a woman—Astrid Nystrom, according to the employment roll, though the roll misspelled her surname, which told me no one senior had ever troubled to look at it twice. She sat at the smallest desk, nearest the door, and she had the posture of someone who had learned that the safest thing to be in a room is unnoticed. I had been that person once, in my first posting, and I recognized the discipline of it the way one soldier recognizes another's drill.

I also recognized, from the first day I had set her to copying requisition schedules, that she was the only person in that building who knew where anything was.

"Miss Nystrom." I had her shown into the back room and offered her the chair; she took it on the front third of the seat, as if the rest might be reclaimed from her at any moment. "You've been with the company how long?"

"Nine years, sir. Since I was fourteen."

"Fourteen. And in nine years, who has taught you the entries?"

She considered the question with a seriousness that I found—there is no better word for it—refreshing. Most people answer questions the way they throw stones, hoping to hit something. She answered them the way she presumably made entries: precisely, and only as far as the evidence went.

"The ledgers taught me, sir. Master Halvard's system is—was—consistent. Once you see the pattern of a hand, you can read the intention behind it."

"The intention behind it," I repeated. "That is an unusual way for a clerk to speak."

"It's the only useful way, sir. A figure can be wrong in a hundred ways. An intention is usually wrong in only one."

I confess the answer pleased me more than it should have. I have spent my career among men who believe records are a burden, a formality, a sheaf of papers to be produced when the admiral's aide comes calling and burned when he leaves. To sit across from someone who spoke of entries as though they had intentions—as though they were testimony—was like hearing my own private liturgy read back to me in a stranger's voice.

I pushed the diver's roll across the desk.

"Thirty-one men marked lost in seven years," I said. "No bodies. No petitions. No funerals. Explain it."

She looked at the roll for a long moment. I watched her hands. They were ink-stained at the second knuckle, the right hand more than the left, and they did not move at all. I have interrogated smugglers and mutineers and one very memorable forger, and I will tell you what I know about hands: the guilty ones fidget, and the frightened ones go still. Hers went still.

"The sea is hard here, sir," she said. "The beds are deep, and the company sets the season short, so the divers work the deep beds past safe measure. Men drown."

"Men drown," I agreed. "And then their widows petition for wages, and the company pays or refuses, and either way it goes in the book. Where are the petitions?"

"The company handles those privately, sir. Family accounts. It wouldn't go in the master roll."

"Everything goes in the book, Miss Nystrom. That is what a book is *for*." I kept my voice even. I was not angry with her; I was angry at the shape of the silence she was sitting inside, which was a shape I recognized, because I had audited enough companies to know that the shape is always the same. "You are the junior clerk most familiar with these entries. That is not an accusation. It is a fact, and facts are the only currency I deal in. So I will ask you plainly, and you may answer plainly or not at all: is this roll a true record of the men who have died in the company's waters?"

The lamp hissed. Below us a pearl boat came in on the evening tide, and I heard the crew singing some low work-song in their own language, the sound rising through the floorboards like water.

"No, sir," she said.

I will not pretend the answer surprised me. I had known it since the fourth day, the way you know a false bottom in a strongbox—by the weight being wrong. But knowing a thing by inference and hearing it said aloud are different economies, and I sat for a moment in the strange quiet of a lie finally priced.

"Tell me what you know," I said. "Not what you suspect. What you can stand behind."

And here she did something I have thought about many times since. She did not tell me. She looked at the roll, and at my lamp, and at the window where the harbor was going dark, and she said, "Sir, if I tell you what I know, will it go in *your* book?"

"It will go in my report."

"Then whoever reads your report will know it."

"Presumably. That is the intention of a report."

"And after that?" Her voice was still level, still clerk-precise, but something underneath it had begun to move, the way water moves under ice. "After the report, sir. Where does the knowledge go?"

I opened my mouth to give the correct answer—the answer I had believed my whole career, the answer they had given me at the academy with charts and solemn voices: *it goes into the record, and the record is justice, and justice is the empire's promise to every soul under its flag.* And then I thought of Vesselink handing over the ledgers like a borrowed umbrella. I thought of the parish rolls with their tidy absences. I thought of thirty-one men whose grief had produced no paper at all, as if the company had somehow gotten to sorrow itself and audited it down to nothing.

I did not give the correct answer. I gave an honest one, which is rarer and worth more.

"I don't know," I said. "I would like to tell you it goes into the record and the record protects the weak. Some days I still believe that. But I have seen records sealed, and I have seen records rewritten, and I have seen honest reports die in a filing cabinet in the capital while the men who burned the original pages were promoted. So I will not promise you the system works. I will only promise you that I read what is put in front of me, and I write down what I find, and I sign it. That is all I have. It is not nothing. But I will not dress it up as more than it is."

She studied me for a long moment, and I had the uncomfortable sensation of being audited myself—of being an entry in someone else's book, weighed for accuracy.

"That's more than most officers offer," she said at last.

"Most officers don't read."

"No," she said, and there was the ghost of something in her face that was not quite a smile but lived in the same house. "I'd noticed that."

* * *

I gave her the diver's roll to take back and reconcile against the wage books—officially, so that no one could object to her handling it—and she rose to go. At the door she stopped, her hand on the frame, and stood with her back to me for a moment in a way that made me think of a person standing at the edge of deep water, testing it.

"Lieutenant," she said. "The men on that roll. The lost ones. Some of them have families still in the town. If your report leads to—questions. Interviews. I'd ask you to be careful who you ask, and in front of whom."

"You're concerned for their safety."

"I'm concerned that paper doesn't drown, sir, but people do."

She left before I could answer, which was perhaps the point, and I sat alone with the lamp and the ledgers and the sound of the tide coming in under the floorboards, and I turned her sentence over the way I turn over a suspect figure, looking for the error in it, and finding none.

*Paper doesn't drown, but people do.*

It is a curious thing, to meet a mind that works like your own. I had spent eleven days in this town believing I was the only person who took the record seriously, the only one who understood that a ledger is not an account of money but an account of lives—that every column is a census of what was done to whom, and that the empire's whole claim to legitimacy rests on the proposition that someone, somewhere, is keeping the true count. And then a junior clerk with ink on her knuckles had looked at me across a desk and asked where my knowledge would go after my report, which is the question I ask of every record I audit, and which almost no one has ever thought to ask of *me*.

I should have been warned by the pleasure I took in the conversation. I have known since I was a young officer that the most dangerous thing in any inquiry is not the liar. Liars are easy; they contradict themselves, and arithmetic catches them. The dangerous one is the person who tells you the truth in careful portions, watching to see what you do with each piece. That is not deception. That is *custody*. And I recognized, though I did not yet name it, that Miss Nystrom was holding something in custody, and that my audit was circling it the way a patrol boat circles a reef—sounding the depth, without yet knowing what the depth conceals.

I worked late. I always work late. Around midnight I went back to the diver's roll one more time, and I did what I should have done on the first day: I read the names. Not the columns, not the statuses—the names. Elias Voss, lost three years past. Marten Oduya, lost the spring before. I read them aloud to the empty room, quietly, the way one reads the dead, because I have an old belief—unscientific, unprofessional, and mine—that a name spoken aloud is a small audit of heaven, and that the dead are owed at least that much from the man who holds their file.

Then I took a fresh sheet and began a private memorandum, the kind I keep separate from my official reports: *Discrepancies in the diver's roll suggest systematic falsification of mortality records. Recommend interview of company divers and families. Recommend seizure of all loose correspondence and draft ledgers prior to final inventory.*

And it was in writing that last line that my pen slowed, because I remembered that Vesselink had surrendered his records with suspicious promptness, and that the inventory of loose papers was scheduled for three days hence, and that a man who hands over his ledgers like a borrowed umbrella is a man who has already decided which pages he can afford to lose.

I looked at the memorandum for a long time. Then I rang for the sergeant and gave an order I would think about often in the weeks to come: that the comptroller's offices and all company storage be sealed at first light, and that no paper, scrap, or binding leave any of them before my inventory was complete.

I went to bed believing I had moved in time.

I did not yet know how much a fire can burn in one night.
