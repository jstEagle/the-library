## 7. Debt of Breath

There is a particular silence a man makes when he decides to drown himself slowly. I had heard it before, in my father's chest, in the wet rattle of men who'd gone down too deep too many times and come back up with their lungs full of someone else's debt. It is not a dramatic sound. It is the sound of a man agreeing to terms.

I heard it in my own chest the morning the navy's flag went up over the pearl beds, and I have been hearing it ever since.

* * *

The requisition notice was nailed to the customs house door in three languages, none of them ours. The imperial fleet wanted pearls. Of course it did. Wars are expensive, and empires have never once paid for themselves—they pay with other people's water. The beds off the eastern shoals, which the Halvard Company had worked for forty years and which had worked half the town to death in return, were now His Majesty's beds. The divers were now His Majesty's divers. The word *requisition* does a great deal of quiet work that way. It takes a thing and makes it sound as though it were always going somewhere else.

Old Tammas read the notice aloud for those who couldn't, his finger moving under each line like a man walking a plank.

"Quotas," he said at last, folding the paper with the care of a priest handling relics. "Daily quotas. And a naval overseer on every boat."

"Overseer," said Jorunn, spitting into the tide. She was sixty and had more scar tissue than skin on her hands. "We had company foremen. We had tally-men. Now we're to have a boy with a musket watching us hold our breath. Progress."

Nobody laughed, which was its own kind of agreement.

I stood at the back where I always stand, being nobody—being, officially, a drowned man named Soren Kelst, which is the name on the rival buyer's rolls and the name I answer to when soldiers ask. Three years dead on paper. There are worse things to be. A dead man can't be pressed into salvage labor; a dead man's debts died with him; a dead man can stand in a crowd and listen and no one thinks twice about what he hears.

But a dead man also can't say anything. And that morning, listening to Tammas read out quotas that would put three dives a day on men whose lungs were already borrowed against, I understood that staying dead was becoming its own kind of lie.

"Three dives," Jorunn said again, quieter. "Tammas, you know what three dives does to a man your age."

"I know what one dive less than quota does," Tammas said. "It does a flogging. I've seen the army's arithmetic. They count our breaths like coin."

They did. That was the whole genius of it. You couldn't argue with a quota the way you could argue with a foreman. A foreman could be bribed, flattered, hated. A quota just sat there, indifferent as tide, and behind it stood men with guns who had never once been underwater and therefore believed drowning was a rumor.

That night I walked the length of the shingle thinking, and by the time I reached the breakwater I had arrived at the stupidest, most necessary idea of my life.

* * *

Here is what a diver knows that an empire doesn't: you cannot force the sea. You can order a man down, but the oysters don't take orders. The beds bloom and rest on their own calendar, and a good diver reads that calendar the way a clerk reads a ledger—which is to say, closely, and with an eye for what's been written over.

So when I called the older divers together in the net loft on Thursday—Jorunn, Tammas, the Brandt brothers, old Maren who taught me to equalize my ears before I could write my name—I didn't propose anything so grand as rebellion. Rebellion gets you flogged or worse. I proposed arithmetic.

"They want a daily weight of shell," I told them, crouched among the nets with lantern light making shadows of all our faces. "Fine. Give it to them. Slowly."

Maren narrowed her eyes. She has eyes like a gull's, all edge. "Slowly how?"

"You know the eastern shoal after the spring tides—the silt comes back and the beds go quiet. Every man knows it. So we tell the overseers the beds are silted. We dive the edges, the thin ground, bring up enough to show effort and never enough to fill the quota. Not refusal. Slowness. A boat that rows hard and arrives late. Nets that need mending. Weather that turns." I spread my hands. "The sea is unreliable. Let us be the sea."

"And when they figure it out?" Jorunn asked. She wasn't objecting. She was checking my work, the way she used to check my knots.

"They'll suspect. Suspecting isn't proving. An overseer can't report 'the oysters are conspiring.'" I let that sit a moment. "Every day short of quota is a day the beds rest. Every day the beds rest is a day Tammas keeps his lungs. We're not fighting them. We're outlasting them. Fleets move on. Quotas end. Lungs don't come back."

Tammas laughed—a dry sound, like rope over stone. "Boy wants to fight the empire with laziness."

"With patience," I said. "Laziness is when you don't care."

It passed, seven hands raised around the lantern, Maren's last and slowest, because Maren always understands first and agrees last. That's wisdom, probably. Or exhaustion. At our age the two wear the same coat.

* * *

It worked for eleven days.

Eleven days of boats that rowed beautifully and found nothing. Eleven days of honest, exhausted faces reporting silt, cold currents, a sickness among the shell. The young divers caught on fast—younger divers always do; they've inherited the skill of looking stupid from generations of ancestors who needed it—and soon the whole eastern fleet was performing a masterpiece of incompetence. One overseer, a pale lieutenant's-warrant named Fisk who got seasick in harbor, wrote in his log that the beds were "materially diminished," which may be the only true sentence the imperial navy recorded that month.

On the twelfth day, they sent a man who could swim.

He came out on the patrol boat, this one—not a warrant officer but a proper lieutenant, young, precise, with the look of a person who reads documents for pleasure. He watched two dives from the rail without blinking, then stripped off his coat, handed it to a sailor, and went over the side.

You could have heard the whole fleet inhale.

He wasn't good. He was clumsy, air-hungry, down barely a fathom and a half before he came up blowing like a landed fish. But he'd seen enough. He'd seen the beds lying clean and open on the bottom like an unguarded purse, and he'd seen us diving the margins around them with the tragic diligence of men hunting a fortune that wasn't there.

He hauled himself aboard, dripping, and looked across the water at our boats with an expression I recognized from my father's face the day he caught me skimming the buyer's scales: not anger. Recalculation.

That evening the quota didn't change. Instead, a notice appeared: divers would henceforth be assigned to wreck salvage on the two warships lost in the storm, "as supplementary labor, rotated weekly."

Salvage. On wrecks. In those waters, with those hulls broken open and the current running through them like a knife through a throat.

I stood on the shingle reading that notice and felt the slow-work die in my hands. We hadn't bought time. We'd bought attention.

* * *

Astrid found me at the tide pools two nights later, which is where we find each other, because the tide pools are the only place in this town where the ledgers don't reach.

She came down the rocks with her skirt hem dark with spray and her face already set in the shape of an argument, and I thought, absurdly, *she's beautiful when she's about to lose*, which is the kind of thought that gets a man drowned twice.

"They've posted the salvage rotation," she said, without greeting. We don't waste greetings anymore; we spent them all years ago. "Elias. Tell me you've seen it."

"I've seen it."

"Salvage crews from the eastern beds. Rotated weekly. Do you understand what that means? The *Vasa* went down in nine fathoms with her stern stove in. Men have died in six feet of calm water. You're sending—" She stopped herself. Corrected. "They're sending Tammas. Maren. Men who surface bleeding from the ears after a normal dive."

"Maren's rotation isn't until next week."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't a joke. It was a stalling tactic." I crouched by the pools, watching a crab conduct its small doomed business between stones. "The rotation buys another fortnight. Maybe three. Salvage crews get reassigned, storms come, officers rotate out—"

"Elias." She said my name the way you press on a wound to see if it's still bleeding. "Listen to yourself. You're doing the ledger's arithmetic. Fortnights. Rotations. You're counting breaths like coin."

That landed harder than she knew, because I'd said almost exactly those words to Tammas ten days before, standing proud in a net loft, certain I'd invented courage. It's strange how a sentence can be wisdom in your own mouth and an indictment in hers.

"The Patience sails on the spring tide," she said. "Nine days. I can get you aboard. Elias, I can get *you* aboard—a dead man, a false name, a crate with your name on it if it needs one. Once you're past the capes, no requisition reaches you. No quota. No salvage. You'd be free."

"And the others?"

The question hung between us over the black water, and I watched her hear it, and I watched her hate it, because she'd known it was coming the way you know a wave is coming—by the pull of the sand under your feet before it breaks.

"Tammas is sixty-one," I said. "Jorunn's hands shake so badly she can't mend her own net. Maren taught half this town to breathe underwater. If I run, the rotation still stands. The salvage still stands. All that changes is one fewer body to share it, and one fewer voice saying *slowly, carefully, not that line, that hull's rotten.*" I picked up a shell and turned it over. "You of all people should understand. You're copying names out of a ledger so they won't be erased. I'm not going to erase myself from mine."

"It's not the same."

"No. Yours is braver."

"Elias—"

"Astrid." I made myself look at her. The moon was doing its usual cape-town trick, half-hidden, showing just enough to make everything look possible. "If I go, I live. I live a long time, maybe, in some port town with salt money and no history. And every morning I wake up knowing that Tammas went down into the *Vasa*'s stern because I wasn't in the boat beside him saying *not today, the current's wrong, wait for the slack tide.* Knowing that Jorunn took a flogging for a quota I helped invent. You want to save one man. I love you for it. But I'm not one man. I'm the grandson of everyone who ever dove these beds and came up owing. If I leave them, I'm free the way the comptroller is free. Free because somebody else paid."

She was crying, quietly, the way she does everything—efficiently, without permission. "And if you stay, you die the way my father died. Slowly, in installments, and the ledger writes *lost at work* and nobody searches."

"Yes," I said. "Probably."

"*Probably.*"

"Astrid, I've been dead three years. I know exactly what living costs here, and I know exactly what it costs elsewhere, and I'm telling you they're closer in price than you think. At least here the dying means something. At least here I'm spending it on something."

"That's what the empire says about war," she said, fierce and wet-eyed. "That's what every fool with a flag says. *At least it means something.* Meaning is what they pay you in instead of wages."

I laughed. I couldn't help it—she's the only person alive who can gut me and make me laugh while I bleed. "Then I'm rich," I said. "Look at all the meaning I've got saved up."

She didn't laugh. She took my face in both hands, her palms cold with sea spray, and held me like something she was trying to keep from washing away.

"Nine days," she whispered. "The Patience. If you change your mind, the crate will be there. Marked with a blue thread. I'll wait until the tide turns."

"And if I don't come?"

Her jaw tightened. She is the bravest person I know and I include every man who's ever gone down on a single breath. "Then I'll put you in the record anyway," she said. "Alive. Under your own name. Where their historians can't lose you."

* * *

She left before the tide fully turned, climbing the rocks with her lantern bobbing, and I stayed in the pools until the cold climbed through my clothes, doing the arithmetic I'd sworn off.

Nine days. The Patience. A crate with a blue thread.

Here is the truth I didn't give her, because she'd have used it against me, and rightly: part of me wants to go. God help me, part of me is already on that ship, already breathing air that belongs to no quota, already in a port where my chest is just a chest and not a company asset. Survival isn't cowardice. It sings in the blood like any other hunger.

But then I think of Tammas in the water beside me, saying, the way he said it yesterday on the boat, "Slow down, boy, the shells aren't going anywhere, and neither are we"—and I understand that he's wrong about the second part. We are going somewhere. All of us. The only question the sea ever asks is whether you go down alone or together, and whether anyone writes down which it was.

Two mornings later, the patrol boat anchored early off the eastern beds, and a navy launch pulled for our fleet with a folded paper in the coxswain's hand. Through Old Tammas's glass I watched the lieutenant in the stern—Brandt, the one who swims, the one who reads—checking names against a list.

The salvage rotation had moved up. Ours was first.

We had eight days instead of nine, and the water, when I tested it at dawn, was already turning cold.
