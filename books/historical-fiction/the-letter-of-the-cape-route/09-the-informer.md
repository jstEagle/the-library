## 9. The Informer

The trouble with working by lantern light is that lantern light works against you. It draws moths, it draws cats, it draws the eye of any man walking past the counting house at an hour when honest clerks are asleep. I knew this. I had known it since the second night, when I watched a moth batter itself against the glass of my lamp with the dumb persistence of a creature that cannot understand why the thing that looks like salvation keeps hurting it. I remember thinking: there is a lesson in you, little fool, and I am choosing not to learn it.

By the ninth night I had developed a system worthy of a smuggler, which was what I had become, if you counted paper as contraband, and I did. I copied only between the last bell and the first grey light, when the garrison patrols changed over and the streets belonged to drunks and gulls. I kept the company's legitimate work stacked on my desk in plain view—invoices to be fair-copied, letters to be entered—so that anyone glancing through the window would see exactly what they expected to see: Astrid Nystrom, the quiet one, killing herself over correspondence because she had no husband to go home to. And beneath that, under a false bottom in the invoice drawer, the true work: the master ledger's hidden pages, translated page by page into my mother's shorthand, each sheet folded small and carried home sewn inside a sail I was mending for Elias, stitch by stitch, like a woman embroidering a confession.

My mother taught me the shorthand when I was eleven, on the theory that a girl who could write fast could earn more. She never imagined I would use it to commit treason at the rate of four pages a night. She died before the fleet came. Sometimes I think that was mercy, and sometimes I think it was cowardice on her part, which is an unkind thing to think about the dead, but grief makes you petty.

That ninth night, the moth came back. So did something worse.

I heard boots on the boardwalk outside—not the patrol's measured tread, but a loitering kind of walk, the walk of a man who wants to be heard without being seen hearing. I turned down the lamp to a bead and sat still among my shadows, listening. The boots stopped. A shadow crossed the gap in the shutters, paused, moved on. When I finally breathed again, my breath fogged in the cold, and I thought: *Piet.*

It could only be Piet Alders. He was the only other soul with a key to the counting house after hours, the only one with a reason to be abroad at that hour, and—the decisive evidence—the only one who had taken to looking at me lately the way a man looks at a ledger entry he suspects does not balance.

* * *

Piet Alders had been a clerk for nineteen years, and in nineteen years he had learned one lesson so thoroughly it had grown into his bones the way salt grows into a ship's timbers: *the man who notices nothing survives longest.* Piet noticed everything. That was the whole of his tragedy, though he would not have called it a tragedy. He would have called it diligence.

He noticed that Astrid Nystrom stayed late. He noticed that the candles she drew from stores lasted half again as long as they should have, which meant either she was stealing tallow—a small sin, forgivable—or she was burning more than she reported, which meant she was doing something after dark that she did not wish entered anywhere. Piet understood unentered things. Unentered things were the currency of his entire existence. He had spent nineteen years making sure that certain sums, certain shipments, certain names appeared in no book at all, and he had been paid modestly and threatened occasionally for the privilege, and he had told himself it was prudence.

But here was the thing that gnawed at him like shipworm, the thing he turned over at night in his narrow bed while his wife snored beside him: Astrid Nystrom was not afraid.

Piet was afraid all the time. It was his natural condition, like nearsightedness. He was afraid of the comptroller, who smiled at him with wet eyes; he was afraid of the army now camped in the customhouse; he was afraid most of all of being caught between them, a scrap of paper in a wind that blew from two directions. And yet Astrid sat in that counting house night after night with her lamp lit like a signal fire, copying God knew what, and when Piet passed her in the corridor she nodded to him—nodded!—with the calm of a woman who had already made her peace with whatever came.

People who are not afraid are either innocent or resolved, and Piet had seen enough of the world to know that innocence was rare and resolution dangerous. If she was copying the master ledger—if she had found the hidden pages, the ones Piet himself had helped bind into the false spine nine years ago, sweating, while old Halvard stood over him with his soft voice and his hard hands—then she was not merely brave. She was a fuse. And Piet was standing on the fuse.

There was also, he admitted it to himself in the dark honesty of three in the morning, the matter of the promotion. The junior clerkship under the requisition officer had gone to her. Lieutenant Brandt wanted someone who knew the entries, and the comptroller had recommended Astrid with a smile that Piet had not liked at all, because the comptroller smiling at a clerk meant the comptroller had a use for her, and the comptroller having a use for a clerk meant the clerk was either rising or bait. Nineteen years Piet had waited for such a recommendation. It had gone to a woman who mended sails and smelled of salt and looked through him as though he were furniture.

So on a raw morning with rain coming in off the water like a debt collector, Piet Alders presented himself at the customhouse and asked to speak with Lieutenant Brandt, and told himself, all the way up the stairs, that he was doing his duty.

* * *

Brandt received him in the room that had once been the harbormaster's and was now hung with charts and inventory schedules, and Piet saw at once that this was a man who frightened him in an entirely new way, because Lieutenant Brandt listened.

"Sit," said Brandt, and Piet sat, and the lieutenant did not look up from the ledger open before him for a full minute, which gave Piet time to observe that the page was one of the shipping entries from March, and that in the margin, in pencil, in a small precise hand, were notes. Corrections. Queries. The lieutenant was reading the company's books the way a surgeon reads a body.

"You're Alders," Brandt said at last. "Senior clerk. You've been with Halvard nineteen years."

"Yes, sir."

"Then you know these entries better than anyone." Brandt turned the ledger around. "Explain to me why the *Meridian*'s cargo manifest lists forty barrels of pearl-shell, when the harbor dues receipt—which your own office issued—says fifty-two."

Piet's mouth went dry. This was the trap he had feared all his life, except that in his nightmares the trap was sprung by the comptroller, not by an imperial officer with ink-stained fingers and patient eyes. "Errors occur, sir. In a busy season—"

"In every season, apparently." Brandt leaned back. "You didn't come here to discuss barrels. You walked past two sentries and asked the sergeant specifically for me, at an hour when your office expects you. So. What do you want to tell me?"

And there it was, the open door, and Piet discovered that walking through it was harder than standing before it. Because once he said the words they could not be unsaid, and if Astrid Nystrom was innocent—if she was only a tired woman copying invoices—then he would have handed a jealous man's spite to an officer of the fleet, and officers wrote things down. Officers remembered.

But then he thought of the hidden pages. He thought of the comptroller's wet-eyed smile. He thought of nineteen years.

"The junior clerk," Piet said. "Nystrom. She keeps late hours at the counting house. Alone. With the master ledger accessible." He heard his own voice, thin and reasonable, and hated it, and went on anyway. "I only mention it because accuracy matters to you, sir. And because—" he cast about for the respectable reason, the duty-shaped reason, and found it waiting, as such reasons always are, "—because if anything irregular were happening to the company's records during a requisition, it should be noted. By someone. For the record."

Brandt regarded him for a long moment. "For the record," he repeated, and Piet could not tell whether the faint dryness in the officer's voice was approval or contempt, and that uncertainty followed him out of the customhouse and all the way home through the rain.

When he had gone, Brandt sat alone with the ledgers and did not write anything down. Not yet. He had interviewed forty people in this town and most of them lied to him fluently, and he had learned to weigh informants the way he weighed manifests: by what they volunteered, and what they strained to keep back. Alders had volunteered jealousy dressed as duty. But underneath the jealousy there had been fear, real fear, the specific fear of a man who knows where bodies are buried and has just realized someone else owns a shovel.

Brandt pulled the March ledger toward him and looked again at the marginalia in his own hand. Fifty-two barrels received, forty recorded. Twelve barrels vanished between the dock and the page. Multiply that across six years of entries and you had a fortune flowing somewhere it was never recorded—and a company whose books were less a record than a curtain.

He had come to this cape believing that accurate records served justice. He still believed it. What unsettled him, sitting in the rain-grey light with a jealous clerk's testimony cooling in the air, was the growing suspicion that justice was not what the records had ever been *for*—that the ledgers were not a mirror of the town but a muzzle on it, and that everyone here, from the comptroller down to the pearl divers, understood this perfectly and had understood it for generations.

Someone was working late over those books. He would find out whether it was theft, or sabotage, or—this possibility he turned over carefully, the way he turned over any document whose contents might change everything—something else entirely.

Before he could decide how to proceed, the comptroller requested an audience.

* * *

Comptroller Vessik arrived at the customhouse that afternoon under an umbrella held by a hired boy, smelling of pomade and brandy, carrying a leather case the way a priest carries relics. Astrid saw him arrive, because she was at her desk in the outer office fair-copying requisition forms, and she had learned in three weeks of working under the army's nose to watch everything and appear to watch nothing.

She heard most of what passed behind Brandt's door, because the door was thin and Vessik's voice had been built for counting houses, where a man must be heard across a room without seeming to raise it.

"—naturally, Lieutenant, we wish to cooperate fully. The original volumes suffered, ah, exposure. Damp. Salt air is ruinous to paper, as I'm sure you've observed. What I have brought you is a fair copy, consolidated, verified against the originals by my own hand. Every debt, every shipment, every diver's account, rendered clean and legible." A pause, the sound of leather buckles opening. "You will find it answers every question your audit has raised."

"And the discrepancies?" Brandt's voice, level. "The twelve barrels in March. The diver accounts that terminate without settlement."

"Bookkeeping errors, Lieutenant. Corrected herein." A silk-smooth pause. "The company is old, and its methods older. What matters now is that the fleet has an accurate account going forward. I trust—" the voice dropped, became almost intimate, "—that a man of your responsibilities appreciates the value of a clean record. One need not dig through mud when the well has been freshly drawn."

Silence. Then Brandt, cool as tide-water: "I'll compare it against the originals."

A rustle that might have been a shrug. "The originals, as I said, are damp-damaged. Comparison may prove... difficult. But of course, do as you see fit."

Astrid copied the same line of the requisition form three times, her hand steady and her heart hammering, because she alone in that building knew what *damp-damaged* meant, knew what Vessik had burned in his courtyard grate while she crouched behind the woodpile with a half-scorched page pressed against her ribs like a coal. He was not hiding the crimes anymore. He was replacing them. He was handing the victors a history already laundered, smooth and legible and empty, and Brandt—who trusted paper, who believed paper—was holding it in his hands.

When Vessik emerged, smiling, umbrella bobbing away through the rain, Astrid bent over her forms and let him pass without a glance. But inside her, something closed like a fist.

They were coming from both directions now. Behind her, Piet's sidelong looks and his loitering boots; ahead of her, a corrected ledger that would bury every name she was trying to save. She thought of the sheets sewn into the sail in her lodgings, four pages' worth of drowned men and erased debts, and how far they still had to travel, and how narrow the harbor gate had become.

Outside, the wind had begun to rise. It came off the sea in long hard gusts, rattling the shutters, driving the rain sideways, and the gulls wheeled low over the harbor screaming like things with a warning to deliver. Old divers said a sky like that meant a storm was stacking itself up beyond the horizon, gathering weight.

Astrid looked out at the darkening water, at the warships riding heavy at their anchors, and felt the walls drawing in from every side at once—and above them all, unseen, the sky itself beginning to close.
