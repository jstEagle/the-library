## 12. The Price of the Pearl Beds

There is a particular silence that falls on men who have been told to work faster in water that wants them dead. I had heard it before, in the years after the ledger wrote me off—a silence like a held breath, like a whole beach deciding at once not to speak. I heard it again the morning the soldiers came down to the beds with their new rules and their new whips, and I understood that the silence was not obedience. It was arithmetic. Every man on that shingle was counting, and the sum kept coming out the same: not enough air, not enough time, not enough of anything except water.

The flogging happened on the third day of the new regime.

His name was Tammas Reeve, though the army's list called him Diver Nine, which told you everything you needed to know about how the empire counted. Tammas was fifty-one years old and had been putting his head under the cape's water since he was nine, which meant he had forty-two years of judgment stored in his body—when to dive, when to wait, when the current said *not today, boy, not today*. The army had no column for judgment. The army had a quota.

He came up empty from the third bed that morning. Not because the beds were empty—the beds were never empty, that was the company's whole genius—but because the water was wrong. A greenish murk had come in with the tide, the kind that swallows a man at four fathoms and hands him back to the sharks. Any diver with a season behind him would have read that water and stayed on the line. Tammas went down anyway, because a corporal with a slate told him to, and he came up with two oysters and a bleeding ear where the pressure had found some old wound, and the corporal marked him *slow*.

Slow. As if the sea were a shop and the men were behind the counter.

The flogging was carried out at midday, on the shingle, in front of all of us, because that is the entire grammar of flogging—the audience is the sentence. They strung him to a capstan post that had heard worse, and a young soldier with the unhappy face of a man doing arithmetic of his own laid on twelve strokes with a cat that had been soaked in seawater, because salt in the wounds is the cape's own contribution to the empire's punishments. We do our part.

Tammas did not cry out. I have seen men cry out, and I have seen men like Tammas, who have spent their lives learning that the water takes the ones who panic, and who have therefore made a religion of stillness. He took his twelve the way he took his dives: breath held, body loose, eyes fixed on some middle distance where the pain could not follow. When they cut him down he stood on his own legs, which was its own kind of answer, and walked past the corporal without looking at him, which was a second answer, and the whole beach watched him go and said nothing, and the nothing was louder than the cat.

I stood in the front rank with my arms crossed and my face arranged into the dull mask I had worn for three years—the face of a man called Jonas Kell, a name I had picked the way you pick a coat off a wreck: it fit well enough and it wasn't mine. Behind the mask, something was happening that I did not yet have words for. It was not the old cold anger, the kind you bank and feed slowly and call prudence. It was hotter than that. It was the anger that comes when you realize the arithmetic has changed—when you understand that the people doing the counting have decided the count doesn't have to come out with you still in it.

I had been angry before. I had not yet been *certain*. There is a difference, and the difference has a price, and the price is always paid by somebody.

* * *

I found out about the salvage two days later, and I found out the way I found out everything worth knowing on this cape—not from the men who made the decisions, but from the men who carried the decisions in buckets.

The bucket-carrier in this case was a corporal named Adeyemi, a supply clerk with soft hands and a guilty conscience, who came down to the drying sheds ostensibly to count oyster knives and actually to drink the cheap arrack the divers distilled from anything the army hadn't thought to lock up. He was a talker when he drank, and a sorry one, and by his third cup he was telling the shed full of us that the salvage details were being drawn up for the two wrecks out on the point—the *Resolute* and the *Meridian*, the warships the gale had eaten.

"Salvage," I said, in my flattest Jonas Kell voice. "You'll be wanting the harbor crew for that. The cranes. The lighters."

He shook his head with the ponderous regret of a man who has drunk just enough to feel the tragedy of his position and not enough to stop talking. "No local laborers. Specialist divers." He tapped his cup against his knee. "The holds are at depth, see. The guns, the specie, the dispatch chests. The captain wants it all raised before the weather turns again. And there's—" he lowered his voice, though the arrack had already lowered it past hearing for anyone sober, "—there's the dead. They want the dead recovered too. For the lists."

The shed went quiet in that particular way again. Forty men, and the silence of forty men, which is heavier than any cargo I ever carried.

"Depth on the *Meridian*," I said, "is better than nine fathoms on a spring tide, and she's lying on her beam with her masts down-current. You'd need the navy's own hard-hat rig, and they haven't got one, or they'd not be asking."

Adeyemi shrugged the shrug of the empire, which is the shrug that says *not my department, not my dead, not my nine fathoms*. "You're the specialists," he said. "That's what the paper says. *Skilled local divers, suitable for recovery work.*" He said *recovery* the way the corporal had said *slow*, and I understood that the army had a whole vocabulary built for the purpose of describing our deaths in terms that would not alarm the auditors.

Nine fathoms. Free diving, breath held, in water cold enough to stop a heart that was already working too hard, on a wreck tangled in its own rigging, in current, in murk, to pull up guns that weighed as much as a horse and the bodies of men the sea had already claimed. I have known divers to die in four fathoms for a pearl worth a week's bread. The beds at least gave you a line and a partner and water you could read. The *Meridian* would give you a drowned ship's open hatches, sucking like mouths, and a quota, and a corporal with a slate.

It was a death sentence written in the polite language of requisition. *Skilled local divers, suitable for recovery work.* Suitable. The word sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

I looked down the shed at the faces. Old Pieter Sond, sixty-three, who could still hold his breath longer than men half his age and had buried two sons in the beds. The Brand brothers, who dove as a pair and had never in their lives been separated by more than a rope's length. Young Filip, seventeen, all shoulders and hunger, who had never seen a wreck except from above the water and thought them romantic. Forty men, and every one of them doing the same sum I was doing, and every one of them arriving at the same figure, and every one of them looking at me, because the ledger had drowned me three years ago and the army's list called me Jonas Kell, but the beach knew whose voice carried.

I had spent three years being no one. It is remarkable how much weight a no one can be made to hold, once the real someones have been flogged into silence or written off in ink.

"Finish your arrack, corporal," I said, "and give my regards to the paper."

He left. The shed stayed quiet a while longer. Then old Pieter Sond spat into the sand, which in the grammar of this cape is a complete sentence, and it said: *we know*.

* * *

I went to find Astrid at the tide pools, because there are two kinds of decisions in a man's life: the ones you make alone, and the ones you cannot make alone even though you will have to, and I could already feel which kind this was.

She was there at the low tide, in the place where we had found each other again after three drowned years, her skirts pinned up and her sleeves rolled, prizing limpets off the rocks with the efficiency of a woman who had learned that the sea's margins feed you when the ledgers don't. When she saw my face she stopped prying. That is one of the things I love about her, though the word *love* seems too small and too large at once, like trying to measure the harbor with a footrule: she reads faces the way she reads entries. She saw the figure at the bottom of my column before I said a word.

"They're going to press the divers for the salvage," I said. "The wrecks. All forty of them. Adeyemi let it slip."

She sat back on her heels. The tide filled the pool between us with its small busy sounds, indifferent and eternal, the way the sea is about everything.

"Nine fathoms," I said. "Free diving. The *Meridian*'s holds. They've written it up as *recovery work*." I heard my own voice go flat and hard and I let it. "Astrid, it's a butcher's list with a requisition number. They'll work through them until the weather turns, and the weather turns in six weeks, and there won't be forty men on this beach by then. There won't be twenty. Tammas Reeve got twelve strokes for coming up empty in bad water—what do you suppose happens to a man who comes up empty from a wreck? What do you suppose happens to a man who doesn't come up at all? They'll write *lost* in a column and move to the next name, and we both know how this town writes *lost*."

She was very still. Astrid is still the way deep water is still, and for the same reason.

"You've been slowing the beds," she said. "Buying days. I thought that was the whole of the risk."

"So did I. Slowing work is a quarrel. This is—" I looked out at the harbor, at the gray shapes of the wrecks out on the point with their broken masts showing at low water like the fingers of drowned men, which is exactly what they were. "This is the empire deciding that the cheapest way to raise its guns is with our lungs. And they'll call it suitable. There'll be a form. There'll be a signature."

"Whose signature?"

"That's the joke, isn't it." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "There's always a signature. The company had signatures. The ledger had signatures. My drowning had a signature—some clerk's hand, some comptroller's flourish, and I was dead for three years because ink is cheaper than a search party. Now there'll be a new signature on a new list, and it'll be a *naval* signature, and everyone will say well, at least it's official."

Astrid stood up. She stood the way she does when a decision has already happened somewhere behind her eyes and the rest of her is only catching up. I knew that posture. I had learned to fear it and lean on it in equal measure, because it meant she was about to do something that would cost her, and something that was right, and she had never once in her life believed those were different things.

"Then it has to go now," she said. "The ledger. The copies. Everything I've written—the drowned men, the debts, the pages the comptroller burned, Tammas's flogging, all of it. It has to be off this cape before the salvage order is signed, because after that, no ship leaves this harbor with anything aboard the army hasn't counted, and no one on this beach will be in a position to carry anything anywhere except oysters and guns." She turned to me. "You said it yourself. There's always a signature. Well, there has to be a counter-signature. Something in the world that says *this happened, and these were their names, and it was not suitable*."

"The harbor's sealed," I said. "You know it's sealed. Every crate since the storm goes through the soldiers' hands. They're searching for smugglers' goods and finding nothing, which makes them more certain than ever that there's something to find."

"Then we don't put it in a crate."

"Astrid—"

"The *Patience*," she said. "The neutral merchant. She sails on the spring tide—everyone on the waterfront knows it, which means the army knows it too. But a spring tide is a night tide, and the patrol boat can't watch black water, and the divers—" she stopped, and I watched her arrive at it, watched her understand what she was about to ask of my people, and watched her ask it anyway, because that was who she was. "The divers can put the patrol boat somewhere else. One night. One signal. One false alarm, one accident, one anything, and I row the ledger out to her in the dark."

I stood there with the salt wind coming off the pools and the wrecks showing their broken fingers out on the point, and I did the arithmetic I had been doing for three days, and for the first time the sum came out different. Not safe. Nothing on this cape was safe, and nothing on it ever would be. But different. A night's risk against forty men's lives. A signal against a signature. My people, who had spent their whole history being written down by other people's hands, becoming for one night the hand that held the pen.

"They'll flog us for it if it fails," I said. "They'll flog us for it if it *succeeds*, if they ever learn."

"Yes," she said.

"That's your whole argument? *Yes*?"

"It's the truest word I have." She looked at me, and her eyes were doing the thing they did when the fear and the resolve were in there together and neither one was willing to leave. "Elias. Tammas took twelve strokes without a sound because he's had a lifetime of practice at being still. You've had three years of practice at being dead. I've had a lifetime of practice at being invisible. None of us is going to get better at hiding than we already are. The only thing left to practice is being *seen*."

The tide was turning. It always turns; that is the one promise the cape keeps. I thought of old Pieter's spit in the sand, of the Brand brothers roped together, of Filip at seventeen who thought wrecks were romantic and was about to learn otherwise unless somebody did something, and I thought of a ledger with my name in it and the word *drowned* beside it, and of a girl with lantern-blackened fingers writing the true names into mended sails night after night because the world had never once written hers.

"All right," I said. "One night. One signal. I'll call the shed together after dark—Pieter, the Brands, the ones who'll stand. We'll give your patrol boat an accident it can't ignore." I looked out at the water, black already in the late light, patient as a ledger. "And Astrid—when you row out there, you row like every name in that book is pulling the oar. Because they are."

She took my hand, and her fingers were cold from the pools and steady as a signature, and far out on the point the *Meridian* showed her broken masts above the turning tide, and somewhere in the town a soldier was drawing up a list of suitable men.

We had six weeks once. Now we had one night.
