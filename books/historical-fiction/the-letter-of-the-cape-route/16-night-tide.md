## 16. Night Tide

There is a particular kind of silence a man learns underwater. It isn't the absence of sound — the sea is never silent; it groans and clicks and whispers against your ears like an old woman muttering over her knitting. It's the silence you make yourself: breath held, heart slowed, hands still. You learn it because the things that live in the dark are drawn to movement, and because the men above you on the boats don't count the divers who come up quiet.

I had been practicing that silence for three years. Ever since the ledger said I was dead and I decided, in my coward's wisdom, to let it stand.

Tonight the whole town practiced it with me.

* * *

It began, as most dangerous things do, with an old woman refusing to be reasonable.

Maren Skov had been diving since before I was born. Her lungs were shot, her hands shook so badly she had to tie her knife to her wrist, and she had buried two husbands and one son in the pearl beds off the point. When Elias Voss — a name I wore like a borrowed coat — laid out the plan in the smoke room behind Halvard's old drying shed, she listened all the way through, nodded once, and said:

"You'll need someone to scream."

"Maren—"

"I've been screaming at this town for forty years and nobody listens. Tonight they'll have to." She folded her arms across a chest like a barrel of salt pork. "Besides, if your false distress goes wrong and they hang whoever made it, better it be me. I'm worth less than a torn net."

That was the thing about the cape that no ledger ever recorded. The company wrote down what we owed them. Nobody ever wrote down what we owed each other. But the debt existed all the same, older than any charter, and tonight every soul in that smoke room — eleven divers, two net-menders, a boy who ran messages for the garrison and hated the garrison with the pure hatred only a fourteen-year-old can sustain — had decided to pay it.

The plan was simple, which is the only kind of plan that survives contact with soldiers.

The *Patience* lay at anchor beyond the mole, riding out her last two days before the spring tide took her east. The harbor mouth was sealed. A patrol boat — the *Vigilant*, a shallow-draught sloop crewed by men who had learned to hate the cape's fog as much as we loved it — crossed the channel every hour, lanterns sweeping the water. Every crate bound for any ship was searched. Every rowboat was counted at dusk and counted again at dawn.

But the navy could not search the sea itself.

At half past nine, a fishing skiff would put out from the north shore under oars — Maren Skov aboard, alone, visible, unmistakable. She would light the flare-pot we'd salvaged from a wrecked signal station, the one that burned green and foul and could be seen from the headland. Distress. Fire aboard. A woman alone on black water.

The *Vigilant* would come. She had to come — Brandt's orders were explicit about answering distress signals, because Brandt was the sort of officer who believed rules were load-bearing walls. He would find Maren, furious and unharmed, her "fire" a pot of burning pitch that had conveniently guttered out. He would waste an hour being lied to by a woman who had been lying to naval officers since before he was born.

And in that hour, Astrid Nystrom would row a mended sail full of names through the dark water to the *Patience*, and the truth would leave this harbor ahead of anyone who meant to bury it.

Simple. I kept telling myself it was simple. My hands didn't believe me. They hadn't stopped shaking since sundown.

* * *

I found Astrid at the tide pools an hour before midnight, where I always found her, where I had found her since we were children picking periwinkles off the rocks while our fathers' boats went out. She sat with her back against the basalt, and beside her lay the bundle: a sailcloth wrapped around perhaps sixty sheets of paper covered edge to edge in her mother's cipher, tight little hooks and strokes that looked like bird tracks on a frozen pond.

"You're late," she said.

"I'm early. Everyone else is late." I sat down beside her. The rock was cold enough to feel like a warning. Below us the harbor lay flat and black, the warships at anchor showing their riding lights like a string of patient, malevolent stars. Somewhere out there, the *Patience* rocked at her mooring, carrying a cargo of salt fish, dyewood, and — unknown to her master until tomorrow — the true history of this town.

Astrid was looking at the water, not at me. That was how I knew she was frightened. When she looked at you directly, she was performing calm. When she looked away, she was having the feeling itself.

"Say something," she said finally. "You've been rehearsing something all evening. You get a line between your eyebrows when you rehearse."

"That's not a line, that's a scar."

"Elias."

I took a breath. Underwater, I could hold it for four minutes. Above water, with her, I couldn't hold anything at all.

"If it goes wrong," I said, "if the patrol boat doesn't take the bait, or the fog comes in wrong, or one of Brandt's sentries hears your oarlocks — you swim for the north shore and you let the bundle go. Do you hear me? Paper sinks. You float. I have checked this arithmetic personally."

"Have you?" Now she looked at me, and there was that wry tilt to her mouth that had undone me at fifteen and was undoing me now, at an age when I should have known better. "Because from where I sit, the arithmetic runs the other way. If it goes wrong, you swim for the north shore and let *me* go. One of us has to survive to testify. That's the whole point of the exercise, isn't it? The record has to reach a living voice."

"The record reaches the *Patience* without either of us. That's the beauty of it. Paper doesn't need its author."

"No," she said quietly. "But it needs somebody who remembers what the cipher means. And right now that's me and a dead woman's notebook." She picked up the bundle and held it against her chest the way you'd hold a child, or a weapon. "Three years ago the ledger drowned you and you let it, because staying dead felt safer than being alive and hunted. I'm not letting the ledger drown this. Not tonight, not ever. So save your arithmetic, diver. I've done mine."

There was nothing to say to that. There rarely was, with Astrid. I had spent three years learning that the quiet ones keep the loudest accounts.

I did the only useful thing left. I reached into my shirt and brought out the cord I'd been wearing since the day I faked my own death — a diver's charm, a bit of shell and knotted twine, the kind we tie to our wrists before a deep descent — and I tied it around her wrist instead.

"For luck," I said. "It's worked for me. Mostly. I mean, I died, but I survived it."

She laughed — a short, wet sound, quickly swallowed — and then she kissed me, tasting of salt and ink, and for a moment neither of us was afraid, which I count among the great achievements of my life.

Then the chapel bell rang the half hour, and it was time.

* * *

The fog came in exactly wrong, which is to say it came in at all.

We had prayed for clear air — clear enough for the *Vigilant*'s lookout to see Maren's green fire, clear enough for the patrol boat to commit herself fully to the rescue. Instead a grey wool blanket rolled off the headland at eleven and settled over the harbor like a lid.

In the smoke room, faces turned to me. Eleven divers, two net-menders, one treasonous boy. Waiting for the man the ledger killed to tell them whether their night's work was wasted.

"Fog helps us," I said, and heard my own voice steadier than I felt. "Fog slows the *Vigilant*. She'll send her boat rather than weigh anchor. And a ship's boat in fog hugs its lantern, and a lantern can be seen a long way off." I looked at Maren. "Can you burn hot enough to punch through?"

"Boy," she said, "I once set fire to my own kitchen punching through less than this."

So we went.

I won't pretend I remember it cleanly. Memory underwater is a series of bright fragments separated by darkness, and that night was the same. Maren pushing off from the north shore with the flare-pot between her feet, small and straight-backed in the skiff, looking for all the world like a grandmother out for moonlight. The green flame going up at half past twelve — a sick, beautiful bloom in the fog, the color of deep-water phosphorescence, the color of drowning. Then voices. The creak of the *Vigilant*'s davits. A ship's boat pulling toward the fire, oars muffled, a lieutenant's voice calling *ahoy* into the murk with that particular naval hopefulness that believes every distress is a drill until proven otherwise.

And Maren's answer, floating back across the water, pitched perfectly between terror and indignation:

"Fire in the pitch-pot! Come and help me or come and watch, but hurry either way, I'm eighty-one years old!"

God love her. God forgive me for using her. She'd have said she didn't need either.

* * *

Astrid launched from the shingle below the old salt house, where the town's boats had been beached and counted at dusk. The counting had been done by a sentry who owed his mother's rent forgiveness to the net-mender Sigrid, and so the count had somehow come to one boat fewer than the army believed the town possessed. The whole town was her accomplice now. The boy who ran messages had reported the *Vigilant*'s watch rotation to us at supper. The cooper had left a barrel of "spoiled" herring standing open at the molehead so that any curious patrol would find a plausible reason for strangers on the beach. Even the widow who kept the tavern nearest the garrison had gotten the off-duty sentries drunk on credit, which she informed me cheerfully she intended to collect from heaven, since the company certainly wouldn't pay.

I was in the water before Astrid's keel cleared the shallows. That was my part — the part I had insisted on, the part she had argued against and lost, because some arguments a drowned man wins by default. I swam thirty yards off her beam, barely breaking the surface, breathing through a hollow reed like a boy playing at pirates, and I watched the black water around her boat for the flash of a patrol lantern.

She rowed the way she did everything: precisely, silently, without a single motion wasted. Sixty sheets of other people's lives rode in the bottom of that boat, wrapped in sailcloth and tarred canvas, and she pulled them through the dark like a ferryman of souls, which is, I suppose, exactly what she was.

Twice, a lantern swept the water near us. Twice, we stopped — her oars shipped, my body flat beneath the surface, the reed cold against my lip, watching the light slide across the swell like a searching eye. Once it passed so close I could hear the sailors talking inside it, complaining about the fog, about the cape, about a mad old woman and her burning pots. The navy's whole empire, and it could be blinded by one grandmother and a barrel of spoiled herring.

The third time the light came, it came from ahead — the *Patience*'s stern lantern, swinging gently at her mooring beyond the mole, and hanging from her side, dim and steady, the single lamp we had asked her second mate to show. A ship making ready to receive a pilot, nothing more. To any watcher, a routine light. To Astrid, the door at the end of everything.

I surfaced alongside her hull just long enough to say it.

"There's your ship."

She looked down at me treading water, hair plastered to my skull, probably resembling the drowned thing the ledger insisted I was, and she smiled the way people smile at funerals when they remember the deceased was a fool.

"Come with me," she said. "Elias. There's room. There's a whole ocean."

For one breath — one held, four-minute, diver's breath — I wanted it. The *Patience*, the east, a life where my name belonged to me again. Her hand reaching down from the gunwale, real as rope.

Then I thought of Maren out on the black water buying this hour with her bones. Of Tomas Lindqvist, flogged bloody last week for slow-working the beds, who would be pressed into wreck salvage within the month if no one spoke. Of forty names in a ciphered hand, and the ones not yet written. You cannot carry a town in a rowboat. I had done the arithmetic after all, and she had been right about which way it ran.

"Somebody has to be here," I said, "when the record comes home. Otherwise it's just paper that got away." I pushed her hull toward the lamp. "Row, historian. You're keeping the tide waiting, and the tide doesn't wait for anybody — ask any man in these beds."

She held my eyes one moment longer. Then she rowed.

I watched the boat shrink into the fog toward that patient light, and I turned and swam for shore, cold to the bone and warmer than I had been in three years.

* * *

They say — the boy told us, breathless, at dawn — that the *Vigilant* found Maren at half past one, scolded her for an hour, and towed her skiff home, whereupon she informed the boarding lieutenant that the navy's rescue was slower than the company's funeral barges and demanded compensation for her burnt pitch. By then Astrid was aboard the *Patience*, the bundle stowed in the second mate's sea chest beneath a layer of shirts, and the ship's papers listed her, at the master's raised eyebrow and the mate's smooth lie, as a passenger engaged at the last moment for passage east.

By then it was done. The truth was on the water, moving at four knots, wearing the flag of a neutral merchant house, and no requisition order in the empire could reach it.

I stood on the shingle as the sun came up grey through the fog, wringing the sea out of my shirt, and I let myself feel it for exactly as long as it takes to fill your lungs — the whole way, the ache and the triumph of it together.

Then the boy came pelting down the shingle path, white-faced, and the feeling ended.

"They've taken her quarters apart," he gasped. "The clerk — Wessel — he went to Lieutenant Brandt at first light. And sir—" he swallowed, "—they're saying he found a page. Unciphered. In her handwriting."

Behind me, out past the mole, the *Patience* rode at anchor, waiting for the spring tide, holding sixty pages of every name that mattered.

And walking down from the fort along the seawall, alone, unhurried, his coat buttoned to the throat against the fog, came Lieutenant Brandt — coming, whether he knew it yet or not, to decide what the truth was worth, and to whom.
