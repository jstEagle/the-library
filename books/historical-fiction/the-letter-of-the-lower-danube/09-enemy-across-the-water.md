## 9. Enemy Across the Water

The news came up the river the way bad weather does — first as a change in the gulls, then in the ferryman's face, then in the voice of the man who read the gazette aloud in the market square.

Ilinca heard it third-hand, from a fishwife who had heard it second-hand from the pilot of the *Corabia Mică*, who had heard it first-hand from a customs officer at Silistra with a nose like a plum and no talent for keeping his mouth shut behind closed doors. The Austro-Hungarian regiment that had wintered at Ruse had not gone home. It had not even stayed where it was. It had moved downriver, company by company, until its guns and its gray-green uniforms faced the town across the water like a man leaning on a fence to watch his neighbor's field burn.

And the officer commanding the outpost on the far bank — this was the part the fishwife relished, lowering her voice over her baskets of carp as though the river itself might carry gossip back — was one Anton Varga. A Hungarian. An officer of the Dual Monarchy. An enemy, now, in every way that paper could make one.

Ilinca stood in the market with a string bag of onions going soft in her grip and felt the word *enemy* settle over the square like frost forming on a window: slowly, invisibly, and all at once complete.

She thought of Wendell Delacroix, who had been in the workshop since dawn, sleeves rolled to the elbow, hands black with clock oil, bent over the master clock's open heart as though she could hold its pulse steady by will alone. She thought of the way Wendell went quiet when soldiers passed the observatory gate — not afraid, exactly, but folded inward, like a letter creased along old lines.

She thought, though she tried not to, of the packet Ilinca had seen once, weeks ago, wedged behind the escapement before Wendell reseated the plate over it too quickly, too smoothly, the way people do when they have practiced being unremarkable their whole lives.

*No,* Ilinca told herself, walking home through streets that smelled of woodsmoke and freezing mud. *You don't know what you saw. You saw paper. You saw a girl hide paper. Half the town hides paper — debt notices, love notes, letters from cousins in Bucharest asking for money.*

But the fishwife's voice followed her up the hill anyway. *Varga. The name is Varga.*

* * *

The observatory sat above the town like a lighthouse for stars, its dome catching the last copper light of afternoon. Inside, the great room smelled of brass polish and cold stone, and the master clock ticked with the uneven, arrhythmic stutter that had become so familiar Ilinca no longer heard it — except now she did, hearing it fresh, the way you notice a limp in someone you love only when a stranger points it out.

Wendell was on the ladder inside the case, head and shoulders swallowed by the mechanism, a lamp hooked on a bracket throwing her shadow enormous against the wall. Her tools lay on a cloth in strict order: files by size, pliers by size, the small screwdrivers ranked like soldiers at inspection. Everything about Wendell was order, Ilinca thought. Order was the wall she built, brick by precise brick, between herself and the world.

"You're back late," came Wendell's voice from inside the clock, muffled and echoing slightly, as if speaking from within a bell. "The onions are bruised."

"The onions are fine. You can't see them from in there."

"I can smell them." A pause, a metallic click. "Third wheel's wearing again. I've shimmed it twice. Shimming is lying, my master used to say. Sooner or later the truth comes out."

"What did your master say about talking to people while they're inside a clock?"

"That it's the safest place to do it. Nobody argues with a clockmaker mid-repair. They're too afraid she'll stop."

Ilinca set the onions on the workbench and did not sit, because sitting would mean staying, and staying would mean looking at Wendell's ink-stained fingers and the pale line where her collar usually hid a scar, and asking questions she had promised herself she wouldn't ask. She had been raised by a father who believed that curiosity was a form of respect — that to truly wonder about someone was to honor them — and by a mother, dead six years now, who had believed the opposite, and Ilinca had inherited both beliefs and no way to reconcile them.

"I heard news in the market," she said.

"Mm." Click. Clatter. A curse in French, soft and automatic, the language Wendell swore in when she forgot to guard herself.

"The regiment across the river. The one at Ruse. It's moved down. It holds the opposite shore now — the whole stretch, from the reed beds to the old mill. And the commander—"

The clicking stopped.

It was such a small thing, the stopping. The clock kept its broken time; the lamp flame swayed; outside, a crow argued with the wind. But Wendell's hands had gone still inside the machine, and Ilinca understood, with the sick clarity of a person watching ice crack underfoot, that she had known. Some part of her had known since the packet, since the seal, since the foreign postmark she'd glimpsed once on an envelope Wendell burned in the stove and watched to ash.

"—the commander," Ilinca finished, because finishing was better than retreating, "is named Varga."

Silence. Then Wendell backed out of the case into the lamplight, and her face was doing something complicated — the blankness sliding on like a visor, almost fast enough, not quite. There was a smear of oil across her cheekbone. Her eyes were the color of the river in November, gray-green and moving over things beneath.

"Varga," she repeated. "Is that supposed to mean something to me?"

"Yes," Ilinca said. "It is. Because you went white as the dial just now, and because you've been reading those letters at night when you think everyone's asleep, and because you burned one in the stove three weeks ago and watched it like it was a funeral. And because—" She made herself say it plainly; kindness sometimes required bluntness, her father always said, like setting a bone. "Because there's a packet hidden in the fourth wheel train with your master's seal on it, and I have known about it since October, and I have said nothing, and I am tired of saying nothing."

Wendell stood very still. Outside, the light failed another degree. Somewhere below in the town, the church bell rang the hour wrong — eleven minutes off, as it had been for months, the whole town living eleven minutes sideways — and the sound seemed to hang between them like a held breath.

"You could have reported me," Wendell said at last. Her voice was level. Too level. "Weeks ago. To your father. To Captain Sorin, who asks about me at the café like he's ordering coffee. Why didn't you?"

"Because I wanted to know first what I'd be reporting."

"And now?"

"And now I'm asking."

* * *

They went up to the keeper's kitchen, because Ilinca refused to have the conversation standing among gears like conspirators in a melodrama, and because her father was at the meteorological society meeting and would not be back till dark, and because the kitchen stove threw real heat and heat made honesty easier — or harder, which amounted to the same thing.

Wendell sat with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea she didn't drink. The firelight found all the places the workshop lamp had missed: the shadows under her eyes, the calluses ridging her palms, the way her shoulders curved inward as if the room were smaller than it was. She looked, Ilinca thought, like a person who had spent seven years fitting herself into spaces barely her size.

"My master's name was Émile Delacroix," Wendell said. "He took me as an apprentice when I was fourteen. He never asked where I came from, which should have been a warning, because men who don't ask have usually decided the answer doesn't matter, or already know it." She turned the cup a quarter-turn, precisely, the way she did everything. "He taught me wheels and springs and how to listen to a movement the way a doctor listens to a chest. And once a year — I thought it was a business trip, at first — he traveled downriver. Here. To this town. To this clock."

"He and Anton Varga repaired it together," Ilinca said quietly. "My father has told me. Before the borders hardened. They were friends."

"They were more than friends. They were—" Wendell searched for the word and rejected several. "They trusted each other with the insides of things. Varga knew Émile's politics, his debts, the name he wasn't born under. Émile knew Varga's doubts about the army, the brother he'd lost, the letters he wrote that he never sent. Every year they met here and wound this clock together and talked until dawn, and every year I waited at whatever inn we'd stopped at and wondered why my master, who trusted nothing to anyone, trusted this."

She reached into her waistcoat — Ilinca realized she had been carrying it, all this time, against her body like a second heartbeat — and laid the packet on the table between them. Oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, sealed with the stamp Ilinca had seen pressed into red wax a hundred times on the observatory's own correspondence: a wheel within a wheel, Émile Delacroix's mark.

"There are nine letters," Wendell said. "Written over twenty years. And a final one, written last spring, after Émile knew he was dying. His instruction to me was simple. If I ever learned where Varga was — if the chance ever came — I was to deliver it. By hand if possible. He said Varga would understand why it couldn't be posted." Her mouth twisted. "He said a great many things as if the world of 1890 were still the world. As if rivers didn't close. As if friends didn't become enemy officers."

Ilinca looked at the packet and did not touch it. "Do you know what's in the last one?"

"No. He sealed it himself. I know only that he wrote it knowing he was dying, and that he asked me — me, out of everyone, me who hides from my own shadow — to carry it across a border in wartime." Wendell laughed, short and without humor. "Perhaps he mistook me for someone brave. Perhaps dying men flatter their apprentices."

"Or perhaps he knew exactly what he was doing."

"Then he was crueler than I thought."

Outside, full dark had come. The wind had shifted north and carried the smell of snow. Down in the town, lamps were being lit one by one, and beyond them the river ran black and broad, and beyond the river, invisible, campfires of men with rifles faced them across the water.

Ilinca thought about treason. The word had a shape in her mind, learned from newspapers and her father's careful talk: it was a large, cold, official thing, done by large cold official people, punished by firing squads in courtyards at dawn. It had never occurred to her that treason might be the size of a packet of letters, might smell of clock oil, might sit at her kitchen table with chapped hands around an untouched cup of tea, looking at her as if Ilinca's answer were the last door in a corridor of doors, most of them already locked.

"If Sorin finds this," Ilinca said, "he won't care what's in the letters. He won't read past the seal. Carrying correspondence to an enemy officer is treason — not nearly-treason, not technically-treason. Treason. They'd take you to Galați. There wouldn't be a trial anyone attended."

"I know."

"Do you? Because you sit in that workshop like the worst that can happen is the clock stops. Wendell. Listen to me." She leaned forward. "If he finds it, they won't only take you. They'll ask who helped you. Who fed you. Whose roof you slept under. They'll come to this house, and they'll ask my father what he knew, and my father — my honest, decent, hopeless father — will not lie well. Do you understand what you're carrying? It isn't a letter. It's a lit fuse, and you're storing it in our walls."

Wendell set down the cup. For a moment her composure cracked clean through, and underneath it Ilinca saw not fear but something worse — guilt, arriving late and finding everything already ruined.

"I could leave it," Wendell said. "Sink it in the river. Burn it, like the others. No one would ever know. The promise dies with me and no one is harmed." Her jaw worked. "That's what the sensible part of me says every night. And then I hear him dictating it — he was too weak to hold the pen, I wrote under his direction, he made me read it back twice — and I hear him say, *she'll get it there, Varga, I promise you she will*, and I realize he wasn't talking to me at all. He was making a promise to a dying man's friend, using my hands. And I cannot—" Her voice caught and steadied. "I cannot be the reason two promises fail. I've spent my whole life breaking things by leaving. Not this."

The stove ticked. Snow had begun against the window, fine and dry, ticking also, a thousand tiny clocks.

Ilinca looked at the packet. She thought of her father's stories — the two young men laughing on the observatory stairs, arguing over the escapement, drinking plum brandy out of coffee cups because proper glasses were for strangers. She thought of the world that had made that friendship possible and the world that now forbade it, and which of those worlds she preferred, and what it cost to prefer it out loud.

She thought of Wendell's face when she'd said *you could have reported me*, and the naked astonishment under it — genuine astonishment, the astonishment of a person who had never once been chosen.

"All right," Ilinca said.

Wendell looked up.

"All right," Ilinca said again, angrier now, at the situation, at the century, at herself. "I won't report you. I should — any sensible loyal subject of the Crown would march you to Sorin's office tonight — and I'm not going to. But hear the terms, because I'm apparently the only person in this town who thinks ahead. First: the packet does not stay in the clock case anymore. Sorin's men sweep that building when I'm not there. Second: you tell me everything from now on — everything, Wendell, no more burning letters and pretending. Third—" and here her voice did something she hadn't authorized, dropping softer, "—third, you stop deciding alone what you're worth risking. That's not a promise you get to keep by yourself anymore."

Wendell stared at her. The firelight moved in her eyes. "Why?" she asked finally, simply, the question of a person to whom kindness arrived without paperwork. "Why would you risk this?"

Because you are infuriating and brilliant and you flinch when doors slam. Because you fixed Mrs. Petrescu's mantel clock for free and let her believe it was under warranty. Because I watched you stand in the snow for an hour teaching the baker's boy to file a flat surface and you never once checked whether anyone saw. Because I have known you four months and I cannot remember what the observatory sounded like before your footsteps.

"Because someone has to keep time in this town," Ilinca said, "and it clearly isn't going to be the clock."

Wendell laughed — really laughed, brief and startled out of her — and something in the room changed pressure, some valve opening, some winter loosening by a single degree.

Then boots crunched on the path outside, and the door opened on her father, stamping snow, cheeks red, mid-sentence about barometric pressure — and he stopped. He looked at the packet on the table. He looked at his daughter's face. He looked at Wendell, whose hand had already begun, instinctively, to move toward the oilcloth.

"No," the keeper said quietly, closing the door behind him. "Leave it where I can see it. Because if it's what I think it is, then God forgive us all — I've been waiting thirty years for that letter to arrive, and I want to know why it's sitting on my table next to the salt."
