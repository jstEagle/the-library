## 15. Sorin's Summons

Captain Emil Sorin had learned to read a town the way other men read weather.

He had been posted to the river for eleven years now — long enough to know that the Danube in November spoke in two voices: the low groan of water shouldering its way under the first skin of ice, and the higher, thinner sound of human worry, which traveled faster than any official dispatch. The town had been humming that second note for weeks. Rumors came upriver like fog off the water — regiments moved, mobilization papers signed in capitals whose names the gendarmes pronounced carefully, as if not to wake them — and every rumor settled here, in this narrow shelf of town between the bluff and the crossing, and thickened.

Sorin sat in his office above the customs house with the window cracked despite the cold, because he liked to hear the river while he worked. On his desk: a ledger of arrivals and departures grown suddenly thin since the bridge closed, a tin cup of coffee gone bitter and cold, and a single sheet of paper he had read four times without learning anything new from it.

*Request for verification of identity papers — Wendell Delacroix, clockmaker, apprenticed to one Aurel Stanciu (deceased), no record of residence prior to 1905. No travel documents on file. Response pending.*

Pending. The word sat there like a fishhook in his palm. In the capital, *pending* meant nothing; clerks buried requests under mountains of more urgent paper. But out here, where the bridge was shut and the ice was forming and an enemy regiment had dug itself into the far bank like a tick, *pending* meant that the woman up at the observatory existed in a kind of administrative fog — a person the empire could not quite confirm and could not quite dismiss.

Sorin did not like fogs. Fogs were where things happened.

He poured out the cold coffee, watched it steam briefly against the frost on the sill, and told himself — as he had told himself for three weeks — that there was nothing strange about a clockmaker with irregular papers. Half the artisans on the river drifted across borders the way silt drifted across channels. Men changed names the way they changed coats. It meant nothing.

It meant nothing, except that she read foreign postmarks. Except that she stood at the observatory rail in the blue hour before dawn, studying the far bank through a spyglass she claimed to be using to check the clock faces of the church tower — a tower whose clock, everyone knew, had been dead for a year. Except that her master, the late Aurel Stanciu, had once kept company with men on both sides of the water, back when the border was a line on a map instead of a line of bayonets.

And except that when Sorin passed her on the street, she looked at him the way a mouse looks at a hole in the wainscoting — not with fear, exactly, but with the precise, calculating attention of a creature measuring its exits.

He had been a soldier long enough to know that look. He had worn it himself, once, in another country, in another uniform, before the borders rearranged themselves around him and made his home into his posting.

Sorin put on his cap, took his stick, and went out into the cold to do the thing he had been putting off for three weeks.

* * *

The observatory sat at the top of the bluff like a stone head turned toward the water, its dome frosted white, its lower windows lit against the early dark. The climb was steep and the wind at the top came straight off the river with nothing to slow it, and by the time Sorin reached the door his mustache had frozen stiff and his bad knee — the left one, the one that remembered a cavalry charge in '04 — had filed its usual complaint.

The keeper's daughter opened the door before he knocked.

Ilinca. He knew her the way he knew all of them — the ferryman's debts, the priest's doubts, the innkeeper's second set of books — because knowing a town was his trade. She was perhaps twenty-four, built like her father, with a face that gave away nothing it didn't choose to give. Most people found her blunt. Sorin found her careful, which was different, and more interesting.

"Captain." Not *good evening*. Not *what brings you*. Just his title, laid on the doorstep like something she'd rather not pick up.

"Miss Ilinca. Is your father in?"

"He's at the instrument. You can shout down the stairwell if it's urgent."

"It isn't urgent." He let the pause stretch just long enough. "It's Miss Delacroix I've come about."

Something moved behind her eyes — quick, controlled, gone. "She's working. The clock doesn't repair itself."

"No," Sorin agreed. "Though from what the town says, it's been trying to." He stepped back from the doorway, out of the light, and made his voice mild. "I'll need a quarter hour of her time. Down at my office, if she prefers. Or here, if she prefers. Preference is a luxury I'm happy to extend."

"And if she doesn't prefer either?"

"Then I'll extend less pleasant alternatives." He said it without heat, the way one remarks on the weather. "You know how the season is, Miss Ilinca. Everyone's patience is shorter than the days."

She held his gaze for a moment longer than politeness allowed — testing him, or testing herself — then stepped aside. "Wait there."

She left him in the entry hall, among the smell of lamp oil and cold brass, and went up the stairs two at a time. From somewhere above, muffled by floors, he heard the low exchange of voices. He could not make out words. He didn't need to. He had detained enough people in eleven years to know what a household sounded like when it closed ranks around one of its own: the sudden hush, the rapid murmur, the small domestic machinery of protection engaging gear by gear.

*One of its own,* he thought, and found the phrase had already lodged in him like a splinter. The Delacroix woman had been in town barely two months. And yet the keeper's daughter climbed those stairs like a soldier answering an alarm.

The clockmaker came down alone.

Up close, in the lamplight, Wendell Delacroix was smaller than he remembered — a compact figure in a man's coat too big at the shoulders, hands stained faintly with brass polish, dark hair cut short in a way that was practical rather than fashionable. Her eyes went to his face, then to his stick, then to the door behind him — the exits, always the exits first — and then came back and fixed themselves into stillness.

"Captain Sorin."

"Miss Delacroix. Thank you for receiving me." He gestured toward the sitting room, where the keeper's wife had left bread and a cooling samovar, domestic evidence everywhere, a family mid-evening. "Shall we sit?"

"I'd rather stand."

"Of course." He remained standing too. It seemed only fair. "I'll be brief. Your papers."

"What about them?"

"They're irregular." He said it plainly; there was no kind way to dress it. "No travel document. No residence permit older than seven years. Your apprenticeship papers name you as ward of Aurel Stanciu, but there is no record of you before 1905 — no birth registration I can find, no school record, nothing. To the ministry, you are a person who appeared out of thin air."

"People do," she said. "War does it to them."

"Yes." He let that stand a moment. "And war makes us careful about such people. That's all this is. A formality. I've sent to the capital for verification. Until it comes—" He paused, and chose the next words the way he'd choose footing on river ice. "Until it comes, I'm obliged to ask you some questions. Here, informally. Or at my office, formally. Your choice."

Her hands had gone quiet at her sides — too quiet, the stillness of someone holding something very carefully closed. "Ask."

"You were born—"

"Wendell Delacroix. My mother was French, my father — I don't know. My mother worked the river towns. She died when I was nine. Master Stanciu took me in after." Each sentence delivered flat and complete, like items read from an inventory. "There's nothing before that worth recording."

"There's always something before."

"Not always," she said, "worth recording."

From the stairwell came a creak — Ilinca, standing in the shadow of the turn, listening. Sorin pretended not to notice. It was better for everyone that she believed he hadn't.

"Your master," he said, "kept a correspondence. Letters. Some of them foreign."

The change in her was almost invisible — a fractional tightening around the eyes, a breath taken in and not released. Almost invisible. Sorin had spent eleven years training himself to see almosts.

"A master clockmaker writes to parts suppliers in three countries," she said. "That's hardly a correspondence. That's commerce."

"It can be both." He softened his voice, and meant the softening, which was the dangerous part. "Miss Delacroix, I'm going to tell you something I don't tell many people. I don't want to arrest you. Arresting you would be easy — easier than this conversation, certainly. What I want is to understand why a woman with no history reads postmarks through a dead spyglass at dawn, so that when the capital answers me — and they will answer me, eventually, with either a clean file or a warrant — I know which of those two papers I'm holding."

For a long moment she said nothing. The samovar ticked as it cooled. Somewhere below, the river groaned against its new ice.

"The spyglass," she said finally, "is because the church tower clock has a broken minute hand, and from the observatory rail it's the only angle where you can see that it hangs wrong. I look at it every morning because it irritates me. That's the whole of my espionage, Captain. A crooked minute hand."

It was a good answer. It was even, he suspected, partly true. Good answers usually were.

"And the letters?"

"Are in my master's handwriting, addressed to a supplier in Vienna, concerning escapements." Her voice did not waver. "Would you like me to fetch them? They're riveting reading."

They were not in Vienna. He was nearly certain they were not in Vienna. And she knew he was nearly certain, and he knew she knew, and between those two certainties stretched the whole narrow, frozen span of the conversation, each of them standing on their own bank of it.

"No," he said quietly. "Not tonight."

He took out his notebook, wrote a few lines slowly enough for her to watch him write them — date, time, statement given voluntarily, subject cooperative — and closed it.

"Cooperative," she repeated, when he'd gone.

"For now." He put on his cap. At the door he stopped, hand on the latch, and spoke without turning around. "Miss Delacroix. When I was your age I crossed a border in the wrong direction, with papers that would not have survived a candle held behind them. Someone chose to believe me anyway. It's the only reason I'm standing in this room." He glanced back at her. "I am telling you this so you understand I am not your enemy. But I am also telling you so you understand what it costs me to say it. If there is something in that clock case besides escapements — bring it to me yourself. Before I have to come for it. There are kinds of mercy, and only one of them arrives in time."

He heard her breath catch — a small, involuntary sound, quickly mastered.

"Good night, Captain," she said.

"Good night, Miss Delacroix."

* * *

Outside, the cold hit him like a wall of water. The wind had risen while he was indoors, dragging fine ice dust off the river and flinging it against the bluff, and the stars had gone smudged and watery behind fast-moving cloud. Below him the town lay in its shallow bowl of lamplight, chimneys smoking, dogs barking at the wind, everything ordinary, everything asleep.

Except that as he started down the hill, the observatory door opened above him, spilling yellow light onto the frost, and two figures came out onto the step — the keeper's daughter and the clockmaker, standing close together against the cold, heads bent toward each other over something held between them. Even at this distance, even in the dark, Sorin could see the shape of it: not a part, not a tool. Paper. An envelope, or several, fanned open like a hand of cards.

They stood there a long moment. Then Ilinca said something — he couldn't hear the words, only the shape of them, urgent, low — and Wendell shook her head, and Ilinca took the letters out of her hands and held them against her own chest, and the two of them stood shoulder to shoulder looking out at the black river as though it might answer a question neither of them would ask aloud.

Sorin stood in the wind and watched them, and felt the old familiar ache of the man who watches — the loneliness of the office, the ledger, the window cracked to hear the river because no one else in the house wanted to talk to him at the end of the day.

*Bring it to me yourself,* he had said. *Before I have to come for it.*

They would not bring it. He had known it before he finished saying it. People never surrendered the things they loved to save themselves; they only surrendered themselves to save the things they loved. He had watched it happen in barracks and border posts for eleven years, and it never once went the way the regulations assumed.

He turned up his collar and continued down into the town.

At the bottom of the hill he stopped at the guard post by the customs house, where young Corporal Munteanu was warming his hands over a brazier and pretending not to be asleep.

"Corporal."

"Sir!"

"Two watches a night on the observatory road. One at the foot of the bluff, one at the bend by the chapel. Nothing formal — no posts, no lanterns. Just eyes." He counted coins out of his pocket for the brazier coal. "If anyone goes down to the river after dark — anyone, but especially a small person in a large coat — I want to know before their footprints cool."

Munteanu wrote it down, tongue between his teeth. "Trouble at the observatory, sir?"

Sorin looked back up the hill. The light in the observatory windows burned steady against the dark, and the wind carried down the faintest thread of sound — the two voices inside, rising and falling, arguing or planning or simply talking, the way people talk who have decided, without ever saying it, that whatever comes will come to both of them together.

"No trouble, Corporal," Sorin said. "Just time running short. Go and get warm."

He walked home through the freezing streets, past the shuttered inn, past the church with its crooked minute hand hanging in the dark like a wound that wouldn't close, and behind him the net he had ordered drawn around the observatory settled into place, silent and patient as the ice coming down the river — and far upstream, in a capital full of sleeping clerks, a file was moving through the night toward a decision that would arrive, as all such decisions did, sooner than anyone wanted and later than it mattered.
