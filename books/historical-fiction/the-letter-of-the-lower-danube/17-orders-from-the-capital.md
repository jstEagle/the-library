## 17. Orders from the Capital

The telegram came on a Tuesday, which Ilinca would remember afterward because Tuesdays were when her father wound the regulator in the front hall, and she was standing beside him with the key in her hand when the boy from the post office clattered up the observatory steps with his breath smoking and his cap in his fist.

"Captain Sorin's compliments," the boy said, though his eyes said nothing of the kind. His eyes said: *I am sorry, and I was told to say compliments.*

Her father took the envelope without hurry. That was the thing about Papa — he had hands that could dismantle a chronometer in the dark, and they never trembled at paper. He read the telegram once, standing in the hall where the light came down gray through the cupola glass, and then he folded it along its crease and put it inside his waistcoat, over his heart, as if it were a letter from someone who loved him.

"Well," he said.

"Papa." Ilinca heard her own voice come out flat and hard, the way it did when she was most afraid. "Well *what*."

"The capital has answered him." He looked past her, through the frost-ferned window, toward the river. "About the Delacroix woman's master. About the school in Vienna."

Outside, the town went about its morning as if the world were ordinary. A cart groaned past on the frozen cobbles. Somewhere below the hill, the market women were arguing about salted fish. The master clock ticked above them all in its tower room, steady as a pulse, because Wendell had made it so — Wendell, who had been among them since autumn, who had machined a new escape wheel by lamplight until her fingers cracked and bled at the knuckles, who had eaten at their table and lost to Ilinca at cards and laughed, once, actually laughed, at something Ilinca's little brother had said about the priest's dog.

*They have confirmed it,* the telegram had said, in the bloodless grammar of ministries. *Investigation opened 1909. Subject deceased before conclusion. Recommend detention of associate pending review.*

*Associate.* As if Wendell were a loose gear that had rolled out of some dead man's works.

"She repaired their clock," Ilinca said. Her voice cracked on the second word and she hated it. "She gave them back their time. The whole town — they rang the bells for her—"

"Yes." Her father wound the regulator anyway, because the regulator did not care what the capital thought, and there was a lesson in that which Ilinca was too frightened to learn just then. "And Captain Sorin has his orders, and orders do not care either. He'll come quietly. That's how men like Sorin do it — no gendarmes on the steps, no scene. He'll knock, and smile, and ask her to come answer a few questions, and she will understand exactly what it is, and so will he, and nobody will have said anything true out loud."

"When?"

"He'll wait for dark. Men like Sorin believe shame needs darkness." Her father set the key down on the hall table with a small precise click. "Which gives us the day."

Ilinca stared at him. "Gives *us*—"

But he was already climbing the stairs toward the tower room, and after one breath of standing alone in the hall with her heart going like an unbalanced flywheel, she followed him.

* * *

Wendell was inside the clock case.

That was where she always was now, in these last weeks of winter — half-swallowed by the great oak body of the machine, sleeves rolled, a jeweler's lamp hooked over the framework so that its light fell into the gears like water into a well. She had her ear pressed near the escapement, listening the way other people listened to music or to confessions. She did not hear them come up the tower stairs; she heard nothing but the clock, and Ilinca understood suddenly, watching her, that this was the only place in the world where Wendell Delacroix felt safe enough not to listen.

"Wendell." Her father said it gently.

Wendell backed out of the case, blinking, a smear of oil across one cheekbone, a pair of pliers still in her hand like a small silver bird. When she saw their faces she went still in a way Ilinca had learned to dread — the stillness of an animal that has spent its whole life learning what stillness is worth.

"What is it?" Wendell asked. "What's happened?"

"The capital wrote back to Sorin," Ilinca said. No gentleness in her; she had never learned it, and there was no time to learn it now. "About your master. They investigated him, Wendell. Before he died. Two years of files, and now Sorin has them, and he has orders about you."

The pliers did not fall. Ilinca had expected them to fall. But Wendell's hand simply closed around them until the knuckles went white, and she set them down on the workbench with exaggerated care, the way you set down something when you are afraid that if you do it any other way your whole body will come apart.

"Investigated him," Wendell repeated. "For what? He was a clockmaker. He was — he mended watches for nuns. He—" She stopped. Ilinca watched her run through everything she knew about the man whose tools she carried, whose name she had taken, whose silence she had inherited, and watched her arrive, as Ilinca herself had arrived weeks ago, at the letters hidden behind the escapement. At Anton Varga. At the promise. "Oh," Wendell said softly. "Oh, it was the correspondence. It was always the correspondence. They knew about Varga even then."

"It doesn't matter what they knew," her father said. "What matters is that Sorin will be here tonight, and he will be polite, and he will take you away, and once you are in a cell in the capital no one in this town will be able to tell them what you actually are. Which is a girl who fixed our clock."

"Then I should go now." Wendell was already moving, already reaching for the canvas roll of tools, and her voice had gone quick and thin and practiced, and Ilinca recognized it with a lurch — it was the voice from the first week, from before the suppers and the cards, the voice of a person packing a life into a bag she'd kept ready by the door. "There's the ice road, the ferryman said—"

"No," Ilinca said.

"—the ice is thick enough now, he told us himself, if I go this afternoon—"

"*No.*" It came out louder than Ilinca intended, loud enough to startle the pigeons under the eaves, and she crossed the tower room and took the tool roll out of Wendell's hands and held it against her own chest like a stolen thing. "You'll walk onto that river in daylight with Sorin's men watching every crossing since the bridge closed, and they'll take you on the ice where there's no one to see and no one to ask questions after. Is that what you want? To vanish? Because that's what vanishing looks like from the outside, Wendell. It looks like exactly what they'll say happened."

"And what's the alternative?" For the first time since Ilinca had known her, Wendell raised her voice too, and it broke in the middle like a dropped plate. "He comes tonight and takes me and you two stand here and watch? You'll be implicated just for — for feeding me, for God's sake, Ilinca, you don't owe me—"

"I know what I owe you," Ilinca said, and the words came out of her before she could weigh them, before she could calculate the cost, which was the entire point of them. "Nothing. You're right, I owe you nothing. I'm not doing this because of debts."

The tower room went very quiet. The clock ticked. Down below, faintly, the town breathed its smoke and its cart-wheels and its church bells, all of it running true now because of the woman standing in front of her with oil on her face and terror in her eyes.

"Then why?" Wendell whispered.

"Because you stayed," Ilinca said. "When the wheel cracked and everyone said send for a man from Bucharest, you stayed. When the frost took the workshop and my father's hands were too old for the lathe, you stayed. Everyone in this town leaves, Wendell. My brother left. The engineers leave, the officers leave, the river itself leaves every winter and comes back because it has no choice. You had a choice every single day since autumn and you stayed and you *mended things.*" She heard her own voice shaking and let it shake. "So no. I'm not watching them carry you off into the dark while I stand in a doorway being innocent. We do this together or not at all."

Her father cleared his throat. When Ilinca turned, he was looking at the great clock case with an expression she had never seen on him before — not the craftsman's fondness, but something older and sadder, the look of a man greeting a grave he dug himself.

"There is a place," he said, "where those letters began. And there is a logic to it that Sorin, for all his cleverness, may not think to defeat. Come. Both of you. We have until nightfall, and we will need every hour of it."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon with the shutters closed.

It was strange, Ilinca thought, how terror sharpened the hands rather than blurring them. Her father directed; Wendell and Ilinca executed. The plan was simple, which is to say it was the kind of plan that only works because it is too obvious: the letters would go back behind the escapement, into the cavity her master had built thirty years ago, wedged in the same gap between the great wheel and the case wall where Wendell had found them in the autumn. But not merely hidden — *installed*. Her father had Wendell cut a false backing for the maintenance plate, thin pine stained to match forty years of dust-darkened oak, screwed down with the same pattern of screws, aged with ash and tallow until even Ilinca, who knew, who stood there and watched her father do it, could not find the seam.

"If he opens the case," her father said, "he sees a clock. If he searches the workshop, he finds nothing, because there is nothing. If he takes the clock apart bolt by bolt—" he paused, tightening the last screw, "—then we are lost, but no one searches a clock that way, not in a few hours, not politely. Politeness is his method and his limit."

"And if he arrests me anyway?" Wendell asked quietly. She was holding the lamp again, steadying it for him, and her hand had stopped trembling somewhere in the second hour of work. Craft did that for her, Ilinca had noticed. The work gathered her up and made her whole. "On the papers. On the investigation. The letters won't matter if he doesn't need them."

"Then the letters stay here," her father said, "safe, waiting. And Ilinca and I will spend every coin and every favor we have getting word to Varga across the river, because a man who wept over your master's handwriting will move heaven and the Danube both for you. That is the shape of the promise, child. It protects in both directions, if we let it."

Wendell looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked at Ilinca, and something passed between them that had no words in any language Ilinca spoke — a recognition, maybe, that they had already crossed their own frozen river together, in the nights of oiling and confession, and that there was no far bank anymore where one of them could stand safe from the other's drowning.

"Finish the plate," Wendell said finally, and her voice was steady. "Then help me dress the clock. If Sorin comes, I want him to find a machine running so perfectly it insults him."

* * *

Dusk came down over the river like a lid.

Ilinca lit the lamps in the observatory's lower rooms and made supper she knew none of them would taste — bread, the last of the good cheese, tea brewed strong enough to strip paint. Wendell washed the oil from her face and hair and put on the clean blouse Ilinca's mother had given her at Christmas, the blue one, and sat with her hands folded on the table like a schoolgirl awaiting examination. Only her eyes moved, tracking the window, the door, the window.

At half past seven, boots on the stairs. Not hurrying. Never hurrying.

"Good evening," Captain Sorin said from the doorway, and he removed his hat, which was worse somehow than if he hadn't. Snow had gotten into the brim of it; he shook it off with a small apologetic motion. "Forgive the hour. And forgive me more for what follows, because I suspect you've all guessed it, and guessing it in advance is the cruelest part."

"You have your orders," Ilinca's father said. He did not rise.

"I have my orders." Sorin laid a folded document on the table, next to the untouched bread, and Ilinca saw Wendell look at it the way one looks at a snake. "Miss Delacroix. Or whatever name you are using this decade — no, don't. Don't perform surprise; it wastes both our evenings. There is a file in the capital with your master's name on it, and your name appears in the margins, and I am required to ask you to accompany me tomorrow morning to give a statement. You would return within the week, I am assured." He paused, and something moved behind his eyes that Ilinca had seen there before, on the night he'd questioned her at the ferry landing — not suspicion. Loneliness. The look of a man carrying out a sentence of his own. "I am also required to search these premises tonight. You understand that I cannot skip that part."

"Search," her father said, gesturing broadly at the room, the shelves, the stairs. "Of course. And the tower?"

"And the tower." Sorin's gaze lingered a half-second too long on Wendell's face, and then he put his hat back on, which meant the officer had replaced the man. "Begin downstairs, Corporal. I'll take the clock myself. I'm told it's the finest machine between Vienna and the sea. I should like to see the work."

Ilinca's heart stopped, started, stopped.

He climbed the tower stairs ahead of them, unhurried, his lamp raised, and in the tower room he set the light down on the workbench and stood before the great clock case the way a man stands before an altar — or a locked door. Wendell had dressed the mechanism that afternoon until it gleamed; the brass sang under Sorin's lamplight; the pendulum swung with the deep patient rhythm of something that would outlast them all.

"Beautiful," Sorin said quietly. "My grandfather kept a regulator like this. Smaller. He used to let me hold the key." He reached out and touched the case door, and Ilinca felt Wendell go rigid beside her, felt her own lungs refuse.

Sorin opened the case. He looked for a long time. The escapement flashed and caught, flashed and caught, keeping perfect time above the hidden letters, above the false pine plate stained with ash and tallow, above everything they had risked the whole day to build.

Then he closed the door, gently, the way one closes a coffin or a book one has decided not to finish, and turned around.

"A fine piece of work," Captain Sorin said. "Whoever maintains it loves it very much." He picked up his lamp. "Corporal! Nothing upstairs. We're done here. Miss Delacroix — eight o'clock tomorrow, the gendarmerie. Bring your tools if you like. You'll want something to do with your hands, where you're going."

At the top of the stairs he paused, his back to them, snowmelt dripping off his coat hem onto the stone.

"For what it's worth," he said, without turning, "I read the file twice. Your master was never charged with anything. The investigation died with him. Files like that have a way of... losing pages, when the right people ask." And then he was gone, boots descending, the front door closing behind him with a click soft as a screw set home.

For a moment none of them moved. The clock struck eight above their heads, each note falling into the silence like a stone into a well.

"Ilinca," Wendell said at last, very quietly, her eyes still fixed on the closed door. "How long does it take to reach the ferryman's house?"

Ilinca followed her gaze — past the door, past the walls, out into the dark where the river lay freezing and waiting, where the window of ice was open now and would not stay open long.

"Twenty minutes," she said. "If we leave now."
