## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The mule refused the last stretch of the hill, so Rafael walked the rest, his toolbox swinging against his hip like a second heartbeat. Below him, Valparaíso lay folded in its winter grays — the harbor a sheet of hammered tin, the ships at anchor riding low, patient as vultures. Somewhere down there was a berth with his name on it, if he could ever scrape together the fare. Somewhere down there was also a port captain who had a list, and his father's name on it, and his father's face on a wall, and Rafael's face — God help him — close enough to that face that strangers sometimes squinted at him on the calle and asked if they'd met.

He had learned to answer that question with a shrug and a different surname.

"Rafael Soto," he told the gatekeeper at the observatory, and the word came out smooth from years of use. "Sent for the clock."

The gatekeeper, a boy of maybe fifteen with ink-stained fingers, looked him over with the frank boredom of someone paid to notice nothing. "You're the third clockmaker they've sent. Don't get your hopes up. The last one cried."

"Did he fix it?"

"He cried first. Then he left." The boy waved him through. "It's at the top. Everything here is at the top."

The path climbed through eucalyptus, the wind coming off the sea with a salted edge that made Rafael think of Hull, of his mother's voice going thin in the English winters, of a house he had left at nine and a country that had never once asked after him since. He counted the steps instead of thinking about it. Clockmakers counted. It was cheaper than praying.

* * *

The master clock lived in a stone room at the heart of the observatory, a vaulted chamber that smelled of oil, cold brass, and something older — paper, maybe, or dust that had once been paper. The clock itself was a giant, a regulator built in Valparaíso's confident youth, its case of dark mahogany gone honey-colored at the edges where a century of hands had touched it. Its pendulum hung dead. Its face stared at nothing with the blind patience of a thing that had been told it was important too many times.

Rafael set down his toolbox and did what he always did first: he listened.

Silence, but not empty silence. The building breathed around him — wind in the tower above, a door somewhere, footsteps crossing a floor two rooms away. He put his ear to the case, near the movement, and heard what the other clockmakers had probably missed: not a broken escapement. A broken *tooth*. Somewhere in the going train, a wheel had lost a tooth, and every gear downstream had been compensating for years, wearing itself crooked, until the whole mechanism had simply refused.

"Who hurt you," he murmured, and opened the case.

The work took him out of himself the way it always did. That was the mercy of the trade — hands so busy the mind could rest. He removed the movement in stages, each part wrapped in cloth and laid out in order on the bench, and the order itself was a kind of language only he and the dead maker of the thing could speak. He was three hours in, sleeves rolled, a smear of oil across his forearm, when he reached the weight chamber at the base of the case and found the false back.

He found it because it was too clean.

Everything else in the chamber wore its years — grime in the corners, the ghost-rings of old weights, a dead spider curled like a comma. But one panel of the inner wood sat a half-shade lighter than the wood around it, its edges crisp, as though it had been cut recently and fitted carefully and oiled just enough to slide.

Rafael sat back on his heels.

He had grown up in a house where hiding places were discussed at dinner. His father had taught him that every respectable piece of furniture had a second life inside it — that the world was full of drawers within drawers, and that a man who could not keep a secret kept nothing at all. Rafael had spent twenty years trying to forget those lessons. His hands had not.

He pressed the panel. It gave, slid, whispered open.

Inside: letters. A bundle of them, tied with a faded ribbon gone the color of weak tea, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its work. Perhaps thirty. Perhaps forty. He did not count them. He could not, because he had seen the direction of the topmost address before his mind could stop his eyes, and the handwriting on it reached out of the compartment and took him by the throat.

He knew that hand. He had learned his letters under it. He had been beaten — gently, lovingly, with a ruler — for the way his *r*s looped, and had spent a childhood trying to make his writing look like his father's, and had succeeded so well that at fourteen he had signed a school ledger with a name not his own and no one had questioned it.

*To the Director of the Observatorio Astronómico de Santiago,* the letter said. *Sir — I write to you a fourth time, though I doubt this fourth will fare better than the others...*

His father's hand. His father's formal, over-corrected Spanish, the Spanish of a man who had learned it late and never trusted himself with it. His father's *fourth time* — meaning there had been a first, a second, a third, a whole correspondence Rafael had never known existed, written years after the trial, years after the exile, years after the night his father had walked down to a ship in this same gray harbor and not come back.

Rafael did not touch the letters.

That was the discipline. That was the whole of his life's discipline, learned in a house of secrets: when you find a thing that is not yours, you put your hands in your pockets and you walk away and you never come back. He stood, wiped his hands on his apron, and told himself he would finish the wheel, collect his fee, and go.

Then he sat back down and dismantled the escapement, because his hands needed work, and because the compartment was *inside the clock he had been hired to repair*, and because — this was the thought that arrived last and stayed longest — the panel had been oiled. Recently. By someone who wanted it to open easily.

Someone had cut that compartment. Someone had placed those letters where a clockmaker — a clockmaker summoned to this room, in this winter, in this year — would be the one man in all of Chile with both the skill and the reason to find them.

The wind rose against the tower. Somewhere above him, the great dome groaned on its track like a ship in a swell.

Rafael picked up a file and went to work on the replacement tooth he would have to cut from blank brass, and he worked for a long time without looking at the letters, and the letters sat in their dark compartment three feet from his elbow, patient as the sea, waiting for him to be brave or tired enough to read.

He was neither, he told himself. He was a man named Soto with a wheel to fix.

But when the light through the high windows went from gray to the color of old brass, and he finally, finally untied the ribbon with fingers that would not stay steady, the first line of the first letter stopped his breath:

*My son, if you are reading this, then Inés has kept her promise, and you have kept yours, and I am prouder of you than I have ever had the right to say.*

Outside, the first thunder of the coming storm rolled up the valley, and far below, the bridge that was the observatory's only road to the city stood swaying on its cables like something deciding whether to hold.
