## 2. What the Bridge Keeps Out

The storm arrived the way bad news arrives — first a rumor on the wind, then all at once.

Rafael heard it before dawn: a groaning from the direction of the estuary, deep and structural, like a giant testing a lock. By the time he climbed to the observatory's eastern gallery, the bridge was gone. Not destroyed — merely closed, its iron arms folded against a swell that hurled driftwood and half a fishing skiff against its pilings. On the far shore, the quarantine flag had already climbed the customs house mast: yellow square beneath black, the port sealed until further notice.

He stood there with his hands in his pockets and did the arithmetic of a trapped man. No bridge. No boats willing to cross that water. No letters out, no wires through. The observatory sat on its hill like a held breath.

"Breakfast is at seven," said a voice behind him. It was Tomás, the young clerk with ink-stained cuffs and the permanent expression of a man who had mislaid something important. "Doña Inés insists. She says men who skip breakfast make errors in declination."

"I'm not an astronomer."

"Nobody here is what they say they are." Tomás shrugged cheerfully and went back down the stairs.

* * *

The letters were still in his coat pocket. He had not slept for thinking of them.

Eleven envelopes, tied with string gone soft as cloth, each addressed in a hand Rafael knew better than his own — because it was the hand that had taught him his own. His father wrote his *r*s like small waves. Rafael had spent his childhood imitating those waves, and had spent the years since scrubbing them from his penmanship entirely, until his writing looked like no one's at all.

The letters were unsent. No stamps, no postmarks. And they had been inside the clock case — inside *his* clock case, behind a panel only a clockmaker would think to open — wrapped in oilcloth that had been replaced recently. The oilcloth was new. That was the detail that kept him awake. Someone had maintained those letters. Someone had expected them to be found.

At breakfast he watched the staff over his cup: Doña Inés at the head of the table, reading a barometric chart as though it were scripture; old Fermín the cook, who salted everything twice and apologized for neither; Señora Aroa from the records office, who corrected Tomás's arithmetic without looking up from her toast. They moved around each other like gears in a train — worn smooth, meshing without complaint. Twenty years, some of them, in this building above the gray water.

Rafael had never stayed anywhere twenty years. He had never stayed anywhere two.

"You're staring at your bread," Doña Inés said, not raising her eyes from the chart. "It hasn't done anything to you."

He put butter on it to prove her wrong.

* * *

Inés Okafor de Vergara had known who he was from the moment he signed the work order.

*Rafael Serrano.* The boy had written the false name with a certain grim fluency, the way liars do when the lie has become muscle memory. But she had seen Sterling hands before — her father's colleague Tomás Sterling had those same long fingers, that same habit of holding a gear up to the light as if interrogating it. And she had seen the way the boy flinched at the word *observatory*, as if the building itself might recognize him.

She had been twenty-three when the arrests came. She remembered the wagons, the neighbors suddenly studying their shoes, the way her father burned papers in the kitchen stove while insisting loudly that he was doing nothing of the kind. Tomás Sterling had been taken in the second wave. His wife had fled north with the children. Inés had been the one who carried the last bundle of his letters out of the city — testimony, sworn statements, pages that would have hanged him faster if found, and might yet clear him if ever the country grew honest enough to listen. There had been nowhere safe to keep them except somewhere no policeman would ever look.

So she had hidden them in the master clock, in the compartment Tomás himself had built years earlier for smuggling out instruments during the first purges. A joke between the two of them, once. Now a reliquary.

For twenty years she had oiled the hinges and waited for the country to change, or for a Sterling to come. She had not expected both to fail and arrive together.

* * *

The storm made prisoners of them all, and prisoners, Rafael discovered, invent their own society.

Fermín taught him to make sopaipillas in the afternoon because "a man who can't fry dough shouldn't be trusted with machinery." The astronomers argued about a comet nobody could see through the cloud deck, with the passion of men defending a lover's honor. Señora Aroa recruited him to re-ink the rainfall ledgers, and he found, to his horror, that he liked it — the columns, the quiet, the way she said *good* when a number came out clean, as if cleanliness were a moral achievement.

He kept waiting for the trap to spring. For someone to ask about his father, about the name he wasn't using, about why a clockmaker from Santiago would take a commission in a storm season. He kept his answers short and his hands busy. Busy hands were the family religion.

On the third night, the lamp in the clock room was burning when he came down. Doña Inés sat on the stool beside the dismantled movement, and in her lap lay the string-tied bundle.

His stomach dropped through the floorboards.

"You opened the case," she said. Not an accusation. An inventory.

"The escapement needed—"

"Rafael." Just the name. His true one, spoken quietly, in a room with no witnesses but a clock.

Every instinct he owned screamed at once — run, deny, lie, lie bigger. He had rehearsed this moment his whole life and discovered, standing in it, that he had rehearsed nothing at all. "I don't know what you're talking about."

"He wrote his *r*s like little waves," she said. "You write yours like fences. I've often wondered which cost you more."

He sat down on the floor. It wasn't a decision; his legs simply resigned. From down there the great clock loomed above them both, its open heart gleaming brass in the lamplight.

"Who are you?" he asked.

"Someone your father trusted more than he trusted God, which he told me was saying something." She set the bundle in Rafael's hands. "Twenty years I kept these where only a Sterling would find them. I couldn't send them — the addresses were death warrants. I couldn't burn them — they're the only proof of what happened. So I waited for the country to grow honest, or for you to come. I'd have settled for either."

"Why tell me now? Why not let me leave with my lies?"

"Because the bridge is closed," she said simply, "and the port is sealed, and you cannot run for a few days yet. I thought you might spend them learning what it feels like to be known by people who aren't holding knives."

He looked down at the letters. His father's waves, undulating across twenty years.

"What happens," he asked, "when the bridge opens?"

"That depends," said Doña Inés, rising, "on what the tide brings in. Rest. Tomorrow we talk about your sister."

He froze. "I never mentioned a sister."

"No," she agreed, switching off the lamp. "But your father did. Goodnight, Rafael."

* * *

He did not sleep. He sat by the dark window watching the storm hammer the headland, the letters heavy in his lap, the word *sister* ringing in him like a struck bell.

Below, the sea kept throwing itself at the closed bridge, and the bridge kept refusing it, and somewhere in the clock room the pendulum swung on in the dark, keeping a time none of them had chosen.
