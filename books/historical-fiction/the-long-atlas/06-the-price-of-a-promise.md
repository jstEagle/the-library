## 6. The Price of a Promise

The meeting house smelled of tar, tallow, and old rope — the smell, Ines thought, of every kindness Captain Voss had ever done. It was a net-loft above a chandlery on Cordage Lane, reached by a stair that groaned like a man confessing. Eleven people sat in a half-circle of crates and upturned barrels, lamplight catching the salt still crusted in their collars. They had come at Marta Oduya's word, one by one, by different streets, at different hours, the way water finds its way downhill without ever appearing to move.

Ines stood at the center with the atlas open across her knees.

She had not meant to show it whole. But when she'd unwrapped it from its oilcloth, the room had gone quiet in a way that demanded honesty, and so she turned the pages slowly while they watched: the inked coastlines, the depth-soundings written in Voss's careful left-slanting hand, the little annotations that were not navigation at all but testimony. *Twelve souls off the Craglight shoals, March '91. Nine from the *Sable Ann*, four children, delivered to the Sisters at Ternholm.* Page after page of it. Hundreds of names in the margins, written small as prayers.

"That's my brother's name." A young woman near the window — Ines placed her as one of the Ternholm orphans, grown now, a fish-gutter's calluses on her hands. "There. *Tomas Ferrow, age nine.* He's twenty-two. He works the herring boats."

"He's alive because your name is in that book," Ines said.

"And now we're all dead because of it," said the man beside her. Halvard Brine, Marta had called him — a former mate under Voss, big through the shoulders, with a face weathered into permanent skepticism. "You don't understand what you've carried here, girl. That isn't a book. That's a confession. Every route, every rescue, every name — signed in his own hand. If the Crown gets hold of it, they don't just hang Voss in absentia. They work down the margins. Name by name. Every soul he saved becomes a criminal retroactively, because the paper proves we knew him, took his help, owed him."

"Then burn it," said the orphan girl, Tomas's sister, her voice cracking on the words. "Burn it and be done."

"No," Ines said.

The word came out harder than she intended, and eleven faces turned toward her. She made herself breathe. She was the youngest person in this room by a decade, an apprentice among sailors, and she knew exactly how she must look: a girl holding something too heavy for her arms. She set the atlas down on the crate between them, flat, both hands on its cover, the way she'd rest a palm on a patient clock.

"Listen to me," she said. "I've spent six days inside that clock's housing. I know what hiding looks like. I know what it costs. You hide a thing, you spend the rest of your life listening for footsteps. And the men hunting this atlas — I've met their instrument. A census man named Quill came to my door with questions folded up inside polite ones. They will not stop because we burned one copy. They'll only stop believing anyone has proof, which is worse — then every one of us is a rumor they can erase at leisure."

"Burning it protects us," Brine said.

"Burning it protects *them*," Ines answered. "That's the whole design. Voss didn't run rescue routes out of vanity. He kept records because he understood that erasure is the crown's favorite weapon. They declared him an enemy not for what he did but for what he *proved* — that their shipping laws drown people on purpose, that their press-gangs take children, that all of it can be documented in a neat hand on good paper. This atlas is the only copy of the truth. Burn it, and in fifty years Voss is a pirate story told to frighten dock boys, and the drowned stay drowned twice."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below them, the chandlery keeper rolled a barrel across the floorboards.

Marta Oduya spoke from her corner, where she sat with her bad leg propped on a coil of spare line. She was sixty if she was a day, brown as old brass, with eyes that had read weather and men for forty years and been wrong about neither often. "The girl's right about the burning," she said. "I ran those routes. I know what's in those margins because I helped put some of them there." She looked around the room. "But I'll tell you what the girl hasn't said yet, because she's saving it. Ask her where the book needs to go."

Every face came back to Ines. She felt the heat climb her neck.

"There's a trial," she said. "January the ninth. They're finally putting a name to the charges against Silas Reeve — Voss's first mate. Treason, piracy, conspiracy. The man who steered the boat on more than half these rescues while Voss planned them. He's been in Marshgate Prison since autumn, and the prosecution has been building its case on exactly one thing: the claim that no evidence exists of lawful purpose. No logs. No record. Just a pirate's word against the Admiralty's silence." She lifted the atlas. "This ends that argument. This is the lawful purpose, documented. Every rescue in these pages was a salvage operation under maritime custom — wreck assistance, distress response, right of rescue. It's not even illegal. It was only ever *inconvenient*."

"You want to walk that into a courtroom," Brine said flatly.

"I want it in Reeve's hands before the ninth. His advocate can enter it. Once it's on the court record, it can't be quietly lost. Burning it saves our skins and hangs him. Delivering it might hang us all —" she looked around the room, refusing to soften it — "but it saves him, and it saves the truth, and those are the only two things Voss ever asked anyone to protect."

"Tomas's name is in there," the orphan girl whispered. "If it goes to trial, it's read aloud. In court. On the record."

"Yes," Ines said gently. "And in a courtroom, read aloud under oath, it's not a crime. It's a rescue. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

Brine stood. He was not a tall man seated or standing, but he filled space the way ballast fills a hull. "You're asking eleven people to bet their necks on a courtroom in a city where the judge is appointed by the same ministry that pays the men hunting this book. You're asking us to trust paper over fire." He shook his head slowly. "Voss would have loved you, girl. That's how I know you're dangerous."

"Then vote," Marta said. "All in favor of delivery to Reeve's advocate before the ninth."

Five hands. Then the orphan girl's, slowly, tears running freely down a face that didn't bother wiping them. Then a sixth, a seventh. Brine looked around the room, saw the arithmetic done, and put his hand up last, like a man paying a debt he disputed.

"It goes," he said. "But not by your hands alone, apprentice. And not through any street you've walked before. We do this the way Voss moved cargo — slow, split, unseen."

* * *

They split the risk three ways. Marta copied the marginal names onto separate slips to be scattered among trusted households, so that no single seizure could destroy the record of who had been saved. Brine took the original routes — the coastlines, the soundings — to be hidden aboard a collier sailing on the eighth, on the theory that the Admiralty would never think to look for contraband truth in a ship full of coal dust. And the heart of it, the pages bearing the rescues most cited in Reeve's coming trial, went with Ines, sewn into the lining of her coat, because she was the one nobody had ever seen with them.

She left the net-loft alone, near dawn, by the roofline.

The fish market was already waking when she came down into it — the great shed by the harbor mouth lit with hanging lanterns, the day's catch sliding out of the boats in silver avalanches, gulls screaming their eternal extortion overhead. It was the best crowd in Vellamere at that hour: five hundred bodies, all of them moving, none of them looking at each other. She told herself she was safe in it. She told herself she'd chosen it for exactly that reason.

She was halfway down the main aisle, between a mackerel stall and a barrow of cockles, when she felt it — the particular pressure on the back of the neck that clockmakers learn to feel when a mechanism is being watched. She did not turn. She altered nothing. She bought a pennyworth of smoked herring she did not want, counted her change with exaggerated care, and used the reflection in the fishmonger's polished scale-plates to sweep the crowd behind her.

He was good. She'd give him that. A grey man in a grey coat, hat brim low, standing at the mouth of the aisle with a clerk's leather satchel and a clerk's patient stillness, pretending to read a manifest. Ambrose Quill. He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at the space she would occupy in ten seconds, the way a good chess player watches squares instead of pieces.

So he'd found the chandlery's street, or Marta's, or one of the others, and he'd staked the market because it was the only road south. Her coat lining suddenly weighed forty pounds.

*Don't run,* she told herself. *Running is a confession with legs.*

She finished her purchase. She smiled at the fishmonger. Then she stepped sideways into the current of the crowd and began to work — not fleeing, but *flowing*, the way Voss's routes had always flowed: never straight, never hurried, every turn justified by some ordinary errand. She stopped to examine a basket of eels. She doubled back along the ice wagon. She ducked beneath a trestle table as a fight broke out between two deckhands over a card debt, letting the sudden knot of shouting bodies close behind her like a door.

Quill moved well for a bureaucrat. Twice she caught his grey coat in her peripheral vision, unhurried, always angling to cut ahead of her probable path rather than chase her actual one. He was reading her, she realized, the way she read a stuck escapement — not forcing the wheel, just finding where the resistance was and waiting for the mechanism to reveal itself.

Fine. Then she would give him a false resistance.

At the market's east end, where the gutting tables stood and the cobbles ran black with scales and meltwater, she let herself be seen checking the sky, hesitating, turning toward the harbor stairs — the obvious route, the route a frightened courier would take toward the boats. She gave him three full seconds of her profile, her face turned just enough, lamplight on her features. *Look at me,* she thought. *Remember me, and follow where I'm going.*

Then she plunged into the gutters' alley — the narrow, reeking lane behind the tables where the fishwives worked in shifts — and did the thing no gentleman could follow her through. She took off her coat, turned it inside out so the drab lining showed, wrapped it around the packet of pages, and walked out the alley's far end bareheaded among the fishwives' daughters, one more working girl heading home from the night shift, unremarkable, unmemorable, gone.

Behind her she heard the clatter of Quill reaching the stairs, the frustrated pause, the questions beginning — polite, immaculate, useless. *A young woman? Which way? What did she wear?*

By the time the sun cleared the harbor fortifications, Ines Blackwood was three streets away, sitting in the back of a chapel that served the Ternholm orphans, watching Marta's contact sew the trial pages into a hymnal's binding with fingers steadier than Ines's own heart.

It had worked. She had lost him. She had saved the pages.

And yet her hands would not stop shaking, and it took her a long time to understand why. Not fear of capture. Something worse. She had shown him her face on purpose — given Ambrose Quill the one thing he'd lacked. Before today she had been a rumor to him, a name in a census ledger, a theory. Now she was a face he could carry into every office, every constabulary, every port from here to the capital. *Find this girl.* She had traded her anonymity for the atlas's survival, and she understood, sitting in that chapel with the hymnal heavy in her lap, that the trade was permanent.

Somewhere across the city, a grey man was describing her to men who paid for erasures. And somewhere beyond the sea, a dying captain was waiting to learn whether his promise had been kept by someone worth the cost.

Nine days until the trial. Six until the century turned.

Ines pulled her collar up and went to find out which of them would run out of time first.
