## 10. Apprentice No Longer

The master clock's case stood open like a ribcage, and Ines Ashgrove knelt inside it with lamp oil on her hands and soot in her hair, listening to the harbor breathe.

Three months since New Year's Eve. Three months since she had thrown the escapement lever and let a century die with a sound like a struck bell, buying forty sailors and one atlas their passage through the observatory tunnels while the chimes rang false and glorious over Vellamere. The clock had given everything it had that night. Its mainspring had snapped with a crack she still heard sometimes in her sleep — not the spring itself, but the way old Herr Brandt had flinched at his workbench across the city, as every clockmaker does when something irreplaceable breaks.

Now she was putting it back together.

Not alone. That was the difference.

"Your third wheel's off-center again," said Marta Oduya from the floor of the housing, where she sat cross-legged among gears wrapped in sailcloth like relics of a drowned saint. Marta handled the heavy brass the way she'd once handled a pilot's wheel — with an affection that looked like roughness. "You keep seating it by eye."

"I'm seating it by *feel*."

"Feel is what you have when you haven't learned better yet." Marta held up the wheel, turned it against the lamplight, and found the true center with her thumbnail in four seconds flat. "There. A pilot learns the channel or she learns nothing. Same with wheels."

Ines took it back and seated it properly, and did not say thank you, because Marta had made it clear on the first day that thanks between crew were debts, and they were past debts now.

Above them, boots on the catwalk: Tam Broadstairs, who at fourteen was the youngest of Voss's people and the loudest, hauling the counterweight chains with two other hands while he narrated his own heroism to no one in particular. Old Sef, half-deaf, kept time for them by tapping the rail — a man who had spent thirty years listening to engines and could not bear silence even now. And at the top of the tower, where the dome opened its slit to the February sky, Widow Calloway stood watch out of pure habit, though there was no one left in Vellamere who would come asking questions.

Quill had seen to that. Ambrose Quill, who had stepped aside in the tunnel mouth with his ledger under his arm and his employers' names in his mouth like stones he'd finally decided not to swallow. He had testified in March — quietly, thoroughly, in the gray voice of a man reading aloud the only honest document of his life — and the men who had paid him to erase Captain Elias Voss were now themselves being erased, name by name, in courtrooms and newspapers. Quill had sent Ines a letter afterward. It contained no apology. It contained a list: every record of Voss's rescues he had recovered before his employers could burn them, cross-referenced, dated, witnessed. At the bottom, beneath his signature, he had written: *Some ledgers should be read aloud.*

She had put it in the clock case, beside the atlas. Where else.

* * *

The journeyman's papers came on a Tuesday, delivered by a courier who clearly did not know what he carried, because he complained about the stairs.

Herr Brandt had summoned her to the shop for the signing. He was older than the century, it seemed to Ines — a small dry man whose hands shook now unless they were holding tools, in which case they were steadier than the earth. He laid the certificate on the bench between them and did not slide it toward her.

"Read it."

She read it. Her own name in formal ink: *Ines Ashgrove, having demonstrated competence in the theory and practice of horology...* The words swam a little. She blamed the lamp.

"You broke my finest apprentice contract," Brandt said. "You spent four months rebuilding another man's clock instead of finishing your regulator piece. Your bench is a disgrace. You smell of lamp oil and harbor." He folded his hands. "You are also the best I have trained in forty years, and if I do not sign these papers today I never will, because I am a coward about goodbyes. So."

He signed. The pen scratched like a small animal. Then he blew on the ink, blotted it, and pushed the papers across the bench with two fingers, as though they might bite.

"There is a tradition," he said, "among clockmakers. When a journeyman leaves, the master gives him a tool. Not the best tool — the tool that taught him most. Usually by breaking." He opened a drawer and took out a pair of pliers, old ones, the jaws worn bright. "You dropped these down the observatory stairwell your first week and told me a seagull took them. I have never forgotten the lie. It was well constructed. Structurally sound. Like your work."

Ines took the pliers. They were warm from the drawer, warm from forty years of his hand, and she found her throat had closed entirely.

"He wasn't another man," she managed. "Voss. He was—"

"I know what he was." Brandt began wiping the bench, which was how he ended conversations. "I read the newspapers. Everyone reads the newspapers now; it's very inconvenient. Go and be a journeyman, Ashgrove. And when you are a master — and you will be, God help the trade — teach someone badly at first, so they learn to argue with you. That is the whole secret."

She got as far as the door before he spoke again, without turning around.

"The clock at the observatory. When it strikes midnight again — I would like to hear it. From my window. I am told the chimes will be true this time."

"They'll be true," Ines said. "I checked them twice."

"That," said Herr Brandt, "is why the papers say what they say."

* * *

They raised the bell hammer on the last night of April, and at nine o'clock exactly the master clock of Vellamere Observatory struck for the first time in four months, and the sound went down through the city like water finding its old channels.

Ines stood in the tower with the crew around her — Marta with her arms crossed and her eyes shut, listening; Tam hanging off the catwalk rail pretending he wasn't crying; Sef feeling the vibration through the stone with both palms spread wide, grinning his ruined grin. Below them the harbor lay black and patient, masts against the stars, and somewhere out on that dark water were ships whose masters owed their lives to a man the law had tried to unmake.

The clock ran true. She had rebuilt it gear by gear, and the crew had carried, hauled, held, and argued, and what stood in the case now was not the clock that had been but the clock that was — same bones, new blood. She had cut one mark into the inside of the case door, small and deep, where no inspector would ever find it: the date of the night it died, and the date of the night it woke.

Marta touched her shoulder. "He'd have hated the noise," she said. "Elias. Said bells were showing off."

"He rang them anyway. Every Christmas."

"Aye. Showing off." Marta smiled at the dark. "Showing off is how you tell people you're glad to be alive."

The letter had come in March, forwarded through three ports and a woman in Lisbon who signed herself only *the Lighthouse*. Voss's hand had gone thin — the letters leaned like masts in weather — but the words were steady:

*They tell me the atlas came home. Good. It was never mine; I only drew what other people's courage looked like from above. Keep it moving. An atlas that stops being added to is just a graveyard with pretty borders. Tell the girl with the steady hands that the promise was hers to fulfill and she has fulfilled it, and that I am proud of her in the specific way a captain is proud — which is to say, completely, and without ever once saying so out loud.*

*Fair winds, little gear.*

She had read it eleven times. She had cried after the fourth and stopped apologizing for it after the seventh.

* * *

The atlas lay open on her workbench at midnight, weighted at the corners with four brass nuts from the old escapement.

It was stranger now than when she'd first pulled it from behind the false panel — stranger because it was no longer only his. Marta had added the pilotage notes in her square sailor's hand. Two widows from the Harbor of Strangers had drawn in the safehouses, room by room. Tam, forbidden ink until he was fifteen, had been permitted a single contribution: a tiny drawing of himself in the margin of the northern routes, waving. Quill had submitted, via letter, a page of corrections so precise it was almost an apology.

And now the book waited for her.

Ines dipped her pen. Her hand did not shake; her hands had not shaken over work since she was twelve years old. But she understood, kneeling there in the lamplight with the century already four months old, that this was the true signing of her papers — not the certificate in her coat pocket, not Brandt's worn pliers, but this: the moment an inheritance stops being a thing you guard and becomes a thing you add to.

She drew the first line carefully. A route, north along the coast, to a cove where — so the word passed quietly from ship to ship — men were still being smuggled out of prisons that called their rescue crime. The line branched. Tides noted. A lighthouse marked with the symbol Marta used for *trustworthy keeper*. At the end of the route she wrote the coordinates, and beneath them, in a clear apprentice's hand grown sure:

*Route the first, by Ines Ashgrove, journeyman. For those the maps forget.*

Then she sat back, and looked at what she had made, and felt the whole weight and lightness of it settle onto her — not the weight of what Voss had left her, but the weight of what she chose to carry forward, which is the only inheritance worth the name.

The clock downstairs struck one. True, and clear, and hers.

She was still bent over the atlas, pen moving, when she heard the knock — three soft raps at the shop door, in a rhythm she knew from the Harbor of Strangers. The signal that meant: *someone needs carrying home.*

Ines capped the ink, took up the atlas, and went to answer it.
