## 13. The Commission's Historian

The Historical Commission occupied the old Mint, a building Casper had walked past a thousand times without once considering what happened behind its soot-darkened windows. Now he stood across the street from it in the grey light of a December morning, watching clerks arrive with their collars turned up against the wind, and understood that he was looking at the place where the past was manufactured.

He had dressed carefully. Not his workshop coat with its burnished stains and its smell of oil and brass, but the good wool jacket Marta had brushed that morning without being asked, and the white shirt Piet had pressed with an iron heated on the stove, holding it up afterward to inspect his work with the grave satisfaction of a man judging a finished escapement. Tomas had wanted to come. Marta had put her body in the doorway and said no, and for once the boy had not argued—though Casper had caught him at the window as he left, watching, hands jammed in his pockets, wearing the expression of someone counting the hours until he could stop being protected.

The ledger rode against Casper's chest, wrapped in oilcloth inside a leather document case that Aldous had used, years ago, to carry customer invoices. It was a risk beyond any he had yet taken. He knew that. If Kessel's men stopped him on the street, if anyone searched him, everything ended there in the snow between the tram lines. But he could not go to Ehrlund empty-handed, could not ask a man whose profession was evidence to act on faith alone. And there was something else, something he had not admitted even to himself until he stood buttoning the good jacket: he wanted the truth to be *seen*. He was tired of carrying it alone in the dark of his own chest like a stolen watch.

He crossed the street before he could reconsider.

* * *

The interior of the Mint smelled of paper, dust, and the particular staleness of rooms where no one had opened a window in twenty years. A porter took his name—Casper gave it, all of it, feeling the absurd exposure of it—and left him standing in a corridor lined with framed engravings: battles, proclamations, the burning of the old palace rendered in careful crosshatch, smoke rising from the roof in tidy curls. The Abdication Fire, as the victors drew it. He had grown up under these images. He had never questioned them. Standing now beneath the engraving of flames that he knew were lies, he felt the strange vertigo of a man who has discovered his own reflection is painted wrong.

Dr. Ehrlund received him in a small office on the third floor, a room that seemed designed to contradict every expectation Casper had brought up the stairs. He had imagined marble, banners, the trappings of state authority. Instead there were bookshelves sagging under the weight of unsorted volumes, a desk buried in galley proofs, a cold stove, and a small round man with ink-stained fingers and spectacles mended at the hinge with wire. Dr. Ehrlund looked less like an officer of the Republic than like a schoolmaster who had lost a war with his own filing.

"Blackwood," Ehrlund said, reading the card the porter had brought up. "The clockmaker's apprentice. The one who repaired the Bureau's master clock." His eyes came up over the spectacles, and they were sharper than the rest of him suggested. "You'll forgive me for knowing who you are. Your master's name crossed my desk more than once. Herr Aldous consulted for the Commission in the early years. Chronology, dating of documents, that sort of thing." He gestured to the chair opposite the desk. "Sit. What does a clockmaker want with the Historical Commission?"

Casper sat. His heart was going hard enough that he fancied the man might hear it, might mistake it for a clock ticking in the case of his ribs.

"I've found some documents," he said. "Documents concerning the night of the Abdication Fire."

Something moved behind Ehrlund's face—not surprise, Casper thought afterward, but a kind of weary recognition, the look of a man hearing a sound he had been listening for so long he had stopped expecting it.

"Go on," said the historian quietly.

"They're in the hand of my late master. They're dated over several years before the fire. They record—" Casper made himself say it plainly, without decoration, because decoration would make it sound invented. "They record that King Aldric abdicated peacefully. That he signed the instrument of abdication, that it was witnessed, that copies were made and dispatched to foreign courts. And that the fire was set afterward, by officers of the Provisional Government, to destroy those copies and everyone who could confirm them."

The stove ticked as it cooled. Somewhere down the corridor a door closed. Ehrlund did not move for a long moment; then he removed his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve with great care, and put them back on, as though the world required refocusing before he could answer.

"You understand," he said, "what you are telling me."

"I understand what I'm telling you."

"And you understand that I am obliged to report this conversation. That is not a threat, young man—it is simply the shape of my employment. Anything touching the founding narrative comes to Director Kessel's desk within the day. It has been that way since the Commission was chartered." He said it flatly, without apology, and Casper realized with a small cold shock that this was the historian's way of being honest: laying out the machinery first, so that whatever came after could be weighed against it.

"Then I'd rather you reported it accurately," Casper said. "I'm offering corroboration, Doctor. Not rumor. Documents, cipher keys, witnesses. There is a man alive who saw the fire from the Long Bridge and can name one of the officers who set it—an officer now serving on your own Commission. There are letters. There is a ledger kept in a cipher my master devised himself, which I have spent three nights breaking, and which I can prove is his hand by a hundred examples in the workshop's own books." He heard his own voice steadying as it went on, the way his hands steadied when they worked. "I didn't come to accuse. I came to offer the Commission the chance to correct the record before the centenary volume goes to print. Isn't that what the Commission is *for*?"

Ehrlund looked at him for a long time. Then he rose, went to the door, opened it, and examined the empty corridor with the thoroughness of a man checking a watch case for dust. He closed the door again and turned the key.

"That," he said, "was a question I asked myself eleven years ago, and I have been paying for the asking ever since." He came back to the desk but did not sit. He stood with his hand resting on a stack of proofs—the centenary volume itself, Casper realized, the galleys already set, already corrected, already dead. "Sit down properly, Blackwood, and let me tell you how things stand, because you strike me as a young man about to do something brave and stupid, and I have watched brave and stupid men walk into that building across the square and not walk out."

* * *

What Ehrlund told him, over the next hour, while the light through the frosted window went from grey to the color of dirty pewter, was a history of the Commission that appeared in none of the Commission's own publications.

He had joined it in the first year, full of the belief that a republic founded on documents would honor them. He had catalogued the seized royal archives. He had argued, in writing, for the publication of the complete diplomatic correspondence of the last reign—including, he said now, with a dry smile that held no humor at all, the very instruments of abdication Casper described. His memorandum had gone up the chain of command and come back down annotated in red ink by a man whose name Casper recognized from the newspapers: Kessel, then a junior director of records. Two months later the palace annexes burned. Three months after that, Ehrlund's proposed volume was replaced by the official account—the treason narrative, the mad king, the loyal officers forced to act—and Ehrlund was promoted to a position with a better title, a better salary, and no authority whatsoever over anything that mattered.

"I have spent eleven years editing footnotes," Ehrlund said. "I have fought for commas. I saved two dates from falsification in 1894 and considered it a victory. Do you know what that is, Blackwood? To be the keeper of a nation's memory and to be permitted to remember nothing?" He touched the stack of galleys almost tenderly. "And now the centenary volume. Four hundred pages. The definitive account, they call it. It goes to the printer on the thirty-first of December, and on the first of January it becomes the past—not a version of the past, but the past itself, for everyone born after us. Sealed. Kessel signs the seal personally. There is no appeal, no correction, no second edition. A hundred-year embargo on revision." He looked up. "So you see the arithmetic. Whatever you carry in that case against your chest—and don't look so startled, boy, I've been a historian forty years, I can see a man carrying documents the way other men carry weapons—whatever you have, it must enter the record before midnight on the thirty-first, or it enters nothing. Ever."

"The record can be corrected," Casper said. "You just told me the Commission corrects—"

"The Commission corrects what Kessel permits it to correct." Ehrlund lowered himself into his chair at last, and for the first time Casper saw how tired the man was, tired in the marrow, the fatigue of long obedience to a thing he despised. "Listen to me. I believe you. That is worth saying plainly, because you will hear it from few enough people: I believe you. I believed Aldous, too, in the early years, though he was cleverer than you—he never showed me anything, only asked questions shaped like small talk, and I only understood later what he had been doing. He was building a case the way you build a clock, wasn't he? Piece by piece, hidden in plain mechanism." Something warmed briefly in the historian's face. "I would give a great deal to read that ledger."

"Then read it."

"No." Ehrlund said it gently, and the gentleness was worse than refusal. "If I take those documents into this office, they are logged. If they are logged, they are seized within the day. If they are seized, your master's work dies in a Bureau strongbox and you die shortly after it, or wish you had. No, Blackwood. You brought me hope, and I must give it back to you with the institutional stone attached, because you cannot fight this building from inside this building. There is no procedure. There is no committee. There is Kessel, and there is the thirty-first, and there is the printer's seal."

Casper sat with that. Through the frosted glass he could see the pale blur of the observatory hill across the city, the mast of the time-ball a thin dark line against the sky. The master clock was up there. The compartment was still there—he had rebuilt it exactly as he'd found it, screws torqued to the same quarter-turns, the false back seated flush. A hiding place inside the machine that told the whole city what time it was.

"Then help me another way," he said slowly. "Not with the documents. With the form of the thing. If the truth were to reach the public record—by whatever means—what would it need to be unanswerable? What would Kessel not be able to bury?"

Ehrlund studied him for a long moment, and Casper watched the historian's mind working behind his eyes, cautious and hungry at once, a man being offered a taste of the only food he had ever truly wanted.

"Three things," Ehrlund said at last, counting on ink-stained fingers. "First: provenance that survives scrutiny. The documents themselves, available to independent examination—not copies, not summaries, the objects, with a chain of custody a court would accept. Second: living testimony under oath, entered by name, by someone with standing—a witness to the events, or an officer of this Commission speaking in his official capacity, which is why I am useless to you, since my capacity has been hollowed out like a gourd. Third—" he paused, and his voice dropped, "—irreversibility. That is the one everyone forgets. Truth that exists in one place can be destroyed in one place. Kessel knows this better than any man alive; it is his entire method. Burn the copies, kill the witnesses, seal the archive. Whatever you intend, it must exist in many places at once, or in a place he cannot reach, or—" the ghost of a smile, "—in a form he does not know how to read."

"A form he doesn't know how to read," Casper repeated.

"I once wrote a paper arguing that a nation's true archive is not its vaults but its habits—what its people repeat, what its children learn, what its ceremonies require. Kessel had it struck from the proceedings." Ehrlund took off his spectacles again and rubbed the bridge of his nose. "I was younger then. I thought being right was a kind of armor."

Outside, the light was failing early, the way it did in December, the city sliding toward lamplight. Casper felt the ledger's weight against his chest, and beneath it the other weight, the one he had carried since the night he climbed into the clock's housing: Aldous's patience, Aldous's decades of quiet work, all of it converging now on twelve days.

"Why are you telling me this?" Casper asked. "You said yourself you're obliged to report this conversation. Why warn me instead?"

Ehrlund was quiet for a moment. When he answered, he looked not at Casper but at the engraving hanging above the stove—a small one, not a battle this time but a portrait of the old king, hung high and half-forgotten, dust on the frame.

"Because in eleven years, you are the first person who has walked through that door offering me the truth instead of asking me to bury it. Because I am an old man whose life's work is a lie he maintains with his own hands. And because—" he put the spectacles back on, and the schoolmaster returned, wry and precise, "—because I am obliged to report that a clockmaker visited me today regarding the Abdication Fire, and I intend to write that report tonight, and it will say that the young man was confused, grieving his master, chasing a fantasy of conspiracy, and that I sent him away with a lecture on the dangers of historical romance. Kessel will read it and feel nothing. Let him feel nothing. Every day he feels nothing is a day you keep."

He stood, and Casper stood with him, and the historian extended his hand—ink-stained, warm, gripping harder than his size suggested.

"Twelve days, Blackwood. Provenance, testimony, irreversibility. Build me those three things and I will find a way to be useful—I don't yet know what way, but I promise you I will be looking for it with more attention than I have given anything in eleven years. And until then—" his grip tightened, "—do not come here again. Do not write to me. If we must speak, it will happen the way such things happened in the years I studied: through intermediaries, through accident, through the ordinary noise of a large city. Trust the noise. Noise is the one archive Kessel has never learned to index."

* * *

Casper walked home the long way, along the river, because the long way gave him time to think and because he could not bear yet to bring either his news or his fear through the workshop door. Snow was beginning again, fine and dry, sifting down through the lamplight. On the far bank the gasworks flared and dimmed, flared and dimmed, breathing like something asleep.

Provenance, testimony, irreversibility. He turned the three words over the way he'd turn a difficult wheel in his fingers, feeling for the teeth.

Provenance: the ledger itself, real and examinable—but hidden, and hunted. Testimony: Piet, who could name the officer, if Piet could be persuaded to speak before the machinery that had silenced him. Irreversibility—

He stopped at the railing. Below him the river ran black under the ice shelves at its edge, patient, going somewhere whether anyone watched or not. Across the water the observatory stood on its hill with its dome lit faintly from within, and he thought of the master clock up there in the dark, striking the hours over ten thousand rooftops, into ten thousand kitchens, into the ears of every child falling asleep in this city—and of Aldous, who had spent thirty years keeping that machine alive, winding it, correcting it, loving it, and who had chosen *that* machine, of all the machines in the world, to hide his secret in.

A form he does not know how to read. A place he cannot reach. Many places at once.

Casper's breath went out of him slowly, and what replaced it was not quite courage and not quite terror but the specific, humming clarity he knew from the bench at three in the morning, when a broken movement finally showed him how it wanted to be fixed.

Kessel controlled the printers, the archives, the census, the streets. But the clock belonged to the city. And on New Year's Eve, the whole city would be looking up at it, waiting for midnight.

He pulled his collar up and turned toward home, toward lamplight and Marta's kitchen and the low murmur of voices that had become, without his ever deciding it, the sound of his own life. He did not see the figure detach from a doorway two hundred paces back and follow at an even pace, nor notice how it stopped when he stopped, nor mark the way it turned down a side street at the bridge, unhurried, a man with nowhere particular to be.

But someone had seen him leave the Mint. And in a warm office above the square, a director of records was already reading a porter's list of the day's visitors, running one finger down the column of names, and pausing.
