## 22. The Clockmaker's Apprentice

The workshop smelled the same in every season, though Casper had long since stopped being able to say which of the smells belonged to which year. Oil and beeswax and the faint iron tang of filings. Marta's bread from the kitchen below, because Marta had never surrendered the kitchen to anyone, not even to the young woman who now did most of the cooking while Marta supervised from a chair by the window with the authority of an empress reviewing troops. Pine shavings from Piet's bench, where the old engraver still worked three mornings a week, his hands steady as they had ever been, his silence more eloquent than most men's speeches.

Casper stood at the long window with his tea going cold and watched the snow come down over the river city the way it had come down on the night everything changed, twenty-two years ago almost to the day. The Bureau of Time was gone now—absorbed, renamed, its records folded into the National Archive like a letter into an envelope—but the observatory still crowned its hill above the rooftops, and the master clock still kept the hour for everyone who lived beneath its face. He could see the top of the tower from here if he leaned close to the glass and looked past the chimney pots. He didn't need to look. He knew exactly where it stood, the way a man knows where his own heart is beating.

"Master Blackwood?"

The voice came from the doorway behind him—young, careful, pitched low the way apprentices learned to pitch their voices in their first month, when they were still afraid of disturbing the delicate machinery of the shop. Casper turned.

Tomas's daughter would have been too much symmetry; life was kinder than that, and stranger. The boy standing there was named Anselm, fourteen years old, all elbows and watchful eyes, sent to him two years ago by a mother who wanted her son learning a trade that couldn't be conscripted. He had the particular quality Casper always looked for and could never teach: he listened with his whole body. When a gear turned somewhere in the shop, Anselm's head turned with it, like a compass finding north.

"You're supposed to be cleaning the escapements," Casper said.

"I finished them." Anselm hesitated in the doorway, working up to something. Casper waited. Waiting was half the trade. A clock never told you what was wrong with it until you were quiet enough to hear.

"There's a question I've been wanting to ask," the boy said finally. "About the master clock."

"Ask it."

Anselm came into the room properly now, wiping his hands on his apron out of habit even though they were clean. "I went up last week. For the quarterly winding. You said I should learn the great clock before anything else, so I did." He paused again, and Casper recognized the pause—it was the pause of someone who has noticed something he isn't sure he was meant to notice. "There's a gear in the strike train that doesn't do anything."

Casper felt the old warmth move through his chest, slow and certain, like the descent of a weight in a well-made case.

"Doesn't do anything," he repeated.

"It meshes with the third wheel but it drives nothing. No hand, no hammer, no release. I checked four times. I thought maybe it was a repair—that something had been removed and the gear left in place. But it's not worn like a leftover part. It's been *kept*. Polished. There's brass under the brass." Anselm's hands moved as he talked, unconsciously sketching the train in the air: wheel, pinion, arbor. "And it's marked. A tiny stamp on the face, near the rim. I needed a loupe to read it. It says—" he frowned, retrieving it, "—*ad memoriam*. And a date. The thirty-first of December, 1899."

The snow ticked softly against the window. Downstairs, Marta laughed at something, a sound like a bell with a crack in it that made it more beautiful, not less.

"Why," Anselm said, "would anyone build a silent gear into the most important clock in the city?"

Casper set down his tea. He crossed to the cabinet against the far wall—the tall one with the double-locked doors that Anselm had never seen opened—and took from inside it a flat wooden case, older than the boy, older than the Republic's second constitution, older than the marriage certificate hanging framed beside Piet's engravings of the bridge. He laid the case on the workbench between them and rested his hand on its lid.

"Sit down, Anselm," he said. "This will take longer than a question deserves."

* * *

He began, as he always began when he told it, with the fire.

Not the Abdication Fire itself—he had never seen it, had only ever known it secondhand, first through the victors' histories and then through the truth—but with the smaller fire, the one that lived in every story worth telling: the fire of not knowing. He told the boy about a winter when he was younger than Anselm, grieving a master who had died without telling him the weight of what he carried, and about a summons that arrived on ordinary paper for an ordinary repair, and about climbing into the housing of the great clock and finding a ledger hidden in a compartment no blueprint admitted existed.

"A ledger," Anselm said. "In the clock."

"In the clock. Written in cipher by the man whose bench you sweep. Herr Aldous had spent years recording what actually happened on the last night of the monarchy—that the old king abdicated peacefully, signed the instruments of transfer, and ordered his own guards to stand down. That the fire that 'destroyed the royalist archives' was set afterward, by Republican officers, to burn the evidence of a peace they needed to call a treason. Everything the city believed about that night was built on ash." Casper turned the case over in his hands. "Your great clock carried the truth for years before I found it. Aldous built the hiding place himself, decades earlier, waiting for someone to come looking. That's why the compartment fit my hand like it had been measured for it. In a way, it had."

"And the census woman." Anselm had heard fragments, of course; the whole city had heard fragments by now. The Midnight Pages, people called them, though the name had softened over the years into something almost fond. "Ilse Varga. She was sent to find you."

"She was sent to erase me. There's a difference, and she taught it to me herself." Casper smiled despite himself, the smile he still caught on his face sometimes in mirrors, the one that hadn't existed before a certain night on the Long Bridge. "She asked one question too many at this very bench, and it saved my life and ruined her career and gave us both everything we have. She's upstairs arguing with Marta about whether the roast needs another half hour, if you want to verify any of this. She'll tell you the same story with better grammar and worse patience."

But Anselm wasn't ready to be released into the upper rooms yet. He leaned forward over the case, eyes bright. "The pages. At midnight. How did the clock *print*?"

So Casper told him that part too—the strike train rebuilt in secret, each blow of the hammer on the great bell releasing one leaf of the corrected account through the carillon's paper roll, twelve strikes, twelve pages, falling into the New Year's crowd below like snow that refused to melt. He told him about dismantling the mechanism by lamplight while the guards stamped cold outside the door, about engraving the truth into brass because guards cannot read a gear train, about Kessel's face in the moment he understood that the clock he had guarded for twenty years had been armed against him all along. He told him about Ilse stepping forward with her commission held like a blade, and about Marta coming home through the snow, and about Piet walking into the archive the following spring and setting down, page by drawn page, the testimony he had carried in silence across the bridge on the worst night of the century.

"The extra gear," Casper said, and tapped the case once, gently. "I added it myself, after. The year after. You found the only part of that clock I ever built alone."

"But it doesn't *do* anything."

"No," Casper agreed. "It doesn't do anything. Not today. Not tomorrow. Not for a long time, if the world stays kind." He opened the case at last.

Inside, nested in green felt gone soft with age, lay a small brass ring—a gear blank, uncut, unpolished, its teeth not yet formed. Beside it, a letter in Casper's own handwriting, sealed with wax, and beneath both, a thin envelope bearing the archive's seal, renewed and countersigned every few years by Dr. Ehrlund's successors, each one more bewildered than the last by the request that accompanied it.

"When I rebuilt the strike train," Casper said, "I put in that silent gear for the same reason Aldous built his hidden compartment. Because a record is not the same thing as a memory. Records can be burned—I've seen it done. Memories fade—I've felt it happen. But a thing built into the working heart of the city, a thing every winder touches and no one questions, a thing that waits—" he looked at the boy, and let the sentence arrive at its destination slowly, the way a striking train arrives at midnight, one measured beat at a time. "Some things are built to be found later. By whoever comes looking. By whoever is quiet enough, and patient enough, and stubborn enough to ask why a gear turns without doing anything."

Anselm stared at the uncut blank. Casper watched understanding arrive in the boy's face the way dawn arrives over the river—not all at once, but completely.

"You're giving it to me," Anselm said. "Someday."

"I'm giving it to you now. Keeping it is just a matter of when you carry it." Casper closed the case and pushed it across the bench. "Inside is a letter explaining everything, and instructions for a modification to the strike train that no one alive but me knows how to make—which means, if you're wise, you'll let me teach you before I'm too stiff-fingered to demonstrate. Inside also is a sealed letter from the National Archive, to be opened only if the true history is ever challenged, denied, or burned. If that day never comes, the gear keeps turning silently forever, and that will be the best possible use of it."

"What does the letter say? The archive one?"

"It says: *We were here. We saw. Here is how it happened.*" Casper shrugged, and the shrug cost him nothing now, though there had been years when such a gesture would have been worth his life. "That's all any honest record ever says. The rest is gears."

* * *

They went upstairs together when the light began to fail, because in this house the light failing was a summons and everyone obeyed it. The kitchen was warm and gold-lit and loud. Marta presided from her chair, gray now and magnificent, directing operations with a wooden spoon like a marshal's baton. Piet sat in the corner with Tomas's youngest on his knee, teaching the child to draw bridges—long ones, impossible ones, spanning pages and rooms and, who knew, perhaps someday rivers wider than any river. Tomas himself came in from the courier office smelling of cold air and ink, grown broad and dependable, still running, though now mostly toward things instead of away from them.

And Ilse stood at the stove arguing about the roast, dark-haired still with silver coming in at the temples like frost forming on a fine mechanism, and when Casper appeared in the doorway with Anselm behind him, she looked at him the way she had looked at him across the width of the Long Bridge on a night of lies and lantern-light—seeing straight through him, and staying anyway.

"Well?" she said. "Did you finally tell the boy?"

"He found the gear himself. Four times checked."

"Mm." She turned back to the stove, but Casper caught the corner of her mouth. "Then he'll do. Sit down, both of you. Anselm, tell your mother's ghost I fed you, next time you write."

They ate the way they had eaten for twenty-two years: crowded, overlapping, Marta's bread torn rather than cut, Piet conducting conversation in gestures that everyone understood and no one translated because translation would have diminished it. Outside, the snow thickened over the city, over the rooftops and the river and the long stone span that had carried them all, in one way or another, from one shore of their lives to the other. Up on its hill, invisible in the weather but unfailingly present, the master clock moved through its evening hours, and somewhere in its strike train a small brass gear turned and turned and accomplished nothing at all, except everything.

Later, when the plates were cleared and the household had drifted to its corners—Marta dozing in her chair, Piet walking Tomas's daughter home through the white streets, Ilse reading by the lamp with her feet tucked under her—Anselm stood at the door pulling on his coat, the wooden case wrapped in cloth and tucked carefully inside it, against his chest, where a person carries the things that matter.

"Master Blackwood," he said, from the doorway. "The date on the gear. *Ad memoriam*, the thirty-first of December, 1899. Is that when it happened? The pages?"

"That's when we finished," Casper said. "When it started is a longer story. Ask me again when you've swept the bench a hundred more times."

Anselm grinned, bowed the awkward apprentice bow that Casper had never managed to correct and secretly hoped he never would, and went out into the snow.

Casper stood a moment in the doorway watching the boy's footprints fill with white, and then he crossed the room and sat down across from Ilse, and she lowered her book, and neither of them said anything for a while, because they had long ago used up all the words that mattered and settled comfortably into the ones that didn't.

"The gear," she said eventually. "You never told me why you really added it. Not the archive reasons. The real one."

Casper considered. The fire ticked. Somewhere below, the shop's regulators breathed in their cases, keeping time the way hearts keep time—without being asked, without being thanked, without stopping.

"Because Aldous waited forty years for someone to open a compartment," he said. "And I waited one winter for someone to ask one question too many. And Piet waited on a bridge for fifty years to be heard. Everything good that ever happened to this city happened because somebody built a truth into a quiet place and trusted the future to come looking for it." He reached over and took her hand, and she let him, the way she had let him for twenty-two years, which is to say: as a decision renewed daily. "I wanted to leave one more quiet place. In case the future gets careless again."

Ilse squeezed his fingers. Outside, the snow went on falling, patient as parchment, covering the streets and the bridge and the tower on the hill, and beneath the snow the city slept, and beneath the sleeping city the sewers and cellars and foundations held their old secrets like stones hold heat, and above it all the master clock counted out the small hours toward morning—toward, though no one in the house knew it yet, a letter that would arrive within the week bearing the seal of the National Archive, addressed to Casper Blackwood in a hand he did not recognize, asking a single line:

*Who else knows how to build a silent gear?*
