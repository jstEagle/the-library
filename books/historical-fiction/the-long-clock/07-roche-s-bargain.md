## 7. Roche's Bargain

The summons came the way winter came to Saint-Aubin — quietly, and then all at once.

Coral was at the case, setting a funeral notice for old Madame Perrin, when the knock sounded: two raps, a pause, a third. Not the gendarmes' rhythm. The gendarmes knocked like men who expected doors to open for them. This was a knock that pretended to be polite.

She opened the door on Captain Roche standing alone in the blue hour of evening, his greatcoat beaded with sleet, his hat held against his chest like a man come to pay respects rather than make arrests.

"Mademoiselle Joubert." His breath smoked. "You'll forgive the hour. I find that evening calls are less... observed."

"Captain."

"May I come in? It's about your father's case. I'd rather not discuss it on the step where the whole street can count my words."

Every instinct she had honed since the night of the raid told her to refuse him. But refusing a captain of gendarmes was its own kind of confession, and so she stepped aside and let the cold walk in wearing a man's shape.

He did not sit when offered a chair. He stood instead before the press, running one gloved finger along the frame with something that might have been reverence, might have been appraisal. The lamp threw his shadow long across the floor, so that it seemed there were two of him, one upright and one sprawled toward her father's empty stool.

"You set type," he said. It was not a question.

"I do."

"Your father taught you?"

"He did."

Roche nodded slowly, as if she had confirmed something he'd written in a report years ago. "I've been in this town eleven years, mademoiselle. Do you know what I've learned about Saint-Aubin?" He turned to face her. "That everyone here knows everything, and no one knows anything. It is a town of perfect ignorance, achieved by great effort."

Coral kept her hands still at her sides. She had learned in the weeks since the arrest that her body could lie even when her voice could not; she had made it practice.

"What is it you want, Captain?"

"What I want," Roche said, "and what I am permitted to want are different animals. What I *want*—" he drew off one glove, then the other, folding them together with the care of a man handling evidence, "—is to understand why a printer with no politics, no debts worth mentioning, and no taste for martyrdom would risk his neck on pamphlets so badly printed they insult the trade itself."

Her heart performed a single hard stroke, like a platen coming down. She thought of the pamphlets she had examined by candlelight — the crooked justification, the ink too thin, the paper stock wrong for anything Auguste Joubert would have bought. She had told herself the sloppiness proved nothing except that someone else had done the work.

"They were badly printed," she said carefully.

"They were *deliberately* badly printed," Roche said. "There is a difference. A man forging a hand makes his errors look honest. These errors look lazy, which is what an honest hand looks like to a man who has never held one." He smiled without warmth. "I have held one. Before the uniform, I was apprenticed to a bookbinder in Lyon. I know what a well-made page feels like under the thumb. And whoever printed those pamphlets wanted them to feel like yours."

The room seemed to tilt very slightly, the way the floor of the shop tilted toward the river, a settling that had happened over decades and that no one noticed anymore. Coral became aware of the type cases above her head, forty drawers of lead and antimony, and somewhere among them the font that weighed too much, that carried its secret stamped into every sort like grain in wood.

"I don't know what you're suggesting," she said.

"No," Roche agreed pleasantly. "You don't. That is the arrangement we are operating under." He moved to the window, parted the curtain a finger's width, looked out at the street as though checking whether the darkness was doing its work. "Here is what I know, and I will say it once. The pamphlets came off a press in Vireuil. There is a jobbing house there run by a man named Delacour — Lucien Delacour. You may know the name."

She knew it. Everyone in Saint-Aubin knew it. Delacour was the magistrate's cousin, the magistrate being the man who would sit in judgment on her father when the ice went out of the river. Delacour owned half the tolls on the east road and all of the discretion in three parishes.

"The magistrate's cousin," she said, keeping her voice flat as a composing stone.

"The magistrate's cousin," Roche repeated. "Who commissioned those pamphlets through intermediaries, who chose your father's shop as the place to hang the crime because Auguste Joubert prints things that are true, and true printing is the best disguise for lies. Who assumed that a small man in a small town would hang quietly before anyone thought to ask questions." He let the curtain fall. "And who assumed correctly, until now."

* * *

Roche had rehearsed this conversation on the walk over, and again while waiting for the boy to answer the door, and found now that none of the rehearsal survived contact with the actual room.

It was not the daughter who unsettled him. He had interrogated smugglers and deserters, men who wept and men who spat, and none of it moved him much anymore. What unsettled him was the shop. Eleven years he had walked past this window, and he had never once considered what happened inside it — that letters were not merely printed but *made*, cast by hand, each one a small act of deliberate creation. He had spent his career among documents and never thought of them as objects, as things with weight and origin, as things that could be forged the way coins were forged.

His informant had told him about the girl carrying loaves through the mill gate. The miller's boy had talked — they always talked eventually, courage being a currency that devalued overnight. Roche had gone to the mill himself, examined the guard's ledger, and found the entry for the Joubert girl's basket, weight noted, contents noted as bread. He had held one of the remaining loaves afterward. It had been heavier than bread should be, and when he broke it open in private, he had found nothing but bread — but the crumb around the hollow center had been disturbed and re-baked, clumsily, by someone working alone at night with more nerve than time.

Whatever she had carried, some of it had already passed the first checkpoint. Some of it might already be across the river, or hidden, or destroyed. He could not know. That was the trouble with clever people: you could never audit what they had done, only what they might yet do.

He could arrest her now. He had enough — not for a tribunal, perhaps, but enough to hold her, and holding her would stop whatever she was doing. That was the clean move, the move his superiors would applaud. It was also, he understood with cold clarity, the move that would accomplish nothing. Arrest the daughter and the evidence stayed hidden. Convict the father and Delacour stayed fat and safe behind his cousin's bench. The machine would grind on exactly as designed, and Roche would remain what he had been for eleven years: a capable man in a provincial uniform, watching lesser-connected men rise past him on the strength of their uncles' names.

He was forty-three years old. He had stopped believing in the Republic sometime during the second one, and in justice shortly after, but he had never stopped believing in leverage. And the girl standing before him with her hands folded like a schoolmistress was sitting on the largest piece of leverage this town had produced in a decade.

"Delacour will be at the tribunal," he said aloud. "Not on the bench — he's careful — but in the room. When your father is condemned, he'll want to watch. Men like him always want to watch. It's how they confirm the world still works in their favor."

"And if he isn't condemned?"

Roche met her eyes. They were steady eyes, he gave her that. Most people, by this point in a conversation with a captain of gendarmes, had begun arranging their faces into compliance. Hers had arranged themselves into arithmetic.

"Then Delacour has a problem," he said. "And I am offering to be that problem — provided the problem lands on him and not on his cousin. Do you understand me, mademoiselle? I can destroy Lucien Delacour. I cannot touch Magistrate Delacour. If your evidence names both, it dies in committee, or worse, it lives long enough to be ruled inadmissible on some technicality my superiors will invent between lunch and dinner. But if it names only the cousin — if the trail stops at Vireuil, at the jobbing house, at a man with no family shield — then it goes forward. Then there is a trial. Then your father walks out in spring."

"And what do you get?"

The question landed harder than any accusation could have. He almost admired it.

"A promotion, if I'm lucky," he said. "A transfer to Lyon, if I'm luckier. Credit I will take and you will never publicly contest. Is that what you want to hear? Very well: I am not doing this for your father. I am doing this because a man who prints pamphlets for a magistrate's cousin has printed other things for other cousins, and when he falls, he will fall loudly, and the noise will bury several men above my rank. I intend to be standing somewhere better when it does." He put his gloves back on, one finger at a time. "Justice in this country is not a temple, mademoiselle. It is a market. I am offering you the only price currently quoted."

* * *

After the door closed behind him, Coral stood a long time without moving, listening to his boots fade down the rue des Tanneurs, listening to the river muttering under its lid of ice.

So. He knew. Perhaps not everything — perhaps not the font, not the marks, not the duplicates sleeping in candle stubs and the one original she had kept back from superstition — but enough. Enough that her whole architecture of secrecy was revealed as what it had probably always been: a screen, not a wall. He had watched her carry loaves and said nothing, and let her believe she was invisible, and why? Because an invisible woman was a useful woman. Because he had wanted to see where she would go.

She sat down at her father's stool and put her hands flat on the composing stone, as if the cool iron could settle her.

The vertigo was not fear. Fear she knew; fear had been her constant companion since the raid, a dull toothache of a thing. This was worse. This was the ground shifting under the distinction she had built her life upon — the difference between the law and the truth. Her father was innocent; that was the truth. But innocence, Roche had just explained to her with the courtesy of a man discussing freight rates, was not a legal fact. It was a bargaining position. Justice would not be done in spring; a transaction would be conducted, and her father's freedom would be the consideration paid, and Captain Roche would collect his commission, and the magistrate — the man whose cousin had tried to hang an honest printer — would keep his bench, his robes, his power to do this again to someone else, someone with no daughter who could read type.

Could she live inside that bargain?

She thought of the widow Vasseur, who had informed on her own husband to survive, and who had told Coral: *the trick is not to stay clean, girl, the trick is to know exactly how dirty you've become.* At the time it had sounded like wisdom. Now it sounded like a receipt.

But — and here her hands curled slowly against the stone — if she refused him, what then? Roche arrested her tomorrow. The originals sat in a drawer ten feet from where she stood; he would find them within the hour. Her father would hang in spring, and Delacour would watch, and the world would work in his favor, precisely as Roche had said. Refusal was not purity. Refusal was just another price, and a worse one.

Unless.

Unless the evidence could be made to speak without her, without Roche, without any bargain at all. Unless there were copies enough, scattered wide enough, that no single man could gather them up and control the market. Roche thought she had carried bread through one gate. He did not know what she had planned for the customs house. He did not know about the ferry landing, or about what a woman could do simply by walking openly, daring the world to stop her.

Roche wanted to own the truth. Very well. Let him try to buy a thing that was already multiplying in the dark.

Coral rose, lit a fresh candle, and pulled the drawer of the heavy font. The little stamped sorts caught the light like a field of frost. Outside, far downriver, the ice groaned — a long, deep sound, like a giant turning in sleep, like something vast beginning to remember it was water.

Three days, the ferryman had said. Three days, maybe four, before the thaw.

She picked up her casting ladle and began to feed the fire.
