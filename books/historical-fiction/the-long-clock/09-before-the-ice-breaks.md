## 9. Before the Ice Breaks

The last sort weighed almost nothing.

That was the cruelty of it — after all the candle stubs and bread loaves, after the mill gate and the fire and the long winter of hiding metal inside ordinary things, what remained fit in the palm of her hand. A single piece of type, cast in her father's own mold, stamped along its side with the tiny characters she had spent a month learning to read. The date of a printing that could not have happened in Saint-Aubin. The name of the man who had ordered it. Enough to hang the magistrate's cousin; enough, if it came to that, to free her father.

Coral held it in her closed fist as she dressed in the dark, and it seemed to pulse there like a second heart.

She had planned to hide it again. She had a dozen schemes ready: sewn into her coat seam, dropped into the hollow heel of an old boot, wrapped in oilcloth and swallowed down the well to be retrieved by rope. But standing at the window before dawn, watching the river steam where the current ran fast beneath the ice, she understood that every one of those schemes belonged to the woman she had been — the woman who made herself small, who memorized vanishings and said nothing, who had survived eight years by being too quiet to notice.

The customs house fire had taught her what hiding earned. Ashes. Someone had burned a building full of sleeping clerks to destroy her duplicates, and they would burn whatever else stood between them and spring. There would be no more loaves. No more candles. Whatever she carried now, she would carry in the open, daring the world to look at it.

She wrapped the type in a square of clean linen, tied it with string, and set it in the center of her palm like an offering.

* * *

The town had come out to watch the river die.

That was how old Vasseur's ferryman nephew described it once, years ago, when Coral was small enough to stand at his elbow: *the river dies every winter and is born again every spring, and we are all witnesses.* Now the whole of Saint-Aubin seemed gathered along the bank — mill workers with their hands in their pockets, fishwives with baskets set down, children perched on the mooring posts. They had come because the ice was talking. Everyone could hear it, a deep groaning that rolled up from under the town like the voice of something turning over in its sleep, and everyone knew what it meant. Within days, perhaps hours, the thaw would come for real, the bridge road would open, and the tribunal across the water would convene to decide Auguste Joubert's life.

Coral moved through the crowd and felt them part around her without her asking. She had expected to be invisible still — she had been invisible so long it was nearly a trade, like typesetting, a craft of negative space — but something had changed since the fire. Perhaps it was the way she walked. Perhaps it was simply that grief, carried long enough, shows in the body like grain in wood. Women nodded to her. A man she did not know touched his cap. She kept her eyes forward and her fist closed around the linen bundle, and let them look.

The third checkpoint stood at the ferry landing itself: a plank table, two gendarmes, a brazier burning against the damp, and behind them the flat-bottomed ferry frozen into the shallows, waiting for water. Beyond the landing, the river ran a quarter mile of gray-white ice to the far bank, where the tribunal's windows caught the early light. She could see figures moving there. Clerks. Her father might be watching from a cell window this very minute, seeing the same crowd, hearing the same groaning ice, knowing what day it was.

She stopped ten paces from the table and made herself be seen.

"I'm carrying type to the tribunal," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Evidence for the defense of Auguste Joubert. I'm asking safe passage across."

The nearer gendarme — a young one, chapped-lipped, unhappy in the cold — looked at her, then at the older one, then at the growing silence of the crowd. "No crossings," he said. "Bridge is closed till the tribunal sits. Orders."

"The tribunal sits within days," Coral said. "I'm delivering evidence *to* it. Are you going to explain to the magistrates why their evidence arrived late?"

"Orders," the young one repeated, but weaker, and the older gendarme shifted, and Coral saw it happen — the flicker of eyes toward the crowd, the arithmetic of a man calculating which order would cost him more to obey. Power was made of cracks. Captain Roche had taught her that, in his cold office, with his cold bargain half-offered: *give me the cousin instead of the magistrate, mademoiselle, and I will find myself looking elsewhere at convenient moments.* She had not answered him then. She understood now that she was answering him now, here, in front of all of them.

Because the crowd had begun to murmur. And in the murmur she heard names — *Joubert's girl, Joubert's daughter, the printer's child* — and she realized with a strange, floating clarity that Saint-Aubin had known all along who she was. All those years of being small, and the town had seen her anyway, the way you see a thing you are careful never to look at directly.

A wagon creaked up the lane behind her. She knew the sound before she turned. Captain Roche stepped down from the seat, buttoned his coat against the wind off the water, and surveyed the scene with the expression of a man arriving precisely when he intended to.

"Mademoiselle Joubert." He inclined his head. To the gendarmes: "What seems to be the difficulty?"

"She wants to cross, Captain. Says she's got evidence for the tribunal."

Roche walked past her slowly, close enough that she caught the smell of tobacco and wet wool, and stopped at the table. He picked up the checkpoint ledger, turned its pages without reading them, and set it down again.

"Regulation fourteen," he said, to no one in particular, in the tone of a schoolmaster. "Persons bearing materials subpoenaed or tendered as evidence before a sitting tribunal may not be detained at a military checkpoint, detention constituting obstruction of judicial process." He glanced at the young gendarme. "You were about to obstruct judicial process."

"Captain, I—"

"You were. It happens. The regulations are dull reading; I don't blame you." He turned to Coral, and for one unguarded moment she saw something move behind his eyes — not kindness, nothing so warm, but recognition. Two people who both understood exactly what was being done and why. "Go on, then, mademoiselle. The ice will hold for a woman. It has opinions about wagons."

"Captain Roche." She made herself say it loudly enough for the crowd. "You're letting me pass."

"I am enforcing regulation fourteen," Roche said, "with great rigor." And the crowd — she heard it, she would hear it for the rest of her life — the crowd laughed, a short rough sound with relief in it, and someone began to clap, and someone else took it up, and Captain Roche stood very straight and let them believe he had done a brave thing, because being seen to win was a currency he had always preferred to winning itself.

Coral looked at him a moment longer. *The cousin,* his eyes said. *Remember our arrangement.* She gave him the smallest nod — justice served by a self-serving man was still served, and she would take it, and she would never pretend it was clean — and turned to the ice.

* * *

The first steps were the worst.

The river surface was a foot of gray ice over black moving water, and every stride produced a sound like a struck bell, low and resonant, traveling out ahead of her and behind. Halfway across, the groaning began in earnest — not the distant sleep-talk of the shore ice but a live, structural complaint, the whole sheet flexing as the current underneath quickened with the thaw. Coral kept walking. She did not run. Running was for people who had somewhere to hide, and she had finished with hiding.

Behind her, the crowd had gone silent. She could feel their attention on her back like sunlight.

In her fist, the type pressed its small edges into her palm. She thought of her father teaching her to read sorts by touch, blindfolded, laughing: *a compositor knows letters the way a mother knows faces, in the dark, by feel alone.* She thought of the widow Vasseur, who had refused to help and given her everything anyway. She thought of the miller's boy at the first gate, lying to a guard with his heart hammering, and the weight of other people's courage she had carried all winter alongside the metal.

Then the ice spoke.

Not groaned — *spoke*, a crack like a rifle shot running the length of the river behind her, and the crowd on the bank cried out as one animal, and Coral felt the sheet shudder under her feet and kept walking, faster now, her breath tearing, the far bank suddenly the only real thing in the world. Another crack, closer. Water welled up through a seam twenty paces behind her and spread in a dark tongue. She heard Roche's voice on the shore, sharp, holding the crowd back from the ice — *stand fast, none of you follow, regulation fourteen covers one woman only* — and she laughed, actually laughed, a wild sound the wind took away, because even now, even here, the man was finding technicalities.

Ten paces. Five. The ice bellied and sagged. She threw herself the last distance onto the mud of the far bank, rolled, scrambled up — and turned around.

The whole crowd of Saint-Aubin stood at the water's edge, and behind them the river gave way. The ice broke in great grinding plates, tilting, sinking, the black water surging up through the fracture lines, and the sound was enormous, a sound like the end of something. The ferry lifted and knocked against its moorings. Spray came off the broken sheets. Spring had arrived while she stood on it.

And Coral Joubert stood on the far bank, soaked to the knees, hair whipping in the river wind, holding one small piece of type above her head — openly, visibly, in front of God and the tribunal windows and every soul in her hometown — and for the first time in eight years she did not make herself small.

She was still catching her breath when the doors of the tribunal building opened, and a clerk in a green sash came hurrying down the steps toward her, calling out a question she had waited all winter to answer:

"Who carries this evidence? Give me a name."
