## 17. Harrow's Men

The knock came at half past four in the morning, which is the hour when honest men are asleep and dishonest ones are either finishing their work or coming to stop yours.

I knew the difference between a neighbor's knock and an official one the way you know the difference between rain and a dropped glass. A neighbor knocks twice, apologetically, with the knuckle. What I heard was the flat of a fist, repeated with the patience of a man being paid by the hour.

"Tobias," I whispered across the workshop. "Back door."

Tobias Voss had been sleeping in the loft since the night we raised the strongbox — he'd refused to go home to his cabin on the flats, saying a man who'd spent seven years keeping his mouth shut had earned a few nights of not being findable. He sat up in the dark, and even in that darkness I could see him doing the arithmetic every old man on that river does: *how fast can these knees carry me, and where.*

"Where would I go?" he said. "Town's the size of a hymnal. There's nowhere to go."

"Then don't go. Be asleep. You've been asleep all night and you know nothing about anything."

"And you?"

"I'm a mapmaker," I said, and heard how it sounded — the first time in my life that sentence had ever been a boast. "I know this building better than the men who built it."

The fist stopped. That was worse than the knocking. A fist that stops means someone has decided the door is taking too long.

* * *

They came through the front, of course. Four of them, constables under Harrow's commission, though only two wore their badges and the one in front — a broad, damp man named Pell whose chief talent was looking sorrowful while doing damage — carried a paper he held out before him like a lantern.

"Warrant," he said. "Search of premises. Unlawful instruments, forged documents, stolen property of the county survey."

It was still dark enough that he couldn't read his own warrant without tilting it toward the lamp, which I found quietly satisfying. I lit the lamp properly for him. Courtesy costs nothing and buys seconds.

"You'll want the atlas plates," I said. "Third drawer, north wall. The copper ones are heavy; take both hands."

Pell blinked at me. Men like Pell are prepared for flight, for tears, for denial — the whole repertoire of the guilty. They are not prepared for a mapmaker who offers directions to his own ruin. I have since learned that this is Elias Crane's real legacy to me: not draftsmanship, not the steady hand, but the understanding that the best way past a man is to let him believe he has already won.

They pulled the drawers. Copper plates sang against each other, that bright ringing note I'd lived inside for seven years, and it went through me like a fishhook. Every plate was a season of my life: the Verity's Landing approaches drawn when I was nineteen and still ruled my lines freehand; the northern territories Elias never finished, finished now in my hand; the false river, the impossible river, drawn twice over because I could not bear to leave it out even knowing what it meant.

A constable named Bricker — young, freckled, the kind of boy who joins the law because he wants to be on the winning side of something — held up the plate of the false river and squinted at it.

"This river ain't right," he said.

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

He looked at me a long moment. I gave him nothing back but the face of a tired apprentice watching his life's work get crated. He put the plate down with more gentleness than the others. I have thought about that boy since. Somewhere in Verity's Landing there is always one Bricker, and the whole future depends on whether anyone ever tells him what the river ain't right *about*.

They worked through the workshop with the thoroughness of men who expected to find guilt and were annoyed to find only ink. Pell read me the charges as if reciting a grocery list: forgery of a judicial endorsement, theft of county property, conspiracy to defraud. I listened with my hands folded, and behind my folded hands I counted.

Four constables. One at the door, one at the crates, Pell, and Bricker. Which left the back stair unwatched, which left Tobias in the loft above us breathing like a man trying to breathe quietly, which left —

Which left the fact that they hadn't come for me at all.

"Where's Tobias Voss?" Pell asked, and I understood the shape of the morning. Harrow wanted the witness, not the mapmaker. Take the old ferryman, sweat him in a cell until memory became confusion, confusion became silence. The plates were plunder. The arrest was the point.

"Voss?" I said. "He went home days ago. His cabin's on the flats. You'd know that if your judge paid his informants' rent."

Pell's jaw tightened. He sent two men anyway — to the flats, to a cold cabin with a cold stove and no old man in it — and that bought me exactly what I needed, which was less.

Because here is what I had not told them, and what I will tell you now: while they were pulling my copper plates from their drawer, I had already taken the thing that mattered. It was flat as a wafer and thin as a lie, and it was buttoned inside my shirt against my ribs, and it was the ledger. Not the original — the original was three feet under the floorboards beneath the press, wrapped in oilcloth, where no warrant written by Judge Harrow would think to look, because Harrow believed secrets lived in drawers and strongboxes and never in the plain dirt under a workingman's floor. What I carried was a copy. Every page, every entry, every signature, pressed in my own hand over three nights while Adela slept upstairs and the ice groaned outside like a ship coming apart.

Elias taught me that too. *Never keep one of anything,* he said. *The river keeps duplicates. That's why it wins.*

* * *

They took Tobias at the dock at dawn.

I watched it from the loft window, lying flat so the glass wouldn't catch me, and I will be honest with you because there is no one left in this story to be dishonest to: I did not move. I lay on the floorboards with the ledger copy against my heart and I watched two constables walk Tobias Voss off the ferry landing with his wrists tied in front of him like a man going out to mend a net, and I did not move.

He never once looked up at the window. That was the worst of it. He knew I was there — of course he knew, the old fox had slept under my roof for a week — and he never once looked up, because looking up would have told the constables where to look next. Seventy-one years old, hands like braided rope, and he walked to jail performing the one role that could save us both: a confused old ferryman hauled in on some misunderstanding, nothing up here but weather.

Harrow's mistake, and I have had years to savor it, was that he thought fear was the same in every man. He assumed Tobias would break the way a young man breaks. But Tobias had spent 1842 to 1849 practicing silence the way other men practice violin, and a cell is just a smaller room to practice in.

Still. Watching him go, I felt something crack in me that had nothing to do with the ice on the river. There is a particular shame in being the one who stays free. It fits badly and it never wears out.

* * *

By noon the workshop had been searched twice, sealed with Harrow's ribbon across the door, and placed under the eye of a single bored constable who spent most of his attention on a ham sandwich. I observed all of this from the attic of the chandlery next door, through a gap in the shingles I had known about since I was fifteen, when Elias sent me up to retrieve a runaway kite and I discovered you could see into our own workshop like a stage play.

Elias. I should have known, standing there with dust in my teeth, that everything else that day would also turn out to be Elias.

Here is what I mean, and I want to set it down carefully because it is the strangest true thing I know:

The workshop's map drawers ran along the north wall, fourteen of them, oak, iron-handled, each deep enough to hold a full folio. Everyone knew those drawers. Clients had seen them for thirty years; judges had leaned on them; Corliss himself had once rested his boots on the lowest one while negotiating ferry charters. The drawers were the most public furniture in Verity's Landing.

What nobody knew — what I did not know until my third year, when a drawer jammed and Elias swore at it in French and then laughed in a way I had never heard him laugh — was that the seventh drawer was deeper than its neighbors by the width of a man. Behind it, when you lifted the false back and stepped into the space where the wall's framing should have been solid, there was a passage. Narrow, black, smelling of cedar and forty years of lamp smoke, running down behind the drawers and out through the foundation into the old ice-house cellar that connected, by a tunnel Elias had certainly not dug himself but had certainly known about, to the waterfront sheds.

*A mapmaker's first rule,* Elias said that day, wiping his eyes, *is that every map lies somewhere. A mapmaker's second rule is that his workshop should follow the same rule.* And then he made me promise never to show another living soul, and I kept that promise for four years, and I am breaking it now only because everyone who needed keeping it from is dead, fled, or on trial.

So when the constables came at half past four in the morning, I did not go out the back door. I went into the seventh drawer.

Picture it. Picture standing inside a wall, in the dark, listening through an inch of oak to four men cataloguing your life's work into a government crate. Picture hearing Bricker say again, puzzled, *"This river ain't right,"* and hearing Pell answer, "Everything in this shop ain't right, that's why we're here." Picture the vibration of their boots through the framing timbers, close enough that I could have counted their heel-nails. I stood in Elias's last design with my hand pressed over the ledger copy and my breath held like a debt, and I understood, finally, what the old man had been building all those years. Not maps. Exits. He had charted every river in the territory and then built one into his own wall, and he had died before he could tell me why a man might need it, and then I had needed it, and it had been waiting, patient as water.

The dead man speaks. I told you that in the first week. He is still speaking.

* * *

I came up through the ice-house cellar into the smell of sawdust and old winter, and from there into the shed, and from the shed into the gray afternoon like a man surfacing from a dive. The town had that particular hush it gets when everyone is indoors pretending not to discuss the same thing. Smoke stood straight up from the chimneys. Somewhere a dog was barking at the jail, which struck me as either loyalty or commentary.

I had three problems and one advantage.

The problems: Harrow had my plates, which meant he had the false river, which meant within a day he would understand what it pointed to and send men to the drowned boat — except the strongbox was no longer there, thanks to Tobias and me and a frozen week of crowbars, so Harrow would dig and find nothing and grow frightened, and frightened powerful men do arithmetic in lives rather than dollars. Second problem: Tobias was in a cell, and every hour I stayed hidden was an hour the old man sat alone answering questions with weather reports. Third problem: the ferry sailed in three days, and Adela's berth existed now only as ink in Harrow's own hand, forged by mine, and if Harrow had found the plates he had perhaps also found the manifest discrepancy, and if he had found that —

That was when I stopped walking.

Because he had. Of course he had. Why else raid at half past four in the morning? Not for plates he could have seized any afternoon with a smile and a subpoena. He raided because the endorsement on the manifest was wrong — the ink aged wrong, the pressure of the pen wrong, the little hesitation-stutter in the H that Harrow himself made and I had failed to counterfeit because I had never seen the man write anything but signatures. Elias would have caught it. Elias caught everything. I had been in too much of a hurry to save my sister, and hurry is the one flaw no craft can forgive.

The advantage, such as it was: Harrow now had to choose what to hunt. A fugitive mapmaker, or a sailing date. He could not do both well, and the ferry was his own escape, and men like Harrow always protect their own exits first. It is the one law of the powerful I have found to be perfectly reliable.

So I did what he would least expect, which was the second thing Elias ever taught me: *when you cannot outrun them, stand still and become scenery.*

I walked down the middle of Water Street, in plain view, wearing Tobias's spare coat and a borrowed hat pulled low, carrying a crate of chandlery goods I had actually purchased with the last of my coin, and I passed the constable on the workshop door close enough to smell his sandwich. He nodded at me. I nodded back. Two men, nodding, in a town where everyone nods, and underneath my ribs the ledger copy lay flat and warm as a second heartbeat.

At the corner I allowed myself one look back at the workshop windows, dark now, ribboned, emptied of seven years. Somewhere in that building, behind the seventh drawer, the passage stood open behind its false back, holding my shape in its darkness like a mold. I thought: *he built it for this. He built it for the day the town came for the truth.* And I thought, with a grief that had teeth: *and he built it knowing he would probably die before using it himself, because Elias Crane was the sort of man who dug tunnels for other people.*

Then I stopped being sentimental, because the church bell began to ring — not the hour, not a fire, but the quick triple stroke they used for assembling the watch — and down at the far end of Water Street I saw Pell and two fresh constables moving fast, and moving not toward the jail, not toward the docks, but toward the chandlery. Toward the attic. Toward the gap in the shingles where someone had recently disturbed forty years of dust.

They had found where I'd been watching from. Which meant they were closer than I'd calculated, which meant Harrow was not protecting his exit after all — or not only his. Which meant the three days I thought I had were maybe one, maybe less, and the reading I had planned for the eve of sailing would have to happen tomorrow, and the copies I had planned to press overnight would have to be pressed tonight, and the town that had spent seven years not seeing would have to see all at once, whether it wished to or not.

I turned the corner with my crate of rope and tar, an honest tradesman in a dishonest world, and I began, calmly, in the direction of the press.

Tomorrow, Verity's Landing would learn what its river kept. Tonight, I had ink to mix.
