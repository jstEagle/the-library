## 2. What the Mistress Never Sent

There is an art to steaming a letter open, and like most arts it looks easier than it is. Too much heat and the paper buckles, curls at the corners like a scolded child. Too little and the glue holds fast, and you're left at two in the morning with a half-lifted flap and a decision you can't unmake. I learned the craft in my first winter at Devereux House, kneeling on the cold flagstones of the coach house with a kettle borrowed from the kitchen and a letter from a solicitor in Queenstown that I had no business reading.

I have had ten years to refine the technique. I mention this not as a boast but as a confession, because a woman who is good at something has usually been doing it too long.

The letter sat in my apron pocket for the better part of a day before I could bring myself to deal with it. I drove Mrs. Devereux into town that morning as though nothing were amiss—her hands folded in her lap, her chin at its customary angle of a woman tolerating the world rather than enjoying it—and all the while that Dublin postmark pressed against my ribs like a second heartbeat. She spoke of the meeting of the Women's Franchise League, of a Miss somebody who had spoken too long and said too little, and I made the appropriate noises from the box seat. I have always been able to make the appropriate noises. It is perhaps my single greatest talent, and I have never once been thanked for it.

* * *

Midnight found me in the still room off the servants' hall, where the lamp burns low and the kettle is always warm because Mrs. O'Riordan cannot sleep without her tisane. I locked the door out of habit, which should tell you something about how long I'd been doing this. I set the letter on the scrubbed table and looked at it for a long moment the way one looks at a wasp on one's sleeve—calculating whether stillness or motion is the greater danger.

The envelope was heavy stock, good paper, the kind that costs more than a maid earns in a week. The hand on the address was firm, upright, a man's hand that took no trouble to be beautiful. *Mrs. Edith Devereux, Devereux House, Blackrock Road, Cork.* No flourish, no courtesy beyond what the post demanded. And beneath the postmark, faint but unmistakable, a name I didn't yet know to dread: *T. Bell.*

I held the envelope over the steam and worked the flap loose with a thin knife, slow as surgery. The paper gave with a whisper. Inside: two sheets, closely written, both sides.

I read it once quickly, the way you read anything you might be interrupted at. Then I read it again slowly, because the first time I was certain I had misread it.

*Madam,* it began, and then did not trouble itself with courtesy for some considerable time.

*You will know the name Bell, though your household has spent forty years pretending otherwise. My father was Thomas Bell senior, junior partner in Devereux & Bell, shipping merchants of this city, until the year 1874, when he was accused—by your husband, and by none other—of embezzlement, stripped of his share, driven from Cork, and dead within five years of a broken heart and a broken name. I was eleven years old when we left. I remember the dock. I remember your husband's face.*

*I have spent my life acquiring proof of what was done. My father kept papers. Others kept theirs. What was taken from him was not merely his investment but his good name, and the fortune your family has lived upon since—that house, those horses, that motor car you ride about in—is built on the theft of it.*

*I do not write to threaten. I write to inform you that I will be coming to Cork, and that when I come, I intend to see restitution made. You may choose the manner of it. That courtesy I extend because no one extended any to my father.*

*You will not answer this letter. There is nothing you can say that I wish to hear. But you will want to prepare your household, madam, for what is coming.*

*Yours, etc.,*
*Thomas Bell*

*Yours, etc.* Even in ruin and grievance, the man had manners. Something in that small civility frightened me more than the rest of it put together. Rage you can brace against. Courtesy arriving with a knife is another matter entirely.

I sat in the still room until the kettle went cold, and I thought about the arithmetic of households. A great house is a machine for keeping things quiet. Every servant knows where the bodies are—not the flesh-and-blood kind, mostly, but the paper kind, the ledger kind, the kind that live in drawers and solicitors' offices and the memories of old men in port-wine parlors. We keep them because our bread depends on the machine running smooth. Ten years I had kept Mrs. Devereux's letters, sorted the poison from the gossip, burned what needed burning, and told myself it was service. Loyalty, even.

But there is a difference between keeping a secret and being kept inside one, and standing there with Thomas Bell's accusation in my hands, I felt the walls of the thing close around me like a coat that no longer fit.

Because here is what I knew, you understand. Here is what a decade of lamplight had taught me. Mrs. Devereux's correspondence ran on two rails: the public rail, all committees and suffrage and the endless polite warfare with the *Cork Examiner*, and beneath it a private rail, older, darker, along which certain names travelled rarely and carefully. *Bell* was one of those names. Twice in ten years I had seen it—in an old letter from a solicitor marked *closed*, and once in a nightmare of the mistress's own dictation, a draft she'd torn up herself, muttering. I had filed both away under *before my time, not my business*. That is the liturgy of service: *not my business, not my business, not my business.* Say it enough times and it almost sounds like a prayer.

Thomas Bell had just made it my business. He had posted it to me, in effect—because who else in that house would open it first? He had made a driver and reader-of-letters the first witness to his father's ghost.

* * *

Morning came grey and soft, rain threatening and not delivering, the harbor holding its breath under a lid of cloud. I brought the mistress her tea and laid out the day's letters on the silver tray, all except one, which traveled in my waistcoat pocket like a coal I couldn't put down. She was at her writing desk already, upright as a mast, her spectacles low on her nose. Seventy-one years old and she sat straighter than I ever have in my life.

"Rosalind." She didn't look up. "The post."

"Nothing of consequence, ma'am. A circular from the League. An invitation from the Beamishes. The gas bill."

"The gas bill is always of consequence. It is the only honest document in this house." She signed something, blotted it, and only then raised her eyes to me over the rims of her spectacles. She has eyes the color of a January sea, and she has used them to make stronger women than me look away. "Anything else?"

And here I must record, honestly, that I hesitated. One breath, maybe two. Long enough for her to notice—she notices everything; it is her profession, in a way, noticing what men prefer unnoticed. I said, "Nothing else, ma'am," and heard my own voice perform its little trick, the appropriate noise, and hated it.

She went back to her writing. I withdrew. And if she suspected—if some instinct older than either of us told her that her mail had been tampered with—she gave no sign. That is the other thing about Edith Devereux: she keeps her own counsel so completely that you can never tell whether you've deceived her or merely been permitted to go on believing you have.

* * *

I expected her to burn the letter. That would have been the clean way, the way of a woman with forty years' practice at burying things. Instead, three days later, she wrote a reply.

I know because I watched her do it—or rather, I watched the machinery of it. Tuesday evening she dismissed the household early, unusual in itself. Wednesday morning the ash in her study grate was not the ordinary grey of newspaper and envelopes but held fragments of heavier stock, cream-colored, the same paper she used for everything important. And Thursday, the Dublin postmark came again—a different hand this time, but I knew the weight of the envelope, the good paper, the firm upright address. Her reply had gone and something had come back, and whatever it was, it never reached the tray.

That night I took my chance. She had retired early, complaining of her hip, and the study stood dark. I let myself in with the key I am not supposed to have—I'll spare you the inventory of things I am not supposed to have; it would take longer than this chapter—and went straight to the grate.

The ashes were fresh. She had burned it that evening, after I'd seen her to her room. Among the char I found three fragments large enough to read, and I knelt there on the Turkish rug with a candle stub like a burglar in my own house, piecing them together in my palm.

One fragment said: *...whatever you believe occurred between your father and my late husband...*

Another: *...the courts settled these matters in 1875, and I will not rehearse...*

And the third, the smallest, the one I have carried ever since like a stone in the boot:

*...if you come to Cork, sir, you will find nothing here but grief, and I advise you—*

Advise. Not warn. *Advise.* As though he were a lost traveler asking directions and she a helpful soul at a crossroads. As though the whole monstrous weight of it were a matter of etiquette.

I sat back on my heels. The candle guttered. Somewhere below, the house ticked and settled, that great patient organism of oak and plaster digesting its secrets, and I understood—with the flat, cold clarity that comes at midnight and does not lie—that I had been wrong about the nature of my work all these years.

I had thought I was the keeper of the silences. Standing guard, choosing what passed and what burned, a customs officer at the border of her private world. Flattering, that. Necessary-feeling. But a keeper implies ownership, and I owned nothing. The silences were hers. They had always been hers. I was not the wall around the garden; I was a brick in it. And bricks, when the garden is condemned, are pulled down and carted away with everything else, and nobody asks the brick what it thinks.

Some silences were never mine to keep. Worse: some were never mine to *give*. If Thomas Bell came to Cork—and he would come; men who write *yours, etc.* after forty years of grievance do not stay home—then the truth would arrive in this house like weather, indifferent to all my careful architecture. And I would have to choose, then, whether I was the mistress's brick or my own woman.

I swept the ashes flat with my sleeve, snuffed the candle, and let myself out. In the hall the grandfather clock struck two, each note falling into the dark like a coin down a well.

Three days later, driving her home from the League meeting along the Blackrock road, I saw her stiffen slightly in the seat beside me—just a fractional change, a bird dog going still—and follow with her eyes a man standing at the tram terminus. Grey coat. Firm upright posture. A face I couldn't see, but a stillness I recognized from the letter itself: the stillness of a man who has finished waiting.

"Drive on, Rosalind," she said quietly. "Not too fast. Let him see the house."

I drove on. And behind us, unhurried, the man in the grey coat turned and began to walk—toward Devereux House, toward forty years of buried paper, toward everything I had spent a decade deciding was not my business.
