## 2. Five Years of Practiced Silence

There was a game my mother used to play with me at the kitchen table, back before the war had a name we were allowed to say out loud. She called it *Smaller Questions*. The rules were simple: she would ask me something ordinary—*What did you learn in school today?*—and I was not allowed to answer. Instead I had to ask her a question so small, so dull, so perfectly unremarkable that no one listening at the window could possibly find it interesting.

*What did you learn?*

*I don't know, Mama. Did you know the baker's cat had kittens?*

*Did she? How many?*

*Four. Do you think it will rain tomorrow?*

By the time the conversation ended, neither of us had said anything at all, and we had both laughed until our ribs hurt, because there is a particular comedy in two people working very hard to be nobody. I was nine years old and thought it was only a game. It took me five more years to understand that my mother was not playing. She was drilling me, the way other mothers drilled their children in piano scales or catechism. She was teaching me the one skill that would keep me alive in Vellmark under occupation: how to be forgettable.

I have been practicing ever since. I am, if I may say so without vanity, quite good at it.

* * *

The morning after the checkpoint incident—the one where the guard held my papers a moment too long, the one I told myself meant nothing—I walked to the ministry by my usual route, which is to say the route that is never exactly the same twice. There is an art to this. If you take the same street every day, you become a fixture, and fixtures get noticed precisely because they are always there. But if you vary your route too much, you become a pattern of a different kind, the kind that makes a man in a gray coat reach for his notebook. So I varied it the way a river varies its course: always moving, never announcing it.

That Tuesday I went down Ferenc Street past the shuttered bookshop, cut through the courtyard behind the tobacco kiosk—old Piotr nodded at me, or perhaps at the space just over my shoulder; Piotr had perfected the art of nodding at nothing, which in Vellmark was practically a civic honor—and emerged onto the boulevard half a block from the ministry steps.

The city was wearing its winter face. Snow had come in the night and lain down on Vellmark like a sheet pulled up to the chin, and the whole town pretended to sleep beneath it. Smoke rose from chimneys in thin, apologetic columns. A tram rattled past with three passengers, all of them studying their boots as though the boots had recently confessed to something.

At the ministry door, Herr Brandt was waiting for me, pretending to adjust his glove.

"You're late," he murmured, which meant: *you're early, well done.*

"I'm always late," I murmured back, which meant: *I know.*

Brandt was the senior clerk in the diplomatic registry, a small round man with the complexion of a man who had spent thirty years indoors apologizing. He handed me the day's pouch—a leather satchel, heavier than it looked, the way all official things are heavier than they look—and I signed the ledger with the name on my papers, which was almost my name, which was close enough. In occupied Vellmark, close enough was a country all its own, and most of us lived there.

"Mind the third bridge," Brandt said, in the tone of a man discussing the weather. "They've been slow lately."

Slow meaning thorough. Slow meaning a young guard with cold hands and something to prove. I nodded and took the pouch, and felt again that small cold weight settle behind my breastbone, the one that had been there since yesterday, since the checkpoint, since the man across the square who had watched me not-watch him.

But that comes later. First I have to tell you about my father, because everything I know about carrying things quietly began with the day he stopped being allowed to carry anything at all.

* * *

In the spring of 1941, they came for Papa on a Thursday.

I remember it was Thursday because Thursdays Mama made plum dumplings, and the smell of them was still in the house when the black car pulled up outside, and afterward—for years afterward—I could not smell plum dumplings without my chest going tight, which is a cruel thing for a fruit to do to a person.

There were four of them. They did not shout. That was the thing people got wrong about arrests, I think, from the pictures in the old newsreels—they imagined banging and bellowing, doors kicked in. But by '41 the occupation had grown efficient, and efficiency is quiet. One man knocked politely. Another stood by the gate smoking, unhurried, a man on his break. The one who spoke to Papa had a soft voice and a notebook, and he asked questions in the manner of a doctor confirming symptoms he had already diagnosed.

Papa put on his good coat. He kissed Mama on both cheeks—he never did that, he was a handshake man, a clap-on-the-shoulder man—and he knelt down in front of Marta and me and said, "Look after your mother," and then, to me specifically, because I was twelve and he believed I was listening harder than I was: "August. Remember what we practiced."

I didn't understand. I said, "The dumplings?" because the pot was on the stove and my mind, at twelve, went where it went.

Something moved across Papa's face—not a smile, but the place where a smile would have gone if the room had permitted one. "Yes," he said. "The dumplings."

They took him away in the black car, and the man by the gate finished his cigarette before getting in, and the whole operation had taken eleven minutes. I know because Mama counted. Counting was her way of fighting. She could not stop them, so she measured them, and there is a stubborn dignity in that.

He was gone four months before we learned where. Eighteen months before we were allowed a letter, censored into lace, every second line blacked out so that what remained read like a poem about weather. *Dear ones. The work here is steady. The bread is adequate. Tell August the pigeons will remember.* We never kept pigeons. Somewhere in that sentence was a message, and somewhere in the message was a man, and somewhere in the man was my father, and none of us could reach any of it. That is what occupation does first: not to bodies but to sentences. It takes the words away and leaves the paper.

Mama changed after that Thursday, though she would have denied it with her last breath. She grew—there is no better word for it—*smooth*. Not soft; smooth. Grief rolled off her like water off waxed cloth. She answered the neighborhood committee's questions about her loyalty with smaller questions about their health. She smiled at the right officials with the exact warmth of a woman thinking about laundry. When the requisition notice came for our upstairs rooms, she thanked the billeting officer for his consideration and served him coffee and asked, so innocently, whether his superiors preferred the blue curtains or the green, and by the end of the hour he had decided the rooms were unsuitable after all—too drafty, he said—and left, and Mama washed the cups and said nothing, and I understood that I had just watched a duel fought entirely in teacups.

"August," she told me once, folding sheets in the dimness of the parlor, her voice level as a shelf. "A question is a door. Every door can be answered, but not every door must be opened. When they knock, hand them a window instead. Windows are harder to walk through."

"And if they insist?"

"Then you give them a corridor," she said, "that goes nowhere." She smoothed a crease from the linen. "People follow corridors. It's the doors that frighten them."

I was fourteen and I wrote it down in my head, in the ledger I kept behind my eyes, and I have been handing out windows and corridors ever since. It sounds like deceit. Perhaps it is. But I have come to believe that in a country where telling the truth is a capital offense, silence is not cowardice—it is a form of housekeeping. You keep the truth swept clean, tucked in a drawer, safe from muddy boots, until such a day as it can be brought out and aired. My mother kept our family's truth in that drawer for five years. She polished it in secret. She never once let it rattle.

* * *

Marta chose differently. I want to say that fairly, because fairness was scarce in those years and someone ought to have saved some.

My sister was two years older than I and built from entirely different timber. Where I learned to fold myself flat, Marta learned to stand up straighter. Where I heard Mama's lessons and thought *survival*, Marta heard them and thought—this is the only way I can put it—*insult*. She was seventeen when Papa was taken, and she had his temper and Mama's memory, and the combination made her dangerous in a household that could not afford danger.

She argued with Mama the way weather argues with a coastline. Quietly at first, then all at once.

"You want us to be furniture," she said one night, when the billeting officer's boots were audible through the ceiling, pacing his borrowed rooms above us. "Tables don't grieve, Mama. Chairs don't remember. Well, I'm not a chair."

"You're a girl who wants to see her father again," Mama said, not looking up from her mending, "and girls who shout don't get letters."

"He's been gone a year. What letters? You dust the drawer where hope used to be and call it patience."

"Marta." And Mama said her name in a voice I had never heard, low and final as a lid closing, and Marta went white and sat down, and the argument died there, but not really—it just went underground, where all the real arguments in our family lived, in the tunnels beneath the teacups.

Two years later Marta took the position at the Grand Hotel. Social secretary to Administrator Vogel's office. Everyone in the neighborhood had an opinion, delivered in whispers: *collaborator*, some said; *clever girl*, said others; *poor family*, said the kindest, which stung worst of all. Mama said nothing, which from Mama was a sermon. I said nothing, which from me was simply habit.

But I remember the evening she came to tell us, standing in the parlor in her new coat—good wool, the kind of coat that announces itself—and she looked at Mama and then at me, and her chin was up but her hands were not steady, and she said, "Someone has to be inside the rooms where they decide things. You've taught me every trick you know, Mama. Don't pretend you didn't build me for this."

And Mama set down her mending and looked at her daughter for a long moment, and whatever passed between them passed without a single word, which was, after all, the family language.

I told myself then that Marta had chosen the enemy's table. It has taken me until now—until this winter, until certain events I have not yet told you about—to wonder whether she had chosen the longest corridor of all, one that runs straight through the middle of the house we were trying to survive in. Sisters are like that. Just when you've finished deciding what they are, they turn out to be a sentence you misread.

* * *

Here is the part I do not tell people, the part I keep even from myself on most days, folded small in the drawer behind my eyes:

Five years of practiced silence works. That is the terrible thing. It works beautifully. I have crossed checkpoints with contraband in my bag and boredom on my face. I have watched men I knew be marched through the square and kept my gaze at the regulation height—shop windows, boot level, anywhere but their eyes—because looking is a transaction and I could not afford the purchase. I have become so skilled at being no one that sometimes, alone in my rented room, I take inventory and find the stock running low. August Galloway, son of Tomas, keeper of pigeons that never existed. Somewhere under all the smoothness there is a boy who smelled plum dumplings and thought the world was a kitchen. I check for him the way you check for a pulse. Still there. Faint.

Grief does not heal in a country like ours. It wears smooth, like a stone in a stream, and you carry it in your pocket until you forget it's a stone and think it's just the shape of your pocket. That is what five years taught me. That is what Mama paid for, with her smooth face and her unanswered grief, so that her children might live to be smooth too.

And it might have gone on forever—me, my windows and corridors, my pouches and ledgers, my life spent being nobody with great precision—if Herr Brandt had remained a healthy man.

But Brandt was dying. I had known it since autumn, the way you know things in ministries: the cough he hid behind the ledger, the gray creeping up past his collar, the way he'd begun to look at documents not as paper but as evidence, as testimony, as things that outlive the men who file them. Dying men in occupied countries get strange ideas. They start wanting to hand things over.

That Wednesday, when I brought back the empty pouch, Brandt did not take it. He stood in the registry doorway with his glove in his hands, turning it, turning it, and he looked at me with an expression I had never seen on his apologetic face—something fierce and frightened and resolved, a man standing at a door he had spent a lifetime keeping shut.

"Galloway," he said. Not my cover name. My actual name, spoken softly, in a government building, which was either madness or trust, and I would spend the rest of the week finding out which.

"We need to talk," said Herr Brandt. "Not here. And not—" he glanced down the corridor, at the windows, at the world, "—in answers. For once, courier, I need you to listen."

Behind my breastbone, the small cold weight shifted, and settled, and waited.
