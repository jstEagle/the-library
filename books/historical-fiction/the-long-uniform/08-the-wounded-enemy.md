## 8. The Wounded Enemy

They brought him in on a Thursday, on the back of a supply truck that had no business carrying anything but flour and boot polish. Ilse saw the truck from the laundry yard, where she spent her days now folding other people's sheets under another country's flag stitched at the collar. She watched two military policemen drag a stretcher down the tailgate, and she knew before they reached the doors — by the angle of the leg, by the way the blanket over his chest did not rise — that the man was either dead or very close to it.

She kept folding. That was the discipline of hiding: you kept your hands busy and your eyes dull, and you became furniture, like the mattresses, like the crates of requisitioned bandages stacked in the east corridor. A deserter learns to be a thing that no one looks at twice.

But she counted. Two MPs. One driver. No officer. They wheeled him into Ward Four, which was the ward furthest from the officers' quarters and closest to the morgue, and someone with a clipboard said something to someone else, and then the MPs left, and the man lay alone in a hospital that was not allowed to heal him.

Ilse finished the sheet in her hands. Folded it into a crisp rectangle. Set it on the pile. Then she went to find Vesper Okonkwo.

* * *

"The order came through this morning," Vesper said. They were standing in the dispensary, which smelled of carbolic and old wood, and Vesper's voice had the flatness Ilse had learned to read as fury held at arm's length. "Prisoner-of-war, enemy national, wounded in an engagement that officially did not occur. He is to be 'held for transfer.' No treatment authorized beyond 'maintenance of life.'"

"Maintenance of life," Ilse repeated.

"Which means water, if anyone remembers to bring it." Vesper took off their spectacles and cleaned them on their apron, a gesture Ilse had seen a hundred times and still could not decode — grief, or arithmetic, or both. "He has a femoral wound. I saw the intake note. He'll be dead in two days if nobody touches him."

"Then somebody has to touch him."

Vesper put the spectacles back on and looked at her for a long moment. In the months since the laundry sacks, since the uniform that made her one of them, Ilse had learned the geography of Vesper's face: the ledger-keeper's calm, the nurse's impatience, and underneath both, a stubbornness like bedrock. What she saw now was all three at once.

"You understand what you're volunteering," Vesper said. "If they catch us treating him, it isn't a reprimand. It's treason. For you especially."

"For me especially," Ilse agreed. "I'm already a traitor. I'm efficient at it now."

Something moved at the corner of Vesper's mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go if there were time for one. "Tonight," Vesper said. "After the night orderly makes his rounds. Adaeze will keep watch at the stairwell. And Ilse—" They paused. "You don't have to be the one in the room. If he's wearing the wrong uniform, if it's—"

"It's exactly why it has to be me," Ilse said. She heard her own voice come out steadier than she felt. "You think I haven't lain in a ward like that? You think there wasn't a hospital outside Metz where men in my uniform died because the nurses had orders too?" She stopped. The words had come out faster than she intended, and now they were out, and Vesper was looking at her with that terrible gentleness that made her feel seen all the way through.

"All right," Vesper said quietly. "Tonight."

* * *

Ward Four at midnight was a country of its own. Moonlight came through the high windows in pale trapezoids and lay across the empty beds like unopened letters. The war had emptied this wing when it requisitioned the town; now the emptiness itself was requisitioned, assigned, inventoried. Ilse moved between the beds with a lamp turned low and a basin of warm water balanced against her hip, and she thought: this is what I am now. A woman who steals warmth.

He was worse than the intake note suggested. Young — younger than her, maybe twenty-two, twenty-three — with a clerk's soft hands and a clerk's ink stain on the middle finger of his right hand, though the rest of him was caked in mud and someone else's blood. The femoral wound had been dressed in the field, badly, the tourniquet tied by a man who cared more about stopping the bleeding than about keeping the leg alive. His lips were gray. His breathing was the shallow, fast breathing that Ilse had heard so many times that it had its own grammar, its own vocabulary of endings.

She cut away the field dressing as gently as she could. He stirred and made a sound — not a word, just a sound, the sound a man makes when he surfaces from unconsciousness into pain — and his eyes opened halfway, unfocused, finding her shape against the lamplight.

"Mutti," he said. Mother. In her own language.

Ilse's hands went still in the basin water.

Of course. Of course he was hers. Of course the war had taken a boy who spoke her mother tongue and dropped him bleeding into a hospital three hundred kilometers from home, in a town where she wore the enemy's uniform and answered to a name that wasn't quite hers anymore. She looked at his ink-stained fingers, his gray lips, the wound that stank faintly of the beginning of gangrene, and she thought of every man she had failed — the ones at Metz, the ones on the train, the ones whose names she had never known because the ledgers burned before she ever saw them.

"I'm here," she said, in German, and her voice cracked on the second word like ice on a pond.

His eyes found her face properly then. Confusion first — the wrong ceiling, the wrong light, the wrong everything — and then something else, something that might have been recognition or might only have been the animal relief of hearing your own language when you are dying far from home.

"Wasser," he whispered.

She gave him water, a little at a time, the way you do when a man is too weak for more. Then she set to work. Vesper had left instructions written in pencil on a scrap of paper, in English, in that small precise hand: clean, debride, the sulfa powder in the third tin — the good tin, the one Vesper had been hoarding against some future emergency that had apparently arrived tonight. Ilse worked by rote and by memory, and her memory was full of wards, and every bed she passed in her mind held a man she had not saved, and she worked faster.

The boy fainted twice. Both times she brought him back with ammonia and cold water and talking — talking, in German, nonsense talk, about the weather, about bread, about anything, because she had learned somewhere that a dying man will hold onto a voice the way a drowning man holds onto a plank.

Near dawn, his fever spiked and broke, and he slept — real sleep, not the gray counterfeit — and Ilse sat on the edge of the next bed and put her face in her hands and shook, silently, the way you learn to shake when any noise is death.

Adaeze appeared at the foot of the bed with the particular lightness of a girl who has been standing watch for four hours and has decided not to complain about it. She looked at the sleeping prisoner, then at Ilse.

"He'll live?" Adaeze asked, in a whisper.

"Maybe. The leg, maybe. Him, probably." Ilse wiped her face with her sleeve. "Why are you smiling?"

"Because you talked to him all night in your language and he didn't die." Adaeze sat down on the bed beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. "That seems like a kind of miracle to me."

"It's surgery," Ilse said.

"It's both," Adaeze said, with the serene authority of the young, and Ilse found she hadn't the strength to argue.

* * *

They built a system, because systems were how you survived. Vesper falsified nothing — that was the beauty of it, the terrible elegance. Ward Four was officially empty. An empty ward required no rations, no dressings, no attention. So everything Tomas received was subtracted from elsewhere: a spoonful less broth in the officers' kitchen, a dressing counted as spoiled, a tin of sulfa powder that had, on paper, been ruined by damp. Vesper kept a second ledger for these thefts — a small one, hidden differently — and when Ilse saw it, she understood something about the woman she had followed out of the laundry sacks: Vesper Okonkwo did not break rules so much as balance them, moving weight from one side of the scale to the other, and calling the result mercy.

Ilse took the nights. It made sense — she needed less sleep than the others, having spent two years sleeping badly in ditches and haylofts, and her German let her calm him when the fever dreams came. The fever dreams always came. He fought battles in his sleep, small ones, losing ones, and called out names of men Ilse suspected were dead, and once, heartbreakingly, a street name — Lindenstraße, he said, Lindenstraße, number fourteen — and she understood that he was trying to get home even in his sleep, and she said, yes, Lindenstraße, we're nearly there, and he settled.

By day he was a body in an empty ward. By night he was a person, and persons had names, and on the fourth night he gave up his: Tomas. Tomas Reiner. Clerk, third class. He said it the way soldiers say it, rank first, as if the rank were the self, and Ilse told him her name — just Ilse, no rank, no regiment, because she had none anymore — and he frowned at the gap where her identity should have been and was too polite, or too weak, to ask.

He asked instead, on the fifth night, whether she was a prisoner too.

"No," she said.

"A collaborator?"

The word landed like a slap. She kept her hands steady on the basin. "No."

He studied her. Fever had thinned him, but his eyes were a clerk's eyes, careful, cataloguing. "Then what?" he asked. "This is a military hospital. I'm the enemy. Someone is washing my wounds at night, in secret, in my language. There is a word for that, and it isn't nurse."

Ilse wrung out the cloth. Laid it across his forehead. Took her time about it.

"There's a word," she said finally. "But it costs too much to say in wartime. Ask me again after the armistice."

"If there is an armistice."

"When there is one," she corrected him, and was surprised to find she believed it.

He almost smiled. His lips were cracked and the smile hurt him, and he did it anyway. "You sound like my sister," he said. "She corrects everyone. It's how I knew she loved me — she never let a sentence of mine stand as spoken."

"Then she sounds like a good woman."

"The best." His eyes drifted toward the window, toward the moonlight. "Is it true they're going to burn the records? Before the delegation comes?"

Ilse's hands went still again. This boy, half-dead in an unauthorized bed, knew about the burning. Of course he did. He was a clerk. Clerks know what happens to paper; clerks are the ones who carry it to the stove.

"Who told you that?"

"Nobody. I heard the MPs talking when they carried me in." He turned his head on the pillow, wincing. "Both sides do it, you know. At the end of a war. All the unofficial lists, the corrections, the names that don't match the official totals — it all goes into the stoves, and then the peace is signed over clean books." He said it without bitterness, the way you report weather. "I filed things for burning once. In Verdun sector. Three crates. I didn't look inside them. I wish now that I had."

"Why?"

"Because I've been lying in this bed for five nights thinking about those crates," Tomas said, "and I can't stop wondering how many names were in them. How many mothers got a letter saying missing, presumed, when the truth was sitting in a crate waiting for a match." His breath caught; the wound punished him for talking. He rode the pain out, jaw tight, and continued. "Whoever keeps names here — whoever writes them down — that person should get them out. Before the matches."

Ilse sat very still. Outside, somewhere across the dark town, a train shunted in the rail yard, coupling clanking against coupling, the sound of the war's machinery still running, still counting.

"You should sleep," she said.

"I'm a clerk," Tomas said, closing his eyes. "Sleeping is the one thing I've never been any good at."

* * *

On the seventh night, Vesper came down to Ward Four during Ilse's shift, which was against the system, because the system existed precisely so that Vesper would not be seen entering an empty ward at hours when officers still walked the corridors. But Vesper came anyway, carrying the small second ledger, and stood at the foot of Tomas's bed reading him the way they read everything — completely, in silence, for a long time.

"He's asking the right questions," Vesper said at last.

"He's a clerk. Clerks ask about paperwork."

"Clerks also make paperwork." Vesper glanced at the door, then lowered their voice further. "Ilse. The transit papers we'd need to move anything out of this town — passes, manifests, authorization stamps. Do you know who understands that language better than any forger in the province?"

Ilse looked at the boy in the bed. The ink stain on his finger. The careful eyes, now open, watching them both with the expression of a man following a conversation conducted slightly above his head and catching most of it anyway.

"You want to ask the prisoner," she said, "to forge documents. To help smuggle contraband past his own army's allies. While he can't walk."

"I want to ask him," Vesper said, "if he's willing."

There was a silence. The moonlight lay across the beds like unopened letters, and somewhere upstairs a clock struck two, and Tomas Reiner, clerk third class, cleared his throat.

"In my sector," he said, in careful, accented English that he had apparently been saving for exactly this moment, "we used a certain stamp for priority freight. I drew it perhaps four hundred times." He looked from Vesper to Ilse and back. "I assume you have paper and a steady hand. Because I have been lying here for seven nights listening to this hospital count its inventory, and I promise you—" the cracked almost-smile again, costing him, given freely— "nobody counts like a clerk."

Vesper took out the small ledger and opened it to a blank page.

Ilse stood in the moonlight between them — the deserter, the keeper of names, the enemy who was not one — and understood that whatever happened next, whatever they carried and wherever they carried it, the family had just gotten larger by one, and there was no longer any version of the story in which she walked away from it.

Above them, faint through the floorboards, boots crossed the corridor. Someone was awake. Someone was walking toward Ward Four, unhurried, at two in the morning, when no one had any reason to be.

Vesper closed the ledger without a sound.
