## 15. Tomas Runs

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and Tomas Ravensworth walked through the wet streets of the river quarter with his collar up and his heart going like a paddle wheel.

He had not slept. He had sat in his rented room above the cooper's shop with a single candle burning down to a stub, turning the thing over in his mind until it wore smooth as river stone: *I will tell Brandt everything. The orchard, the cellar, the girl, the ship, the night, the tide. All of it.* And in exchange — in exchange he would walk out of that office with his indenture papers torn in half, or better, signed over, paid off by the Company's own hand. Free. The word had lost its taste years ago; now it came back to him sweet as stolen sugar.

*It isn't betrayal,* he told himself for the hundredth time, and for the hundredth time the telling did not take. *It's arithmetic.* Hazel would run her course whether he spoke or not — she was like that, she always had been, she'd dive deeper and hold her breath longer than any sensible person and come up with the pearl in her fist. Father had made his bed in the orchard dirt decades ago. Ilse was never Tomas's concern; he'd known the child three days and she was a stranger's ghost, a name on a manifest, a weight in someone else's ledger. But if he said nothing, they would all be taken anyway — Brandt would find out, Brandt always found out — and Tomas would be taken with them, swept into the same net, drowned in the same river of consequences. Whereas if he spoke first, if he came forward as a man cooperating with the authorities rather than a conspirator caught out —

That was the story. That was the shape he'd hammered the truth into so it would fit inside him without cutting.

The Meridian offices didn't open until eight, but Brandt kept early hours; Tomas had seen the lamp burning in the second-floor window at six on more mornings than he could count, back when watching that window had felt like a duty instead of a debt. He cut along the fish market before it woke, past stalls shuttered and dripping, the cobbles slick with herring brine and last night's spilled wine. A cat watched him from atop a barrel with yellow eyes and made no sound, and somehow that silence felt like judgment.

At the corner of Weir Lane he stopped.

Not because anything was wrong. Because something was *right*, horribly right, in the way that a trap is right — sprung, clean, efficient. Two Company men stood beneath the awning of the closed chandlery, and between them, on his knees in a puddle, was a man Tomas knew.

Jorin Vale. A courier. One of Father's people — Tomas had seen him twice at the orchard, once carrying a crate of apples down into the cellar that clinked when it shouldn't have, once standing at the treeline at dusk scanning the river road with the patience of a man who had done it a hundred times. A quiet, competent man with grey in his beard and a limp in his left knee from some old injury he'd never spoken of.

Now Jorin knelt in the puddle with his hands bound behind him, and one of the agents — Tomas knew his face from the office corridors, a heavy man called Dresch — held a paper in front of Jorin's face, reading aloud from it in the flat tone of a clerk confirming figures.

Tomas should have walked away. He knew the lane, knew the shadows, knew that at this hour no one looked at a young man in a wet coat hurrying anywhere. He could have turned down Tanner's Row and been at Brandt's door in ten minutes, and whatever happened in Weir Lane would have remained a story he never learned the end of.

Instead he stepped back into the doorway of the chandlery's neighbor, a rope-maker's shed, and pressed himself against the tarred planks, and watched.

"—the names," Dresch was saying. "We have the names. What we want is the route. Where does the cargo move, Vale? Where does it sleep?"

Jorin said nothing. His breathing was loud even across the lane, a wet whistle through his nose — they'd broken something in his face, Tomas realized, the nose itself perhaps. Blood had dried in two dark lines from his nostrils to his chin, and his shirt, Tomas saw with a strange distant clarity, was the good one, the one a man wears when he expects to be somewhere that matters tonight.

"I'll ask differently." The third man stepped out of the awning's shadow, and Tomas's stomach dropped through the cobbles and into the mud beneath the town. Not Dresch. Not any of the ordinary faces. It was Herr Falkenrath, who was not listed on any roster Tomas had ever copied, who appeared in the Meridian building perhaps four times a year and was spoken of, when he was spoken of at all, in the lowered voice reserved for weather at sea and other forces beyond negotiation. He was a small man, neat, with spectacles and soft hands, and he crouched down beside Jorin Vale as though they were two gardeners examining the same sickly plant.

"You carried for the old network eleven years," Falkenrath said gently. "Eleven years is a long marriage, Herr Vale. I understand loyalty. I am not your enemy — I am simply the man asking what you already know, and offering you the only morning you're ever going to get. The route. One sentence. Then you go home, and I burn my copy of this paper, and we are both men who were never here."

And Jorin Vale — bleeding, kneeling, ruined — smiled. It was a terrible smile, half of it blood, but it was unmistakably a smile, and he said, in a voice cracked but clear enough to carry across the lane:

"The route sleeps where you'll never dig, and it moves on nights you'll never guess. Ask your boy instead. He's standing in the ropemaker's doorway."

The world went white and silent.

Tomas did not remember moving. He was three steps into the open lane before his mind caught up with his legs, and then it was too late, because Falkenrath had turned, unhurried, adjusting his spectacles against the grey light, and found him with eyes that held no surprise at all.

"Ah," Falkenrath said. "There he is."

"No—" Tomas's voice came out as a croak. "No, I'm not— I don't know what he means, I was just walking, I—"

"Herr Vale has a poet's imagination," Falkenrath said pleasantly. "It's why we're executing him."

The word went into Tomas like a blade slipped between ribs — entered cleanly, hurt late. *Executing.* He stood in the middle of Weir Lane in the dripping grey morning and heard the word again in Falkenrath's mild voice, and understood that it had not been a figure of speech, because Dresch had already drawn his pistol and was checking it with the bored competence of a man preparing a tool, and the third agent had moved to the mouth of the lane to watch the street, and none of it was theater. There was no cell waiting. No trial, no transport, no ledger entry. The Company did not put couriers in its books. It put them in the ground.

"He told you nothing," Falkenrath continued, conversational, almost kind, as if continuing a seminar. "Eleven years, and he gave us a poem. Do you understand what that costs me, Herr Ravensworth? Patience. And patience, in my profession, is a budget like any other." He sighed and straightened his cuffs. "You may look away if you like. Most people can't."

Tomas could not look away. That was the horror of it — not the pistol, not the puddle, but his own eyes, which refused to close, which took in every detail with the merciless fidelity of the ledgers he'd spent three years copying: the way Jorin Vale lifted his chin, the way his bound hands flexed once behind his back, the way his lips moved around something — a word, a prayer, a name, Tomas would wonder for the rest of his life which — and the crack of the shot that knocked all three possibilities out of the world forever.

Jorin went sideways into the puddle and did not finish falling. The water took him the rest of the way, slowly, rocking him like a mother.

Tomas made a sound. He would not remember making it. Dresch holstered the pistol and looked at him with something that was almost sympathy, the way a butcher might regard a calf that had wandered into the abattoir on its own feet.

"Now," Falkenrath said, taking off his spectacles and polishing them on his handkerchief, blinking myopically at the grey sky as though the morning were merely adequate. "Let us discuss your morning, Herr Ravensworth. You were coming to see Agent Brandt. Don't trouble yourself denying it — you've been coming to see Brandt for months, and a man only walks toward a door that often when he intends to enter it. You were coming to sell us the orchard." He replaced the spectacles and smiled, and the smile was the worst thing Tomas had ever seen, because it held no cruelty in it at all, only bookkeeping. "And here is what you must understand about that transaction. It was worth something yesterday. Last week it was worth a great deal. But we have Vale's network now — most of it — and we have the harbor manifests, and we have a girl in the Palatinate archives who reads our codes faster than the men who wrote them, which is its own problem for another day. Your information, Herr Ravensworth, is becoming redundant. Do you know what happens to redundant assets?"

Tomas's mouth worked. Nothing came.

"They are not shot," Falkenrath said. "Shooting is for men who matter. Redundant assets are simply *closed*. An entry is made, a file is stamped, and one day you notice that no one at the office answers your letters, and your room above the cooper's shop has been let to someone else, and there is a new clerk copying manifests where you used to sit, and no record anywhere that a Tomas Ravensworth ever drew breath. Or—" and here the small man tilted his head, considering, "—there is a record. A very detailed one. Of a Ravensworth who informed on his own father, his own sister, and a child of twelve, and whose testimony hanged them all, and whose name every man in this province will know by harvest festival. Both versions are available to you. I find people choose the second more often than you'd think. Guilt wants witnesses."

"You said—" Tomas heard his own voice from far away, thin and childish. "Brandt said my papers. He said if I delivered—"

"Brandt offers what closes the sale," Falkenrath said, not unkindly. "I decide what the Company honors. And the Company honors nothing that it can take for free." He glanced at the body in the puddle, and for a moment something moved behind the spectacles — not remorse, Tomas understood, but inventory. "You have until tomorrow evening. Bring us the girl and the route, and you will be a free man with a new name in a city of your choosing. Fail, and you will be whichever version of nothing I find more administratively convenient." He turned to go, then paused. "Your father carried for us too, once. Did you know that? Before he understood what we honor. Ask him, sometime, why he stopped."

Then they were gone, all three, folding into the waking streets as if the lane had swallowed them, leaving Tomas alone with the rain-washed cobbles and the body of Jorin Vale rocking gently in its puddle of pink water.

He ran.

He did not decide to run; his legs decided, and his mind followed the way a dog follows its master, yelping. He ran up Weir Lane and down Tanner's Row and along the levee with the brown river sliding beside him, indifferent, enormous, carrying its barges and its secrets toward the sea. He ran until his lungs burned with the same salt ache he'd watched Hazel carry home from her dives a thousand dawns, and then he stopped at the foot of the ferry stairs, bent double, hands on knees, and was sick into the water.

*They killed him.* Not imprisoned. Not tried. A man Tomas had eaten bread with, in Father's kitchen, laughing quietly at some joke of Father's — shot in a lane at dawn like a dog, and the town would say he'd gone off to the capital, and no one would ever ask twice, because no one asks twice about a courier.

And they had known Tomas was coming. Had *counted* on it. Every meeting, every whispered report in the smoke room behind Brandt's office, every promise of freedom — it had all been a leash measured out link by link, and now the leash had played out to its end and Tomas could feel the collar closing.

*Redundant assets.*

His hands shook so badly he had to press them flat against the wet stone of the stair rail. He thought of Hazel at eight years old, holding a split lip and refusing to cry, saying *don't tell Father, he'll worry* — she had been protecting people since before she could swim. He thought of Father's hands among the apple branches, patient as prayer. He thought of Ilse, twelve years old, with her dead father's chin and her whole unwritten life folded up in a cellar under an orchard.

And he thought of the ledger. God help him, even now, he thought of the ledger — of the column where his own name would be written, in whatever hand, in whatever ink, and how there was still, impossibly, a choice about which line it went on.

The ferryman was unlocking his chain at the bottom of the stairs, yawning. Downriver, the morning fog was lifting off the water in long pale banners.

Tomas straightened up. Wiped his mouth. Turned his back on the Meridian offices, on Brandt's lamp, on the whole glittering arithmetic of his survival — and started to run again, this time upriver, toward the orchard, toward the family he had spent a year selling, with nothing in his hands but the truth and no plan at all except the oldest one left to him:

to arrive before the Company did.

Behind him, faint on the wind off the water, he could almost believe he heard the locks opening for the morning traffic — and somewhere below them, a ship riding low in the water, waiting out its two nights.
