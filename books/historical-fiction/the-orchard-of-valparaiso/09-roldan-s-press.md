## 9. Roldán's Press

The cellar beneath the Casa de Aduanas did not appear on any surveyor's map of Valparaíso, and Governor Roldán intended to keep it that way until the day he died—which, if God was merciful and his ledgers stayed balanced, would be a long time from now, in a bed, with the sea still paying him tribute.

He descended the forty-one steps each morning before the sun cleared the hills, because a man who inspected his own machine every day never had to be surprised by it. The stairs were narrow and smelled of lamp oil, damp stone, and hot lead—that last scent he loved best. It was the smell of authority being poured into shape.

"Governor." The foreman, a stooped Castilian named Herrero, bowed at the bottom of the stair. Behind him, twelve presses stood in two silent rows like cattle waiting for the yoke, and thirty men bent over them in the yellow light of a hundred tallow lamps.

Roldán walked the rows without speaking. He liked to begin in silence. It reminded the men that their work was not conversation; it was obedience rendered in ink.

At the third press, a boy no older than fifteen was setting type for a Peruvian manifest—*Doña Carmen*, out of Callao, declared cargo: salted fish, four hundred quintals. Roldán stopped. The boy's hands trembled so badly the type chattered in its stick like teeth in a cold wind.

"Read me your line," Roldán said.

"*D-o-ñ-a C-a-r-m-a,*" the boy recited, voice cracking on the second word.

"And the cargo?"

"S-salted fish, Excellency."

"What is actually in the hold?"

The boy looked down. "Gunpowder, Excellency."

"Good." Roldán touched the boy's shoulder, lightly, almost tenderly, and felt him flinch under the wool of his coat. "Your hands will steady. Fear does that—it burns off like morning fog once you understand there is nowhere else for you to be. There is no other life for you now but this one. Isn't that so?"

"Yes, Excellency."

"That's all any of us are," Roldán said, loud enough for the nearest presses to hear. "Men who have understood where they belong."

He moved on. He had learned long ago that terror administered in small daily doses kept better than terror administered in floods. A flood drowned a man or hardened him. A drip wore him smooth as river stone.

* * *

At the far end of the cellar, behind a screen of oiled canvas, sat the press he thought of as his conscience—or rather, as the machine that made conscience unnecessary. It printed nothing but paper for foreign eyes: shipping papers in French, in English, in the careful commercial Spanish of Buenos Aires merchants. Papers that said *this vessel is clean, this cargo is lawful, this captain owes no duty.* Papers that opened ports the way keys opened doors.

Herrero followed him there and lowered his voice, though the presses were loud enough to swallow anything.

"The Valparaíso run came back through the garrison gate at midnight. All seals correct. The new ink took well—the violet fades convincingly after three days, just as you specified."

"And the other matter?" Roldán asked, examining a sheet fresh from the press. He held it to the lamp, tilting it, watching how the light lay in the fibers of the paper. Good stock. Dutch, bought through a house in Lima that asked no questions because they were paid in questions of their own.

"A letter, Excellency. Or the rumor of one."

Roldán set the sheet down with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down a glass when his hand wants to close around something softer—a throat, perhaps.

"Tell me precisely what you know, and nothing more than you know. I have no use for embroidery."

Herrero swallowed. "A courier came off the hills two nights ago. A woman, a dockworker by her clothes. She was seen at the harbor asking after the fleet's anchorage. And there is talk—only talk—in the cantinas of the Almendral that Fuentes wrote letters before—" He stopped himself, which showed some intelligence. Men who finished certain sentences in front of Roldán did not finish many more sentences at all.

"Before he was hanged," Roldán supplied pleasantly.

"Yes, Excellency."

"You mean before I hanged him."

Herrero's face went the color of the tallow. "Yes, Excellency."

Roldán turned back to the press and ran his thumb along the edge of the forme, feeling the type stand in its bed like a field of tiny soldiers. Two years ago, Aurelio Fuentes had been the most dangerous man on this coast—not because of his ship, though his frigate had been fast enough, but because of his eyes. He had watched the harbor the way Roldán watched it, and he had counted things. Tonnage that arrived without leaving. Seals that appeared on papers whose voyages had never happened. And then he had begun writing letters—to Santiago, to the admiralty, to anyone whose rank might outrank a governor's patience.

So Roldán had arranged for him to die publicly, spectacularly, in front of a crowd primed with broadsheets about treason and foreign gold. The crowd had gotten its spectacle. The broadsheets had gotten their truth, typeset and official. And Fuentes had gotten a cell in San Isidro, alive, because dead men cannot be persuaded to sign confessions—and a signed confession, properly witnessed, was worth more than ten hangings. Roldán had simply never found the right price. The man had proven maddeningly unbuyable.

But a letter was another matter. A letter in Fuentes's hand, loose in the world, was a fuse looking for powder.

"How many people knew the courier reached the city?" Roldán asked.

"Perhaps six. Perhaps ten, counting the cantina talk."

"Ten." Roldán let the number sit in the air between them. "Then we have perhaps ten funerals' worth of margin. Find her. Quietly. And find whoever she carried the letter to. If it has reached a printer—" he paused, and something cold moved behind his eyes, "—then it has already begun to multiply, and multiplication is the one thing I will not permit."

* * *

He climbed back up into daylight near noon and received his visitors in the customs house above, because a governor's cruelty worked best when it wore the face of ordinary administration. The room smelled of sealing wax and coffee. Through the tall windows, the harbor lay glittering and innocent, masts thick along the mole like reeds in a marsh.

Don Baltasar, the customs clerk, came in wringing his hat. He was a careful man, Baltasar, careful the way small animals are careful, and Roldán valued him for exactly that quality.

"Excellency, a widow came through yesterday. Shipping mourning stationery to Concepción. Her papers were in order—seal, signature, everything. But—" Baltasar hesitated.

"But you remembered her face."

Baltasar blinked. "I—yes, Excellency. Young for a widow. Very composed. Composure costs money, in my experience."

"It does indeed." Roldán poured two cups of coffee and pushed one across the desk, an intimacy he deployed like artillery. "Describe her."

"Tall. Dark hair pinned plainly. Gloves worn at the fingertips—working gloves, whatever the dress said. And her seal impression was..." Baltasar searched for the word. "Too perfect, Excellency. Real seals get tired. They blur at the edges, the wax pools unevenly. Hers was flawless. Like something cut by hand rather than stamped by habit."

Roldán drank his coffee slowly. Somewhere below his feet, forty-one steps down, his presses thundered on, forging the honest paperwork of a dishonest world. And somewhere out in that glittering harbor town, someone with a cutter's hands and a widow's costume was walking around with his own craft turned against him.

A forger. A good one. Trained, probably, at a type case—there were only a handful of shops in Valparaíso capable of producing that quality of work, and he knew every one of them, because he owned two of them outright and had informants in the rest.

"Send me the impression," he said. "And put a quiet watch on the print shops. Not raids—raids scatter birds. Eyes. Especially the Eastwick shop. Old Tomás Eastwick printed for the wrong side once, years ago, and men who have sinned once keep their tools sharp."

"He's half-blind now, they say."

"Half-blind men see best with their hands," Roldán said. "Go."

* * *

That evening he dined alone in the governor's residence, as he preferred, with the window open to the sound of the surf. His steward served sea bass and a heavy red wine from the Maule, and Roldán ate methodically, thinking.

People imagined that power was a throne, a uniform, a signature at the bottom of a death warrant. They were wrong. Power was a press. Power was the ability to decide what a piece of paper said, and therefore what the world believed, and therefore what the world was. Every manifest, every passport, every broadsheet announcing Aurelio Fuentes's execution—each one a brick, and bricks, laid patiently, built walls that even admirals could not see over.

He had consumed printers before. There had been a man in Coquimbo, years back, who tried to print the true tonnage figures; his shop burned, and the man himself had ended up setting type in Roldán's cellar, hands steady as stones, until his heart gave out one night over the forme. Couriers, too—they passed through his machine like thread through a loom, useful until frayed, then replaced. Even captains. Even Fuentes, once the finest officer on this coast, now a name on a stone that didn't exist and a body in a cell that did.

The machine consumed. That was not its cruelty; that was merely its function. Cruelty would imply appetite, preference, passion. Roldán felt none of these. He felt the way the press felt, he sometimes thought: pressure applied evenly, impressions taken cleanly, waste swept away each night.

After dinner, his adjutant brought the evening dispatches, and among them, a report from the garrison gate: routine traffic, nothing held. And beneath it, a note in Baltasar's careful clerk's hand—the wax impression lifted from the widow's stationery, pressed onto a slip of paper.

Roldán held it to the candlelight.

Flawless. Every serif exact, every counter open, the eagle of the customs office rendered with a precision no working stamp would survive a month of service. This was not a stolen seal. This was a *made* one. Someone had studied the original, measured it, and cut a copy by hand—cut it with love, almost, with the terrible devotion of a craftsman.

He found, to his own mild surprise, that he was smiling.

There was pleasure in meeting a worthy instrument of destruction. He had felt it once with Fuentes, admiring the man's stubborn spine even as he arranged to break it. He felt it now, looking at those perfect serifs. Somewhere in his city was a pair of hands that understood, as he did, that the world was made of type—and unlike his cellar full of trembling boys, these hands did not shake.

"Find the shop that cut this," he told the adjutant. "Not tonight. Not tomorrow. Let her think she's passed. A fish pulls harder against a slack line."

"And when we find her, Excellency?"

Roldán set the impression down beside his wineglass and looked out at the harbor, where the lights of the anchored fleet rode the black water—thirty ships, mutinous in mood if not yet in fact, their crews fed on grievances and cheap rum and rumors that refused to die.

"When we find her," he said, "we will follow her. To the letter. To whoever holds it. To everyone who has touched it." He turned the candle down with two fingers, unhurried. "Paper remembers everyone who handles it, Captain. So do I."

Far below the city, forty-one steps beneath the customs house, the presses ran on through the night, printing the world as he wished it to be—and somewhere in the Almendral, in a shop that smelled of ink and lamp oil, a girl with ink-stained fingers was about to learn what it cost to print the truth.

He did not know her name yet.

He would.
