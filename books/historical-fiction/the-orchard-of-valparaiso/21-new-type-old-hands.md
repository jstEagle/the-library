## 21. New Type, Old Hands

The smell came back before anything else did.

I had thought I would never smell it again without grief attached to it—ink and lamp oil and the particular sweetness of paper curing in a warm room—but when I climbed the stairs of the new shop on the Calle del Almendro that first morning of October, the scent rose up to meet me like a dog that had waited faithfully at a door. It was the same smell. It was not the same shop. And somehow both of those things were true at once, the way so many things had become true at once since the night the harbor burned.

The building had been a chandlery before, and then nothing at all for three years, its windows boarded against the salt wind. Now the boards were down, the windows scraped clean by a dozen hands, and light fell across a floor we had scrubbed on our knees until the old whale-oil stains gave up their ghosts. The press itself—Tomás's press, pulled smoking and warped from the ashes of the old shop—stood in the center of the room like a veteran who had refused to die with his regiment. Its iron frame was scarred. One of its cheeks bore a blister of blackened metal where the fire had kissed it. But Inés had hauled it up the hill on a cart borrowed from a fisherman who asked no questions, and Emiliano had spent two full days with files and oil, coaxing the screws back into obedience, and now when you pulled the lever it sighed rather than groaned, which Tomás said was the sound of an honest press.

"Mirabel." My father's voice came from the corner where he kept his type cases, and I turned to find him standing with his face very close to the cabinet, as close as his failing eyes demanded, one hand flat against the wood the way a man touches the flank of a horse he is learning to trust again. "Come tell me if this is the fourteen-point or the eighteen. My eyes say fourteen. My eyes have been liars lately."

"It's the eighteen," I said, crossing the room. "It has been the eighteen every morning this week."

"And every morning I doubt it," he said, and smiled—not the careful, folded-away smile of the years before the fire, but something wider, unguarded, almost boyish. "Doubt keeps a printer honest. Certainty is how Roldán got his seals onto paper that lied."

He had changed since the customs house. Everyone said so—the neighbors who had watched him led away, the ones who had watched him come back half-blind and leaning on the shoulder of the apprentice who had sold him. Pascual, the boy's name was. He came to the shop most mornings still, though no one had asked him to, and swept the floor and carried water and stood near my father the way penitents stand near altars. Tomás had forgiven him—or was in the act of forgiving him, daily, the way one rows against a current: not once but continuously, arms burning, choosing it over and over. I understood now that forgiveness was not a door you walked through. It was a craft, like cutting type. You got better at it slowly, and you were never finished.

* * *

By mid-morning the shop filled the way a tide pool fills—quietly, from all sides, until it was brimming.

Inés arrived first, as she always did, with her delivery satchel already heavy and her boots still wet from the pier. She had made herself indispensable to the fleet's officers in the weeks after the mutiny, running letters between the ships and the shore because she knew every alley and every guard who could be trusted and every one who could not, and now she ran our deliveries too, out of what she claimed was boredom and what I knew was devotion.

"The harbormaster wants forty copies of whatever you're printing today," she announced, dropping the satchel on the counter. "He says the fleet's been arguing about what actually happened, and half of them believe the version where Fuentes flew over the customs house on wings of fire."

"He believes that?" I asked.

"He *started* it," she said. "He thinks it's good for morale."

Emiliano came next, in civilian clothes that fit him badly, as though his body still expected a uniform. He had resigned his commission formally—a document he'd written himself, and I had set the type for it, and there was a strange satisfaction in printing a man's freedom—and now he stood at the door of the shop each day with a musket slung across his back, guarding presses instead of garrisons. Some called it a demotion. He called it the first honest work of his life.

"Nothing on the street," he reported. "Two of Roldán's clerks were seen at the port looking for passage north, and nobody will sell them any. Otherwise it's quiet." He paused, and something moved behind his eyes—the same thing that always moved there, the brother-shaped absence. "Quiet is strange. I keep waiting for it to be a trick."

"Let it be quiet," Inés said, not unkindly. "You've earned a boring month."

"I don't know what I'd do with one."

"You'd learn," she said. "That's the whole point of them."

And then Pascual, slipping in through the back with his head down, and Doña Rufina from the corner house with bread wrapped in cloth because she had decided, unilaterally and permanently, that a print shop must be fed; and two of Tomás's old journeymen who had scattered after the fire and now returned carrying their own battered type cases like pilgrims bearing relics; and a girl of perhaps twelve, Marta, whose father had died on Roldán's smuggling ships and who had begun appearing at the window each morning to watch us work until Tomás finally waved her inside and put a composing stick in her hand and said, "Hold it like you mean it, or don't hold it at all."

I stood in the middle of it all, in the noise and the flour dust and the smell of ink reborn, and I felt something I had not permitted myself to feel in years: visible. Seen, and glad of it.

* * *

The typeface took me four days.

I had cut faces before—small repairs, a replacement e here, a fresh ampersand there—but never a whole alphabet, never a voice. This one would belong to the broadsheet, to the truth about the mutiny, and I wanted it to sound like the truth: clear, unhurried, without ornament. I worked at the bench by the north window where the light was steadiest, graving the steel punches with the small sharp tools my father had taught me to hold when I was nine years old, my thumbs remembering everything.

There is a moment in cutting a letter when the counter—the enclosed space, the eye of the o, the bowl of the b—opens under your burin and the letter stops being a shape and becomes a sound. I lived for that moment, stroke after stroke, all four days. The a was stubborn; I cut it twice. The g I loved immediately, with its deep descender like a rope thrown down into darkness. When I struck the first proof on Tuesday afternoon—ink dabbed on with a leather ball, dampened paper pressed by hand, no press at all, just my palm and my whole weight behind it—and lifted the sheet to the window, my father came to stand beside me.

"Well," he said, squinting at it from an inch away. "Whose face is this? It's not mine."

"It's mine," I said. "Or—it's the shop's. Ours."

He was quiet a long moment. His hand found my shoulder, and stayed.

"Your mother had hands like yours," he said. "She mended sails. Every stitch exactly like the last stitch, because a sail doesn't forgive uneven work." He tapped the proof. "This is even work, Mirabel. Your mother would have—" His voice caught, and he pressed his lips together, and finished anyway, which was its own kind of courage. "She would have liked the g."

* * *

We printed on Thursday.

The broadsheet was short—that had been argued over for days, everyone wanting to add more, more names, more dates, more proof—and in the end we kept it to what could be read aloud in a minute on a street corner. The facts of the mutiny. The seizure of the smuggling ships without slaughter. The arrest of Governor Roldán's officers. The promise of public trial, with evidence open to any citizen who wished to see it: the forged seals, the false manifests, the private orders carried out of the garrison by a soldier who had chosen to look *toward* instead of away. At the bottom, in my new face, larger than everything else, a single line:

*Printed openly, on the Calle del Almendro, by people who sign their work.*

Tomás insisted on that line. "Roldán's press printed in the dark," he said, "even when it printed by daylight. That's the whole disease. We print in the open or we're only a different symptom."

The first pull came off the press at dawn, and I held it while the ink was still wet enough to shine, and I confess I wept a little, and pretended I hadn't, and Inés saw, and said nothing, which is the highest courtesy one working woman can pay another.

Then the run began. Two hundred copies. The press spoke its old rhythm—pull, lift, turn, lay—and the room filled with the slap and sigh of it, and Marta learned to feed the paper without creasing it, and Pascual stacked the drying sheets in fives with a care that looked like prayer, and by noon the broadsheets hung in rows across the shop like laundry, like flags, like leaves on some tree we had planted without knowing.

Inés took the first bundle out at one o'clock. By three, she was back, breathless, her satchel empty and her eyes bright.

"They're gone," she said. "All of them. The fishermen are reading them aloud on the pier. There's a crowd at the customs house—people just standing there, reading, passing copies hand to hand. A woman asked me if it was really true that there'd be a trial, a real trial, and I told her yes, and she said—" Inés stopped, and laughed, and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. "She said her husband died on one of those ships, and she'd been told he was a criminal, and she wanted to know if someone would say out loud that he wasn't. And I said yes. Someone will say it out loud. In ink."

* * *

Evening came, and the others drifted home, and I stayed to clean the type.

This was my oldest ritual, older than invisibility, older than fear: washing the ink from each letter, sorting each back to its case, the day's words returned to their sleep. I worked by lamplight while my father dozed in his chair by the cooling stove, and outside the window Valparaíso lay in its blue dusk, the harbor dotted with the riding lights of a fleet that belonged, for the first time in years, to no governor's secret.

I thought about the letter that had started everything—the dead captain's hand pressed into mine by a bleeding stranger on a dark doorstep—and how I had believed, in those first terrible days, that I was carrying something fragile, something that might shatter me. It had not shattered me. It had *made* me, the way pressure makes a diamond, or makes rubble; you don't get to choose which, only whether you go on.

My father stirred. "Leave the rest till morning," he murmured.

"A few minutes more."

"You always say that." He opened one eye. "Your grandfather said it too. Then your grandmother said it. It's the Eastwick litany." He sat up slowly, joints cracking like small type being distributed. "Mirabel. Come here. I want to show you something before my eyes quit entirely."

He led me to the type cabinet, and drew out the bottom drawer, the one I had always assumed held broken sorts and lead waste. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, lay a punch—steel, worn smooth at the grip, and beside it a paper impression of the letter it had cut. An M. Old-fashioned, heavy-footed, beautiful.

"Your grandfather cut that," Tomás said. "Before either of us was born. It's the M from the first Eastwick press, the one he brought over in pieces in a sea chest." He pressed it into my palm and closed my fingers around it. The steel was cool and dense and alive. "A typeface lasts longer than a government. Remember that. Roldán is in a cell, and his press is scrap, and this—" he tapped my closed fist "—this is still here. Still cutting."

I held it a long time.

* * *

Later, when the lamp was trimmed low and my father had gone up to bed, I sat at the bench with a fresh blank of paper and my own new punches laid out in order, and I did what I had been turning over in my mind for a week and had not yet dared say aloud.

I began sketching a second face.

Not for the broadsheet—for a book. A small book, octavo, the kind that fits in a coat pocket and travels in a satchel and gets passed hand to hand down a dock or up a hillside. A book that would hold the whole of it: the letter, the checkpoints, the fires, the trial testimony as it came, the names of the dead from the smuggling ships, all of it, set in type that signed its own work. A history that could not be burned, because it would live in two hundred pockets instead of one drawer.

Fuentes had spoken to me of it once, standing at the rail of the flagship with the harbor smoking below us—of how erasure was the truest weapon, how a man declared dead was more thoroughly killed than any hanging could manage, and how the only answer was record. *Someone must write it down,* he had said. *Someone with steady hands.*

I tested the sketch against the lamp. The letters were smaller than the broadsheet face, quieter, built to endure rather than to shout. In the corner of the page, where a colophon would someday sit, I wrote in pencil the words I would one day cast in metal:

*Set by Mirabel Eastwick, who was unseen, and chose to see.*

Outside, far off toward the water, a ship's bell rang the hour, and beneath it—faint, unmistakable, rising from the direction of the prison where a governor waited for the trial he had never intended to give anyone else—I heard the crowd still reading, still passing the papers hand to hand, the sound of a city teaching itself its own true name.
