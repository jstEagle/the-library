## 4. Every Line They Set

The winter came down the river like a debt collector, and the town paid it in smoke and quiet.

Paloma Voss stood at the case in the front room of the old shop, her breath silvering in the cold, and set the last line of the broadside by lamplight. *Proclamation of the Provisional Council of the Palatinate.* Legal, now. Lawful. The word still tasted strange on her tongue, like fruit out of season. For eleven years she had set type the way other women committed sins — by night, by feel, with the shutters drawn and the stove banked low — and now the garrison had fallen, the flag over the customs house was red-black-gold, and a clerk from the new council had stood in her doorway with his hat in his hands, asking — *asking*, with a bow — whether Frau Voss might consent to print for the republic.

*Frau Voss.* Not the girl from the shop. Not the mute one, the one who kept her hands ink-stained and her eyes down. The name had gone around the town the way ink goes through damp paper, blooming outward from the center, until even the fishwives on the quay said it with a nod.

She lifted the finished line of type and held it to the light. Her father's letters — the ones she had cut at fifteen, the ones Anselm had sworn were melted down and scattered, the ones she had buried in a flour barrel beneath the floorboards through two searches and a winter of fear. They caught the lamplight along their bevels, sharp as the day she'd finished them. *Every line they set*, old Grett used to say, *is a line someone will read. Make it worth the reading.*

Outside, the Neckar ice groaned and shifted. Somewhere a dog barked at the river fog. Paloma set the line into the chase, locked the forme, and let herself feel, for one full minute, the ordinary miracle of it: that she was cold, and tired, and afraid of nothing in this room.

* * *

The press had a new voice.

It had always been a temperamental old thing, her father's handpress, prone to sighing and shuddering like a horse asked to work past its years, but the months had changed it — or changed her, which amounted to the same thing. She had rebuilt the platen herself, planed the tympan, replaced the frisket's worn cords. When she pulled the bar now, the press spoke in a clean, deep note, and the sheet came away wet and whole.

She printed the first hundred copies of the proclamation that night and the second hundred by dawn, and when the sun came up she stood in the doorway and watched them go out into the town. A carter took a bundle for Mannheim. The baker's boy ran a copy up to the castle hill, where the student militia had posted themselves. An old woman — Frau Behringer, who had once complained for a decade about the ink smell — pressed a copy flat against her chest like a psalm and walked off weeping without a word.

Paloma leaned in the doorway and let the cold air fill her. The shop behind her smelled of lead and linseed and the onion soup she'd left on the stove, and it smelled, for the first time in her life, like the inside of her own name.

She had given up the berth on the last barge in October. She had done it in the space between one heartbeat and the next, the way one steps off a curb — no ceremony, no time for the body to argue. Grett had refused, had gripped her wrists with his ink-cured old hands and called her a fool in three languages, and she had put the baby into his arms and said, *He needs a master printer more than I need a river.* What she had not said — what she had kept in her chest like a coin held under the tongue — was that someone had to stay. Someone had to print. A press that falls silent is a press that dies, and her father had not buried his letters in a flour barrel so that his daughter could sail away from them.

The barge had gone downriver through the fog with the child's small fist wrapped in Grett's beard, and Paloma had stood on the quay until the fog took them both, and then she had walked back to the shop, lit the stove, and set type until her fingers bled.

* * *

They came back on a Tuesday, at the hour when the light goes long and gold across the water.

She heard them before she saw them — Grett's voice, unmistakable, carrying up the lane the way it always had, a voice built for reading aloud over the clatter of a composing room. She was at the case, mid-line, and her hands stopped as if the type had frozen them.

Then the door opened, and the cold came in with them, and Grett stood in it — thinner, grayer at the beard, his coat too big for him now — and in his arms, bundled in wool that had clearly been knitted and re-knitted by a dozen different women in a dozen river towns, was a boy of nearly a year, round-faced and solemn-eyed, with her father's stubborn chin and a fine dust of dark hair.

"Well," Grett said, and his voice cracked on it, the great voice, the voice that had read Voltaire to apprentices through three wars' worth of censorship. "Well, girl. Look what the river brought back."

Paloma crossed the room. She did not remember deciding to. She took the boy the way she took a fresh sheet — carefully, with both hands, as if he might tear — and he looked up at her and did not cry. He reached out one small fist and closed it around her inking thumb, the one permanently stained to the second knuckle, and held on.

"His name's on the papers," Grett said, gruff now, blinking hard. "Official. Council seal and all. *Lorenz Voss*, heir of the house — though there's precious little house left to inherit but this." He looked around the shop, at the press, at the cases, at the broadsides drying in rows along the walls like flags of some small and stubborn nation. "Though I'll say this, Paloma. I've seen print shops in Basel, in Strasbourg, in Paris itself. I never saw one that smelled like it meant it."

She laughed — a short, startled sound, rusted from disuse — and the boy laughed too, a bark of pure delight at the mystery of laughter itself, and Grett laughed until he had to sit down on the composing stool, wheezing and wiping his eyes.

* * *

They hung the new sign on the third day.

The carpenter's apprentice brought it wrapped in sacking, and Grett insisted on doing the honors, though his arms shook and Paloma had to steady the ladder. The sign was oak, the letters painted deep black on cream, and when the sacking fell away the whole lane went quiet — the fishwives, the carter, the baker's boy, all of them stopped to look.

**VOSS & SOHN**, it said. *Printing — Typesetting — Bookbinding.*

"Voss and son," Grett said, climbing down. "The council clerk insisted. Said the town would have it no other way. Said—" he paused, and his voice did something complicated, "—said every line this shop ever set in the dark was worth more than everything the garrison ever printed in the light. They wanted your name on it, girl. Yours. Not the dead man's. Yours."

Paloma stood in the lane with the boy on her hip and looked up at the letters. Behind her, through the open door, the press waited with its forme locked and its ink rolled, patient as a held breath. The proclamation was done; the next job sat in the basket — a school primer, of all things, the republic's first, commissioned for the town's children, to be set in letters large and kind.

She had spent her whole life cutting letters for other people's words. There was a thought she had been carrying all winter, small and warm as a banked coal, and she turned it over now in the gold light: that when the primer was done, when the council's work was printed and the ledgers were balanced, she would sit down at the case by lamplight, the way she had as a girl, and cut a new alphabet. Every letter from scratch. Her own hand, her own steel, in her father's tradition but not his shadow. And the first word she would cast in it — the first word the new type would ever spell — was already chosen.

The boy tugged her thumb. Below the lane, the river ran open and bright toward the sea, and above the door the new sign caught the light, and inside the shop the press waited for her hands.

Paloma Voss carried her brother inside, and shut the cold out, and went to work.
