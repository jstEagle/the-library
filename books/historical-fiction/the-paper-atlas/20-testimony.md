## 20. Testimony

The mainland smelled of coal smoke and tar and a hundred things I had no names for. I stood on the ferry dock at Thorshavn with the confession letter pressed flat against my ribs, inside my coat, inside my shirt, where it had ridden for six days like a second heartbeat, and I could not stop looking up.

There was no sky-line to it. That was the first thing. In the strait town, the sky was a lid; you learned its moods by the color of the water beneath it. Here the sky went up and up and did not end in fog, and the buildings rose into it four and five stories tall, their windows catching the low winter sun, and the streets ran away from the harbor in straight lines as if no one had ever needed to ask their permission.

Anders gripped the rail beside me, his bandaged hands curled into loose fists. The burns had scabbed over in the shape of rope-blisters, and the ship's doctor said he would keep most of his grip, though perhaps not all of it. He had not spoken since we passed the point where the strait narrowed and the ice gave way to open black water. He had only watched our town shrink behind us—the quay, the burned quarter still smoking, the bridge with its chains half-raised—and then turned his face forward and kept it there.

"Sixteen years," he said now, quietly, "and I never once wondered what was on the other side of the water."

"I wondered," I said. "I just never believed I'd see it."

* * *

The magistrate's office occupied the third floor of a customs house that smelled of ink and wet wool. We were let in past a clerk who looked at our clothes—company-issue wool, patched at the elbows, salt-stiff at the hems—and then looked again at the letter in my hand, and his manner changed the way weather changes.

The letter was addressed to Magistrate Halvard Renn. Not the company. A different Halvard—a cousin twice removed, the clerk explained, who had spent eleven years trying to pry the trading charter out of the family's hands and had finally been given a bench to do it from. Whether Signe had known this when she chose her recipient, I could not say. She had spent sixty years in that town reading currents no one else could see. Perhaps she had read this one too.

Renn was a heavy man with a scholar's stoop and eyes that moved quickly, cataloguing. He broke the seal of the letter, read it once fast, then again slowly, and when he looked up at me his voice was careful, the way Mr. Voss's voice had been careful, but without the kindness underneath—or with a different kind underneath, one I hadn't yet learned to price.

"Theo Eriksen," he said. "Daughter of Eirik Eriksen, pearl diver, indentured 1897."

"Yes."

"And your grandmother writes that she sold your family's freedom papers to the Halvard Trading Company in 1903, and that the company has since forged contracts across the whole waterfront quarter to conceal the practice." He set the letter down flat, smoothing it with one large hand as if it might try to escape. "Do you understand what you're handing me?"

"No," I said. "I understand what she handed me. I'm just carrying it."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile. "That may be the most useful thing anyone has said in this office in a year." He rose, took down a ledger of his own, and began writing. "You'll testify before the commission. Formally, under oath, on the record. It will not be gentle. The Halvards have friends on that bench."

"I know how to be talked at gently by people who mean me harm," I said. "I grew up on it."

He stopped writing and looked at me for a long moment. "Yes," he said. "I imagine you do."

* * *

They gave us three days. Three days in a boarding house near the docks that cost more per night than my mother had earned in a month of net-mending, paid from an advance against testimony that Renn arranged without explaining and I didn't ask about. Three days during which Maren lay in a rented room upstairs, coughing, fevered, lucid in patches, while a mainland doctor—who charged what the doctor in the strait town would have charged ten families—tapped her chest and frowned and said words like *consolidation* and *the left lung* and *if the spring is kind.*

Three days during which I learned that the mainland had bread made of wheat instead of barley, that gaslight hummed, that strangers did not know my name or my debt or my father's drowned face, and that this anonymity was its own strange country, one I did not yet have papers for.

On the second night Anders sat on the edge of my bed in the dark, his bandaged hands open on his knees, and said, "I keep waiting to feel glad."

"Glad about what?"

"Any of it. Being out. Being free, if that's what this is." He turned his hands over. "I burned down the warehouse, Theo. Half the waterfront with it. Old Sorensen's boathouse. The net shed where Papa taught us knots. And I keep thinking I should feel something besides—" He searched for it. "Besides like I'm still standing on the dock watching the boat leave."

"You are," I said. "We both are. The boat just hasn't finished leaving yet."

He was quiet a long time. Then: "When you go before them tomorrow. Tell them about the net shed. Not because it matters to the case. Because someone should say its name somewhere it counts."

* * *

The commission met in the assize hall, a room built for sermons, with a vaulted ceiling and windows of colored glass that threw red and blue light across the benches like water stains. There were five commissioners. Two were old men with the Halvard look about them—the same long jaw, the same way of listening as though already composing their reply. One was a woman, which surprised me until I saw that she took no notes and asked no questions and might have been furniture. The last two sat apart from the others: Renn, and a lean, gray man introduced as Commissioner Ask, who had come up from the southern ports and whose reputation, the boarding-house keeper told me breathlessly, was that he had once shut down a cannery empire with nothing but a boy witness and three weeks of patience.

I swore an oath on a book I had never been allowed to touch in the strait town—the company chapel kept its Bible chained to the lectern, which should have told us everything—and then I stood in the well of that enormous room, sixteen years old, in my dead father's coat cut down, and told the truth.

It sounds simple, written that way. It was not simple. Truth under oath has a weight that truth in the attic or truth in the kitchen does not; every word had to be lifted and placed, and the whole time I could feel the two Halvard commissioners watching me the way Voss used to watch me file documents—with attention, with patience, with the settled certainty that everything in the room belonged to them eventually, including me.

I told them about Ledger 114. About my father's contract bearing his signature dated the eleventh of March, 1904, when everyone on the waterfront knew he had gone down off the sandbar channel on the twenty-eighth of February. About how the company had used that signature to renew his indenture posthumously and transfer the debt to his widow and children, and how no one had ever questioned it because no one on our side of the water had ever been permitted to read.

"How do you know the date of death?" the elder Halvard commissioner asked. His voice was dry as old rope.

"Because I was there," I said. "I was nine years old. I stood on the shingle beach while they dragged the channel for three days. You don't forget the day your father drowns, sir. Though I understand your company found it easy enough to forget."

A sound went through the gallery—half gasp, half something else—and the woman commissioner looked up for the first time.

I told them about the freedom papers. About the Emancipation Act of 1889 and the certificates it granted to divers' families who had bought out their debts, and how my grandparents had held theirs, and how those papers had passed to my mother, and how they had been sold—to whom, my mother did not fully know, though I knew, though I carried the knowledge of it in my mouth like a stone—so that the company could hold a family it legally had no claim on, and forge the paperwork backward to make the claim look old.

"Your grandmother sold these papers," the younger Halvard commissioner said. "Under her own hand. Isn't it possible, Miss Eriksen, that this entire account is a family quarrel dressed up as fraud? That grief has made you inventive?"

"It's possible," I agreed, and heard Renn shift behind me. "But grief didn't write my father's signature eight days after he died. Grief didn't alter forty-one contracts in the company attic—I counted them, and I can name the ledger numbers, and I did count them because Mr. Voss caught me at the thirty-ninth and warned me off so gently I nearly apologized to him." I took the folded pages from my coat—the copies I had made in the fire's aftermath, working by lantern in the ruins of the records room while the ash came down like gray snow. "These are the forgeries. The originals burned, or some of them did. But paper remembers being written on even after it burns, and so do I."

Commissioner Ask spoke for the first time. "The sandbar channel," he said. "Your atlas marks it as passable at low tide, drawn in your father's hand, dated five days before his drowning. Who else had access to that atlas?"

The hall went very quiet. This was the question I had been circling for weeks, the one I had assembled in sleepless pieces, and here it was, offered to me plainly, and I understood that answering it would cost something I could not yet name.

"The company knew," I said. "The pearl beds beyond the channel were company-leased. My father found a shortcut through the sandbar that would have cut their transport costs in half—and then he found something else in the shallows there, or someone decided he had. His contract was signed eight days later. My grandmother's papers were bought the following spring. And the channel was marked closed on all company charts within the year." I looked at the elder Halvard commissioner and did not look away. "You didn't kill him with your own hands. You never have to. You build a town where a man can drown in a mapped channel and everyone shrugs, because the sea takes people, doesn't it, and the sea isn't on trial."

The silence afterward lasted long enough for me to hear the colored light, if light can be heard—a high cold ringing in the ears.

Then Commissioner Ask said, "Call the brother."

* * *

Anders testified with his bandaged hands resting flat on the rail, and he was better than me, in the way that fury is sometimes better than grief—he had no architecture, no careful lifting and placing, he simply told it. The medicine he stole, and why. The berth list, and how our names had been struck from it. The fire, and he did not excuse himself, which undid the gallery more thoroughly than any defense could have. When the younger Halvard commissioner asked whether he regretted it, Anders said, "I regret Sorensen's boathouse. I regret the net shed. I don't regret the ledgers. You can rebuild a boathouse. You can't rebuild what was written in those books unless somebody stops you, and nobody ever stopped you, so I did."

"They'll want to charge you," Renn told us afterward, in the corridor, his voice low. "Arson, at minimum. The Halvards will push for it as retaliation."

"Let them push," Anders said.

"No," I said, and both men looked at me. "No more payments. We've paid this company everything we have—my father's life, my mother's lungs, my grandmother's whole soul, Anders' hands. They don't get his freedom too. That's the one thing left in the account, and it stays ours."

Renn studied me for a moment. "You argue like a ledger," he said, and there was no insult in it. "Everything balanced. Everything named."

"My father drew maps," I said. "It's inherited."

* * *

The ruling came on the ninth day, in the same sermon-hall, delivered by Commissioner Ask while the two Halvard men sat with faces like closed doors and the woman commissioner, for the first time, took a note.

The charter of the Halvard Trading Company was suspended, effective immediately, pending full audit of its contract rolls. All indentures executed or renewed under the company's authority were declared void ab initio—void from the beginning, as if they had never existed, as if the nineteen years of my family's lives spent in service of a lie had been returned to us, which of course they hadn't, which nothing could be, but the law reached for the word anyway and I held onto it the way a drowning man holds a plank that is technically seaworthy. An administrator was appointed for the strait town. Wages owed were to be calculated. And the matter of the sandbar channel was referred onward—upward, to investigators who would come north when the ice allowed, to ask questions of a company that had, as of that morning, lost the power to make questions unwelcome.

Void from the beginning. I wrote the phrase in my notebook that night, over and over, testing it. Somewhere across the frozen strait, in a town with no road out, my grandmother was living in the consequences of a sentence like that, and had chosen it, and had sent me here to speak it aloud.

Maren heard the news propped up against pillows, gray-faced, her breathing a thin laboring sound, and when I told her she closed her eyes and said, "Your father used to say the charts were wrong on purpose. He said it as a joke. He'd laugh." Her hand found mine, light as ash. "He wasn't laughing, was he. At the end."

"No," I said. "I don't think he was."

She was quiet a while. Then: "Go back for her in the spring."

"Mother—"

"Not forgiveness. I'm not asking you for that, and neither is she, whatever she pretends. Just—don't let the ice decide who we are. It decides enough already." She coughed, and the cough went on too long, and when it ended she smiled at me with my father's exact crooked smile, and I understood that she had given me the inheritance she had left, and it was this: the instruction to return.

* * *

Anders and I walked down to the harbor on the evening after the ruling. The ferries were laid up for the season; the strait would not open for months. But the piers were public here—no chains, no company lock—and we stood at the end of one looking north across the black water toward a coast we couldn't see, where a town waited under the ice with its burned quarter and its freed indentures and its one remaining Eriksen, kneeling in a kitchen above the floorboards she had pried up herself.

"Do you hate her?" Anders asked. He asked it the way he asked everything now—flatly, without flinching, having learned that flinching costs more than it saves.

I thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty and so did he. "I hate what she did," I said. "I hate that she did it to us, and I hate that she thought the sea would forgive her, and I hate that part of me understands exactly why she thought that, because I grew up in the same town she did." I watched the water. "Hate needs a person to land on. Most days I can't find one. Some days I find her."

"That's not an answer."

"No," I said. "It's a map. Answers come later, if the channels stay open."

Anders laughed—a short, cracked, real laugh, the first since the fire—and put his bandaged hand briefly on my shoulder, and we stood there until the harbor lights came on, gaslit and humming, a whole coastline of them, burning without permission from anyone.

In my coat pocket, against my ribs where the letter had ridden, I carried a different paper now: a copy of the suspension order, and beneath it, in Renn's hand, a note that had come that afternoon. *The ice breaks early some years,* it read. *When it does, the commission will want a witness on the ground who knows the ledgers and the people both. The post pays poorly. It comes with a boat.*

I read it again by the pier lamp, and then I folded it away, and looked north, and began, in the place where dread used to live, to make plans.
