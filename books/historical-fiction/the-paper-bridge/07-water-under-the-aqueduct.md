## 7. Water Under the Aqueduct

The aqueduct had carried water into Vellamare for four hundred years, and it had never once been asked to carry a secret.

Kestrel stood beneath its arches in the hour before dawn, when the rain had thinned to a fine grey mist and the channel above them ran loud and black with spring melt. The stone was slick under her palms, cold as a dead man's cheek. She thought of her master's hands—she thought of them constantly now, of the ink ground into his knuckles, of the way he'd held a pen the way other men held a knife—and she pressed her palm flat against the arch as if she could take warmth from four centuries of masonry.

"Again," Tam whispered beside her.

"You don't have to."

"I want to get it right." He was six years old and he said *get it right* in exactly the cadence her master had used over a misdrawn coastline, and something in her chest folded like wet paper.

They had rehearsed it eleven times in the workshop's cellar: the climb to the maintenance stair, the crawl along the service ledge where the channel wall came only to the shoulder, the wade through the flooded inspection gallery where the water would be chest-high and moving. Then the drop down the old sluice shaft into the tanner's yard inside the second ring of checkpoints, where Sef the aqueduct-keeper would be waiting with dry clothes and a cart of lime barrels. It was a good plan. Sef had drawn it himself on the back of a work order, with the confidence of a man who had spent thirty years walking these channels and knew their moods better than his wife's.

"He can't swim," Kestrel said quietly, when Tam had gone ahead to the foot of the stair.

Sef didn't look at her. Rain dripped from the brim of his hood. "Neither can I, mistress. The water doesn't care what we know." He shifted the lantern so its light fell away from the channel. "But the garrison is searching every cart, every barge, every face that crosses the ring. They've doubled the watch since the barge business. This is the road nobody watches because nobody's fool enough to use it." A pause. "We are fools enough. That's our whole advantage."

She should have refused then. She knew that later, in the long arithmetic of everything that went wrong. She could have left Tam hidden among the apprentices one more day, one more night, could have found another way or waited for the rain to stop or simply been wise instead of desperate. But the signing was nine days away, and desperation is a kind of courage, and courage is very often just fear wearing its good coat.

"All right," she said.

* * *

The service ledge was narrower than Sef had promised—or perhaps the dark simply made it so. Kestrel went first, her back against the outer wall of the channel, the black water rushing a hand's breadth from her ear. It made a sound like a crowd murmuring before an execution. Below her, through gaps in the arches, the city lay drowned in fog, the checkpoint lamps burning small and orange, patient as coals.

Tam came behind her, then Sef, moving with the unhurried certainty of a man who had done this walk ten thousand times in daylight. For twenty minutes it almost felt possible. The ledge turned twice. The water rose at the low places until it pulled at her knees, then her thighs, and still they moved, and she began to let herself believe the lie that all plans tell: that because you have imagined a thing, the world has agreed to it.

Then the sluice gate opened upstream.

It wasn't sabotage. Later she learned that—the garrison raised the winter gates early that year to flush silt before the treaty delegations arrived, a piece of municipal housekeeping decided by some clerk who would never know what he'd nearly done. There was no drumbeat of disaster, no warning shout. There was only a change in the sound of the water, a deepening, the murmur becoming a roar, and Sef's voice cutting through it, high and cracked: *"The gallery—it'll back up—move, move—"*

The flood came around the third bend like a hand closing.

Kestrel had hold of Tam's wrist when it hit. She felt the water take him the way she'd once seen a wave take a sandcastle—not violently, not even quickly, just completely, the whole shape of him erased between one heartbeat and the next. Her grip tore open. She heard him make a sound, one small syllable of terror, and then the channel was full and brown and boiling and there was no boy in it anywhere.

"Tam!"

Her own voice came back at her wrong, flattened by the roar. She lunged along the ledge, arms out, striking the water, striking stone. The current shoved her against the wall hard enough to grind breath out of her lungs. Somewhere behind her Sef was shouting, but the words were gone, torn up by the noise. She got a boot wedged in a drain gap and hung there, half-drowned already, sweeping her arm through the black water again and again and feeling nothing but water.

*Forget the face,* the note had said. As if she ever could. As if she hadn't memorized him in six days—the gap in his teeth, the way he counted things when he was frightened, the map fragment he slept with under his pillow like a talisman.

Her hand struck cloth.

She seized it and pulled, and the current fought her for him with the full indifferent strength of a river, and she understood in some animal register below thought that she was going to lose, that no one's arm is stronger than spring melt in a four-hundred-year-old channel—and then Sef was there, his great scarred hands closing over both of theirs, hauling, roaring something that might have been a prayer, and together they dragged the boy onto the ledge and rolled him over.

He coughed. That was all. He coughed up half the aqueduct and began to cry, and Kestrel gathered him against her chest and shook, and shook, and did not let herself weep, because weeping would come later and cost more than she could spend here.

"We go back," she said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "We turn around. Now."

Sef looked at her across the boy's head, and in the thin light she saw him do a thing she would remember for the rest of her life: he smiled. Not bravely. Almost gently, the way a man smiles at weather he cannot argue with.

"No time, mistress. Listen." And she listened, and beneath the water's roar she heard it: boots. Many boots, on the stair below, coming up. Lantern light swinging wild against the wet stone.

The garrison had not opened the gates. The garrison had followed them.

* * *

They ran. There was nowhere to run to; they ran anyway, because the body prefers any motion to surrender. The ledge climbed toward the old inspection chamber, a vaulted room where the channel narrowed over a grate, and Sef pushed them through it and slammed the iron door and dropped the bar, and the three of them stood in the dark listening to fists begin to hammer on the far side.

"There's another way out," Sef said. He was breathing badly. She noticed that only then, how badly, how each word seemed to be paid for. "The shaft. Down to the tannery yard. You remember the plan."

"The plan was you meet us there."

"The plan changed." He fumbled at his coat, at the inner pocket, and his hands were shaking, and she saw now in the grey seep of light from the grating that there was blood on his shirt, high on his chest, spreading. One of the bullets that had chased them up the ledge had found him after all. He had run the length of the aqueduct bleeding and had said nothing, because there had been no time worth spending on it.

"Sef—"

"Hold this." He pressed a packet into her hands, oilcloth-wrapped, small, dense, tied with waxed cord. It was heavier than paper had any right to be. "From Aldous Verren. The surveyor. Before they took him last autumn he gave it to me for safekeeping—for whoever came asking about the Lantern District. Nobody came. Six months, nobody came." His laugh turned into a cough. "And then a woman walks into my yard with a dead man's eyes and a child who draws maps in the dirt."

"What is it?"

"His confession. Written, signed, witnessed. Names, mistress. Not lines on a fragment—*names*. Who ordered the border drawn where it's drawn. Who told him to survey the district out of existence. He wrote it knowing what writing it would cost him." Sef sagged against the wall, sliding slowly, and Kestrel caught his arm. "Take it to the delegation. Take it and the plate and the boy and finish it. Promise me you won't bury it in some drawer with the rest of the truth in this city."

"I promise."

"Good." He looked past her at Tam, huddled and dripping against the far wall, and his voice gentled. "You keep drawing, little man. Somebody has to remember how the city really looks."

The door shuddered. Iron groaned.

"The shaft's behind the grate panel—third from the left, it lifts. Go down, count forty steps, push north." He was fumbling at his belt now, and she realized with horror what he meant to do, and grabbed his wrist, and he covered her hand with his own, enormous and warm and going cold. "Mistress Reyes. I'm sixty-one years old and I've spent my life carrying water for this city. Let me carry one last thing."

"They'll kill you."

"They were always going to kill me. I helped you, didn't I?" He smiled again, that same terrible gentleness. "Go. The water's rising in the shaft—you haven't long. And girl—" his grip tightened, the last of his strength funneled into five words— "*don't let them redraw the world while everyone's looking at the paper.*"

She went. She lifted the grate panel with Tam's sobbing breath hot against her neck, and she lowered the boy into the dark first, and then herself, and the last thing she saw from below was Sef standing straight against the far wall, lantern raised, facing the buckling door like a man greeting an old acquaintance.

The bar broke. Light flooded the chamber. And Kestrel turned north into the black and began to count.

* * *

Forty steps. Forty-one. The water in the shaft rose to their waists, then their ribs, fed by the same flood that had nearly taken Tam, and she climbed with the boy on her hip and the confession pressed between her body and the stone, wrapped in oilcloth, held above the waterline by a grip she did not know she possessed. At the top, the hatch gave onto the tannery yard, empty, stinking, silvered with rain. No cart. No dry clothes. No Sef.

Above the aqueduct, faint through the rain, she heard shouting, and then—terribly, briefly—nothing at all.

Tam was shivering so hard his teeth chattered like a draftsman's dividers. She stripped off her own coat and wrapped him in it, and crouched with him behind the lime kilns, and unwrapped the packet with fingers that would not quite obey.

Inside, protected by layers of oilcloth and wax, was a slim sheaf of pages in a careful engineer's hand, and a folded document bearing a seal she recognized from her master's reference books: the Provisional Commission on Territorial Boundaries. The confession named three men. One was dead. One had fled beyond the sea. And the third—

The third signed the order that drew the border through the Lantern District, and his name was on the delegation itself. He would sit at the table in nine days. He would smile for the portrait engravers. He would shake hands over the atlas her master had died for, unless the pages in her hands reached the right eyes first.

Tam touched the paper with one small finger. "Is that the bad man?"

"That's the man who thinks he's already won," Kestrel said. She folded the confession away, next to her heart, where the map fragment rode. "Come on. We're going home. We're going to need the workshop, the press, and every plate in the atlas."

Because there was one road left, and it ran through her own hands.
