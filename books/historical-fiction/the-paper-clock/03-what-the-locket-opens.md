## 3. What the Locket Opens

The checkpoint announced itself an hour before we reached it.

Jonah learned this from the road itself — from the carts pulled over to the verge, their drivers sitting on their wheels with the particular stillness of people waiting to be inspected; from a woman ahead of them who stopped walking, looked back at her children, and began braiding their hair with quick, urgent fingers as though neatness might be a kind of passport; and finally from the smoke, thin and gray, rising over the bend where two stone pillars marked the old boundary of the Vellin district. Somebody was burning papers. In Valdera that winter, burning papers was what passed for a town crier.

"Right," Jonah said to the girl, who had not given her name and who walked the way cats walk through unfamiliar houses, as if apologizing for existing. "We're going to do something now, and I need you to be very good at it."

The girl looked up. She had been carrying the bread crusts Jonah had bought her in her coat pocket like treasure, occasionally touching the pocket to confirm they hadn't evaporated. At the word *good* she straightened slightly, the way soldiers did when an officer's voice changed registers.

"Can you be quiet? Not normal-quiet. Important-quiet."

The girl nodded, then considered, then held up one finger — *wait* — dug into her pocket, and ate both crusts in three efficient bites. Preparation complete, she nodded again.

"Good," Jonah said, and meant it more than the child could know. "Important-quiet it is."

The problem was simple and terrible. The satchel across Jonah's chest contained the diplomatic codes — forty pages of cipher keys and authentication tables sewn into oilcloth, the last working channel between the Chancellor's office and whatever neutral parties still answered Valdera's letters. It was the single most incriminating object a person could carry through a coalition checkpoint, and there was nowhere to put it. Her boots were searched first, always. Her coat second. A courier caught with codes wasn't arrested so much as *processed*, and processed couriers did not arrive anywhere, ever again.

But a child's coat — a small, shabby, tear-stained coat belonging to a six-year-old girl with no name and no papers and no reason on earth to interest a bored sergeant — that was different. That was furniture. Nobody searched the furniture of a frightened little girl.

The trouble was asking her. Jonah crouched in the ditch before the pillars, out of sightline, and tried to figure out how you explained contraband to someone who still believed adults were a reliable category of person.

"I have something," she said slowly, "that some men might want to take away. Not yours. Mine. And I need to keep it safe, and the safest place is—" she touched the girl's coat lining, where a loose seam gaped along the hem, "—here. Just for a few minutes. Then I'll take it back and give you more bread."

The girl watched the flat packet slide into the seam with the grave attention of a surgeon's assistant. Then she patted the hem once, decisively, and said her first full sentence in two days:

"You owe me bread."

"Two pieces," Jonah said. "Contractual."

The girl stuck out a grubby hand, and they shook on it, which Jonah would later identify as the moment the mission stopped being a mission and started being something with a witness.

* * *

The checkpoint at the Vellin pillars was run by coalition regulars, which was almost good news. Regulars were bored. Regulars wanted lunch. It was the irregulars — the local auxiliaries, men who'd known these roads before the war and had scores folded into their pockets — who searched with enthusiasm.

There were four soldiers and one auxiliary. The auxiliary was young, maybe nineteen, with the terrible earnestness of someone who has recently been given authority and is still surprised by it daily. He waved Jonah forward while the regulars argued about a card game abandoned mid-hand.

"Papers."

Jonah produced her transit documents — genuine, expensive, purchased in the capital from a man who had apologized twice while taking her money. They named her a textile factor's clerk traveling to arrange winter shipments. Boring paperwork was the best camouflage; nothing made a soldier lose interest faster than the word *textiles*.

The auxiliary read them twice. Behind him, one of the regulars shouted something about cheating and was ignored.

"Traveling alone?"

"With my daughter." Jonah let a note of weary domesticity into her voice, the universal music of mothers everywhere. "Her father's family took her in when things got bad in the city. I'm collecting her before the roads close entirely."

It was a risk, claiming the child. But an unexplained child raised ten questions; a claimed child raised one, and it was a boring one.

The auxiliary looked at the girl. The girl looked at the auxiliary with enormous, watery eyes, and then — Jonah watched it happen with something close to awe — the girl's lower lip began to tremble, and she stepped behind Jonah's legs and gripped her coat with both fists, a perfect portrait of a frightened child clinging to her mother.

*Where did you learn that,* Jonah thought, *and who taught it to you, and how long ago.*

"She's shy," Jonah said. "We've been walking since dawn."

The auxiliary softened visibly. He was, Jonah guessed, somebody's son, and probably somebody's younger brother, and homesickness was doing half her work for her. He waved them toward the search table with something almost like apology.

"Just the coat and bags. Standard."

A private frisked Jonah's coat, found her ration tin, her matches, a folding knife she surrendered with theatrical reluctance, and nothing else. Another turned her satchel inside out: spare stockings, a mending kit, a book of accounts with realistic-looking entries about wool prices. The codes rode warm against the girl's spine, under four layers of patched cotton, and Jonah kept her face arranged into the mild boredom of a woman thinking about wool.

Then the auxiliary crouched by the girl.

"And what's your name, little one?"

Silence. The girl pressed her face into Jonah's hip.

"She doesn't talk much yet," Jonah said smoothly. "Since the shelling. The doctors say it takes time."

This was a gamble built on a guess, but it was a good guess. Children who went silent in wartime were so common a category that they had their own tired nod among soldiers, their own shorthand shrug. The auxiliary nodded the nod. He patted the girl's shoulder — she flinched, he withdrew, everyone performed the correct emotions — and then his hand drifted, absently, professionally, along the hem of her coat.

Jonah's heart performed a maneuver she felt in her teeth.

He pinched the seam. Frowned faintly. And at that exact moment, one of the regulars knocked over the card table, cards scattering like startled birds, and swore roundly, and the auxiliary's head came up, and his hand moved on, and the moment passed the way trucks pass other trucks on mountain roads — close enough to count the rivets, gone forever.

"Fine," he said. "Move along. Before dark, the road past Kessel isn't safe."

"Thank you," Jonah said, and gathered up her daughter, and walked.

They didn't speak until the pillars were out of sight and another bend stood between them and the smoke. Then the girl stopped, planted her feet, and held out her hand.

"Bread."

"Two pieces. Yes. I remember the contract." Jonah dug in the satchel and produced them, plus, because her hands were shaking slightly and generosity was easier than steadiness, a hard yellow apple from a farm cart they'd passed. The girl bit the apple whole, like a small horse, and the crisis of the checkpoint was formally concluded.

"You were extraordinary," Jonah told her. "That lip thing. Devastating. I nearly believed you myself."

The girl shrugged, chewing, uninterested in praise. Somewhere behind them the auxiliary was probably telling the card players about the shy little girl, and somewhere ahead of them was Kessel, and somewhere underneath all of it was the fact that Jonah had just hidden the fate of a republic in a six-year-old's coat hem, and the republic had fit.

She decided not to think about what that said about the size of fates.

* * *

They slept that night in the loft of a barn whose farmer charged them nothing because he charged everyone nothing and called it patriotism, though Jonah suspected it was mostly loneliness. Straw, a lantern turned low, the horses below breathing their warm animal patience into the dark.

The girl fell asleep mid-chew, the way children do, an apple core still in her fist like a scepter. Jonah lay beside her listening to the small engine of her breathing and told herself, for perhaps the hundredth time, that tomorrow or the day after she would find a constable, a priest, a matron — some functioning adult institution — and hand the child over to people whose job this was.

Then she remembered the locket.

The girl wore it always, even sleeping, a small silver oval on a fine chain, and Jonah had noticed it the first morning and catalogued it and dismissed it: a keepsake, a mother's gift, the sort of thing every refugee child clutched like a talisman. But tonight, with nothing else to examine and the particular insomnia that checkpoints produce, she eased it gently from beneath the girl's collar and held it to the lantern light.

It was heavier than it looked. Good silver, worn smooth at the edges by handling. On the front, so small and so worn she'd missed it entirely until now, was stamped a seal: a tower above crossed wheat sheaves.

Jonah knew that seal. Everyone in Valdera knew that seal. It was on the currency, on the ministry doors, on the broadsheets that had praised the Chancellor's wisdom right up until the week they started praising the coalition's instead. It was the seal of the Chancellery itself — not the republic's arms, but the personal device of Aurel Vance, Chancellor of Valdera, architect of the war, author of the peace nobody believed in, the most photographed man in the country.

Which could mean anything. Lockets got sold, traded, stolen, inherited. Half the nation owned something with that stamp on it; the mint had churned out commemorative rubbish by the crate during the Chancellor's ascension year. A peddler's trinket. A lucky piece. Nothing.

Except that the hinge was wrong.

Jonah felt it before she saw it — the locket opened too smoothly, oiled, maintained, cared for by someone who understood that its contents mattered. She pressed the catch. The case sighed open like a well-made drawer.

Inside, on the left, a curl of fine brown hair tied with thread. Ordinary. Motherly. The kind of thing that broke your heart in any century.

On the right, a miniature portrait behind a sliver of glass, painted no larger than a thumbnail, the kind of work that cost more than a farmhand earned in a year.

It showed a man of about fifty, heavy-browed, gray at the temples, with a mouth that looked as though smiling had been explained to him once and rejected. He wore the high collar and plain black coat that every citizen of Valdera could identify from thirty meters, from a moving train, from a bad engraving on a wall poster.

Chancellor Aurel Vance. Painted from life, by a master, small enough to hide against a child's heart.

And around the eyes — around the particular set of the brow, the exact stubborn architecture of the jaw — the painter had done something miniature artists sometimes did, something Jonah had read about in a magazine article years ago in another life: he had painted the sitter *through* his bloodline. The face was the Chancellor's, but softened, rounded, made young and female. It was the Chancellor's face as it would look on a daughter.

It was, Jonah realized with a cold that started in her fingertips and climbed, the girl's own face. Thirty years early. Waiting for her.

The lantern flickered. Below, a horse shifted and sighed. The girl slept on, apple scepter in hand, breath whistling faintly through a nose still pink from the cold, wearing around her neck the answer to a question that could end the war — or end her.

Because that was the arithmetic, and Jonah ran it twice hoping the second sum would come out differently, and it didn't. If the child was the Chancellor's daughter — his hidden daughter, smuggled out of the capital with a note saying *forget the face*, which only made sense if the face was worth remembering, worth hunting, worth using — then she was not luggage. She was leverage. To the coalition, she was legitimacy: the conquerors holding the conquered ruler's own blood, paraded, adopted, displayed. To the Chancellor's loyalists, she was a banner to rally around. To anyone at all, she was proof — living, portable, photographable proof — of secrets the Chancellor had thought buried.

Proof that could end the war, one way.

Proof that could end the girl, several ways, most of them faster.

*Forget the face.* Jonah heard the note's words again in whoever had written them — the mother, surely, the woman who'd tied the hair in the left side with thread — and understood them at last. Not an apology. An instruction manual. The face was the danger. The face was the whole cargo.

And Jonah had spent two days walking that face down the public road, feeding it apples, teaching it to say *important-quiet*, registering it in her own memory with the helpless precision of a courier who never forgot a route, a name, a detail. She had already memorized the face. She was, in fact, the only person in Valdera currently doing it on purpose.

Very carefully, she closed the locket and slid it back beneath the girl's collar, where it lay against the small heartbeat like a second, colder heart.

"Right," she whispered to the rafters, to the horses, to no one. "New plan."

There wasn't a new plan. There was the old plan — next town, authorities, hand her off, wash your hands — except that the old plan now required finding authorities who wouldn't sell the child to the first coalition officer who sneezed, in a country where every authority Jonah could name was either fleeing, collaborating, or dead. The old plan had quietly died somewhere between the pillars and the barn, and nobody had sent flowers.

The girl stirred. Her eyes opened halfway, found Jonah's face in the lantern light, and did something worse than crying or fear. She smiled — small, sleepy, unguarded, the smile of a child who had decided, on insufficient evidence and with total commitment, that this sharp-tongued stranger with paper hands was a safe place to keep herself.

"Milk?" she asked hopefully.

"In the morning," Jonah lied, having no milk, no plan, and no idea how to tell a child that she had just become the most dangerous object in the eastern provinces. "Sleep."

The girl slept. Jonah lay awake watching the lantern guttershadow crawl across the beams, and thought about the road ahead — the river crossing at Kessel, the long causeway over the marshes, and the great iron bridge at Vellin Ford where, if the rumors on the road were true, coalition patrols had begun checking travelers against lists.

Lists. Names. Faces.

Somewhere out there, she suspected, someone was already looking for a little girl with a familiar face.

And tomorrow, whether she liked it or not, Jonah Ishikawa was going to have to decide how far she'd go to make sure they never found her.
