## 14. Tomas Comes for Her

The safehouse above the chandlery smelled of tar and old rope and, faintly, of Anselm's cough medicine — a bitter camphor that had soaked into the walls over years and would probably soak into them long after the war ended, if the war ever did anything so civilized as end.

Jonah had been there two days. Two days of teaching Mira to play knucklebones with dried beans, of listening to the old schoolteacher's breathing rattle like a shutter in wind, of watching the harbor through a crack in the shutters while the *Meridian* sat at her moorings looking, from this distance, like salvation with a funnel.

Forty-one hours left on Voss's clock. Jonah had stopped counting in her head and started counting everywhere else instead: in the beans Mira stacked into little towers, in the candles Anselm rationed, in the slow erosion of the bread.

"You're doing it again," Mira said.

"Doing what?"

"The face." The girl held up two fingers pressed together, an expression of profound disapproval she had almost certainly learned from her mother. "The thinking-about-terrible-things face."

"I don't have a thinking-about-terrible-things face."

"You do. It looks like you ate something." She considered. "Something old."

Jonah laughed despite herself — a short, cracked sound, the first in days — and was still laughing when Anselm's boy came up the stairs three at a time and said, breathless, "There's a man asking after you at the door. He says he's your brother."

* * *

She should have known. Of course she should have known. You could hide from the coalition, from the checkpoints, from the posters on the walls — but you could not hide from family, because family knew the shape of your hiding places the way water knows the shape of a cup.

She put Mira in the back room with Anselm's housekeeper, a silent woman named Petra who had once, according to Anselm, smuggled an entire printing press out of the capital under a load of cabbages, and who now looked at Jonah with the calm of someone who had seen every kind of trouble and found most of it repetitive.

Then Jonah went down the stairs and opened the door.

Tomas stood in the gray afternoon light with snow in his hair and two coalition soldiers behind him, and for one absurd moment — because he was her brother, because he had taught her to swim by throwing her off the pier, because his face was the oldest map she owned — she felt only gladness.

"Tomas."

"Jonah." He looked terrible. Not wounded-terrible, not starving-terrible, but the particular terrible of a man who has been sleeping in rooms where he is hated, wearing a coat that isn't quite his. "You look awful."

"You always did know how to open a conversation."

"It's a gift." His eyes went past her shoulder, up the stairs, and she saw him cataloguing the chandlery, the exits, the thin walls, everything, because that was what he did now; he had become a creature made entirely of assessment. "Can we talk? Just talk. They'll wait outside." He said it loudly enough for the soldiers to hear, and something in his voice — a plea dressed as an order — told her they were not really his men. They were his keepers as much as hers.

"Inside," she said. "Not upstairs."

They sat in the chandlery's front room among coils of rope and barrels of pitch, on two crates that might have been the same crates they'd sat on as children in their father's warehouse, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the silence between them was so familiar it hurt, because it was the silence of people who had shared a childhood and then spent twenty years pretending they hadn't.

"You sold me," Jonah said finally. "At the bridge. I've had time to think about it, and there's no version where you didn't."

"I bought you back," Tomas said. "That's why I'm here."

"Go on, then. Buy."

He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and when he spoke it was fast and low, the voice of a man reciting terms he'd rehearsed until they sounded like convictions. "The colonel wants the girl. That's the whole war to him now — one child, one photograph, one press conference. 'The Chancellor's daughter welcomes the liberators.' It ends the eastern resistance overnight. It ends *this*" — he gestured vaguely at the window, the city, the smoke on the horizon — "weeks early. Maybe months. Thousands live who wouldn't."

"And her?"

"She'd be fine. Fed, clothed, educated. A ward of the provisional government. Jonah, I've seen the arrangements. It's not a cell. It's a palace with guards."

"A gilded cage."

"A cage that keeps her alive!" His voice cracked and he mastered it, and Jonah watched him master it and understood how much practice that took. "You want to know what happens otherwise? Otherwise the colonel stops being patient. Otherwise he sends men who don't knock. Otherwise she dies in a fish cellar in a city that's falling, and you die with her, and nothing changes except the number of graves." He reached across and took her hands — her paper hands, their father used to call them, hands made for documents and never for holding — and squeezed hard. "I can get us all out. All three. There's a plane Thursday, there are papers with my name on them, real ones, and room for two more names. You, me, and the girl, on a plane to neutral territory before anyone realizes we're gone. That's the offer. That's the whole offer. Nobody else has to burn."

"And the testament."

His hands tightened, just slightly. "What testament?"

"Don't." She pulled free. "Don't insult both of us at once, Tomas. You didn't come here for the girl. The girl is the bait. You came for what she carries."

For a long moment he said nothing, and in that nothing Jonah heard the truth settle between them like ash.

"They told you about the locket," she said quietly.

"They told me there were documents. I assumed—" He stopped. Started again. "I assumed codes. Your codes. The surrender terms. I thought—" And here his voice did something she hadn't heard since he was nine years old and caught in a lie about broken crockery: it wavered. "I thought if I got the terms delivered, the war ends cleanly, and everyone comes home, and I'm the man who ended it. I thought I was buying peace, Jonah. I swear to you, on Mother's grave, I thought I was buying peace."

"With whose blood in the price?"

"I didn't know. I *didn't know.*" He stood up and walked to the window, and she watched his back, the back she'd followed through flooded streets and festival crowds, and hated how much she still loved it. "And now I do know, some of it, and I'm standing here anyway, because the alternative is worse. If I walk away empty-handed, the colonel sends Kessler's squad, and Kessler doesn't negotiate, doesn't stage rescues, doesn't leave witnesses. This—" he turned, arms open, "—this *is* the mercy, Jonah. This ugly, compromised, humiliating thing is the best outcome available. I need you to be smart enough to see it."

And the terrible thing — the thing she would turn over for years afterward, in hospitals and border towns and the long insomnias of exile — was that he believed it. Every word. He believed he was saving them. He had built a cathedral out of his own compromise and moved in and hung curtains, and he genuinely could not see the walls.

"Tomas," she said gently, "what did they tell you happens to the girl after the photograph?"

"She becomes a symbol. A bridge."

"A symbol gets used up. A bridge gets walked on." She rose too. "I've read the terms, brother. All of them. Did you read the annex? Clause eleven? Did anyone show you clause eleven?"

He frowned. "There is no clause eleven."

"There is. Political prisoners. Immediate execution upon delivery of capitulation. It's in the cipher. I decoded it myself, twice, hoping I'd made an error." She let that land. "Your peace has a purge inside it, Tomas. Like a knife inside a loaf of bread. And the girl's mother is on that list."

"That's not—" He shook his head. "That's not how the colonel described—"

"No. I imagine it wasn't."

He stood very still. She watched him try to rebuild the cathedral, watched the argument assemble itself behind his eyes — *she's lying, she's been turned, she's sentimental, it's the fever of it all* — and watched it fail, because underneath everything Tomas Ishikawa had always been able to read his sister, whatever else he could read.

"If I believe you," he said at last, hoarsely, "then I'm already dead. Do you understand that? The moment I believe you, everything I've done since the bridge is—" He laughed, a horrible small sound. "You always were the honest one. Father's little courier. Carrying truth around like it weighs nothing."

"It weighs everything. That's the point."

"Jonah." He stepped toward her, and his voice dropped to the register he'd used when they were children and the world was simple and he was trying to convince her that the dog had run away rather than been drowned. "Give me the girl and whatever she's carrying. I will get you out. I will spend every favor, forge every paper, I will carry you myself if your wound won't hold. But I cannot protect you from what comes next if you make me your enemy. Please. For once in your life, let someone else carry it."

Outside, the snow had thickened. Through the frosted glass, the two soldiers had become gray shapes, and beyond them the harbor bells rang the quarter hour, and somewhere in that arithmetic forty-one hours had become thirty-nine.

"No," Jonah said.

"Jonah—"

"No." She said it again, steadier, and found that beneath the fear there was something else, something new and clean, like ice forming over deep water. "You asked me once, when we were kids, why I always had to be the one who told the truth even when it cost us. Remember? You said honesty was a luxury for people with nothing to lose." She smiled at him, and it was not a kind smile, but it was not unkind either. "I have almost nothing left to lose, Tomas. Which means I can finally afford it. The answer is no. Take your soldiers and go home, whatever home is now."

He closed his eyes. When he opened them, the brother was gone and the officer was back, and that was its own grief.

"I'm sorry," he said. And meant it. That was the worst part. He knocked twice on the doorframe — the old signal, *all clear, come in*, from a hundred games of hide-and-seek in their father's warehouse — and the door opened, and the soldiers came in, and the world became very fast.

* * *

Later, Jonah would reconstruct it in fragments, because that was how it lived in her memory: not a scene but a series of flashes, each one lit like a match struck in a dark room.

The first soldier reaching for her arm, and her twisting away, and the hot tear of stitches giving way along her side.

Mira's face at the top of the stairs — she had disobeyed, of course she had, six-year-olds always disobey exactly when obedience matters most — and Petra's hand closing over her mouth, pulling her back into the dark.

Tomas shouting *not like this, not the girl, I said the girl unharmed* — and hearing, even in the chaos, the sound of a man discovering the fine print of his own bargain.

And then the ceiling.

That was the part nobody expected, least of all the soldiers: the ceiling, which gave way with a groan of old nails and released a hundredweight of salted herring, packed in barrels directly above by Anselm's order two days earlier, onto the heads of the coalition escort. Fish rained down. Barrels rolled. The room filled with a smell so profoundly, apocalyptically marine that one soldier simply stopped fighting and began to retch.

From the loft above, Anselm's voice, thin but triumphant, drifting down through the wreckage: "*Class, if you'll note the load-bearing properties of poorly maintained chandleries—*"

A second figure dropped through the hole — Anselm's boy, the breathless one, sixteen and wiry as a whippet — landed on the remaining soldier's shoulders, and steered him, with the casual expertise of a dockhand, headfirst into a barrel of pitch.

"Back door!" the boy yelled. "Go, go, Auntie says the market, the market's the maze!"

Petra appeared with Mira already bundled in an oversized fishmonger's apron, hood up, and pressed a knife handle-first into Jonah's hand. "Anselm says the fish market. Everyone owes Anselm. Go."

Jonah grabbed Mira's hand and ran.

Behind her, in the ruin of the chandlery, she heard her brother's voice one last time — not calling after her, not threatening, just her name, *Jonah*, spoken the way you speak the name of something you have lost, and she did not stop, because stopping was death and also because she was crying and you cannot fight and cry at the same time, you can only do one properly.

* * *

The fish market of Callow's Reach in winter was a country unto itself: a labyrinth of stalls and hanging lanterns and ice-slicked stone, loud enough to swallow gunfire and crowded enough to lose an army in. Anselm's network moved them through it in relays — a woman selling eels who turned her back at precisely the right moment; two boys who "accidentally" overturned a cart of mackerel across the lane behind them; an old man mending nets who pointed, without looking up, down an alley that smelled of brine and kerosene.

Mira kept pace, small hand clamped in Jonah's, breathing hard through her nose, and when they finally ducked under a tarpaulin behind the oyster bar — hidden, momentarily, in a canyon of shellfish crates — the girl looked up at her with the serious, unsentimental eyes that undid Jonah completely.

"Was that your brother?" Mira whispered.

"Yes."

"He threw fish on you."

"He did," Jonah managed. "It's a family tradition, apparently."

Mira considered this with great gravity. Then she reached up and picked a small silver fish scale off Jonah's collar, examined it against the lantern light, and closed Jonah's fingers around it. "Keep it," she said. "So you remember him how he was before. Not how he is now."

And Jonah, kneeling in fish guts and gunpowder smoke with her wound bleeding through her bandage and thirty-eight hours left on Captain Voss's merciless clock, held a fish scale like a relic and understood that the child had just done what no adult in this war had managed: she had found the exact weight of the thing, and named it, and handed it back.

Somewhere across the market, a whistle blew. Coalition boots on stone, many of them, moving in their direction.

"Stay close to me," Jonah whispered, pulling Mira up. "Whatever happens — stay close."

They ran deeper into the maze.
