## 13. Checkpoint Two: The Railway

The widow's weeds had belonged to Mrs. Ashworth, who had worn them for eleven years after her husband drowned in the Thames and who had once told Jonah, over a cup of tea she was not supposed to be serving, that mourning clothes were the most honest garments a woman could own. *They tell everyone you've lost something,* she'd said, *and nobody asks what.*

Jonah thought of this as she buttoned the black jacket over the ledger.

The book itself — Edith's ledger, every name, every date, every act of conscience written in a hand so small it might have been a hymn — lay flat against her ribs now, sewn into the lining by Greta's steady nurse's fingers while Jonah sat very still and tried not to breathe like a woman being sewn into her own coat. Greta had used a curved needle and surgical thread and had made exactly one joke, which for Greta constituted a season of comedy.

"You will not run," she said, snipping the thread. "If you run, it will tear. So you must walk. Slowly. Like a woman who has buried a husband and has nowhere urgent to be."

"I have somewhere extremely urgent to be."

"Then you must walk slowly anyway." She pressed her palm flat against the lining, feeling the shape of the book through the wool, and her face did something complicated. "It is strange. I have held this book before, when Edith wrote in it. It never felt heavy until now."

"That's because now it's worth hanging for."

Greta looked at her over the spectacles she wore for close work. "No," she said. "It felt heavy then too. We simply didn't say so."

* * *

The ring came from Ruth.

She brought it to the house on the Tuesday, wrapped in a scrap of tissue paper, and set it on the kitchen table between them as though it were a lump of coal she was slightly ashamed of.

"It was my mother's," she said. "I don't wear it. I haven't since—" She stopped, and Jonah watched her decide how much truth to spend. "Since my brother's funeral. Gold band, plain. Nobody looks twice at a wedding ring. Everybody looks twice at a woman without one who claims to be respectable."

"And the weeds?"

"Mrs. Ashworth. I asked her. She nearly wept with joy — said they were finally doing their duty." Ruth almost smiled. It was a strange thing, watching Ruth almost smile; it happened around the edges of her mouth first, like frost forming on a window. "You'll want gloves. Your hands are—"

"Working hands."

"Beautiful hands," Ruth said, and then went pink to the ears, and busied herself with the tissue paper as though it required folding into an origami crane. "Practical hands. Hands that look like they've done things. A widow of your apparent age ought to have softer ones, or at least gloved ones."

Jonah picked up the ring. It was lighter than she expected. Rings usually were, she supposed; people imagined them heavier because of what they carried.

"Ruth Calloway," she said, "are you proposing?"

The pink deepened to something approaching crimson. "I am lending you my dead mother's ring for the purposes of committing a crime, which I believe is the opposite of a proposal."

"In some families it's practically the same thing." Jonah slid the ring onto her right hand, then moved it to the left, where it sat snug against the second knuckle. "It fits."

"Of course it fits. My mother had working hands too." Ruth said it lightly, but her eyes stayed on the ring a moment longer than lightness warranted. Then she straightened. "King's Cross. You're certain about King's Cross?"

"Certain enough. Mr. Ellery keeps a room above the stationer's on Pentonville Road. He's handled documents for Edith since before the war. He'll know where it needs to go next."

"And if he doesn't?"

"Then I'll improvise."

"I've seen you improvise," Ruth said. "You once told a census taker that the spare beds were for a lodger named Mr. Pemberton, and then, when pressed, described him as a night watchman with a limp and a fondness for racing forms. You built an entire man out of thin air in under four seconds."

"He was a very convincing man."

"He was a ridiculous man. I only didn't report him because I was too busy trying not to laugh." Ruth took a breath, and the humor drained out of her face the way water drains from a basin. "Jonah. The checkpoints. Hale's men are at the stations now. They're stopping women especially — anyone traveling alone, anyone with luggage. They're looking for papers."

"I know."

"They're looking for *you.*"

"I know that too." Jonah folded the weeds' black veil back from her face, an old woman's gesture, and saw Ruth register the transformation: the way grief, worn correctly, made a person invisible. "That's why I'm going as nobody. Nobody gets searched the way somebody does. A widow going to identify her husband's effects — that's a sad story, and men in uniform hate sad stories. They wave them through just to be rid of them."

"And if they don't wave you through?"

"Then I'll cry," Jonah said. "I'm told I do it very convincingly. Twenty years of practice."

* * *

She practiced walking slowly all Wednesday morning, up and down the length of the cellar, Greta timing her with the kitchen clock and shaking her head each time.

"You walk like a woman late for a train."

"I *am* a woman late for a train."

"A widow is never late for anything. A widow has surrendered her claim to urgency. Again."

By the fourth circuit Jonah had found it — a gait with no destination in it, feet lifting and setting down like thoughts that go nowhere. It frightened her how easily she found it. It frightened her more that part of her recognized it from her mother, from the years after her father died, when the whole village had moved at that pace and called it dignity.

At noon Edith came down to see her off. She stood at the foot of the stairs in her grey day dress, looking smaller than Jonah had ever seen her, and for a long moment neither of them spoke.

"You'll take the 2:40," Edith said finally. "Not the 1:15. The 1:15 is full of soldiers on leave, and soldiers on leave are bored, and bored men volunteer for inspections to relieve the tedium."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And you'll carry nothing but the ring, the veil, and a letter from the War Office — I've written one, it's in your pocket, it says your husband's effects are held at the North Eastern office. It's forged, obviously, but it's forged well. Thomas did it. He's rather proud."

"Thomas should be proud. His forgeries have kept three people alive."

Edith crossed the cellar and took Jonah's face in both hands, the way she had done once, twenty years ago, on the dock at Tilbury, when Jonah had stepped off the boat with everything she owned in a canvas bag and no idea that the woman meeting her would become the fixed point her whole life turned around.

"Come back," Edith said. "That's not an instruction. That's a request. I find I'm still allowed those."

"You're allowed anything," Jonah said. "You just rarely ask."

"Well." Edith released her, and her voice steadied itself with visible effort, like a woman smoothing a wrinkled skirt. "Consider this an exception. Come back, Jonah. The house is unbearable when you're not in it. Everyone walks quietly and pretends it's out of respect, and really it's because you're the only one of us who makes any noise at all."

* * *

King's Cross at half past two in the afternoon was a cathedral of smoke and grief.

Jonah had been coming to London stations for twenty years — delivering, collecting, standing in queues with other people's secrets in her handbag — but she had never seen it like this. Every pillar wore its billboards stripped to recruitment posters gone soft with damp. Every bench held a woman in black. Half the country seemed to be wearing Mrs. Ashworth's clothes now, and the other half seemed to be waiting for them.

She joined the queue for the northbound platform behind a young mother with twins and an old man carrying a cage of pigeons, and she walked slowly, exactly as Greta had drilled her, and she let the veil do its work. People parted for grief. People apologized to grief. A porter touched his cap to her, and she inclined her head with the gracious exhaustion of a woman who had accepted condolences until the words had worn smooth as pebbles.

The checkpoint stood at the mouth of Platform Three: two military policemen, a railway constable, and a table. On the table, a rubber stamp, a ledger of their own, and a stack of travel permits held down by a brass weight shaped like a lion.

The queue ahead of her thinned. The twins went through — the mother showed a permit, got a bored nod. The pigeon man argued about whether birds counted as luggage, lost the argument, and went through looking personally betrayed by the entire British state.

Then it was Jonah.

"Surname?" The nearer policeman — young, freckled, a voice still cracking at the seams — didn't look up from his ledger.

"Okonkwo," she said, and heard the name come out of her mouth in her own accent, unguarded, and corrected instantly, flattening it into the careful London vowels she'd worn for twenty years like the veil. "*O-con-wo. My husband's effects are held at the North Eastern office. York.*"

Something shifted in the young man's posture. Not suspicion — something worse. Pity. His eyes came up, took in the veil, the weeds, the gloved hands, and softened.

"I'm sorry for your loss, ma'am."

"Thank you." She produced the forged letter. Her hands, inside the gloves, were perfectly steady, which surprised her; she had expected them to shake, and their steadiness felt almost like a betrayal of the performance. "They wrote to me last week. I'm to identify his things."

The policeman read the letter. Jonah watched him read it and understood, with the clarity of a woman reading letters by lamplight for two decades, exactly what he saw: official crests, official phrasing, the magic furniture of authority that made ordinary people stop thinking. Thomas was indeed rather good.

"Purpose of travel?"

"To collect my husband's effects." She let a crack enter her voice, small and controlled, a hairline fracture in porcelain. "He was at Passchendaele. They — the letter says there wasn't much. A watch. Some letters. I'd like to bring them home myself rather than have them posted. It didn't seem right, posted things. For — for that sort of thing."

The freckled policeman swallowed. Beside him, the older one — sergeant's stripes, a face like a closed door — glanced over.

"Effects, is it," the sergeant said. "Open your bag, please, ma'am."

"Albert," said the young one, with quiet horror.

"It's procedure, Danny. Ma'am, I'm sorry. Open the bag."

Jonah opened the bag. Inside: a prayer book, a handkerchief, a small photograph of a man she had invented on the walk from the taxi — fair-haired, serious, borrowed from a studio portrait she'd once delivered to a widow in Camberwell — and nothing else. Nothing else at all. The ledger rode against her ribs, sewn into the lining of a coat that no one, she prayed, would ask a grieving woman to remove in a public railway station, because Englishmen would rather let a spy through than witness tears.

The sergeant looked in the bag. Looked at the photograph. Looked at her.

"Passchendaele," he said, and his closed door opened perhaps an inch. "My brother's there. Or was. We don't rightly know which word fits yet."

"There isn't a word that fits," Jonah said, and meant it so completely that it stopped being performance, and the sergeant heard that — heard the true thing under the false thing, the way she had spent twenty years hearing true things under false ones — and nodded slowly.

"Platform Three," he said. "God go with you, ma'am."

She walked through. She did not look back. Looking back, she knew, was how people were caught — not by searches but by the guilty reflex of turning around, the body's confession. She walked slowly toward the train, a widow with a watch and some letters to collect, and boarded, and found a seat by the window, and only when the platform began to slide away did she allow herself one breath — long, silent, enormous — that she had been holding since the queue.

Against her ribs, the ledger pressed its small weight, patient as a heart.

* * *

Pentonville Road smelled of coal smoke and fried onions. The stationer's shop stood where it had always stood, its window full of exercise books and ink bottles and penny notebooks, and above it, the narrow door with ELLERY painted on the glass in flaking gold.

Jonah climbed the stairs slowly — genuinely slowly now; the performance had merged with the fatigue — and knocked the pattern: two, pause, three.

No answer.

She knocked again. Two, pause, three.

From inside, faintly, a clock ticked. That was wrong. Mr. Ellery's clock had never ticked audibly from the landing in fifteen years of visits; the man kept it oiled like a rifle and wound it like a sacrament. If she could hear it ticking, either the door was open or—

The door was unlocked.

"Mr. Ellery?" She pushed it open. "It's Jonah. From Mrs. Vane's."

The room was cold. That was the first thing. November sat in the room like a tenant who had stopped paying rent. The second thing was the chair — Ellery's chair, the wingback by the window where he received visitors with his bad leg propped on a stool — and it was empty, tipped slightly sideways, as though he had risen quickly or fallen slowly.

He was on the floor beside the desk.

Jonah knelt. She knew before she touched him — the stillness told her, the particular finished quality of a body that has stopped being occupied — but she touched him anyway, two fingers to the throat, out of respect and out of the old nurse's habit she'd caught from Greta. Cold. Hours, maybe a day.

Mr. Ellery, keeper of documents, forger of permits, the man who had taught her the difference between a good lie and a survivable one ("A good lie is small and boring, girl; make them wish they hadn't asked"), lay on the floor of his own room in his waistcoat, one hand stretched toward the desk drawer, which hung open and empty.

There was no wound. There was no disturbance, beyond the chair. On the desk, weighted under the same brass lion that had held down permits for twenty years, sat a single sheet of paper in Ellery's cramped, beautiful hand.

*Jonah — if it's you, and it will be, because you knock properly —*

*I'm sorry. Heart, the doctor said, months ago, and I told no one because telling people means becoming a project, and I refuse to die as a project. But I am dying faster than the doctor's schedule and slower than your errand requires, and I cannot carry what you're carrying.*

*The solicitor. Wetherby. Lincoln's Inn Fields, number 14. Tell him "the compass points north in winter." He'll understand. He's slow, he's cautious, he charges too much, and he is the only man left in this city whose discretion I would bet a war on.*

*Burn this note. And girl — the book matters more than the grief. Take the grief with you. It travels well. It always has.*

*E.*

Jonah sat down on the floor beside him, the way you sit with the dead, and for a while she did nothing at all. The clock ticked. Somewhere below, the stationer served a customer; she heard the till ring, absurd and ordinary, the world continuing its small commerce directly beneath a dead man's floor.

She had known him fifteen years. He had never once asked her a personal question, which she understood now, sitting on his cold floor, had been its own form of love. He had read her handwriting across two decades of forged signatures and had never once remarked that her letters grew rounder when she was happy.

And now the contact was dead, the route was broken, the ledger pressed against her ribs with nowhere to go, and somewhere behind her, in the smoke of King's Cross, Inspector Hale's men were learning to look for a woman in widow's weeds.

Jonah took the note. She struck a match from the box on his mantel — he would have approved of using his own matches — and held it until the paper curled and the words went orange and then black and then were ash in the cold grate, joining whatever else of Ellery's secrets had already gone that way.

Then she stood, straightened her veil, and began, slowly, in the manner of a widow with nowhere urgent to be, to plan a journey to Lincoln's Inn Fields.

Behind her, on the landing, she heard footsteps begin to climb the stairs.
