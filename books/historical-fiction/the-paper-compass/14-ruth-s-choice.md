## 14. Ruth's Choice

The photographs arrived on a Tuesday morning, in a buff folder tied with the particular shade of government string that always reminded Ruth of the twine her mother used to tie up the Christmas goose — the kind of string that meant something was about to be carved.

There were six of them. A woman at a post office counter, caught mid-turn, her face half-shadowed by her own collar. The same woman crossing a street near King's Cross, blurred as though the camera itself had been afraid to look directly at her. The same woman again, sharper this time, standing outside a house in Camden whose number Ruth knew by heart now, though she had never written it down anywhere and never would.

Jonah Okonkwo. Driver to Mrs. Edith Vane. Twenty years in that household, according to the file's thin biography, which was less a life than an inventory: name, date of arrival, employer, address. As if a person could be summed up the way one summed up a parcel awaiting collection.

"Take your time," said Inspector Hale, who had come down from London himself for this, which told Ruth precisely how much the matter mattered to him. He sat across from her in the census office with his hands folded on his knee like a man waiting patiently for tea he intended to complain about. "Look carefully. Take all the time you need."

Ruth looked carefully. That was, after all, what she was paid eleven shillings a week to do. She had spent four months looking carefully — at doorsteps and dustbins, at the arithmetic of households, at the small lies people told about how many slept under their roof. Looking carefully had been her whole profession, and she had been good at it, right up until the day being good at it became the same thing as being dangerous.

The trouble was that she recognized more than the woman in the photographs. She recognized the expression. It was the expression of someone carrying something heavy through a crowd and hoping nobody asked her to set it down.

"Well?" Hale said.

"She might be the driver," Ruth said. "I couldn't say for certain. I only saw her briefly."

"You saw her twice."

"I saw a woman twice. Women in dark coats look alike when you're not paying attention."

"And were you paying attention, Miss Calloway?"

It was a trap laid so neatly that Ruth almost admired it. Say yes, and she confirmed her competence and therefore her identification. Say no, and she confessed her reports were worthless, which was true but unhelpful, since the reports existed already, filed and stamped, in a drawer somewhere behind Hale's left shoulder.

"I pay attention to houses," Ruth said. "That is my work. I count beds and I count names and I note where the two don't agree. Faces are not my department."

"No." Hale smiled, and it was a good smile, the kind that made men trust him in courtrooms. "Your brother was in the army, wasn't he? Private Thomas Calloway. Fourth Battalion."

The room went very quiet, or perhaps it was only Ruth who went quiet, everything in her drawing inward like a snail touched on its shell.

"He was shot at dawn in March of 1916," Hale continued, gently, horribly, the way a man reads out a menu. "For desertion. I mention it because I understand these things can complicate a person's judgment. Sympathy is a fine quality in a woman, Miss Calloway, but it has no place in an investigation. So I'll ask you plainly, and you may answer plainly. Is this the woman from the Vane household?"

Ruth looked at the photograph again. At the half-turned face. At the collar riding up as if against the cold, or against being seen.

She thought: Tom was nineteen years old and he cried the night before he went back to the front, and he said, *Ruthie, I can't, I can't go back there,* and she had held his hand and said nothing, because what was there to say? And then he had gone anyway, because Tom always did what he was told, right up until the morning he didn't, and the army had put him against a wall for it and called it discipline, and their mother had received a letter saying he died of wounds, which was a lie so tidy it might have been typed by the same clerks who filed census anomalies.

She thought: somewhere in that house in Camden there may be another Tom. Another boy who cannot, who will not, who has run out of obedience.

And she thought, with a clarity that felt almost like laughter: *I have been filing reports about frightened people for four months, and every single report has been a stone thrown into somebody's window.*

"No," Ruth said. "I can't identify her."

Hale studied her for a long moment. Outside, a cart rattled past; somewhere a whistle blew; the ordinary world went on conducting its business of moving things from one place to another.

"That's a pity," he said at last, pleasantly. "Because I have three witnesses prepared to say otherwise. Your hesitation has been noted, Miss Calloway. You may consider yourself suspended pending review."

He gathered his photographs, tapped them into a neat stack, and left. He did not slam the door. Men like Hale never slammed doors; they simply closed them behind themselves with the confidence that they could open any other they liked.

* * *

Ruth sat at her desk for a while after he'd gone. The office around her carried on — Mrs. Prewitt sharpening pencils with the ferocity of a woman whittling stakes, young Ellery copying figures with his tongue between his teeth. Nobody looked at her. In offices, disgrace announces itself by silence, and everyone had heard the silence.

Her report lay in front of her. The anomaly report, the one she had filed weeks ago and regretted within the hour: *Residence, 14 Alderton Road. Five persons listed. Seven beds observed. Recommend inquiry.* Eleven words that had set all of this in motion, that had brought Inspector Hale and his buff folders and his gentle voice into a war already too full of gentle-voiced men doing terrible bookkeeping.

She read it once more. Then she picked up her pen, crossed out each word with a single deliberate stroke, wrote beneath it in her best clerk's hand — *Withdrawn. Filed in error. R. Calloway* — signed it, and dropped it into the tray for the shredding room herself, walking it there like a woman escorting a prisoner she was also setting free.

Mrs. Prewitt watched her do it and said nothing, but as Ruth collected her coat and hat, the older woman pressed a paper bag into her hand. Inside were two rock buns, slightly stale, absolutely sincere.

"For the walk," Mrs. Prewitt said. "Wherever it is you're going."

* * *

Ruth did not know where she was going, which was a novel sensation for a woman who had spent her whole life knowing exactly where she was going and arriving there punctually and alone. She ate one bun on the bus and saved the second, out of habit, for a Tom who had been dead two years and would never eat another.

What she knew was this: the Vane house stood at 14 Alderton Road, and the woman in the photographs came and went from it, and Ruth had destroyed her career rather than point at her, and if she was going to be ruined she might as well be ruined for something instead of merely beside it.

So she got off the bus two stops early and walked, and stood across the road from the house for the better part of an afternoon, feeling extremely foolish, until the door opened and there she was — the driver, Jonah Okonkwo, in a plain grey coat, a basket over her arm, looking exactly like a woman carrying something heavy and hoping nobody would ask her to set it down.

Ruth crossed the road before her courage could file a withdrawal of its own.

"Miss Okonkwo."

Jonah stopped. Her face did not change, which was itself remarkable; most faces Ruth knew announced their contents like shop windows. This one was shuttered, courteous, and entirely unreadable.

"Miss Calloway," Jonah said. "You have my address memorized, I see."

"I've come to apologize. And then to offer you something, and I'd take it kindly if you'd let me finish before you decide what I am, because I'm not entirely sure myself yet."

Jonah's eyebrow rose a fraction. "Go on."

"The census. The question about the beds. I reported it. I want you to know that I reported it, because I'd rather you heard it from me than found out some worse way. And this morning a man named Hale showed me your photograph and asked me to identify you, and I refused, and I've been dismissed for it, and I shredded my own report with my own hands, and—" Ruth stopped, drew breath, and discovered that the speech she had rehearsed on the bus had run out exactly here, at the edge of the thing she actually wanted to say. "And I think my brother was one of the men you're hiding. Not literally. But the same kind of man. The kind the army shoots for being afraid. And I have spent two years being polite about it, and politeness turns out to be just cowardice with better posture, and I'm done with it."

A long silence. Somewhere down the street a dog barked twice and gave up, having concluded it wasn't worth the effort, which Ruth sympathized with entirely.

"You shredded it," Jonah said at last.

"With my own hands. Well. With the office shredder. But I fed it in personally."

Something moved at the corner of Jonah's mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile kept its coat. "You walked it there yourself?"

"Like a prison escort. I'm told I have a talent for ceremony."

"Hm." Jonah looked at her for a long moment, and Ruth had the distinct sensation of being counted — not the way the census counted her, as a name beside a bed, but the way one counts money one has been handed unexpectedly, checking the notes against the light. "You understand," Jonah said, "that I have no reason to believe you. You understand that a woman who could turn so easily against her own office might turn as easily against us."

"Yes."

"And you came anyway."

"I came anyway. Because I've realized something standing on street corners this afternoon feeling like an idiot, which is that I have spent four months learning everything about that house except whether anyone in it needed help. And I think they do. And I think you do. And I think—" Ruth hesitated, then finished, because she had promised herself she would stop being tidy. "I think I'd rather be useful than respectable. Respectable hasn't done anything for me lately except get me suspended."

Jonah was quiet a moment longer. Then she shifted the basket on her arm and said, "There's a bench by the church on the next street. If we're going to ruin each other properly, we shouldn't do it standing in the road like a pair of suffragettes. People talk."

They walked. At the corner, without appearing to look at him, Jonah paused to let a man in a brown suit cross ahead of them — and Ruth saw the man's step falter, just slightly, the way a man falters when he realizes he has been seen seeing. Jonah resumed walking as though nothing had happened.

"We're followed," Ruth murmured.

"We're always followed. He's new; the last one chewed tobacco. You learn to prefer the ones with vices, they're easier to predict." They reached the bench and sat. Jonah set the basket between them, and Ruth noticed, with the eye of a woman who had spent her career noticing, that the basket was lined with a false bottom and that whatever lay beneath the vegetables was flat and rectangular and heavy as a verdict. "Now," Jonah said. "Tell me about your brother."

So Ruth told her. About Tom, and the night before he went back, and the letter that lied about wounds, and the mother who never quite believed it and never quite stopped believing it either, because the truth required a kind of strength their family had spent on other things. She told it plainly, without performance, and found that telling it to this shuttered, careful woman was easier than telling it to anyone, because Jonah listened the way one reads a difficult page — completely, without hurrying, willing to go back over the hard lines.

When she finished, Jonah said, "In that house there is a boy named Albert who is twenty and shakes when the knockers come. There is a nurse called Greta who has tended English soldiers for nine years and is treated as an enemy for the crime of her vowels. There is a man who makes papers so good that even I sometimes forget they're lies. None of them are traitors. All of them are being hunted by a man who needs traitors the way a tailor needs cloth." She turned to Ruth fully then, and for the first time the shutters came down — not all the way, but enough that Ruth glimpsed what was behind them, which was exhaustion, and iron, and something that might have been hope worn down to the wick. "You have cost us something already, Miss Calloway. Your report. Your eyes. Four months of a trained observer counting our beds. I won't pretend otherwise. But you've also just paid for it with your livelihood, which is more than most people ever risk. So I'll ask you once, and I want you to hear the whole weight of the question before you answer it. What I'm about to do in the next few days may destroy me, or save everyone, or both. If you walk beside me, you cannot walk back. Do you understand?"

"I understand," Ruth said. "I've been walking back my whole life. I'd like to try forward."

Jonah regarded her a moment longer. Then she lifted the false bottom of the basket an inch — just an inch, just enough for Ruth to see the edge of a ledger bound in green cloth — and closed it again.

"Then come," she said. "And mind the third step inside; it complains, and the man in the brown suit has ears like a tax collector."

They rose together. Behind them the church clock struck five, and somewhere across the city, in rooms Ruth was only beginning to imagine, guns waited for orders that might never come — or might come tomorrow, or tonight, and every hour between now and then was a corridor with doors opening and closing.

As they turned into Alderton Road, Jonah said quietly, without breaking stride:

"There's something else you should know. Edith means to give herself up. Tomorrow."

Ruth stopped dead on the pavement. "Give herself up—"

"To Hale. Publicly. To buy the rest of us the hours we need." Jonah's face had gone shuttered again, but her voice, for one syllable, cracked like ice on a pond. "And I intend to stop her. Or fail. Come inside, Miss Calloway. There isn't much time, and there's a great deal you need to learn."
