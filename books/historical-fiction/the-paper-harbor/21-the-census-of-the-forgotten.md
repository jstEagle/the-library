## 21. The Census of the Forgotten

The census taker who came to the harbor district this spring was a boy. That's what they are now — boys, mostly, with soft hands and government-issue pens and a form that asks fewer questions than Voss's did, because the state has learned that too many questions make people remember things worth hiding.

He knocked on my door on a Tuesday, because the state still believes Tuesdays are honest days, and he asked me my name, my age, my occupation.

"Archivist," I said.

He looked at my room — the one room I've kept all these years, with its single window over the water and its shelves of hand-bound books — and wrote something down. Probably *eccentric*. Probably *harmless*. Both would be accurate enough, in the way that a photograph is accurate: true, and missing everything that matters.

"And your family?" he asked, pen hovering.

"Gone," I said, which was true, and also not, which is the condition most truths aspire to around here.

He thanked me and moved on to the next door, and I stood at my window watching him work his way down the street like a man collecting debts no one owed, and I thought: twenty-two years ago, a man with a form came asking questions, and it cost me everything I had left. This year's version will cost the district nothing at all, because there is nothing left to take. The strongmen won their armistice that never was, consolidated their republic that isn't one, and discovered that governing a people you've emptied out is remarkably cheap. No bread riots from the dead. No strikes from the disappeared. The census of 1945 asks fewer questions because the answers have been simplified for our convenience.

But here is what the boy with the soft hands doesn't know, walking past my window with his forms flapping: across the street, behind the shuttered chapel where the pigeons have taken up permanent squatter's rights, there is a wall with a cavity in it, and in that cavity there is a book. Hand-copied. Four hundred and eleven pages as of last night. And every name in it belongs to someone the official census has decided never existed.

I call it the Census of the Forgotten, though I didn't name it. The district named it, the way districts name things — by need rather than elegance. Old Ferro started calling it that when I recorded his wife, Marta, who was taken in '31 for the crime of teaching children the old anthem. He stood in my doorway while I wrote her name, her age, the date she vanished, the ship or truck or cellar if anyone knew it, and he said, "So. A census." And he laughed, badly, the way men laugh when the alternative is worse. "They count us to erase us. You count us to keep us. Same word. Different arithmetic."

Different arithmetic. I've thought about that more times than I can number, which is fitting, since numbering things is the whole business.

* * *

Let me tell you how the book works, because method matters. Memory without method is just grief wearing a costume.

Each entry gets a page, or part of one. Name first — full name, the one given at baptism or registry, not the nickname, though I record the nickname too, because a person is both. Then birth year if anyone knows it; half these entries say *circa*, and I've come to love that little word, *circa*, with its shrug built right in. Then the last known address. Then the date and manner of disappearance, as best witnessed. Then — and this is the part I'm proudest of, the part that took me years to understand was necessary — then a line about them as a person. Not a statistic. A sentence.

*Marta Ferro. Born circa 1889, Calle del Sol. Taken 14 March 1931, from the schoolhouse, by three men in a black car. Taught the old anthem to children who were not supposed to know it had been replaced. Sang it once, loudly, through the window of the car, so the whole street heard.*

That last line came from Ferro himself, weeping in my doorway, insisting I write it down exactly. "The car," he said. "Put down that she sang from the car. Let them read that someday, whoever they are, whenever this ends."

This hasn't ended. But someday is a direction, not a destination, and I keep walking toward it one page at a time.

The ink is iron gall, made the old way, because it lasts centuries and because buying ink in quantity invites questions. The paper comes from a stationer two districts over who owes me — everyone owes someone in this city, that's the one economy that never collapsed — and who has learned not to ask why an old woman needs reams of good rag paper. The binding I do myself, learned from a bookbinder who died in '29 and left me his awl and his patience, though not in equal measure.

And the wall keeps the finished volumes. The chapel cavity holds six now, wrapped in oilcloth, stacked like loaves. Six volumes. Four hundred and eleven pages. Nine hundred and sixty names.

Nine hundred and sixty. I know the number the way I used to know the weight of a satchel against my ribs: by carrying it daily.

* * *

People come to me the way they used to come to wells, I imagine — quietly, at odd hours, carrying something heavy they can't set down anywhere else. There's no sign on my door. There couldn't be. Word travels the way it always has in the harbor district: down stairwells, over fish stalls, through the confessional of a priest who has decided that some secrets belong to God and some belong to me, and has stopped worrying about the distinction.

A woman came last month with her grandson translating, because she'd lost her tongue to a stroke but not her memory, and between her gestures and the boy's halting voice I got down her sister's entry: taken in '33, pregnant, from a house on the quay. The woman held my wrist while the boy read the finished entry back to her, and when he reached the line I'd written — *made lace finer than anything sold in the shops, gave it away to brides who couldn't pay* — the old woman closed her eyes and nodded, twice, like a judge accepting testimony.

That nod is the payment. I want to be clear about that, because people sometimes try to give me money, and money is the one thing I refuse. Money makes it a service. This is not a service. This is a reckoning, and reckonings don't run on coin.

What I am, officially, is nothing. Unofficially — well. Titles accumulate the way dust does, without effort and against your wishes. The district calls me the archivist. Some of the younger ones, who've grown up in the shadow of the thing, call me *la contadora* — the counter, the teller. One child, Ferro's great-granddaughter, aged perhaps seven, informed me solemnly that I was "the lady who remembers everybody," and then asked if I remembered her. I told her yes, and wrote her down that same evening, alive and present and seven years old, because the book is not only for the dead. It is for everyone the official record has gotten wrong, and the official record has gotten wrongness down to a science.

* * *

Here is a confession, since the book has taught me that confession kept too long goes septic: some nights I hate the work.

Not the purpose. Never the purpose. But the labor of it — the steady, unglamorous accumulation of other people's worst mornings — wears at a body like tide wears at pilings. There are entries I cannot finish in one sitting. There was a father, years back, who brought me the names of his three sons, taken together, in one night, and I wrote the first son fine, and the second son fine, and then somewhere in the third entry my hand simply stopped, mid-word, and I sat in the dark for an hour before I could dip the pen again. The father waited. He understood. Everyone who comes to that chair understands, because they've all had the hour in the dark themselves.

And there are nights — rarer now, but they come — when I dream in the old grammar. Satchels and seals and dockside dawn light, Voss's polite voice asking his polite questions, the smell of the burning dispatches, ash settling on black water like the world's worst snowfall. I wake with my hand pressed flat against the wall, as if checking that the cavity is still there, still full, still mine.

It always is. That's the strange mercy of it. Everything else in my life has been surrendered, spent, or stolen, but the wall holds.

* * *

Tomas would have laughed at the book. Or he'd have cried, which for Tomas was the same gesture wearing different clothes.

My brother stayed, after the dock. After I put Marisol on that ship and handed him the choice — testify or flee — and watched him walk, shaking, toward the tribunal with his whole life folded up small in his chest. They took him that afternoon. What they did to him took eleven days, according to the guard who later sold the information to the district for cigarettes, and I will not write those details here, even now, even in my own head, because Tomas earned the right to be remembered as more than what was done to him.

*Tomas Reyes. Born 1896. Clerk of the provisional government. Forged a list to save his family and confessed to a greater forgery to save a country's memory. Testified until they stopped his mouth. Brother. Father. The bravest coward I ever knew.*

That's the entry. I wrote it the year the book began, before any other name, because the book needed a cornerstone and he was it. Some nights I read it aloud to the empty room, and some nights I only look at it, and either way it does what stones do: holds things up.

Marisol writes. Twice a year, letters that cross the water like slow birds, arriving creased and censored and occasionally missing paragraphs the postal authorities found inflammatory — which, knowing Marisol, they usually were. She's a teacher now, in the country that took her in. She teaches the old anthem, among other things, and when I read that in her last letter I had to set the page down and laugh until it turned into something else, because history doesn't repeat, it just changes addresses.

She asks, in every letter, when she can come home. And in every reply I give the same answer, which is the true one: when home can hold you. The warrant lists were purged in the amnesty of '39, but amnesties here are weather, not climate, and the men who signed hers are mostly dead but their filing systems survive them, as filing systems do. Soon, I write. Sooner than you think, maybe. Things are moving. Even stone moves, if you're patient enough to watch.

* * *

Because things are moving. I should say that plainly, since plainness is the whole point of the book.

For two years now, the regime has been fraying in the way regimes fray: not dramatically, not with banners in the streets, but in small administrative humiliations. The census takers get younger and more frightened. The informants get greedier and less reliable. Last winter, a colonel defected with a trunk full of internal memos, and copies of certain pages reached the district through channels I neither ask about nor advertise, and those pages confirmed what Doña Ilsa told me over tea twenty years ago: the armistice was theater, the evacuation was theft, the whole harbor was a stage set for betrayal.

We knew. We always knew. But there is a difference between knowing a thing in your bones and seeing it printed on a colonel's letterhead, and when those pages circulated through the district — passed hand to hand like communion — I watched grown men stand very still in doorways, reading, and then fold the papers carefully and put them inside their shirts, over their hearts, the way you'd carry a letter from someone you loved.

The new volume — the seventh — has a section now that isn't names. It's documents. Copies, in my hand, of the memos, the manifest falsifications, the order that authorized the '31 schoolhouse removals. Because the Census of the Forgotten was never meant to be only a ledger of loss. It was meant to be the corrected edition. The true record. And a true record needs its evidence the way a ship needs ballast: invisible, heavy, and keeping everything upright.

Someday — and I permit myself this word more often now, because someday has begun to lean toward soon — someday the district will have a real archive again, with shelves and catalogues and a boy with soft hands doing honest work. And when that day comes, someone will open the chapel wall, and find six volumes wrapped in oilcloth, and understand that while the official census was counting the living to manage them, another census was counting the erased to return them.

Same word. Different arithmetic.

* * *

Last night I finished entry nine hundred and sixty-one. An old fisherman, Aurelio, taken in '37 for lending his boat to a family the regime had scheduled for drowning-by-weather, which is what we called it when the coast guard was ordered to be elsewhere. His granddaughter brought me the details, and his nickname — *el Faro*, the lighthouse, because he'd guided boats home in weather no sane man sailed — and when I'd written the closing line (*never lost a soul he set out to bring home*) she touched the wet ink before it dried, lightly, with one finger, the way you'd touch a sleeping child.

Then she said, "There's a letter for you. Came on the morning boat. Foreign stamps."

I don't get foreign stamps. In twenty-two years, the mail that finds me is local, hand-delivered, cautious. Foreign stamps mean customs, and customs means inspection, and inspection means someone official has already read whatever the letter says.

But I took it anyway. Of course I took it. Curiosity was the first muscle I ever trained and it has never once atrophied.

The handwriting on the envelope stopped my breath before I'd broken the seal. I knew that hand. I'd taught it, in a kitchen, twenty-five years ago, correcting a girl's slanting letters over flour-dusted wood — *press lighter, Marisol, the pen is not an enemy* — and the girl had laughed and said the pen was exactly that, and written her name in strokes like a storm coming off the water.

The storm has calmed since. The hand on the envelope was steadier, rounder, a teacher's hand. But I knew it the way you know a face across twenty years of distance: instantly, entirely, and with the whole body.

I haven't opened it yet. It sits on my table beside the ink pot, in the lamplight, and I sit beside it, an old woman being patient with the last mile of a very long road, because some letters you read standing up and some you read sitting down and some — the ones that change the shape of everything after — you have to sit with unopened for a while first, letting yourself become the person who is about to receive them.

Outside, the harbor breathes. Somewhere down the street, a door closes softly. The wall behind the chapel holds its six volumes like a heart holds blood: invisibly, constantly, against the day of the wound.

Tomorrow I'll open it. Tonight I'll write one more name.

The book, after all, is never finished. That was always the point.
