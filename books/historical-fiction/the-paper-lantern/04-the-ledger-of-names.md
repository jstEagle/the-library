## 4. The Ledger of Names

The drums had stopped by the time Doña Inés came to the shop, and somehow that was worse. All morning the square had throbbed with that iron heartbeat—the requisition read aloud in the crier's brass voice, the names of houses called out like a litany of the condemned—and now the silence after it pressed against the windows of the loft like deep water. Vesper could hear everything in that silence: the scratch of his own pen nib, the tick of Ambrosio's clock that no longer kept honest time, and beneath both, faint as a pulse through a wall, the boots of the patrol making its first circuit of the Calle de los Tintoreros.

He was working on the estuary of the Río Salado when she knocked—three knocks, unhurried, the knock of a woman who had spent forty years rapping on doors to collect tithes and births and deaths and who did not know how to announce herself any other way. He knew her knock. Everyone in Caldera knew it. It was the sound of the town's memory arriving at your threshold.

For one long moment he considered not answering.

It would have been easy. The lamp was low; the stair to the street was dark; a man bent over a drafting table with his head down is a kind of ghost, and ghosts are excused from everything. That had been his doctrine for twenty-six years, learned young and kept close like a stone warmed in the pocket: *be small, be useful to no one's story, be the margin and never the text.* Ambrosio had taught him the trade but Vesper had taught himself the rest—how to walk streets without being remembered, how to let conversations pass over him like weather, how to be so precisely unremarkable that even informers' eyes slid off him as off wet slate.

But it was Doña Inés, and Doña Inés had signed the paper that had made him, on record, an apprentice rather than a stray. So he set down the pen, capped the ink, and opened the door.

She stood in the stairwell holding a lantern, though the hour hardly demanded one, and he understood at once that the lantern was not for seeing by. It was for being seen by—a clerk's habit, light carried openly, nothing hidden. Her face above it was gray as old vellum, and her hands, which he had only ever seen dry and quick over ledgers, were trembling around the handle.

"Apprentice," she said. Not his name. She had never once used his name; he had sometimes wondered if she even knew it. "May I come up? I would rather not say it on the stairs."

The stairs in Caldera had ears. Everyone said it; everyone meant it. He stepped aside and let her pass, and the smell of her came with her—tallow smoke, dried lavender from the sachets she kept among the parish rolls, and under it something acrid, fear-sweat, the smell of a body that had been running its whole day ahead of itself.

* * *

She did not sit. She stood in the middle of the loft with her back very straight, the way she stood at the front of the church on days of general confession, and she looked at the drafting table, at the half-finished coastlines, at the great atlas lying open under its weights, and Vesper saw her take all of it in with a clerk's eye—the same eye that could find a misfiled baptismal record across a room.

"You're finishing it," she said. "Ambrosio's work."

"I'm copying fair sheets. For the family." There was no family. There had never been a family. It was the lie they had agreed on years ago, the master and the apprentice, two people with no one to inherit them inventing heirs between themselves.

Doña Inés looked at him for a long moment, and he had the uncomfortable sense of being read—not skimmed, but read closely, line by line, the way she read deeds. Then she set the lantern on the table, reached into the deep pocket of her cloak, and drew out a book.

He knew what it was before she held it up. Every soul in Caldera over the age of ten knew what it was, though almost none had seen it. The parish ledger—the second one, the true one, the one that lived behind the false spines in the sacristy cabinet. Bound in black calfskin gone soft at the corners, its clasps worn to dull brass, thick as a Bible and heavier than sin. In it, in Doña Inés's small upright hand, were written the names of every household in Caldera and the hills beyond that had given bread, beds, bandages, or silence to the rebels during the two hard years. Every farmwife who had left a basket on a fence post. Every mason who had looked away from a cellar door. Every name the crown had been hunting since the war ended, and had not found, because the ledger had not been found.

"Three hundred and eleven households," Doña Inés said quietly. "I counted them again last night. I have counted them every night this week, as if the number might change if I stopped watching it."

Vesper did not touch the book. He made himself stand still, hands loose at his sides, the posture of a man with nothing to hide, and hated how practiced the posture was.

"They posted the inventory order," she went on. "You heard it. Every house, every shop, every record. *Record*, apprentice. They used the word. The colonel's clerks will be going through the parish chest within the week—they'll start with the false ledger, the clean one, and then they'll pull the cabinet apart looking for more, because there is always more, that is what clerks believe, God forgive us, we believe it about each other." Her voice stayed level, but he could hear the effort of it, like a hand pressing down a lid. "When they find this book, they will not need a trial. A list is a verdict already written. Three hundred and eleven households, apprentice. Perhaps twelve hundred souls. They will hang them in batches, alphabetically, because that is how the army does things—alphabetically, so no one can say the order was personal."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft from the shutter seam, and for a moment the shadows of the hanging weights swung long across the floor like gallows rehearsing.

"What do you want of me?" Vesper asked, and heard how flat his own voice came out, how carefully empty.

"I want you to hide it."

"In the shop?" He laughed—a short, ugly sound he didn't recognize as coming from himself. "Doña Inés. They are requisitioning this building. It is on their list. I heard the crier read it myself, this morning, between the chandler's and the widow Ferrán's house. This loft will be turned inside out by men whose profession is turning lofts inside out. There is no hiding place in Caldera from men with a list of places to look."

"There are hiding places," she said. "Your master found them."

The words landed in the room and lay there, and neither of them moved toward them nor away.

"He hid survey plates," Vesper said at last, carefully. "Everyone knows Ambrosio hid things. Maps, sketches. Papers of no interest to soldiers."

"Papers of no interest," she repeated softly. "Yes. Well. This book is of no interest either, to anyone who has no wish to see three hundred families die. Hide it where he hid his papers. You were his apprentice. You swept his floors. You must know where a man keeps what he cannot afford to lose."

And there it was—the thing he had felt coming from the moment she stepped onto the stairs, arriving now exactly on schedule, patient as debt. She knew. Or she guessed, which amounted to the same thing in arithmetic like hers. She had looked at the orphan boy taken in by the mapmaker, watched the mapmaker die his convenient fall from the bridge stairs a year ago, watched the boy stay on alone in the loft keeping the shop alive with work too fine for a mere copyist—and she had done what clerks do. She had entered him in her private ledger of the town's quiet truths, under whatever heading she used for such things, and waited until the day came when the entry mattered.

"I sweep floors," Vesper said. "I grind ink. I don't know anything about my master's private affairs, and I'd thank you not to make me party to them. Party to yours, either." He nodded at the black book. "Take it to the priest. Take it to the convent. Bury it in the salt marsh, for God's sake—it's a book, it can go anywhere a body can't be buried."

"The priest is gone. Fled Tuesday, with the silver and the altar cloth, and God help him, I don't blame him. The convent is full of soldiers' laundry. And the marsh—" She stopped. Drew breath. When she spoke again her voice had dropped into something older and wearier, the voice she used for reading burial entries. "The marsh floods, apprentice. Paper drowns. And I am sixty-one years old, and my knees are gone, and they took my assistant this morning—that boy Tomás, sixteen, they conscripted him off the church steps while he was sweeping, just took him, and his mother screamed and they let her scream because screaming is permitted now, it's the only thing permitted." Her hands tightened on the ledger until the soft calfskin dimpled. "There is no one else. Do you understand? I have gone down my own list in my head, every name, every household, asking *who, who*—and every answer comes back the same. Afraid, or gone, or dead, or bought. Except you. You are the only person left in this town who owes nothing to anybody and is therefore free to do one small dangerous thing."

"That's not freedom," Vesper said. "That's just having nothing worth losing. It's not the same thing."

"No," she agreed. "Perhaps not. But it is adjacent."

* * *

He turned away from her then, turned to the window, because he could not look at the book and think at the same time. Below, the Calle de los Tintoreros lay blue in the dusk, and the patrol had passed, and somewhere a shutters were closing one after another down the street, the sounds of the town putting itself away like a shop closing early forever. On the corner opposite, a soldier stood with his pike grounded, motionless, a dark nail driven into the evening.

He thought about the compartment.

He could not help thinking about it—he had thought of little else for a year, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Beneath the drafting table, under the drawer of pens, where the wood was scarred and someone less attentive would swear there was nothing but joinery: a shallow space lined with oilcloth, wide enough for perhaps thirty copperplates, closed by a panel that lifted on a finger-hold worn nearly smooth by Ambrosio's thumb. Vesper had found it in the first raw week after the death, hunting for a receipt, and had knelt before it for the better part of an hour without touching it, feeling like a man kneeling before an open grave that turns out to hold treasure instead of bones. The plates slept there still. He had told himself he was leaving them because moving them was risky. He had told himself many things that year.

If the compartment could hold thirty plates, it could hold a book. He measured it in his mind without wanting to—the ledger was thicker than the plates, yes, but the plates were rigid and the book would flex, it would fit, it would fit—

And then he made himself do the other arithmetic, the cold kind, the kind Ambrosio had taught him without ever meaning to: *a mapmaker survives by being nobody's witness.* If the ledger lay beneath his floor and the soldiers found it, they would not ask who had hidden it. They would hang him beside the three hundred and eleven, and rightly, and the atlas—the atlas, the master's life's work, the only true map of Caldera in existence, the thing Vesper had given a year of lamplight and steady hands to finish—would burn in the square with everything else, unread, unmourned, its testimony lost before it was ever given. One book saved, four hundred lives' worth of truth destroyed. Or: one book refused, and the truth preserved, and three hundred and eleven households left to the alphabet.

That was the arithmetic. He knew it was the arithmetic. He also knew, standing there with his back to the old woman, that arithmetic was not why he was refusing. He was refusing because he was afraid, and because he had spent his whole life perfecting the art of being afraid in ways that looked like prudence, and because the ledger asked him to stop—to stop being the margin, to put his hand on the page where everyone could see it—and he was not sure he could survive becoming visible. Some men are killed by the rope. He had always suspected he would be killed by being *known.*

"Doña Inés," he said, still facing the window, "if I'm caught with that book, they take the shop. Everything in it. Everything my master left. Everything I've—" He stopped. Started again, meaner, because meanness was easier than honesty. "Why should I burn for your names? I never sheltered a rebel in my life. I'm not in your ledger. Those aren't my dead."

The silence behind him lasted long enough that he turned, finally, to see if she had gone.

She had not gone. She stood with the ledger held against her breast with both arms now, the way one holds a child, or a wounded bird, and she was looking at him with an expression he had never seen on a human face before and would spend years afterward trying to name. It was not anger. Anger would have been a mercy. It was the look of a woman confirming an entry she had hoped not to have to make.

"No," she said. "You're not in my ledger, apprentice. You're right. Three hundred and eleven households, and not one of them your name, because you gave no name to give. I've known you eleven years and I could not write you down if I tried—I don't know your father's trade, your mother's parish, whether you were baptized, whether you exist anywhere but in this room." She lifted the lantern from the table. The light climbed her face and showed him that her eyes were dry, terribly dry, the eyes of someone past the country where tears are issued. "My husband used to say a man is the sum of what he's willing to be counted for. He died owing the crown a tax dispute and the church a roof, and he was counted, and I bury him every day in the margins of my books. But you—" She shook her head slowly. "You are the emptiest page I have ever stood in front of. And I had forgotten, God forgive me, I had actually forgotten that emptiness could look like safety until it looks like nothing at all."

She moved to the stairhead. At the top step she paused, and did not turn around, and spoke to the dark below as much as to him.

"Ambrosio Villalobos owed nothing to anybody either. That's what everyone said of him, all those years. So careful, so quiet, so wise, so safe. And then one day he surveyed a field outside the walls that the crown says contains nothing, and wrote down what he found, and six weeks later he fell down some stairs." Her voice did not waver. "Owing nothing buys you nothing, apprentice. It only delays the bill. Ask your master. Oh—forgive me. You can't."

Her footsteps went down the stairs, unhurried even now, the knock of her shoes on the treads like a slow tally being kept. The street door opened. Closed. Through the window Vesper watched the small glow of her lantern move away along the Calle de los Tintoreros, past the soldier on the corner—who straightened, watched it pass, and let it pass—and onward toward the church square, steady, unhurried, carrying its three hundred and eleven names out into the occupied dark to wherever she would hide them alone.

He stood a long time after the light was gone.

Then he went back to the drafting table, sat, uncapped the ink, and took up the pen, and set it to the estuary of the Río Salado where he had left it, and found that his hand would not obey. The nib hovered. The coastline waited, patient, indifferent, needing nothing from him. In the lamplight the finished portion of the atlas gleamed faintly, all those roads and rivers and bridges drawn in Ambrosio's exacting hand, and Vesper understood suddenly that the master had drawn them the way a man whistles walking past a graveyard—not because the road matters, but because the road is what lets you keep moving.

Below, on the corner, the soldier shifted his weight. Somewhere across the town a dog began to bark and was silenced. And Vesper Villalobos, the emptiest page in Caldera, sat with a pen he could not lift and a shame he could not put down, listening to the silence where a black book had been, and understanding—for the first time, fully, the way one understands a thing only after the door has closed behind it—that he had just made the first entry in a ledger of his own.

It was two hours later, with the candle burned to a thumb's width and the estuary still unfinished, that he heard the boots on his stair—several pairs, heavy, ascending without hurry, without apology—and the knock that followed was not three knocks, and was not a question.
