## 7. The Atlas That Should Not Exist

The requisitioned mapmaker's shop smelled of linseed oil and cold iron, and beneath both—faintly, like a word spoken under the breath—of old paper. Elena Serrano stood in the doorway a long moment before entering, because she had learned that rooms told you things first if you let them finish speaking.

She had been given the shop at dusk, along with three other buildings on the Lantern Row: the chandler's, the notary's, and this one. The inventory clerk, a boy with ink-stained cuffs and the nervous diligence of someone who had never been hit, handed her the ledger of contents and waited for her to sign.

"Surveyor's establishment," he said. "Deceased proprietor. The apprentice remains in residence below—he sleeps in the back room, sir. Ma'am." He corrected himself with a small flinch. He had seen her face when he said *sir*.

"I'm no one's ma'am," Elena said, and took the ledger.

It was thin. That was the first wrongness. A working cartographer's shop should have filled pages: instruments, papers, copper plates, inks, varnishes, the carcasses of unfinished commissions. This inventory listed eleven items, as though Ambrosio Ferrán—the name was already entered in the crown's lists, struck through with the neat diagonal that meant *deceased, seditious*—had spent his last year making nothing at all.

Men who make nothing are either finished or hiding something. She signed, dismissed the boy, and lit the lamp on the drafting table herself.

* * *

Outside, Caldera was settling into its first night under occupation. She could hear it through the shutters: the changed rhythm of footsteps in the street, boots where there had been slippers; a dog barking itself hoarse at the sentry post by the well; somewhere a woman singing to a child, low and steady, the song chosen deliberately because it was an old one, an innocent one, a song that predated armies. Elena had grown up in a town like this one—not this town, but shaped the same way, walled and riverbound and proud of things it did not say aloud—and she knew what the second night would sound like, and the tenth, and the hundredth. The music went first. Then the laughter. Then the names.

Her orders lay in her breast pocket, folded twice. *Survey the town and approaches. Render all existing surveys obsolete. Certify destruction of superseded materials.* The language was beautiful; she had always admired how the crown wrote, how it could make burning sound like housekeeping. *Superseded.* As though a map were a fashion.

She had come to Caldera believing exactly what she'd been trained to believe: that maps were instruments, that accuracy was a virtue, that whoever held the best survey held the territory in some way deeper than garrisons could manage. Her father had been a mason. He had taught her that a wall built on a lie about the ground would fall, and that the man who measured the ground honestly was worth more than ten men who argued about whose king God preferred. She had carried that into the corps of royal surveyors like a coal cupped in both hands, and for nine years it had kept her warm, and she had never once let herself look down at her palms to see whether it had begun to burn them instead.

Now she turned up the lamp and looked at the drafting table.

* * *

It was a magnificent piece of furniture, older than the master who had died at it—oak gone the color of dark honey, its edges worn round by decades of forearms leaning. The surface was scarred with compass points, hundreds of them, a constellation of tiny punctures. Elena ran her fingers across them and felt, absurdly, like a woman reading braille written by a stranger's whole life.

The atlas was not on the table. It was under it—or rather, the boy's copy-work was, a half-finished sheet of coastline in a young hand, careful but not yet certain, weighted down with a brass protractor. But the atlas itself stood on the shelf behind the desk, bound in grey boards with no title, no gilt, nothing on the spine at all. It sat among blank folios the way a hawk sits among pigeons: still, unremarkable, and entirely wrong.

Elena took it down.

The first page stopped her breath for a moment, and she was not a woman who lost her breath easily. It was not a title page. It was a key—a legend so complete, so disciplined, that it read like a manifesto written in symbols. Depth soundings in fathoms. Road surfaces distinguished by weight-bearing capacity. Well water marked sweet or bitter or fouled. Roofing materials. Kilns. Granaries, with capacities estimated. And then, in a smaller, tighter hand than the rest—added later, she judged, added over years—a symbol she did not recognize: a small circle with a single line drawn through it, repeated here and there across the early sheets like a rash.

She turned the page and found the town of Caldera itself, surveyed at a scale finer than anything the crown's own corps produced. Finer than anything she had ever seen outside the capital's sealed collection, and better than most of that. Every street. Every courtyard. Every well with its depth noted in a hand so small and even it might have been printed. The bridge over the Aldera with its pier-stones counted. The tanneries downstream with their effluent channels mapped, because whoever made this had understood that water is a political fact.

She checked it against her own preliminary survey, done from the ridge two days ago with the army's best theodolite. Her work was good. She knew it was good; she had staked her career on being good. His was better. Not by much. By exactly the amount that separated competence from devotion.

*Who were you?* she thought, to the dead man whose name was struck through in the crown's lists. *What were you for?*

* * *

She worked through the atlas sheet by sheet, and the shop grew colder around her without her noticing, and the candle burned down past its first knuckle, and still she turned pages.

The wonder came first. She wanted to be honest with herself about that, later, when honesty became expensive: it began as wonder, pure and professional and almost joyful. Here was the river road rendered with every culvert marked. Here were the salt meadows east of the walls with their drainage ditches dated—*cut 4th year of the old duke, deepened after the flood*—in marginal notes in two hands, the second younger, the apprentice's, learning his letters in the margins of his master's genius. Here was the mill race, the fish weirs, the ford that appeared only in dry summers, marked with a note: *passable mid-July to mid-September in ordinary years; do not trust after rain in the hills.*

Do not trust after rain in the hills. She read that note three times. It was the kind of knowledge that could not be bought or ordered. It could only be earned, season upon season, by a man who walked his own town until the town lived in him like a heartbeat. No conquering army could produce this. Armies produced sketches, estimates, the cartography of the first glance. This was the cartography of the fiftieth year.

And then, near the middle of the volume, the wonder curdled.

There was a sheet of the land outside the north wall—the common pasture, the alder stands, the bend of the river where it went shallow and slow. It was surveyed with the same loving precision as everything else. But the margin notes here were different. Denser. In the master's tight hand, in a ciphered shorthand she could not yet read, running along the edges like a fence. And scattered across the open ground, each one circled with that same strange symbol from the legend—one line through a circle—were marks. Dozens of them. Small crosses, precisely placed, clustered in a long shallow arc as though the ground itself had been annotated with a sentence.

She counted them. Then she counted again, because the first number was impossible.

Two hundred and six.

She knew what the crown's account of the pacification said, because she had been required to read it before deployment. She had read it in the officers' mess in Vellamora with the colonel's adjutant watching her read it. The account said that following the suppression of the rebellion, the bodies of the condemned had been *disposed of according to regulation*, that casualties among the civilian population had been *regrettable but limited*, and that reports of mass burials outside the northern wall were *rebel fabrications circulated to inflame provincial sentiment*. The official history, now being drafted in the capital by a historian whose name she'd heard spoken with a certain careful irony—Ocaña, Señor Ocaña, the pen that outlived the sword—would set all of this in permanent type within the year.

Two hundred and six crosses, in an arc four hundred paces long, on ground the crown insisted held nothing at all.

Elena set the atlas down on the drafting table very carefully, the way one sets down something that has become, in the space of a page, dangerous.

* * *

The lamp guttered. She trimmed the wick with fingers that were not quite steady, and hated them for it.

Think, she told herself. Think like a surveyor. What does the evidence say?

The evidence said: this map was made over years, by a methodical man, and the marks were integrated into the survey's own system—they had their entry in the legend, their defined symbol. They were not an accusation scrawled in rage. They were data. Someone had walked that ground, or measured it, or been given measurements, and had recorded what was there with the same discipline he gave to wells and culverts. Which meant the marks were either true, or they were the most elaborate forgery ever committed by a provincial mapmaker against his own crown.

And if they were true—

If they were true, then the order in her breast pocket, the beautiful order that made burning sound like housekeeping, was not housekeeping. It was evidence destruction. It was the crown paying her, feeding her, promoting her, to stand in this cold shop and certify that the ground did not say what the ground said.

Her father's voice, unhelpfully, arrived: *A wall built on a lie about the ground will fall.* Yes, Papa. And what of a country built that way? What of a history?

She thought about Colonel Vade's face when he had briefed the surveyors in Vellamora. Not cruel, exactly. Worse: administrative. A man who would sign whatever was put in front of him because signing was his function, the way a stamp's function is to press down. She thought about the promotion that waited for whoever certified the new survey cleanly and quickly. She thought about her mother's grave in Vellamora, which she visited once a year, which existed, which had a stone, which had her mother's name cut into it in letters deep enough to survive weather—because someone, once, had bothered to count her mother as a person who had existed.

Two hundred and six people outside the north wall had no stones. Had, officially, never been. And the only object in the world that insisted otherwise was lying open under her lamp in a requisitioned shop, waiting for her signature to send it to the fire.

* * *

She heard the stair creak and closed the atlas too fast, her heart slamming once against her ribs like a door in wind.

The apprentice stood at the top of the stairs with a candle stub in his hand, and she saw him take in the scene in a single flick of his eyes: the open atlas, her hand still on it, the lamp, her uniform, her face. He was younger than she'd expected. Thin, dark-eyed, with the particular stillness of people who have learned that stillness is safety. He looked at her the way mice look at owls—not frozen from stupidity, but frozen from arithmetic.

"You're the apprentice," she said. "Villalobos."

"Yes, Captain." His voice was flat and careful. "I wasn't told the shop was assigned."

"It's assigned now. I'm conducting the survey verification." She heard how the words arranged themselves into a wall between them, smooth and official, and despised how easily they came. Nine years of practice. "You'll continue to sleep below. You'll touch nothing on this floor. If any item is missing or moved, you'll be answerable for it."

He nodded. His eyes went, just once, just briefly, to the atlas under her hand—and in that glance she saw something that frightened her more than the crosses had. Not fear for himself. Fear for the book. A specific, personal, grieving fear, the look of a man watching a stranger hold a dying relative.

He knows what's in it, she thought. He knows, and he's terrified, and he will say nothing, because saying something is how people in this town stop existing.

"Good night, Captain," he said, and went down the stairs, and his candlelight shrank away along the passage below until the shop was hers and the dark's again.

* * *

She sat for a long time with the atlas open to the northern fields.

The sensible thing—the thing that would be in her report, the thing Vade would expect, the thing that would carry her upward through the clean bright corridors of the capital—was to log the discovery tonight. *Recovered: one atlas, author deceased, contents include unsubstantiated rebel claims regarding burial sites. Recommend destruction per standing order.* By week's end it would be ash, and Ocaña's history would stand uncontradicted, and the ground outside the north wall would hold nothing at all forever, because paper was the last place those people lived and paper could be burned.

Two hundred and six.

She took out her field notebook, meaning to write the log entry, and found herself instead making a sketch: the arc of crosses, reduced, exact, copied in her own hand onto her own paper without quite deciding to do it. When she caught herself doing it she sat very still, listening to the occupied town breathe around her, and understood that she had already crossed some line whose location she had never been shown. A surveyor's sin: crossing a boundary you hadn't bothered to map.

It was not yet treason. She examined the thought honestly, turning it like a specimen. It was not yet treason. It was verification. An officer verifying a document before certifying its destruction—that was protocol, that was diligence, that was defensible in any court in the kingdom. She would verify. Thoroughly. Slower than efficiency required. There were procedures; she would follow them all; she would measure everything twice.

And if the verification confirmed what the atlas claimed—if the ground outside the north wall said what the ground said—then she would stand at a different kind of border, and she would have to choose which side of it to plant her flag.

Elena closed the atlas, locked it in the field chest, and put the key in her breast pocket beside the orders, where the two documents lay folded together like strangers forced to share a bench.

Below her, faint through the floorboards, she could hear the apprentice moving. Not sleeping. Walking a small circuit, over and over, the pacing of a man guarding something or counting something or praying. She listened until the candle failed, and in the dark she made herself a promise that felt, even then, like the beginning of a fall:

*I will know the truth of this ground before anyone burns it.*

Outside the north wall, under frost and alder roots, two hundred and six people waited to find out whether she meant it.
