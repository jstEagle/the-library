## 1. The Weight of Envelopes

There is a particular weight to a sealed envelope that an open one does not have. I don't mean the paper and the gum — though gum has its own small gravity, a tackiness that holds a secret the way a flypaper holds a fly. I mean the weight of the not-knowing. A sealed letter is a closed room. And I have spent thirty-one years standing outside closed rooms, listening at the wood.

By day I drive for Mrs. Edith Marlowe, of Portland Place, who is either a menace to the British Empire or a saint, depending on which newspaper you've had with your breakfast. By night I read her post.

Let me explain. Not excuse — explain. Excuses are for people who believe they'll be caught.

* * *

The Daimler is a 1914 model, black as a magistrate's mood, and I have kept it running through two years of war, petrol rationing, and one incident with a cart horse named Admiral that I do not care to revisit. I collect Mrs. Marlowe at half past eight each morning from the house on Portland Place, and I deliver her wherever the cause requires: Caxton Hall, a printing works in Clerkenwell, once — memorably — a field in Hertfordshire where she addressed four hundred women in a wind that tried to steal her notes and very nearly succeeded. She spoke without them. She knows most of what she believes by heart.

She is sixty-one years old, built like a churchwarden, and she has been fed through a tube twice for the sake of the vote and would do it again before luncheon. The first time I saw her she looked me up and down and said, "You're Yarrow. Good. I'm told you don't talk much. Excellent quality in a driver. Catastrophic in a friend, but I've given up on friends."

I said, "I'm told you don't pay much."

She laughed like a door coming off its hinges and hired me on the spot. That was March of 1916. I have been there since, and in all that time she has never once asked me a personal question, which is either respect or indifference, and I have never decided which I prefer.

The letters come to the Portland Place house in the morning post — two trays of them, some days three — and a portion go out again in the afternoon, sealed, addressed, stamped, riding in a leather satchel on the seat beside me while I drive them to post offices and private houses and, twice a week, a stationer's shop on Chancery Lane that keeps a private wire. I am, officially, a courier. Unofficially, I am a woman with a kettle, a saucer, and a great deal of patience.

* * *

Here is the method, and I confess it with the flat pride of a craftsman: you do not tear an envelope. Tearing is for amateurs and burglars. You steam.

A kettle brought to a whisper — not a boil, a whisper — and the envelope held in the steam at an angle, mouth down, for a count of forty. The gum softens like a promise being reconsidered. Then a thin knife — I use a bone folder I bought for sixpence at a stationer's on Charing Cross Road, before the war made such purchases suspicious — and the flap lifts without a sound. You read. You dry the flap under the lamp, press it shut with the saucer, and in the morning the letter goes on its way carrying its secret intact, and no one — not the sender, not the recipient, not the censor at the post office with his blue pencil — knows that a third pair of eyes has been there.

I do this in my lodgings on Store Street, a room on the third floor with a gas ring, a bed, a washstand, and a window that looks out on a lightwell and, if I lean far enough, a slice of sky the size of a tea towel. The landlady believes I am a correspondent. In a sense I am. I have corresponded with several hundred strangers, none of whom know my name.

I tell myself it is harmless. I have told myself this so many times that the words have worn smooth, like a coin passed hand to hand until the king's face is gone. Harmless. Who is harmed by a seamstress in Peckham learning that her sweetheart in the Rifle Brigade has lice, and a cough, and a girl in Rouen he swears means nothing? Not the seamstress. Not me. I am nobody in the transaction. I am the draught under the door.

But I will be honest, since no one is listening: it is not the information I want. It is the touching.

* * *

You must understand what my life was before the letters. I was a chauffeur before that — before that a parlourmaid, before that a girl in a house in Camden with a father who repaired umbrellas and a mother who died of the influenza when I was nine, leaving behind a smell of camphor and a hat I was not allowed to touch. My father died in 1911 of his lungs, which had been eating coal dust since before I was born, and after the funeral I sat in his shop among all those broken umbrellas and understood that I was now entirely alone, in the way that a chair is alone in an empty room. Not sad, exactly. Unclaimed.

The war made unclaimed women useful. Every man who could drive had been driven into a uniform, and so I went from being invisible to being indispensable, which is the closest a woman of my class comes to a promotion. I can strip an engine, mend a tyre, and out-stubborn a petrol queue. I can also sit for six hours outside a hall in the rain, waiting, and think about nothing, which is the true skill of my profession. Thinking about nothing is a kind of hunger. You fill the nothing with whatever is nearest.

For me, the nearest thing was the post.

It started the second week, I think. A letter left on the hall table at Portland Place, and Mrs. Marlowe called away to a telephone, and I stood there with my cap in my hands like a fool, and the envelope was there, and it was addressed in a hand so shaky it looked like handwriting learning to be handwriting again, and the return address was a convalescent home in Surrey. And I thought: someone wrote this with fingers that don't work properly, and someone will read it, and I will never know either of them, and I will drive this letter across London carrying their whole lives in my satchel like a delivery of boots.

I steamed it open on the hall table with a kettle Mrs. Marlowe's cook had just boiled. I read it in four minutes. It was a mother writing to Mrs. Marlowe to thank her for a parcel — socks, a tin of toffee, a copy of *The Vote* smuggled in past the hospital matron — and to say that her boy had lost his left arm at Loos but could still hold a cigarette, and wasn't that something.

I sealed it back up. I drove it to the post. And that night in my room on Store Street I lay awake and felt — I want to say ashamed, but the word doesn't fit, it's a coat cut for someone else. What I felt was *included*. For four minutes I had been in a family. I had been the kind of person to whom things are written.

After that, the kettle was never far.

* * *

I have rules. A person in my line of — well, I won't say work; habit, perhaps — needs rules, the way a tightrope walker needs a wire.

First: I never keep anything. No letters, no copies, no souvenirs. What goes through my hands stays in my memory and nowhere else, and my memory is a well-kept room with the furniture polished and the curtains drawn.

Second: I never act on what I read. I have known, since last autumn, that a grocer in Islington is writing to two women at once, telling each she is the only one. I have said nothing, driven nothing, done nothing. It is not my war. It is not my marriage.

Third: I read only what comes through the Marlowe post. I am a reader of one household, not a spy of the world. There is a distinction, and I cling to it the way a drowning man clings to a barrel — not because it will hold, but because letting go is unthinkable.

The letters are mostly what you'd expect. Mothers, wives, sweethearts, sisters. Requests for Mrs. Marlowe's help: a husband imprisoned for throwing a brick through a window, a daughter on hunger strike, a boy at the front who needs a blanket and a Bible and, in one unforgettable case, a false tooth, the original having been left in a trench near Ypres. Mrs. Marlowe answers them all, in a hand like a row of bayonets, and I carry the answers, and the whole apparatus of grief and hope passes through the Daimler's leather seat twice a day.

Some of the writers are famous — I have steamed open letters from names you'd know from the front pages, women who have been in prison and will be again — but fame is the least interesting thing in the post. It is the small hands that stay with me. The woman who writes in pencil because ink costs. The boy — he cannot be more than nineteen — who writes to Mrs. Marlowe to ask whether it is true that a man who refuses to fight can still be a Christian, and signs it *yours obediently* as though she were a colonel.

I read him, that night, three times. Then I sealed him up and sent him on, and I have wondered about him ever since, the way you wonder about a face in a train window. Did she answer? What did she say? I delivered her reply to the address in Walthamstow, a narrow house with a lace curtain, and I sat in the Daimler outside it for two minutes longer than I needed to, watching a woman draw the curtain back and look out at the street as though the letter might have brought the answer with it, visible from the pavement.

It hadn't. But it might have. That is what the letters are: the might-have. The closed room I am briefly inside of, before I am put out again and the door shuts and I am on the street, alone, with the engine ticking and the gas lamps coming on.

* * *

Tonight is Thursday, and the post was heavy.

I sit at my table with the kettle whispering and the lamp turned low — you learn to work in low light; it is kinder to the paper and to the conscience — and I open them one by one, the bone folder moving in its small precise surgery. Outside, Store Street is quiet except for a drunk singing something patriotic two streets over, badly, and the rattle of a cart, and the ordinary sounds of a city holding its breath through its third year of war.

The first letter is from a woman in Bristol whose son has been reported missing and whose letters to the War Office have come back unanswered, and she writes to Mrs. Marlowe because Mrs. Marlowe is a person who answers. She writes nine pages. In the tenth, the writing stops mid-word — *I keep thinking if I just keep writ* — and starts again on a fresh page as though nothing happened. I read it twice. I press it flat under the saucer and sit for a moment with my hands in my lap.

The second is a bill. Even Mrs. Marlowe gets bills. I don't steam those. A woman has to have standards.

The third is addressed in a hand I don't know, from an address I don't know, in a part of Kent I have no reason to think about. And it is heavier than it should be — not much, a gram or two, the difference a pressed flower makes, or a photograph, or a folded slip of paper that was never meant for the post.

I hold it up to the lamp. The envelope is good stock, better than the hand that addressed it. The flap has been sealed twice — you can see the ghost of the first gum line under the second, a letter sealed, opened, and sealed again before it ever reached me.

Someone else has read this letter. Someone else has been the draught under the door.

I set it down. I pick it up again. The kettle whispers. The drunk two streets over has reached the chorus and shows no sign of stopping.

I have been reading other people's mail for a year and a half, and I have never once found a letter that was already opened. In my experience, secrets that travel are sealed by people who trust the seal — or by people who want you to trust it.

I put the bone folder to the flap, and the flap lifts, and inside there is a letter in a careful, educated hand, and beneath the lines of it, faint as breath on glass, a row of small pencil marks that mean nothing at all — unless they mean everything — running between the lines like stitching in a seam.

I read the letter. Then I read the stitching.

And somewhere below me, in the street, a cart stops, and a door closes, and I realize my hands have gone quite still, and that the harmless thing I have been telling myself for eighteen months has just, very quietly, stopped being true.
