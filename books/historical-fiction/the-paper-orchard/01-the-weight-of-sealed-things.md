## 1. The Weight of Sealed Things

The morning the Republic of Vessany stopped functioning, Odile Nystrom woke before the bells and lay listening to them fail.

There should have been nine rings from the Chancery tower, the signal for a full session of the Council. He counted four, then silence — the bell-rope frayed, or the ringer gone, or someone deciding that four was enough for a country that had already been divided on paper. He dressed in the dark out of habit rather than necessity. The gas lamps along the Rue de la Chancellerie had been dark for eleven days; the victors' engineers were said to be cataloguing the mains before they restored service to their own quarters first. A city learned its new rank by who got light.

His satchel weighed almost nothing. That was the cruelty of it. Twenty years of carrying the republic's voice — treaties, mobilizations, the small furious notes between ministers at midnight — and on the last morning it all fit in a leather bag he could have lost in a market crowd without anyone mourning it. Three sealed packets. One for the Naval Ministry, one for the Legation of Halvard, and one addressed in the Chancellor's own cramped hand to an office that Odile suspected no longer existed: the Bureau of Continuance, which had been created in October to plan for a future no one believed in.

He checked each seal against the wax impressions in his memory book, then again against the register stubs, though the register itself had been packed into crates two weeks ago and shipped toward the mountains with the rest of the archives. What remained in the capital was what could not be carried or could not be saved. Odile had spent his career measuring documents by weight and consequence. Now he measured his world the same way, and found how little of it there was.

* * *

The streets wore snow like a bandage over a wound nobody wanted to look at directly.

He walked the route he had walked a thousand times, past the Ministry of Ways, where the bronze doors stood chained and a commission's chalk marks already crossed the lintel — white crosses, the victors' inventory notation, as if the building were a crate of goods awaiting valuation. Past the Printers' Row shuttered behind sandbags. Past the Café Aurel, open only because its owner had decided that closing would be a kind of surrender, serving chicory to three old men who still argued about fleet dispositions as if the fleet existed.

At the Naval Ministry, a single clerk received him in a corridor because the offices had no heat. The clerk broke the seal, read, went gray, read again, and said only, "Thank you, courier." Not his name. For twenty years they had called him by name. *Courier* now, the way you'd address a function of a machine whose operator had left.

The Halvard Legation was worse, because it was better. The chargé d'affaires took the packet with both hands, thanked him warmly, offered him coffee that was mostly roasted barley, and spoke of the republic in the present tense with such practiced steadiness that Odile understood the man had already written his final dispatch home. Somewhere in Halvard's files, Vessany was already past tense. The coffee tasted of nothing. Everything did, lately.

The third packet he carried to the address on the envelope and found a boarded window and a door with the paint peeling in long curls. He stood in the street holding the Chancellor's last instruction to a bureau that had been evacuated, or dissolved, or never properly founded, and after a while he tucked it inside his coat against his chest, because throwing it away felt like a thing he would have to answer for someday, to someone, even if only to himself.

By afternoon the snow had turned to a fine dry grit, the kind that squeaked underfoot. On the Pont des Archives he passed a column of soldiers moving the other way — not the republic's grey-green but the victors' slate blue, their boots wrapped in cloth so they made no sound. A silent army crossing a silent bridge. One of them glanced at him, at the satchel, and looked away with the particular indifference of a man who has been told that everything here is already his.

Odile walked home the long way, past the records annex, and noticed the water standing black in its lower windows. Flooded since the November bombardment, they said. Nobody pumped it out. Who pumps out the cellar of a house being sold?

* * *

His rooms above the chandler's shop on the Rue du Four were cold but honest about it. He lit the stove, ate bread and oil, and set the unclaimed packet on the table where he could see it, the Chancellor's wax seal catching the lamplight like a small red wound.

He was copying the day's deliveries into his personal ledger — a courier's habit, the record of a record-keeper, though he no longer knew who might ever read it — when he heard the step on the stairs.

Not a neighbor's step. Neighbors announced themselves with weight and coughing. This was a step that tried to be nothing: heel-first, quick, the tread of someone who had learned to move through reading rooms and among sleeping guards. It stopped outside his door long enough for him to set down his pen. Then three soft knocks, and the sound of retreat already beginning.

He opened the door onto the landing's darkness and nearly stumbled over the bundle.

A child. Perhaps three years old, perhaps four, asleep or pretending to sleep, wrapped in a man's overcoat with the sleeves knotted. Beside him, a note weighted with a button.

And at the far end of the landing, at the top of the stairs, a woman turning away — tall, coat collar up, a scarf across the lower half of her face, but the lamplight from his room caught her eyes and the line of her jaw and the way she held her head slightly tilted, listening, the way she had held it in the annex reading room two winters ago when she showed him—

"Wait," he said, and his voice came out wrong, too loud for the stairwell.

She froze. Half a second. In that half-second she looked back at him, and he saw her decide something — not whether to run, but whether to speak.

"Forget my face," she said.

He knew the voice. Of course he knew the voice. Low, unhurried, with the faint northern vowel that softened every hard consonant — the archivist from the Bureau of Records, the one who had pulled the treaty folios for him in the winter of the armistice negotiations, who had worn cotton gloves and turned pages as if they were sleeping things, who had once — he had thought of it a hundred times since, trying to make it mean less than it seemed to mean — paused over a particular folder and looked at him for a moment too long before sliding it back into the drawer.

"You're—" he began.

"Forget my face," she said again, and this time it was not a request. It sounded like a mercy she was performing for both of them. Then she was down the stairs and gone, and the street door closed below with a click so quiet it might have been the building settling.

The child opened his eyes. Dark, calm, unafraid eyes that looked at Odile with the frank assessment of someone checking whether a thing was true. Then the boy reached up one mitten and touched Odile's hand where it gripped the doorframe, and kept it there.

Odile crouched, joints cracking in the cold, and picked up the note. The button on top was military issue, brass, the republic's anchor-and-star stamped shallowly — a child's toy, or a keepsake, or a message. The note beneath was written in a cipher he recognized, because every courier learned it in his first year and was taught to forget it in his second: the old routing cipher, the one used for documents too sensitive for the registry.

Nine characters. He read them twice, and the cold that came over him had nothing to do with the season.

They were coordinates. An address. The flooded records annex — sub-level, west stack.

And below the coordinates, a single word in plain script, small and deliberate, as if the writer had pressed hard to keep her hand from shaking:

*Remember.*

The boy watched him read. Outside, somewhere down the Rue du Four, boots were passing — many boots, unwrapped, loud, the victors walking their new city at night. Odile stood in his doorway with a stranger's child in one arm and the Chancellor's undeliverable last orders in his coat, and understood that the ordinary world, the world of routes and registers and seals, had cracked open sometime in the last minute, and that everything he chose to do before morning would be done on the other side of the crack.

The boy's hand was still resting on his.

"All right," Odile heard himself say, to the child, to the note, to the face he was supposed to forget and already couldn't. "All right."

He brought him inside, and barred the door, and did not sleep.
