## 1. The Weight of Other People's Letters

The rain came in off the Atlantic the way it always did in October—sideways, patient, as though it had all the time in the world and intended to use it. Jude Fairweather drove the little Humber through the lanes above Portmellon with her collar up and her hat pinned down, one hand on the wheel and the other resting on the leather satchel that held the morning's post, and she thought, not for the first time, that a letter was a strange kind of cargo. It weighed almost nothing. A sheet of paper, an envelope, a seal or a stamp. And yet she could feel the weight of them against her hip like ballast, the way a sailor feels the sea's intention before the storm has a name.

"Mind the bend," Edith Marlowe said from beside her, though there was no need. Jude had driven this road two hundred times. She knew where the hawthorn leaned too far after rain, knew which culvert flooded first, knew the exact spot where the lane narrowed between granite walls and a cart coming the other way meant reversing forty yards in the wet. She said nothing. She simply took the bend a half-mile slower than instinct asked, because that was what Edith wanted, and what Edith wanted was, most days, indistinguishable from what the world required.

"I've been thinking about the Penzance meeting," Edith went on, as though the pause had been hers alone. "Mrs. Tregowan will want to speak again. She speaks for twenty minutes when five would do."

"She speaks well," Jude offered.

"She speaks *long*. There is a difference." Edith adjusted her gloves—kid leather, worn at the knuckles, mended twice by her own hand because she would not spend on herself what could be spent on the cause. "You'll take notes. You always take better notes than I do; you have a criminal's memory for other people's words."

Jude smiled at the windscreen, at the grey wall of rain beyond it. *A criminal's memory.* If Edith only knew how literally she ought to take the compliment.

They crested the hill above the village and the harbor opened below them: a stone crescent cradling a dozen fishing boats, the masts swaying like reeds, the water the color of hammered pewter. Gulls hung motionless in the wind, screaming their grievances at nothing. Beyond the harbor arm the Channel was running white, and somewhere out past the horizon—Jude did not let herself think precisely where—the ships were moving. Troopships. Merchantmen. The war was three months old and already it had gotten into everything, into the sermons and the shop windows and the way men looked at strangers on the platform at St Austell. Into the post, especially. The post had changed before anything else did.

She had noticed it first in August. Letters that used to travel with a frank confidence now arrived bearing questions. *Return address? Nationality of sender?* The Post Office had grown a new vocabulary overnight, and Jude, who read more letters than any woman in Cornwall had a right to, felt the change in her hands before she understood it in her head.

The satchel held fourteen pieces this morning. She had sorted them at the cottage before they left, standing in the scullery with her coat still dripping, dividing the day's correspondence into the three piles that governed their lives: what Edith must see immediately, what Edith should see eventually, and what Edith need never see at all. The third pile was the smallest and the heaviest. It contained, this morning, a note from a man in Truro who signed himself *A Well-Wisher* and whose well-wishing ran to three paragraphs of anatomical threat; a bill from the printer that Edith would fret over for a week; and a letter from Edith's sister-in-law in Falmouth that was less a letter than a wound dressed in violet ink, alluding to things Jude had no right to know and therefore knew perfectly well.

She read everything. That was the arrangement, though neither of them had ever spoken it aloud. Edith's eyes tired now—she was fifty-six and refused, magnificently, to admit it—and so Jude read aloud in the evenings: petitions, minutes, resolutions, the furious and the pleading and the occasionally unhinged. She read the movement's whole nervous system through her own voice, and somewhere along six years of service she had stopped being a reader and become something else. A filter. A vault. The woman who knew everything and appeared to know nothing.

Invisibility, Jude had learned, was not the absence of being seen. It was the discipline of being seen *wrong*—seen as plain, as useful, as furniture with good posture. She had practiced it so long it had worn grooves in her, the way water wears limestone. Stand slightly behind. Speak slightly softer. Let your competence look like diligence rather than intelligence, your silence look like deference rather than judgment. Men never suspected a woman who handed them exactly the pen they needed at the exact moment they reached for it.

And women—women of Edith's generation, women forged in the suffrage years, women who trusted nothing and no one—those women suspected everyone except the one who made their lives run smoothly. That was the great secret of service: devotion was the best disguise ever tailored.

* * *

They took tea at the Tregowan house in Mevagissey, because Mrs. Tregowan had written twice and Edith believed in paying debts of attention. While the two women talked committees in the parlor, Jude sat in the kitchen with the housekeeper, a round, shrewd woman named Mrs. Pascoe who fed her seed cake and gossip in equal measure.

"They're saying at the harbor office," Mrs. Pascoe said, lowering her voice over the teapot as if the pot itself might repeat her, "that there's a new man come down from London. Census work, they call it, but it's more than census. Asking after origins. Who's got German clerks, who's got German cooks, whose grandmother came over in 'sixty." She sniffed. "Half this coast couldn't pass such an examination, and that includes the vicar."

"Is that so," Jude said, in the tone of a woman filing the information under *other people's concerns.*

"That's so." Mrs. Pascoe gave her a long look, the look of a woman who had fed half the parish's secrets through her kitchen and kept score of every one. "Name of Pell, they say. Mr. Pell. Comes with a little notebook and a manner like a bill collector." She refilled Jude's cup unasked. "You want more cake."

It was not a question either. Jude took more cake.

Later, driving back with the light failing early behind the rain, Edith was quiet in the way that meant her mind was working hard. Jude let the engine do the talking. The wipers—hand-cranked, her job at junctions—squeaked their small protest, and the hedgerows streamed, and somewhere a church bell counted four o'clock into the wet air.

"The Trewartha boy," Edith said at last. "The one who wanted to enlist. His mother wrote to me."

"I know. I read it."

"He's seventeen."

"He says eighteen."

"He says a great many things. He wants to be a hero before he's old enough to know what heroes cost." Edith turned her face to the window, watching the water bead and race across the glass. "I told his mother I'd speak to him. I don't know what I'll say. Six years I've spent teaching girls that their lives belong to themselves, and now the country is telling boys the same thing in a voice they can't refuse, and I find I don't—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I find the words have moved house without leaving a forwarding address."

Jude said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't dangerous. *Forwarding address.* The phrase snagged in her like a fishhook, though she could not have said why—not yet, not consciously. Somewhere in the satchel at her feet, in the third pile, in the stratum of paper marked *never*, there was a letter she had not yet opened. It had come on Tuesday, forwarded twice, its envelope stamped and restamped with the wandering patience of something that had crossed borders looking for its owner. The handwriting on it was foreign. Not German—she'd have known German instantly, the way one knows a mother tongue even in a stranger's mouth—but foreign nonetheless, angular and careful, addressed to the harbor master at Fowey with a request for redirection in a formal, schoolroom English that sat oddly against the script beneath it.

She had set it aside. She had told herself she would deal with it Thursday, when the cottage was empty, when she could open it without the superstitious feeling that the walls were listening. Three months into a war, a letter in a foreign hand was no longer merely a letter. It was a question being asked of you, and the question was *which side are you on*, and Jude had spent twenty-nine years making sure no one ever thought to ask her anything at all.

* * *

That evening the rain eased to a fine drifting mist, and the cottage filled with lamplight and the smell of coal smoke and Edith's tobacco—she smoked a pipe in the evenings, an affectation she defended with the ferocity of a woman defending a besieged city. Jude sat in her usual chair by the window with the day's remaining post on her knees and read aloud: a resolution from the Bath branch, a subscription reminder, a furious letter from a Mrs. Cobbledick of Bodmin regarding the moral character of the WSPU, which Jude edited gently in the reading, softening the adjectives the way one rounds off stones.

"And the rest?" Edith asked, when the pile was done. Her eyes were closed, her pipe drawing slow. She looked, in the lamplight, like a general at rest—which is to say, like a blade in a scabbard pretending to be a walking stick.

"A printer's bill. A circular from the federation. Nothing requiring your blood pressure."

"You're a tyrant of triage, Jude Fairweather."

"Someone has to stand between you and Mrs. Cobbledick."

Edith laughed—a real laugh, low and surprised out of her—and for a moment the room was warm in a way that had nothing to do with the fire. Jude looked at her employer and felt the familiar ache, the one that lived just under the competence, under the efficiency, under the smooth machinery of her usefulness. Longing, but not the kind the novels wrote about. Or perhaps exactly the kind, only aimed sideways: she longed to be *known*. To sit in this lamplight as a person rather than a function. To say *my name is not the name on my papers* and watch the sentence survive the air between them.

Six years she had been Jude Fairweather. Before that—before the summer of 1908, before a boy with ink-stained fingers and a forger's calm had made her English with a pen—she had been someone else entirely, in another country, in another language, in a life that had ended the way lives end in wartime: suddenly, administratively, with stamps and seals. She kept that girl folded up inside her the way she kept the third pile of post: present, weighted, never mentioned.

"There's one more thing," she heard herself say, and was surprised by her own voice. "A letter came Tuesday. Forwarded. Foreign handwriting—it's addressed to the harbor master at Fowey, asking redirection here." She rose and fetched it from the drawer where she kept the *never* pile, and stood holding it, feeling its small weight, its enormous weight. "I haven't opened it. It didn't seem... it seemed like it might be yours rather than mine."

Edith opened her eyes. For a moment—just a moment, gone so quickly Jude could almost believe she'd imagined it—something moved behind them. Something old and guarded, a door closing softly in a far room.

"Foreign handwriting," Edith repeated, neutrally. Too neutrally, a note held perfectly flat, the way a person holds a note when they are afraid of what vibrates underneath it.

"It may be nothing. Business, perhaps. Shipping."

"Everything is shipping, on this coast." Edith held out her hand. "Give it here."

And Jude, who had read every letter that entered this house for six years, who had made invisibility into an art and triage into a tyranny, found her own hand hesitating—an inch of reluctance, no more, but enough that she felt it like a crack in ice. Because the handwriting was not entirely foreign. Now that she looked at it properly, in the full lamplight, with the envelope turned so the address faced her—the angular letters, the careful downstrokes, the particular way the writer formed his *k*s like small ladders—

She knew that hand. She had watched it forge her into existence across a borrowed desk one desperate summer, six years ago, in a room that smelled of ink and fear.

*Karl,* she thought, and the name went through her like cold water through cloth.

"Jude?" Edith's voice, gentle. Watching her too closely.

"Nothing." She put the letter into Edith's palm and stepped back into the shadow past the lamp's reach, into the place where she had lived so long it fit her like skin. "Only the light in my eyes."

Edith broke the seal. Outside, the mist pressed against the windows, and the sea worked at the harbor stones with its endless patient hands, and somewhere down the coast, in a camp Jude did not yet know existed, a man was writing her name—or the name she had been—on paper that would take four days to arrive and change everything.

Edith read. Her face did not move. But her thumb passed once, slowly, over the signature at the bottom, back and forth, back and forth, like a woman touching a scar.

"Well?" Jude asked.

"It's nothing," Edith said, and folded the letter away, and did not offer to show it, and did not meet her eyes. "Business. As you said."

Jude nodded and banked the fire and carried the lamp to the stairs, and lay awake long after the cottage slept, listening to the rain begin again, telling herself the tightness in her chest was only the weather coming in.
