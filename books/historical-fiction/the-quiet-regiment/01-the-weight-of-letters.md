## 1. The Weight of Letters

There is a science to carrying things, and I flatter myself I have mastered it.

A hatbox holds up to four pounds without shifting on the seat. A document case, if you wedge it against the spare tyre with the leather strap cinched tight, will survive even the cobbles down Fetter Lane. A woman's valise weighs nothing at all if she carries it herself and only pretends to let you take it — that is a lesson in vanity rather than physics, but it belongs in the same ledger. I keep a ledger in my head. Everything that can be carried, and what it costs to carry it.

My name is Wendell Crane. Officially, I drive the motorcar for Miss Edith Marlowe, who is a lady of independent means and inconvenient opinions. Unofficially, I am the most dangerous postman in London.

I did not set out to be either. Two years ago I was a mechanic's hand in my cousin Alfred's garage in Bermondsey, content to know an engine by its cough the way a doctor knows a patient by his breathing. Then Miss Marlowe's chauffeur took a position with a shipping firm — better wages, he said, and fewer windows to duck behind — and Alfred recommended me, and I came upriver to a mews off Great Ormond Street where a Darracq sat gleaming like a well-fed animal and a woman was waiting to ask me, before she asked my name, whether I could keep my mouth shut.

"Perfectly," I said.

"I don't mean discretion," she said. "Discretion is for footmen. I mean silence under pressure. There is a difference."

There is. Discretion is not telling your mates at the pub where you drove the lady on Tuesday. Silence under pressure is standing in a police yard while a sergeant inventories your car and your face does exactly nothing. I had not yet been tested, but I said I understood, and she hired me anyway, which tells you something about how desperate the movement was for men who could drive and about how good a judge of character Miss Marlowe is when she is being reckless, which is always.

The letters began within the month.

They come to her from everywhere — from Manchester and Glasgow, from a mill town in Lancashire where the women have organised so thoroughly that the foremen have taken to locking the gates at lunchtime; from a schoolmistress in Kent who signs herself *A Friend* and means it; from Holloway itself, smuggled out in the lining of a released prisoner's coat, written in pencil on toilet paper because paper is contraband when you are being force-fed. I know this last because I carried one once, flat as a communion wafer between two sheets of ordinary correspondence, and my hands were steady, and afterwards I was sick behind the mews wall, and then I washed my face and drove Miss Marlowe to a garden party in Kensington where she smiled at a Conservative MP until he agreed to read a report he had sworn never to open.

That is the trade. That is the whole of it: you carry the thing, you say nothing, you smile at the right people, and somewhere downstream a law shifts by half an inch.

I read them, of course. Every letter.

I want to be clear that this is not simple snooping, though I grant it looks like snooping and would be prosecuted as such if anyone ever caught me, which no one has, because I am invisible. It is arithmetic. Miss Marlowe keeps carbon copies of her outgoing correspondence in a locked cabinet, and her incoming letters she reads twice and burns, because paper is evidence and evidence gets people imprisoned. But a burned letter is a dead letter. If she is arrested — and she has been arrested twice since I've known her, both times briefly, both times expensively — everything in her head goes into a cell with her. Somebody outside must hold the same information or the movement loses its memory every time the police get ambitious.

So I memorise. Names, dates, sums pledged, routes planned, the address of the printer in Southwark who runs off the pamphlets after hours. I file it all away in the same ledger where I keep the weights of hatboxes. A letter takes me perhaps four minutes to commit to memory if it is well-organised, longer if the writer is the sort who digresses, and there is one correspondent in Birmingham whose letters run to eleven pages and include recipes, and I confess I have retained more of her plum cake method than of her fundraising figures.

Miss Marlowe does not know I do this. Or — no. Let me be honest, since honesty appears to be the day's theme. She suspects. She has never caught me at it, but she is not a careless woman, and twice now she has handed me a letter sealed with wax and watched me a moment too long before turning away. The first time I thought nothing of it. The second time I thought about it for three days.

Here is the thing about invisibility that nobody warns you about: it fits beautifully, and then one morning you notice it is a shroud.

* * *

The letter in question arrived on a Thursday in October, by the second post, in a grey envelope with a Harrogate postmark and no return address.

I was in the mews, polishing the Darracq's lamps, when the girl from the corner house brought it round with the rest of Miss Marlowe's mail. Miss Marlowe was out — a committee meeting in Holborn, expected back at four — and the envelope sat on the hall table among the bills and the circulars looking, I thought even then, like a guest who has come to the wrong party and knows it.

I should explain my system, such as it is. Letters marked *private* I leave unopened; they are usually from solicitors and contain nothing worth the risk. Letters with a known correspondent's hand I read quickly and file. Letters with no return address go to the top of the pile, because anonymous correspondents are either cranks or heroes and it is useful to know which.

This one was neither, at first glance. The handwriting was small and upright, a schoolroom hand gone slightly ragged at the descenders, as though the writer had learned beautiful penmanship and then spent some months writing while lying down, or shaking, or both. The ink was watery, diluted past economy. Prison ink, I thought, or the nearest thing to it. And the date at the top stopped my thumb over the seal entirely.

It was dated the ninth of March.

Eight months ago. And the name signed at the bottom — I had not yet opened it, mind, only seen through the translucent paper the shape of the signature — was one I knew from newspapers and from Miss Marlowe's own mouth, spoken once, quietly, on the anniversary of something she did not explain.

Alice Fenwick.

Alice Fenwick was dead. That was not rumour; it was administration. She had gone on hunger strike in Holloway in the spring, she had been transferred — the word the Home Office used, *transferred*, as though she were luggage — and she had died of it, and there had been an inquest of sorts, and Miss Marlowe had worn black for a week and then stopped wearing black, which frightened me more than the black had, because it meant she had decided grief was a luxury the schedule could not afford.

And here was her handwriting, dated March, arriving in October, alive as a wasp in a drawer.

I stood in the hall with the lamp unlit and the afternoon going grey at the windows, and I told myself all the reasonable things. Cranks forge signatures. Grief makes people cruel jokes. The postmark proves nothing; envelopes can be steamed and reused; I had done it myself, once, for reasons I still consider sound.

Then I broke the seal, because I am not a reasonable man, whatever I tell my ledger.

*Miss Marlowe — They tell me if I write to you it will be returned unread, so I am writing anyway, as I have written every week since March, and one of these days a nurse with a conscience will post one, and today is that day, God rest her. I am at —*

The address that followed was one I did not recognise. A private house, or what claimed to be one, in Hertfordshire. Not Holloway. Not any prison whose name appeared in Hansard.

*I am not permitted visitors and I am not permitted to die, which between them tells you what I am worth to them. I have given my account of the transfer to Mrs. Pankhurst's people and to the coroner's clerk and to a journalist who took my statement and then discovered his editor had other views. My account exists in four copies. Three have been destroyed, I am assured. I am the fourth. If anything happens to me, remember that I wrote it all down, and that I spelled the names correctly.*

She spelled the names correctly. I have read enough letters to know what that sentence costs. There were names in the body of the letter — officials, a magistrate, a doctor whose signature appeared on certificates I suddenly understood in a new and colder light — and I read them the way I read everything, once, twice, filing each one into the ledger with the dates and the sums and the routes, except that this time my hands were not steady, and this time the thing I was memorising was not strategy but testimony, and the difference between those two words is the difference between a fine and a hanging.

At the bottom, beneath the signature, someone else had added a line in a different hand, hurried, the ink blotted:

*Posted at risk of my position. She is not mad. They are making her mad. For God's sake send someone.*

* * *

I put the letter back in its envelope. I put the envelope on the hall table, precisely where the girl had left it, at precisely the angle, because I am a man who notices angles. Then I went out to the mews and polished lamps that were already polished and listened to my own heart behaving badly.

Here is what I want recorded, since I am apparently keeping a record of everything now: I considered burning it. The thought arrived whole and reasonable, dressed in my own voice. *You are a driver. You carry parcels. This is above your weight class.* The ledger logic of it, the clean arithmetic — one letter, destroyed, and Wendell Crane returns to invisibility, to hatboxes and lamp-polish and the comfortable dark.

But the letter was addressed to Miss Marlowe, and Miss Marlowe had worn black for a woman who was, by every official account, dead, and had then taken the black off and gone back to work, and I found — standing in the mews with a chamois in my hand and the light going — that I could not do the arithmetic. The sum kept coming out wrong. Somewhere in Hertfordshire a woman was writing weekly letters that were returned unread, spelling the names correctly, and the entire machinery of the state was arranged around the proposition that she did not exist, and I had just become one of perhaps six people in England who knew otherwise, and knowledge, I discovered, has weight. More than a hatbox. More than a valise a lady only pretends to let you carry.

I heard the motor turn into the mews at ten past four — early, which meant the committee had gone badly or brilliantly — and I went in through the side door and made tea, because that is what invisible men do.

Miss Marlowe came into the kitchen still wearing her gloves, which meant fury, and took off one glove finger by finger, which meant calculation, and said, "Anything while I was out?"

"Bills," I said. "Circulars. And a letter from Harrogate."

Her hands went still over the second glove.

"Harrogate," she repeated.

"No return address." I set the cup down in front of her. "Small, upright hand. Schoolroom penmanship, gone a bit ragged. Watery ink. Dated March."

For a moment — I will swear to this before any inquiry they care to convene — for one full moment, Edith Marlowe looked at me the way a card player looks at a man who has just shown a hand he should not have been dealt. Not surprise. Recalculation.

"You read my post, Mr. Crane," she said.

"Every word," I said. "Someone has to remember it."

And she looked at the envelope on the table, and then at me, and whatever she saw in my face made her sit down slowly, still holding the glove, and say, in a voice I had never heard her use — quiet, and old, and afraid:

"Then you'd better tell me what she wrote."

Which is when I understood that she had known. Some part of her, all along, had known.
