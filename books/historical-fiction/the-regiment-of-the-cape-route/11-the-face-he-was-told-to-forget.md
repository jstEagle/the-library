## 11. The Face He Was Told to Forget

Elise had learned her father's face the way other children learned scripture: by heart, by rote, by the small daily liturgy of watching. She knew the crease between his brows that meant a calculation had gone wrong twice. She knew the particular stillness he wore when Corvijn's name was spoken in his presence, a stillness like water deciding whether to freeze. And she knew—had known for years, in the wordless way daughters know—that there was another arithmetic running beneath his life, some sum he carried always and never set down.

She did not expect to find its answer standing in Oscar Thornbury's doorway, dripping rain and glaring at her with her father's grey eyes.

* * *

It began, as most of Elise's discoveries began, with numbers.

The clock's errors had been eating at her for a fortnight. A mechanical fault had a signature; it repeated itself the way a limp repeats itself, faithful in its imperfection. But these errors wandered. They drifted east one week and west the next, as though the clock were not broken but *persuaded*, and Elise had spent three nights at her father's desk with the transit observations spread before her, matching star to clock, clock to star, until the pattern emerged from the noise like a figure from fog.

The time was being bent toward the convoys.

She had gone to the tower to say so—to confront the apprentice, or perhaps only to look at him while she said it, to watch his hands and see whether they went still the way guilty hands do. What she found instead was a girl of seven sitting on an upturned crate beside the great clock's case, sorting screws into a wooden box with the fierce concentration of a surgeon, while Oscar worked above her on the escapement, his sleeves rolled, his face half-lit by the lantern.

The girl looked up first.

Elise felt it before she understood it—a jolt, low in the body, older than thought. The child's hair was dark and unruly, cut short and unevenly, the work of someone who had used scissors in anger or haste. Her chin was small and pointed. And her eyes—

Her father's eyes. Not similar. *His.* The same pale storm-grey, the same slight downward tilt at the outer corner that Elise saw every morning when she leaned over the basin, that she had stopped seeing because it was simply her own face looking back.

"You're staring," the girl said, with the flat accusation children deploy like small knives.

"I beg your pardon." Elise heard her own voice from a distance. "I'm Elise Van Reenen."

"Sparrow." The girl considered this, then apparently decided no further information would be issued. "I'm helping. Don't touch the screws, they're in order."

"In order of what?"

"Size," Sparrow said, with contempt for the question. "Obviously."

Above them, Oscar had gone very still on his ladder. Elise looked up and met his eyes, and what passed between them in that moment was not words but weather—the pressure drop before a storm, both of them aware that something in the room had just changed weight and neither willing to name it aloud in front of the child.

"May I help sort?" Elise asked.

Sparrow weighed this offer with the gravity of an admiralty board. "You can hold the box."

So Elise sat on the cold flagstones of the tower with a box of screws in her lap and her father's eyes looking up at her out of a stranger's small face, and she made herself breathe slowly, and she sorted nothing, and she watched.

The girl hummed while she worked—an old tune, a sailor's shanty about a maid left waiting in Rotterdam—and she hummed it slightly flat, in the same place her father always went flat when he sang at Christmas, the one indulgence he permitted himself after the second glass of Madeira. When she counted the screws she counted in Dutch first and English after, the way their household counted, the way a child counts who has been raised by one language and schooled in another.

And there was the hands. Elise's mother had died when Elise was nine; her memories of her were few and worn smooth from handling. But one memory remained sharp as a splinter: her mother's hands, turning and turning a wedding ring around her finger when she was thinking, round and round, an orbit without end. Sparrow turned an imaginary ring on her small finger while she waited for Oscar to pass her the next screw. Round and round. An orbit without end.

Some things are not taught. Some things travel in the blood like ballast, invisible and decisive.

* * *

She stayed an hour. She could not have said why, except that leaving felt impossible, the way it feels impossible to leave a fire unbanked. Sparrow interrogated her about Cape Town—who lived where, which streets flooded, whether the sea could be seen from the mountain—and answered her own questions when Elise hesitated too long. She had been in the town, it emerged, less than two months. Before that, a ship. Before that, she would say only: "A lady."

"What lady?" Elise asked gently.

Sparrow's hands went still over the screw box. For a moment her whole face closed like a shop shutter. "One who gave me away," she said. "I don't remember her face. I remember her hands." She held up her own small fingers, splayed. "Like this. Cold. She held them too tight and then too loose, like she couldn't decide which was worse."

Elise's throat ached. "And after?"

"After there was a man who smelled of tar, and a ship's bell, and then a house where they didn't want me, and then—" she shrugged, seven years old and already fluent in the grammar of abandonment— "here. He doesn't want me either." A jerk of her chin toward Oscar. "But he hasn't said so yet, so I'm staying till he does."

"I haven't said so," Oscar said quietly from the ladder, "because it isn't true."

Sparrow received this the way sailors receive a forecast of fair weather—with hope carefully rationed against experience. But she glowed anyway, briefly, like a lamp turned up and quickly down, and Elise saw Oscar see it, and saw him look away as though the brightness were more than he could bear.

When Elise rose to go, Sparrow caught her sleeve.

"You look like someone," the girl said. The grey eyes narrowed, working at the problem with a child's terrible directness. "I keep thinking of who. Someone at church? No." She shook her head, dissatisfied. "Someone sad."

"Perhaps you'll think of it," Elise managed.

"Perhaps," Sparrow agreed, and returned to her screws, and released her, and let her go.

* * *

Outside, the rain had thickened. Elise walked home through streets running silver with it, past the fishwives huddled under awnings and the sentry at the castle gate blinking water from his eyes, and all the while her mind performed its merciless work, laying evidence upon evidence like bricks in a wall she did not want finished.

Her father's locked drawer, which she had never been able to pick and which he checked each night with a touch so habitual he no longer knew he did it. The money that vanished from the household accounts in sums too regular to be losses and too irregular to be salary. The year of her mother's deepest sorrow—Elise remembered now, remembered her mother weeping over mending one autumn and refusing to say why, remembered the word *Rotterdam* spoken once in a low furious voice behind a door and never again. The shanty. The hands. The eyes.

Seven years old. Seven years ago her father had traveled to the Netherlands to confer with the astronomers at Leiden. Seven years ago her mother had stopped singing.

Elise stood in the doorway of her own home, streaming rain, and could not make herself go in.

Because here was the thing she had discovered about truth, in her years of doing her father's calculations in secret: truth is patient. It does not knock. It sits down inside you and waits, and it weighs exactly as much as whatever you are carrying already, and then a little more.

She went in. Her father was at his desk, as always, the lamp turned low, his pen moving through figures. He looked up and smiled at her—the smile he kept for her alone, warm and tired and real—and she smiled back, and said she was wet through and going to change, and climbed the stairs with her father's eyes burning behind her own eyelids, and did not tell him.

Not yet. Not yet, she told herself, and heard in the phrase how much it sounded like a lie told slowly.

* * *

She went back to the tower the next evening, and the next. She brought bread and cheese wrapped in cloth, ostensibly for Oscar, and ate half of it with Sparrow perched on the crate, and listened to the girl talk, and catalogued her the way she catalogued stars: by position, by magnitude, by the certainty of her motion across the sky of Elise's heart.

On the third evening Oscar asked her to stay after Sparrow fell asleep—which she did, abruptly, mid-sentence, her head tipping against the clock case, her breath going deep and even, one hand still curled around a screw she had refused to surrender. Oscar lifted her with a care that undid something in Elise's chest, carried her to the cot in the corner that had clearly been built for her from packing crates, and tucked a blanket that had once, judging by its weave, been a sailcloth bag.

Then he turned, and they looked at each other across the lamplit floor, and Elise said: "You know."

"I know she looks like someone," Oscar said. "I've known since the first week. There's a portrait miniature in your father's study—your mother, young. The chin. The eyes." He rubbed his jaw. "I hoped I was wrong. Hoping's cheap, but it's what I had."

"You haven't told her."

"She hasn't asked me the right question yet." He came and stood near her, not touching, the way he always stood near her—not touching, and yet closer than most men ever come to anyone. "What will you do?"

That was the fracture line, though neither of them saw it yet. Later, in the bad nights that followed, Elise would trace everything back to this question and his tone when he asked it—gentle, practical, a craftsman's question, the question of a man who fixes things by identifying the broken part.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "Tell my father I know? Tell Sparrow whose she is?"

"Does your father deserve to know that you know?"

"That's not mine to decide for him."

"No," Oscar said. "But it's yours to decide for yourself. Those aren't the same thing, and people spend their whole lives confusing them." He paused. "Elise. If Corvijn learns what she is—if he learns your father has a daughter he cannot acknowledge, a daughter already delivered into the hands of the one man investigating him—"

"He won't learn it from me."

"He might not need to. Men like Corvijn don't need facts. They need leverage-shaped holes, and they wait for someone to fall in." His voice stayed level, but she heard the fear under it, banked like coals. "Every day she stays here, she's safer from the street and less safe from him. That's the arithmetic. I hate the arithmetic. I do it anyway."

"And I am telling you," Elise said, and heard her own voice rise, "that she is not arithmetic. She is a child who remembers a woman's hands and a ship's bell, who was instructed to be forgotten before she could write her own name, and the one unforgivable thing—the one *unforgivable* thing—would be for the adults who finally know whose she is to begin managing her again like a ledger entry. Deciding what she may know. Deciding what protects her better than the truth. My father decided that seven years ago, and look what it made. Look what it made *her*."

The lantern flame swayed. Somewhere below, the town struck eight, and the sound came up the stairwell muffled, like a voice through a wall.

Oscar was quiet a long moment. "You think I'd manage her," he said finally. "You think that's what I'm doing."

"I think you're frightened," Elise said, more gently, because his face had changed. "And I think frightened men reach for control, because control is the shape fear takes in a craftsman's hands. You want to fix her the way you'd fix a clock. Identify the broken part. Replace it. Seal the case." She stepped closer. "But she isn't broken, Oscar. She's *buried*. And you don't dig someone out by deciding for them how much light they can bear."

"And if the light kills her?" His voice cracked on it, just barely, and she saw suddenly how young he was under the competence, how much he had already lost to fires he hadn't started. "If knowing whose she is puts her in Corvijn's path? You talk about truth as if it's clean. Truth is a blade. It cuts whoever holds it."

"Then we hold it together," Elise said, "and we bleed a little, and she grows up knowing there was never a moment when the people who loved her chose her ignorance over her. That's all I ask. That's everything I ask."

He looked at her for a long time. The clock ticked above them, vast and steady, keeping its corrupted time, and Elise thought: *even this, even the great master itself, lies by minutes. Why should we be better than clocks? Because we must. Because someone has to be.*

"I won't lie to her," Oscar said at last. "If she asks, I'll answer. But I won't go hunting for the moment to tell her, Elise. I won't hand her a blade because the truth deserves an audience. If that makes me a man who manages things—" his mouth twisted— "then I'll be that, and you can hate me for it in the mornings and sit with me in the evenings, because I don't know how to do this any other way."

"I don't hate you," Elise said. "That's what frightens me. I could forgive almost anything faster than I could survive agreeing with you and being wrong."

He laughed then, low and startled, and the fracture did not close—it never fully closed, that night's argument ran beneath everything that came after like groundwater—but it became a seam rather than a wound, the place where two different kinds of love had learned to meet without pretending to be the same kind.

They sat on either side of the sleeping child until late. At some point Sparrow turned in her sleep and murmured, and her hand found the hem of Elise's sleeve and gripped it, the way a sailor grips a line, and did not let go.

Elise did not move until her arm went numb. She would have sat there until dawn.

* * *

She walked home under a sky scrubbed clean by the storm, the stars hard and brilliant over Table Mountain, and at her own door she paused, because the study lamp still burned.

Her father was awake. Of course he was awake. He was always awake lately, a man keeping a vigil over his own guilt.

Through the window she watched him at his desk, and she saw him do the thing he always did: pause, touch the locked drawer, resume. Touch, resume. A man checking that his secret still breathed.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow I will decide. Tomorrow I will weigh the blade and choose which hand holds it—his, hers, mine—or whether it goes back in the drawer where it has bled us all for seven years.

She climbed the stairs to her room and took out her star charts, and began, because her hands needed work, to calculate tomorrow's true noon.

Below her, in the tower by the Parade, the great clock ticked out its beautiful, purchased lie, twenty-two minutes fast and gaining, and somewhere at sea, unseen, the convoy was already sailing toward the hour that would kill it.
