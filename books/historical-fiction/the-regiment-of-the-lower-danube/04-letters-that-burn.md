## 4. Letters That Burn

The laundry came up from the village on Thursdays, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with the same grey twine that had bound it every week for the three years Beatrix had kept the countess's household accounts. He had never once thought about the laundry list. It was a document of profound dullness: twelve sheets, four pillowcases, two tablecloths, the countess's blue dressing gown with a note about the frayed cuff. He read it standing in the boot room with his gloves still on, because reading was his work and he did it the way other men breathed.

Twelve sheets. Four pillowcases. Two tablecloths.

*The river is low at Orsova and the ice will not hold past the month's end.*

He read it again. The words sat between *tablecloths* and *the blue gown*, in the same careful clerk's hand as the rest of the list, and if you were not looking — if you had not spent five years learning to look — they were nothing at all. A complaint about weather. Peasants talked about the ice the way they talked about their knees.

But the hand was wrong. Not the handwriting itself; the pressure of it. He knew Stefan's writing from a single note left under a stone five years ago — *go north, don't write* — and he knew now what he had not known then: that a man who lived by moving forbidden things across a border learned to make every line of ink carry two cargoes. The letters were steady. The spaces between them were not. Someone had written this list slowly, deliberately, choosing each word the way a man chooses footholds on a rotten stair.

Beatrix stood very still in the boot room. Somewhere above him the house went about its morning: the rattle of the stove door, Marta humming off-key in the corridor, the countess's cane tapping twice on the floorboards of her study — her signal that she wanted her correspondence brought down, all of it, read aloud in her dry unhurried voice while she signed what needed signing.

*The river is low at Orsova.* Orsova was forty kilometres downstream, past the cataracts, where the Danube narrowed and the customs men grew rich and suspicious. *The ice will not hold past the month's end.* It was the middle of February. The sweep would come before the thaw — gendarmes walking the frozen villages of the riverbank, going door to door with a list of names, and one of those names, Beatrix understood with a cold that had nothing to do with the season, would be Stefan's.

He folded the list along its old creases. His hands were steady. He noticed that they were steady and found it obscene.

* * *

He burned it at noon, in the copper boiler room where the ash from the day's fires was already grey and blameless, and he chose noon deliberately because at noon everyone ate and no one walked behind the coach house. The paper caught reluctantly, the way paper does when the air is damp, curling brown at the edges before the flame took the words whole. He watched the last corner go — a fragment of *Orsova*, then nothing — and stirred the ash with the poker until even the char was indistinguishable from the morning's cinders.

It was the first thing he had ever destroyed in the countess's service. Three years of reading everything aloud, deciding which silences protected whom: the debt collector's letter softened by omission, the cousin's plea for money passed over in silence, the anonymous note calling the suffrage petition *a fever of spinsters* delivered in full because Ilona Vay laughed at it and laughter was armor. He had built his invisibility out of these small mercies and small betrayals, and he had told himself they balanced. They had never once touched the boy asleep upstairs in the linen room with a name stitched into his collar that half the river police could probably recite by now.

Now the arithmetic had changed. Now there was a date attached.

He thought about the promise. It had been made in peacetime, which is to say it had been made cheaply, the way promises are made by men sitting on a warm deck with plum brandy between them and the border lights sliding past. *If anything ever happens to me,* Stefan had said, laughing, not meaning it, meaning it completely, *you take Milos downriver to my sister in Semlin. You're the only man I know whose face no one remembers.* And Beatrix had said yes because saying yes cost nothing, because the ice was solid, because the war that everyone whispered about was a story told to frighten children, and because he owed this man his life and had been paying the debt in silence ever since, in unasked questions, in letters read and forgotten on purpose.

The debt was due now. The ice would break in six weeks, and after it broke there would be no crossing at all — only patrols, only papers, only the river running wide and brown and watched.

* * *

"You were a long time with the laundry," the Countess said that evening.

She said it without looking up from her desk. She was correcting the petition draft, her pen moving in small decisive strokes, and the lamp threw her shadow enormous against the bookshelves. Beatrix stood in the doorway with the evening tray and felt the lie assemble itself in his mouth like a bone setting wrong.

"The washerwoman's boy had lice again," he said. "I waited while she checked the linens."

"Mm." The pen scratched. "And?"

"Clean. All but the pillowcases."

"Burn the pillowcases." She signed something. "You may go."

That was all. That was the whole of it, and Beatrix carried the tray back down the corridor with his heart hammering against his ribs like a fist on a locked door, because it had been so easy. She hadn't looked up. She hadn't cared. And that — the ease of it, the smoothness — was worse than any interrogation, because he understood suddenly that lying to her was possible, and a man who discovers a door will eventually walk through it, and he had just discovered that the one person in the world whose trust he had guarded like a coal in cupped hands could be deceived with three sentences and a shrug.

He lay awake that night in the narrow bed behind the coach house and listened to the wind working at the eaves, and he made himself say it plainly, the way he made himself do all unpleasant things: *You lied to her. First time. It worked. It will work again.* And beneath that, quieter, in Stefan's voice across five years and a frozen river: *You take Milos downriver.* The boy was six years old and called him Uncle Beatrix now, had done for eleven days, and had stopped flinching at loud noises on the ninth.

Resolve is a strange thing. It does not arrive like courage arrives, in a hot rush. It arrives like frost, silently, over everything, so that in the morning the world looks the same and nothing moves the way it moved before. Beatrix woke with it already on him. He fed the horses. He polished the carriage lamps. He took the countess into town through the frozen market square, past the fish stalls iced into silver sculpture, past the gendarme at the customs house stamping papers with the bored violence of a man who has been told to expect trouble, and he drove home with his face arranged into its usual nothing, and inside, under the nothing, he was measuring the road to Orsova in his head. Forty kilometres. Two days' drive if the snow held off. One night on the ice if it didn't.

* * *

On Sunday the countess read aloud at the schoolhouse — her petition, her arguments, her voice pitched to carry over the heads of farmers' wives and the two or three men who came to scoff and stayed to listen — and Beatrix stood at the back with his cap in his hands and let the familiar words wash over him. *Half the Empire pays taxes and tills fields and buries its sons, and may not sign its own name to its own future.* He had heard it forty times. He mouthed it sometimes in the stable when the horses were restless; it calmed them, or calmed him.

Afterward, wrapping herself in furs on the schoolhouse steps, she said, almost idly: "Marta tells me the boy sleeps with his boots on."

"He's six," Beatrix said. "They all do that."

"Do they?" She settled into the carriage and arranged the rug over her knees. "I wouldn't know. Drive on."

But she looked at him then — one long look, unhurried, the look she gave a letter whose seal she didn't recognize before she decided whether to open it — and Beatrix held the horses steady and returned nothing, gave her the blank pleasant surface he had worn for three years, and understood that she had seen it anyway, whatever *it* was, and had filed it away in whatever drawer she kept such things. The horses' breath steamed. The runners hissed over the packed snow. Behind them the schoolhouse bell rang once, twice, and stopped.

Six weeks, he thought, watching the frozen river gleam between the bare poplars. Perhaps less. The ice would not hold past the month's end — and somewhere downstream, in a village of smoking chimneys and stacked firewood, a man who had pulled a drowning stranger out of black water was waiting for gendarmes who would come with a warrant and a list, and the only message Beatrix could send him was the one already sent, already burned, already read: *run, brother, the water is rising.*

He turned the carriage toward home. In the linen room upstairs, a small boy was napping with his boots on, and the promise Beatrix had made in peacetime sat in his chest like swallowed ice, cold and growing, and he knew — with the terrible clarity of a man who has just lied for the first time and found it easy — that before the month was out he would have to learn the river at night.
