## 5. The Ice Road

The river froze properly in the third week of January, and by the first week of February it had become a road.

Beatrix had watched it happen from the coach house window with a feeling he could not name—something between relief and dread, as though the weather itself had conspired to make his lie possible. For months the Danube had been a wall of black water, impassable, indifferent. Now it was a grey-white plain stretching from bank to bank, ridged and seamed like old parchment, strong enough in places to bear a cart, a horse, a man carrying a child. Strong enough, if you chose your line carefully, to carry two fugitives into Serbia.

He told himself he was only scouting. That was the word he used when the Countess asked why he wanted the trap on the light carriage greased and the horse shod for ice—*scouting*, as though the river were an estate boundary and not the edge of an empire. She had looked at him over her spectacles that morning, pen suspended above the suffrage petition she was correcting for the third time, and said nothing at all. Her silences had begun to acquire weight. He felt them settle on his shoulders like snow.

"Take Milos," she said instead. "The boy has been underfoot all week, and Janka swears he's eaten through a month of preserves. Fresh air will do you both good."

It was a kindness dressed as an inconvenience, which was how the Countess delivered most of her kindnesses. Beatrix bowed and went to find his coat.

* * *

They went out on a Tuesday, when the sky hung low and colourless as unpolished pewter and the wind came down the valley with the smell of distant snow. Beatrix had dressed Milos in his own dead brother's coat, let out at the seams, and taught him one story on the walk down from the estate: *You are my sister's son, visiting from Mohács, and your name is whatever they ask it to be.* The boy had nodded with a gravity that sat strangely on six years old, the way gravity sat on everything he did now. Children learned silence quickly, Beatrix thought. It was the first language of the frightened.

The bank path descended through frozen reeds that rattled like dry bones in the wind. At the bottom, where the mud of summer had hardened into ruts glazed with ice, the old ferry landing stood abandoned—its rope rotted, its pontoon hauled up and chained, its sign hanging crooked: *ÁTKELÉS — FERRY — ПРЕВОЗ*, the three languages of three empires that had all claimed this water and none of them owned it now but winter.

Beyond it, the river.

Up close the ice was not the smooth white sheet of picture books. It was a landscape entire—swollen ridges where pressure had buckled whole fields of it upward, dark veins of open water along the current lines, patches of wind-scoured black glass so clear you could see the slow green shadows of fish beneath. Here and there the surface held the fossil impressions of things caught mid-crossing: a crow's prints stitched in a neat double line, the exploded star of where something heavy had fallen through and refrozen over.

Milos stopped at the edge and would go no further.

"It holds," Beatrix said. "I checked yesterday."

The boy looked up at him. His face was Stefan's face reduced to miniature—the same stubborn jaw, the same dark eyes set too deep—but the expression on it was his mother's, whoever she had been, or perhaps simply the expression of every child who has been handed to strangers and told to trust them. "Papa said the river eats people," Milos said. "He said it ate his friend."

"The river eats careless people." Beatrix crouched to the boy's level, breath fogging between them. "So we will not be careless. We step where I step. If I say run, you run for the far bank and you do not stop, not for me, not for anything. Do you understand?"

"You'd stay behind?"

"If I had to."

Milos considered this with terrible seriousness. "Papa says promises made on ice are the strongest kind."

"Papa," Beatrix said quietly, "is a man who knows about debts." He took the small mittened hand. "Come. We'll keep to the shallows' line, where the current runs slow under the ice. There—" he pointed to a pale seam running diagonally across the river toward a bare willow on the Serbian side, "—that's our road."

* * *

They walked out onto the Danube the way pilgrims walk, testing each stride, listening. The ice spoke constantly—a groaning here, a high thin singing there, sudden reports like distant rifle fire that made Milos flinch and grip Beatrix's fingers until the blood left them. Beatrix talked the whole way, low and steady, because he had learned long ago that fear shrinks when given a voice to listen to.

"When I was nineteen I crossed a frozen lake in Galicia," he said. "Deserting, though I didn't have the word for it yet. I thought I did. I thought desertion was what happened when you ran. Later I understood it's what happens before—you desert a little each day, and then one morning there's nothing left to desert *to*, so you just keep walking."

Milos didn't understand half of it. He understood the tone, which was the important part. "Were you scared?"

"Frightened to death. And the strange thing is, the fear made me careful, and being careful kept me alive, and staying alive turned out to matter very much later on. To you, as it happens." He squeezed the mitten. "Fear is just care wearing a uniform."

That got the ghost of a smile. They crossed a pressure ridge together, Beatrix lifting the boy over the jagged crest, and stood a moment on its summit looking back the way they'd come: the Hungarian bank a smudge of bare trees and chimney smoke, the Vay estate invisible among them, everything he was supposed to be reduced to a point on the horizon. Ahead, the Serbian shore lay closer than he'd ever seen it—a country he could almost touch, marked only by a different quality of smoke and a church dome glinting dully.

"There," Milos whispered, pointing. "Aunt Ruža's village is past the willows. Papa drew it for me." He traced the map in the air with a mittened finger. "There's a plum orchard, and a dog named Cesar who bites gendarmes."

"A useful dog."

"The most useful dog in Serbia."

Beatrix laughed—an actual laugh, startled out of him—and felt something loosen in his chest that had been clenched since the note arrived at the gate. This was the treachery of children, he thought. They made hope feel permissible. You could hold your resolve like iron right up until a six-year-old described a dog that bit gendarmes, and then some warm, foolish part of you began building futures out of plum orchards.

They mapped the crossing twice more that afternoon. Beatrix drove stakes of memory into the route: the safe seam past the sunken barge, the ridge to skirt wide, the stretch near the Serbian bank where the current ran fast beneath and the ice thinned to the colour of smoked glass. Twice they reached the far side, stood in the willow scrub breathing hard, and walked back. By the last crossing Milos went without taking his hand.

On the return leg, near the Hungarian shore, the ferryman was waiting.

* * *

He sat on an overturned skiff above the landing, wrapped in a sheepskin coat gone the colour of tallow, smoking a pipe that seemed mostly ember. Old Ferenc—Beatrix knew him distantly, as everyone on the river knew everyone distantly; a man who had poled passengers across these waters for forty years and watched three flags change over the customs house. His eyes were rheumy and missed nothing.

"Afternoon," Ferenc said.

"Afternoon."

"Fine ice this year. Best in a decade, the fishermen say." He drew on the pipe. "You'll want the seam past the barge. Current's lazy there. The rest of it lies."

"We were only walking," Beatrix said. "The boy wanted to see the river."

"Mm." Ferenc looked at Milos, who had gone very still in the way children go still when adults begin lying near them. Then he looked at Beatrix, and the look lasted one second too long, and in that second five years fell away—the night water, the gasping, the strong hands hauling him over a gunwale while a smuggler's voice swore at him in three languages for being fool enough to swim the Danube in April. Ferenc had been there that night. Ferenc had held the lantern.

"I knew a young fellow once," the old man said, to the river rather than to either of them, "who couldn't swim and tried anyway. Foolish business. Everyone said he drowned." A pause. Smoke curled. "I always thought that was a shame. A man who drowns can't grow a proper beard, can't drive fine carriages for countesses. Can't walk his nephew on the ice on a Tuesday." He knocked the pipe out against the skiff. "Drowned men should stay drowned. Rivers remember, but old ferrymen forget easy. Too much water under us." He rose, joints cracking like the ice itself, and shuffled toward the reed path. At the edge of it he paused. "Ice breaks early this year, they're saying. Warm winds out of the west. A week, maybe less. Anyone with business across oughtn't dawdle."

Then he was gone, and the wind filled the space where he'd been.

Milos tugged Beatrix's sleeve. "Did he know you?"

"No," Beatrix said, and heard the lie land flat between them. He crouched. "Yes. Once. He pulled me out of this river, years ago, the same night your papa did."

Milos studied him with those deep-set eyes. "Are you scared of him telling?"

"Yes."

"But he said he'd forget."

"He said he'd forget." Beatrix looked out across the ice, at their road traced in memory from bank to bank, at the willows on the far shore going gold-grey in the failing light. Hope and dread had become, over these weeks, indistinguishable—the same tightness in the chest, the same sleeplessness, the same taste of copper in the morning. "People say many things on rivers, little one. Your papa taught me that too."

They climbed the bank path as the light died, the boy's hand in his, the ferryman's words walking beside them like a third passenger. *A week, maybe less.* Behind them the Danube groaned once, long and low, like a giant turning in its sleep—or like something vast beginning, at last, to move.

When they reached the estate, there was a carriage standing in the courtyard that Beatrix did not recognize, and a man in a gendarme's greatcoat on the steps, shaking snow from his hat.
