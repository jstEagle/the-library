## 8. The Captain's Net

The warrant arrived on a Tuesday, folded inside a leather dispatch case that Captain Aurel Brandt set down on the Countess Ilona Vay's writing desk as though it were a gift he had brought her from Vienna.

Ilona had been expecting him. She had dressed for the occasion in her grayer wool, the one without ornament, and had arranged her study accordingly: the suffrage petition stacked in plain view on the side table, its signatures bound in blue ribbon, deliberately legible to any man who entered. A woman with nothing to hide invites inspection. It was an old trick of her mother's, who had survived three confiscations by keeping her most valuable things in the open.

"Countess." Brandt did not sit until she gestured. He was younger than his rank suggested—thirty perhaps, with the careful hands of a man who had learned paperwork before he had learned violence, though she suspected he was learning the second now. "You received my letter."

"I receive many letters." She poured coffee herself rather than ring for a servant. Fewer witnesses, fewer tongues. "Yours was among them."

"You did not answer it."

"I rarely answer questions I have already answered in person." She smiled and offered the cup. He did not take it. "You visited in January, Captain. You asked whether I employed a woman's servant seen upon the ice. I told you what I knew then, and I tell you now: my driver is Beatrix Iversen, a Swede by birth, engaged through the agency in Budapest with references I verified myself. On the day in question he took my nephew—" she let the small lie sit there, smooth as river stone, "—for a walk along the levee. Children require air in winter, whatever the gendarmes may believe."

Brandt opened the dispatch case at last and drew out a single sheet, laying it beside the warrant with the tenderness of a priest arranging relics.

"Then you will want to explain this, Countess." It was a photograph, gray and grainy. Two figures on white ice, one tall in a coachman's greatcoat, one small, hand in hand. Taken from the Serbian bank, the caption said. Dated the fourth of February.

Ilona looked at it for exactly as long as a confused aristocrat would look at it—three seconds, no more—and then laughed.

"That is my coachman and my late sister's boy, whom I have just described to you. Captain, if the Empire's security rests on photographs of servants walking children on frozen rivers, then God help the Empire, because the Russians certainly intend to try."

The joke landed where jokes land with career officers: nowhere. Brandt turned the photograph around and tapped the smaller figure.

"This child is registered in Mohács as Milos Stefanovics. Son of Stefan Petrovics, currently held at the county seat on charges under Article One-Seventy-Nine. The child's mother is deceased. The father has no sister in this district." He paused, watching her. "But he does have a sister across the water. In Serbia."

"The river freezes every winter," Ilona said. "People walk on it. I am given to understand this is why God made winters."

"Countess." For the first time he leaned forward, and she saw beneath the procedure something hungrier—the particular zeal of a mediocre man who has finally found a case that might make him. "Harboring the kin of a traitor is itself an offense. Concealing the flight of such kin is a greater one. And obstructing an officer of the Crown—" he touched the warrant, but did not push it toward her, not yet; he wanted her to reach for it, "—is the offense I should most regret charging you with. Your petition. Your trip to Vienna next month. Your name in the papers. All of it becomes very difficult for a woman under investigation. You know how they talk."

There it was. Not the law—the gossip. He thought her vanity was the soft place in the armor, and he was not entirely wrong, which was the humiliating part. Ilona folded her hands and made her voice bored.

"Captain, I have spent eleven years being threatened with how they talk. Proceed."

* * *

He proceeded. By four o'clock there were six gendarmes on the estate, and the arithmetic of mercy had turned against her.

They did not search the house—not yet—but established themselves in the gatehouse with the patient confidence of men who had all the time in the world and knew it. Brandt himself walked the grounds with her housekeeper, noting the coach house, the stables, the laundry yard, making a map of her life in his notebook. Ilona followed at his elbow, narrating, charming, obstructing—one acre at a time—while behind her, in the corner of her mind, a single question beat like a trapped bird:

*Where is the boy?*

She had sent Marta the laundry maid a whispered instruction at two o'clock, when the gendarmes' wagon was still a quarter mile up the drive: *the boy goes in the big hamper, the one with the false bottom your cousin built for the smuggled brandy, and he stays there, and you stay near it.* Milos had gone without a sound. That frightened her more than anything—a six-year-old who had already learned to fold himself into silence like linen into a chest.

At five, they detained Beatrix.

She watched it happen from the study window. They did not cuff him; they were too clever for theater. Two gendarmes simply walked out to where he was mucking the stable—it was his habit, she had learned, to do the low work himself when his thoughts grew loud—and stood on either side of him, and walked him back across the yard to the coach house, and closed the door, and stood outside it. An arrest shaped like a conversation. By supper the whole household would understand that the coachman was in custody, and by morning the village would know, and by Thursday the newspapers, if Brandt wished it.

*And he wishes it,* Ilona thought. *That is the whole design.*

She went down to the coach house at dusk, carrying a covered dish she had no intention of letting anyone believe contained more than soup. The gendarme at the door examined it, found nothing but paprika and bread, and stepped aside with the smug courtesy of a man permitting a lady her sentimental errand.

Inside, one lantern burned. Beatrix sat on the mounting block, his greatcoat still on, his hands quiet in his lap. He looked, she thought, remarkably calm for a man whose life had just been taken apart screw by screw—and then she saw that the calm was the same quality as the stillness of ice under snow, and understood it completely.

"They showed me the photograph," he said quietly. "I told them it was Milos and me on the levee."

"I know. I corroborated it. We are now both liars on the record, which means we are allies on the record, which is the only kind of alliance the Crown understands." She set down the dish. "The boy is in the laundry hamper. Marta is watching him. He has bread and the little tin soldier."

Beatrix closed his eyes for one breath. When he opened them, they were wet, and he did not wipe them, and did not apologize for them either, and Ilona found she respected him more for it than for anything else he had ever done.

"The ice," he said. "It started talking today. Did you hear it? Out past the pilings—that deep groan, like a door in an empty house. That's the current underneath, eating it from below. Three days, maybe four. After that, no crossing until the barges run, and the barges won't run for weeks, and by then—" He stopped.

"By then the inquiry will have moved to Vienna, and Stefan will be transported, and the boy will be a ward of the state, and you will be a deserter in a cell." She said it plainly because he needed it said plainly; kindness, at this hour, would be a sedative, and she required him awake. "Yes. I have done the same sums."

"Then I go tomorrow night."

"You go tomorrow night, and I remain here, and here is the part you will hate." She sat down beside him on the mounting block, wool against wood, close enough that the lantern threw their shadows together on the plank wall. "Brandt has backed me into a corner and I mean to walk out through the wall. Tomorrow afternoon I shall send for the district magistrate—I have dined with his wife for nine years—and I shall demand, formally, in writing, that the warrant be produced with its supporting affidavits, and I shall inform him that any detention on my property without proper process will be answered by my solicitor in Pest, who is, conveniently, also counsel to three deputies. It is all paper, Beatrix. Paper and delay. But paper is the one weapon they assume women cannot lift, so they never see us swing it."

"And while you delay them?"

"While I delay them, you will be a dutiful prisoner. Fed, visible, boring. Until tomorrow night, when Marta takes the last laundry down to the gatehouse for collection—and the hamper goes with it—and somewhere between the yard and the road, a hamper becomes a boy again, and a coachman becomes a fugitive, and the Countess Vay becomes, briefly and unforgivably, a woman who looked away at precisely the wrong moment." She heard her own voice steady and marveled at it; inside, some older, girlish self was screaming at the size of the fall. "I have spent thirty years building a name they cannot touch. Tomorrow I spend it. I find—" and here she did smile, small and fierce, "—I find I am getting a fair price."

Beatrix reached over and took her hand. His palm was rough as rope, and warm.

"Countess—"

"Ilona," she said. "If we are to hang together, you may as well use my Christian name."

* * *

She could not sleep, and so past midnight she stood at her window with the lamp unlit, watching the river.

The moon was nearly full, and the Danube lay white from bank to bank, a road of bone. And it was singing. There was no other word for it—a long, rising moan that came up through the frozen ground and into the stones of the house, swelling and falling, swelling and falling, the sound of a thing immense and patient beginning to move. Down at the landing, the pilings creaked. Somewhere mid-river, with a crack like a rifle shot, a fissure opened and ran fifty yards in the moonlight, a black seam stitching itself across the white.

The ice was breaking. Not tonight, not tomorrow—but soon, days sooner than Beatrix's estimate, days sooner than anyone's.

Below, in the dark yard, the lantern in the coach house window went out, then lit again. Twice. Their signal, arranged at dusk. *All well. Ready.*

Ilona pressed her forehead to the cold glass and did the arithmetic one final time, the only sum that had ever mattered: one petition, one name, one reputation, weighed against one sleeping child folded in a hamper among the linens, breathing through a false bottom built for brandy.

The seam in the ice caught the moonlight and held it.

Tomorrow night, she thought. God help us—it must be tomorrow night, or never.
