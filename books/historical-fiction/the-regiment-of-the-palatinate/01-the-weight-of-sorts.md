## 1. The Weight of Sorts

The type knew her hands better than her own face did.

Wren Joubert sat at the sorting frame in the back room of the Joubert foundry, the lamp turned low to save oil, and let her fingers do what they had done since she was seven years old: read. A ten-point Caslon lowercase, its *a* worn round at the shoulder from a decade of service. A long-s that some apprentice had dropped and chipped, so that it printed a hairline scar down every word that used it — her father had refused to melt it down, saying a letter earned its wounds like a soldier did. She set it aside in the damaged tray without thinking, the way one steps over a crack in a familiar stair.

Outside, on the Rue des Serruriers, boots were coming.

She had learned to count them now, the way other people counted bells. Four men this time, maybe five, the stride of soldiers rather than clerks, and beneath the boot-rhythm the particular dragging scrape of a handcart loaded heavy. Paper. They were taking paper again. The requisition carts had come twice this month already, rolling up from the river depots with their army stamps and their signatures in triplicate, and each time they left the stationers along the street lighter by half their stock, and each time the stationers stood in their doorways afterward wearing the particular expression of men who had been robbed politely and could not even call it robbery because there was a form for it, stamped and sealed, a theft with excellent penmanship.

Wren kept sorting.

That was the discipline her father had drilled into her, or perhaps not drilled but simply demonstrated, day after day, until it became the architecture of her mind: whatever is happening, keep your hands moving through the case. The work does not stop for the world; the world must wait for the work. He had said it once when she was small and the press had broken a bolt and thrown metal across the room, and he had walked calmly into the shrapnel of his own livelihood and begun picking pieces out of the sawdust before anyone else dared move. *The work does not stop.* She heard him say it still, in the voice memory had flattened and made slightly more patient than the living man had ever quite managed to be.

Two years dead now. Two years since the fever took him in eleven days, from a strong man to a stranger to a name on a parish register, and left her the foundry, the press, the house above it, and — though the notary had smiled when he said it, a kind smile, an apologetic smile — the debts.

She sorted on. The *e*s went into their compartment. The *n*s. The *r*s, which she always sorted last because they were her favorite, the little shoulder of them, the way they leaned forward as if eager to be printed.

* * *

By midmorning the requisition was finished and the street had resumed its ordinary noises — the knife-grinder's wheel, a woman arguing cheerfully with a fishmonger about the honesty of eels — and Wren allowed herself to climb the narrow stair to the front of the shop and look out through the diamond panes at the aftermath. The cart stood at the corner, half-loaded. A sergeant with a red-trimmed collar was making a show of consulting his list while a stationer named Aubert stood beside his own empty shelves with his hat in his hands, turning it, turning it.

She knew the arithmetic Aubert was doing. She did it herself every week now, in the ledger with the cracked spine, in her father's old shorthand that no clerk would recognize as accounts: what remained, what it cost, how long it lasted. Paper by the ream. Lead by the pound. Oil, leather, ink — the ink was the worst of it, good printing ink gone nearly impossible to buy at any price a struggling shop could pay, so that she stretched what Father had left, thinning it, and the last job she'd printed had come off the press gray as dishwater, and the customer had looked at it and said nothing, which was worse than complaint.

Debts. The word again. Three hundred and forty livres to the paper merchant alone, and the interest compounding with the patience of frost cracking stone. She had taken work she would once have refused — broadsides for the wine merchants, playbills, a funeral notice for a family who paid in eggs — and still the ledger's left column grew faster than the right, the way water finds the low places.

And yet.

She pressed her palm flat against the cold glass of the window and looked down at her own shop — hers, whatever the creditors believed, hers — at the great iron press squatting in the middle of the floor like a sleeping animal, at the composing stones, the galley racks, the cases upon cases of type in their wooden cities, each compartment labeled in her father's handwriting, the letters of his labels curling in that way he had of making a *g* look like it was about to change its mind. Forty years of craft stacked to the rafters. Her inheritance. Her prison. Both, and she had stopped trying to separate them.

Below, the sergeant folded his list away and clapped Aubert on the shoulder in a gesture meant to look like comradeship. The cart creaked away toward the river. Aubert put his hat back on and went inside and closed his door, and the street pretended nothing had happened, which was the century's chief accomplishment, Wren thought. It could make anything disappear if everyone agreed to look elsewhere at the same moment.

* * *

In the afternoon she did what she did every Sunday afternoon and most evenings besides, though she never called it by any name that might frighten it: she took inventory of what could be carried.

It had started, she supposed, the month after the funeral, when the first creditor had come not to argue but simply to stand in the shop doorway and look around slowly, the way a man looks over livestock he intends to bid on. She had lain awake that night doing sums of a different kind. Not what the shop was worth. What *she* could carry. If they came for her too — and the thought arrived without drama, the way weather arrives, simply a fact of the season — what would go into her arms in the quarter hour she might be given?

The matrices came first, always. Not all of them; the full cabinet weighed more than a horse could want to pull. But the letterpress matrices, the ones cut by her grandfather and improved by her father, the punches and matrices for the Joubert text face that three generations of Alsatian printers had set their books in — those fit in a satchel. She knew because she had measured. She had actually packed the satchel, once, at two in the morning, and unpacked it before dawn, ashamed, and then done it again the following month because shame, she had discovered, does not survive contact with dread. Dread always wins the second round.

Then the ledger — no. The ledger stayed. Let them read the debts; debts were honest at least. But the small notebook in her apron pocket, the one with the casting recipes in her father's cipher, the alloy proportions, the mold temperatures written as temperatures were written by men who had no thermometers, only hands and eyes and generations of burned fingertips — that went in the satchel. That was worth more than the lead itself. Metal could be requisitioned. Knowledge had to be carried out by hand or it died.

Her mother's ring. There was no reason for it to be on the list; it was worth almost nothing, the garnet clouded, the band thin as conscience. It went on the list anyway, near the top. Some items earn their place by weight of another kind.

And then, always last, the drawer.

She stood now in the back room, lamplight coming up gold as the afternoon failed outside, and looked at it: the locked drawer in the bottom of her father's writing desk, the one key he had kept on his person and which had never been found among his effects, so that the drawer had sat shut for two years like a held breath. Oak, iron-bound, unremarkable. She had tried the obvious keys. She had considered, more than once, the crowbar, and more than once decided against it — not from reverence exactly, or not only. From something closer to fear. Her father had been a straightforward man in all things she had ever witnessed: blunt in business, plain in speech, incapable of flattery. And yet he had kept one locked thing, and had never once, in twenty-six years of sharing a roof and a trade and a thousand evenings of talk about kerning and alloys and the dishonesty of customers, mentioned that it existed.

Men lock up either treasures or evidence. She had never decided which possibility frightened her more.

The soldiers' cart was long gone. Somewhere across the city, in offices that smelled of sealing wax, men were adding numbers to lists, and somewhere a historian — she'd read the announcement in the *Gazette*, the official history of the Palatine affair, commissioned, subsidized, soon to be bound and placed in every library in the province — was setting down the final version of events, the version that would outlast everyone who remembered otherwise. Ink becoming law. She understood that mechanism better than most. She had spent her life watching words turn solid. Type was language given weight, and weight, once cast, did not forgive.

*The Regiment of the Palatinate.* Even the title had the sound of a door closing.

She banked the lamp, checked the forge was cold, and climbed the stairs to the rooms above, where the inventory of the carryable lived in her head, ordered and rehearsed: satchel, matrices, notebook, ring, and — her hand paused on the banister — and the crowbar, after all, tomorrow, perhaps, for the drawer. Or not. She had said *tomorrow, perhaps* for seven hundred tomorrows now.

Down in the dark shop, the type waited in its cases, forty years of letters standing ready to say anything at all, and said nothing.

* * *

She woke past midnight to the sound of knocking.

Not the creditors' knock — she knew that one, apologetic, arrhythmic, a knuckle dragged reluctantly across wood. This was firm. Measured. The knock of a man who expected the door to open because doors opened for him.

Wren sat up in the cold dark, heart going hard, and ran the possibilities the way she ran the sorting frame: gendarmes checking lamps after curfew, no, too early for the patrol; a night message, possible, printers got those, death notices wanted by dawn; a drunk, unlikely, drunks knocked without measure. She rose, pulled her father's old coat over her shift, took the candle, and stood a moment at the top of the stairs listening.

The knock came again. Three strikes, pause, two. Patient. Certain.

Through the landing window she could see the street below, washed in the color of pewter, and a figure standing at her door. A traveler by the cut of him — road dust on a long coat, a satchel of his own riding his hip the way hers would ride hers, if it ever came to that. He was looking up at the windows as he waited, reading the dark building the way she read a page of type, and when the candlelight touched the glass he saw her, and he did not look away and did not wave. He only lifted one hand slightly, as if to say: *I know you can see me. I will wait.*

Everything in her that had spent two years cataloguing escape routes went quiet, and everything older than that — the part of her that was her father's daughter, that had never once in her life left a customer standing at the door — put its hand on the latch.

She went down. She lit the shop lamp first, because her father had taught her never to receive anyone in a darkness you hadn't chosen yourself, and then she drew back the bolt.

The man stepped in out of the cold, bringing the road with him — wet wool, woodsmoke, the river far upstream somewhere in his clothes — and set his satchel down on her floor with the care of a man setting down something that mattered more than his luggage.

"Mademoiselle Joubert." His voice was hoarse, unused, a voice that had traveled a long way between sentences. "Forgive the hour. I was told you keep your father's foundry, and I was told—" he stopped, steadied himself, and she saw now that under the dust and the weariness his eyes were the eyes of a man carrying something breakable, "—I was told you are the only printer in Strasbourg who can match an old hand."

"Whose hand?" she asked.

He looked at her for a long moment, and then he bent to the satchel, and began to unbuckle it.
