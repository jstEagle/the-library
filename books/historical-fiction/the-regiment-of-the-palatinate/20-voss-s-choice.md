## 20. Voss's Choice

The letter of reinstatement arrived on the twenty-ninth of December, carried by a courier who had ridden through sleet for three days and who looked at Aurel Voss the way men look at a house that has burned and been rebuilt badly — with curiosity, and a little pity.

Voss read it standing in the doorway of the safehouse at Erbach, letting the cold come in over his boots. The paper was good stock, heavier than anything he had touched in two years. The seal was intact, the wax a deep arterial red. *Captain Aurel Voss is hereby restored to full rank and honors, all charges struck from the record, the regiment to be reformed under his command in the spring.* Somewhere below the formal language, in the spaces between the phrases, he could hear the voice of the ministry that had written it: *we are sorry in the way that costs nothing; we will make you whole; please go away now.*

He held it a long time without reading it twice. Once was enough. He had spent two years learning to read documents the way Wren Joubert read type — not for what they said but for what they were made of, what hands had shaped them, what weight they would bear when set against others. This one was made of absolution. It weighed almost nothing at all.

Behind him, in the low room that smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool, Elias Brandt was mending a boot with an awl and waxed thread, working the leather with the same patient violence he brought to everything. Voss watched him a moment before speaking. There was a scar along Elias's forearm where the cat had opened him eleven times on Voss's behalf — a flogging meant for a captain, taken by a sergeant because the sergeant had stepped forward before anyone could stop him. Voss had counted each stroke from the square, forbidden to move, forbidden even to close his eyes. Eleven. He had never thanked him. Some debts are too large for thanks; they can only be paid forward or carried.

"They've cleared me," Voss said.

Elias did not look up. "I know. I read it when I took it from the courier."

"And?"

"And nothing." The awl went through the sole with a small grinding sound. "It's your name. Your choice." A pause, and then, quieter: "Though I'll say the men would stand for you again. What's left of them. Half the Palatine boys who survived are scattered through the garrisons like seed corn, and every one of them knows your handwriting better than his own mother's face, after those letters went round."

Voss came inside and shut the door. The wind outside had a knife's edge to it; it came down off the Odenwald carrying snow that fell as much sideways as down, and the river beyond the millrace was the color of old pewter. Two years in this country had taught him its moods the way a husband learns a wife's — the gray mornings, the sudden rages of weather, the long patient evenings. He knew every beam of this room, every knot in the floorboards, the exact spot where the chimney drew badly and filled the corner with smoke on damp nights. He knew it the way you know a place you have been hiding in. That was not the same as knowing a place you live in.

He sat across from Elias and laid the letter on the table between them, face up, seal broken, like a card turned over in a game already lost.

"Read it aloud," he said.

Elias wiped his hands on his trousers and took it up. His voice was a soldier's voice, flat and clear, built for orders given across the noise of drill. He read the whole thing through — the restoration, the honors, the reformed regiment, the phrase *the unfortunate events of the summer of '97*, which was how the ministry now described the day when four hundred petitioners had come down the road to the garrison with bread and petitions and gone home fewer than they came, shot down not by the Palatines who refused the order but by the Marburg grenadiers who did not, and blamed afterward on the men who would not fire. When he finished, Elias set the letter down and looked at it as if it might bite.

"'Unfortunate events,'" he said. "Four hundred souls, and it reads like weather."

"It reads like history," Voss said. "Which is worse. Weather at least doesn't pretend to remember."

* * *

That evening they walked down to the river. It was a habit they had fallen into without discussing it, two men who had learned in the army that grief moves easier when the legs are moving too. The ice was forming at the edges in white lacework; the middle still ran black and fast. On the far bank a fisherman was pulling in his nets by lantern light, and the light swung and steadied, swung and steadied, like something breathing.

"I've been thinking about the Atlantic," Voss said.

Elias said nothing. He was good at silence; he had been Voss's best at it since the first year, when silence between officers and sergeants was either a wall or a door and you learned quickly which men could make it a door.

"There's a shipping agent in Mannheim," Voss went on. "A Hessian, half-crippled, who owes me from before the war. He says there's a brig out of Rotterdam in February, bound for Philadelphia, taking passengers if they can pay and don't ask questions. Land out there is nearly free. They want farmers more than they want soldiers." He turned a stone over with his boot, considering it, then let it lie. "I've been thinking about whether a man can plant things after what he's seen done with fields."

"You could stay," Elias said. "You could take the command. Reform the regiment under your own hand. Make it what it should have been."

"With whose men? Whose colors?" Voss heard his own voice come out harder than he intended, and made himself slow down. "Elias. You stood in that square. You know what a regiment is. It isn't a thing you reform. It's a thing that reforms you — grinds you down until the shape it leaves is the shape it wants. I gave that machine eleven years of my life and it asked me to fire on bakers and schoolteachers, and when I wouldn't, it used my name to bury the ones who died. Now it writes me a pretty letter and offers me the machine back, polished, with my name engraved on it." He looked out at the black water. "Some men belong to no regiment after what regiments have done. I'm one of them. I don't say it proudly. I say it the way you'd say a man belongs to no church after what was done in the church's name. It's not a wound talking. It's just arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," Elias repeated. There was something in his tone — not quite argument, not quite agreement. "And the dead? Does arithmetic cover them? Margarethe Falk's husband hanged for a mutiny he helped prevent. The names we put into print. You cleared them and then you leave?"

"I cleared them so they could rest," Voss said. "Not so I could stand guard over their graves until I'm buried among them. There's a difference between duty and haunting, Elias, though I grant it took me two years and a river crossing to learn it." He paused. The lantern on the far bank had gone out; the fisherman was a shape now, and then less than a shape. "While I ran, while I hid, living was a debt I owed them. Every morning I woke and the waking itself was owed. But the account's settled now. The truth is in type. It crossed the Rhine in a peddler's cart and it will not be recalled — you told me yourself the pamphlets are being read aloud outside the garrison, that the inspector resigned rather than burn them. A man can't haunt a thing that's walking on its own legs. All I'd be, staying, is a monument. And monuments are for cities. Cities are for men who can look at what the city remembers."

"And America remembers nothing," Elias said. "Is that the attraction?"

"The attraction," Voss said, "is that it remembers nothing *yet*. Everything over there is unwritten. Unset type, if you like — a whole continent of loose sorts waiting in the case. A man could put his hands in it and make something that hasn't already been decided for him." He smiled, and felt how strange the motion was in his own face, like a tool long disused. "You've been spending too much time around printers. You're starting to talk like one."

"I'm starting to talk like someone who's about to lose an argument," Elias said, "and knows it."

* * *

They climbed back up through the village as the last light failed. Snow had begun again, fine and dry, ticking against the shutters. In the safehouse Voss lit the lamp and began, without quite deciding to, to pack.

There was not much. That was the lesson of two years underground, and he had learned it well: a man traveling under a false name owns only what he can deny owning. A spare shirt. A razor. The Bible he had never once opened but carried anyway, because a man without luggage draws eyes. And at the bottom of the chest, wrapped in oilcloth, the things that mattered: a lock of hair from a sister dead ten years; a button off the coat of Sergeant Falk, given to him by the widow with a handshake that had nearly broken his fingers; and the letters themselves — not the printed copies, which belonged to the world now, but his own drafts, in his own hand, the words as they had come out of him at three in the morning in this very room, written fast and ugly and true. He weighed them in his palm. Paper was a country; Elias had said that once, quoting the printer woman, and Voss had thought it a figure of speech. It was not. It was the plainest fact of the age. Armies moved borders; paper moved everything else.

He heard Elias stop in the doorway behind him and knew, without turning, what the man was seeing: a captain packing exile into a chest no bigger than a coffin lid.

"When do you tell the others?" Elias asked.

"There are no others. There's you, and there's a letter I'll write to the widow, and there's whatever the ministry chooses to believe." He closed the chest and sat on it, as if to keep it shut by weight alone. "I'll cross at Mannheim after the new year. The agent says the roads will be quiet then — everyone drunk, everyone watching the century die. Fitting, I think. Let the old hundred years carry me out with the rest of its rubbish."

"You could wait for spring."

"If I wait for spring I'll find reasons to wait for autumn." Voss looked up at him. In the lamplight Elias looked older than his years, the way soldiers did, the way all of them did now; the whole generation had been aged like wood left out in weather. "Elias. Sit down. There's something I want to say while my courage's fresh, because courage goes stale faster than bread."

Elias sat on the stool by the hearth. The fire had burned down to a red heart, and the light moved on his face.

"Eleven strokes," Voss said. "I counted them. I stood at attention in that square and I counted every stroke of the cat on your back, and I have never said thank you, because thank you is a word for borrowed horses and passed salt, and what you did wasn't in that country at all. You took my punishment. You didn't know then that I'd live to repay it, and neither did I. And then two years later you walked into a printer's shop in Strasbourg with my letters in your coat and a stranger's name in your mouth, and you spent everything you had — your safety, your trade, very nearly your neck — to make sure the record said what it should say." He stopped. His throat had gone tight, and he waited it out the way you wait out pain in an old wound, breathing shallowly until it passes. "I won't insult you with gratitude. But I'll tell you what I've decided, and you can carry it back across the Rhine with you, because I think you're the kind of man who needs cargo."

"Say it, then."

"Aurel Voss died in the summer of '97. The ministry killed him when they pinned the massacre on his regiment; the historians finished the job when they set the lie in type. The man sitting in front of you is nobody — a farmer going to Pennsylvania, name of Vogel, no history, no debts. He'll plant wheat. He'll probably be bad at it for the first three years. And on certain nights — I know myself, Elias, I'll know exactly which nights — he'll take out these letters and read them by candlelight and remember that somewhere across the water there's a press with his true account standing in type, and a woman who runs it who burned her own father's name to save strangers', and a sergeant who took a flogging that wasn't his." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the lamplight caught the gray coming in at his temples, hair he had not earned honestly. "That's not homecoming. But it's not nothing. Survival without homecoming — I've decided that's allowed. I've decided a man can carry a country inside him the way the widow carries her husband's note, and never set foot in it again, and still call himself a citizen of it."

The fire ticked. Outside, the snow thickened, erasing the lane, the fences, the dark shapes of the mills, laying the whole world smooth and unprinted.

Elias was quiet a long moment. Then he said, "She'll want to see you before you go. The printer. She and Brandt — " he corrected himself, a ghost of a smile — "*we*. We're coming up for the new year. She said she wanted to shake the hand that wrote the letters, and she strikes me as a woman who gets what she wants."

"She sounds terrifying."

"She is. Wait until she measures you for type. She does it with her eyes. You'll feel your soul being sorted into cases."

Voss laughed — actually laughed, a sound that startled both of them, ringing strange and alive in the low room. Then he reached into the chest and took out one of the draft letters, the first one, the ugliest, the truest, and held it out.

"For her," he said. "Tell her the foundry keeps what the foundry keeps. And tell her — " he searched for it, and found it, and gave it plainly, the way you give a man ammunition — "tell her she was right. Whatever it cost her, whatever it cost her father's memory, she was right to print it whole. Whole or not at all. That's the only honest arithmetic left in this century, and she's the only one I've met who does it without cheating."

Elias took the letter and folded it away inside his coat, over the heart, the way soldiers carry things they intend to deliver or die trying.

"The new year," Voss said, looking at the window, where the snow was building its blank page against the glass. "A hundred years closing like a book. God knows what they'll set on the next page. But at least — " he tapped the folded letter through the cloth of Elias's coat — "at least this line is in type now. At least it can't be unprinted."

Down in the village, faint through the storm, the church bell began to toll the hour, and the sound came up the hill through the falling snow like something being counted — the last days of the old century, each strike a sort dropped into a form that would soon be locked, and broken, and distributed to the winds.
