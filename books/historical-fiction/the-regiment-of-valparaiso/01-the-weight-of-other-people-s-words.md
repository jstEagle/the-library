## 1. The Weight of Other People's Words

The kettle had a voice like a man deciding whether to confess. Theo Underhill knew this because he had spent four years listening to it, four years of mornings in Mariana Fuentes's kitchen on Cerro Alegre, waiting for it to make up its mind while he made up his own.

He set out the tools the way a surgeon lays out instruments: the bone folder, the cake of sealing wax, the brass stamp with the Fuentes crest — a lighthouse, of all things, which Mariana had chosen with the particular irony of a woman who had spent her life signaling to people who refused to look. He laid the morning's letters in a fan across the scarred pine table. Eleven of them. He would read every one before it left this house, and he would reseal each with wax so precisely matched that not even the recipients — bankers, admirals, deputies, one archbishop — would ever know their correspondence had been opened by a twenty-three-year-old man with ink on his cuffs and no legal existence at all.

This was the arrangement. It had been the arrangement since the winter of 1913, when Mariana had found him on her stairs.

He slit the first envelope with the bone folder, one clean stroke, and began to read.

*Señora Fuentes — I write in confidence regarding the matter we discussed at the Almendral reception...*

A deputy's wife, this one, writing about a cousin's debts. Theo filed it in the ledger behind his eyes — *Deputy Ortega's household, insolvent, will vote against the naval appropriation to hide it* — and moved on. The ledger was not written anywhere. It could not be. It lived in the same place his other name lived, that locked room of the skull where he kept everything that could not survive being known.

*...and so I must ask whether the committee will fund the printing of two thousand pamphlets for the Valdivia campaign...*

*...my husband does not know I am writing to you, and if he learned of it I would be ruined, and worse, he would be ruined, and I find I care about both...*

*...the girl is fourteen and there is no one else. You were recommended to me by a woman whose name I will not put in a letter.*

He read them all. He read them the way a priest reads confession, which is to say without judgment and without innocence, and when he had finished he held the last page of each over the kettle's breath until the paper went soft and pliant, and he folded each along its original creases — he was careful about creases; creases were testimony — and slid each home, and dripped the wax, and pressed the lighthouse into it, and held the stamp the full three seconds so the impression would be deep enough to satisfy a suspicious eye and shallow enough to look casual, because casualness, Theo had learned, was the most demanding thing in the world to fake.

The archbishop's letter was the hardest. The man wrote with a heavy hand that gouged the paper, and his seal was a blob of crimson the size of a coin, and Theo had to rebuild it in two pours, tilting the stick of wax so the second melt married the first at the exact temperature of forgiveness. He held the finished envelope to the window light, checking for the seam, and found none, and allowed himself the small nod that was, for Theo Underhill, the equivalent of another man's shout of triumph.

Outside the window, Valparaíso was doing what Valparaíso always did at seven in the morning: arguing with the sea. The harbor lay below the hill like a spilled jewelry case, masts and funnels and the gray shoulders of warships — British, Chilean, one Dutch freighter with a list it had carried since before the war like a limp it had grown fond of. Gulls wheeled and swore. A funicular car groaned up its inclined track, hauling a dozen passengers who had no idea that the letters in the satchel of the young man at the window could unmake at least three of them.

That was the arithmetic Theo performed each morning, the way other men checked the weather. Eleven letters. Eleven levers. He did not pull them. He had never pulled one. But he knew — and the knowing was the thing, the knowing was the whole architecture of his safety — that he *could*. A man who could destroy everyone was a man no one thought to destroy. Invisibility was not the absence of power. It was power wearing a borrowed coat, standing very still.

He had learned that in another life, under another name, in a year he did not speak of even to himself except at three in the morning when the harbor fog came up the hill and stood in the streets like a congregation.

* * *

Mariana Fuentes came down the stairs at half past seven with the unhurried tread of a woman who had decided long ago that rushing was a concession to a world that did not deserve it. She was fifty-eight and built like the prow of a ship, gray hair pinned in a knot that could have survived a broadside, and she wore black not for mourning but for efficiency — black went with everything and forgave everything and made men underestimate her, which she regarded as the single greatest gift fashion had ever bestowed.

"The archbishop," she said, taking the letter he held out.

"Sealed. He'll never know."

"He always knows. He simply can't prove it, and that is the entire difference between us and the government." She broke the seal without ceremony — her own seal, and she opened her own mail in front of him, which was either trust or a demonstration that the seal had never been the point — and read it standing, her lips moving slightly, a habit she would have denied under oath. "He wants to meet. He wants to tell me that women's souls are too fine for the franchise. He has told me this four times. I begin to suspect he is composing a sermon on it and using me for rehearsal."

"He's afraid of you," Theo said.

"Of course he's afraid of me. I'm the only person in Valparaíso who has ever asked him a question he couldn't answer with Latin." She looked at him over the top of the letter, and her eyes did the thing they always did, the quick inventory, cuffs, hands, face, the way a quartermaster checks a soldier. "You slept badly."

"I slept."

"You slept badly. There's a difference, and it shows in your left eye." She set the archbishop down. "The Valdivia pamphlets. Two thousand. The money exists, but it has to move through three hands before it reaches the printer, and one of those hands belongs to a man who talks in his sleep. Read the letter again and tell me if she names him."

Theo didn't need to read it again. "She doesn't. She writes around him. 'Our friend in the customs house.' But she's changed her ink — it's a violet tone, new bottle — and she presses harder on the downstrokes than she did in March. She's frightened."

Mariana regarded him for a long moment with an expression he had never fully learned to translate, something between pride and grief, as if his competence were a wound she was responsible for. "You read a woman's fear out of her penmanship."

"I read everyone out of their penmanship. It's the only place they tell the truth. People think a letter is a message." He gathered the sealed envelopes into the satchel, arranging them by route — the climb to the Almendral first, then the flat, then the customs house last, when the clerks had gone to lunch and the letterboxes could be serviced without witnesses. "It isn't. It's a confession with the names left out."

"Sometimes," Mariana said, "you sound like a man of sixty who has buried two wives."

"Sometimes I sound like whoever's handwriting I read last."

It was meant as a joke. It came out as something else, and he heard it land between them, and Mariana's face did the thing it did — the flicker, quickly mastered, of a woman remembering a night five years gone, a boy on her stairs, a satchel full of a dead man's papers. She had never asked him directly about the papers. Not once in four years. This restraint was either the deepest kindness he had ever been shown or the most sophisticated trap he had ever stood in, and on some mornings he genuinely could not tell which, and on most mornings he had decided it didn't matter, which was itself a kind of answer.

"The customs house letter," she said, recovering, "goes last. And Theo—" She stopped. Recalibrated. He watched her do it, watched her choose, with the visible discipline of a woman who had spent thirty years choosing her sentences the way he chose his seals. "Come back by four. There's a letter I want you to take personally. To Santiago. There's a train at six."

"A courier could take it."

"A courier could lose it. You won't." She turned to the stairs, then paused, one hand on the banister, and said, without turning around: "The fog will burn off by nine. It always does. Whatever you're brooding about will still be there at nine, so you may as well brood about it in the sun."

Then she was gone up the stairs, and the house settled around him like a ship's crew returning to stations.

* * *

He went out into the morning with the satchel against his hip and the hill under his feet, and Valparaíso received him the way it received everyone: without interest. That was the beauty of the city. It was a port, and ports are machines for not looking at people. Sailors, smugglers, widows, deserters, men with two names and men with none — the city swallowed them all into its staircases and its alleys and its perpetual argument with the wind, and asked nothing, because asking cost money and everyone in Valparaíso was counting.

He took the letters down through the zigzag streets, past the house where the British consul's clerk kept a mistress whose letters Theo had read for two years — she wrote in French, thinking it was safe, and Theo's French was better than the consul's clerk's — past the chandlery whose owner was quietly bankrupt, past the school where the headmistress had written to Mariana last spring in a hand that shook so badly Theo had read the trembling before the words: *they are watching the teachers now, they are watching everyone.*

The ledger turned its pages as he walked. *Consul's clerk, adulterer, malleable. Chandlery, insolvency, will sell information by autumn. Schools under surveillance since the strikes.* Eleven letters this morning, but the ledger held hundreds, years of hundreds, the whole soft underbelly of a city laid out in a young man's memory like a diagram of a body he had never been allowed to have.

He delivered the deputy's wife's letter. He delivered the frightened one about the fourteen-year-old girl — that one went to a doctor on Cerro Bellavista who asked no questions and was paid in a currency Theo preferred not to itemize. He delivered the pamphlet money authorization to the woman who kept the committee's books, a seamstress with forearms like cable who looked at Theo the way one looks at a reliable mule, which he understood and did not resent. Reliability was a kind of love, if you were starving for it. He had learned to eat it.

At the customs house he posted the last letter and stood a moment in the shade of the arcade, watching the harbor.

The warships rode at anchor with the patient menace of furniture. There were more of them every month — the war had made this coast a waiting room, every navy on earth wanting a chair near the door. The sailors came ashore with their money and their loneliness and their accents, and the city took their money and returned the loneliness with interest, and in four years Theo had read the letters home of a hundred of them, bought and sold through Mariana's network, letters written by boys who spelled badly and missed their mothers and described a war none of them had seen except as cargo manifests and casualty lists.

Theo had never seen it either. That was the joke the universe had made of him. He carried a dead man's name and a war he had watched from a continent away, and he had built himself so carefully around that absence that some days he suspected the absence was the only solid thing in him, the keel, the one true plank.

A man rehearsing a life, Mariana had called him once, to his face, in one of her moods. *You do everything correctly, mijo, and correctness is not the same as living. A rehearsal, done well enough, for long enough — one day you'll look up and find you've missed the performance.*

He had told her she was wrong. He had known, telling her, that she was not.

* * *

He was climbing back toward Cerro Alegre at half past three, the Santiago letter in his breast pocket where he could feel it like a second heartbeat, when the funicular let off its passengers at the upper station and one of them did not disperse with the others.

Theo noticed him the way he noticed everything — sideways, without turning his head, the habit of a man whose safety lived in his peripheral vision. A stranger, tall, gaunt in the specific way of men who had been fed by institutions rather than by love, in a coat too good for Valparaíso and too worn for Santiago. He stood at the station railing looking out at the harbor with the fixed attention of a man who was not looking at the harbor at all.

The stranger turned. His eyes found Theo with an accuracy that raised the hair on Theo's neck, because the street was full of people and the stranger had not been looking at the street.

"Señor." The voice was English, flattened by years of service into something that had given up on its vowels. "You are the one who carries letters for the widow Fuentes."

"I carry letters," Theo said, in Spanish, "for many people."

"You carry hers." The stranger came down the steps toward him, and Theo saw that his left hand was gloved and did not move, and that he held an envelope in his right, and that he held it the way men hold things they are afraid of. "I have a delivery. A private one. It requires... discretion. I was told you were discreet."

"Who told you?"

"A person who knows your work." The stranger stopped one pace away — close enough to speak low, far enough not to touch, a distance Theo recognized as trained. "The fee is whatever you judge it to be. The address is the difficulty."

He held out the envelope.

Theo took it, because not taking it would have been a confession of something, and read the direction, and the world did the thing it had not done in five years: it tilted.

The address was in English. The address was in a hand Theo knew better than his own — better than his own, because his own he had invented, but this one he had *learned*, stroke by stroke, year by year, copying it at midnight in a condemned workshop until he could produce it dreaming. A hand with a forward lean like a man hurrying, a crossed t like a sword cut, an *E* built in three strokes where any sane hand used two.

The envelope was addressed to **Captain Elias Underhill**, and beneath the name, in the same confident hand, as though the writer had never heard of Gallipoli, as though the sea had never closed over that name like a door:

*Hospicio de Convalecientes, Camino a Melipilla, Santiago.*

The harbor went on arguing with the sea. The gulls went on swearing. A funicular car groaned on its cable somewhere below, hauling its indifferent passengers up and down the face of the hill, and Theo Underhill stood in the sun on Cerro Alegre holding a letter addressed to a dead man in the dead man's own hand, and heard himself say, from a very great distance:

"Captain Underhill is dead."

"Yes," the stranger said, and his face did something complicated. "That is what I came to discuss."
