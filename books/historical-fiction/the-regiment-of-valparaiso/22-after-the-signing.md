## 22. After the Signing

The bells began at dawn, and by seven they had not stopped.

Theo stood at the window of the workshop — *his* workshop now, the deed signed two days ago with ink that had still been wet when the first telegram arrived — and watched Valparaíso come apart in the streets below. Not violently. Joyfully. Sailors climbed the lampposts on the Calle Blanco, waving caps they could not afford to lose. Women leaned from balconies and threw flowers down onto the hats of strangers. A man played an accordion badly outside the customs house, and no one minded, because the whole city was playing something worse and calling it music.

The armistice was signed. The war that had killed Elias Underhill on paper four years ago, and un-killed him last month in a hospice outside Santiago, was over.

Theo pressed his palm flat against the cold glass and let himself feel it — not triumph, exactly. Something quieter. The sensation of a held breath finally released, so slowly he hadn't noticed he'd been holding it for years.

Behind him, the workshop smelled of cedar shavings and fresh plaster. The navy's demolition order had been rescinded in October, buried under the weight of everything Delacroix's resignation had dragged into the light: a memorial archive, the decree read, to house the restored records of the *Valparaíso Inquiry*, open to the public, administered by one T. Underhill. He had read his own name in the government gazette over breakfast and laughed until Mariana had come to see what was wrong with him.

Nothing was wrong with him. That was what was strange.

He turned back into the room. The long tables where he had spent three years hunched over water-stained pages now stood empty, scrubbed, waiting for their public. Along the far wall, glass-fronted cases held the first arranged exhibits: the deserter file, open to the page where Captain Elias Underhill had written — in a hand Theo knew better than his own — *I recommend posthumous restoration of rank and honor for Private Tomás Vergara, whose conduct throughout was beyond reproach.* Lucía had stood before that case for an hour yesterday without speaking. When she left, she had touched the glass once, lightly, the way you touch a wound to confirm it has closed.

Tomás Vergara. Restored. His name read aloud in a courthouse while his daughter sat in the front row with her spine straight as a mast. Theo thought of her often now — more than he admitted, even to himself. The way she had mistaken him for a ghost and embraced him anyway. The way she had learned, faster than he did, the difference between the man she'd imagined and the man standing in front of her, and chosen the man.

* * *

Mariana arrived at ten, carried up the cliff road in a hired motorcar she distrusted on principle. She came through the door already talking, which meant she was happy; when Mariana Fuentes was unhappy she went silent as a locked drawer.

"—and the *Mercurio* wants a serial, twelve installments, on the inquiry, and I told them absolutely not unless a woman writes it, and they agreed, which shows you how far we've come, or how tired they are of fighting us, which amounts to the same thing." She set down a basket of rolls and looked around the room with the appraising eye she usually reserved for enemy fortifications. "You've hung the photographs crooked."

"They're level."

"They're crooked. Everything in this building leans toward the sea, like it's trying to escape." She crossed to the window and stood beside him, small and straight-backed, watching the celebration below. For a while neither of them spoke. Below, someone had begun singing, and a hundred voices took it up raggedly.

"Five years ago," she said at last, "you walked into my kitchen half-starved, carrying a satchel of papers you were supposed to burn, and you lied to me about who you were within the first minute. Badly, I might add. You said your name was Theo and then flinched when I asked for the rest."

"I remember."

"I kept the papers and I kept the boy, and I told myself it was politics. Preservation of evidence. Strategic value." Her voice was dry, but her hand found his sleeve and stayed there. "It took me an unconscionably long time to admit I simply didn't want to lose you. Mothers are permitted a certain amount of cowardice in their reasoning. I used all of mine on you."

"You weren't a coward. You hid the workshop's location from me for a month while they were hunting us."

"And you never once asked me directly where it was, because you understood I needed you to trust me more than you needed to know." She smiled faintly. "We were both lying to each other out of love. It's the family disease."

Theo looked at her — this woman who had built a movement out of other people's secrets and given a stranger her own — and felt the ledger in his mind, the old catalog of borrowed names and guarded truths, quietly close itself. He would keep other people's words for a living still. But they would be given to him now, not taken.

"The gazette says the archive opens in March," he said.

"The gazette also said the war would end by Christmas 1914. Read the gazette skeptically." She picked up her basket, then paused at the door. "Elias telegraphed again?"

"This morning. From Santiago. He says the doctors are talking about letting him travel by spring." Theo hesitated. "He says he'd like to see the sea. He doesn't remember the sea, Mariana. He grew up on this coast and he doesn't remember it."

"Then you'll bring him to it," she said simply. "You're good at giving men back their names. It seems only fair you should give him back the view that goes with it."

She left. The door swung shut behind her, and Theo stood alone in the room that had almost been demolished, holding the morning like something breakable.

* * *

In the afternoon he cleared the last crate from his desk — a naval-issue writing table salvaged from the demolition pile, its legs scarred where the workmen's crowbars had tested it and found it stubborn. On the blotter lay a single sheet of good paper, and beside it his pen, and beside the pen nothing at all: no alias, no dead man's letters to answer, no fiction to maintain.

For five years he had written as other people. As the courier who sealed and re-sealed Mariana's correspondence. As the invented correspondent who comforted a grieving fiancée with words in a dead officer's hand. As Elias Underhill, forged and then genuine, authorized at last to speak in a living man's name. Every letter had been a mask worn so long he had forgotten there was a face beneath.

Now the paper waited, blank, and there was no one to be but himself.

He thought about what Renn had told him once, drunk on cheap pisco in the ruins of the workshop, the night after the fire: *A name's just a coat, mate. Question is whether you stole it or bought it.* Renn had gone north afterward, to Coquimbo, of all places — to look, he said, for a childhood Theo might have actually had. Whether he'd find it, whether it mattered, Theo no longer knew. Some inheritances you're born to. Some you choose. He had stopped being able to tell the difference, and then he had stopped needing to.

He dipped the pen.

*Dear Lucía,*

He stopped. Looked at the two words. In another life — in almost any month of the past year — he would have hesitated here, calculating tone, weighing what the recipient expected of the name at the bottom. But Lucía Vergara had never received a letter from him before. There was no precedent to forge against. Whatever he wrote would be the first true document in his own archive.

*Dear Lucía,*

*The bells have not stopped since dawn, and the harbor is full of ships sounding their horns like geese, and I have spent the day pretending to arrange exhibits while actually watching the door.*

*Your father's file is in the third case from the window. I moved it there myself, so that the morning light falls on the page where he is restored. If you come tomorrow, come early. The light is better then, and the tourists haven't arrived, and we can stand in front of it together for as long as you want, saying nothing, which I have learned is a thing we do well.*

*I am signing this letter with my own name. You will notice it is the same name I have always had, and entirely different. Elias wrote to me last week that he is learning to remember, the way a man learns to walk again — badly, with help, falling often. He asked me to tell you that he remembers your father's face. He says it is the first thing that came back, and that it came back smiling. I believe him. I have spent years learning to detect a forged hand, and his is not forged. Grief made it strange for a while, but it is his.*

*The archive opens in March. There is a position here for a keeper of records, if you want it. The pay is poor and the hours are worse and the work consists mostly of protecting other people's words from the people who would rather they were lost. I can think of no one better suited. I can think of no one I would rather sit across a table from, these next forty years, doing it.*

*Come to Valparaíso. The city is celebrating a war it survived. Come and celebrate something smaller and harder: a name given back, a letter answered at last, a man who was nobody choosing, today, to sign himself.*

He read it through twice. It was imperfect. It said more than he intended in some places and less than he felt in others, which he supposed was the definition of every honest letter ever written.

Then he signed it.

*Theo Underhill.*

Not the captain's brother. Not the widow's courier. Not the ghost's understudy. His own hand — the one he had taught himself, stroke by stroke, in a condemned workshop below the cliffs, copying a dead man's letters until the copy became a self and the self became real. The ink caught the afternoon light coming off the harbor, and for a moment the wet signature gleamed like something freshly christened.

Below the stairs, the bells were still ringing. Somewhere out on the water, a ship was sounding its horn — long, steady, arriving.

He folded the letter, addressed it, and set it by the door to go out with the morning post.

Then he opened the window, and let the sound of the world come in.

* * *

That night, unable to sleep, he walked down through the celebrating streets to the water's edge. The harbor lay black and silver under a sky still trembling with fireworks from the hills. On the mole, a figure stood looking out at the anchored fleet — a tall man in a long coat, hatless, the firelight from a brazier catching the ruined left side of his face.

Elias had said spring. Elias had lied, or the doctors had relented, or the man had simply walked out of the hospice and onto a ship because some part of him, deeper than memory, had known the way home.

Theo stopped a few paces away. The other man did not turn, but he spoke, and his voice was roughened by disuse and distance, and it was — unmistakably, impossibly — the voice of all those letters.

"They told me there'd be a keeper of the archive," Elias said. "They told me he'd know my handwriting better than I do." He turned then, slowly, and the scarred face and the unscarred eye regarded Theo with something between recognition and wonder. "I've read the file. I've read the letters. Mine, and yours, and I can't always tell them apart." A pause. The harbor boomed softly beneath them. "So I've come to ask the only man alive who'd know. Which ones were really me?"

Theo looked out at the black water, at the ships riding at anchor, at the city burning with light above them both — and reached into his coat for the letter he had decided, an hour ago, not to send until morning.
