## 1. The Letters I Shouldn't Have Read

The kettle was the oldest thing Rosalind Amberdale owned, and it had never once been used for tea.

He set it on the spirit stove in his room at the top of Mrs. Brandt's boarding house on Zawalna Street — a narrow room that smelled of coal smoke and cheap soap and, if you knew what you were smelling, of glue. From the window he could see the slate roofs of Vilna running downhill toward the river, chimneys breathing their evening gray into a sky the color of unpolished pewter. October was dying. Somewhere below, a cart driver cursed his horse in three languages, which was about right for this city.

Rosalind laid out his tools on the desk with the care of a surgeon and the shame of a man who had stopped apologizing to himself some years ago. The kettle. A shallow dish of water. A bone folder, thin as a knife's promise. A stack of clean blotting paper. And the letters themselves — seven of them tonight, taken from the leather satchel that sat, unguarded and unsuspected, on the hall table downstairs.

They were not his letters. That was the whole point of them.

He worked quickly, because quick work left fewer marks. Each envelope went into the steam until the gum surrendered its grip — ten seconds, fifteen, no more — and then the bone folder slid along the seam like a whisper along a wall, and the flap lifted without so much as a sigh. He read standing up. Standing made him faster, and speed was mercy; a letter held too long under lamplight began to feel like a guest who wouldn't leave.

The first was from a grain merchant in Grodno to a man named Feliks Dorn, who occupied the second-floor front room and called himself an exporter. It concerned freight rates. It also concerned, in a postscript written in a different hand, the movement of two hundred rifles through Białystok, disguised as agricultural machinery. Rosalind read it twice, closed his eyes, and put it away in the cabinet of his memory, third shelf, behind the tariff schedules where no one would think to look.

The second letter was a love letter. Married man, woman in Warsaw, the usual architecture of guilt and longing. He skimmed it, felt the old familiar nothing, filed it.

The third made him sit down.

It was addressed to Herr Doktor Weiss, who taught at the gymnasium and tutored the sons of men who mattered. The handwriting was small and precise, the kind of hand that had learned to write quickly in rooms where slowness cost money. *The list is confirmed,* it said. *Six names. The arrests will come after the harvest festival, when the garrison has its orders and the newspapers have their distraction. Your fee as agreed. Do not write again.*

Rosalind read it four times. Six names. He did not know the names — they were not given — but he knew what the sentence meant, the way a man who has never been struck still knows what a fist looks like. Someone would be taken after the harvest festival. Someone would be made into an example, and someone else would be paid for arranging it.

His hand hovered over the letter. The stove ticked. Downstairs, Mrs. Brandt was arguing with the milk boy about the price of cream, her voice rising through the floorboards like water through a cracked foundation.

*Do something.*

The thought arrived on schedule, the way it always did, punctual as a train. And Rosalind did what he always did with it: he watched it pass through him and go on down the line without stopping at his station. Because here was the arithmetic he had built his life upon, the sum he performed every night and never got wrong: a man who reads letters and does nothing is invisible. A man who reads letters and acts becomes a name on somebody's list — perhaps even one of six. He had seen what happened to names on lists. Everyone in this city had seen it, or had carefully arranged not to.

He resealed the letter with a touch of paste thinned to exactly the right consistency, pressed the flap flat beneath blotting paper, and set it back in the satchel in its original position, second from the left, corner aligned with the others. Then he carried the satchel downstairs and placed it on the hall table, precisely where it had been, and went back up to his room, and lay on his bed with his boots off and his coat on, because a man in his trade slept ready to move.

*I never intervene,* he told the ceiling. *That's the whole of it. I'm a reader, not a writer. Nothing I do changes a single word in a single letter.*

The ceiling, being a ceiling, did not point out that knowing a thing and doing nothing about it was itself a kind of action. Rosalind already knew that too. He simply kept it on a shelf he didn't open.

* * *

Miss Havel came into his life the way weather comes into a valley — suddenly, entirely, and with a change in pressure you felt before you understood it.

She arrived at Mrs. Brandt's on a Tuesday in November, having taken the entire second floor for herself and her secretary, and within a day the whole house was talking about her in the lowered voices people reserve for opera singers, defrocked priests, and women who give speeches in public squares. Her name was Miss Havel, first name unoffered. She was perhaps forty, tall, gray at the temples in a way that looked decided rather than inevitable, and she wore black not as mourning but as uniform. She was, Mrs. Brandt confided over supper, a suffragist. A prominent one. The kind whose photograph appeared in the papers next to words like *agitation* and *disorder*, printed by editors who hoped the spelling would protect them.

"She'll want a driver," Mrs. Brandt said, ladling out barley soup with the air of a woman distributing prophecy along with dinner. "A woman like that doesn't take the tram."

Rosalind kept his eyes on his soup. "No," he said. "I suppose she doesn't."

He had, in fact, driven for hire in Vilna for six years — first horses, then the new machines, which he liked better because a motor did not spook and could not be bribed to remember your face. He was known at the livery houses as steady, silent, and expensive only when he could afford to be. He had driven magistrates' clerks and timber brokers and once, for a memorable afternoon, a bishop who talked the entire time about the moral dangers of cycling. He had never driven anyone whose enemies might be armed.

Which is why, when the note came up to his room two days later — heavy paper, a crest he didn't recognize, four lines requesting his presence at eleven o'clock sharp — he read it three times before answering, and spent the interval telling himself that coincidence was coincidence, and that a boarding house landlady recommending her steadiest lodger was the most ordinary transaction in the world.

Miss Havel received him in a sitting room that had been converted, in the space of seventy-two hours, into something between an office and a field headquarters. Maps of the city hung on the walls, marked with pins. Pamphlets stood in stacks like cordwood. A young woman with ink-stained fingers typed in the corner with the rhythmic violence of someone shelling walnuts. And Miss Havel herself stood at the window with her hands clasped behind her back, watching the street below with the expression of a general counting the enemy's tents.

"Mr. Amberdale." She turned. Her handshake was brief and dry and entirely without performance. "Mrs. Brandt tells me you're discreet."

"I'm a driver, madam."

"Everyone is something ordinary. Discreet is what they are underneath it." She studied him the way he studied envelopes — quickly, thoroughly, missing nothing. "I hold meetings. I speak in public. I receive correspondence from people whose names I do not know and whose faces I will never see, and some of those people are followed home. I need a man who drives well, notices everything, repeats nothing, and asks no questions he isn't prepared to hear answered."

"And if I ask questions anyway?"

Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile; more the place where a smile would be stationed if it were ever needed. "Then I'll know you're honest, and I'll answer the ones worth answering. Can you start Thursday?"

He should have said no. He understood this even then, with the clarity of a man watching his own foot slide toward ice. A low profile was a possession, and he had guarded his for years the way misers guard gold — and here was a woman made entirely of profile, high and bright and conspicuous, asking him to stand beside her in full view of a city that photographed its troublemakers for the police gazette.

"Yes," said Rosalind Amberdale. "Thursday."

Later — much later, when he understood what she had actually been hiring, and why, and what it would cost — he would wonder whether she had chosen him at all, or whether she had simply recognized, in the quiet man across her sitting room, another person reading the city's mail and pretending not to act on it.

But that was later. On Thursday morning it snowed the season's first dry powder, and Rosalind brought the motor around at eight o'clock sharp, and Miss Havel descended the boarding house steps in black wool and gloves, carrying a leather portfolio thick enough to stop a bullet, and gave him an address near the river.

"The bridge road," she said, settling into the seat beside him. "There's a rally at the mill workers' hall. Take the long way. I want to see who's on the streets today."

He took the long way. He saw the streets: the fish sellers, the garrison patrol outside the bank, two boys sliding on the frozen gutter-water, a priest, a drunk, a dog. Ordinary Vilna, going about its morning.

And then, as they swung onto the bridge road with the river glinting gray-green below and the far hills white to their waists, Rosalind saw the man.

He stood at the edge of the road where the trees came down to meet the embankment — gaunt, coatless despite the cold, hat pulled low, hands jammed in his pockets as though holding something there. He did not watch the motor as a passerby watches traffic. He watched it the way a drowning man watches a boat: fixedly, hungrily, with his whole body leaning toward it, and as they passed — close, too close, near enough that Rosalind caught the hollows of his cheeks and the dark stain spreading slowly down the man's left side where the pocket was — his lips moved.

Rosalind heard nothing over the engine. But he read the shape of the words in the rearview mirror, and the shape of them stayed with him all day, and all night, and would stay with him for the rest of his life:

*Wait for me.*
