## 2. A Passenger for the Bridge Road

The motor coughed twice before it caught, which was its way of clearing its throat before a performance, and Rosalind Amberdale sat with his hands on the wheel and let it grumble itself awake in the blue dark of the livery courtyard. Frost had climbed the windscreen in ferns. He scraped a porthole clear with the flat of his thumb and watched Miss Havel come down the steps of the boarding house with her collar turned up and her breath trailing her like a scarf.

"You're early," she said, folding herself into the passenger seat with the economy of a woman who had spent her life being watched and had learned to take up no more room than necessary.

"The roads aren't."

"The roads are a metaphor for everything wrong with this province, Mr. Amberdale. Slow, badly maintained, and maintained anyway by men who profit from the slowness." She settled her satchel on her knees — the satchel he had learned not to look at too directly, though he had long since catalogued its contents by weight and rattle: pamphlets, a petition with four hundred signatures, and a flask of something medicinal that she pretended was for emergencies.

He said nothing. He was very good at saying nothing. It was, in fact, the only marketable skill he had ever cultivated, and he had cultivated it the way other men cultivated orchards — patiently, in rows, with an eye on the frost.

They rolled out through the waking streets of Vilna, past the bakery where the queue already bent around the corner, past the regimental barracks with its flag hanging stiff as a shingle. The bridge road opened ahead of them, running down the valley between black poplars, and the mountains on either side wore their first serious snow like men who had decided to keep it.

"The rally's at the weavers' hall," Miss Havel said. "Two o'clock. We'll be back before dark, and you'll say nothing to the landlady about where we've been, as usual."

"As usual," Rosalind agreed.

He drove. She read. This was the arrangement, and it had held for eleven weeks, and in eleven weeks she had never once asked him what he thought about suffrage, or strikes, or the magistrate, or anything at all beyond the condition of the tyres. He was furniture that could steer. He had been furniture in a great many houses — a clerk's desk that could add, a footman who could remember, a courier who could forget — and he had come to think of himself as a well-made piece with good joints and no opinions.

Except, of course, for the reading. The reading he did not count. The reading was a private vice, like drink, except that it left no smell on the breath. Every letter Miss Havel sealed, he had already opened with steam and a thin blade and read twice, and every letter she received he read before she did, and in eleven weeks he had built in his head a map of the province's corruption so detailed that he could have drawn it from memory: which constable took which bribe, which editor had been bought at what price, which factory owner had moved his ledgers the week before the inspection. He had never used a word of it. That was the covenant he had made with himself, the one that let him sleep: *I read, but I do not act. I am a window, not a door.* A man who merely looked through other people's correspondence was a voyeur. A man who acted on it was a conspirator, and conspirators were hanged, and Rosalind had a highly developed sense of self-preservation and a poorly developed sense of belonging, and the first had always outvoted the second.

The gaunt man was standing at the bend where the road came down to the river meadows, and he was not looking at the river.

Rosalind saw him the way drivers see things — in a half-second flick, the eye doing the arithmetic before the mind is consulted. A man at the roadside, coat too thin for the season, hands in pockets, grey face, and his head turned not toward the approaching motor with the dull hope of a man wanting a lift, but tracking it, the way a man tracks a thing he has been told to track. As they passed — the motor doing its stately twenty miles an hour — Rosalind caught the detail that mattered: the man's eyes went first to the passenger seat, to Miss Havel, and only then to Rosalind, and there was recognition in them, quick and unwelcome, like a man meeting a debt in the street.

"Pedestrian," Rosalind said, for something to say.

"Mm," said Miss Havel, not looking up from her petition.

The mirror showed him the man for three more seconds, standing at the bend, not moving. Then the poplars took him.

* * *

The weavers' hall was hot and loud and full of the smell of wet wool and coal smoke, and Miss Havel disappeared into it the way a match disappears into a bonfire — one bright strike and then she was everywhere at once, at the platform, at the door, in the middle of the crowd with her voice pitched to carry. Rosalind parked the motor in the yard, chocked the wheels, and did what he always did at rallies: found the wall, put his back against it, and counted the exits.

There were four. There were also, he noted, two men near the doors who were not listening to the speeches. Their coats were better than the weavers' coats, and their boots were garrison boots, and they watched faces instead of the platform. He filed them away with the gaunt man at the bend and told himself the file was thin. Two watchers at a suffrage rally was weather. A watcher on the bridge road was something else. He could not have said what, yet. He only knew that in eleven weeks of driving Miss Havel he had never been looked at, and this morning he had been, and the looking had a direction to it, and the direction was *him*.

Miss Havel spoke for forty minutes and brought the hall to its feet twice. Rosalind watched the garrison-booted men leave during the second ovation, unhurried, and something in his chest went cold and quiet, the way it went before a horse threw a shoe on a downhill stretch.

* * *

It was full dark when they started back, the lamps throwing a yellow cone onto the frost, Miss Havel asleep against the window with the petition still in her lap. The town came up out of the dark in its usual order — the church spire, the gasworks, the crooked roofs — and Rosalind was turning the motor into the livery yard when he heard the sound.

Not a sound, exactly. An absence. The yard behind the stable, where the grooms stacked the night's straw, was usually loud with cats. It was silent, and silence in a place that should be loud is the loudest thing there is.

He left the motor running — he would not understand until later why, only that some part of him had already decided he might need to leave quickly — and went along the stable wall with his hands in his pockets and his heart doing a slow, deliberate work, like a man walking to a door he already knows is bad news.

The gaunt man was sitting against the boards in the straw with his back to the wall and one hand pressed flat against his side, and even in the dark Rosalind could see that the hand was wrong, that it was too dark and too wet, that the straw beneath him had a black sheen.

"Sir," Rosalind said. "You're hurt. I'll fetch—"

"No." The word came out with almost no breath behind it, but it stopped Rosalind the way a hand on the chest stops a man. The gaunt man's eyes found his and held them, and they were clear eyes, terribly clear, the clarity of a man who has already done all his deciding and is now only executing it. "You drive for her. The motor. I saw you this morning. I've been — looking for the motor. Not her. You."

"Me," Rosalind said. It was not a question. It was the sound a man makes when the floor moves.

"You read," the man said. A bubble of something came with the word. "Don't — don't waste my time looking innocent. A man who drives for Havel and has never read her letters is a fool, and you're not a fool, I've watched you three days. You read. Good. Then you'll read this, and you'll understand it, and you'll move it. That's all I need you for. That's all."

"I'm not — I drive a motor. I'm not a courier, I'm not—"

"You're whatever is holding that packet," the man said, and brought his hand out from his side.

The oilcloth packet was the size of a prayer book, wrapped tight, sealed with tar along every edge, and it was warm from his body, which was the worst thing about it, warmer than the night, warmer than Rosalind's hands when they closed around it because they closed around it, he realized, without his having decided to. The man pressed Rosalind's fingers shut over it with his own, and his grip was still strong, which meant he was dying slowly, which meant there was time for him to say the rest.

"Kroll," he said. "The magistrate. What's in there — hangs him. Or hangs you. Depends on — where it is when the thaw comes." He coughed, and the cough went on too long, and when it stopped he was greyer. "The bridge closes with the snow. You know that. Everyone knows that. When it closes, the valley is his. His garrison, his toll, his law. Three checkpoints between here and the capital, and every man at them eats from his hand. You get past all three before the thaw, before the passes open and his people can move freely — you get it to the *Vremya* office, or to the procurator, or to anyone in the capital with a conscience and a printing press. Past the checkpoints. Before the thaw. Say it back."

"Past the checkpoints," Rosalind said. His voice sounded like someone else's. "Before the thaw."

"Good." The man's head tipped back against the boards. Somewhere beyond the wall the motor ticked and cooled, and a cat resumed its argument with the night. "Don't take the main road. Don't tell the woman. She's — brave, and brave is the most dangerous thing a person can be, it makes them predictable. And don't open it by the lamp in your window, boy, the landlady's son is Kroll's. I'm sorry." He said it simply, the way a man apologizes for the weather. "I'm sorry it's you. It was going to be someone. Better someone who knows how to be invisible."

"I don't know how to be invisible," Rosalind said. "I know how to be ignored. It's different."

The gaunt man laughed, and the laugh cost him, and he paid it anyway, as if it were the last thing on his list. "Then you'll do," he said. "Go. Go now, before someone comes to finish what they started. And boy —" Rosalind was already three steps into the dark, and the voice caught him like a hand on the collar — "the men who did this will find out what they failed to find. They'll start looking at motors."

Rosalind walked back to the idling car with the packet against his chest and the cold coming through his coat, and he did not run, because running was a thing that got noticed, and he had spent his whole life not being noticed, and he understood, with a dread that settled into him like the frost into the ground, that the life was over. The low profile, the window-not-door, the covenant of reading and never acting — the gaunt man behind the stable had put his dying hand through all of it in four minutes.

He drove Miss Havel home. She woke at the boarding house steps, thanked him, gathered her satchel, and paused with the door half open.

"You've gone pale, Mr. Amberdale."

"The cold, miss."

"Go in and get warm." She looked at him a moment longer than she needed to — she had a way of looking at people as if reading them, which was a habit he normally admired and tonight found unbearable — and then she went inside.

Rosalind sat in the yard until the engine cooled. Then he carried the packet up to his room, did not light the lamp by the window, and lit the stub of candle in the wardrobe instead, and sat on the floor with the oilcloth in his lap and the tar seal under his thumb, listening to the house breathe around him, and did not open it.

Not yet. But the candle was lit. And in the dark of the wardrobe, the packet seemed to warm again, the way it had been warm from the dying man's body, as if it were alive and patient and quite certain that sooner or later, he would.
