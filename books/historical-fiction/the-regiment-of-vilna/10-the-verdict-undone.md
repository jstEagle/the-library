## 10. The Verdict Undone

The ice went out of the river in the third week of March, and with it went the last excuse for the way we had all been living — crowded into the printer's cellar, sleeping in shifts, Anka's father's press thumping through the nights like a second heartbeat under the floorboards. When the thaw came, the valley smelled of wet earth and coal smoke and, for the first time in a season, of possibility.

The verdict reached the capital in February. I know the date because I was there when the telegram arrived, and because I have never in my life forgotten a date that mattered. The packet — Kroll's ledgers, the magistrate's own hand selling four men to the gallows for the price of a quiet garrison — had done its work in the offices of men who had spent years pretending not to know what Kroll was. Justice, when it finally moved, moved with the ponderous, self-satisfied speed of an institution protecting its own reputation. They tried him in April. They could hardly do otherwise; the pamphlets had been in every market square from Vilna to the coast since before Christmas, and you cannot hang a lie over a town for two years and then pretend the rope was never there.

I did not go to the trial. Anka did, and came back with her face set in a strange expression, half triumph and half disgust.

"He sat there," she said, unpinning her hat in the cellar doorway, ink to the wrists as always, "like a man watching someone else's funeral. He never once looked at the gallery. Never once looked at where the families were sitting." She set the hat down. "The widow of Tomasz Reiner was in the second row. She knitted the whole time. I don't know how she did it. I would have thrown something."

"Maybe that's how she threw things," I said. "Slowly. One stitch at a time."

Anka looked at me for a long moment, and then she laughed — the first real laugh since the ford, since the night the ice went red and I learned what a bullet through the shoulder costs you when the surgeon is a smuggler with a bottle of spirits and no anesthesia but a belt to bite. It hurt to laugh. I laughed anyway.

* * *

The overturn came on a grey Tuesday in late April. Four names read out in a courtroom in the capital — Reiner, Kowalczyk, Brandt, and the boy, Emil Sartorius, who had been nineteen and whose only crime had been standing too near the front of a strike line — and four verdicts struck from the record as though a clerk had dipped a sponge in water and wiped them clean. Posthumous. The word the papers used was *exonerated*, and the papers printed it in bold, and the families received formal apologies from men whose signatures had once sent the same four men to the scaffold.

Anka printed a special edition. We ran it off two thousand copies in one night, the whole household working — her father at the press, the deserters Jan and Milo folding, the old smuggler Wenzel, who had driven the sledge at the ford and still walked with a limp from the ice, carrying stacks up the stairs two at a time. I set type with my left hand, my right still stiff in its sling, and Anka stood beside me pulling proofs, and at some hour past three in the morning she paused, and looked at the four names in their black-bordered box, and said quietly, "It isn't enough."

"No," I said.

"But it's something."

"It's something," I agreed. And it was. It had to be, or else the shoulder, the scar, the boy deserter we'd lost at the mill road — Marek, who had carried the decoy packet and spent five months in a cell before we bought him out — all of it would have been nothing, and I could not afford for it to be nothing.

Wenzel brought down a bottle of plum brandy that night, and we drank to the four names, and to Marek, and to the courier whose name none of us ever learned, the gaunt man who had bled out behind the livery stable with an oilcloth packet in his fists and three words in his mouth: *before the thaw*. I had made it by eleven days. I thought about that sometimes, the eleven days, the way a man thinks about a door he walked through just before it closed.

* * *

Miss Havel's scandal broke in May, and it broke exactly the way she had always known it would.

They could not touch her for the false ledgers — she had been too careful, and besides, the trial had made Kroll's corruption a matter of public record, and no prosecutor in the capital wanted to open the question of who else had been feeding the magistrate fiction. So they did what institutions do when they cannot punish a woman for the crime: they punished her for the appearance of it. A suffragist, the papers said. A woman of property and reputation, driving about the countryside with an unexplained courier's death on her doorstep, harboring printers, lending her carriage to — the articles never finished the sentence. They didn't need to. The innuendo did the work that evidence couldn't, and by the middle of May her committee had dissolved, her subscription lists had dried up, and two of her oldest allies had published letters regretting their former association.

She took it the way she took everything — standing very straight, with her gloves on.

She came to the cellar once before she left. I remember every detail of it, the way I remember the eleven days. The way she stood with her back to the press, so the ink smell would get into her coat, as though she wanted to carry something of us out of the valley. The way she did not sit.

"They've given me a choice," she said. "Retract, denounce the pamphlets, and keep my name. Or go." She smiled, and it was the old smile, the one that had made me feel, in my first week as her driver, that I was being let in on a joke the whole country was in on. "As if I would retract. As if a name were worth what those four men paid."

"Where will you go?"

"Zurich, first. Then — I don't know. There are women in every capital doing this work, Rosalind, and most of them are doing it better than I did, because they have less to lose." She looked around the cellar — at Anka's type cases, at Wenzel's crates, at Jan asleep in the corner with a proof sheet over his face like a shroud. "You built something here. All of you. I watched you do it and I told myself I was only lending the carriage."

"You did more than lend the carriage."

"Yes," she said simply. "I did. And that is why I have to go, and why you have to let me."

I knew what she was asking. She was asking me not to follow her. She was asking me to stand on the platform and watch the train go, and to understand that she had chosen the work over the man, and that she needed me to choose the work over the woman, so that neither of us would ever have to wonder whether love had been the thing that bent us off our course.

I could have argued. I could have said the work was in every capital, that a driver is a driver in any language, that Zurich had roads and she would need someone at the wheel. All true. None of it the point. The point was that she had spent two years feeding false ledgers to a murderer, alone, with no cellar and no family and no one to pull proofs beside at three in the morning — and that she had done it because she believed the cause was bigger than her solitude, and she was not going to stop believing it now that the solitude had a name and a face and a bad shoulder.

"I'll write to you," I said. "Through the network. It'll take three weeks each way and half of it will be in cipher."

"I would expect nothing less." She put out her gloved hand, and I took it, and we stood like that for a moment, two people who had driven a thousand miles together and were about to drive no more. "You were the best employee I ever had, Mr. Amberdale. You read all my letters and never once blackmailed me. In this country, that's practically a marriage."

"Miss Havel—"

"Edith," she said. "You've earned the name. Use it when you write."

* * *

The first open train of the season left on the last morning of May. The valley had turned green while we weren't looking — I never knew how it did that, how the whole world changed its clothes overnight while men were busy with trials and type and grief — and the station platform was bright with it, wildflowers coming up through the gravel between the rails like the earth itself had opinions about the season.

Half the household came to see her off, which she pretended to find excessive and did not. Anka brought a parcel of pamphlets, "for the journey — Zurich has printers too, you know, and ours are better." Wenzel loaded her trunks with the tenderness of a man who had once driven a sledge through gunfire for her cause and would never speak of it. Jan and Milo saluted her with such solemn absurdity that she laughed, really laughed, on a platform in front of forty strangers, and I thought: *there it is. That's what they took from her. Not the house, not the committee. The laugh.*

The whistle blew. We said our goodbyes in a cluster, and then the others drew back, and it was the two of us on the platform with the train breathing steam beside us.

"You could still come," she said. She said it lightly, which was how I knew it cost her.

"I know," I said. "I'm not going to."

"I know," she said. "That's why I asked."

She kissed my cheek — once, firmly, the way a general decorates a soldier — and boarded, and found her seat by the window, and did not look at me again, because we had agreed without ever saying it that looking would be the one thing neither of us could afford. The train pulled out. I watched it go the whole length of the valley, a thin dark line against the green, until the line bent at the far hills and was gone.

I stood there a while. The platform emptied. The stationmaster called something about the afternoon freight.

And then a hand took mine.

Anka had come up beside me without my noticing — she had that trick, the smuggler's trick, arriving like weather — and her fingers laced through mine, ink-stained and steady, and she did not say anything at all. She just stood with me, looking down the empty rails, holding on.

"She'll be all right," Anka said finally. "Women like her are always all right. It's the rest of the world that has to catch up."

"Yes," I said.

"Come on." She tugged my hand, gently, toward the station door and the road beyond it, where Wenzel's cart was waiting and the valley lay green all the way to the river. "Papa's got a new pamphlet to set. Kroll's appeal, if you can believe it. We'll have it torn apart by Sunday." She glanced up at me, and there was the old dangerous warmth in her face, the one I'd first seen across a type case two nights before everything changed. "Besides. I don't print with just anyone, Rosalind Amberdale. You should feel honored."

I did feel honored. I felt the whole strange weight of it — the dead courier, the four names, the burned carriage on the bridge, the woman on the train growing smaller and smaller in my mind and never, ever small — and I let it settle into the place where scars live, and I took Anka's hand properly, and we walked out of the station together into the green.

Behind us, down the line, the afternoon freight was already coming — a long black shape rounding the far bend, carrying, as I would learn by nightfall, a passenger with a warrant and questions about the printer's cellar in Vilna.

But that, as Anka liked to say, was Sunday's problem.
