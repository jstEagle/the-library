## 6. Master Hartwell's Smile

The summons came on a Tuesday, which was wrong in itself. Tuesdays were ledger days, and Josiah Hartwell did not interrupt ledger days for anything short of fire, flood, or the death of a partner — and one partner, as far as the world knew, had already managed that.

"Boy." Mr. Tredgett, the head clerk, did not look up from his column of figures when he said it. He never looked up. Looking up cost a man time, and Mr. Tredgett had calculated to the farthing what his time was worth. "Upstairs. The master's room. Wash your hands first."

I washed my hands. I had learned in my first month at Hartwell & Vane that a boy's cleanliness was read like a document — clean hands meant a careful boy, careful meant trustworthy, trustworthy meant invisible, and invisible was the whole of my ambition. Seven years I had been invisible. I could count bales faster than any two men on the floor, I could tell by the set of a foreman's shoulders whether it was a day to be seen or a day to be elsewhere, and I had not spoken more than four hundred words aloud in any given year since I arrived.

Now I dried my hands on the rough towel by the door and climbed the stairs to the master's room, and every step felt like walking up into weather.

* * *

Josiah Hartwell's office sat above the counting-house like a good hat above a respectable face. It smelled of beeswax and pipe smoke and, underneath both, of money — not coin, exactly, but paper that had become money, bales and bolts and bills of lading all breathing together in the warm air. There was a Turkey carpet worn thin in front of the desk where a hundred frightened feet had shuffled. I made a note to stand beside the worn place rather than on it. Masters noticed wear patterns. It was the sort of thing they noticed instead of people.

"Ravensworth." Mr. Hartwell stood at the window with his back half-turned, hands clasped behind him, gazing down at the yard as though the carts below were pieces on a board he was winning. "Come in. Sit."

There was no chair indicated. This was a test of its own. I chose the hard wooden one against the wall — the servant's seat, the one that said *I know my place* — and sat with my hands on my knees.

He turned then, and gave me the smile.

I want to be fair to that smile, because fairness is a thing I came to care about later, and because the smile deserves its history written down somewhere. It was a handsome smile. It was a well-made smile, like everything about Josiah Hartwell — the silver at his temples arranged just so, the waistcoat of dove-grey silk, the signet ring worn loose enough to suggest a man who had never once gripped anything harder than a pen. It was the smile he wore in the annual report engraving. It was the smile the Sunday school children were told about: *Mr. Hartwell, who built the new schoolroom roof out of his own pocket.*

It was also, I understood in that moment with a clarity that made my stomach go cold, a smile that had never once reached his eyes. His eyes were doing sums. They were summing me.

"You've been with us seven years now," he said.

"Yes, sir." Four words. I was already over budget.

"Seven years. Time flies." He settled behind the desk, drawing a ledger toward him without opening it, the way a card player draws a deck. "Tredgett speaks well of you. Says you've an eye for weights. Caught young Pilling miscounting a cotton lot last month, I hear."

"He'd transposed two figures, sir. Easily done."

"Mm." The smile widened a fraction. "Honest, too. Defending the boy. That's a rare quality, Ravensworth. Honesty." He let the word sit between us like a coin on a table. "Especially these days. One hears such stories. Such *rumours.*"

I said nothing. Silence, I have found, is often mistaken for agreement by men who are used to being agreed with, and for wisdom by men who are used to being flattered. With Mr. Hartwell it bought me three full seconds, which I spent remembering how to breathe.

"You'll have heard," he went on, "that we've had strangers about. At the docks. Asking questions."

Here it was, then. Ada Quill had come to the loading dock eleven days ago with a crate of stationery she had no business delivering and questions she had no right to ask, and I had lied to her badly — so badly that she had looked at me the way one looks at a locked door with light coming under it — and I had thought about her every day since, and I had told no one, and now the master himself was asking, gently, in a warm room, with a smile like a hand extended in friendship.

"No, sir," I said.

And there it was. My first lie to a master's face. It came out smaller than I expected — two syllables, hardly any weight to them at all — and yet I felt it leave me like something physical, like a pearl slipping off a wet rope into deep water. My father had dived for pearls before the debt took our boat and then took him, and he used to say you always know the exact moment the pearl leaves your fingers, because your hand keeps closing on nothing ever after. That was Tuesday. My hand has been closing on nothing ever after, and I have made my peace with it.

"No?" Mr. Hartwell raised his eyebrows, pleasantly surprised, the way a man is pleasantly surprised by good news he does not quite believe. "A woman, perhaps. Middle-sized. Ink on her fingers. Carrying a delivery crate she seemed in no hurry to deliver."

"She came to the dock, sir. Delivered stationery for the counting-house. Signed the book and went."

"Did she say anything? Anything at all?"

I considered. Here was the arithmetic of survival, done at speed: if I claimed total ignorance, he might check the delivery book, and the book would show her name, and a lie with a hole in it is worse than a lie without. If I admitted too much, I became interesting, and interesting boys do not stay invisible. So I gave him the truth, or the shape of it, wearing the flat voice I kept for foremen and customs men.

"She asked whether the Vane office still received post, sir. Whether letters for Mr. Vane should go to the dead-letter office or be held here. I told her I only carried crates."

"And what did she say to that?"

"That she'd write and ask the Post Office." A pause, precisely measured. "She had ink on her fingers, sir. Clerking sort. Not worth troubling over."

I watched him weigh it. I watched him decide — visibly, the way a scale tips — that I was exactly what I appeared to be: a quick, obedient, incurious boy whose horizon ended at the loading dock wall. And I will confess something here that shames me even now, writing it in the safety of lamplight and finished things: for one heartbeat, watching him decide I was nothing, I felt *proud*. Terror and exhilaration, the plan promised, and both were there, tangled like rope. Terror because his eyes had rested on me and might rest again. Exhilaration because I had stood inside the machinery of a great man's attention and moved one small gear of it myself, and the machinery had not caught me.

"Very good," said Mr. Hartwell. "If she returns — if anyone asks after Mr. Vane again — you'll come to me directly. Not to Tredgett. To me. Do you understand?"

"Yes, sir."

"The firm has enemies, Ravensworth. Men who'd tear down what honest industry has built, out of envy or worse. A firm is like a ship." He gestured at the window, at the yard, the warehouses, the district beyond, all of it his. "Everyone aboard lives or drowns together. You'll remember that."

"Aye, sir," I said, and thought of the red-bound book sleeping in the rafters above the very desk he leaned on, and of the names inside it that had drowned while the ship sailed on, and I kept my face as smooth as poured wax.

He dismissed me with a nod and returned to his window. At the door I paused — not from courage, but because my legs nearly betrayed me — and he spoke again without turning round.

"Ravensworth. You're a Pearl Island lad, aren't they saying? Diver's son."

"Yes, sir."

"They say divers can hold their breath a long while." Still the window, still the yard below. "Longer than most. It must be a useful habit."

"It's kept me alive, sir," I said, and meant every word in ways he could not count, and went out.

* * *

My knees did not properly forgive me until I reached the canal bridge, and even then they trembled on the ironwork like a struck bell. I stood a while watching the water carry its cargo of rainbow oil-slick past the gasworks, and I took inventory of myself the way I took inventory of bales: heart, present, beating badly; hands, present, cold; tongue, present, and newly guilty of treason.

Because that is what it was. However kindly the word *lie* dresses itself, however necessary the garment, I had stood in a master's room and spoken falsehood to protect a stranger with ink-stained fingers, and some part of me — the part trained by seven years of Grady's foremanship and Petch's slow dying and the general doctrine that the powerless keep safe by keeping quiet — that part wanted to run back upstairs and unsay it.

But another part, a newer part, only eleven days old, kept turning over what Ada Quill had shown me. A letter dated last month. Signed Edmund Vane. A dead man's hand, alive and writing.

And beneath that, older than either, the promise. Mr. Petch, coughing his life out in the loft above the counting-house while the fever took the district, gripping my wrist with fingers gone light as paper: *You can read, boy. Nobody thinks you can, but you can. When I'm gone, you remember what's wrote in the red book. Don't read it now. Just remember where it sleeps, and remember that I asked.* And I had nodded, and he had died on a Thursday, and the red book had slept on in the rafters through seven years of Tuesdays while I counted bales and kept silent and called it survival.

Survival, I saw now, standing on the bridge with the gasworks stink in my nose, was a debt like any other. It compounded.

A barge went by loaded with coal, the driver whistling something tuneless and cheerful. I thought: he knows nothing. He knows the whistle, the tide, the price of ale. Ten thousand people in this district know nothing, and their not-knowing is load-bearing; the whole grey edifice of Hartwell & Vane stands upon it. Mr. Hartwell had smiled at me today because my silence was part of his architecture. He had counted me among his bricks.

Well. Bricks had feelings too, as it happened. Bricks could walk.

* * *

Ada was waiting where she had said she would be, in the shadow of the print-shop alley off Chapel Lane, pretending to study a broadsheet pasted to the wall — an old one, half torn, advertising a lecture on phrenology that had taken place before I was indentured. She had the trick of looking busy and unremarkable at once, which is rarer than honesty and more useful.

"Well?" she said, low. "You look like a man who's been swimming in his coat."

"Hartwell sent for me." I glanced up the lane, down the lane. Empty except for a cat with proprietary opinions about a fishmonger's crate. "He knows about you. Knows you asked after Vane. He didn't know I'd talked to you — I think — but he's watching the dock now. And he wants to be told personally if anyone comes asking again."

"Personally." Her eyebrows went up. "Not through Tredgett?"

"Not through anyone. He said the firm has enemies, and everyone aboard lives or drowns together."

Ada was quiet a moment. She had a way of going still when she was thinking, utterly still, like water deciding whether to freeze. "That's not the talk of a man worried about stationery sales," she said at last. "That's the talk of a man standing guard over something. Did he seem frightened?"

"Frightened men fidget. He didn't fidget." I turned the memory over, examining its underside the way I examined invoices for forged totals. "But he watched me the whole time I was leaving. Through the window. I felt it on my neck like sun."

"Then he's frightened of exactly one thing," Ada said, "and he's checking whether you're it." She folded her arms. "Silas. Why would a senior partner care whether a delivery girl asked after a drowned man — unless the drowning isn't the end of the story?"

"Because the story hasn't ended," I said. "It's just been filed."

She laughed — a short, surprised laugh, quickly smothered, as though laughter in Chapel Lane were also contraband. "Filed. God help us, you sound like Petch already. Everyone says you've his manner."

"I don't have anybody's manner. I have a face masters believe." I hesitated, and then, because the exhilaration from the morning was still fizzing in my blood like bad beer, I added: "He offered me trust today. Asked me to bring him any stranger asking questions. Which means—" I worked it forward, step by step, the way Tredgett had taught me to work a column, "—he doesn't know what you have. If he knew, he wouldn't recruit the errand boy. He'd have the constables at the dock by now. He's hunting blind, and he's using me for eyes."

"So we let him use you," Ada said slowly, "for eyes pointed the wrong way."

"That's treason," I said. "Against the firm."

"Is it?" She tilted her head, and there was something almost gentle in it. "Or is it loyalty to the other crew aboard his ship? The ones who drowned?"

The cat abandoned the fishmonger's crate and regarded us both with the weary contempt of the truly self-employed. Somewhere across the rooftops the mill whistle began its long complaint, calling the night shift, and the sound rolled over the district like a lid settling onto a pot.

"There's a printer," Ada said quietly. "Off Chapel Lane. Obadiah Finch. His press has been silent two years — libel laws, fines, the usual slow murder. But he knew my father, and he owes me a favour, and I think it's time we showed someone what's in that red book besides each other. Tonight, if you're willing. After dark, when the lanes are empty."

I thought of Mr. Hartwell's smile, and of the worn patch of carpet where a hundred frightened feet had shuffled, and of Petch's fingers light as paper on my wrist.

"After dark," I said.

And that is how, on a Tuesday in the year of grace 1848, I committed my second treason of the day: I walked willingly, with my eyes open and my heart hammering like a loom, toward the cellar where a silenced press waited in the dark — carrying, tucked inside my shirt where the master's eyes could never follow, the knowledge of where the red-bound book slept.

Behind me, high above the counting-house, I would have sworn a window curtain shifted. But perhaps it was only the wind learning, as I was learning, which way the district was about to turn.
