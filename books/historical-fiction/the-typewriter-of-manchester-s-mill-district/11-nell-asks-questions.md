## 11. Nell Asks Questions

Nell Barrow had decided three things in her sixteen years, and she held to all of them the way she held to a loom-frame when the shuttles ran fast: that her mother's temper was worse than any foreman's, that Sunday pudding was worth enduring six days of porridge for, and that when people whispered near her, they were whispering about her.

The third decision had been tested many times and never once found wanting.

So it was that on the fourth morning after the machine had tried to take her arm — and been beaten to it by half an inch, thanks be to God and to Silas Ravensworth's quick hands — Nell lay propped on Mrs. Tredgar's good chair by the infirmary window, her bandaged forearm resting on a pillow like a duchess, and watched the two of them at it again.

Silas and the stranger woman. The one with ink on her fingers.

They stood in the corner past the linen shelf, heads together, doing their whispering. The woman — Miss Quill, though nobody had properly introduced her, which was its own kind of suspicious — held a folded paper she kept turning over in her hands as though it might bite her. Silas said something low. She shook her head. He said something else, lower still, and glanced — *there* — glanced straight at Nell and away again so quickly you'd have missed it if you weren't the sort of person who made a study of people.

Nell was exactly that sort of person.

"Mrs. Tredgar," she said sweetly, "how long does a bandage want changing, would you say? Only mine feels loose."

"It doesn't want changing till Thursday and it isn't loose, you little schemer." Mrs. Tredgar didn't even look up from her mortar. "I've known you since you were in petticoats. You're after something."

"I'm after nothing. I'm an invalid."

"You're a barrow, is what you are. Your whole family could smell out a secret through six feet of clay and a locked chest."

This was true, and Nell took it as the compliment it was.

* * *

Here is what Nell knew, laid out neat as thread on a bobbin:

Item: four days ago the carding machine on the second floor, the one every soul in the mill knew wanted a guard and had wanted one for two years, caught her sleeve. She'd have lost the arm entire if Silas Ravensworth hadn't come out of nowhere — quiet Silas, mouse-Silas, the boy who spoke maybe forty words a week and all of them careful — and hauled her back by the collar hard enough to bruise, then wrapped her arm in his own shirt before anyone thought to fetch the proper dressings.

Item: Silas had looked at the machine afterward with an expression she'd never seen on him. Not fear. Something older than fear. Like a man reading a letter he'd hoped never to see again.

Item: the next day, a stranger came to the loading dock claiming to sell stationery, and stationery-sellers did not, as a rule, stand in the yard for an hour asking questions of everyone who passed, and did not, as a further rule, end up talking to Silas in corners.

Item: today, said stranger was sitting in the mill infirmary under pretense of delivering lint and calomel, whispering with Silas not ten feet from Nell's invalid chair, and both of them kept looking at her when they thought she couldn't see.

She could see. She could see everything. It was practically a family trade.

* * *

When Mrs. Tredgar went down to the storeroom for more lint — taking her time about it, because the storeroom boy had a face like a full moon and a way of laughing at her jokes, and Mrs. Tredgar was fifty-one and entitled — Nell acted.

She didn't act loudly. That was the art of it. Her mother acted loudly; Nell had learned early that loudness got doors slammed in your face, whereas patience got them opened for you. So she simply sat up straighter, winced artistically, and let out a small, well-judged hiss of pain.

It worked inside a minute. It always worked. Guilt was the most reliable machine in Manchester; it never needed oiling.

Silas crossed the room first, of course. He always came first, quick and silent as a cat that had been taught manners. "Is it your arm? Should I fetch Mrs. Tredgar—"

"It's my arm, and you'll fetch nobody," Nell said, "because there's nothing wrong with my arm that a bit of attention wouldn't cure, and the attention I'm wanting is yours." She looked past him to the doorway, where the ink-fingered woman had paused, paper still in hand, watching with an expression Nell couldn't read and disliked accordingly. "Both of yours. Sit down, the pair of you. My mother says a person owed an explanation should collect it standing up, but my arm's bad and I'll allow you chairs."

Silas actually sat. That startled her more than anything. Mouse-Silas, who flinched from foremen and masters alike, sat down on the stool by her chair like a man reporting for duty.

Miss Quill did not sit. She came closer instead, and looked at Nell — really looked, the way you'd look at a document, checking it against something — and said, "You asked him to save your arm. On the second floor. You told him the guard was missing and he said he knew."

Had she said that? She had, yes, screaming it, mostly. "I say a lot of things when a machine's eating my sleeve."

"And he said—" Miss Quill's voice dropped, not to a whisper now but to something quieter than a whisper, the voice people used for true things, "—he said, 'I know. I've always known.'"

The infirmary was very still. Somewhere below, the looms went on knocking out their thousand-heartbeat rhythm, the sound Nell had slept inside since she was five years old and would sleep inside until she died, probably, and had never once thought of as anything but the sound of the world being the world.

"That's what you've been whispering about," Nell said. "Four days of whispering. About my machine."

"Not only yours," Silas said.

And there it was — the thing underneath, showing its edge. Nell had spent her whole life around adults who told children comforting lies with great skill, and she knew the taste of one, and this wasn't it. This was a true thing, and it was heavy, and Silas Ravensworth was holding it the way she'd held her own arm yesterday: carefully, and because he had no choice.

"Then tell me the rest," she said. "All of it. And mind—" she raised her good hand before either of them could begin the gentle business, the *you're too young* business, the *it's better you don't know* business, "—before you start, you should know I've already heard half of it. I hear everything. Betsy Crompton saw the stationery woman at the dock and told our Alice, Alice told me, and Alice also says Grady the foreman went white as flour when your name was mentioned, miss, and Grady's never gone white as flour in his life, not when the belt snapped in '44 and took Joseph Mellor's two fingers clean off. So whatever it is, it's bigger than a missing guard, and I'll thank you not to decide for me how much of it I can bear. I bore the machine. I can bear the truth. It's smaller than a machine."

There was a silence.

Then Miss Quill laughed — a short, surprised laugh, quickly bitten off, but a real one — and looked at Silas, and said, "Where did this district find her?"

"She found us," Silas said. "We've never once managed to find her. She turns up."

"I turn up," Nell agreed, "and I stay turned up. Now. Talk."

* * *

They talked. Or rather, Miss Quill talked, in a low steady voice, while Silas stood at the window with his back half-turned, watching the yard, and put in a word here and there like a man laying stones into a wall.

Nell learned that Edmund Vane, partner of Hartwell & Vane, drowned gentleman, memorialized gentleman, was alive. Learned that letters in his own hand had come through the post last month. Learned that the woman telling this was Ada Quill, whose father had been ruined by debts connected to the firm, and who had followed those debts across three counties like a bloodhound that had taught itself to read.

Learned — and here her stomach did something slow and cold — that workers written down as *transported*, sent off to Australia with wages owing, had in fact never left Manchester at all. That some of them had died in accidents like hers. That the accidents had been arranged to be small enough to bury and frequent enough to save money on guards and repairs, and that a ledger existed with the names in it, red-bound, hidden, waiting.

"Names," Nell said. "What names?"

Ada hesitated. Silas didn't. He turned from the window and recited them, quietly, without hurrying, the way you'd recite a psalm you'd had by heart so long you'd forgotten learning it. Six names. Two of them Nell knew — or had known. Thomas Ackroyd, who'd worked the spinning mules when she was small and used to whistle opera badly. Martha Sykes, whose sister still lived on Chapel Lane and told everyone Martha wrote regularly from Van Diemen's Land, though the letters had stopped years ago and nobody liked to ask why.

Martha Sykes had never been to Van Diemen's Land. Martha Sykes was under the flagstones somewhere, or in the canal, or in a pauper's grave with a lie carved where her name should be.

Nell discovered she was gripping the arms of the chair hard enough to hurt her good hand, and made herself stop.

"The machine that caught me," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "It's the same pattern. Unguarded, reported safe, reported safe again last quarter — I heard Grady dictate the report myself through the door, he said *all machinery in good order*. If my sleeve had been an inch longer, or Silas a minute slower, there'd have been a seventh name. And it would've gone in a book somewhere, and someone would've written *transported* after it, and my mother would've gotten a letter saying I was bound for Australia and wished her joy of my new life."

Nobody contradicted her. That was how she knew it was all true.

* * *

"Now," Nell said, when she had let the cold settle and found she could still think inside it, "here's what happens next. You're going to do something dangerous with that ledger. Don't insult me by denying it; nobody hides a paper in rafters and whispers for four days to plan a picnic. And I'm going to help."

"Nell—" Silas began.

"No. Hear me out, because I've thought about it longer than you have, seeing as I only learned the facts ten minutes ago and have therefore spent the whole ten minutes thinking." She counted on her fingers, which was difficult with one of them bandaged and improved the effect considerably. "One: I work the second floor, where the machines are, and I can watch what Grady watches and hear what Grady hears, and neither of you can, because Grady watches you like a hawk watches a mouse that owes it money. Two: I know every worker in that mill by name, and their mothers, and their aunties, and which of them can keep a secret and which would sell it for a pork pie — that's intelligence, that is, real intelligence, better than any spy you'd hire. Three: it was nearly my name in that book. Mine. And I will not sit in an invalid chair eating possets while other people fight over my grave. Four—"

"Is there a four?" Ada asked. There was a light in her face now that Nell decided she approved of. It was the light of a person recognizing a fellow soldier.

"There's always a four. Four: my mother says the Barrows are like ferrets, and ferrets go down holes other animals won't, and come out the far side with the rabbit. I'm offering to be the ferret."

Silas made a sound. It took Nell a moment to recognize it as a laugh — a small one, strangled at birth, but a laugh, from Silas, who she'd have sworn couldn't laugh, and it changed his whole face, opened it up like a shutter coming back on a window.

"You should do that more often," she told him. "Laughing. Suits you. Very shocking for the neighbors."

"I'll consider it," he said gravely, and there was the ghost of it again, and Nell felt something settle in her chest, some piece sliding home. She'd known Silas Ravensworth for seven years the way you know a fixture, a pump handle, a stair rail — useful, reliable, invisible. In four days she'd seen him pull her from a machine, recite the dead like a priest, and nearly smile twice. It was like discovering the stair rail had opinions.

"All right," Ada said. "But understand what you're asking for, Nell. This isn't a game with rules. Vane has bought men before — magistrates, constables, boatmen. If he learns a mill girl is carrying messages, he won't write you a stern letter. People who get in his way have a way of ending up *transported*."

"My mother says the same about dancing with the Wrigley boys," Nell said. "Everything worth doing ends somebody up transported, according to her. I've weighed it." She held out her good hand, palm up, the way her father used to when sealing a bargain over the garden fence. "Partners?"

Ada looked at the hand. Then at Silas.

And Silas — quiet Silas, careful Silas, the boy who had spent seven years perfecting the art of saying nothing — reached over and set his own hand on top of Nell's, first, deliberate, like a man laying the first stone.

"Partners," he said.

Ada put her ink-stained fingers on top of both theirs, and laughed her short surprised laugh again, and said, "Lord help Vane. He thinks he's fighting a clerk and a stationer. He doesn't know about the ferret."

* * *

They were still arranging things — Nell was explaining the geography of the second floor with the aid of a lint-scissors and a pincushion, and doing it rather well, if she said so herself — when Mrs. Tredgar came back up the stairs, took one look at the three of them bent together over the pincushion battlefield, and stopped dead in the doorway.

Her eyes went from the scissors-map to Nell's face to Silas's to the stranger's, and Nell watched her arrive at a conclusion, and watched her decide — visibly, the whole process happening behind her eyes like weather — not to ask.

"Hadn't you a delivery to finish, miss?" Mrs. Tredgar said at last, gruff, to Ada. "Lint won't deliver itself."

"It's nearly done," Ada said.

"And you." This to Silas, and her voice softened in a way Nell had never heard it do for anyone above the age of eight. "Master Hartwell's clerk came by while you were abovestairs. Says Mr. Hartwell wants to see you. Today. At his own house — dinner, he said." She paused. "Dinner, Silas. A mill boy asked to a partner's table. When has that ever happened?"

Never, was the answer. Never in the history of the firm, and everyone in the room knew it.

Nell watched the color leave Silas's face — watched the shutter come down over the window that had just, briefly, been open — and understood, with the ferret's instinct for what waits at the bottom of holes, that the picnic was over.

"He knows," Silas said quietly. Not a question.

"Or he wants to find out if he needs to," Ada said. "Those aren't the same, but they rhyme."

Silas looked at the two of them — at Nell in her invalid chair with her scissors and her pincushion army, at Ada with her folded paper and her ink-stained fingers — and something passed over his face that Nell would remember afterward all her life: not fear, exactly, though there was fear in it, but the look of a man checking his pockets before a long journey and finding, to his own surprise, that he is not traveling alone.

"If I'm not back by tomorrow noon," he said, "go to the printer. Finch, on Deansgate. Tell him Petch's book is safe and the promise stands."

"And if you are back?" Nell asked.

He almost smiled. Almost. "Then I'll tell you what Hartwell serves for dinner, and whether it was worth nearly dying for."

He went out. They listened to his steps fade down the stair — quick, light, silent, the steps of a boy who had learned to walk unheard through a house that owned him — and then the looms filled the silence back up, thousand-hearted, indifferent, and Nell picked up her scissors and moved a pincushion two inches west.

"Dinner at Hartwell's house," she said. "That's not a summons, that's a trap with napkins."

"Yes," Ada said. She was looking at the door Silas had gone through, and her jaw was set in a way that made Nell revise her estimate of the woman upward, considerably. "Which means we've got until tomorrow noon to make sure he walks out of it."

"Well then," said Nell Barrow, and reached for the lint with her good hand. "Ferret's ready. Where's the hole?"
