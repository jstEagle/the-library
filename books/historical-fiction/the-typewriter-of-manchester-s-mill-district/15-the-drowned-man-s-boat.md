## 15. The Drowned Man's Boat

There is a particular smell to a riverside tavern at eleven in the morning, and it is the smell of men who have given up pretending they came for the company.

Ada Quill had catalogued that smell across three counties — in Liverpool, where her father had drunk away a printing business; in Hull, where she'd learned to copy a hand so well she could forge her own apologies; and now here, on the mud-flat edge of the Irwell where Manchester went to forget itself. The Anchor & Eel did not so much stand beside the canal as lean against it, weary of the arrangement, its sign hanging by one chain like a man considering resignation.

"You're certain this is the place?" Silas asked. He stood close behind her in the doorway, hands tucked into his sleeves, doing his best impression of furniture. He was very good at it. Seven years of practice will do that.

"The boatman's name is Tam Rudge," Ada said. "Finch remembers him from the old days — before Finch got careful. Rudge worked the coal barges, then the pleasure boats, then nothing at all. And three years ago, on the night Edmund Vane allegedly drowned, someone rowed a boat out past the weir and came back alone."

"Allegedly," Silas repeated, tasting the word. He was getting better at tasting words. It gave Ada an unreasonable amount of hope for him.

"Allegedly," she confirmed. "Now. Rules."

"You have rules." It wasn't quite a complaint. It was more the tone of a man noting the weather.

"I always have rules. One: I talk. Two: you watch his hands. Liars gesture with their right; frightened men protect their left. Three: if anyone asks, we are a married couple come about a debt, because nobody pries into a married couple's misery — they've heard it all before and it bores them."

Silas considered this. "Married," he said.

"Temporarily."

"I see." A pause. "Do we argue? Married couples argue. It would be convincing."

"We are not going to rehearse an argument, Silas."

"It wouldn't take long. You'd say something sensible, and I'd fail to understand it, and there we are."

Despite herself — despite the forged letter burning in her pocket, despite the magistrate's dinner still fresh in her memory, despite everything — Ada laughed. It came out of her sideways, unexpected, and an old man by the door looked up from his gin as though laughter were a coin dropped on the floor.

"Inside," she said. "Before I remember why we're here."

* * *

The Anchor & Eel's interior had been decorated, sometime around the Flood, in a style best described as Despairing. Smoke-stained beams. A bar polished by elbows rather than cloth. Behind it, a landlady with forearms like a lock-keeper watched them enter with the flat assessment of a woman who had seen every kind of trouble float down the canal and had priced all of it.

Two gins, Ada ordered, and paid with a shilling she didn't begrudge, because a shilling spent on atmosphere was cheaper than a guinea spent on bail.

Tam Rudge sat at the far end of the room, in the corner seat that every tavern reserves for its most regular ruin. He was perhaps fifty, built like a bollard that had taken one ship too many, with a face gone red at the edges and white at the center, like meat cooked badly. A pot of ale sat before him untouched, which told Ada more than any testimony could. A man who orders ale and doesn't drink it is a man waiting for something, or hiding from something, or both.

She crossed the room without hurrying. Hurrying announced intention. She set her gin down across from him and sat, and Silas drifted after her like a shadow that had learned to carry its own chair.

Rudge looked up. His eyes did the arithmetic of strangers in his corner and arrived at a sum he disliked.

"Don't want company," he said.

"Lucky," said Ada, "because what you want and what you get stopped corresponding some time ago, Mr. Rudge."

That landed. She watched it land — a small flinch around the eyes, the hand twitching toward the untouched pot and stopping. *Left hand*, she noted. *He protects the left.* There was a story in that hand somewhere.

"I don't know you."

"No. But you knew Edmund Vane."

The tavern noise went on around them — dice rattling, a man complaining about the price of cotton, the landlady thumping a barrel — but their little table became an island, and on that island the air turned cold.

"Never heard of him," Rudge said.

Ada took out the letter. Not Vane's letter — she hadn't brought that, she wasn't a fool — but a fair copy she'd made in Vane's hand, because if a man was going to lie to her she preferred he do it while looking at the handwriting that owned him. She laid it on the table between the gins.

Rudge looked at it the way a horse looks at a whip. His whole body changed — shoulders dropping, chin tucking, the posture of a man making himself smaller inside his own coat.

"That's not mine," he whispered.

"No," Ada agreed pleasantly. "It's his. Dated last month. Signed by a man who has been dead for three years, which makes him either a ghost or the hardest-working corpse in Lancashire. You helped make him dead, Mr. Rudge. I'd like to hear how."

"I never—" He stopped. Looked at the door. Looked at the window, where the canal slid past, grey and patient. When he spoke again his voice had dropped to the register men use in confessionals and courts. "Who sent you?"

"A friend of the drowned," Ada said, which was true in a way she didn't stop to examine.

* * *

It took another gin — his, this time, which he finally drank, all at once, like medicine — and twenty minutes of Ada talking in a low, unhurried voice about nothing much: the weather, the price of coal, the way the mills ate men and called the bones *output*. She was not, during all of it, asking him anything. That was the trick her father had taught her without meaning to, in the years when she'd listened outside doors to keep him out of worse ones: people fill silence the way water fills a hole. Give them enough quiet and they'll tell you everything just to hear a human voice answer theirs.

Silas, meanwhile, watched. She felt him watching — a steady presence at her shoulder, reading Rudge's hands the way she'd asked, and once, when Rudge's left hand crept inside his jacket, Silas shifted his weight slightly, and Ada understood without a word passing: *he keeps something there.*

It was Rudge himself who broke first. They always did. That was the tragedy of it, and the mercy.

"They said it would be quick money," he said, staring into the middle distance where, presumably, the night in question lived. "Three pounds. Three pounds, missus, for one evening's work. Do you know what three pounds was to me in '45? My girl had the fever. The doctor wanted two pound just to come up the stairs."

Ada said nothing. She let the number sit there, ugly and small, doing its work.

"There was a man met me at the weir steps. Never gave a name. Well-dressed — too well-dressed for the river, gloves and all. Had a chest with him. Long, like a coffin for a thin man." Rudge's mouth twisted. "Which I suppose is what it was."

"What was in it?"

"Clothes. Boots. Papers, maybe — I didn't look, and he didn't let me look. And a hat. Good hat." He laughed, and it was the worst sound Ada had heard in weeks, dry as a cork pulled from an empty bottle. "You know what I remember? The hat. Floating off downstream after we tipped the chest over, bobbing along like it owned the river. I thought: *that's a good hat to waste.* Three years, and that's what comes back to me at night. The hat."

"And the man in the well-dressed clothes," Ada said gently. "Was he the dead man?"

Rudge shook his head slowly. "The dead man wasn't there at all. That's the thing nobody asks. There was no body. There was a hat and a coat and a story, and I rowed home alone, and by morning there were posters on the bridge saying Mr. Edmund Vane, partner of Hartwell & Vane, had fallen from his boat in the dark, poor gentleman, drowned, a loss to the district." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I told myself it was harmless. A man wants out of his debts, a man pays three pound to be dead — who's harmed? Nobody's harmed. That's what I told my girl, when she grew up and asked why I couldn't sleep."

"But someone was harmed," Silas said.

It was the first thing he'd said since they sat down, and it went through the table like a nail through plank. Ada didn't look at him, but she felt him — felt the seven years of counted bales and swallowed words standing up inside him and taking shape as a sentence.

Rudge stared at him. "What would you know about it, lad?"

"I know what happens to men who fall," Silas said. "In the mills. On the boats. In the dark. Somebody always rows home alone afterwards, and tells themselves nobody was harmed." He paused. "My mother dived for pearls. She taught me that the water gives back whatever you put in it. Eventually."

The silence that followed had a different quality than the one before it. Rudge looked at Silas, then at Ada, and something in him settled — the settling of a man who has been carrying a sack of stones and has finally been given permission to put it down, even if only for a moment.

"They found bodies," he said quietly. "After. Did you know that? Two men, that winter. Mill men. Fell in the cut, the papers said. Drunk, the papers said." His jaw worked. "I saw one of them hauled out. There weren't a drop of drink in that lad — I knew him, he drank nothing but tea, his sweetheart told everyone, tea and chapel. But drunk was easier to print. Drunk explains everything in this town."

Ada's skin prickled. Two mill men, the winter after Vane's death. She thought of Petch's red-bound ledger, of names entered as *transported*, of Nell Barrow's arm nearly taken by an unguarded machine, of a pattern so consistent it might have been drawn with a ruler.

Vane hadn't faked his death and vanished. He'd faked his death and *stayed* — stayed in the dark of the district, growing fat on other men's misfortunes, and the drowning had merely been the first page of the new ledger.

"Mr. Rudge," she said. "Has anyone been to see you lately? About that night?"

And there it was — the flinch again, the left hand pressed flat against his jacket, over the breast pocket, over whatever paper or coin or threat lived there.

"He came," Rudge whispered. "Month ago. Same gloves. Older, greyer, but I knew the voice. He sat where you're sitting and he bought me a gin and he asked me did I remember our evening together, pleasant-like, friendly-like. And then he said the district was full of curious people these days, asking questions about old drownings, and that a man who'd helped stage one might find himself in the papers himself. Manslaughter, he said. Accessory. Said it like he was discussing the price of hops." Rudge's eyes were wet now, and furious with being wet. "Then he left this."

His hand came out of the jacket. Not a note. A coin — a guinea, gold, bright as a new sin.

"He pays me a guinea every month," Rudge said. "To stay quiet. Do you understand? He *pays* me. Three pound to kill him, and a guinea a month to keep him dead. I'm the only man in Manchester drawing wages from a corpse." He pushed the coin across the table with one finger, as though it burned. "Take it. Take it and take me with it. Because if he finds out I've talked, I'm a dead man, and I don't mean figures of speech. I mean the cut. I mean drunk in the papers. I mean my girl reading it over breakfast."

Ada looked at the guinea. Evidence, she thought. The first piece of the man himself — his money, his habit, his method. Buy the small men, frighten the middle ones, own the top. A whole architecture of fear, funded monthly.

"We'll take you to Finch," she said. "Obadiah Finch, the printer. You'll be safe there — safer than anywhere on this river. And your testimony goes into print, Mr. Rudge, with your name attached if you'll allow it, and the whole district will know exactly whose boat came back empty."

"My name in print." Rudge tested the phrase like a bad tooth. Then, slowly, he nodded. "Aye. All right. I've been dead three years myself, missus, walking about. Might as well come alive loud."

* * *

They walked him out through the grey drizzle, Silas on one side of the boatman and Ada on the other, and if the landlady noticed the arrangement she had the professional wisdom to notice nothing at all. The canal kept pace with them, sliding alongside like a chaperone who knew all their secrets.

Rudge talked as they walked — the dammed-up talk of three years, spilling in fits and starts. The gloves. The voice. How Vane — though he still wouldn't say the name above a whisper, as if the dead might sue — had known things about the mills no outsider should know. How he'd mentioned men by name, mill men, in a way that made Rudge's blood run cold even then.

"He said something," Rudge said suddenly, stopping under the gasworks wall. "That night, in the boat. I never told anyone, because it made no sense, but it's been rattling in my skull ever since. I asked him — foolish, I know, but I'd had a gin myself — I asked him, *why fake it, why not just run honest?* And he laughed. Gentle laugh, like I'd asked a child's question. And he said—"

Rudge's throat moved.

"He said, *'Because a dead man can't be ruined, Tam. And I intend to do a great deal of ruining.'*"

The rain ticked on the gasworks roof. Somewhere across the water, a mill whistle blew the noon hour, and ten thousand souls Ada could not see bent to their machines.

"He's still ruining," Silas said quietly. "He hasn't stopped. Hartwell pays him. Crowther takes his money. And in a fortnight, by our count, he means to sell everything and vanish for good — unless we finish this first."

"Then we'd best be quick," Ada said, and meant it with her whole cold heart.

They turned toward Finch's cellar, three abreast against the drizzle, the guinea safe in Ada's pocket and the boatman's testimony safe in her notebook. Evidence gathering, she thought. Piece by piece, name by name, the ghost was acquiring a body.

And then, as they crossed the bridge, she saw it — a figure on the far bank, standing motionless in the rain beneath an umbrella too fine for that neighborhood. Gloves. Even at this distance, even through the grey curtain of the weather, she would have sworn to the gloves.

The figure touched the brim of its hat — a good hat — and turned, unhurried, and was gone.

Ada's hand found Silas's sleeve.

"He knows," she said. "Walk faster."
