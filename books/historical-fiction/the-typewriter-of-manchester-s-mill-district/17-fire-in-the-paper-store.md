## 17. Fire in the Paper Store

The night the paper store burned, I smelled it before anyone else did.

Seven years in a mill district teaches you the smell of things. Coal smoke and wool grease and the sour breath of the dye vats — those you learn to sleep through. But burning paper has a different voice than burning coal. It is thinner. Sweeter. It sounds like something being *said* quickly, all at once, before it can be unsaid.

I was awake because I had not been sleeping well since the chapel meeting. Sleep had become a negotiation between my body, which wanted rest, and my conscience, which wanted to rehearse every name in Petch's red-bound book until I could recite them standing on one leg in front of ten thousand people. My conscience was winning, which is why I was lying flat as a plank at half past eleven when the smell came up the stairwell of the counting-house lodgings like an unwelcome visitor.

I sat up. Listened. The district made its usual noises — a cart somewhere, a dog with opinions about the cart — but underneath them, faint and wrong, that sweet thin burning.

Then the bell began.

Not the mill whistle. The *fire* bell, the old cracked one outside the parish hall that sounded like a spoon dropped down a well. And beneath it, voices. Shouting. Boots.

I was dressed before I finished deciding to be.

* * *

The Hartwell & Vane paper store stood at the back of the main yard, a long brick building with a slate roof, built by men who understood exactly one thing about paper: that it burns. They had built it apart from everything else for precisely this reason, which meant that when it finally did burn, it burned alone, magnificently, and nothing could be done.

By the time I reached the yard there were perhaps forty people out, most of them strikers who had no love for Hartwell & Vane and even less reason to save its records. A bucket chain had formed anyway — that is the thing about mill folk that outsiders never understand. We will hate a master his whole life and still run toward his fire, because a fire does not care whose property it eats, and next week it might be ours.

"Where's the water?" someone shouted.

"The cistern's locked!" That was Nell Barrow, arriving at a dead run with her arm still stiff from the machine, her hair escaping its cap in furious wisps. "Locked! Who locks a cistern at night?"

"Men with something to hide," said Ada Quill, materializing at my shoulder the way she always did, as though she'd been folded into the darkness earlier and merely unfolded now. Her eyes were doing their quick arithmetic — flames, roofline, wind direction, exits. "Silas."

"I know," I said.

"The archive's in there."

"I know."

"Everything. Every indenture. Every accident report Grady hasn't burned yet. The shipping manifests. The—"

"*I know.*"

She stopped. Looked at me properly. Whatever she saw in my face made her go quiet, because she understood what I was not saying, which was: seven years of my life are filed in that building under somebody else's name.

The heat came off the doors in waves. The brick was glowing along one seam like a kiln. Through the high windows the light pulsed orange, and papers — actual sheets of them, freed and flying — went up the chimney of the night air like black birds.

"They'll say it was the strikers," Nell said grimly. She was wringing her hands without noticing she was doing it. "You mark me. Ten thousand of us out of work and angry, and a convenient fire? Crowther will have warrants written before the ash cools."

"He already has warrants," Ada said. "He just needs an excuse to use them." She turned to me. "There's a loading door on the canal side. Old, rusted, half off its track. If the fire came in from the office end—"

"It did." I don't know how I knew. I simply knew, the way I knew masters' moods, the way I knew weather coming off the Irwell. "It started small and low and at the far end, near the clerk's desk. Somebody poured something and walked away."

"Then the canal-side racks might still be whole. For another few minutes, at least."

We looked at each other. Behind us the bucket chain sloshed uselessly against a wall of heat; ahead of us the loading door glowed at its edges like a mouth deciding whether to speak.

"You can't swim," Ada reminded me. It was true. I had never learned, which is a strange confession from a pearl diver's child, but my father drowned before he could teach me, and after that my mother forbade deep water with a ferocity I inherited whole. "And the smoke—"

"I grew up holding my breath," I said. "For pearls. My mother used to say I could outwait a heron." I heard my own voice and was surprised by how steady it was. "The racks on the canal side hold the older files. Pre-1845. That's where mine would be. That's where *his* would be."

"Whose?"

"Petch's. He kept duplicates. He told me once — 'a clerk who keeps only one copy of anything deserves whatever happens to him.' I thought he was making a joke about himself." My throat tightened. "He wasn't."

Ada took both my shoulders. She was smaller than me by half a head and yet for a moment I felt held in place like a nail under a hammer. "Two minutes," she said. "Wet cloth over your face. If the door sticks, you come out. If the rack falls, you come out. If God Himself tells you one more box, you come *out*, Silas Ravensworth, because a dead witness saves nobody."

"And you'll be—"

"At the door. Counting."

* * *

I have been asked since — by Finch, gently, over pipes that needed relighting more often than they were smoked — what it was like inside. People expect poetry. Fire, they think, must be described in poetry.

Here is the truth. It was loud. Louder than any machine in the mill, louder than the whistle, a sound like the building tearing its own shirt. The heat did not feel like heat; it felt like a hand pressing on your chest, insisting. The smoke came down in layers, black below and brown above, and I went through it bent double with a soaked sacking sack over my head, breathing through wet wool and thinking, absurdly, of my mother's kitchen, of bread rising under a cloth.

The loading door gave on the third pull. Inside, the canal-side racks stood in a pocket of relative mercy — the fire had gone the other way, greedy for the newer papers, the ones Vane wanted dead. I found later that this told its own story. An accidental fire eats indiscriminately. This one had manners. It had a menu.

The boxes were labeled in Petch's own tidy hand: *INDENTURES 1839–41*, *INDENTURES 1841–43*, and so on, year by year, each one a drawer full of children sold on paper. I had perhaps ninety seconds. Maybe less. The smoke was lowering; the heat on my neck was turning from insistence to command.

I could take one box. Ada had said two minutes and I am a man who keeps count.

So I did the arithmetic of a coward and a hero at once, which is to say I did it fast. The newest boxes — my own year, 1841 — sat nearest the door. But the oldest box, 1839, held the first forged indentures, the ones with the guardian signatures that matched across a dozen children who had never shared a guardian in their lives. That was the pattern. That was the proof that it wasn't one clerk's error but a system, a trade, a *business*. One box proves a mistake. Two prove a method.

I dragged the 1839 box to the door. Then I went back for 1841.

I want to be honest about why. Partly it was the proof — two years, two patterns, unanswerable. But partly — and Finch would nod sadly at this, because he understands that honesty costs more than courage — partly it was because my own name was in that box. *Silas Ravensworth, indentured to Hartwell & Vane, with consent of guardian:* and then a signature belonging to a man who had never existed, selling a child who had never been asked. I wanted that paper. I wanted to hold the thing that had owned me, wanted it in my hands where it could be torn in front of witnesses someday, wanted it the way a prisoner wants the key even after the cell door swings loose.

The rack groaned as I pulled. Above me, something in the roof shifted with a sound like a giant clearing his throat.

I got the second box to the door. My arms were shaking. The wet cloth over my face had dried to a hot iron taste. And then — because the universe enjoys testing its hypotheses — I saw, wedged behind the last box, a slim red spine.

Petch's duplicate ledger. Or a part of it. The red-bound book itself we had already pulled from the rafters months ago; this was its working twin, the daily copy, the one with entries dated *after* Petch died. Someone had kept writing in it. Someone had kept recording 'transportations' and 'discharges' in that same careful hand, right up until three weeks ago.

Vane had kept the books going. The dead man's ghost had been filing reports.

My lungs were screaming. The doorway was four steps away and the smoke had decided, all at once, that it was tired of letting me live. I grabbed the red book, tucked it inside my shirt against my chest like a stolen loaf, and ran.

* * *

I came out of the loading door into the night air the way a fish comes out of water — too much of everything, light and cold and noise — and collapsed onto the cobbles with two boxes and a heartbeat going like a loom.

Ada was there instantly, hauling the sacking off my face, and Nell was there, and then there was a general commotion involving water, which I drank half of and wore the other half.

"Two boxes," Ada said, counting aloud as promised, her voice cracking slightly on the number. "You absolute — I said if God told you one more box—"

"God didn't tell me," I wheezed. "Petch did."

From the safety of twenty feet, the paper store roared through its roof and began, at last, to die. There was nothing left in it worth saving and nothing left in it worth burning either; the fire seemed to understand it had been hired for a job and, having done it, lost interest. The bucket chain gave up with the collective sigh of people who had known all along it was hopeless. Somebody's grandmother appeared with blankets. Somebody else's uncle produced, from nowhere, a flask of gin that made the rounds and improved everyone's account of events considerably.

We carried the boxes to Finch's cellar by the back ways, wrapped in blankets like foundlings, and it was only there, under the lamp, that we opened them.

Finch stood over the first box with the expression of a man at a graveside who has just heard the ground speak. His great blunt fingers hovered above the papers without touching them, as if they might bruise.

"Every one of these children," he said quietly, "was pronounced transported. Australia. Van Diemen's Land. And here's the paper saying so, signed, witnessed, filed." He looked up. "They never sailed. Did they."

"No," I said.

"And this—" he lifted a sheet, read the guardian's name, lifted another, read the same name again, and his jaw worked like a press. "*One* guardian. Forty-one children. One man signing himself father to the entire orphan population of Manchester." He set the paper down with exaggerated gentleness, the way you put down something you want very badly to throw. "This is the whole rotten machine in a box."

Nell had found her own family's name. She didn't say anything. She just held the page and read it over and over, her lips moving, and Ada put an arm around her, and none of us pretended not to see, because pretending not to see is the disease we are all trying to cure.

It was Ada, finally, who drew the red book from my shirt and opened it — and went very still.

"Silas. Look at the dates."

I looked. Entries in Petch's careful hand — or a hand imitating it well enough to fool a tired eye — running month after month, year after year, past the date of Petch's death, past the date of Vane's drowning, right up to three weeks ago. The last entry was a discharge. A girl of eleven, discharged to a factory in Bolton, witnessed by J. Grady.

Three weeks ago. While we had been gathering evidence, the evidence had been *maintained*. Updated. Tidied.

"He wasn't just erasing," Ada said slowly. "He was keeping the fiction alive. A forgery you stop tending starts to smell. He's been watering it like a plant." She looked up, and I saw the thing in her face that I had seen only twice before — once at the magistrate's dinner, once the night we decoded the blackmail letters. Fear, wearing calculation's clothes. "Silas. If he's still writing entries, he's still here. Not abroad. Not hiding. Here, close enough to walk into the archive tonight with a lamp and a can of oil."

"And now he knows what survived," Finch rumbled. He had begun, without appearing to decide to, stacking the saved papers into a waterproof oilcloth with the practiced motions of a man who had hidden pamphlets through two decades of libel prosecutions. "A fire destroys everything or it destroys nothing. Ours destroyed *almost* everything. When Vane hears what's missing — two boxes and a book — he won't wonder. He'll know. And he'll come looking."

Outside, far off, the mill whistle blew the midnight shift change to empty streets. Ten thousand silent windows. A district holding its breath.

Ada smoothed the last entry flat on the table and read it aloud, softly, like a prayer said in a language she was only learning:

"*Discharged, Ellen Marsh, aged eleven, to Bolton. Witnessed, J. Grady.*"

She looked around the lamplit cellar, at each of us in turn.

"Grady signed it three weeks ago. Which means Grady is still signing. Which means—" she tapped the page, once, twice, "—we have days, not weeks. Maybe not even that. Because whoever wrote this entry doesn't know yet that we're holding the proof of every other one."

Finch tied the oilcloth shut with a sailor's knot and set his palm flat upon it, the way a man lays a hand on a Bible.

"Then let's make sure he finds out," he said, "at a time and place of *our* choosing."

Somewhere above us, boots crossed the street overhead — slow, unhurried, passing — and every one of us stopped breathing until they faded.
