## 31. Vane's Last Purchase

Edmund Vane had always believed that a man's character could be read in the way he handled money, and so he handled his own the way a surgeon handles a scalpel: precisely, without sentiment, and with a full appreciation of how much damage it could do.

The shipping office on Quay Street smelled of tar, tea, and the particular desperation of men who wanted to leave England quickly. Vane found it bracing. He had taken a private room at the Mermaid's Rest — not the best inn in Manchester, but the one whose proprietor owed him eleven pounds and therefore asked no questions about guests who arrived after dark and paid in advance. Eleven pounds bought a great deal of discretion in this city. It always had.

"Passage for two to Hamburg," he told the clerk, laying the coins out in neat rows, "and from there, arrangements south. I'll want a cabin with a window. My wife is delicate."

The clerk wrote *Mr. and Mrs. Ashworth* in the ledger, because that was the name Vane had given him, and because clerks, like magistrates and partners and boatmen, were all of them men who could be purchased at rates Vane considered frankly insulting. He had once bribed a Member of Parliament for less than it cost to re-roof a warehouse. The MP had wept with gratitude. Vane had found the tears distasteful but useful; they meant the man would never dare speak of it again.

"The *Cormorant* sails on Thursday's morning tide," said the clerk. "Weather permitting."

"Weather," said Vane mildly, "has never once permitted anything. It merely declines to interfere."

He collected his receipt, tipped the boy who held the door — another small investment, another pair of eyes that would remember nothing — and stepped out into the grey Manchester afternoon.

* * *

The noise reached him before he was halfway down the street.

It was not an unusual sound, these days. For three weeks the mill district had been growling like a dog kept from its dinner: the strike had emptied the mills, filled the squares, and turned ten thousand idle hands into ten thousand clapping, chanting, singing nuisances. Vane heard them now from the corner of Withy Grove — a low oceanic roar punctuated by the shriek of a brass band that had no business being within a mile of respectable commerce.

He stopped, adjusted his gloves, and listened the way a physician listens to a chest complaint.

*Justice. Justice. The ledgers. Read the ledgers.*

Ah. So they were still on about the ledgers.

Vane smiled. It was a genuine smile, which made it more effective than any performed one. He had spent thirty years cultivating the ability to be genuinely amused by things that would destroy other men, and it had served him admirably. Grief, fear, guilt — these were expenses. Amusement was free.

They thought the ledgers mattered. That was the charming part. They had seized a stack of paper from a counting-house and believed themselves armed, as though ink were iron and arithmetic a cannon. He had watched the crowd from the upper window of the Mermaid's Rest two nights past — a sea of shawls and flat caps, lit by flares, listening while some printer fellow read names off a page in a voice like a Sunday sermon. And the crowd had roared at each name as if roaring could raise the dead or unmake a signature.

Money, Vane reflected, walking on, was the only thing in this world that remembered properly. A man might forget a face, a promise, a debt of gratitude. But money kept accounts with perfect fidelity. Money did not tire, did not die, did not stand in the rain shouting. Money simply waited, patient as tide, and went where it was sent.

And Vane had been sending his for years. Quietly. Carefully. Into Hamburg banks and Antwerp houses, into names that existed only on paper he controlled, into accounts that no magistrate in Lancashire could freeze because no magistrate in Lancashire knew they existed. The fortune of Hartwell & Vane had leaked out of Hartwell & Vane drop by drop for a decade, the way a barrel leaks in a damp cellar — imperceptibly, until one day you tipped it and nothing came out but the smell.

Josiah Hartwell still believed the firm was worth saving. That was the beauty of Josiah: a man who would rather bleed his own workers white than admit he'd been bled himself. Every month the hush payment went north, and every month Josiah paid it with the expression of a man donating to a charity he despised, and every month Vane raised the price by a fraction small enough to swallow and large enough to taste. By now Josiah was paying him to preserve the fiction of his own respectability. There were worse businesses. Not many, Vane granted, but some.

As for the rest of it — the boatman, the foreman Grady, the girl with the crushed arm, the Quill woman with her ink-stained fingers and her inconvenient talent for reading other people's correspondence — well. Problems were like debts. You did not panic over them. You serviced them, you consolidated them, and when the time came, you left the country holding the paper and let the creditors sort out the ruins.

Thursday's tide. Two days.

* * *

Back in his room, Vane locked the door, drew the curtain, and set about the evening's business, which was fire.

He had a small tin stove in the corner, the kind sailors used, and into it he fed his remaining letters one by one, watching each take the flame with the mild satisfaction of a man settling accounts. This was not haste. Haste was for amateurs. This was hygiene.

The letter from the Hamburg house — confirming the transfer, signed with the initials he used for such things. Burned.

The note from the boatman Hobbs, three years old now, requesting — *requesting*, the word still amused him — assurance that the arrangement regarding "the unfortunate accident of the 14th" would remain between gentlemen. Hobbs drank too much and talked too much and had recently begun talking to people Vane did not control. A problem for another day, or possibly for no day at all, since by Friday Hobbs's testimony would be worth precisely what a drunk man's word was worth against a gentleman already safely beyond the jurisdiction. Burned.

The letter to Magistrate Crowther — not a bribe, never a bribe, Vane prided himself on never writing anything that could be called a bribe — but a courteous note regarding "the matter of the seditious publications," with a suggestion about warrants enclosed in language so oblique that Crowther himself might not have understood it fully. Crowther hadn't needed to understand it. He'd needed only to feel it, the way a horse feels the rein. Burned.

Last of all, a letter he had written and never sent, addressed to Josiah Hartwell, in which Vane had set down — purely for the pleasure of writing it — the entire history of their acquaintance: the debts Josiah didn't know he'd inherited, the indentures forged in a hand so perfect that even Petch, that tedious little mole of a clerk, had filed them without a murmur, the drowned men written down as *transported*, the fires recorded as *misadventure*. Vane had composed it one insomniac night in Liverpool, seven years ago, and had kept it ever since the way other men kept a lock of hair. It was, he felt, the truest thing he had ever written. Nobody would ever read it. That was rather the point of truth, in his experience.

He held it over the stove a moment longer than the others.

Then he let it go, and watched Edmund Vane curl up into ash, and felt nothing whatsoever, which he took as proof of his excellent health.

* * *

There came a knock at the door around nine o'clock. Vane opened it to find the innkeeper's daughter, a girl of perhaps fifteen with a chilblained nose, holding a tray of supper he had not ordered.

"I didn't order this," he said pleasantly.

"No, sir. It's from the gentleman downstairs. He said you'd know."

Vane looked at the tray. Cold mutton, bread, a pot of mustard, and a folded note tucked under the plate. He took the note, gave the girl a coin — investments, always investments — and shut the door.

The handwriting was Josiah Hartwell's, and it had deteriorated badly since Vane had last seen it, slumping across the page like a tired man in a chair.

*E.,*

*The price has gone past bearing. I have given you everything and it is not enough, and I begin to think nothing will ever be enough, which I suspect you knew before I did.*

*I am told the district means to read the ledgers publicly whether the courts allow it or no. If my name is in those pages — and we both know it is — then I am finished regardless of what you do or where you go. So consider this my last letter on the subject: I will pay Thursday's sum, and not a farthing more, and after that you may go to the devil or to Hamburg, whichever is farther.*

*You were the cleverest man I ever met. I used to think that was a recommendation.*

*J.H.*

Vane read it twice, smiling more broadly the second time. Then he tore it into strips and fed it to the stove, and sat down to the cold mutton, which was excellent, because misery always catered well.

So Josiah was cracking. Of course he was. Josiah had been cracking for years; he was simply a slow crack, the kind that takes a whole winter to split a beam. It changed nothing. By the time Josiah worked up the courage to confess anything to anyone, Vane would be two hundred miles away and gaining, and whatever Josiah said would land on ears that had already heard it from a thousand shouting mill hands, and would therefore be old news, and old news buys no convictions abroad. Extradition? Vane nearly laughed into his mustard. He had watched English law attempt extradition twice in his career, and both times the process had died of paperwork before reaching the coast.

He ate, and poured himself the last of the claret, and stood at the window looking out over the rooftops toward the district, where the flares were burning again tonight and the band was playing again — the same tuneless, defiant thing — and the crowd was roaring again, and somewhere in all that noise a printer and a pearl-diver's whelp and a crippled girl and a widow's daughter with ink on her fingers believed they were building something that would last.

Let them build it, Vane thought generously. Let them have their reading and their rally and their grand yard-trial, their little theatre of justice with the whole district for an audience. Theatre was exactly the right word. He had seen crowds before. He had seen Chartists by the ten thousand, and plug riots, and general strikes that were going to remake England by Saturday, and every single one of them had ended the same way: hunger, rain, and forgetting. Memory was a currency that devalued overnight. In six months the names read aloud in the yard would be a story grandmothers told inaccurately. In six years, a rumor. In twenty, nothing at all — while his money, quietly compounding in Hamburg, would still be there, faithful, patient, remembering everything on his behalf.

A man could lose his name, his country, his reputation, even his life. Vane intended to lose all four, in that order, and come out ahead.

He finished the wine, banked the stove, and checked his valise one final time: papers under false names, coin sewn into the lining, the passage receipt, and — because he was not entirely without sentiment, only nearly — a single pearl, small and grey, bought long ago from a diver's family at a price that would have made the diver weep had he known what it cost to get. Vane had always liked pearls. Formed by irritation, worn by the wealthy, and worth whatever the buyer said they were worth. He had built a career on the same principle.

He slept soundly. He always slept soundly. Guilt, like memory, was a luxury for men with less capital.

* * *

In the morning he rose early, settled his account with the innkeeper — overpaying, deliberately, so that the man would swear forever afterward that Mr. Ashworth had been a perfect gentleman — and walked down through the waking streets toward the quay with his collar up and his step unhurried.

Fog lay on the Irwell like a laid tablecloth, thick and white, and the masts along the quay rose out of it like the ribs of drowned ships. Somewhere out on the water a bell was ringing, slow and regular. The *Cormorant*'s crew were loading the last crates, their voices muffled to ghosts by the fog. It was, Vane thought, a fitting morning for departure: grey, quiet, and blind in every direction.

He presented his ticket at the gangplank. The mate checked his list by lantern light, lips moving.

"Ashworth," said the mate. "Party of two. Where's your lady, then?"

"My wife follows with the luggage," said Vane smoothly. "She'll be along directly."

The mate shrugged and waved him aboard, and Vane walked up the plank onto the deck of his deliverance, and stood at the rail with his hands clasped behind him, watching the fog, feeling the particular deep contentment of a man whose accounts are all balanced.

Behind him, back along the quay, footsteps were coming fast through the fog — two sets of them, one light and quick, one with the odd, rolling gait of someone who had learned to walk on a dock before learning to walk anywhere else — and voices, low and urgent, asking the harbormaster a question.

Vane did not turn around. He had won too many games by refusing to look over his shoulder, and besides, no one could possibly have learned the ship, the name, the tide.

No one.

The fog swallowed the footsteps, and the bell rang on, and the tide began to turn.
