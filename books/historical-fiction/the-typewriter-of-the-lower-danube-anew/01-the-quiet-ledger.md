## 1. The Quiet Ledger

The ledgers of Herr Brandl's grain concern were, by any reasonable standard, a work of art.

Anton Reiter had made them so. He arrived at the office on the Fleischmarkt at seven each morning, before the stove had warmed and before Frau Pichler the bookkeeper's widow could begin her daily campaign to feed him, and he ruled his columns with a straightedge and the patience of a monk. Every figure sat in its place like a soldier at inspection. Every entry balanced. If a barge came in short forty kilos of wheat, Anton Reiter found those forty kilos on paper before lunch, because a man who could not account for forty kilos of wheat was a man who would eventually be asked to account for himself — and that was a question Anton Reiter had arranged never to answer.

Oscar Winterbourne, who did not exist in this town except as a rumor of a clerk from Linz with no family and dull eyes, understood this better than anyone alive.

He was twenty-nine years old and he had been dead for two years, which he had found, on the whole, restful.

* * *

"You've ink on your ear again," said Frau Pichler, materializing beside his desk with the plate of pastries she deployed each morning like artillery. "A growing boy needs more than coffee."

"I'm not growing, Frau Pichler. I'm twenty-six." He had settled on twenty-six; it explained the smoothness of his face and excused him from conversations about the war, since a man of twenty-six might plausibly have spent it anywhere, doing nothing, knowing nothing.

"Twenty-six," she sniffed, setting down the plate anyway, because refusal was not among her recognized outcomes. "In my day a young man your age had a sweetheart and a Sunday suit. What do you have?"

"A very good pen," said Anton Reiter, and she laughed and swatted at him with her dish towel, and he endured it with the mild smile that was one of the four expressions he permitted himself. The others were: attentive, faintly bored, apologetic, and — reserved for Herr Brandl's rants about the Russians and the Americans and the price of everything — sympathetic but noncommittal. It was a small repertoire. It had kept him alive.

That was the thing about being nobody, Oscar had learned. Nobody is a full-time occupation. You cannot be nobody carelessly. You must be nobody at breakfast, nobody when the gendarme Kralik strolls past the window and glances in out of habit rather than suspicion, nobody when the radio in the café plays the news and every head bends toward it like grass in wind. You must be nobody even in your own skull, or nearly so, and this last part was the hardest work of all, harder than the ledgers, harder than the Austrian accent he had sanded down syllable by syllable through a winter in Passau.

Because the skull, unfortunately, kept its own books. And those columns did not balance.

* * *

Each night, in the rented room above the cobbler's shop on Wasserstrasse, Oscar performed his other accounting.

The room contained a bed, a chair, a washstand, a candle stub, and a tin box that had once held tea and now held the only true things left in the world: thirty-one pages of his former life. A photograph of a garden in Vienna, taken the summer before everything. A railway ticket, unused. A laundry list in his mother's hand, kept for the handwriting. And beneath these, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, the documents — the ones he had been ordered to destroy in the last week of the republic, the ones he had carried instead across three borders in the lining of a coat, the ones that had cost two men their lives and were still, somehow, in his possession, like a debt that followed a man across water.

And there were the pages he burned.

This was the ritual. Each night he took one page — never more, never fewer — from a separate folder, pages of no operational value, pages that existed only because he could not bring himself to throw them away whole. A letter from his sister. A program from a concert. A page torn from a notebook in Ilse Brandt's slanted, impatient hand, a recipe for plum dumplings annotated with her opinion of the cook. He read it once, slowly, letting the words do whatever they did to him, and then he held the corner to the candle and watched it curl and blacken and go up the chimney flue into the Danube fog, where no one could ever read it again, including him.

It was, he recognized, a strange form of mourning: grief administered in nightly doses, like medicine. His superiors — the dead men who had given him orders — would have called it discipline. Destruction of compromising material, one item per diem. But it wasn't discipline. It was the opposite. It was the only way he knew to keep saying goodbye without ever having to finish saying it, because a man who finished saying goodbye to all thirty-one pages would be no one at all, and then what would be left to hide?

Tonight's page was the concert program. Dvořák. He remembered the evening entire: the heat of the hall, Ilse whispering that the second movement went on too long, his own scandalized delight. *You're not supposed to say that,* he'd told her. *I know,* she'd said. *That's why I said it.*

He read it twice, which broke his rule, and then he burned it, which restored it, and stood at the cold window watching the ash rise until his fingers ached from the chill.

Below, the river moved in the dark, patient as arithmetic. Somewhere downriver, barges were loading. Somewhere upriver, the world continued to sort itself into new regimes, new papers, new names for old crimes. And here in the town of Krems-und-Stein-or-nearly, Anton Reiter blew out his candle and lay down and slept the dreamless sleep of a man whose columns balanced.

Mostly.

* * *

Thursday brought the quarterly inventory, which meant Herr Brandl spent the day in a state of ceremonial outrage, discovering discrepancies that Anton Reiter had already resolved and taking credit for solutions Reiter had written up in triplicate. By four o'clock the old man had worked himself into a fine lather about the flour mill at Mautern ("thieves, the lot of them, thieves with invoices") and collapsed gratefully into his chair, and Oscar took the opportunity to walk down to the post office, because walking to the post office was part of being nobody. Nobodies collect mail. Nobodies stand in queues and complain about the weather with the other nobodies.

There was never anything. In two years there had been exactly eleven items, all bills, all addressed to Anton Reiter, all disappointingly ordinary. He had come to regard the post office as a kind of chapel of anonymity: you attended, you stood, you received nothing, you were confirmed in your invisibility. It was almost liturgical.

The postmistress, a narrow woman named Fuchs who had the eyes of a customs inspector, sorted through the pigeonholes with her usual air of finding the entire postal system personally distasteful.

"Reiter," she said. "Nothing." Then she paused, and looked again at something in her hand, and looked at him over her spectacles in a way that made the back of his neck prickle. "Although."

"Although?"

She held up an envelope. Ordinary cream paper. Ordinary stamp, smudged cancellation. But she held it the way one holds a fish that may or may not still be alive.

"It came up from the sorting depot with the wrong mark on it," she said. "They sent it back twice. Third time they just let it come." She turned it over. "No return address. Odd paper, too. Heavy." Her eyes narrowed. "You expecting anything?"

"No," said Anton Reiter, with the apologetic expression, number four in the repertoire, though inside, somewhere below the ribs, something had begun — quietly, terribly — to hope.

"Hm." She surrendered it across the counter as though releasing a prisoner. "Sign."

He signed. His false name, in his careful clerk's hand, on the line, and the pen scratched, and outside the gulls were arguing over the fish market as they always argued, and the world proceeded in its ordinary grooves.

Except.

He stood under the arcade across from the post office for a full minute before he dared look at the envelope properly. Heavy cream stock. Good paper — the kind of paper that had become nearly unobtainable, the kind that got hoarded and traded. And the address, in blue-black ink, in a hand that was slanted and impatient, the letters leaning forward as though they were in a hurry to be done with politeness:

**Herr Anton Reiter, Buchhalter, bei H. Brandl Getreidehandlung, am Fleischmarkt.**

That was correct. That was all correct. That was his life, addressed accurately, delivered to the right man.

But it was the second line, smaller, tucked beneath the address like a signature under a painting, that stopped his breath in his chest and made the arcade tilt gently, like a deck at anchor:

*For the man who owes me a dumpling.*

He knew that hand. He had spent two years burning it, one page per night, teaching himself to forget it, and he could have reproduced it from memory to the millimeter. The impatient forward lean. The way she crossed her t's like small accusations.

Ilse Brandt's handwriting.

Ilse Brandt, who had died in the winter of '44 — so he had been told, so he had believed, so he had built two years of careful grief upon — writing to a name that did not exist, in a town she should not have known, calling in a joke from a garden that no longer existed either.

His hands were steady. Two years of practice saw to that. Only his heart misbehaved, hammering like a debtor at the door.

He did not open it there. A man who was nobody did not stand in public trembling over letters. He walked home the long way, along the water, past the cranes and the coal heaps, composing his face into attentiveness, into boredom, into nothing at all, while inside him the meticulous false ledger of Anton Reiter quietly, catastrophically, refused to balance.

Upstairs, behind the locked door, with the candle lit though it was barely dusk, he opened it.

One page. Blue-black ink. Slanted, impatient, alive.

*You always were difficult to find. Fortunately, I know how you think — you'll hate hearing that. Come alone. Ask for the widow at the flooded mill above the weir, and bring what you were told to burn.*

*— I.B.*

*P.S. Kovac will find you. Don't run. He hates running.*

Oscar Winterbourne — dead two years, buried under a clerk's name, keeper of perfect columns — sat down slowly on the cobbler's spare chair and read it again, and again, while the candle guttered and the river went on moving in the dark below, carrying its barges, its secrets, and now, apparently, its impossible dead.

Somewhere out there in the fog, footsteps were already looking for him.
