## 2. The Stranger at Pier Nine

The ice came early that November, and with it the smell of the river changing — the Neman giving up its summer rot and turning to iron and stone. Juniper Hartwell felt it in her hands first, the way she felt everything the world intended to do to her: through the skin of her palms, cracked from cold water and counting-house ink, before it ever reached her mind.

She was on Pier Nine because Mr. Kessel had decided that crates arriving on the morning tide were to be tallied by noon, and Mr. Kessel's decisions arrived like weather — without explanation, without mercy, and always over her head as though she were furniture that happened to hold a pencil well.

"Count twice," he'd said, not looking up from his ledger. "The Baltic Trading Company does not pay for arithmetic done once."

He did not know she could read what he wrote. That was the arrangement beneath all their arrangements: Juniper Hartwell, indentured, illiterate, useful. A girl who could carry crates and carry silence in equal measure. Her mother had taught her better than anyone alive how much a person could hide inside an ordinary face. *Which silences keep people safe,* her mother used to say, sorting buttons by candlelight, *and which ones only keep them waiting.* Juniper had been keeping silences for five years now, and she still wasn't sure which kind hers were.

The pier smelled of tar and herring brine and the particular sourness of men who hadn't been paid since September. Dockworkers moved along the planking with their collars up, breath steaming, hauling crates stenciled with the company's eagle-and-anchor mark. Somewhere behind the customs shed a gramophone played, thin and warbling, a waltz that had no business being cheerful in a city under occupation.

Juniper worked down the manifest with her pencil stub, marking each crate as it passed. Flour, machine parts, tinned beef, flour again. The war had made everything either food or ammunition, and the paperwork for both looked identical, which was perhaps the most honest thing about it.

Then came the crate that stopped her pencil.

It was small — hardly bigger than a coffin for a child — and it was wrong in a way she couldn't name at first. The other crates were new pine, sharp-edged, smelling of sawmill. This one was old, water-darkened along its lower third, its stenciling half dissolved into gray ghosts. And someone had written across the lid in grease pencil, in a hand that slanted hard to the right as though running:

*PERLA — SALVAGE — HOLD FOR INSPECTION*

Juniper stood very still. Around her, the pier went on breathing — boots, gulls, the groan of rope against bollard — but she heard none of it. She heard instead a kitchen in a house that no longer existed, a man laughing at something her mother said, the particular creak of his boots on the stair, and the word he'd carved into the doorframe the day she was born because he claimed her given name needed a home: *Juniper*, with a little star above the J.

*Perla.* Her father's boat. The pearl boat that went down off the coast five years ago with eleven men aboard, Elias Hartwell among them, and that the Baltic Trading Company had mourned in exactly one paragraph of small print before striking the loss from its books. She had been fourteen. They had told her at the counting-house window, between transactions, the way one tells a girl her fare has gone up.

The crate was supposed to move on. It was addressed onward, to some inspection office whose stamp had blurred beyond reading. All she had to do was mark it received-in-transit and let it pass out of her life the way everything else connected to Elias Hartwell had passed.

Instead she marked it *damaged — held for tally discrepancy* — a lie so small it fit inside a pencil stroke — and had it carried to the back of the shed, behind the barrels of tallow, where discrepancies went to be forgotten.

She told herself it was nothing. She told herself she simply wanted to look at the handwriting, which was foolish, because grease pencil is grease pencil and tells you nothing. But when the last crate was counted and the light had gone the color of weak tea, she pried up one corner of the lid with a crowbar, heart hammering like a fist on a door, and looked inside.

Packing straw. A ship's bell, green with sea-salt corrosion. A coil of rope gone stiff as wire. And beneath it all, wrapped in oilcloth gone brittle as autumn leaf, a ledger.

She knew before she touched it. Some knowledge lives below thought, in the body, in the way a dog knows thunder. The ledger was swollen with water, its cover warped, its spine broken — but the hand inside, even bloomed and blurred by salt water, even faded to the color of old tea, slanted hard to the right as though running.

Her father's hand. Writing entries in a book that had drowned with him.

Juniper sat down on a barrel in the dark of the shed and did not cry, because crying makes noise and noise is a debt, and she put the ledger inside her coat, against her ribs, where it lay like a second heartbeat all the way back through the occupied streets to the counting-house, where she smiled at Mr. Kessel and asked whether he wanted the tally now or after supper.

* * *

She kept it three days before the stranger came.

Three days of carrying it between hiding places — under a floorboard in the tenement on Zawalna Street where Mirele sewed past midnight and her sons slept like felled trees; then back to the shed; then, when that began to feel like leaving a child in a wolf's den, into the false bottom of her own trunk, beneath a layer of mended stockings that smelled of Mirele's lye soap. Three days of reading it by candle stub when the house slept, learning what the dead had written.

And it was strange writing. Strange even for a dead man. The entries began as any cargo ledger might — dates, ports, weights — but the weights made no sense. No sensible skipper loaded four hundred kilograms of *pearls, drilled* on a coaster the size of the Perla; the whole Baltic didn't hold four hundred kilograms of drilled pearls. And the names — there were names threaded through the columns like veins through marble. Not buyers, not consignees. Just names. *R. Abramsky, 12. L. Gordon, 7. The Weisz family, 19.* Numbers beside them, always numbers, and once, in the margin, in smaller script, almost hidden: *count them out, count them all in.*

Juniper read that line forty times. She did not understand it. Understanding would come later, and cost more than she had. For now she only knew that her father's hand had written it, and that the sea had failed to erase it, and that she was committing theft against the Baltic Trading Company every hour she kept it — a crime so small on paper and so enormous in her chest that she sometimes had to stop in the street and breathe until the feeling passed.

The stranger came on the fourth day, at the hour when the counting-house emptied and the lamplighter was still drunk enough to miss half the lamps on Pylimo Street.

He was standing at the end of Pier Nine when she came down with the evening manifests, and she saw him the way her mother had taught her to see people — not his face first but his weight, his placement, the story his body told before his mouth got a chance to lie. He stood like a man who had spent years on moving decks: feet apart, knees soft, one hand resting on a piling as though the ground itself might roll. His coat was good wool gone bad at the cuffs. His face was long and weathered and carried a scar that ran from the corner of his left eye down into his beard, pulling the eye slightly askew, so that his gaze seemed always to arrive a half-second before the rest of him.

"You're the tally-girl," he said. Not a question. In Russian, then, when she didn't answer, and then in Lithuanian, and finally in careful, accented German, as though trying keys in a lock. "Forgive me. I'm looking for someone who handles the salvage."

"The salvage goes through the customs office." Her voice came out flat and bored, a servant's voice, a voice with nothing behind it. "Pier Nine handles crates. Complaints go to the company."

"The Perla," he said.

The name dropped between them like a coin down a well. Juniper kept her face still — she was good at that, she had been practicing stillness since she was nine years old — but inside her something turned over, slow and heavy, like a body in water.

"There's no cargo by that name on my manifest," she said.

"No." He smiled, and the scar pulled, and the smile was worse than not smiling. "There wouldn't be. Salvage comes in quiet. Especially salvage that was never supposed to be found." He reached into his coat, unhurried, and she watched the movement the way prey watches movement, cataloguing where a knife would be, where a paper would be. What came out was neither: a leather wallet, fat, and from it a fold of banknotes. German marks. He peeled two off and held them out.

For a moment she only looked at them. Then she looked closer, and her stomach performed a small cold revolution, because she knew money the way she knew handwriting — from years of counting other people's — and these notes were too clean. Too crisp. The ink too black, the paper too white. Marks this fresh, in a city where honest currency had been chewed to rags by four years of occupation, meant one of two things: a printing press, or a man who wanted very much to seem richer than he was. Either way, they were not the money of an innocent question.

"I don't take tips for counting," she said.

"It's not a tip. It's payment for remembering." He folded the notes away again, unhurried, watching her watch them. "A crate came off a barge Tuesday last. Small. Water-marked. Stenciled *Perla*. It was signed into your shed and never signed out. I've asked at three offices and gotten three different lies, which tells me the truth is worth something." His crooked eye held hers. "You're the last pair of hands it passed through, tally-girl. So. Did you see it?"

Every silence she had ever kept rose up in her like a wall. *Which silences keep people safe.* Her mother's voice, button-soft. And beneath it, another voice — her father's, from the doorframe, from the kitchen, from the drowned pages sleeping under her floorboard — saying *count them out, count them all in.*

"I see a hundred crates a day," Juniper said. "I don't read the stencils. I can't read."

The lie came out smooth as poured oil, and she hated how smooth, hated that five years of practice meant the words cost her nothing visible at all. The stranger studied her for a long moment. Somewhere behind him a gull screamed and was answered by another, and the river slapped the pilings in its patient, indifferent rhythm.

"No," he said at last. "I suppose you can't." He said it the way a man says *of course* when he means *we'll see.* He stepped closer, close enough that she could smell tobacco and cold sweat and, beneath both, faintly, the sea — real sea, deep-water sea, not harbor water. "My name is Tomas. I was asking about the Perla because I have a personal interest. I was her navigator." He let that sit. "Eleven men went into the water that night, girl. Ten were pulled out by a Swedish trawler and told to forget they'd ever been aboard. One was listed as lost who wasn't lost at all. And somewhere between that night and this one, a ledger that belonged to that boat has surfaced in a city full of people who would burn it, or worse, use it. If it reaches certain desks in Berlin, men die. If it reaches certain other desks, other men die. There is no version of this where nobody dies. There is only a version where fewer do."

"And you want me to believe you're the fewer," Juniper said.

Something flickered across his ruined face — surprise, or its cousin, respect. "I want you to remember the crate," he said. "That's all. An arms inquiry is coming, after the armistice, whenever God decides to schedule it. The Perla is evidence. The ledger is evidence. Whoever holds evidence when the inquiry sits will hold power over a great many important men." He produced a card — plain, unmarked except for a number written in pencil — and slid it into the gap between her coat buttons, and she let him, because struggling would have been a confession. "When you decide you've seen it, send word. The marks are yours either way. Consider the first payment an act of faith."

"I told you I haven't seen anything."

"Yes," Tomas said. He was already turning away, coat collar rising, becoming just another scarred man in a city full of them. Over his shoulder, without stopping, he added: "Your father taught me navigation, you know. On the Perla, the summer before she sank. He spoke about his daughter. Said she had his eyes and her mother's caution." The crooked eye found her one last time in the failing light. "He was right about both."

Then he was gone, down the pier, into the smoke and the missed lamps, and Juniper stood alone with the manifests trembling very slightly in her hand and the whole frozen river in front of her and one sentence repeating behind her ribs like a second pulse:

*He knows who I am. He has known from the beginning.*

* * *

She walked home the long way, through streets that smelled of coal smoke and boiled cabbage and the particular metallic dread of a city waiting for a war to end without believing it would. Soldiers lounged at the corner of the bridge, young ones, rifles cradled like infants, and she gave them the flat, lowered gaze of a serving girl and passed through their laughter untouched. At the market square a woman was selling candles stub by stub, and someone had chalked on the customs house wall, in letters a meter high: *THE SEA REMEMBERS.*

Juniper stopped and looked at it for longer than chalk deserved.

At the tenement on Zawalna Street, the stairs smelled of Mirele's soup and Dov's soldering flux, and Frau Zemel was asleep in her chair with her mouth open and the radio — forbidden, priceless — murmuring under a blanket like a guilty cat. Anshel, twelve years old and all elbows, was on the landing doing homework he was pretending was harder than it was, and he looked up when she passed and said, "You've got your thinking face on, Juni. Somebody's going to be sorry."

"Somebody usually is," she said, and ruffled his hair, and went up to her room.

She lit the candle stub. She knelt. She lifted the floorboard, and took out the ledger, and unwrapped it from its stocking shroud, and set it on her knees in the small circle of light.

Outside, the city ticked and settled. Somewhere a patrol boot-heel rang off cobblestones, passed, faded. Somewhere on the river the ice was forming, sheet by silent sheet, closing the water like a book.

*Tomas was the navigator. Ten men lived. One was listed as lost who wasn't lost.*

She turned to the last legible page, to the names in the margins, to the numbers that were not pearls, and pressed her palm flat against the warped paper as though she could hold her father's hand through it.

An arms inquiry. Evidence. Men who would burn it, men who would use it. A stranger paying in freshly printed lies, who had known her name before he ever saw her face.

Everything in her — the trained servant, the keeper of silences, the girl who had survived five years by wanting nothing — said *burn it, sink it, give it back to the river, it is not yours, grief is not a cargo you can salvage.*

But her mother's other lesson was older than the first, and it rose now, quiet as the candle flame: *the silences that keep people safe are the ones you choose. The ones chosen for you are just walls.*

Juniper Hartwell looked at her father's running, rightward-slanting hand, and made her choice the way she made all of them — alone, in the dark, with no one to see.

She rewrapped the ledger. She hid it deeper. And in the morning, when Mr. Kessel demanded the week's tallies, she handed them over neat and exact, and smiled her empty smile, and said nothing at all — while under the floorboard of a rented room, the dead went on writing.
