## 5. Mill Smoke and False Names

There is a particular dignity in being nobody, I've discovered, and I possess none of it. I have been a captain, an heir, a corpse, and now a fitter's mate with grease under his nails and a name — *T. Marlow*, if you please — that fits me like a borrowed overcoat, which is precisely what it is.

The Ashworth mills run six days a week and smell of oil, hot iron, and the particular despair of men who measure their lives in piece-rates. I fit bearings and true up shafts in the machine shop, and I am, I'm told by the foreman, a quick study. This is the first compliment my father's mills have ever paid me. For twenty-nine years they paid me nothing at all but dividends, and I now understand those were an apology made in advance.

The men watch me. That's the thing nobody warns you about when you decide to stop existing: the living are terribly observant. Old Barrow, who runs the grinding wheel, has decided I'm a Scouser on the run from a wife. Young Pritchard thinks I'm a conscientious objector hiding from the tribunals, and treats me with the elaborate courtesy one extends to a man who might be a coward or might be a saint, the distinction apparently not worth the effort of determining. They are both wrong, and I let them be, because a man with a story is a man with a door, and doors get kicked in.

* * *

It was Pritchard who showed me the noticeboard.

"Your lot's up," he said, meaning the war lists, the casualty lists, the little typewritten slips the families pin up beside the shift rosters as though grief were a scheduling matter. "Have a look. Your captain's on it."

My captain. I'd let slip, in my first week, that I'd held a commission — a stupid slip, corrected with a lie about being struck off for health, which at least had the advantage of being true in spirit. Pritchard had never forgotten. He walked me to the board with the eagerness of a man delivering news he'd rehearsed.

And there I was.

*ASHWORTH, Capt. E. R., killed in action, Passchendaele, October 1917. Beloved son of the late Josiah Ashworth, Esq. Deeply mourned.*

The *Manchester Evening Chronicle* had done me the courtesy of a paragraph. I read it four times. It was accurate in every particular except the one that mattered, and it was flattering in the way obituaries are flattering — generously, and to the wrong man. *A soldier of conspicuous gallantry.* I had been conspicuous, certainly. The gallantry was a matter still under review by a court-martial that no longer existed, because neither did I.

"You knew him, did you?" Pritchard asked. "Officer class. Different breed entirely, them."

"Different breed," I agreed. My voice came out steady, which I counted among the day's small miracles. "I met him once."

"What was he like?"

I looked at my own photograph — they'd used the commission portrait, the one where I'm twenty-four and certain of everything — and I said, "Taller than he deserved to be."

Pritchard laughed and clapped my shoulder, and I stood there in the mill yard with the smoke going up black from the stacks and my own death pinned to a board at eye level, fluttering in the wind like something impatient to be taken down.

* * *

Here is what I did not tell Pritchard, or Barrow, or the foreman, or the girl in the canteen who has begun to smile at me in a way I have no right to encourage: I did not die at Passchendaele. I died rather more slowly, over the course of a summer, in my father's study, reading documents I was never meant to find.

Josiah Ashworth kept two sets of books. I mean this in every sense. There were the mill accounts, and there was the other ledger — the one with a house in Chorlton, and a woman's name in it, and a boy's school fees, entered quarter by quarter for fifteen years with my father's meticulous, unembarrassable hand. *W. Winterbourne.* I said the name aloud once, alone in that study, and the sound of it was so strange in my mouth that I said it again, like a man testing whether a tooth is loose.

I was going to report him. That was the intention, dressed up as principle. The court-martial I eventually fled was for something smaller — falsified requisitions, the usual arithmetic of a supply line — but I had already decided, in the pompous way of a twenty-nine-year-old with a commission and a grievance, to expose the great fraud of my father's life. I had even, God help me, drafted a letter. I still had the draft when the requisition charges landed, and I understood, with the clarity that arrives only at the worst possible moment, how things would go. A captain facing disgrace is a useful instrument. A captain facing disgrace who also happens to be the heir, and who also happens to know about the second family — that is a man who must be managed. My father had friends in the right rooms. I would have been managed into silence, or managed into a cell, or managed into France and a hero's grave, and the last of those was the one on offer.

So I took it. I walked out of a dressing station during the worst of the shelling with a dead man's paybook in my pocket and my own left in the mud, and I let the war do what my father would have paid handsomely for, and I have been telling myself for four months that it was cowardice, because cowardice is at least a sin I understand.

It wasn't. I've had four months of mill smoke to think it over, and I know now what it was. It was the sound of a door closing on the only witness.

* * *

The lists go up on the board every Friday, and I have developed the habit — I would call it a compulsion, but that suggests I resist it — of reading them. Not the officers' honours, which are printed in the papers and read aloud in churches. The other ones. The names that appear nowhere official: the missing, the unrecorded, the men who went into an attack and came out of the paperwork entirely.

It was Barrow who explained it to me, in his way, which is to say sideways, over a billy of tea. "There's a fella at the convalescent ward," he said. "Keeps a book. Writes down every lad the Army loses track of. Families come to him when the War Office sends back a letter saying *no record*. He's got the record. Names, units, last seen, all of it. My sister's boy — no trace, eighteen months. This Winterbourne fellow had him written down. Date, place, what he was carrying. Gave her something to put on a stone."

"Winterbourne," I said.

"That's him. Quiet sort. Orderly. Does the work of three and takes credit for none." Barrow spat thoughtfully into the yard. "Officers won't give him the time of day. He's not one of them, see, whatever his manners say. But he's the only one writing our lads down."

I stood very still by the grinding wheel while Barrow went on about the weather, and inside my chest something turned over with the slow, grinding reluctance of a bearing that has run dry.

Wendell. My father's other son. The boy in the ledger, grown into a man who kept a ledger of his own — and what a symmetry my father would have appreciated, the old hypocrite, if he'd lived to see it, which he had not, having died the previous spring with his secrets intact and his estate divided exactly as his conscience required, which is to say entirely to me.

I had gone looking for Wendell, in the beginning. That was the original design of this disguise, before it collapsed into mere survival: find the half-brother, find out what he knew, find out whether Father had told him anything that could still hang me. I had imagined him — I am ashamed of this now, and I record it anyway, because a man who edits his own guilt is only committing it more thoroughly — I had imagined him as a resentful clerk of a man, hoarding grievances the way Father had hoarded money.

Instead he was keeping a book of names. Writing down, in his careful hand, the men the war had mislaid. Spending his invisibility on other people's grief. The boy raised as an error had made himself the keeper of every error the Army made, and I — I had let the world believe I was dead because it was convenient, and my name sat on that noticeboard among the true dead, taking up space, receiving Pritchard's rough sympathy, while somewhere across the district a woman was being told by the War Office that her son had never existed.

I had spent my whole life being the name that counted. Wendell had spent his being the name that didn't. And he had answered it by making himself a ledger for the voiceless, and I had answered mine by erasing myself, and one of us, it seemed, had inherited something of our father after all, and it wasn't the fortune.

* * *

They came on Thursday. Not for me — not yet — but near enough that I felt the draught.

Two men in civilian clothes, but no civilian dresses like that: boots too polished, coats too new, hats worn with the particular arrogance of men who have never once been asked to remove them. They stood in the yard gate while the shift changed and asked the timekeeper questions, and I watched them from the machine shop doorway with an oil rag in my hand, doing the arithmetic of distance to the canal gate.

Pritchard drifted over to me, casual as Sunday. "Askin' after deserters," he murmured. "Says there's one working this district. Says he's an officer gone native." He glanced at me, and there was a long moment in which I understood that Pritchard had done his own arithmetic, and had arrived at a figure, and was deciding — right there, in the smell of oil and the groan of the belts — what he was going to do with it.

"Anybody fit that bill?" I asked.

"Not as I'd know," Pritchard said, and turned back to his lathe.

I worked the rest of the shift with my heart going like a stitch-gun. They would come back. Men like that always come back, and each time they come back they have learned something, and what they would learn, sooner or later, was that the new fitter had held a commission and the new fitter never showed his papers and the new fitter's hands were a gentleman's hands gone soft only recently.

There was one man in Manchester who could get me out of it. One man who owed me nothing and whom I had never once written to, though I had learned his address from Father's ledger the way you learn a wound's location. The ward orderly. The keeper of names. My brother.

That night I took a scrap of paper and a pencil stub, and I sat in my lodgings under a single gas jet, and I wrote: *I know you keep lists. I need the man who keeps them. Please. — E.*

I read it back, and it seemed a poor, thin thing to carry fifteen years of silence. But the detectives would be at the mills again by Monday, and the ship listings were posted at the Exchange, and I was out of time for eloquence.

I addressed it to the convalescent ward, and I paid a boy twopence to deliver it, and I watched him run off through the smoke toward Ancoats, carrying the first true thing I had sent into the world since October.
