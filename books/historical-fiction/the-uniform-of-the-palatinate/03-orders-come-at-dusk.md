## 3. Orders Come at Dusk

The clock had been running true for six days when the soldiers came.

Noor knew it first in their hands. They were up in the tower, oiling the great escapement with a brush no wider than an eyelash, when they felt the tremor come up through the stone — not the clock's own heartbeat, which Noor knew as intimately as their own pulse, but something heavier and less regular. Hooves. Many of them, coming up the hill road from the valley.

They put down the brush and went to the narrow window, and there they were: a column of riders in grey-and-blue, bayonets catching the last copper light of the afternoon, moving through Merenzoll's single street with the unhurried confidence of people who had already decided the town belonged to them. Behind the horses came two supply wagons, and behind the wagons came the sound Noor would remember longest — not drums, not orders, but the ordinary creak of leather and the ordinary murmur of men talking about supper. War, it turned out, traveled mostly as logistics.

"Well," said Herr Brandt's voice below, faintly, from the observatory steps. "Well, well."

Noor had learned in four years of apprenticeship that Herr Brandt's "well, well" was what other men used a whole paragraph for. It could mean *the comet has arrived*, or *the roof leaks again*, or *we are all about to be extremely inconvenienced*. From the tone of this one, Noor guessed the third, possibly with elements of the second.

They wiped their hands carefully on the rag — a clockmaker's hands should never be hurried, even now, even especially now — and went down the tower stairs to see the world they had so carefully arranged begin to be taken apart.

* * *

The requisition happened in the market square, and it happened the way weather happens: impersonally, thoroughly, and without any possibility of argument.

A major with a voice like a filing cabinet read the decree from a sheet of paper while the town stood in the cold and listened. The Palatinate borderlands were hereby placed under imperial administration for the duration of hostilities. All public buildings were hereby placed at the disposal of the army. A census ledger of all residents, with names, trades, and origins, was hereby required to be surrendered for inspection within one day.

Noor stood at the edge of the crowd, hood up, hands in pockets, being nobody with the practiced ease of long habit. It was a skill like any other. You kept your shoulders rounded. You let your gaze rest on middling distances — fence posts, puddles, the middle button of a stranger's coat. You did not look at soldiers, because soldiers noticed being looked at, and you did not avoid looking at them either, because that was its own kind of notice. You simply existed at the exact weight of furniture.

It had worked for four years. Master Albrecht had taught it to them without ever naming it, simply by example: *Be useful, be precise, be dull. Nobody requisitions a man who is essentially a well-made hinge.*

The trouble was that the decree said *all residents*. And somewhere in the clerk's office upstairs lay a ledger in which every resident of Merenzoll was written down in Herr Brandt's beautiful upright hand — including one entry that was not entirely true, altered years ago by a dying old man with a steady hand and a bottle of matching ink, so that a mixed-heritage apprentice from nowhere in particular might pass through the world unweighed.

Noor thought about that entry the way you think about a tooth that has begun, very quietly, to ache.

Beside them, Frau Immel the baker's wife whispered to her neighbor, "Census! As if we were sheep." Her neighbor shushed her with real fear, and Noor understood the fear even while sympathizing with the sentiment. Sheep got counted. Counted things got moved, sold, taken. The whole point of a ledger was that it made people portable.

The major finished reading, rolled the paper up with a snap, and announced that the observatory on the hill — the building currently containing the master clock, Noor's tools, Noor's bed, and the entire architecture of Noor's small safe life — would serve as a signal station for the garrison, effective immediately.

"Effective immediately," Herr Brandt repeated later, in his office, in the tone of a man tasting something. "As though the building might have argued."

* * *

They came up the hill at dusk, and Noor watched the world get inventoried.

There were six of them, led by a sergeant with a red face and a clipboard, and they moved through the observatory with the brisk courtesy of men who had done this forty times and expected no trouble and would have been almost disappointed by it. They measured rooms. They marked doors with chalk. One of them climbed halfway up the tower, looked at the view across the valley toward the enemy lines, and made a satisfied noise, the way another man might look at a good pasture.

"The flag goes on the roof," the sergeant said. "Wire for the signal lamps along the east parapet. The ground floor is barracks. The cellar—" he glanced at his list— "cellar is storage."

"Storage," said Herr Brandt, who had insisted on walking up with them, radiating the indignation of a man whose entire life was paperwork being handled carelessly. "Young man, that cellar contains the observatory's instrument crates, some of which are older than your army."

"Then they'll be stored historically," said the sergeant, without looking up.

Noor, standing by the door with their satchel strap gripped in one hand, felt something twist. Not the cellar itself — though God knew what would happen if soldiers started moving crates around down there and found whatever they found. But the principle of it. A place was only invisible if nobody looked inside it. That was the whole craft. That was the whole religion. And here were men whose profession was looking inside things.

"And the clock?" Herr Brandt asked. "The master clock? I trust the army does not intend to store *that*."

The sergeant finally looked up, and his eyes found Noor with the mild, terrible efficiency of a man checking a box. "You're the clock mechanic?"

"Apprentice," said Noor.

"You're the one who keeps it running?"

"I keep it running."

"Good. Then you'll go on keeping it running. For us." He said it the way he'd say *you'll go on breathing*, presumably. "Garrison needs accurate time. Signal station runs on the hour. If that clock stops, stops early, stops late, runs fast, runs slow — that's interference with military communications, and it gets treated accordingly." He wrote something on his list. "Name?"

And there it was. The question, arriving on schedule, patient as a pendulum.

"Eastwick," said Noor. "Noor Eastwick. Apprentice to the late Master Albrecht Voss."

"Eastwick," the sergeant repeated, writing it. He didn't ask about origins. He didn't need to; the ledger would tell him everything, tomorrow, when it was surrendered. Noor watched the pen move and thought: *somewhere in that book is my name, and beside my name is a lie, and between me and that lie stands one day.*

"Where do you sleep?" the sergeant asked.

"Here. The old watch room."

"Not anymore. Ground floor's barracks. You can have the alcove off the clock room." He said it not unkindly, which somehow made it worse. Kindness in an occupying officer was just efficiency wearing a softer coat. "You'll want to be near the mechanism anyway. I'm told you clock people are particular."

"We're particular," Noor agreed, and heard their own voice come out level and dull and exactly the weight of furniture, and hated how good at it they had become.

* * *

By full dark the transformation was well underway, and Noor sat in the clock room among the ticking weights and the smell of oil and gun grease, listening to the observatory become something else.

Boots on the stairs where there had been slippered feet. A lantern hung on the hook where Herr Brandt's almanac had hung. Two soldiers were already arguing amiably over a card game in what had been the chart room, their voices rising and falling beneath the clock's deep tick like fish under ice. Someone had nailed a board over half the south window without asking whether the draft mattered to the mechanism. (It did. Noor would fix it tomorrow, quietly, because a clock that ran badly reflected on its keeper, and Noor intended to remain the kind of keeper nobody thought about.)

Herr Brandt lingered at the door, hat in hand, looking like a man visiting a grave that had been converted into a tavern.

"They took the ledger at sundown," he said quietly. "Official receipt and everything. Very proper. Very legal." He turned his hat in his fingers. "I copied nothing, you understand. Copying would have been obstruction."

"No," Noor agreed. "Of course."

"But a man's memory is his own property," Herr Brandt said, gazing at the ceiling with tremendous innocence, "and mine happens to be excellent. Page forty-one, third column, fourth entry from the bottom. In case anyone ever needs to know what was written before certain parties improved upon the record." He looked at Noor then, and for once the fussy clerk fell away and something older and steadier looked out. "Master Albrecht did a careful thing, boy. Careful things hold. That's what he always said, isn't it? Careful things hold."

"Yes," Noor said. "That's what he said."

"So hold." Herr Brandt settled his hat on his head. "And oil whatever needs oiling. I suspect we shall all be needing to run smoothly for some time."

He left, and Noor stood alone in the clock room, in the one corner of the building that still smelled of themselves, and took inventory the way they always did when the world shifted: what fits in a satchel. Tools. Files. The good loupe. Three days' bread if they were sparing. That was the whole estate, and it had always been enough, because the rest of safety had never lived in things. It had lived in being overlooked. In the ledger's quiet lie. In the town's habit of seeing a clock mechanic and nothing past it.

And now the ledger was in a soldier's strongbox, and soldiers slept four floors below, and the clock — their clock, the one thing in the world that answered to their hands alone — was officially the army's timekeeper, and Noor was its hired pulse.

Above them, the great wheel turned. Below them, dice rattled. Out in the valley, somewhere past the dark hills, other armies were also counting hours, counting men, counting everything.

Noor climbed the tower stairs to make the midnight winding, because the clock must run, because running was the job, because a stopped clock drew eyes the way nothing else did. At the top, they paused with the crank in both hands and looked out the window at the valley, black and vast and full of things they couldn't count.

The clock struck twelve beneath their palms, twelve clean notes rolling down over the occupied town, and Noor realized, with a small cold clarity, that from tonight onward every note belonged to someone else — and that whoever controlled those notes controlled what the whole valley believed about time.

Below, on the stairs, boots were climbing. Measured ones. An officer's pace, unhurried, stopping at the clock room door.

Someone was coming up to inspect the mechanism.
