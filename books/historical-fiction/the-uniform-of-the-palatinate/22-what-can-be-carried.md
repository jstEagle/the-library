## 22. What Can Be Carried

The paperwork of peace, Noor discovered, was heavier than the paperwork of war.

It arrived in three crates and a leather portfolio, borne up the observatory hill by a clerk so young he still had ink on his knuckles from the academy, and it required signatures — Noor's signature — in four places, seven places, eleven places, until Noor's hand ached with the strange novelty of writing their own name honestly. *Noor Eastwick. Apprentice clockmaker, late of Merenzoll.* The clerk watched each signature with the reverent attention of a man who had heard the story and did not quite believe it: that a clock had ended a battle, that a forged ledger page had been ruled admissible evidence of good intent, that a lieutenant had stood before a tribunal and testified, with a straight face and a steady voice, that *the mechanism acted alone.*

"The tribunal's finding," the clerk said, for perhaps the fourth time, "is that no person struck the hour."

"No person struck the hour," Noor agreed.

"And yet the hour was struck."

"Clocks are like that." Noor blotted the page. "You wind them well enough, and they do what you built them to do, whether or not anyone is watching."

The clerk considered this for a long moment, decided it was probably not sedition, and packed the papers away.

That had been nine days ago. Since then, Merenzoll had been busy learning how to be itself again. The market square smelled of bread instead of gun oil. The garrison's tents came down one gray morning like a circus leaving town, and Captain Reiner left with them without ceremony, without apology, and — Noor noted with satisfaction — without the promotion his arithmetic had been designed to earn him. Elias had told her, in the flat tone he used for things that were true and unpleasant, that Reiner had been reassigned to a depot so far east that the maps ran out of detail before they reached it. "A place where nothing happens," Elias said, "which is, I think, the worst punishment they could have devised for him."

The hidden families had come up out of the winter hills one by one, blinking, thinner, alive. Marta Kellner had met them at the cellar door with soup and a face that gave away nothing except, if you knew where to look, everything. She had spent two weeks in an imperial cell and emerged with the same posture she'd gone in with, which was to say: like a woman who owned the building and was merely lending it to the authorities.

And the ledger had been rewritten.

Noor had been there for that. They all had. Herr Brandt, restored to his office and his tremor, had laid the great book open on the trestle table in the town hall while the whole town filed past, and every crossed-out name had been written back in fresh ink, and every altered entry had been set right — not corrected back into its old lie, but corrected forward into truth. When it came to the page with Noor's line on it, Brandt had dipped the pen and held it out.

"It should be your hand," he said. "The last forgery wasn't yours either. Let this one be."

So Noor had written, slowly, in front of God and the census and half the town:

*Eastwick, Noor. Clockmaker. Born here; made here; staying as long as staying is wanted.*

Marta, reading over their shoulder, had snorted. "'As long as staying is wanted.' You've written yourself a door, child."

"I've written myself a hinge," Noor said. "Doors swing both ways."

Now, on the last morning, the satchel lay open on the workbench beneath the clock room, and Noor was engaged in the oldest argument in the world: the argument between what a person owns and what a person can carry.

* * *

The tools went first, because the tools were non-negotiable. The pliers with the worn horn handles that had been Master Aldric's before they were Noor's. The file — *the* file, the one that had worked through a cell-bar gap and an escapement spring in the same fortnight, now polished bright from use and kept in its own cloth wrap like a relic, which, Noor supposed, it was. The loupe. The oiler with the bent tip that Noor had straightened so many times it had developed opinions. The small brass calipers. The screwdrivers, six of them, because five was cowardice.

Then the clothes, rolled tight. Then the letters — Marta's, in her blunt carpenter's hand, addressed to a cousin in Strasbourg who owed her a favor and a room above a joinery; Brandt's, formal and kind, certifying Noor Eastwick as a journeyman clockmaker with the town's gratitude and, more usefully, the town's seal; and one letter in a soldier's careful script that Noor had read exactly once and then put away, because some documents were for carrying rather than rereading.

The satchel took all of it and had room left over, which felt wrong. A satchel should be full. A life should fit what holds it, snug as a movement in its case.

Which was when Noor looked up at the master clock.

It hung in its tower like a patient heart, ticking away the morning, running true. Noor had spent three days after the armistice doing nothing but cleaning and adjusting it — washing out the fear, in a manner of speaking, along with the grit and the burnt lamp oil and the residue of everything the machine had been asked to do. It struck the hours now honestly, all of them, in order, and would go on doing so long after everyone currently listening to it was dust. That was the bargain of good clockwork. You served it, and then it outlived you and kept serving everyone else.

There was one gear, though. One gear that Noor had cut themselves, years ago, as an apprentice's exercise — a small brass wheel, thirty-six teeth, that Master Aldric had pronounced *adequate* in the tone that meant excellent, and had installed in the strike train where it had turned faithfully ever since. It had counted out every honest hour since. And it had counted out the false one too — the early hour, the merciful lie, the hour that had stopped two armies in their tracks and given a valley time to breathe.

Noor climbed the ladder to the movement, unhooked the retaining clip, and lifted the gear free.

The clock stumbled — a hitch in the tick, a fractional silence — and then the next wheel took up the load and the rhythm closed over the gap like water. Of course it did. Aldric had built it that way. Any single part could be removed, and the clock would go on, because that was what clocks were for: they were machines for continuing.

"You'll want a replacement," Noor told it. "I've left drawings on the bench. Whoever comes after will manage. They always manage. That's rather the point of apprentices."

The gear sat in Noor's palm, warm from the works, light as a coin and heavy as a year. Thirty-six teeth. Noor counted them once, out of habit, and found them all present, which was more than could be said for most things that winter.

Into the satchel it went, wrapped in the same cloth as the file. Two relics now. A person could carry two.

* * *

Marta found them at dawn on the observatory steps, which surprised nobody, because Marta Kellner had a genius for finding people precisely when they were trying to leave quietly.

"You're going," she said. Not a question. Marta didn't traffic in questions when statements would serve.

"I'm going."

"To Strasbourg."

"To Strasbourg first. After that—" Noor shrugged, and the shrug hurt pleasantly, the way a healed wound hurts when you test it. "After that I thought I'd see where the roads go. There are observatories down the Rhine. There are clocks everywhere, when you start looking. Broken ones, mostly. Somebody has to fix them."

"Somebody does." Marta looked them over with the frank appraisal she used on timber and on people, checking for rot and finding none. Then, abruptly, she pulled them into a hug that smelled of woodsmoke and cellar stone and soup, and held on hard enough to make a point. "You came here invisible," she said into Noor's ear, "and you're leaving visible. That's the only kind of progress there is. Don't let anyone talk you out of it."

"Marta—"

"Don't argue with a widow, it's unlucky." She stepped back, scrubbed at her eyes with the heel of her hand as though annoyed by them, and pressed something into Noor's palm: a small wooden whistle, carved from oak, shaped like a chaffinch. "Signal key," she said. "Retired model. If you're ever in a town and hear someone whistling a chaffinch twice and a wren once, you'll know you've found family. We're everywhere, we quiet people. We just don't put it in the census."

Noor laughed — really laughed, the sound surprising them both — and pocketed the whistle next to the gear.

Herr Brandt came up the hill ten minutes later, breathing hard, carrying a parcel wrapped in brown paper, and refused all help on principle. Inside the paper was a new apprentice's apron, leather, good stock, the ties still stiff.

"For the road," he said. "A journeyman ought to look the part. And—" he fumbled in his coat, produced a second, smaller item, and held it out with the gravity of a man handing over state secrets: a single sheet of paper, blank except for the town seal. "A letter of introduction, unwritten. Write it yourself, wherever you land. Say whatever you like about Merenzoll. We've earned a little fiction, I think. We've certainly practiced enough."

"Brandt—"

"Go," he said gruffly, "before I say something sentimental and have to deny it later. The clock's striking eight. You'll want to be past the mill by then."

He was right. The clock was winding up to the hour, gathering itself, and Noor stood a moment longer than necessary to hear it — thirty-six teeth' worth of honest brass, counting out eight strokes across the waking valley, each one landing clean. Somewhere below, shutters opened. Somewhere a dog complained. The ordinary machinery of a town beginning an ordinary day, which was, Noor reflected, the most extraordinary sound there was.

They shouldered the satchel. It sat right against the spine, full, snug, correct.

* * *

Elias was in the tower.

Noor saw him as they came around the last bend of the hill road — a figure at the parapet of the observatory, in civilian coat now, his commission formally resigned in a document Noor had witnessed and found deeply satisfying. He wasn't waving. Elias Voss did not wave; he observed, and had apparently decided that watching someone leave was a legitimate military exercise.

But he raised one hand when he saw them pause, and Noor raised one back, and between them stretched the whole length of the hill and the whole length of a promise, paid.

*One lesson in watchmaking, someday,* a boy had begged at a border fair, and had gotten his lesson at last, lamplit and desperate, and had learned an escapement well enough to lie to a tribunal with a clear conscience. The debt was settled. What remained was not debt. What remained, Noor suspected, was the thing that came after debts — the thing with no ledger entry and no fixed hour, the thing you couldn't requisition or forge or carry in a satchel, only keep.

Elias had said, two nights ago, standing in the clock room with his hand resting on the case as if greeting an old teacher: "I've requested a post with the boundary commission. Surveying. Maps, mostly. It's honest work, and it's near roads, and roads go places." He hadn't looked at Noor when he said it. He had looked at the pendulum, which swung with perfect indifference to human affairs, and Noor had understood that this was his way of saying *someday again*, and had answered in kind: "Roads go everywhere, if you walk them far enough."

Neither of them had promised anything. Promises, they had both learned, were dangerous things to make in wartime, and the war was only barely over. But the armistice had been signed, and the ink was dry, and somewhere south a border commission was waiting for a surveyor who knew terrain and a clockmaker who knew patience, and Noor had a letter of introduction with a blank space in it exactly the size of two names.

They walked.

Down through the town, past the market square where the baker waved with flour-white hands, past the town hall where the great ledger lay open on its stand for anyone to read, every name in its place, nothing hidden, nothing forged. Past the mill, where the wheel turned and threw cold spray into the pink light. Out onto the south road, where the frost was melting off the fences and the ditches ran loud with thaw.

At the top of the first rise Noor stopped and looked back once, because a person was allowed one look.

Merenzoll lay in its bowl of hills, roofs smoking gently, the observatory crowned with morning sun, and high on the tower a figure that had not moved, that would probably stand there until duty or breakfast recalled him, watching the road the way he watched everything — carefully, completely, as if it mattered.

Noor touched the satchel strap. Tools, letters, a retired signal whistle, a wooden apron too fine to wear, and one brass gear, thirty-six teeth, that had told one true lie and thereby told the truth.

Everything important fit. It always had. You just had to know what to keep.

Noor Eastwick — clockmaker, born here, made here, going wherever the roads went — turned south and walked into the day, and behind them, faint and faithful, the master clock began to count out nine.
