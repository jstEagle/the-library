## 6. Dispatches from a Dead Country

Petra had learned, in the years before the sea, that a city could be read the way a tailor reads cloth — by grain, by weight, by the direction of its wear. Valparaíso wore its streets like a coat turned twice: the bright side facing the harbor, the frayed side climbing the hills where the poor hung their laundry over the void. She had been in the city eleven days. Eleven days of measuring, pinning, hemming the uniforms of men packing up legations, and not once had she touched the pouch.

Tonight she would.

She dressed for it the way she dressed for everything — as a woman no one would remember. The grey coat, mended at the elbow with thread a shade too dark, the kind of repair that says *poor* without saying *desperate*. Beneath it, the pouch rode against her left ribs, sewn into the lining where a woman's body would defeat any casual pat-down. It had not left her body since Riga. She slept with it. She had bathed around it. Some nights she woke certain she could feel its pulse against her own, a second heart made of paper and treason.

The address was in her head, nowhere else. Calle Serrano, number 214, a stationer's shop with a green awning, third door past the church of San Francisco. Behind the shop, up a stair that smelled of glue and old paper, lived a man called only *el relojero* — the watchmaker — though no one in the network had ever seen him repair a watch. He was the first link in a chain that ran, in theory, from this frozen harbor across two oceans to a government that might, by now, exist only in the memory of men like him.

She left Casa Herrera at dusk, when the light went bronze and the gulls turned to smoke against it. Doña Inés was on the landing, beating a pillow with the violence of a woman who had outlived three wars and one husband and intended to outlive the pillows too.

"You're going out," Doña Inés said. Not a question. Nothing she said was ever a question; it was a verdict delivered in advance, with the sentence suspended.

"To post a letter," Petra said. "And to buy thread."

"Thread." Doña Inés snorted. "You buy thread like other women buy cigarettes. Emil says you have thread in four colors in your room and you buy more anyway." She struck the pillow again. A ghost of feathers escaped and hung in the lamplight. "Wear your scarf. The wind off the water goes through a woman like a bill through a gambler's hands."

Petra wound the scarf and went down into the street, and behind her she heard the pillow-beating resume, steady as a metronome, keeping time for a house that held its breath around her without knowing why.

* * *

Calle Serrano at night was a river of oil-lamp light and wet stone. The winter storms had passed through two days ago and the gutters still ran loud, carrying the hills down to the sea in brown ribbons. Petra walked the way she had been taught to walk in Vilnius in the last winter of the old world: never hurried, never lingering, a woman with an errand and no opinions. She let two trams pass. She stopped at a window of tin goods and studied a row of kerosene lamps until the street behind her resolved in the glass — a fisherman, a boy with a bundle of newspapers, no one turning when she turned. Only then did she go to the church.

San Francisco's tower rose black against the last light, its paint peeling in long curls, and in the lee of its wall the wind dropped away and the smell of candle wax and wet adobe gathered like a congregation. Third door past the church. She counted them without moving her lips. One — a chandlery, shuttered. Two — a barber, open, the smell of bay rum spilling into the street. Three —

A bakery.

Petra stood before it the way a woman stands before a grave she has walked a long way to find and finds the headstone bearing the wrong name. The green awning was gone. In its place hung a striped canvas, cream and rose, still stiff, still new. The window that had held stationery — she had memorized the description down to the fountain pen displayed in the corner, nib up, like a tiny obelisk — now held bread. Round loaves, braided loaves, a tray of something glazed and golden under glass. The door stood open to the warmth, and a man in a white apron was lifting sheets of rolls from a peel, and the smell that came out was so violently domestic, so innocent, that it made her dizzy.

She did not go in. She had been trained never to enter a dead address; a dead address was a mouth, and mouths could be made to speak.

She walked on. Past the bakery, past the fourth door, to the corner, where she bought a paper of roasted chestnuts from a cart whose brazier threw orange light up into her face. The vendor talked at her — the price of chestnuts, the price of everything, the English, the Germans, the ice in the harbor, *never in forty years, señora, never* — and she nodded and paid and ate one chestnut burning her fingers, and all the while her mind was running the arithmetic of ruin.

The network had been built like a garment: each seam holding two panels that must never see each other. She knew the watchmaker. She knew the phrase that opened him — a question about mending, asked in a particular way, with a word in her mother tongue buried in the middle of the Spanish. She did not know who came after him. That had been the elegance of it. Cut any one thread and the coat still hung.

But a coat could be cut in many places, and she had just found the first cut, and the question that would not leave her was *when*. When had the stationer's shop become a bakery? Weeks ago — the awning was too new, the bread too fresh, the apron unblemished by flour's grey patina. Months? Had the watchmaker been arrested before she ever boarded the freighter in Danzig? Had he been arrested while she stood on the deck watching the Baltic turn to iron behind her, reciting to herself the address like a prayer, like a rope thrown to a drowning woman?

She finished the chestnuts and dropped the paper into a bin and began, without deciding to, to climb.

* * *

The hills above the port were a country of their own — staircases instead of streets, houses stacked like cargo, funiculars groaning up and down their iron spines. She climbed because standing still was impossible and because from the heights a city could be read whole, and she needed, tonight, to read this one. She needed to know if it was watching her.

She took a funicular as far as Concepción, rode up in a car with a nun, a sailor, and a crate of live chickens, and stepped out onto the mirador where the whole harbor lay below her like a spilled jewelry box. Lights on the water. Lights on the waiting ships. The ice — the impossible, absurd ice, ice in a harbor at the edge of the Pacific, a thing the chestnut-seller had called a forty-year wonder — had thinned at the edges but still held the fleet in its white fist. Forty ships, someone had said. Sixty. Ships that had come to this coast for grain and nitrate and found themselves sealed in by a winter that had crossed the mountains to meet them, as if the Andes had decided that in this year of the century's great breaking, no one should be allowed to leave.

She stood at the rail and let the wind take her scarf and thought: *I am an orphan twice over.* Once by blood, in a village whose name the new maps would not carry. And now by craft — because a courier whose network is gone is not a courier. She was a woman with a secret sewn into her coat and no one to give it to, which was worse than having no secret at all. A secret without a destination did not fade. It fermented. It grew heavier by the day, and she could feel it, the pouch against her ribs, gaining weight like a stone in a rising tide.

There was a second address. There was always a second address; the training had been thorough even if the world had not been. A tobacconist on Calle Condell, a man who sold Turkish cigarettes and asked no questions, through whom a sealed envelope could travel to — somewhere. She had held that name in reserve the way a starving woman holds back the last of the bread. To use it was to spend it. Once spent, there would be nothing between her and the open sea of her own ignorance.

But the bakery had spent something first. The bakery had told her that the clock was running on all of it — the network, the pouch, the mission that had seemed so clear in a Riga basement in autumn, when her handler had held her shoulders and said, *You will be our memory, Petra. Everything they burn, you will carry out in your hands.* She had believed, boarding the freighter, that she was a thread in a living garment. The bakery said otherwise. The bakery said the garment had been cut away behind her, panel by panel, and that she might be the last seam of it left anywhere in the world.

She would spend the tobacconist. Tomorrow. Tonight she would go home, and sleep with the stone against her ribs, and in the morning she would buy Turkish cigarettes she did not smoke.

She turned from the rail — and stopped.

Below her, at the mouth of the pier where the pilot boats came and went, a man stood in the lamplight looking up at the mirador. She could not have said what made her certain he was looking at her; distance erased his face, and the harbor wind erased the sound of everything. But his stillness had a quality she recognized the way one recognizes a voice through a wall. It was the stillness of a man who counts things. She had stood like that herself, a hundred times, in a hundred stations.

Then a pilot boat's wake slapped the pilings, a gull screamed, and the man turned and walked away into the geometry of the pier, and Petra told herself she had imagined it, and knew she was lying, and went down the hill.

* * *

The tobacconist's shop on Calle Condell was narrow as a coffin and twice as dim. Cigarette cards papered the walls — boxers, aviators, a king of England in three poses — and behind the counter, in a cage of fly-specked glass, lay the Turkish cigarettes, gold-tipped, absurd, relics of a Europe that had been eating itself for two years and called it appetite.

The man behind the counter was old and yellow-fingered and had the eyes of a man who had watched a great many people buy things.

Petra had rehearsed the approach on the walk down: the ordinary purchase first, the coins counted out slowly, and then, at the end, the question, pitched low, with the buried word. *Do you carry* — and here the Lithuanian word for *thread*, *siūlas*, folded into the Spanish like a needle into a hem — *siūlas, tobacco from further north? Something a woman could order, if her husband wrote to her?*

She bought the cigarettes. She counted the coins. She asked the question.

The old man's face did not change, and that was the answer. A living link would have flickered — a blink a half-beat too long, a hand pausing on the till. She had seen the flicker a hundred times in Vilnius; she had learned to read it in the faces of bakers and librarians and one unforgettable priest. The tobacconist's face was a wall, and walls were what the network had built itself out of, but a wall that had never been told anything was different from a wall keeping a secret, and Petra knew the difference the way she knew the weight of different cloths.

"I can order it," the old man said at last, in the tone of a man humoring a foreigner. "From Valdivia. It takes a month."

"From further north than that," Petra said, and heard the small desperation in her own voice and hated it.

"North of Valdivia is Santiago, and Santiago is over the mountains, and the mountains are closed, señora." He shrugged, a shrug that contained the entire winter, the entire war, the entire frozen harbor with its sixty sealed ships. "Everything takes a month now. Everything takes a season. Come back in spring."

She walked out with the Turkish cigarettes in her pocket, unsmoked, and stood in the street and let the rain, which had begun without her noticing, land on her face.

*Siūlas.* The word sat in her mouth like a coin from a country that no longer minted them. Both addresses spent or dead. The watchmaker gone — sold, fled, arrested, she would never know which, and not knowing was its own kind of wound, because the watchmaker had taught her, in a single afternoon two years ago, how to sew a lining so that no customs man on earth would find it, and she had liked him, in the small bloodless way one likes the instruments of one's own survival. Somewhere he was a bakery now. Somewhere he was a cell. Somewhere he was a name on a list in a Berlin archive, filed under a country that had thirty days, or sixty, left to exist at all.

She thought of Marta, at the boarding house, practicing scales on a piano with three dead keys because the tuner had fled to Buenos Aires and no one else would climb the hill. Marta, who still tuned the dead keys by ear, who still played as if the piano were whole. *You keep the instrument alive by playing it,* Marta had said, laughing, the first time Petra had lingered in the doorway to listen. *Even if half of it is silence. Especially then.*

Perhaps that was what she was now. An instrument with dead keys, played anyway.

The rain thickened. She turned toward the hill and the house with the pillow-beating landlady and the pianist and the Czech clerk who flinched at loud voices, and she climbed the stair-streets with her head down and the pouch riding her ribs like a second, colder heart, and she understood, with the clarity that comes only at the bottom of a long fall, that whatever the pouch contained, whatever her government had trusted her to carry out of the burning house of Europe, she was now its only keeper. No network would come for it. No handler would surface with instructions. There was no chain left to pull.

There was only her, and the seam, and the question she had not yet let herself ask: *what, exactly, am I carrying?*

She reached Casa Herrera as the lamps were being lit. Through the dining-room window she could see the table being set for many more than lived in the house — Doña Inés fed strays, it seemed, the way other women fed birds — and she could hear, faintly, through glass and rain, the piano, all its dead keys and living ones, working through a melody she almost recognized.

Inside, the smell of onions and oil. Emil was arguing with Marta about whether a country was its soil or its songs, and Doña Inés was telling them both that countries were kitchens, and the argument was already turning into laughter, the particular laughter of people with nothing left to lose except each other.

Petra stood in the doorway with the rain still dripping from her scarf, holding her useless cigarettes, and Doña Inés looked up and said, "You're late, and you're wet, and the soup won't wait for the likes of you. Sit."

And Petra, who had spent eleven days being no one's anything, sat.
