## 7. Doña Inés's Table

The smell reached Marta's room before the bell did — onions surrendering to oil, garlic, something with cumin that had no business being in a Chilean kitchen and every business being in Doña Inés's, because the old woman cooked the way she kept the house: generously, inaccurately, as if feeding all of South America at once.

Marta heard the bell through the floorboards, one long pull on the rope that frayed a little more each week, and she sat for a moment longer at the window with her hands in her lap, listening to the harbor below Cerro Alegre. The fog had come in off the Pacific the way it always did in June, erasing the masts one by one until the bay looked like a page with the writing rubbed out. Somewhere down there a ship was loading or unloading or simply waiting, the way they all waited now, frozen into the ice of paperwork and closed passes and wars that had already decided everything except what to do with the people left over.

She counted her fingers the way her mother had taught her before recitals. Ten. Still ten. The arthritis in the left hand's third knuckle was a small betrayal, but a manageable one.

Then she went down to dinner.

* * *

The dining room of Casa Herrera had once, judging by the wallpaper's ambition, been meant for grandeur. Now the roses on the walls had faded to the color of weak tea, and the chandelier hung with two bulbs where six crystals-and-candles had been, and the long table — a proper table, mahogany, scarred by three generations of elbows — was crowded with mismatched chairs gathered like refugees themselves from every auction and alleyway on the hill.

Doña Inés presided from the head without presiding at all. She moved. That was the thing about her that Marta had noticed first and loved most: a woman of seventy-odd years who never sat while there was a bowl to carry, a chair to adjust, a plate to fill a second time for a boy who claimed he wasn't hungry. She hovered like a benevolent weather system.

"Sit, sit," she said to Marta, though Marta was already sitting. "Emil, move your elbow, you're guarding the bread like it's state secrets."

"Everything is state secrets now," Emil said, moving his elbow. He was a narrow Czech man with the posture of a question mark and the eyes of someone who read the last page of books first so he would know how much to hope. He worked — had worked — at a shipping office that no longer required him, and he spent his days now translating newspaper items for anyone who asked, and several who didn't.

The new one came down the stairs last. Petra. The seamstress from the freighter. She entered the dining room the way Marta imagined a cat enters a church: silently, correctly, with an expression suggesting she understood the building was not built for her but she would respect it anyway. She took the chair nearest the door. Of course she did. Marta had watched her do it four nights running now. Nearest the door, back to the wall, hands visible on the table. Marta knew that geometry. She'd seen it in Riga, in the faces of people who had learned that rooms have exits the way pianos have pedals — you don't think about them until suddenly you must.

"You look like a funeral, all of you," Doña Inés announced, setting down a tureen with both hands and a small grunt. "Which is permitted, because it is Thursday, and Thursdays are for funerals. But first, soup."

"It's Wednesday, Doña Inés," said Emil.

"Every day is Thursday when you eat my cooking."

The soup was a fish stew that had started life intending to be bouillabaisse and ended up somewhere near Valparaíso, which was, Marta reflected, true of most of them. There was bread that Emil guarded and wine that Doña Inés poured without asking, cheap red wine in glasses that did not match, and the gas lamp hissed its small yellow light over the table, and outside the fog pressed against the windows like a face trying to remember whose house this was.

It was Petra, of all people, who broke the silence first. Marta marked it. Four nights of silence, and then:

"The harbor's frozen," she said quietly, to her bowl more than to anyone. "Three ships turned back this morning. The pilot boats can't get out past the point."

"Frozen," snorted Tomás Reyes, who had appeared at the table as he sometimes did, uninvited and unwashed from the docks, smelling of diesel and salt, occupying a chair with the ease of a man who had decided long ago that chairs owed him nothing. "Chilean harbors don't freeze, señora. It's the *paperwork* that freezes. The winter storms close the pass, the consuls stop signing, and suddenly the whole bay is a waiting room." He tore bread. "I've piloted ships through worse than this fog. You can't pilot through a ministry."

"You're a pilot?" Petra asked. Her voice stayed level, but Marta saw the small stillness in her, the way a deer goes still — no, not a deer. Something more deliberate than a deer. A sentry.

"Harbor pilot. Twenty-two years. Before that—" he waved a hand, "—navy. Before that, a boy on these hills who thought the sea was a horse you could ride."

"And was it?"

"No. It's a government. It lets you ride only where it has already decided to let you." He drank. "Like everything else."

Marta found herself smiling into her wine. She hadn't spoken yet. She often didn't, at first. Words were not her instrument; she had spent forty years learning that anything worth saying could be said better in D minor, and besides, in the boarding house economy of Casa Herrera, everyone had a role, and hers had settled naturally into the listener, the one who watched the table the way she watched a conductor's baton, waiting for her entrance.

Her entrance came, as it usually did, via Emil.

"I had a letter today," Emil said, and the table quieted, because Emil's letters were always from somewhere gone. "From my cousin in Brno. Or — I say Brno. I say Czechoslovakia. But who knows what it will be called when the letter arrives, or whether the address will mean anything. He writes that the new administration has renamed four streets in his town. Two of them are named after men nobody has ever heard of." He turned his bread over, examining it. "My grandfather was born under Austria-Hungary. My father under the First Republic. Me under the Protectorate, soon maybe under Germany, after the war under the Soviets, God willing under something else again. Four countries. One house. The same stove in the same kitchen under all of them." He looked up. "So I ask you honestly, as a philosophical exercise: did Czechoslovakia ever exist? Or was it just a name we agreed to write between the lines?"

"Oh, here we go," said Tomás, delighted.

"He does this every week," Marta said, and was surprised to hear her own voice enter the conversation, low and accented, her English still wearing its Baltic wool coat even after all these months of practice. "Every Thursday."

"It's Wednesday," said everyone, including Doña Inés, who then contradicted herself by adding, "but it should be Thursday, so continue, Emil."

* * *

The argument, when it properly arrived, came in on the tide of the second glass of wine, as arguments at Doña Inés's table always did.

It began reasonably enough. Emil maintained that nations were stories told by mapmakers with good handwriting. Tomás countered that Chile was older than any story about Chile — older than its name, older than its borders, a fact of coastline and cordillera that existed whether or not anyone got around to describing it. Doña Inés said that countries were like husbands: you defended them in public and complained about them in private, and in the end you buried them and missed them terribly and could not for the life of you remember why.

And then Marta heard herself say the thing she had been not-saying for weeks.

"Latvia existed."

The table turned. She set down her glass. Her hands, she noticed, had gone cold, and she pressed them flat against the tablecloth, against the worn roses, feeling the weave of it, real, present, this table in this room in this city at the bottom of the world.

"You're all very clever," she said. "Very philosophical. Emil renames his streets, Tomás says the mountains don't care. But I will tell you what existed. My grandmother's kitchen existed. It smelled of caraway and dill and the smoke of the stove in November. The river Daugava existed; I swam in it when I was nine and lost a shoe to the current and cried as though I'd lost a limb. There was a piano teacher named Anna Kalniņa who had hands like white birds, and she made me play scales until my fingers bled, and she said, *Marta, music is the only border no army can move.*" Her voice did something she hadn't authorized it to do. She continued anyway. "There was a bench on the embankment where a boy named Jāzeps kissed me in 1919, badly, both of us tasting of herring and courage. And there was a night in 1934 when men came to the conservatory and took the rector away, and we all pretended it was normal, and pretending it was normal was the beginning of the end. All of that existed. Not on maps. In kitchens. On benches. In bleeding fingers." She looked around the table, defiant, ashamed of her defiance, unable to stop. "You can dissolve a country with a signature. Fine. But you cannot dissolve Tuesday afternoons. You cannot annex my grandmother's kitchen. They can take the territory, but the kitchen is *here*—" and she touched her sternum, hard, "—and it is occupied territory now, and I am its last resistance, and I refuse, I absolutely refuse, to be told it never existed."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, a dog barked at the fog.

It was Petra who spoke, quietly, into the ringing silence. "The kitchen survives," she said. "That's what they can't requisition."

And Marta thought: *there it is.* Because it wasn't sympathy in the seamstress's voice, exactly. It was recognition. The tone of someone reading back to you your own handwriting.

"Do you have a kitchen, Petra?" Marta asked gently. "Somewhere?"

Petra's hands went still on the tablecloth. For a moment — half a moment — something moved behind her face like a fish under ice. Then she smiled, a small correct smile, and said, "A ship's galley. It smelled of diesel and boiled mutton. I don't recommend it."

"She's lying," Tomás said comfortably, reaching for the wine.

Everyone looked at him.

"People lie about their kitchens," he said. "It's the first rule of exile. Nobody tells you the truth about home until the third year, or the third bottle." He refilled Marta's glass, then Petra's, unasked, unhurried. "I'll get yours out of you eventually, señora. I count things for a living. Departures, arrivals, tonnage, lies."

"Do you count anchors too?" Emil asked, and it was an old joke between them, and Tomás gave him the finger, and the table laughed, and the laugh broke something open the way laughter does — not the wound, but the seal over it.

* * *

Later — much later, the tureen empty, the second bottle dead, Doña Inés bringing out flan that she claimed was her mother's recipe from Concepción but which tasted suspiciously of condensed milk and improvisation — the talk turned, as it always turned, to the future, which is to say to the passes, which is to say to escape.

"They say the snows won't clear until September," Emil said. "Maybe October. The railway men are betting on it. My contact says eleven centimeters of new snow above Los Andes yesterday."

"Eleven centimeters," Tomás repeated. "Your contact measures snow now. Everyone's a specialist in exile. I'm a specialist in anchor chains. Doña Inés is a specialist in other people's appetites."

"I am a specialist in *feeding* other people's appetites," Doña Inés corrected with dignity, "which is a higher art."

"And what are you a specialist in, Marta?" Emil asked. "Besides Chopin?"

"Chopin," Marta said, "was Polish. I specialize in Latvian composers, of whom the world knows none, which makes me the world's leading authority on nothing." She flexed her fingers under the table. "Though lately I specialize in waiting. It's remarkable how much technique waiting requires. Most people think it's passive. It isn't. Waiting is a discipline. You must hold the silence without letting it curdle. Like a fermata — the note doesn't end, but you must decide how long it lives."

"That's beautiful," said Doña Inés, "and also nonsense, because waiting is just hunger with better manners."

They laughed again. But Marta caught Petra watching her across the table — really watching, the way one musician watches another's hands — and when their eyes met, the seamstress didn't look away quickly, as polite people do, or hold it aggressively, as dangerous people do. She held it exactly as long as one colleague acknowledges another. Then she rose, thanked Doña Inés for the flan in careful Spanish, collected her own plate to wash — she always washed her own plate, always, as if cleanliness were a cover identity — and went up the stairs, silent as dust settling.

"Strange girl," said Tomás, watching the doorway after her.

"Careful girl," said Marta.

"There's a difference?"

"The difference," Marta said, "is that strange frightens people and careful frightens *me*." She stood, gathering the glasses, because her hands needed work when her mind was working. "I played in hotels for twenty years. You learn to read a room. That girl reads the room before she enters it. She sits facing the door. She counts the ships."

"So do I," said Tomás.

"Yes," Marta said, rinsing a glass under the tap in Doña Inés's cramped kitchen, the fog pressing gray against the little window. "And I've been meaning to ask you about that, pilot. Because honest sailors count ships for wages. But I've seen you counting them for free, at odd hours, with an expression I recognize." She dried the glass on her sleeve. "It's the expression of a man keeping a ledger."

Tomás was quiet for a moment. Through the wall they could hear Doña Inés scolding Emil affectionately about crumbs, and the gas lamp in the dining room buzzed its one note, and somewhere far below, a ship's horn sounded once into the fog — long, low, patient, the sound of something that intended to wait as long as necessary.

"Maybe I keep a ledger," Tomás said finally. "Maybe we all do. Hers is just newer than mine."

He left then, whistling something tuneless down the stairs and out into the fog, and Marta stood alone in the kitchen with the clean glasses gleaming in their rack, thinking about kitchens, occupied and otherwise, and about how the strangest thing about Casa Herrera — stranger than the faded roses, stranger than Doña Inés's Thursday-Wednesdays — was that a room full of people from countries that were dissolving, burning, renaming their streets, had somehow begun, without anyone deciding it, to set one extra place at the table.

For whom, she wondered. For whoever came next.

She was carrying the last glasses through when the knock came at the front door — three measured raps, courteous, unhurried, entirely wrong for the hour and the fog — and Doña Inés wiped her hands on her apron and went to answer it, and Marta stopped in the dining-room doorway and listened, her heart keeping time like a metronome set to a tempo she didn't recognize.

Through the frosted glass of the entry hall she could see a tall shape, hat brimmed, umbrella furled despite the mist, standing with the patience of a man who had crossed an ocean and considered a doorstep beneath his notice.

"Señora Herrera," said a voice, smooth and foreign and pleasant as oiled walnut. "Forgive the hour. My name is Vogel. I've just arrived on the steamer from Hamburg, and I'm told your house keeps... interesting company."
